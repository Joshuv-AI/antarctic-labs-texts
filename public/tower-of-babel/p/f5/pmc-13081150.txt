Relationships of Social Support, Motivation, and Self-Efficacy With Physical Activity Among Adolescents Aged 10–14: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Natalia Fraczek, Lorraine B. Robbins, Hesam A. Varpaei, Kimberly Arcoleo, Kimberly Clevenger, Jiying Ling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study involved secondary data analysis to: (1) examine relationships between psychosocial factors (autonomous motivation for physical activity [PA], PA self-efficacy, social support for PA) and various levels of PA intensity (light PA [LPA], moderate PA [MPA], vigorous PA [VPA]), and moderate-to-vigorous PA [MVPA]) in adolescents aged 10–14 years in the U.S.; and (2) investigate the influence of demographic, physiological, and activity-related characteristics (age, sex, race/ethnicity, body mass index [BMI], annual family income, cardiorespiratory fitness [CRF; estimated maximal oxygen uptake: VO2 max], current sports or cheerleading team participation, and non-sport PA program participation) on various PA intensities. In 2022–2024, adolescents completed demographic and psychosocial surveys and wore accelerometers to assess PA. Of 935 adolescents enrolled, 623 (66.6%) provided valid accelerometer data (≥4 days). Structural equation modeling was used. MPA, VPA, and MVPA were positively associated with male sex, higher CRF, and sports or cheerleading team participation. Age and BMI z-score were negative predictors of MVPA and VPA. MPA was negatively associated with age, and LPA was negatively associated with annual family income. Social support for PA predicted autonomous motivation for PA and PA self-efficacy. Indirect effects of social support on the various levels of PA intensity via autonomous motivation or PA self-efficacy were not significant. Strengthening social support for PA may enhance adolescents’ autonomous motivation for PA and PA self-efficacy to help them increase PA. However, indirect effects on the PA levels were not statistically significant. Findings underscore the promise and limitations of psychosocial pathways.ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier NCT04213014.

Despite the World Health Organization (World Health Organization, 2024) calling for adolescents to attain at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) daily (Michael et al., 2023), globally, 81% of adolescents are physically inactive (World Health Organization, 2024). Similarly, in the United States (U.S.), only about 24% of high school aged adolescents meet the recommended level of physical activity (PA; Michael et al., 2023). These data not only highlight the overall insufficiency of adolescent PA but also raise questions about how PA might differ in different demographic groups and by the various intensities of PA adolescents engage in. Investigating these influences requires examining not only demographic characteristics but also the psychosocial factors that shape adolescents’ engagement in PA. Psychosocial influences can be understood using Self-Determination Theory (SDT), which identifies motivation, self-efficacy, and social support as key factors in sustaining PA (Ryan & Deci, 2000).

Several demographic factors have been identified that contribute to this inactive lifestyle. For instance, studies have shown that Black and Hispanic adolescents are less likely to meet PA guidelines compared to their White peers (Xu et al., 2019). Additionally, girls consistently have lower levels of PA than boys. The World Health Organization (World Health Organization, 2024) indicated that 85% of adolescent girls do not meet PA requirements compared to 78% of boys. National data from the Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System (YRBSS) reported that the percentage of youth not engaging in at least 60 minutes of activity on at least one day was highest among Black female students (26.6%), then Hispanic female students (20%), and then among White female students (16.7%) compared to Hispanic male (12.3%), Black male (12.7%), and White male (10.2%) students (Kann et al., 2018). Research examining MVPA among adolescents found that older adolescents (ages 14–17) are significantly less active than their younger counterparts (ages 10–14), indicating a need to intervene with young adolescents before high school is reached (Chen et al., 2021). This information indicates the importance of understanding both the timing and underlying factors contributing to reduced overall PA during adolescence, as such knowledge is critical for developing targeted interventions that help adolescents meet recommendations.

In addition to race/ethnicity, sex, and age-related differences, socioeconomic status (SES) can have a profound impact on PA participation. Higher family income has been associated with greater PA among adolescents (Kantomaa et al., 2007). One reason for this occurrence is that adolescents from low-income families often face substantial barriers to engaging in regular PA, including limited opportunities to participate in sports or extracurricular programs and lack of access to safe and quality recreational facilities (Tandon et al., 2021). These challenges can significantly hinder their ability to participate in structured MPA or VPA, exacerbating the disparities across different socioeconomic groups.

Biological or physiological characteristics, such as body mass index (BMI) and cardiorespiratory (aerobic) fitness (CRF; estimated VO2 max), are also important predictors of adolescent PA, and may influence moderate to vigorous PA intensities. Higher CRF has been associated with increased capacity to engage in more vigorous PA (Black et al., 2016), whereas lower CRF may limit participation in higher intensity activities. Higher BMI has been linked to lower overall PA among pre-adolescents (Raine et al., 2023). One study found that LPA moderated the relationship between sedentary behavior and BMI among adolescents, suggesting that lower-intensity activity may still play a meaningful role in mitigating health risks (Ayala et al., 2022). Examining both BMI and CRF in relation to different PA intensities can provide a more nuanced understanding of the biological and physiological factors that influence adolescent activity patterns.

Additionally, studies have found that adolescents who participate in organized sports have a higher rate of meeting the PA guidelines. Specifically, boys who participated in organized sports at least three hours per week had higher PA (Lagestad et al., 2019). This increase was particularly evident for MVPA, but the impact on LPA or MPA was not examined. Efforts to increase PA and sports participation among adolescent girls have generally had small or limited effects (Owen et al., 2017). One study evaluating the Girls in Sport intervention reported that it was not effective in reducing the decline seen in PA among adolescent girls, primarily due to low implementation in most schools (Okely et al., 2017). This finding highlights the need to develop and implement strategies that effectively support girls’ engagement in PA.

Although these demographic, physiological, and activity-related characteristics warrant consideration when examining various adolescent PA intensities, further study is needed to fully comprehend the psychosocial factors underlying these PA levels. Specifically, a need exists to examine the relationships between the psychosocial factors and different intensities (MVPA, LPA, MPA, and VPA) in young adolescents, while understanding the influence of demographic, physiological, and activity-related characteristics.

According to the SDT, motivation is driven by fulfilling three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Ryan & Deci, 2000). These needs are crucial for fostering motivation for long-term engagement in PA. Motivation plays a central role in driving adolescents to engage and persist in PA, overcome barriers, and achieve long-term health goals (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Motivation for PA can be intrinsic or driven by a personal interest or desire to be active or achieve fitness goals. Intrinsic motivation also reflects personal enjoyment (Ng et al., 2012), which has been linked to more consistent PA participation. Another form of motivation is identified regulation, which refers to behaviors performed because their outcomes are valued (Howard et al., 2020). Intrinsic and identified, which are autonomous forms of motivation, have been positively associated with MVPA (Nogg et al., 2021). The ways in which autonomous motivation influences varying PA intensities remains unclear. Understanding these influences could offer unique insights into strategies that sustain young adolescents’ engagement in PA across different contexts and individual capabilities.

As motivation drives the desire to engage in PA, self-efficacy (e.g., competence) involves the confidence needed to overcome challenges and maintain consistency. Self-efficacy refers to individuals’ belief in their ability to accomplish specific tasks or behaviors, such as engaging in regular PA (Ryan & Dzewaltowski, 2002). Self-efficacy is influenced by past experiences and social support (relatedness), including verbal encouragement. When adolescents receive positive reinforcement from family members or peers, it enhances their belief in their ability to succeed. As adolescents experience success in PA, whether through achieving personal goals or receiving praise from their support networks, they are more likely to feel capable of maintaining an active lifestyle (Lin et al., 2024). Adolescents with high PA self-efficacy are more likely to persist in PA even when faced with obstacles, such as time constraints, lack of resources, or negative self-perceptions (Ryan & Dzewaltowski, 2002). Thus, fostering PA self-efficacy can be fundamental in helping young adolescents overcome barriers to PA and develop long-term active habits; however, it remains unclear whether PA self-efficacy similarly supports engagement across different PA intensities.

Research indicates that the presence of supportive family members, peers, and communities can enhance adolescents’ motivation to participate in PA (Laird et al., 2016). Social support not only provides encouragement but also helps create a sense of accountability, which leads to increased PA (Mendonça et al., 2014). For example, parents or caregivers can support adolescents by providing transportation or sports equipment, while positive peer relationships can make PA more enjoyable and provide social incentives for participation. Adolescents with high social support tend to have greater PA self-efficacy, which is linked to more frequent PA (Lin et al., 2024). However, Mendonça et al. (2014) noted in their systematic review that most studies do not differentiate PA intensities. Among the few studies that examined PA across different levels, social support was found to be associated with VPA but not MPA (Mendonça et al., 2014). This highlights a critical gap in understanding how social support may influence engagement across the full spectrum of PA intensities.

Few studies have jointly examined psychosocial and demographic correlates of objectively measured intensity-specific PA among early adolescents. Although studies have reported direct relationships between each of the three psychosocial factors (e.g., motivation, self-efficacy, and social support) and PA (Li et al., 2014; Mendonça et al., 2014; Ryan & Dzewaltowski, 2002), information is lacking on how these factors influence different PA intensities and if there is any influence from demographic, physiological, and activity-related characteristics (e.g., age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, family income, CRF, and participation in organized sports or non-sport PA programs). These factors may impact each exercise intensity differently due to biological, social, and environmental influences. For example, boys and older adolescents may engage in more MPA and VPA than girls and younger adolescents’ through school or community sports teams. Socioeconomic disparities may limit access to opportunities for MPA and VPA, such as organized sports or structured exercise programs, whereas LPA, such as walking, may be less impacted by socioeconomic barriers. Although attaining adequate MVPA is important for adolescent health, increases in LPA have been associated with improvements in cardiometabolic biomarkers (Carson et al., 2013) and long-term engagement in PA (Telford et al., 2023). Given that PA among adolescents in general is insufficient, promoting any intensity of PA, including LPA, can be beneficial in helping adolescents stay active.

To build on prior research and address these gaps, two aims of this exploratory study are to: (1) examine relationships between three psychosocial factors (autonomous motivation for PA, PA self-efficacy, social support for PA) and PA (MVPA as well as LPA, MPA, and VPA) in young adolescents aged 10–14 years in the US.; and (2) investigate the influence of demographic, physiological, and activity-related characteristics (e.g., age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, annual family income, CRF, current sports or cheerleading team participation, and non-sport PA program participation) on various PA intensities. Given that the 10- to 14-year-old range is a developmental period in which PA typically declines (Chen et al., 2021), interventions offered during these stages of life are most effective in establishing long-term healthy patterns (Todd et al., 2015). This study is expected to offer important information to inform the design of interventions to optimize health outcomes for all young adolescents.

Methods

Design

This cross-sectional study was a secondary analysis of baseline data from a large-scale individual randomized clinical trial (RCT) called Guys/Girls Opt for Activities for Life (GOAL) Trial (Robbins et al., 2024). The main goal of the trial was to examine the effect of a PA and nutrition intervention on improving adolescents’ % body fat, BMI, CRF, diet quality, MVPA, and quality of life. Another goal was to examine how the intervention impacts adolescents’ autonomous motivation for PA, social support for PA, and PA self-efficacy. The trial took place in 14 schools located in underserved urban areas in the Midwestern United States (US; Robbins et al., 2024). The GOAL intervention included an after-school club for the adolescents that involved PA and healthy eating/cooking, three parent-adolescent meetings, and a social networking website that included information and strategies that parents could use to help their adolescents with both behaviors. Baseline data were collected from the adolescents in the fall of 2022 (four schools), 2023 (five schools), and 2024 (five schools). The University Institutional Review Board approved this study (ID# 202500240).

Participants

A total of 935 adolescents in fifth through eighth grade from the 14 schools were enrolled in the study. To be eligible to participate in the trial, adolescents had to be 10 to 14 years of age. Also, they had to be: willing and able to participate in a GOAL after-school club two days a week for 13 weeks if randomly assigned to receive the GOAL intervention (and not to control condition [usual school activities]); able to attend the follow-up nine months after the end of the intervention; and able to read, speak, and understand English or have access to an interpreter. The adolescents also had to have a parent willing and able to serve as a support person to assist them with increasing their PA. If adolescents had any mental or physical health condition that would prevent safe participation in MVPA and cooking, or if they used medications that impact appetite, weight, or growth, they were excluded from the study (Robbins et al., 2024). Information about the exclusion criteria was obtained from parents via an online survey. Parents were asked to respond to related survey items by providing their initials (in spaces provided next to the items) if they agreed that their child had no mental or physical conditions preventing full engagement in PA and/or cooking in a safe manner and their child was not using these types of medications.

Recruitment and data collection

Study team members recruited adolescents at school assemblies, open houses, and lunches; during study or library time; and in the classrooms. Study team members also attended other school events, such as registration, sports events, and parent-teacher conferences, to inform parents and adolescents about the study. After obtained approval from school administrators, information about the study was shared with parents and adolescents via e-mails, text messages, and the school’s social media site. Parents or adolescents received a flyer with information about the study. The flyer included a QR code to connect them to the assent and consent forms. If adolescents received the flyer, they were asked to share the information with their parents first before completing any form. After written consent was obtained, parents were asked to complete the demographic survey and eligibility portion. After providing written assent, interested adolescents progressed to complete an online eligibility screening tool and baseline survey. Any incomplete measures were completed by the adolescents during the school day at a scheduled data collection time (Robbins et al., 2024).

Measures

Demographic and personal characteristics

The demographic survey included data on adolescents’ age, date of birth, sex, race, ethnicity, academic grade, and family income. Adolescents were asked if they were currently on a sports or cheerleading team and also if they were currently in any physically active program at their school or outside their school; responses choice were (1) yes or (0) no. For socioeconomic status (SES), parents were asked to report the family’s annual income.

Body mass index (BMI)

Trained RAs measured each adolescent behind a privacy screen. Height without shoes was assessed to the nearest .10 cm with a Shorr Board (Weigh and Measure, LLC, Olney, MD). To avoid interfering with height measurements, RAs asked adolescents to remove hair accessories or change hairstyles (no ponytail). If an adolescent refused, RAs obtained the measurement, but also measured hairdo height (using small, flexible plastic ruler) and recorded information on the online data collection form. Adolescents wearing light clothing and no shoes had their weight in kg measured to nearest .10 kg using a scale (Tanita, Tokyo, Japan). Two measurements were taken for height and weight. If the 2 differed by <0.5 cm and < 0.5 kg, they were averaged to determine final height and weight. If the 2 height measurements differed by ≥0.5 cm and weight measurements differed by ≥0.5 kg, a 3rd was taken. If the 3rd measurement was ≥0.5 cm or ≥0.5 kg different from the other two, the process was repeated by the measurement coordinator and collected data were recorded online directly into REDCap (Cohort 1) or Qualtrics (Cohorts 2 and 3). Adolescent BMI for age and sex was determined using the online SAS program for Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2024a) Growth Charts (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2022). Adolescents’ weight status was categorized as healthy weight (<85th percentile), overweight (85th to < 95th percentile), or obese (≥95th percentile; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2024b).

Cardiorespiratory fitness

To evaluate aerobic endurance, researchers used the 15-meter version of the PACER (Progressive Aerobic Cardiovascular Endurance Run), following the guidelines provided by The Cooper Institute (2017). Adolescents responded to timed audio prompts by running between two points marked on a flat surface. The test concluded after the individual failed to reach the line twice. At that point, a trained member of the research team noted the number of laps the participant had successfully completed. These lap counts were then processed using SAS software to estimate the participant’s CRF (VO2 max in mL/kg/min), which served as an indicator of CRF (Mahar et al., 2018).

Social support for PA

Adolescents completed the Family and Friend Social Support Scale to report their parents’ and friends’ instrumental assistance and emotional encouragement to exercise, be active, or participate in sports. The eight-item Family Social Support Scale was adapted from other scales demonstrating acceptable test-retest reliability, internal consistency, and validity (Ling et al., 2014; Prochaska et al., 2002). An additional three items comprised a Friend Social Support Scale that also demonstrated acceptable test-retest reliability, internal consistency, and validity (Ling et al., 2014; Prochaska et al., 2002). Slight modifications to the scales were made to increase the clarity and relevance of the items and avoid any ambiguity (Omrani et al., 2019). The internal consistency for this modified version of 11 items was indicated by a Cronbach’s α of .89. Respondents selected from the following five response options: (1) never, (2) rarely, (3) sometimes, (4) often and (5) very often, with higher scores reflecting greater social support (Robbins et al., 2024).

PA self-efficacy

The original eight-item Perceived PA Self-Efficacy Scale developed for use with fifth- through ninth-grade girls (Dishman, Hales, et al., 2010; Saunders et al., 1997) has established test—retest reliability, internal consistency, and validity (Dishman, Hales, et al., 2010). To avoid any overlap with social support for PA items and to reduce the response burden, the scale was modified to delete two items asking about an adolescent’s confidence in asking a parent (1 item) or friend (1 item) about PA, resulting in a total of 6 items. As adolescents are still developing thinking and decisional skills, modification of a scale to decrease the number of survey items for them to complete is justified to reduce their reluctance to participate and increase the response rate (De Vaus, 2014; Omrani et al., 2019). The Cronbach’s alpha of this 6-item version was .76. Respondents used the following four response options: (1) not at all sure, (2) not very sure, (3) somewhat sure, and (4) very sure. Higher scores indicated greater PA self-efficacy (Robbins et al., 2024).

Autonomous motivation for PA

The Behavioral Regulation in Exercise Questionnaire-3 (BREQ-3; Markland & Tobin, 2004; Wilson et al., 2006) was employed and has acceptable validity when used with adolescents (McDavid et al., 2012). Aligned with the SDT (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci, 2000), the BREQ-3 includes six subscales, each with four items, that capture various forms of motivation: intrinsic motivation (highest form), integrated regulation, identified regulation, introjected regulation, external regulation, and amotivation (lowest form; Markland & Tobin, 2004; Wilson et al., 2006). To reduce the response burden associated with a long survey (De Vaus, 2014; Omrani et al., 2019), the scale was decreased in length by deleting 12 items (the 3 subscales representing the lowest forms of motivation, including a lack of motivation; Ryan & Deci, 2020). Some of the remaining 12 items were slightly modified to simplify the wording. Using simple words is recommended to enhance comprehension and prevent reductions in motivation and concentration by this young age group (De Vaus, 2014; Omrani et al., 2019). In this current study, the intent was to focus on autonomous motivation, which included intrinsic motivation (4 items) and identified regulation (4 items; Ryan & Deci, 2020). The 8-item autonomous motivation subscale used to measure the two forms of motivation had a Cronbach’s α value of .88. Response choices ranged from (0) not true for me to (4) very true for me. Higher scores indicated higher autonomous motivation for PA.

PA

Minutes of MVPA per day were assessed via ActiGraph GT3X+ and wGT3X-BT (ActiGraph, Pensacola, Florida, USA) accelerometers, which are reliable for assessing PA duration and intensity (Hänggi et al., 2013). Data were collected using a monitor worn by adolescents on the right hip (attached to an elastic belt) for at least seven days (not while bathing, swimming, or sleeping) and set to record at 30 Hz. The data were then downloaded to the same computer used for setting up the monitors. To analyze the duration and intensity of each adolescent’s PA, the data were processed using the GGIR package (version 3.2–0) in R (Robbins et al., 2024). The GGIR package has been described extensively (Migueles et al., 2019); briefly, data are autocalibrated; accelerometer metrics are calculated (Euclidean Norm Minus One, ENMO in 5-s epochs); non-wear time is identified based on the standard deviation and range of the raw accelerometer data within a rolling time window; missing data (including non-wear) are imputed based on data from similar times of other available days; and time spent in LPA, MPA, or VPA is calculated using the Hildebrand hip-based ENMO thresholds (expressed in milligravity [mg]-based acceleration units [LPA: 63.3–142.5 mg; MPA: 142.6–464.5 mg; VPA: ≥464.6 mg]; Hildebrand et al., 2014). Participants with fewer than 4 days of at least 8 hours of valid daily data were excluded. No weekend data were required for inclusion; 533 (85.5%) of the 623 included participants provided at least one weekend day.

Data analysis

For this cross-sectional study, SAS software (Version 9.2) was used to perform the descriptive and regression statistical analyses and MPLus (V8.4) was employed for the structural equation (SEM) models. All demographic and psychosocial variables were reported as frequency and percentage (for nominal or categorical variables) or as mean and standard deviation (for continuous variables). In SEM, two latent variables were created to capture autonomous motivation for PA and social support for PA. The autonomous motivation latent variable was comprised of the observed indicators for intrinsic and identified motivation. The social support for PA latent variable was comprised of the family and friend social support for PA scores. The following direct effects were specified: 1) age, sex, income, race/ethnicity, BMI z-score, CRF (VO2 max), current PA program participation, and current sports or cheerleading team participation on social support for PA; 2) social support for PA on autonomous motivation for PA; 3) social support for PA on PA self-efficacy; and 4) age, sex, income, race/ethnicity, BMI z-score, current sports or cheerleading team participation, current non-sport PA program participation, autonomous motivation for PA, and PA self-efficacy on each PA intensity variable. Two indirect pathways were examined: 1) social support through autonomous motivation on each PA intensity outcome variable; and 2) social support through PA self-efficacy on each PA intensity outcome variable. It is important to note that the total number of participants may be lower in some areas because only those who wore accelerometers for a minimum of four days were included. An advantage of SEM is the ability to handle missing data through the specification of full information maximum likelihood estimation. Model fit was evaluated using standard indices, including the Akaike Information Criteria (AIC), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) with values indicating acceptable model fit. Standardized beta coefficients and associated standard errors along with p-values are reported.Model fit was evaluated using established criteria, with acceptable fit defined as RMSEA < 0.06, CFI ≥ 0.95, and SRMR < 0.08 (Hu & Bentler, 1999).

Results

Demographic and personal characteristics

Of the 935 enrolled adolescents, 804 (86%) returned their ActiGraph devices, and 623 (adherence rate = 77%) provided valid accelerometer data based on the wear-time adherence criteria (see Figure 1 for flow diagram of participant numbers). Adolescents in the study (N = 613) had a mean age of 12.07 ± 0.97 years. Slightly more than half were female (50.4%), and the majority identified as either White (42.7%) or Black (43.4%). The mean modified BMI z-score was 0.81 ± 1.32. Regarding family/household annual income, 27.1% reported earning ≤$19,999 per year, 36.0% earned $20,000–$69,999, and 36.8% earned ≥ $70,000. Estimated cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2 max) averaged 36.93 ± 3.22 mL/kg/min. About 25% participated in non-sport PA programs, and 36% were involved in sports or cheerleading teams.

Mean scores for psychosocial factors were 3.28 ± 0.93 for social support for PA, 3.18 ± 0.53 for PA self-efficacy, and 2.45 ± 0.88 for autonomous motivation for PA (all measured on standardized scales). Physical activity outcomes showed an average of 25.44 ± 17.60 minutes of MVPA, 56.91 ± 31.90 minutes of LPA, 22.90 ± 15.71 minutes of MPA, and 2.54 ± 3.80 minutes of VPA per day (see Table 1 for demographic, physiological, and activity-related characteristics).

Structural equation modeling findings

The structural equation modeling results (see Table 2) indicated that MVPA was significantly and positively associated with male sex (β = 0.18, p < .0001), CRF (VO2 max; β = 0.13, p = .001), and sports or cheerleading team participation (β = 0.15, p < .0001). MVPA was negatively associated with age (β = −0.12, p = .001) and BMI z-score (β = −0.08, p = .04). MPA was positively associated with male sex (β = 0.17, p < .0001), VO2 max (β = 0.11, p = .007), and sports or cheerleading team participation (β = 0.13, p = .001). MPA was negatively associated with age (β = −0.11, p = .001). Similarly, VPA was positively associated with male sex (β = 0.14, p < .0001), VO2 max (β = 0.16, p < .0001), and sports or cheerleading team participation (β = 0.19, p < .0001) and was negatively associated with both age (β = −0.08, p = .02) and BMI z-score (β = −0.08, p = .04). For LPA, income was the only significant predictor, showing a negative association (β = −0.10, p = .007).

Although social support for PA positively predicted autonomous motivation for PA and PA self-efficacy (β = 0.74 and 0.51, respectively; both p < .0001), the indirect effect of social support for PA on MVPA via autonomous motivation was not significant (β = 0.07, p = .08). The model showed good fit (RMSEA = .05, CFI = .96, TLI = .93, SRMR = .03), with an R2 of 0.30 for MVPA. Race/ethnicity was not significant in social support direct (β = 0.01 p = .67) and indirect (β = 0.003 p = .93) SEM analysis for any outcomes.

Social support for PA had strong direct effects on both autonomous motivation (β = 0.74, p < .0001) and PA self-efficacy (β = 0.51, p < .0001) across all models. The indirect effect of social support on MPA and LPA via autonomous motivation for PA was not significant (β = 0.08 and p = .05 for both), and all indirect effects via PA self-efficacy were nonsignificant. Other variables, including race/ethnicity, non-sport PA program participation, and PA self-efficacy, were not significant predictors in any of the models. All models demonstrated good fit (RMSEA = .05, CFI = .96, TLI = .93, SRMR = .03), with R2 values of 0.30 for each PA outcome (see Figure 2 for structural equation modeling: conceptual path diagram).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to explore the predictors of MVPA as well as other PA intensities in 10–14-year-old adolescents. Additionally, the study examined whether constructs of SDT, such as motivation for PA, PA self-efficacy, and social support for PA, vary by age, race/ethnicity, sex, BMI, family income, CRF, sports or cheerleading team, non-sport PA program participation, and if they are significantly associated with different PA levels. Several demographic, physiological, and activity-related predictors (e.g., sex, CRF, sports or cheerleading team participation, age, BMI, income, social support) were linked in ways consistent with SDT, suggesting that adolescents are more likely to engage in varying levels of PA when their autonomy, competence, and relatedness are supported.

Sex, CRF, and sports or cheerleading team participation contributed to MVPA, LPA, MPA, and VPA. Sex differences emerged with boys engaging in more PA than girls, consistent with previous findings that girls tend to be less physically active than boys (Tandon et al., 2021). This suggests that sex differences are not limited to VPA but extend to LPA and MPA levels as well. Higher CRF was associated with greater participation across all intensities, highlighting role of CRF not only in supporting VPA but also in enabling a broader range of physical movement, emphasizing its potential as a target for interventions aimed at increasing overall activity, including LPA. Participation in sports or cheerleading teams similarly promoted activity at every intensity, highlighting the role of organized team sports in promoting routine PA. These findings align with prior literature indicating that organized sports provide a structured, scheduled environment that fosters a sense of commitment to regular activity (Lagestad et al., 2019). While non-sport programs can offer flexibility and can be adapted to meet adolescents’ needs, non-sport PA program participation was not associated with any of the PA intensity levels in the structural equation modeling findings. From an SDT perspective, organized sport may provide adolescents with an environment that supports competence, autonomy, and relatedness (Ryan et al., 2009). Interventions to promote PA in adolescents may benefit from including organized sports while also considering additional options to avoid a one-size-fits-all approach and accommodate different preferences, time availability, and access to resources. By examining multiple PA intensities separately, and not just overall MVPA, this study provides insights into how demographic, physiological, and activity-related factors influence all PA levels among young adolescents.

Age was a predictor of MVPA, MPA, and VPA, highlighting that even within the 10–14 year age range, PA tends to decline as adolescents get older. Age-related declines were more pronounced for higher-intensity activity, while LPA was less affected. This finding aligns with prior research indicating declines in PA with age, with girls experiencing slightly steeper decreases than boys (Farooq et al., 2020). Additionally, higher BMI was associated with lower MVPA, consistent with prior research suggesting that adolescents with higher BMI may face greater barriers to engaging in MVPA (Raine et al., 2023). For LPA, income emerged as a predictor, with adolescents from higher-income families engaging in less LPA than those from lower-income families. This aligns with previous research suggesting that adolescents from higher-income families participate in more structured or vigorous PA (Kantomaa et al., 2007), whereas adolescents from lower-income families face barriers to such activities (Tandon et al., 2021) and may therefore accumulate more LPA through everyday movement. Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF, estimated VO2 max) was also a predictor for MPA and VPA, suggesting that adolescents with higher CRF have a greater capacity to sustain more intense activity, potentially creating a reinforcing cycle of PA and fitness (Black et al., 2016), which may not meaningfully impact LPA. In contrast, autonomous motivation for PA may play a more critical role for LPA and MPA, reinforcing the idea that internal drive may be especially important for sustaining everyday movements rather than more vigorous activity. For example, school-based activities can offer structured opportunities for LPA (Grasten & Watt, 2016); and according to SDT, these activities can support adolescents’ sense of competence and autonomy, fostering intrinsic motivation to engage in regular PA through personal interest and peer interaction (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Together, these findings provide new insights into how age and physiological factors differentially influence different PA intensities, which is especially important for identifying and supporting adolescent populations who may face barriers to exercise.

Social support for PA emerged as a predictor of both autonomous motivation for PA and PA self-efficacy across all activity intensities, providing new insight into its role in shaping adolescents’ activity levels. Specifically, the relationship between social support for PA and both LPA and MPA through autonomous motivation suggests that supportive relationships may be particularly important for encouraging everyday light and moderately intense activity. These findings indicate that social support primarily influences behavior by first enhancing internal motivation and confidence, which can then lead to behavioral changes in PA. These findings align with SDT, which highlights the importance of social connectedness in fostering autonomous motivation and sustained PA behavior (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Interventions aiming to increase adolescent PA may benefit from prioritizing the creation of supportive social environments, through peers, family, or community, that enhance both motivation and confidence, especially for initiating and maintaining lower-intensity activity. Focusing on LPA may be an effective first step in helping young adolescents build confidence and gradually engage in higher-intensity activity.

This study is unique because it explores LPA, MPA, and VPA separately, whereas prior research has focused primarily on MVPA, masking potential differences in how various intensities relate to demographic, physiological, and activity-related factors. Unlike MPA and VPA, which may often reflect purposeful engagement in exercise or sports, LPA is also important because it can account for a large proportion of adolescents’ total PA, especially among those who are less active overall. Additionally, LPA may be more accessible and sustainable for adolescents who face physical, social, or motivational barriers to higher-intensity PA, making LPA a valuable target for inclusive health promotion strategies. LPA can also replace sedentary behaviors, foster long-term engagement in PA, and perhaps even lead to more intense forms of PA (Telford et al., 2023). From an SDT perspective, LPA may serve as a more manageable activity in which adolescents can begin to develop autonomy and competence, helping to foster the autonomous motivation needed to eventually engage in more demanding MPA and VPA. LPA has beneficial effects on learning and academic outcomes; however, further research is needed to better understand its impact, as studies have yielded inconsistent results (Chim et al., 2024). As adolescent participation in PA remains insufficient, identifying distinct drivers of each PA intensity level is critical to designing interventions that engage all youth. Interventions should use a multifaceted approach, combining opportunities for organized activity with strategies that enhance motivation, strengthen PA self-efficacy, and foster supportive environments, in order to meet the needs of all adolescents and ensure that interventions support participation across all PA intensities.

Limitations and directions for future research

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships; therefore, the findings reflect associations rather than cause-and-effect. Future research using longitudinal designs would help clarify the directionality of these relationships. Another limitation is that the study’s recruitment criteria may have introduced selection bias, such as attracting participants with more engaged parents. For SES, parents self-reported their annual household income. Assessing SES via self-report is commonly used and justified method in survey-based research when objective SES data are unavailable or impractical to obtain. However, self-report is subject to limitations such as recall and social desirability bias (Althubaiti, 2016), as well as variability in participants’ interpretations of what is being asked. Reliance on self-reported data for autonomous motivation for PA, PA self-efficacy, and social support for PA also introduces the possibility of recall and social desirability bias, which may affect response accuracy and limit reliability and sensitivity in capturing nuanced psychosocial influences on PA. These limitations may partially explain the non-significant relationships observed, particularly when the subjective and objective measures (e.g., accelerometers) were associated (Dishman, Dunn, et al., 2010). Additionally, each psychosocial construct was measured with a single broad measure, which may not fully capture the complexity of these variables or various intensity levels of PA. Future researchers may want to consider using multi-dimensional measures of motivation for PA, PA self-efficacy, and social support for PA, and, whenever possible, objective measures. Future interventions should emphasize peer and parental support to foster adolescents’ autonomous motivation (autonomy) and self-efficacy, enhancing the likelihood of sustained engagement in PA.

Conclusion

Given that adolescence is a critical period for developing lifelong PA habits, researchers may want to examine demographic, physiological, activity-related, and psychosocial factors when developing PA interventions. Strengthening social support for PA, particularly from parents, family, and friends, with innovative and effective approaches, may be important for enhancing young adolescents’ PA self-efficacy and autonomous motivation to increase their PA, thus helping them to achieve the recommendations. However, the indirect effects on the various levels of PA were not statistically significant, highlighting both the promise and limitations of psychosocial pathways. Regardless, continued research is still needed to examine factors that uniquely influence PA intensity levels in adolescents and explore whether interventions to increase activity should start with lower intensity levels for some adolescents.

What does this article add?

This article contributes to the literature by using structural equation models to thoroughly examine how demographic, physiological, activity-related, and psychosocial factors (e.g., autonomous motivation for PA, social support for PA, and PA self-efficacy) interact and influence various PA intensity levels (measured via accelerometry). The study addresses a gap in the literature by showing that certain characteristics (e.g., male sex, younger age, increased CRF [VO2 max], sports or cheerleading team participation, lower BMI) are consistently associated with higher PA intensity levels, and lower annual family income is related to lower PA levels. The findings underscore the important role of sports or cheerleading team participation in assisting adolescents to achieve the PA recommendations. The critical role of social support in fostering autonomous motivation for PA and PA self-efficacy is also highlighted. The results provide a comprehensive understanding of how various factors influence PA intensity differently, reinforcing the need to develop targeted interventions that are tailored to address adolescent differences to meet their individual needs effectively.
