Prevalence of dental caries and associated factors in children from a quilombola territory: an intersectional analysis
Authors: Maria Letícia Barbosa Raymundo, Elza Cristina Farias de Araújo, Rênnis Oliveira da Silva, Armando Cabral Lira Neto, Lucas Xavier Bezerra de Menezes, Edson Hilan Gomes de Lucena, Rafael Aiello Bomfim, Yuri Wanderley Cavalcanti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQuilombolas are ethnic-racial groups with black ancestry and territorial ties to quilombo remnants. In 2023, the IBGE identified 494 Quilombola Territories in Brazil, housing about 1.3 million people (0.65% of the population). Historically marginalized, especially in health rights, Social Markers of Difference, such as race, class, and sex, impact the quality of life of Quilombolas. Social determinants like income and education contribute to health inequalities, particularly in oral health. Intersectionality helps analyze how different social markers interact. This study uses intersectionality to examine the oral health of children in a Quilombola community in northeastern Brazil, highlighting complex influencing factors.MethodsA cross-sectional, population-based epidemiological survey was carried out in a quilombola territory in the municipality of João Pessoa-PB, with children aged 5 to 8 years. The dmfs index (decayed, missing due to caries and filled primary teeth) and DMFS (decayed, missing due to caries and filled permanent teeth), and the pufa/PUFA index (consequence of untreated caries) were used. Three multilevel regression models were obtained, where the dependent variables were: dental caries experience, untreated dental caries, and PUFA index. Variables with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant, highlighting the influence of Social Determinants of Health and social markers of difference on the health and intersectionality of vulnerable populations.ResultsIn the Poisson multilevel regression model the intersection between self-attribution as Quilombola and cariogenic diet is associated with dental caries experience (PR = 1.84, CI 95% 1.04–3.26, p = 0.03) and untreated dental caries (PR = 2.82, CI 95% 1.12–7.07, P = 0.02). The intersection between self-attribution as Quilombola and ethnic-racial declaration is associated with dental caries experience (PR = 5.69, CI 95% 3.2-10.11, P < 0.001) and untreated dental caries (PR = 10.34, CI 95% 2.96–36.66, p < 0.001).ConclusionsSelf-attribution as quilombola, socioeconomic factors, and cariogenic diet are associated with the experience of dental caries and untreated dental caries in the quilombola territory, highlighting the influence of SDH and social markers of difference on the health of vulnerable populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24786-7.

Introduction

Quilombolas are ethnic-racial groups that self-identify as such, have black ancestry, and specific territorial ties known as remnants of quilombos [1]. According to the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE), in 2023, 494 Quilombola Territories were officially identified in the country, with a self-attributed Quilombola population of around 1.3 million people, equivalent to 0.65% of the total Brazilian population [2].

Historically, Quilombolas have faced marginalization regarding the guarantee of their rights, especially the right to health [3] The social structure marked by inequalities of race/ethnicity, social class, sex, or age, known as Social Markers of]Difference, impacts the quality of life of Quilombola populations [4, 5] Social Determinants of Health (SDH) such as income and education also reflect health inequities [6, 7]. These inequities directly affect the oral health of Quilombola communities, as observed in previous studies [8–10].

Intersectionality can be considered as an analytical tool to understand and explain the complexity in which one social marker of difference affects another [11]. Although it is a sociological concept, integrating an intersectionality framework into quantitative health research offers the opportunity to deepen analyses among social dimensions that may interact, especially regarding children, where several influencing factors at various levels can affect their oral health condition [12].

The issue of race has been reported as a significant factor in the distribution of health problems [10] and the high prevalence of dental caries in children, considered a global public health issue, has multiple etiological factors, with socioeconomic factors [13–15] and a sugar-rich diet being the main ones [16–18]. It is necessary to understand that these factors do not act in isolation but rather through complex interactions [19–21].

Given the absence of health information systems on the oral health of quilombola communities and the scarcity of studies conducted with children from quilombo remnants, this study aimed to analyze, through the lens of intersectionality theory, the oral health condition of children from a quilombola territory located in a capital city in northeastern Brazil.

Methods

Ethical aspects

The present study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles for research involving human subjects, as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Paraíba under protocol CAAE: 64220900.0.0000.5188.

Consent to participate in the study was obtained from the parents or legal guardians through the signing of the Informed Consent Form (ICF).

Study design

This is a census-based, cross-sectional, observational study conducted in the territory of the Quilombola Community of Paratibe, in the municipality of João Pessoa - PB, involving children aged 5 to 8 years and their guardians.

Study location

The Quilombola Community of Paratibe is located in Health District III of the municipality of João Pessoa-PB and is home to 150 families. According to the IBGE census, the Quilombola territory of Paratibe is the 6th largest Quilombola territory in Brazil, with a total of 3,761 residents. However, only 1,054 self-identify as Quilombola, which is the lowest self-attribution rate.

Sample size determination

The sample size calculation was performed using www.openepi.com/SampleSize/SSPropor.htm, applying the formula n = [EDFFNp(1-p)]/[(d²/Z²1-α/2(N-1) + p(1-p)]. Considering a 95% confidence level, the estimated sample size was 106 individuals.

Data collection

Data collection took place between February and September 2023 at a public elementary school. It involved the administration of a questionnaire to guardians for demographic and socioeconomic characterization, as well as an assessment of the cariogenic potential of the diet. All children aged 5 to 8 years who were regularly enrolled in the school and whose guardians signed the informed consent form were included in the study. Children with incomplete questionnaires or who did not assent to participate in the exams were excluded. The questionnaire used for data collection was developed for this study (supplementary file).

Questionnaire survey

For demographic characterization, the following variables were used: sex, age, ethnic-racial declaration made by the child’s guardians, and self-attribution of the family as Quilombola. For socioeconomic characterization, the variables used were: income, number of residents in the household and receipt of welfare benefits.

Cariogenic potencial of the diet

Data on the cariogenic potential of the diet were collected through a dietary recall of the child, using an adaptation of the model established by Giacaman et al. (2014) [17]. Children with scores between 10 and 33 were classified as having low cariogenic potential, while those with scores between 34 and 144 were classified as having high cariogenic potential.

Oral examination

Dental caries experience was assessed through oral exams following the criteria established by the World Health Organization (WHO) [22, 23]. Dental surfaces were used as the unit of measurement. The decayed, missing, and filled primary teeth (dmfs) index and the decayed, missing, and filled permanent teeth (DMFS) index were used to estimate the prevalence of untreated caries.

Additionally, the pufa/PUFA index was used, which quantifies the prevalence and severity of oral conditions resulting from untreated dental caries. The index assesses whether there is pulp involvement, presence of ulcers, fistulas, or abscesses. In this study, the pufa/PUFA index was dichotomized into categories with and without pulp involvement [24].

Training and calibration

Field teams underwent training in workshops lasting 20 h, divided into 4 h of theoretical activities and 16 h of practical activities. The objective was to discuss the operationalization of the work stages and calibration for the codes and criteria for diagnosing oral conditions. The practical stage involved the in vivo method, with the use of images. The consensus technique was used, comparing with a gold standard reference. The exams were conducted by two trained examiners (Kappa dmfs = 723; Kappa DMFS = 0.842; Kappa PUFA = 0.793).

Data analysis

For statistical analysis, the intersectionality theory was considered to assess factors associated with the prevalence of dental caries. Social intersectional categories such as income, self-attribution as Quilombola, sex, and ethnic-racial declaration of the child were considered. To construct the Poisson multilevel regression model, a cluster was developed grouping the child’s age and receipt of welfare benefits, through principal component analysis (KMO = 0.5; Bartlett’s test of sphericity p = 0.295).

The independent variables considered were: income, cariogenic potential of the diet, number of people in the household, self-attribution as Quilombola, child’s sex, child’s ethnic-racial declaration, intersection between self-attribution as Quilombola and income, self-attribution as Quilombola and cariogenic potential of the diet, self-attribution as Quilombola and child’s sex, self-attribution as Quilombola and child’s ethnic-racial declaration.

Three multilevel regression models were obtained, where the dependent variables were: dental caries experience, untreated dental caries, and pufa/PUFA index.

The first analysis considered the experience of caries measured by the dmfs/DMFS indexes as outcomes. Those whose dmfs/DMFS was equal to zero were considered free of caries experience. Those who had dmfs/DMFS different from zero were considered to have experience of dental caries.

In the second analysis, untreated dental caries was considered the outcome. To construct this variable, only the “decayed” component of the dmfs/DMFS index was counted.

In the third and final analysis, the outcome considered was the pufa/PUFA index, which considers pulp involvement, by involvement of the pulp, presence of ulceration, fistula or abscess. The variable was dichotomized between presence and absence of pulp involvement.

Variables with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using Jamovi 2.2.5 software.

Results

The population of this study was predominantly female (59.6%, n = 87), with a mean age of 7 (± 1.53) years. Of the total number of families that participated in the study, only 46.3% (n = 62) self-identified as Quilombola. Regarding the ethnic-racial declaration made by the child’s guardians, 63.2% (79) identified as brown. In terms of socioeconomic status, 69.7% (n = 83) of the families had an income lower than one minimum wage, 67.4% (n = 95) received some form of government welfare, and the families had an average of 4 (± 1.38) people per household (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study showed that the intersectional approach allowed for the identification of significant associations between specific social categories, such as ethnic-racial declaration and self-attribution as quilombola, and the prevalence of dental caries in children residing in the Quilombola Community of Paratibe.

According to the results of this study, children from families with income below one minimum wage have a higher prevalence of dental caries. Income plays a direct role in socioeconomic status, which impacts oral health, as previously observed in the literature [25]. Families with low income face difficulties in regularly acquiring oral hygiene products, have limited access to health services, and encounter challenges in maintaining a healthy diet. These three factors are strongly associated with oral health [14–16, 26].

In 2004, the federal government of Brazil adopted the measure of direct income transfer with the objective of expanding families’ access to their basic social rights, such as health, education, and social assistance, through the Bolsa Família Program (PBF), a variable used to form a cluster in the present study [27]. Over the years, the PBF has contributed to improving the health of the population[28, 29], and although dental consultations are not included in the program’s conditionalities, the Family Health Strategy, which includes the Oral Health Team, plays a crucial role in identifying and registering families eligible for the PBF, as well as in the exercise of basic social rights in the areas of health.

Taking into consideration the latest survey by IBGE, the average number of residents in quilombola households is approximately 3 people.2 In this study, households in quilombola territory had an average of 4 people, which was associated with dental caries. This result may be related to the fact that larger families may face challenges in maintaining proper oral hygiene for all members, especially children. The greater number of people in the household can result in an overload of available resources, such as access to water, health care, and a higher likelihood of neglecting regular care, such as brushing, dental visits, and lower availability of financial resources.

The quilombola territory, like the Brazilian population in general, is predominantly composed of black individuals [2]. Thus, it is important to evaluate ethnic-racial declaration and its association with oral health problems. The literature shows that, overall, black individuals have the worst health conditions [10]. However, consideration should be given to colorism, a term used to explain the more severe impacts of racism on individuals with darker skin tones [29]. The association between ethnic-racial declaration and worse health conditions may be due to institutional racism, which creates significant barriers to access to goods and healthcare, extending beyond the social domain and becoming a public health issue [30].

Self-attribution as quilombola is related to ethnic identity and the historical context of discrimination and exclusion that quilombolas have faced [2]. At the same time, the declaration of non-whiteness is linked to race and experiences of structural racism. These identities can overlap, resulting in a unique experience of marginalization and unequal access to healthcare, including oral health. Thus, intersectionality, used in this analysis, showed that children from families who self-identify as quilombola and declare themselves as non-white may face additional challenges regarding oral health due to the complex interaction between their ethnic and racial identity. Social markers of difference articulate in a way that the social construction of individuals will produce greater or lesser social exclusion, depending on how much this social construction confronts hegemonic social identities [25, 31]. According to the results presented in this study, this intersection made the experience of caries and untreated dental caries greater in this group.

Another factor associated with the prevalence of dental caries experience in children from the quilombola territory was the high cariogenic potential of the diet. A sugar-rich diet, often driven by the consumption of processed foods, when combined with poor hygiene, significantly increases the chances of developing caries [14–16]. Quilombola communities, often due to a lack of resources to maintain a healthier diet, end up resorting to a diet rich in processed foods [32]. In a similar study conducted in China with school-aged children [33], the prevalence of dental caries was approximately 70%, with a mean dmft index higher than that observed in our study. However, as in our findings, diet was identified as an associated factor. Likewise, a study conducted in Rwanda also highlighted the association between dietary habits and the high prevalence of dental caries [34].

Theresults of this study highlight the need for oral health research that focuses on ethnic-racial factors, in addition to socioeconomic factors, applying the concepts of social markers of difference, intersectionality, and SDH. The actions to address oral health problems, such as dental caries, established by the WHO [35], aim at monitoring and researching in the area. However, it is essential that oral health research specifically targets the Black and quilombola populations, as they 36are a powerful tool for transforming the oral health scenario in Brazil [36].

As limitations, this study did not allow for inference of causality, and it is necessary to develop a prospective cohort. The use of the tooth surface as a unit to measure the experience of caries contributed to a more sensitive analysis of the condition, and the use of multilevel regression allowed for a more complex analysis of its associated factors.

Conclusion

According to the results of this study, self-attribution as quilombola, socioeconomic factors, and cariogenic diet are associated with the experience of dental caries and untreated dental caries in the quilombola territory, highlighting the influence of SDH and social markers of difference on the health of vulnerable populations. rs’
