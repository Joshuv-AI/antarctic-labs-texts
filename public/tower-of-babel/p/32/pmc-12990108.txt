A Quadrupolar Fullerene Model System for Benchmarking Enhanced Sampling of Trapped Waters in Free Energy Calculations
Authors: Swapnil Wagle, David L. Mobley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enhanced sampling methods, such as hybrid Monte Carlo/molecular dynamics (MC/MD), grand canonical MC/MD, nonequilibrium candidate MC/MD, etc., are widely used to sample the slow rearrangement of interfacial water molecules in binding free energy calculations. However, direct comparison of the accuracies of these methods is oftentimes challenging due to differences in studied systems, simulation parameters, and system setup across studies. To overcome this challenge, we introduce a novel and well-defined model system for benchmarking water sampling methods: a closed, custom-defined C 90 fullerene. Our C 90 fullerene inherently adopts a distorted oblate-spheroidal geometry rather than being spherical; essentially, C 90 is large enough to be almost a miniature, capped nanotube, unlike its C 60 “buckyball” sibling. Unlike conventional fullerenes with nonpolar, hydrophobic cavities, our custom-defined fullerene incorporates some level of polarity in the form of modest partial charges, creating a quadrupolar cavity that energetically favors water binding. In our quadrupolar fullerene, positive and negative partial charges are distributed around the polar and the equatorial regions, respectively, on its approximate oblate spheroidal geometry. Due to a significant free energy barrier imposed by the fullerene wall, water exchange between the cavity and bulk solvent is essentially impossible on MD time scales. To allow solvent water to equilibrate in the cavity, we introduce a solvent inlet, by performing Hamiltonian replica exchange (i.e., HREX) simulations, which reveal that the quadrupolar fullerene cavity can accommodate a maximum of two waters. From these HREX simulations, we also identify the water binding sites inside the cavity and their occupancies, which show that fullerene conformations with 2 or 1 waters inside the cavity are dominant, with nearly equal free energies. We further validate a nonequilibrium switching (NES) protocol for computing water displacement free energies, by removing a water from the conformations with 2 waters inside the cavity and comparing with the relevant free energies from the HREX simulations. Our NES free energies agree within statistical error with those obtained from HREX. Our findings establish the quadrupolar fullerene as a well-defined, reproducible test system for rigorously benchmarking current as well as future enhanced sampling techniques targeting slow water sampling in confined environments.

Introduction

Accurate sampling of water in free energy calculations is crucial for the accuracy of the predicted thermodynamic properties, particularly in systems where water plays a key role in ligand binding or conformational stability. In the formation of a protein–ligand complex, the binding of the ligand to its target protein entails the displacement of the water molecules that were previously occupying the protein’s binding site. However, in some cases, some water molecules remain localized in the binding site, mediating interactions between the protein and the ligand. − These localized water molecules present a significant sampling challenge in conventional molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which often struggle to efficiently capture their kinetics due to limited simulation time scales.

In binding free energy calculations, where a ligand is alchemically transformed both in the protein’s binding site and in bulk solvent, it is critical to represent the ligand in its correct binding mode. A ligand’s binding mode refers to its conformation and orientation within the binding site, accurately representing its spatial placement and key interactions, including those with protein side chains and water molecules. Accurate treatment of localized water molecules becomes particularly important when their exchange with the bulk solvent occurs on time scales longer than those typically accessible to MD simulations. Slow exchange kinetics may sometimes result from physical constraints, such as confinement by surrounding protein or ligand atoms. However, the sampling challenge lies not in the spatial localization of these ″trapped″ waters per se, but in the time scale of their exchange with bulk solvent. The problem of slow exchange of the trapped waters may also persist in relative binding free energy (RBFE) calculations, where a ligand is alchemically transformed into another ligand in the protein’s binding site. If the ligand transformation involves trapped water rearrangement/displaceament, such an event might not be accurately captured in the RBFE calculation without carefully treating the water.

Enhanced sampling-based methods, such as hybrid Monte Carlo/MD (MC/MD) and grand canonical Monte Carlo/MD (GCMC/MD), − have been popular choices to improve trapped water sampling in free energy calculations. In these methods, the ligand transformation is carried out using several intermediate states (also called λ states). Water molecules are inserted and deleted in the binding site at each λ state, and the occupancies of the binding sites of the trapped waters are estimated at each λ state. While sometimes effective, these methods can be computationally demanding and sensitive to initial placement of the trapped waters in the binding site. , Despite ongoing advances in enhanced sampling techniques, including leveraging graphical processing units (GPUs) to accelerate simulations , and integrating methods like nonequilibrium candidate Monte Carlo (NCMC) with GCMC, converging the occupancies of trapped water sites in free energy calculations still often demands prohibitively long simulation times in many systems.

To benchmark and rigorously evaluate methods for trapped water sampling, the field needs high quality nontrivial test systems. While a variety of methods have been developed to enhance the sampling of water molecules in buried regions, often, it is difficult to validate these methods, and only few studies on their validation have yielded consistent results. Ideally, new methods should first be tested on moderately complex test systems to ensure consistent results, before being applied to challenging test systems. However, many studies bypass this step and instead focus directly on challenging applications in protein–ligand binding without cross-comparison with other methods. − ,, Here, we propose a nontrivial test system for benchmarking methods for trapped water sampling.

Particularly, in our current study, we present an artificial quadrupolar closed C 90 fullerene structure (referred to as fullerene here after) as a model system for validating methods that sample trapped waters in binding free energy calculations (Figure ). Conventionally, fullerenes have a nonpolar cavity that is energetically unfavorable for water molecules to bind. In our model system, we modify our fullerene structure to make it less hydrophobic, using modified partial charges to substantially increase its polarity and make its cavity an energetically favorable binding site for water molecules (Figure ).

Our study on the fullerene system also may hold broader significance for related studies in areas such as energy storage and nanoscience. − The ability to induce modifications to fullerenes (such as inducing quadrupolar cavities, covalent functionalization, etc.) makes them valuable probe molecules to understand water-cavity interactions. − Understanding these interactions may shed light on the critical role of strong water affinity and selective adsorption/desorption dynamics in confined environments like metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs), hydrates and clathrates, which are commonly used for water treatment, purification, shock absorption, and gas separation applications. ,

The sampling of water in the quadrupolar cavity of the proposed custom-defined fullerene is a nontrivial challenge, because the time scale for the water exchange between the fullerene cavity and bulk solvent is too large compared to typical MD time scales, as the fullerene cavity is a thoroughly buried environment with a substantial free energy barrier for water exchange with bulk.

To determine the binding sites and number of waters inside the fullerene cavity, we simulate the removal of the barrier between the cavity and bulk solvent using Hamiltonian replica exchange (HREX) simulations, and let the water equilibrate in the cavity. In our HREX simulations, we introduce an inlet to allow solvent water to enter the fullerene cavity. This inlet is present only in some of the artificial, unphysical intermediate states of the HREX calculations, so one of the physical end states still has the intact fullerene cavity with no inlet (Figure ). This allows us to identify the binding sites and occupancies of water in the cavity of the full, physical fullerene. Using these HREX simulations, we also estimate the free energy to displace a water from a binding site within the cavity by calculating the equilibrium populations of fullerene conformations with and without a water at that binding site. Converged HREX simulations allow for proper equilibration of water inside the fullerene cavity, and provide a gold standard free energy estimate for this custom system. Our choice of HREX for the gold standard free energy estimate is motivated by the broad availability and robust implementation of the method in widely used MD packages, such as GROMACS (the simulation package used in this study), AMBER, − DESMOND, OpenMM, , CHARMM, , NAMD, , etc., meaning that the HREX framework likely can be employed in this system across packages to provide a common benchmark for method validation.

In this study, we also compute the free energy to displace waters from the fullerene cavity using a nonequilibrium switching (NES) simulation protocol (Figure ). Our NES simulation protocol is based on a previously developed method for calculating RBFEs in ligand transformations involving trapped water displacement from binding sites. The method is grounded in a separation of states approach, , which decouples trapped water sampling from ligand alchemical transformation in free energy calculation. This approach contrasts with traditional enhanced sampling methods that simultaneously sample trapped waters in free energy calculation of ligand transformation. ,

In this study, we benchmark the water displacement free energies obtained from our NES protocol against the gold standard estimates we obtain from the HREX simulations. Our NES protocol requires prior knowledge of the locations of the binding sites of the trapped waters that need displacing, and then it uses that information to calculate the free energy of displacing those trapped waters. We determine the locations of the water binding sites inside the cavity using the HREX simulations. As both the HREX simulations and the NES protocol studied here are formally equivalent if converged, they must yield the same free energies. Thus, benchmarking against HREX results will allow us to identify and better understand potential challenges in modeling the trapped waters in our NES protocol. By directly comparing our NES results with those from the HREX simulations, we avoid confounding factors such as differences in force field and methodologies that often make comparisons across different studies difficult.

Methods

Overview of the Hamiltonian Replica Exchange (HREX) Simulations

HREX simulations enhance the sampling of the phase space of the system by enabling it to explore conformations that are otherwise difficult to access due to high energy barriers. − In an HREX simulation, several replicas of the system are run in parallel, each under a different Hamiltonian. At specified time intervals, exchange attempts are made between adjacent replicas. These exchanges are accepted or rejected based on the Metropolis criterion, with exchange probabilities P ij between replicas i and j being Pij=min(1,exp(−1kBT×((Ui(rj)−Ui(ri))+(Uj(ri)−Uj(rj))))) where k B is the Boltzmann constant, T is simulation temperature, and U i and r i are the potential and coordinates of atoms in replica i. If an exchange is accepted, the coordinates and velocities of all atoms from replica i are subsequently simulated using the Hamiltonian of replica j, and vise versa.

In this study, we have used HREX simulations to enhance the sampling of solvent water within the fullerene cavity. Under standard MD conditions, water exchange between the cavity and the bulk solvent is nearly impossible due to the impermeable nature of the fullerene surface, which acts as a significant physical barrier. To overcome the barrier, we use HREX simulations to make part of the fullerene permeable in some replicas, allowing solvent water to enter and equilibrate inside the cavity in those replicas. Specifically, we define atoms within a specific region of the fullerene surface (i.e., a solvent inlet, as shown in Figure ) as alchemical atoms. We then progressively turn off their nonbonded interactions across intermediate replicas (see Section for simulation details), with the end states being replicas with fully interacting and fully noninteracting inlet atoms. After water enters/exits the cavity in these intermediate replicas (and in the end state with the noninteracting inlet atoms), the resulting conformations can spread to other replicas via HREX.

Using these HREX simulations, we aim to determine the equilibrium population densities of conformations containing different numbers of water molecules inside the fullerene cavity, and by aligning the fullerene configurations in those conformations, we will identify the preferred water binding sites inside the cavity (though these locations are not critical to the present work). In the HREX simulations, the inlet is present only in the intermediate and unphysical states, however, by the virtue of the HREX simulation, the configuration in the intermediate replicas can spread across other replicas, including the physical end state with fully interacting inlet atoms (i.e., in which the fullerene is intact and impermeable). Consequently, in a sufficiently long and equilibrated simulation, the populations of conformations containing different numbers of confined waters will equilibrate. By enumerating different conformations, we can obtain their equilibrium population densities, from which we can estimate their free energies.

Overview of Our Nonequilibrium Switching (NES) Simulation Protocol

Nonequilibrium free energy calculations estimate the free energy difference between two end states. − In an NES simulation, equilibrium simulations of both the end states (referred to as end state simulations) are performed, and representative structures are extracted along the end state simulation trajectories. Starting from these structures, rapid simulations (or switches) are run, which drive the system from one end state to the other via nonequilibrium pathways. To perform the rapid transitions between the end states, the Hamiltonians of the end states are coupled to an external parameter λ that transitions between two discrete values (representing the two end states) during the switches, in both forward and reverse directions. Because of the nonequilibrium nature of the switches, work is dissipated along the simulation trajectories, which needs to be accounted for. By calculating the work in the forward and reverse switches, we can calculate the free energy difference between the two end states by solving the Crooks fluctuation theorem , with the Bennett’s Acceptance Ratio (BAR) estimator, by numerically solving 1 ∑i=1nf11+nfnreβ(Wf−ΔG)=∑i=1nr11+nrnfe−β(Wr−ΔG) where n f and n r are the number of switches between the end states in the forward and reverse directions, respectively, β equals 1kBT , where k B is Boltzmann constant and T is the simulation temperature, and ΔG is the free energy difference between the end states. The work along the forward and reverse directions, W f and W r , respectively, can be calculated by accumulating the changes in the Hamiltonian during the switches.

In this study, we have used our NES simulation protocol (Figure ) to calculate the free energy of displacing a water from within the fullerene cavity. Because our NES protocol requires a priori knowledge of the putative binding site locations, we first identify the water binding sites within cavity using our HREX simulations. To precisely locate each binding site, we align the HREX-based conformations to a reference structure and identify distinct regions within the cavity where water molecules consistently appeared across conformations. For each region, we average the coordinates of the oxygen atom of all the water molecules assigned to that region, to obtain the average binding site position.

In our NES protocol, we calculate the free energy difference between our two end states: Stage 1 and Stage 4 (Figure ). In Stage 1, an alchemical water (i.e., the water that will undergo transformation) is in an interacting state and occupies its binding site inside the fullerene cavity, and in the other end state (Stage 4), the alchemical water is restrained at its binding site in the fullerene cavity in a decoupled (i.e., noninteracting) state. We divide our nonequilibrium free energy calculation using two intermediate stages. In the first intermediate stage (Stage 2), the alchemical water inside the fullerene cavity is restrained at its binding site and a solvent repulsion potential is applied at that site. In the second intermediate stage (Stage 3), a decoupled alchemical water is restrained at its binding site while a solvent repulsion potential at that site is in place. To restrain the alchemical water and to apply the solvent repulsion potential, we need the precise binding site coordinates within the cavity. To obtain these, we first identify candidate pairs of fullerene atoms such that the binding site (determined via averaging the oxygen coordinates of waters, as described earlier in this section) lies approximately on the line connecting the two atoms. From these candidates, we select a pair whose atoms are almost equidistant from the average binding site position. We then define the exact coordinates of the binding site as a linear combination of the coordinates of the selected atom pair. In the simulation software used here (i.e., GROMACS), this construction is implemented using a type 2 virtual site (see Section for details). The virtual sites and their corresponding atom pairs for the binding sites studied here (see Section for details) are shown in the Figure S1 of the Supplementary Document.

Our forward NES transformation begins from the stage where an unrestrained alchemical water is fully interacting in the fullerene cavity (Stage 1). In the first NES switch (i.e., NES f1), we restrain the alchemical water to its binding site (i.e., to the type 2 virtual site at the average binding site position) by a harmonic restraint and simultaneously apply a repulsive potential on solvent waters (including all the waters inside the cavity, except the alchemical water) at the binding site of the alchemical water. In the second NES switch (i.e., NES f2), we turn off the nonbonded interactions of the restrained alchemical water while the harmonic restraint on it and the solvent repulsion potential are still in place. Finally, in the third NES switch (i.e., NES f3), we remove the repulsive potential on the solvent.

Our NES transformation in the reverse direction begins from the stage where a decoupled alchemical water is restrained to its binding site (Stage 4). In the first NES switch (i.e., NES r1), we apply a repulsive potential on the solvent waters (including all the waters inside the cavity, except the alchemical water) at the binding site of the decoupled alchemical water. In the second NES switch (i.e., NES r2), we couple the alchemical water to the system while the harmonic restraint and the solvent repulsion are still in place. Finally, in the third NES switch (i.e., NES r3), we turn off the harmonic restraint on the alchemical water and the repulsive potential on the solvent.

In our NES protocol, we apply a harmonic restraint on the alchemical water to ensure it stays at its binding site while we turn off its interactions in NES f2. Without the restraint, the alchemical water can wander out of the cavity once its interactions are sufficiently weak, which would make the convergence of the decoupling simulation very challenging. Furthermore, a harmonic restraint on a decoupled water in NES r2 is necessary to ensure that the alchemical water is present at its binding site while its interactions are turned on. Without the harmonic restraint, the decoupled water would have to find its way to its binding site inside the fullerene cavity during the short time scale of the switch, which could be quite unlikely. Because all waters in the simulation box are indistinguishable (including the alchemical water), we apply the harmonic restraint to the water molecule that is closest to the binding site of the alchemical water and define this new water as the alchemical water, in case the original alchemical water exchanges with another water molecule inside the cavity in the end state simulation of Stage 1.

In our NES protocol, we apply a solvent repulsion potential at the binding site of the alchemical water to prevent other waters inside the cavity from occupying the binding site once the alchemical water’s interactions are sufficiently weak (in NES f2). In such an event, without the repulsion potential, the end state of our protocol with a decoupled alchemical water (Stage 4, Figure ) would in fact have another water at the binding site of the alchemical water, essentially rendering the simulation protocol ill-defined.

In the simulation software used here (i.e., GROMACS, see Section for details), the external parameter in an NES simulation (λ) can be a vector. Various interactions, such as Coulomb, vdW, bonded interactions and restraint, etc. can be individually coupled to the components of this vector. Here, we couple each of the harmonic restraint on the alchemical water, the solvent repulsion potential, and the nonbonded interactions of the alchemical water to a component of a three-component λ vector (with components λ1, λ2, and λ3). Each of the vector components can transform 0 → 1 or 1 → 0 independent of the other vector components during an NES switch. Switches NES f1, NES f2 and NES f3 constitute the NES transformation in the forward direction, i.e., NES decouple_water (Figure ). Similarly, switches NES r1, NES r2 and NES r3 constitute the NES transformation in the reverse direction, i.e., NES couple_water . In the forward direction, we continue the trajectory of NES f1 into NES f2, and the trajectory of NES f2 into NES f3, essentially making the combined NES f1, NES f2 and NES f3 switches a single NES decouple_water switch between Stage 1 and Stage 4. Similarly, in the reverse direction, we continue the trajectory of NES r1 into NES r2, and the trajectory of NES r2 into NES r3, essentially making the NES r1, NES r2 and NES r3 switches a single NES couple_water switch between Stage 4 and Stage 1. This way, we bypass the need to run end state simulations of Stage 2 and Stage 3, essentially requiring the end state simulations of only Stage 1 and Stage 4 to run our protocol. To ensure continuity between successive NES switches, we take the coordinates and velocities of all atoms in the system from the final state of an NES switch and use those as the starting coordinates and velocities for the simulation of the subsequent NES switch.

To calculate the work in an NES decouple_water switch (W f in eq ), we add the work values from the constituent NES f1, NES f2 and NES f3 switches, and similarly, to calculate the work in an NES couple_water switch (W r in eq ), we add the work values for the constituent NES r1, NES r2 and NES r3 switches, as we discuss further below in Section .

Simulation Details

Structure and Topology File Preparation

We obtained the distorted oblate spheroidal structure of the C 90 fullerene, with no water in the fullerene cavity, from the GitHub repository of timemachine. The fullerene cannot form a perfect oblate spheroid because the geometry of the fullerene is constrained by the arrangement of pentagons and hexagons in the carbon network, which precludes the continuous axial symmetry of a perfect oblate spheroid in a C 90 fullerene. As a result, the cage adopts a slightly distorted oblate-spheroidal form, as can be seen in the equatorial view in Figure . Due to the slight asymmetry of the cage geometry, its dimensions are best described using effective radii: approximately 4.5 nm along the equatorial direction and approximately 3.25 nm along the polar direction. Although the distortion breaks the continuous rotational and mirror symmetries characteristic of a perfectly oblate spheroid, the fullerene nonetheless retains three discrete symmetry elements: a 2-fold equatorial rotational axis (C 2), an equatorial (σ equatirial ) mirror plane, and a polar (σ polar ) mirror plane, as illustrated in Figure . The distortion in fullerene can be measured in the local dimensions: along the 2-fold equatorial axis of rotation, the elliptical major and minor axes measure approximately 4.0 and 3.4 Å at a meridian halfway up the cage from the polar plane perpendicular to the axis of rotational symmetry, and approximately 4.4 and 3.1 Å at a corresponding meridian on the lower half.

The solvent inlet is shown in lines at the bottom. Fullerene atoms with positive and negative partial charges are shown in blue and red, respectively. Given the distorted oblate spheroidal geometry, note that the top and bottom halves of the fullerene in this view are not symmetric (i.e., a mirror plane perpendicular to the equatorial rotational axis of symmetry is absent).

The GitHub repository also provided us with the partial charges for the fullerene atoms, which were assigned according to the Espaloma charge (v0.0.8) model. Espaloma charge employs a graph neural network (GNN) model to predict the hardness and electronegativity of the atoms, before using charge equilibration to preserve the total charge and assign partial charges. , The overall structure of the fullerene had net zero charge. Among the 90 atoms constituting the fullerene, 40 were assigned positive partial charges, primarily distributed around the equatorial region, while the remaining 50 atoms were assigned negative partial charges, predominantly localized near the poles. This particular partial charge distribution resulted in a significantly quadrupolar fullerene structure. This is a result of (Espaloma-predicted) significant variations in electronegativity and hardness of the carbon atoms in the fullerene, with some electronegativity values actually varying in sign depending on connectivity. The physicality of the resulting charges is outside the scope of this study; for the present study it is sufficient that the result makes the cavity at least somewhat polar and favorable for water occupancy.

We used the OpenMM software package and Open Force Field Toolkit to parametrize the fullerene. The bonded parameters and the van der Waals (vdW) parameters for the fullerene were generated using the OpenFF-2.0 (Sage) force field. The fullerene structure was solvated in a box of TIP3P waters.

To create a solvent inlet for the HREX simulations, we searched for a set of connected atoms of the fullerene that satisfy our two requirements: 1) the set of atoms should form an inlet that is large enough to allow solvent waters to go in and out of the fullerene, and 2) the summation of the partial charges of these atoms should be approximately zero. We identified a set of the 14 atoms, as shown in Figure , that satisfied our requirements. The inlet is located on the equatorial region of the fullerene and is symmetric with respect to all three symmetry elements of the overall cage (Figure ).

For the HREX simulations, we set up the GROMACS topology file for the free energy functionality of GROMACS, which requires defining A and B states of the system. We defined the A state as a fully interacting fullerene. In the B state, we decoupled the 14 atoms of the fullerene forming the inlet by setting their vdW parameters and partial charges to zero. In the decoupled state, however, their bonded interactions were kept the same as in the A state. The summation of the partial charges of the 14 atoms forming the inlet was −0.05, and so, to keep the B state of the system uncharged, a −0.00066 (i.e., −0.05/76) charge was added to the B state partial charge of each of the 76 atoms that were not part of the inlet. For reproducibility, we have provided the list of atoms forming the inlet, as well as the partial charges of all of the atoms of the fullerene in both A and B states in the topology file in GitHub repository waterNES.

To set up the GROMACS topology file for the NES simulations, we defined a separate moleculetype for the alchemical water. In this moleculetype, we defined constraints for the hydrogen atoms of the alchemical water according to the SETTLE implementation in GROMACS, using the constraints directive. In GROMACS, the settles directive can not be defined for two moleculetype (in this case, the normal water and the alchemical water). In the topology file for the NES simulations, we defined the A and B states for the GROMACS free energy implementation for the alchemical water, the harmonic restraint on the alchemical water, and the solvent repulsion potential, following the procedure described in the Supporting Information of our prior study. To define the restraining site for the alchemical water in the fullerene cavity, we defined a virtual site of type 2 at the putative location of the binding site of the alchemical water, as a linear combination of positions of two of the fullerene atoms that were selected using a Python script. We have provided the structure of the solvated fullerene, the GROMACS topology files we used in our HREX and NES simulations, and the Python script to select atoms to define the virtual site in our GitHub repository waterNES.

Energy Minimization and Equilibration

All MD simulations were run using the GROMACS 2022.1 simulation package. A time step of 2 fs was used in all MD simulations. Coulomb and vdW interactions were calculated for a 10 Å cutoff radius and long-range Coulomb interactions were calculated using Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) with a Fourier grid spacing of 1 Å.

Starting from the solvated structure of the fullerene we obtained using the procedure described in Section , we performed an energy minimization of the system for 5000 steps using the steepest descent algorithm. Then, we equilibrated the system in an NVT ensemble for 10 ps, stabilizing the temperature of the system at 298.15 K using the stochastic dynamics integrator and an inverse friction constant of 2 ps. During the NVT equilibration, we restrained each atom of the fullerene using a harmonic position restraint with a force constant of 1000 kJ mol–1 nm–2. Finally, we performed a 100 ps equilibration of the system in an NPT ensemble, by coupling the system to the Parrinello–Rahman barostat, in an isotropic pressure coupling of 1 bar using a time constant of 1 ps. During the NPT equilibration, all the atoms of the fullerene were restrained using a harmonic position restraint with a force constant of 1000 kJ mol–1 nm–2.

Full details of the input parameters of the equilibration procedure (GROMACS mdp files) can be found in the GitHub repository waterNES.

Hamiltonian Replica Exchange (HREX) Simulations

We ran Hamiltonian replica exchange simulations between two end states of our system, where in the end state A, the fullerene was fully interacting, and in the end state B, 14 atoms forming a solvent inlet were noninteracting (Figure ). The transformation between the end states was divided using 20 λ states, as shown in Table , where λ state 0 represents state A and λ state 19 represents state B of the system. Across all the λ states, the partial charges of the inlet atoms were coupled to the coul-lambdas input parameter, and the vdW parameters of the inlet atoms were coupled to the vdw-lambdas input parameter. We equilibrated all replicas independently using the procedure described in Section . After equilibration, we ran each replica for 100 ns, where exchange attempts between adjacent replicas were made every 200 time steps. For analysis, the initial 10 ns of each replica was treated as a burn-in period and was discarded to ensure proper equilibration.

Five independent HREX simulations were run, starting from 0, 1, 2, 3, and 6 water molecules randomly placed in the fullerene cavity, in order to ensure that we obtain equivalent results regardless of starting occupancy (i.e., to test convergence).

To determine the population densities of different conformations, we counted the number of snapshots corresponding to each occupancy state. Using the trjconv tool of GROMACS, we aligned all the frames from the trajectory of the fully interacting replica and identified distinct regions within the cavity where water molecules consistently appeared across the trajectory. For each region, we then averaged the oxygen coordinates of all water molecules assigned to that region to obtain the precise position of the corresponding binding site.

Nonequilibrium Switching (NES) Simulations

We determined the binding site locations and their occupancies using the HREX simulations (see Sections for details). To displace a water from its binding site within the fullerene cavity, we used the NES protocol shown in Figure . A displaceable water in the cavity was defined as an alchemical water in the GROMACS topology file for the NES simulations and its binding site was defined as a linear combination of the positions of two fullerene atoms, according to the procedure described in Section . In the end state simulations, we defined the alchemical water as interacting in one end state (Stage 1), and noninteracting and restrained to its binding site in the other end state (Stage 4). We simulated each end state for 20 ns in the NPT ensemble using the parameters described in Section . From the last 10 ns of each of the two end state simulation trajectories, we extracted 200 equally spaced structures. In the structures extracted from the end state simulation of Stage 1, we assigned the water closest to the binding site of the alchemical water as the new alchemical water, in case an exchange between the alchemical water and another water in fullerene cavity took place.

Beginning from each of the 200 structures extracted from the trajectory of Stage 1, we simulated the NES f1 switches for 10 ps, in which we turned on the harmonic restraint on the alchemical water and the solvent repulsion potential by updating the λ value at every time step (Δλ = 0.0002). Similarly, starting from each of the 200 structures extracted from the trajectory of Stage 4, we simulated the NES r1 switches, in which we only turned on the solvent repulsion potential, keeping the harmonic restraint on the alchemical water on throughout the simulation.

From the final structures of the NES f1 and NES r1 switches, we started NES f2 and NES r2 switches, respectively, Each switch was simulated for 50 ps. In the NES f2 and NES r2 switches, we turned the nonbonded (vdW and Coulomb) parameters of the alchemical water off and on, respectively, with the coupling parameter λ updated at every time step (Δλ = 0.00004). The soft-core potential from Beutler et al. was applied to the vdW interactions to avoid singularities in the alchemical transformation. In NES f2 and NES r2 switches, the harmonic restraint and the solvent repulsion potential were both kept on.

From the final structures of the NES f2 and NES r2 switches, we started NES f3 and NES r3 switches. Each switch was simulated for 10 ps with the λ updating at every time step (Δλ = 0.0002). In the NES f3 switches, we turned off the solvent repulsion potential, while in the NES r3 switches, we turned off the harmonic restraint on the alchemical water as well as the solvent repulsion potential.

For each water displacement from the fullerene cavity, we ran three independent repeats of our NES protocol. The input parameter files (GROMACS mdp files) for the two end state simulations and all the NES switches are provided in the GitHub repository waterNES.

Free Energy Calculation and Error Estimation

To calculate the free energy difference between two conformations containing N and M waters inside the fullerene cavity, we counted the number of frames in the trajectory of the HREX simulation with the fully interacting fullerene (λ state 0 in Table , see Section for details) with N and M waters inside the fullerene cavity, and calculated the free energy difference using Equation 5 ΔGN−>M=kBT×ln(nNnM) where k B is the Boltzmann constant, T is the simulation temperature, and n N and n M are the number of frames with N and M waters in the fullerene cavity, respectively. We determined the error in our free energy estimate by performing bootstrap analysis using Python package tbootstrap.

For calculating the free energy to displace an alchemical water from the fullerene cavity using our NES simulation protocol (Figure ), we used the Equation 6 ΔG=ΔGNESdecouple_water/couple_water+ΔGHR−Remove+ΔGW−Couple For the free energy calculation of the NES simulation (ΔG decouple_water/couple_water ), we used the BAR implementation in the PMX software package. BAR requires the calculation of the nonequilibrium work in the forward and reverse switches (eq ). We calculated the nonequilibrium work for NES decouple_water by adding the work values of NES f1, NES f2 and NES f3, i.e., we added the work values of ith NES f1, ith NES f2 and ith NES f3 switches for calculating the work of ith NES decouple_water switch. Similarly, we calculated the work for NES couple_water by adding the work values of the NES r1, NES r2 and NES r3 switches.

The free energy of HR-Remove is equal to the free energy to remove the harmonic restraint on a decoupled water and transfer it to bulk solvent (i.e., pure water at 55.5 molar concentration). Because the water is decoupled from the environment, shifting it from the fullerene cavity to bulk solvent incurs no change in free energy. The free energy to remove the harmonic restraint on the decoupled water and transfer it to 55.5 molar concentration can be calculated analytically − using equation 7 ΔGHR−Remove=RTlog⁡⁡(no(2πRTk)3/2) where R is the molar gas constant, T is the simulation temperature, n o is the water number density at standard concentration (55.5 molar × N A , where N A is the Avogadro’s number), and k is the force constant of the harmonic restraint. A detailed derivation of eq is given in the Supporting Information of our previous study. In the present study, we used a harmonic force constant (k) of 5 kcal mol–1 Å–2 to restrain the alchemical water, for which ΔG HR‑Remove is −2.28 kcal mol–1.

The free energy of W-Couple (ΔG W‑Couple), i.e., to couple the decoupled water molecule to bulk solvent, depends only on the water model used. This free energy is independent of simulation box size for sufficiently large simulation boxes. In the NES protocol employed here (Figure ), the decoupled water is transferred to the bulk solvent (i.e., pure water at 55.5 molar concentration) in HR-Remove. Thus, in principle, the free energy of W-Couple should be computed at a simulation box volume corresponding to the molar volume of pure water to complete the thermodynamic cycle. However, given that this free energy is independent of simulation box volume, we simply used our free energy value precomputed for a box size of approximately 125 nm3. For the water model used in this study (TIP3P), we calculated this free energy value to be −6.12 ± 0.03 kcal mol–1, which agrees well with previous computational as well as experimental studies.

We calculated the final value and statistical error of the free energy estimate to displace a water from the fullerene cavity by taking the mean and the standard deviation, respectively, of the free energies calculated from three independent repeats of the NES protocol for that water.

Widom Insertion Attempts (Not Converged)

In response to a reviewer feedback, we also attempted Widom insertion simulations for computing of the relevant free energies of inserting waters in the fullerene cavity. We have provided the details of the simulations in Sections S1.1 and S1.2 of the Supporting Document.

In this study, we used Widom insertion to estimate the excess chemical potential associated with placing a water molecule inside the fullerene cavity. In Widom insertion, a test particle is inserted into fixed snapshots of the system, and the corresponding change in potential energy is computed. For a test particle inserted at position r, we can define the insertion energy as 8 ΔU(r)=Unew(r)−U where U is the potential energy of the original configuration and U new (r) is the potential energy after adding the test particle. The excess chemical potential can be calculated using Widom’s relation 9 μex(r)=−kBTln⟨e−ΔU(r)/kBT⟩ where ⟨···⟩ is an ensemble average taken over multiple system configurations and insertion trials.

In this study, we computed excess chemical potential of bulk solvent and the free energy to insert water molecules in fullerene cavity, by attempting water insertions at random positions within bulk solvent and the fullerene cavity. Additional details are given in the Supporting Document, but we found it extremely challenging to converge these simulations (as expected for dense condensed-phase environments − ) relative to the other methods employed here.

Results

In this study, we have performed HREX simulations to determine the population densities of conformations with different numbers of waters within cavity, the locations of the water binding sites and their occupancies inside the custom-defined quadrupolar fullerene. In the HREX simulations, we introduced an inlet for the solvent water to populate the cavity of this relatively polar fullerene. We calculated the free energy difference between system conformations with N and M waters inside the fullerene cavity using eq , by counting the number of frames with N and M waters inside the cavity in the trajectory of a fully interacting fullerene in the HREX simulations (see Sections , and for details). We determined the locations of the water binding sites inside the cavity by aligning these frames to a reference structure and identifying distinct regions of the cavity in which water consistently appeared across frames. To precisely locate a binding site in a distinct region, we averaged the oxygen coordinates of all the water molecules that appeared in that region across frames.

We also calculated the free energy of displacing a water from the fullerene cavity using our NES simulation protocol (Figure ), according to the procedure described in Sections and .

In the next sections, we discuss our findings on the free energies of different conformations and the binding site locations of waters inside the cavity that we determined using our HREX simulations, and compare the relevant free energies from the HREX and NES calculations.

The Fullerene Cavity Can Accommodate a Maximum of Two Waters

To allow for proper equilibration of water inside the fullerene cavity, we ran HREX simulations between two end states: a fully interacting fullerene, and a fullerene with an open inlet for water exchange with solvent (Figure ). We ran five independent HREX simulations, starting with 0, 1, 2, 3, and 6 waters inside the fullerene cavity, to check for convergence and to ensure that we obtained equivalent results regardless of the starting number of waters inside.

Across all HREX simulations, we observed that the most populated conformations corresponded to those with 2 or 1 waters inside the cavity (Figure ). In the simulations started with 3 and 6 waters inside, the number of waters in the cavity quickly decreased to 1 or 2 within the first 4 ns of the burn-in period (see Section for simulation details). Similarly, in the simulation started with 0 waters inside, the cavity became populated with 1 or 2 waters within the first 1 ns of the burn-in period. After reaching these dominant conformations with 2 or 1 waters inside, no transitions to conformations with 0 or more than 2 waters inside were observed in any of the HREX simulations, suggesting conformations other than the 1 or 2 waters inside are quite unfavorable for the fullerene studied here. In the HREX simulations started with 1 or 2 waters inside the cavity, no conformations with more than 2 or less than 1 waters inside the cavity were observed. In all HREX simulations, the population fractions of the conformations with 2 waters and 1 water inside the cavity both reached approximately 0.5 after sufficiently long simulations (Figure ), indicating that the equilibrium population densities, and hence the free energies, of both conformations are equal (Table ). Conformations with 2 and 1 water inside the cavity are referred to as the 2-water and 1-water conformations, respectively, here after.

The starting geometry of the quadrupolar fullerene was that of an approximate oblate spheroid (see Section for details), which was maintained over the course of the 100 ns simulations across all replicas. This structural stability of the fullerene was likely influenced by the charge distribution on it (Figure ), characterized by positive charge distributed along the equator and negative charges localized near the poles, giving this custom fullerene its polarity. As discussed before, it is unclearand outside the scope of this studywhether the employed charge distribution is actually physically reasonable for this fullerene.

We Identified the Locations of the Water Binding Sites and Their Occupancies Inside the Fullerene Cavity

By aligning the dominant conformations, i.e., 2-water and 1-water conformations, we identified three distinct water binding sites, labeled 1, 2a and 2b in Figure . To get the precise location of a binding site, we averaged the oxygen coordinates of all waters that appeared within a distinct region of the fullerene cavity. To get the occupancy of a binding site, we counted the number of frames in which a water appeared in the distinct region within cavity corresponding to that binding site. Binding site 1 lies directly opposite to the solvent inlet, whereas binding sites 2a and 2b are located between binding site 1 and the solvent inlet. Sites 2a and 2b are energetically equivalent, hence named as 2a and 2b rather than 2 and 3. The spatial arrangement of the three binding sites reflects the overall symmetry of the distorted oblate spheroidal fullerene (Figure ), exhibiting reflection/mirror symmetry along the equatorial plane and along the polar plane passing through site 1, as well as a 2-fold rotational symmetry about the equatorial axis passing through site 1.

We calculated the occupancies of each of the three binding sites in a conformation by counting the number of frames (in that conformation) in which a water appeared in the spatial region corresponding to that binding site. In the frames corresponding to the 2-water conformation, one water consistently occupied site 1, while the second water either occupied site 2a or site 2b. We also observed frequent translations of the second water between sites 2a and 2b throughout the simulation trajectory, indicating that the sites are energetically degenerate (as expected, given the reflection symmetry of the fullerene along the polar plane passing through site 1, Figure ) and with only a small energy barrier between them for water translation.

In frames within the 1-water conformation, only binding site 1 was consistently occupied. We did not observe any frame in which only 1 water was inside the cavity and it was occupying site 2a or 2b.

In the 1-water conformation, the water molecule at binding site 1 was oriented with its oxygen atom pointed toward the equatorial positive charge on fullerene surface, opposite the solvent inlet (Figure ). In the 2-water conformation, the water at site 1 retained a similar orientation, while the water at binding site 2a (or 2b) aligned its oxygen toward the equatorial positive charge on the fullerene and simultaneously formed a hydrogen bond with the hydrogen of the water at binding site 1.

We computed the free energy difference between the 2-water and 1-water conformations (Table ), using eq , by calculating the cumulative fractional populations of these conformations in the HREX replica with a fully interacting fullerene (Figure ). The 2-water conformation was 0.03 ± 0.08 kcal mol–1 more favorable than the 1-water conformation.

We assessed the convergence of our HREX simulations using various parameters, such as replica transitions across λ states (see Table for details), acceptance probabilities of exchanges between adjacent replicas, and the cumulative population fractions of conformations. In all our HREX simulations, we observed several transitions of the replicas across the full range of λ states, with replica exchange acceptance probabilities between 50% and 90% across all adjacent λ states. We also observed frequent exchanges between the water molecules occupying the binding sites and the bulk solvent, facilitated by HREX, as expected. The cumulative population fractions of the 2-water and 1-water conformations stabilized after 50 ns (Figure ), indicating equilibrium was reached. Together, these indicators confirm that the HREX simulations were well converged within the 100 ns time scale.

Our NES Simulations Correctly Estimate the Free Energy to Displace a Water from the Fullerene Cavity

Since we established the locations of the binding sites and their likely occupancies in the fullerene cavity using the HREX simulations, we computed the free energy difference between the 2-water and 1-water conformations using our NES simulation protocol (Figure ). Specifically, we calculated the free energy to displace a water from binding site 2a/2b while binding site 1 is occupied by another water. The free energy difference between 2- and 1- water conformations, i.e., ΔG 2→1 obtained from the HREX simulations, represents the free energy difference between any fullerene configuration with 2 waters inside the cavity and any configuration with only 1 water inside the cavity. Therefore, this estimate, in principle, includes any 1 water conformation in which the water occupies site 1 and sites 2a/2b. However, since the frames in the 1-water conformation from HREX simulations consistently showed occupancy only at site 1 (see Section for details), the HREX free energy difference effectively corresponds to the free energy of displacement of a water molecule from site 2a/2b. Therefore, we can directly compare our NES-derived free energy estimate with the free energy difference between the 2-water and 1-water conformations calculated from the HREX simulations (Table ).

We started our NES end state simulations from a structure we took from our HREX simulation, where binding sites 1 and 2a/2b were occupied by one water each (see Section and Figure for details). The water at site 2a/2b was designated as alchemical water, i.e., the water that will undergo transformation. To restrain the alchemical water to its binding site and to apply the solvent repulsion potential (see Section for details), we determined the exact coordinates of the water’s binding site by placing a GROMACS virtual site of type-2 at its putative location (Figure S1). The virtual site coordinates were defined as a linear combination of coordinates of two fullerene atoms (see Section for details). In our end state simulation with an interacting alchemical water (Stage 1, Figure ), we frequently saw the two waters at the two binding sites swap their positions. In the end state simulation with a noninteracting alchemical water at binding site 2a/2b (Stage 4, Figure ), the water at binding site 1 remained at its binding site throughout the end state simulation.

We ran three independent repeats of our NES protocol (Figure ) to displace a water from binding site 2a/2b while binding site 1 is occupied by another water. For each repeat, we calculated the free energy estimate using eq . We finally calculated the free energy estimate and statistical error by taking the mean and standard deviation, respectively, of the estimates from the three repeats. The calculated free energy value (and its statistical error) to displace a water from binding site 2a/2b was 0.10 ± 0.07 kcal mol–1, (mean and standard deviation of free energies of 0.12 ± 0.07, 0.17 ± 0.07 and 0.00 ± 0.07 kcal mol–1 calculated from the three repeats). This free energy value is within statistical error of the free energy difference between 2- and 1-water conformations, as calculated from the HREX simulations (Table ). All three NES simulation repeats showed sufficient overlap between the forward and reverse work distributions (Figure ), indicating convergence of the simulations.

The free energy to displace a water from binding site 1, while another water is bound at binding site 2a/2b, could not be calculated. This is so, because the binding site 1 is energetically more favorable than binding site 2a/2b and water exchange between the two binding sites is rapid. So, a water present at binding site 2a/2b will rapidly move to the binding site 1 in case site 1 is unoccupied. Indeed, in our end state simulation of Stage 4 (Figure ), we observed that an interacting water at binding site 2a/2b could not be simulated while a decoupled water occupied binding site 1, as the interacting water at binding site 2a/2b immediately moved to binding site 1.

Discussion

In this study, we have established that our custom-defined fullerene is a simple yet nontrivial system for validating water sampling methods in confined environments. Furthermore, we validated our nonequilibrium switching (NES)-based approach for displacing slow or tightly bound water molecules from confined binding sites. To establish a reference (″ground truth″) for comparison, we first performed HREX simulations to obtain the locations and occupancies of the binding sites inside the fullerene cavity. We also determined that fullerene conformations with 2 and 1 water inside cavity are the dominant conformations, with almost equal free energies. Our HREX analysis revealed three distinct water binding sites within the fullerene cavity, consistent with the underlying symmetry of the fullerene geometry. Using our NES protocol, we computed the free energy to displace a water molecule from the relevant HREX-identified binding sites. The NES-derived free energies agreed well with those inferred from the HREX simulations, differing by less than 1 kcal mol–1. This level of agreement demonstrates that our NES-based protocol can accurately quantify the energetics of displacing waters from spatially confined environments.

In this study, as seen from Figure , we successfully achieved convergence in determining the equilibrium population densities of dominant fullerene conformations, despite extensive sampling required due to the slow exchange of water between the cavity and bulk solvent. Achieving convergence demanded long simulation timeseach of our HREX simulations involved a total of 2 μs of simulation time along with 2.5 million exchange attempts between replicas. In contrast, each of our NES simulations required only 48 ns simulation time (20 ns for equilibrium end state samplings and 28 ns for NES switches). Given the computational effort required for convergence, it would be valuable to compare the performance of other enhanced sampling techniques, such as hybrid MC/MD, NCMC, GCMC, and GCMC/NCMC, etc., for their ability to estimate the equilibrium distribution of water occupancies and their relative free energies for this model system. We propose that the quadrupolar fullerene cavity considered here will be an ideal benchmark system for evaluating both the convergence and computational efficiency of methods designed to sample trapped water in confined environments.

An important and open challenge in occluded water sampling pertains to the coupling between changes in water occupancies and conformational rearrangements of the host. The quadrupolar fullerene system studied here does not capture such coupled behavior, which is part of why we selected it as a relatively simple test system. Its rigid structure eliminates host conformational flexibility, allowing for water exchange in a confined cavity to be studied in isolation, rather than reproducing the full complexity of protein binding sites with coupled water dynamics and side-chain rearrangements. In a similar spirit, this system also does not involve the complexity of ligand binding. In realistic protein–ligand binding sites, water occupancies can strongly couple to ligand poses and conformational heterogeneity, complicating the definition of a unique reference state. By omitting a ligand, the present model isolates the problem of sampling occluded water molecules in a confined cavity with well-defined geometry and energetics, enabling unambiguous determination of equilibrium water occupancies and displacement free energies.

For our custom-defined fullerene system, we combined multiple force fields: the partial charges were assigned using Espaloma, while vdW and bonded parameters were taken from OpenFF-2.0 (Sage). Such a hybrid parametrization is rather uncommon in molecular simulations. Moreover, the assignment of the partial charges to the fullerene produced a quadrupolar cavity, which may be an artifact. Regardless, our work demonstrates that this custom fullerene model provides a simple yet nontrivial benchmark for evaluating methods aimed at sampling slow water exchange in confined environments. It is important to recognize that different methodological frameworks may require distinct system setups or parametrization choices, which may in turn necessitate recomputing the corresponding ground truth free energies. However, if this system is used for validating a new method, the objective would not be to reproduce the exact free energy values reported here, but to establish a self-consistent ground truth within a fixed system framework and parametrization, and then assess the performance of the new method relative to that internally consistent reference.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduced a custom C 90 fullerene structure as an interesting, simple and yet nontrivial test system to benchmark enhanced water sampling methods. We assigned partial charges to our fullerene atoms that resulted in a somewhat polar cavity. Using HREX simulations, we introduced an inlet to allow the solvent water to equilibrate inside the fullerene cavity. Our HREX simulations, started from different numbers of water inside the cavity to remove any initial structure bias, showed that the fullerene cavity can accommodate a maximum of 2 waters, and that conformations with 2 or 1 waters inside the cavity are dominant. The waters inside the fullerene cavity occupy three distinct binding sites, two of which are energetically equivalent (Figure ). Using our HREX simulations, we also determined the occupancies of these water binding sites in the dominant conformations. We calculated the equilibrium population densities of fullerene conformations with different numbers of water molecules inside the cavity using our HREX simulations, and found that fullerene conformation with 2 waters inside is energetically 0.03 ± 0.08 kcal mol–1 more favorable than conformation with 1 water inside.

Our HREX-based free energy difference between dominant fullerene conformations established the ground-truth free enrgy values for our system. We validated our nonequilibrium switching (NES)-based simulation protocol against this ground truth, by computing relevant free energies to displace water molecules from within the fullerene cavity. Specifically, we calculated the free energy to displace a water from a binding site within the fullerene cavity, using our NES protocol, and obtained a free energy vale directly comparable to the free energy difference between the dominant conformations (i.e., with 2- and 1- waters inside the cavity) that we obtained from our HREX simulations. Indeed, we obtained a close agreement, i.e., within 0.07 ± 0.15 kcal mol–1, between the free energies calculated using our NES protocol and the HREX simulations.

Our results demonstrate that the present fullerene system provides a nontrivial yet physically interpretable benchmark for evaluating the accuracy, efficiency, and convergence of slow water sampling methods. By offering a reproducible test case for slow/buried water sampling, it can facilitate systematic comparison across methodologies and guide the development of improved approaches for modeling water dynamics in confined environments. While the present model does not capture ligand binding or host conformational rearrangements, it is intentionally selected to isolate the thermodynamics of occluded water exchange, providing a controlled reference system that complements more complex, flexible biomolecular benchmarks.
