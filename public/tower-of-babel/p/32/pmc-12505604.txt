Clinical, microbiological characteristics and outcomes of carbapenemase-producing enterobacterales bloodstream infections in a pediatric hospital in Brazil
Authors: Derrick A Fassbind, Giovani B Bioni, Fabrizio Motta, Cícero A G Dias
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence of carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE) infections has been rising globally and is associated with high morbidity and mortality. However, data on CPE bloodstream infections (BSIs) in pediatric populations remain limited.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center observational study included pediatric patients diagnosed with CPE BSI between August 2019 and December 2024 at a tertiary care hospital in southern Brazil. Demographic, clinical, and microbiological data were analyzed, along with outcomes including 30-day mortality, need for pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission, and length of hospital stay.ResultsA total of 41 episodes of CPE BSI were identified in 39 patients. Among them, 65.8% had a chronic illness, 41.5% were immunosuppressed, and 97.6% had a central venous catheter. Klebsiella pneumoniae was the most frequently isolated pathogen. Carbapenemase production included KPC (58.5%), NDM (36.6%), and co-production of both enzymes (4.9%). The 30-day mortality rate was 25.6%, rising to 47.0% in patients with sepsis (p = 0.011). Carbapenem use in the previous 3 months were significantly associated with lower mortality (p = 0.008).ConclusionCPE BSI in pediatric patients primarily affects those with underlying illnesses, immunosuppression, or prior healthcare exposure, and is associated with high mortality. These findings underscore the need for targeted prevention, early detection, and optimized management strategies in this vulnerable population.Keywords: Carbapenemase-producing, Carbapenem-resistant, Enterobacterales, Children, Bloodstream infection

Introduction

The emergence of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria has become a global health threat, as recognized by the World Health Organization [1]. Among the principal contributors to this issue are carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE) [2–4]. CPE are an important cause of health-care associated infections and are increasingly reported worldwide [5], with bloodstream infections (BSIs) associated with high mortality rates and morbidity including intensive care unite (UCI) admission and prolonged hospital length of stay. Established risk factors for CPE infections include the presence of chronic diseases, immunosuppression, critical illness, invasive devices, or prior use of broad-spectrum antibiotics [6, 7].

Carbapenemases are classified into Ambler class A carbapenemases (serine-based hydrolytic mechanism)—primarily Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC), class B metallo-β-lactamases such as New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM), imipenemase (IMP), and Verona integron-encoded metallo-β-lactamase (VIM) and Class D carbapenemases (oxacillinases) such as OXA-48-like enzymes.³ Despite a wide global variability in the prevalence of these enzymes, in Brazil, KPC remains the most prevalent carbapenemase, consistent with trends observed across much of Latin America [8]. However, reports of NDM-producing strains have been increasingly identified in the country in recent years [9].

In addition to the type of carbapenemase, intrinsic resistance mechanisms significantly influence antimicrobial susceptibility patterns which can differ substantially across regions, impacting both empirical and definitive treatment decisions. The effectiveness of empiric regimens depends not only on the bacterial species and its intrinsic resistance characteristics but also on acquired molecular mechanisms of resistance which will determine its antimicrobial susceptibility profile and thus the definitive therapy as well [10, 11]. Beyond high morbidity and mortality rates, CPE infections impose significant economic burdens due to diagnostic and therapeutic costs, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to newer antibiotics remains limited [12].

Despite the clinical significance of these infections, limited research has addressed CPE BSI in pediatric populations, with most studies focused on adults. This study aims to describe the clinical and microbiological characteristics, including the characterization of molecular mechanisms of resistance, of pediatric patients with CPE BSI and analyze associated outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study analyzed the medical records of patients under 18 years of age diagnosed with CPE BSI between August 2019 and December 2024 at Hospital da Criança Santo Antônio in Porto Alegre—a high-complexity, 200-bed tertiary pediatric hospital and one of the largest in southern Brazil. The institution provides care to both patients covered by Brazil’s public healthcare system, free of charge, and those with private and paid insurance.

Microbiologic analysis

All cases with the first positive blood cultures for CPE of each BSI event were included and CPE was defined by the laboratory identification of carbapenemases. Blood cultures were processed using the BD BACTEC™ automated system, and bacterial identification was performed via MALDI-TOF (Biomérieux). Carbapenemase production was assessed with the Carba NP test [13], and enzyme identification was conducted using the RESISTIR-3 OKN K-Set™ (Coris BioConcept)—which detects KPC, NDM, and OXA-48—until January 2023, and the KPC/IMP/NDM/VIM/OXA-48 Combo Test Kit™ (Medomics) thereafter.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted as follows: disk diffusion for aztreonam, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (SMX-TMP), and ciprofloxacin; E-test for ceftazidime-avibactam; broth microdilution for polymyxin B; and automated MIC testing (BD Phoenix™) for meropenem, imipenem, tigecycline, and amikacin. Aztreonam susceptibility testing commenced in April 2021, and polymyxin B susceptibility was not assessed in organisms with known intrinsic resistance. Results were interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute 2021 M100 updates criteria [14].

Clinical and demographic data

All patients were managed by the hospital’s Pediatric Infectious Diseases team in collaboration with the primary care team. Collected data included demographics (age, sex), medical history (chronic illness such as congenital heart disease, chronic nephropathy, liver disease, neuropathy, or prematurity), and immunosuppression (e.g., malignancy under chemotherapy, post kidney or liver transplant status or other immunosuppressive treatment).

Information on prior medical interventions was recorded, including hospitalization in the preceding 12 months, PICU admission within 30 days, surgery within 30 days, and carbapenem use in the prior 3 months. CPE colonization was defined by a positive rectal swab, which were routinely collected at hospital and PICU admission and weekly until hospital discharge, or clinical sample in the absence of clinical signs of infections as assessed by the attending clinicians. The presence of any invasive devices (e.g., central venous catheter, mechanical ventilation, or urinary catheter) within the previous 30 days, regardless of when it was placed, was also documented. Hospital-acquired infections were defined as occurring after 48 h of hospital admission.

Other collected variables included infection type (primary BSI or secondary to another focus according to the clinical picture and complementary exams), presence of sepsis according to the 2024 International Consensus Criteria for Pediatric Sepsis and Septic Shock criteria [15], adequacy of empirical antimicrobial regimens based on subsequent susceptibility testing, number and type of antimicrobials used in the definitive regimens, and clinical outcomes, including 30-day mortality, PICU admission at any time from the positive blood culture to completion of the BSI treatment and including patients who were already in the PICU at the time of BSI onset, and hospital length of stay, considered from the positive blood culture to hospital discharge. Outcomes calculations were made using the number of patients rather than the number of events.

Statistical analysis

Age and length of hospital stay were described using medians and interquartile ranges due to non-normal data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as absolute and relative frequencies. Medians were compared using the Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis tests. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, along with the analysis of adjusted residuals, when necessary. Correlation between age and length of stay was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 28.0.

Results

Clinical and epidemiological characteristics

A total of 41 events of CPE BSI were identified in 39 patients. Cases were evenly distributed during the study period, with no outbreaks occurring. All BSI episodes included only one isolate and there were no polymicrobial infections identified. Two patients had two separated episodes of CPE BSI each, from the same organism and type of carbapenemase and the time between those episodes was 4 and 20 months, respectively. The median age was 14 months, ranging from 0 to 199 months, and the majority were male. Three term neonates were included, all of whom developed BSIs in the PICU following cardiac surgery. No cases were registered in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Overall clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Microbiological characteristics

Klebsiella pneumoniae was the most commonly isolated pathogen (43.9%). In 95.1% of cases, a single enzyme was detected: KPC in 58.5% and NDM in 36.6%. Co-production of both enzymes was observed in 4.9% of isolates. No cases were positive for OXA-48, VIM, or IMP, and no negative results were reported for all tested enzymes (Fig. 1).

The greatest susceptibility was observed to amikacin (58.5%). Four patients (9.8%), all with organisms that produce KPC, were susceptible to meropenem. Eight (19.5%) were intermediate—four organisms that produce KPC and four that produce NDM. All KPC-only isolates were susceptible to ceftazidime-avibactam (Table 2). All isolates were multidrug-resistant, defined as non-susceptible to at least 1 agent in ≥ 3 antimicrobial categories.

Outcomes

Thirty-day mortality was 25.6% (10/39), with a median survival of 6.5 days (IQR: 2–15). PICU admission was required in 74.4% (29/39), and the median length of hospital stay among survivors was 33 days (IQR: 19–55).

In univariate analysis, carbapenem use within the past 3 months was associated with lower 30-day mortality (11.5%, p = 0.008). The presence of sepsis was significantly associated with higher mortality (47.0%, p = 0.011). No other variables were significantly associated with mortality (Table 3).

PICU admission was required in 74.4% of patients and was more frequent in younger patients (median age: 7 vs. 42 months, p = 0.02). Other variables associated with PICU admission included congenital heart disease (p = 0.027), prior PICU admission (p = 0.009), indwelling bladder catheter (p = 0.027), and sepsis (p = 0.002). Conversely, solid organ transplantation (p = 0.003), previous hospitalization (p = 0.016) and the use of 2 antimicrobials in definitive therapy (p = 0.04) were associated with reduced need for PICU care (Table 3).

Longer hospital stays were observed in patients with prior carbapenem use (median 37 vs. 14.5 days, p = 0.010) and those with sepsis (median 58 vs. 24 days, p = 0.003). No other variables were significantly associated with length of stay (p > 0.10) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

There is a paucity of data on CPE infections in pediatric populations. Our cohort represents one of the largest and few studies of pediatric patients with CPE BSI globally. The findings demonstrate a predominance of infections in patients with chronic illnesses, immunosuppression, or prior healthcare exposure, being associated with a high mortality rate.⁵⁻⁶.

Consistent with previous literature, all patients in our cohort had at least two established risk factors for carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales infection.⁶⁻⁷ The most prevalent was the presence of a central venous catheter (97.6%), with more than 60% having prior exposure to carbapenems, surgery, PICU, or hospitalization—emphasizing the importance of surveillance and preventive strategies in high-risk pediatric populations.

Our median patient age of 14 months is notably lower than that reported in previous pediatric studies on CRE BSI, which have documented median ages over 40 months [16–18]. This may reflect our hospital’s role as a referral center for congenital anomalies, particularly congenital heart disease, which affected nearly 30% of patients. This also explains the younger age of patients requiring PICU care.

Klebsiella pneumoniae was the most frequently isolated pathogen, as in other studies on CRE infections [19, 20]. However, we observed a high proportion of Serratia marcescens (34.1%), which is intrinsically resistant to polymyxins—the antibiotic of choice, in combination with carbapenems, for the empirical treatment of patients at risk of multidrug-resistant Gram-negative infections in our hospital. This likely contributed to the low rate (53.7%) of appropriate empirical therapy in our cohort. It is well established that inadequate empirical therapy is associated with worse outcomes in serious infections, including those caused by CRE in chidren as demonstrated by Ara-Montojo et al. [21]. Still, inappropriate empirical treatment was not associated with increased mortality in our study, consistent with findings by Zhang et al. in carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae infections [17]. The lack of an observed association in our study may be due to factors such as small sample size, timing of therapy adjustments, or residual confounding.

Carbapenemase types were limited to KPC and NDM. The ratio between KPC (58.5%) and NDM (36.6%) was more balanced in our cohort contrasting with most epidemiological data, which show a significantly higher prevalence of KPC in the United States, Latin America– including Brazil– and Europe, and of NDM in Asia.⁸ This relatively high proportion of NDM in our population may be explained by the fact that the first isolation of an NDM-producing sample in Brazil occurred in Porto Alegre in 2014, after which it spread rapidly and became endemic [9, 22]. We found no significant association between enzyme type and mortality, aligning with other pediatric studies [17–23], but contrasting with adult data from Seo et al., which reported higher mortality with KPC compared to NDM [24].

We observed a 30-day mortality rate of 25.6%, rising to 47% in patients with sepsis—comparable to studies from China and India, which reported mortality rates of 11.5%, 18.5%, and 52% in children with CRE BSI [16–18]. In immunosuppressed patients, mortality was slightly lower (23.5%), in contrast to the 35.7% reported by López-Cubillos et al. in pediatric oncology patients [25]. The lower mortality in our cohort may reflect differences in population characteristics, such as the inclusion of patients with solid organ transplants. Interestingly, carbapenem use was associated with lower mortality. While causation cannot be determined, it is plausible that these patients received more targeted evaluation, early recognition and treatment due to clinician awareness of their risk for CPE infection, or survivor bias. Notably, appropriate empirical therapy did not correlate with improved survival.

PICU admission was less frequent among patients with solid organ transplantation and prior hospital admission—many of whom were diagnosed in general wards, and more frequent among patients with congenital heart disease that requiredperioperative intensive care admissions, prior to BSI episodes, which can explain these findings.

We found no statistically significant mortality differences based on antimicrobial regimens or the number of agents used in definitive therapy. However the use of 2 antimicrobials in definitive therapy was associated with significantly less need for PICU admissions. This could be explained by the high proportion of NDM in patients that didn’t need PICU admission (70%), many of whom were treated with the combination of ceftazidime-avibactam with aztreonam. Nonetheless, given the study’s retrospective and observational nature, it is not suited to assess treatment efficacy. The variety of regimens used also reflects evolving therapeutic strategies, particularly following the 2022 introduction of ceftazidime-avibactam plus aztreonam for treatment of metallo-β-lactamase producers in the Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines [26]. Although ceftazidime-avibactam was already widely used for targeted therapy in our hospital at the time the study began, the association with aztreonam was only routinely used after the guideline release.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the small sample size, which may limit its power to assess factors associated with mortality and some subgroup analyses may be underpowered due to small numbers (e.g., for less common organisms). Due to its small sample size, the study analyzed associations between risk factors and outcomes using only univariate analyses. Without multivariate modeling, potential confounders were not controlled for, which may affect the interpretation of associations with outcomes. Secondly, its observational and retrospective design may contribute to information and observation biases. Finally, it is a monocentric study, where the clinical and microbiological profile may not apply to other populations. Another limitation is the fact that two different carbapenemase detection kits were used during the study period, with the initial kit unable to detect VIM and IMP. This could have influenced detection rates. Additionally, inclusion criteria were based on confirmed carbapenemase production rather than phenotypic resistance to carbapenems. This approach may have excluded carbapenem-resistant non-CPE organisms, possibly underestimating the true burden of CRE and limiting the generalizability of the findings. Nevertheless, carbapenemase production is the main resistance mechanism, with other mechanisms occurring infrequently. Using the definition of carbapenem resistance as an inclusion criteria could lead to the exclusion of CPE isolates with retained carbapenem susceptibility—evident in 9.8% of our cases.

Despite these limitations, our study represents one of the largest pediatric CPE BSI cohorts globally, using high-level laboratory methodologies, which enhance understanding of CPE BSI in children.

Conclusion

CPE infections are an emerging concern in pediatric populations, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where they are associated with high morbidity and mortality. Our cohort—one of the largest reported globally—demonstrated a high prevalence of healthcare-associated risk factors and a substantial mortality rate, particularly among patients with sepsis. These findings underscore the urgent need for improved surveillance, early detection, and targeted therapeutic strategies. This study contributes valuable insights into the management of pediatric CPE BSI and highlights the necessity for continued research and public health interventions.
