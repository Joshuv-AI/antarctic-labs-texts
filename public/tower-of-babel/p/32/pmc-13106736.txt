Intraoperative fluorescence-guided fresh frozen sectioning for margin control in head and neck cancer: phase 2 clinical trial
Authors: Thomas S Nijboer, Bas Keizers, Koos Boeve, Daan Roos, Sebastiaan A H J de Visscher, Gyorgy B Halmos, Boudewijn E C Plaat, Henrik K Huizinga, Hendrikus H Boersma, Jan J Doff, Kees-Pieter Schepman, Bert van der Vegt, Max J H Witjes, Floris J Voskuil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumour-free margins ( >5 mm) in head and neck malignancies improve prognosis, possibly impacting the need for adjuvant treatment. In this phase II prospective, non-randomised trial (NCT05499065), we investigate the use of fluorescence imaging using the tumour targeting fluorescent tracer cetuximab-IRDye800CW combined with fresh frozen sectioning to identify inadequate margins intraoperatively in patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma. Primary objectives of our study are intraoperative detection of inadequate margins and additional resections, and feasibility of implementing fluorescence-guided fresh frozen sectioning (FG-FFS) into the clinical workflow. In this study, 10 out of 20 patients show inadequate margins after initial surgery; FG-FFS correctly classifies margin in 19 patients. Additional resections increase tumour-free margin rates from 50% to 85%, where anatomical boundaries limit further improvement in one patient. Intraoperative margin adjustment reduces the need for adjuvant treatment in seven patients. These findings demonstrate that FG-FFS is a reliable and feasible method for intraoperative margin assessment without delaying surgery.Subject terms: Surgical oncology, Cancer imaging, Cancer therapy, Oral cancer detection

Introduction

Surgery is the primary treatment modality for many head and neck malignancies. Resection margins are considered adequate when at least 5 mm wide1–3. Despite this well-established requirement, tumour-free surgical margins are achieved in only 30–65% of patients4,5. Inadequate resections adversely impact survival and locoregional control: 5-year disease-free survival rates of approximately 79%, 63% and 52% for tumour-free (≥5 mm), close (<5 mm and ≥1 mm) and tumour-positive margins (<1 mm), respectively1–3,6,7.

Call-back surgery for inadequate margin correction is challenging, as accurately relocating inadequate margins is difficult, particularly in patients who have undergone reconstructive surgery8. Achieving tumour-free margins at the initial surgery is of major clinical importance, as adjuvant treatment cannot fully compensate for the negative prognostic impact of initially inadequate margins9,10.

Fresh frozen sectioning (FFS) of the excised tumour specimen is the standard for evaluating margins during surgery11–14. An FFS procedure involves preparation of a 6 µm tissue section from a tissue slice, which has been rapidly frozen after fresh sectioning using a cryostat. This frozen section is subsequently haematoxylin & eosin (H&E) stained to assess tumour margins. Although intraoperative FFS provides a high specificity for margin assessment, sampling error substantially reduces the sensitivity of this technique15–18. Various approaches for improved intraoperative identification of inadequate margins have been investigated19,20. Fluorescence-guided surgery using tumour-targeted tracers has proven to be beneficial in head and neck cancer21–25. The use of cetuximab-IRDye800CW to detect oral cancer cells is feasible, since these cells overexpress epidermal growth factor receptor22. However, the intensity of the fluorescent signal does not reliably correlate with the margin width using current fluorescence imaging approaches25. To overcome this issue, a combined approach, supplementing fluorescence imaging with intraoperative fresh frozen sectioning (i.e., fluorescence-guided fresh frozen sectioning (FG-FFS)), has been developed. Although the combination of fluorescence imaging and fresh frozen sectioning has been suggested previously, this has not been systematically investigated23–25. Krishnan et al. examined the localisation of fresh frozen sectioning based on clinical suspicion compared to a sentinel margin approach, which uses the areas of highest fluorescence intensities as indicators of potentially inadequate margins. They found a strong correlation between these approaches; however, they did not investigate margins not suspected to be at risk24.

In this phase two intervention study, we demonstrate that fluorescence imaging of the whole resection specimen and fresh tissue slices containing cetuximab-IRDye800CW successfully localises margins at risk, while FFS provides margin distance measurements of these margins in millimetres. This combined approach enables fluorescence imaging to guide intraoperative pathology assessment beyond clinical suspicion alone, allowing for timely recognition and intraoperative adjustment of inadequate margins.

Results

The primary endpoints of this phase two study were the detection rates of inadequate margins by fluorescence imaging only and FG-FFS, including the number of additional resections based on FG-FFS. Additionally, tracer distribution (i.e., the spatial localisation of the cetuximab-IRDye800CW probe imaged with a fluorescence camera) was evaluated and compared to pathology margins. Secondary endpoints for the study were patient characteristics and the feasibility of implementation and clinical application of FG-FFS into the current clinical workflow.

Patient and tumour characteristics

Between March 2023 and November 2024, 23 patients with histopathology-proven oral squamous cell carcinoma were included in this study. Study drugs consisting of unlabelled cetuximab and cetuximab-IRDye800CW were administered intravenously 2 days prior to surgery. During study drug administration, two patients showed infusion-related anaphylaxis to the unlabelled cetuximab, leading to termination of intravenous administration and study exclusion. Both patients recovered after intramuscular adrenaline administration and an NaCl fluid challenge and standard of care surgery could continue as scheduled. One other patient was excluded due to deviations from the intraoperative imaging protocol, resulting in a total of 20 patients completing all study procedures. The intraoperative FG-FFS margin assessment protocol is shown in Fig. 1. An overview of the patient and tumour characteristics is shown in Table 1 (secondary endpoint). The mean age of study participants was 57 (SD = 17.7), 13 (65%) patients were female. Tumours were primarily located in the tongue (n = 16) and staged pT1-3. Lymph node metastases were present in four patients.

Surgical outcomes and clinical implications

Figure 1 gives an overview of the intraoperative specimen handling pipeline. Standard of care surgery resulted in one tumour specimen per patient. This standard of care resection and subsequent pathology analysis, prior to any study intervention, would have resulted in ten (50%) inadequate margins (Fig. 2A). For study purposes, the whole resection specimen was sliced into fresh tissue slices intraoperatively at the department of pathology of the University Medical Centre Groningen (UMCG). The first primary endpoint of this study included the evaluation of the detection of inadequate margins by FG-FFS. FG-FFS based on the fresh tissue slices correctly identified margins in 95% of patients (19 out of 20). One inadequate margin was missed where FG-FFS estimated a margin of 5.5 mm, but final pathology showed a margin of 4.5 mm. This resulted in a sensitivity, specificity and accuracy of 90.0%, 100.0% and 95.0%, respectively. Besides the identified closest margins by FG-FFS, no other locations with inadequate margins were identified after complete pathology analysis.

Another primary endpoint was the evaluation of the performed number of immediate intraoperative additional resections. This was performed in eight out of nine patients with an inadequate margin identified by FG-FFS (Fig. 2B). For one patient, anatomical boundaries prevented an additional resection. The eight additional resections resulted in tumour-free margins at final pathology assessment in seven patients (Fig. 2C). Margins remained inadequate in one patient (Fig. 2, patient 9). In this case, the surgeon underestimated the additional resection width; a margin width of 1.1 mm was converted into 3.3 mm. Overall, the final inadequate margin rate was reduced from 50% to 15%. Intraoperative decision-making was performed within one hour (i.e., during treatment of the neck or preparations for reconstruction of the defect) without delaying surgery and was therefore feasible within a clinical time frame (secondary endpoint).

Study-related additional resections led to de-escalation or complete omission of margin-based adjuvant therapy in seven of the ten patients who otherwise would have required therapy. Adjuvant therapy was entirely averted for five patients, thereby preventing chemo-radiotherapy, radiotherapy or call-back surgery. For two patients, adjuvant therapy was minimised to 56 Gy radiotherapy only instead of chemo-radiotherapy or 66 Gy radiotherapy (Fig. 2A, C).

Correlation of FG-FFS margins with final pathology analysis

An important point to consider is whether the intraoperative margin provided by pathology based on FG-FFS is comparable to the margin provided after final pathology analysis, when the tissue is formalin fixed and paraffin embedded. In a retrospective analysis, these margin widths were compared. For this analysis, all patients with at least one exact FFS margin in mm and final pathology margin width in mm of that same anatomical location could be included. Nineteen patients were included and one patient was excluded due to a missing exact FFS margin (recorded only to be greater than 5.1 mm). A significant difference (degrees of freedom (df) = 17, p < 0.001, effect size = −1.07, 95% confidence interval (95% CI) = −1.16 to −0.43) was found between the FG-FFS margin estimate and final pathology margin. Bland-Altman analysis revealed an average margin width overestimation of 0.8 mm by FG-FFS, as shown in Fig. 3. A post hoc correction for this overestimation was performed to evaluate the potential impact on this observed difference. After correcting FG-FFS margins for this overestimation by subtracting 0.8 mm from each determined FFS margin, no significant difference (df = 17, p = 0.591, effect size = 0.13, 95% CI = −0.25–0.43) remained between the FG-FFS margin and final pathology margin.

Analysis of other potential intraoperative assessment points

To further evaluate the first primary endpoint, data from other potential intraoperative assessment points other than fresh tissue slice FG-FFS were separately analysed. These points included fluorescence imaging only of the whole resection specimen, fluorescence imaging only of the fresh tissue slices and whole resection specimen FG-FFS. Note that these assessment points were all performed for every resection, as the steps did not interfere with one another. As shown in Fig. 1, fluorescence imaging of the whole resection specimen was performed first, followed by identification of spots and demarcation with a needle (‘fluorescence imaging only of the whole resection specimen’ in Table 2). Next, the specimen was cut into fresh tissue slices, followed by imaging of these tissue slices (fluorescence imaging only of fresh tissue slices) to identify tissue slices with potentially inadequate margins. For the whole resection specimen FG-FFS, an FFS of the tissue slice with the demarcation needle was acquired; for the fresh tissue slice FG-FFS, an FFS of the tissue slice with the estimated closest margin was acquired. Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) for all four strategies are shown in Table 2. The endpoint tracer distribution and its correlation to final histopathology were assessed using fluorescence imaging of the whole resection specimen. Fluorescence imaging only of the whole resection specimen analysis (Fig. 1.2) accurately identified tumour-positive margins (<1 mm), as all four detected spots (spot-to-background ratios (SBRs) ranging between 1.7 and 2.1) were tumour-positive. However, the absence of a spot was not indicative for tumour-free margins (>5 mm), as shown by the low sensitivity caused by six false negative cases. Contrarily, fluorescence imaging only of the fresh tissue slices (Fig. 1.4) was indicative for tumour-free margins, but did show seven false inadequate margins. During fresh tissue slice FG-FFS, these false inadequate margins were corrected by FFS, increasing specificity. Representative examples of inadequate and tumour-free resection margins are shown in Fig. 4.

Discussion

In this trial, fluorescence-guided fresh frozen sectioning led to correct intraoperative classification of the margin in 95% of the included patients, without extending surgery time. The inadequate margin rate of 50% was decreased to 15%, after correct identification of the margin using fresh tissue slice fluorescence-guided fresh frozen sectioning. Subsequent margin-based adjuvant treatment was decreased or omitted in seven out of ten patients with an initial inadequate margin.

The concept of targeted fluorescence imaging for tumour margin assessment to date has been based on whole resection specimen imaging in surgery and is assumed to yield margin information with sufficient certainty. A recent meta-analysis of five tumour-targeted fluorescence studies in patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma showed a pooled sensitivity and specificity of 95.7% and 82.7%, respectively, for detecting tumour-positive margins26. Close margins have not been systematically investigated26. These studies were performed based on ex vivo whole resection specimens, stipulating the current standard in margin assessment using fluorescence imaging27–31. This is confirmed by the largest current trial in head and neck cancer using fluorescence imaging only of the whole resection specimen25. Whereas this study showed a sensitivity and specificity for tumour-positive margins of 100% and 86%, respectively, close margin detection proved to be more difficult with a sensitivity and specificity of 70% and 76%25. Therefore, correlating fluorescence intensities to margin widths in whole resection specimen imaging is unattainable. First, tissue optical properties and scattering limit penetration depth of near-infrared light to <5 mm, resulting in a limited reliability for detecting close margins. Second, tumour size and tracer accumulation within tumour tissue influence resection side fluorescence intensities. In particular, small tumours and low tracer accumulation substantially reduce fluorescence intensities, complicating the detection of signals originating from tumour tissue on the resection side. The exact margin width in specimens can be provided by FFS, as indicated by a high specificity (99.1%). However, FFS used as a single modality has a limited sensitivity (79.5%), due to sampling error18,28. Tumour-targeted fluorescence imaging and FFS were studied separately before, yet a combination of both had never been investigated. In this study, we investigated this combination, showing a high sensitivity (90.0%) and high specificity (100.0%) for the detection of both tumour-positive and close margins.

In this study, fresh tissue slice FG-FFS outperformed other possible intraoperative assessment points, as shown in Table 2. Subjective measurements of the observers using fluorescence imaging only of the fresh tissue slices often underestimated the margin width, leading to seven false inadequate margins. Non-specific tracer fluorescence signals, such as scattering effects, accumulation of tracer in non-tumour tissue, and presence of haemoglobin, expand the region that fluoresces on which margin estimation was based32. The margin width underestimation did not impact final outcomes, as fluorescence imaging only of the fresh tissue slices served as a red-flag technique to identify potential close margins to be corrected or confirmed by FFS. As a specific fluorescence signals were comparable throughout tissue within a patient, the closest margin could always be identified using fluorescence imaging. The combination with FFS proved to be highly effective, as FFS provided exact margin widths to correct for subjective fluorescence imaging margin measurements, illustrating the robustness of the approach.

Whole resection specimen fluorescence imaging only proved that the absence of a fluorescent spot does not indicate a tumour-free margin for all cases, shown by six false tumour-negative cases. A spot with an SBR ≥ 1.7 indicated a true tumour-positive margin in this trial, showing a high PPV25. Therefore, a potential workflow for future trials could encompass an initial whole resection specimen assessment. In case a spot with SBR > 1.7 is obtained, an immediate additional resection should be performed. In this trial, this approach would have resulted in no unnecessary additional resections. Additionally, when no spot is observed, a fresh tissue slice FG-FFS should be performed to find otherwise missed possible close margins and fully exploit the potential of fluorescence-guided surgery with a targeted tracer. Performing FFS localised by whole resection specimen fluorescence imaging did not increase any of the observed parameters (Table 2) and can therefore be averted in the following trials.

Fresh tissue slice FG-FFS showed a margin width overestimation of 0.8 mm compared to the final pathology margin based on conventional histopathology. This disparity most likely is caused by the additional tissue processing, consisting of dehydration steps that induce tissue shrinkage and subsequent paraffin embedding12,18,33. When correcting the fresh tissue slice FG-FFS margin width for the observed overestimation of 0.8 mm, the single missed close margin of 4.5 mm would have been detected, resulting in a correct additional resection for this patient. Correction for this overestimation can be incorporated in future studies, although the 0.8 mm difference found is currently based on a limited number of samples.

Despite the considerable reduction in inadequate margins in the current study, some limitations should be considered. To facilitate implementation in the current pathology workflow, resections containing bone were not included. A future method processing bone invading tumours within a clinically relevant timeframe could enable incorporating these specimens in the proposed fresh tissue slice FG-FFS method12. Besides, difficulties in assessing margins were caused by tissue artifacts inherent to the frozen sectioning process, mainly consisting of tangential slicing and blade chatter. Omitting FFS and solely relying on fluorescence imaging results in a potential bias due to non-specific fluorescence signals22,24. The addition of FFS substantially increased specificity of margin assessment compared to fluorescence imaging only of fresh tissue slices (Table 2).

Adjuvant treatment was decreased (70%), reducing patient morbidity. Following the increased radical resection rate, an increased (disease-free) survival rate could be expected. This will be monitored both in the population of the current study and in a larger randomised control trial, currently being designed10. Here, the tracer cetuximab-IRDye800CW was used. This approach will also apply to newly developed tracers (e.g., fragmented antibodies and small peptides), as long as a sufficient tracer accumulation in the tumour is achieved. We estimate that a tracer should be clinically usable when a tumour to background ratio of >2.0 is obtained. Possibly, a shorter accumulation time enables administration within an easier clinically applicable timeframe34,35. Future studies using fluorescence imaging could use a step-up strategy in which a first decision could be made on assessment of the whole resection specimen, with immediate additional resections based on the presence of spots with an SBR > 1.7. For inconclusive whole resection specimen fluorescence imaging, fresh tissue slice FG-FFS would result in correct margin assessment, still within a clinically feasible timeframe. Moreover, techniques increasing contrast and signal-depth information, such as tissue clearing and a dual aperture approach, could enable faster and more objective margin assessment36,37. Paired tagging in this study simplified the orientation of the resection specimen and subsequent guidance of the potential additional resection and is thus recommended for future studies16. Orientation and intraoperative feedback from the pathologist to the surgeon could be enhanced further by the addition of upcoming digital 3-dimensional specimen mapping38. Recent advances in oncology treatment consist mainly of the use of immunotherapy (e.g., immune checkpoint inhibitors). We believe the use of the described FG-FFS technique is not mutually exclusive. Previous studies on neo-adjuvant treatment regimens suggest some patients obtain a complete response, while other patient groups only show a partial, heterogeneous response. This second group still requires surgical treatment, which may prove more complex due to the presence of multiple tumour fields, difficult to distinguish by eye. Additionally, a group of patients who are initially ineligible for surgery may be downstaged after immunotherapy and thereby become eligible. Therefore, we expect a large number of patients may benefit from this fresh tissue slice FG-FFS.

In conclusion, intraoperative margin assessment using the fluorescence-guided fresh frozen sectioning approach correctly classified margins in 95% of the patients. FG-FFS-based additional resections decreased inadequate margins from 50% to 15%, resulting in omittance or reduction of margin-based adjuvant treatment in 70% of the patients who would have otherwise required additional treatment. Considering the known side-effects of cetuximab, such as anaphylaxis, clinical implementation was uncomplicated, as the technique did not increase surgery time and direct margin feedback could be provided to the surgeon. Thus, margin assessment using FG-FFS substantially increases correct intraoperative margin assessment and enables additional resections in case of an inadequate margin.

Methods

Study design and participants

This phase two, non-randomised, non-blinded, prospective, and interventional study was performed at the department of Oral & Maxillofacial Surgery of the UMCG, the Netherlands. The Institutional Review Board of the UMCG approved the study (METc 2022/456), and it was conducted according to the Dutch Act on Medical Research involving Human Subjects (WMO) and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (adapted version of Fortaleza, Brazil, 2013). The study is registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05499065).

Patients ≥18 years old with histopathology-proven oral squamous cell carcinoma scheduled to undergo surgery, as decided in the multidisciplinary head and neck tumour board meeting of the UMCG, were eligible for inclusion in the study. All eligible patients were included regardless of sex or gender. Sex was registered for baseline characteristics using electronical patient record data documented in the hospital electronic patient records, as self-reported by each patient. After obtaining written informed consent, the inclusion and exclusion criteria were checked. Briefly, patients were included if they showed no evidence of QTc prolongation, were not pregnant or lactating, and had no history of allergy or infusion reactions to monoclonal antibodies.

Fluorescent tracer administration

The study drug cetuximab-IRDye800CW was produced under Good Manufacturing Practice in the UMCG, and released by a qualified person39. Two days before surgery, study drugs were administered intravenously in accordance with the previously published tracer administration protocol22,25. First, an intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine was given, according to the standard of care cetuximab treatment to reduce allergic reaction risk. Next, patients received 75 mg unlabelled cetuximab as a pre-dose, followed by 15 mg fluorescently labelled cetuximab, cetuximab-IRDye800CW, after 1 h. Pre-dosing using unlabelled cetuximab improves fluorescent tracer localisation in the tumour, as shown previously22.

Intraoperative margin assessment

An overview of the intraoperative margin assessment protocol is shown in Fig. 1. Surgery started with primary tumour resection, followed by treatment of the neck. During tumour resection, paired tagging was performed for orientation purposes. Paired tagging consists of stitches marked with clips placed in the mucosa of both the surgical cavity and the resection specimen on at least four corresponding anatomical locations. Stitches at matching anatomical locations were marked with the same number of clips. Paired tagging ensured accurate relocation of the anatomical site within the surgical cavity in case of an inadequate margin (Fig. 1.1)16. In this study, in-vivo imaging was not performed, since this results in inadequate illumination of the resection margins and therefore limits assessment. Imaging of the excised specimen is more straightforward, enabling proper and standardised illumination of all surgical margins. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging, therefore, served as a red flag technique for fresh frozen sectioning36.

Immediately after tumour excision, all sides of the resected specimen were imaged using a closed-field fluorescence camera (i.e., PEARL-trilogy®, LI-COR BioSciences Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA) at the department of Pathology and Medical Biology of the UMCG. The acquired images were scaled relative to the maximum intensity of the tumour/mucosal side using ImageJ (version 1.53t, Fiji, Bethesda, MD, USA). Subsequently, two observers assessed the images for fluorescent spots using the colourmap MPL-inferno, indicating possible inadequate margins. with I¯ being the mean intensity. The background region of interest was identified on the same image as the spot, based on all tissue adjacent to the spot of a similar origin (e.g. muscle, fat) and identical distance to the camera (as shown in Fig. 4A). A spot with an SBR > 1.5 was marked on the whole resection specimen using a needle, followed by a fluorescence image to confirm the marker location. The location of this needle served as the reference for the whole resection specimen, FG-FFS, as shown in Fig. 1.

Subsequently, the resection sides of the specimen were inked, and the specimen was freshly cut into 3–5 mm thick tissue slices by dedicated pathology staff. Next, images of all fresh tissue slices were acquired using the fluorescence camera. Delineation of tumour tissue on fluorescence images was performed by two observers, based on maximum fluorescence intensity and a substantial drop-off of signal towards all resection sides. From these estimated tumour outlines, resection margins were measured on the images of the tissue slices, identifying the closest margins based on fluorescence imaging. In case of disagreement between observers, a third observer was consulted. If, based on fluorescence imaging, all margins were assessed as being tumour-free, FFS was performed on the fresh tissue slice containing the estimated closest margin. When one or more inadequate margin(s) were suspected based on fluorescence imaging of the fresh tissue slices, FFS of these margins was performed. In case FFS confirmed an inadequate margin, the surgeon was informed by the pathologist. An additional resection was performed if deemed clinically feasible and safe. Determining the exact location for additional resection was essential for effective treatment. To ensure this, the following measures were taken: continuous orientation tracking by two dedicated researchers throughout tissue imaging and processing, paired tagging to preserve anatomical orientation, documentation of cutting planes during tissue slicing to enable accurate relocation of tissue slices in the original resection specimen, and collaborative reporting by pathologist and researchers to the surgeon of the precise location of any inadequate margins identified in the FFS. An exemption to this workflow was made only for cases in which a tumour-positive margin was apparent based on visual and tactile assessment by both pathologist and surgeon and fluorescence imaging of the fresh tissue slices. Even though not proven by FFS, an immediate additional resection was performed. Altogether, two FG-FFS methods were investigated: (1) FFS of the tissue slice identified by a spot and marked with a needle on whole resection specimen fluorescence imaging, and (2) FFS of the tissue slice identified as the slice containing the closest margin by fresh tissue slice fluorescence imaging.

Following the FFS procedure, the fresh tissue slices were formalin fixed for at least 24 h, followed by paraffin embedding and 4 µm sectioning for H&E staining, according to standard of care. Based on the standard of care H&E staining assessment, margins before and after potential additional resections were assessed by a pathologist. Based on pathology results before and after study intervention, adjuvant treatment was determined by the multidisciplinary head and neck tumour board of the UMCG. During the trial, adverse events were reported to the Institutional Review Board using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 6.0.

Endpoints

In this study, three primary and two secondary endpoints were evaluated. The first primary endpoint was the comparison between fluorescence imaging alone and FG-FFS for detecting tumour-positive and close surgical margins. Diagnostic accuracy was evaluated by calculating sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV and NPV for both methods. An additional primary endpoint was the number of additional resections performed based on FG-FFS data and the corresponding postoperative pathology outcomes. Tracer distribution, evaluated by correlating SBR values of the whole resection specimen with margin widths, and conversely, margin widths with SBR values, was the last primary endpoint of this study. The secondary endpoints were patient characteristics, feasibility of on-site intraoperative tissue analysis by the surgeon and pathologist and feasibility of novel 3D imaging methods of the obtained additional biopsies. The feasibility of novel 3D imaging methods of the obtained additional biopsies is not reported here, as it did not align with the primary focus of the trial. The feasibility of the analysis was assessed based on the duration of surgical procedures and the integration of FG-FFS into the operative workflow.

Statistics and reproducibility

The sample size was calculated using a two-proportion power analysis, assuming a baseline proportion of 0.5, an expected proportion of 0.2, a 95% confidence level, 80% power, and resulted in a required sample size of at least 19 patients. Patients were excluded from the analysis in case they did not undergo all study procedures. The patients were not randomised.

Descriptive statistics of patient demographics were tested using the Anderson–Darling test, D’Agostino and Pearson test, Shapiro–Wilk test and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0, GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, California, USA). Normally distributed data were reported using the mean with standard deviation (SD) and non-Gaussian data using the median with interquartile range (IQR). Next, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV and NPV for margin assessment were calculated for both fluorescence imaging only and FG-FFS. Finally, FG-FFS margin widths were compared with final pathology margins using a paired t-test and Bland-Altman analysis to assess a potential bias. As this manuscript concerns a clinical intervention trial, all experiments could only be performed once (during surgery).
