Dephosphorylation-regulated MNAzyme-PER cascade as a programmable converter for multi-task molecular actuation
Authors: Yaoyi Zhang, Yiqi Zhang, Jianbo Jiang, Dan Bai, Li Zhang, Li Wang, Xingping Hu, Jiu Pu, Yu He, Xiaole Han, Weitao Wang, Na Yin, Xiaomei Lin, Xingyu Liu, Tingmei Chen, Yongcan Guo, Guoming Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNucleic acid conformational regulation and enzymatic dynamic modulation play a pivotal role in creating collaborative amplification systems with programmable responses. However, the coordination of sophisticated molecular functions within synergistic cascade networks remains constrained by limited progress in leakage mitigation and modular functionality expansion. Here, we report a dephosphorylation-regulated MNAzyme-primer exchange reaction (PER)-based (Dp-PER) strategy, with its core mechanism centered on cyclic phosphate-locked conformation to suppress signal leakage. This design specifically incorporates upstream primer formulation and an enzyme-orchestrated cascade, seamlessly connecting upstream molecular network to PER circuit through dephosphorylation-induced conformational release. Within the sequential reaction mechanism, the transduction module exhibits intrinsic reactivity confinement and strategic activation in actuation of the amplification module distinct from traditional downstream primer. Importantly, the highly programmable MNAzyme sensing arm enables the Dp-PER to respond flexibly to diverse molecular cues. This capability underpins various tasks, including logical operations (YES, NOT, OR, AND, INHIBIT), multifunctional biosensing (nucleic acid, enzyme), and nanostructure cascading with tetrahedral DNA. The MNAzyme-PER enzymatic cascade is a scalable, compatible system that acts as a universal converter in hierarchical DNA networks. Introduction

DNA has transcended its classical biological roles as genetic information carriers to emerge as core functional materials in dynamic DNA nanotechnology. This evolution is founded on the governing principles of Watson-Crick base pairing, which enable its programmability for nanoscale assembly and sophisticated molecular recognition through conformational switching [1, 2]. These properties enable the construction of highly complex DNA logic circuits [3], precision molecular nanoengineering [4], and the development of a new generation of intelligent diagnostic and sensing systems [5, 6]. Within this framework, nucleic acid conformational regulation and enzymatic dynamic modulation have been established as two fundamental mechanisms for coordinating molecular interactions and implementing sophisticated functions [7, 8]. Conformational regulation endows nucleic acid structures with sensing and responsive control capabilities, whilst also providing highly specific triggering signals for enzymatic cascade processes. This synergy effectively transduces subtle conformational changes induced by molecular recognition events into amplified functional outputs, enabling the construction of adaptive, multi-layered, and programmable dynamic DNA systems [9, 10]. Currently, cascade systems developed based on these principles have become essential tools for advancing applications such as programmable molecular computing and logic gates [11], high-precision biosensing and molecular diagnostics [12], as well as intelligent nanomachines and self-assembling systems [13].

Current cascade strategies typically employ the integration of molecular recognition elements with amplification modules [14], with the multicomponent DNAzyme (MNAzyme) representing a particularly promising transduction platform through conformational assembly [15]. This target-activated molecular switch translates recognition events into defined output sequences to coordinate cascade reactions [16, 17], while its modular programmability has enabled integrated sensing systems for diverse applications including information processing [18], molecular detection [19], biomedical analysis [20], and nanomechanical devices [21, 22]. In several studies, DNAzyme cleavage activity has been utilized to initiate nucleic acid amplification, encompassing rolling circle amplification and exponential amplification reaction, employing downstream products generated from 3’ end of the probe [23, 24]. However, a key limitation of this strategy is that both cleavage products and the original probes are capable of inducing amplification through their 3′-OH termini, resulting in substantial background signal. To mitigate this issue, heterogeneous assays have been proposed to distinguish target products from substrates through additional separation steps, yet such procedures increase operational complexity [23]. Additionally, homogeneous systems commonly necessitate the incorporation of blocking strands or complex stem-loop structures to reduce signal leakage arising from DNA breathing [25, 26], demanding precise melting temperature control and laborious probe optimization. These constraints render system design both cumbersome and unpredictable, while also facing unavoidable background interference. Moreover, such intricate and customized designs hinder modular functional expansion, making it challenging to adapt systems to new functional inputs [27]. Therefore, there is a pressing need for novel strategies that fundamentally circumvent nonspecific amplification while retaining high modularity, in order to enable cascade systems with improved precision and broader applicability.

Enzymatic regulation offers a promising strategy for addressing the coordination of cascade reactions in complex DNAzyme systems [28]. T4 polynucleotide kinase (T4 PNK) displays 5’-hydroxyl kinase, 3’-phosphatase, and 2’,3’-cyclic phosphodiesterase activities against a wide range of substrates, playing an essential role in DNA break repair and ligation processes [29]. The primer exchange reaction (PER), widely adopted for signal transduction and amplification, facilitates programmable synthesis of repetitive single-stranded DNA sequences through DNA polymerase with specifically designed primers [30, 31]. Inspired by this, we introduce a dephosphorylation-regulated MNAzyme-PER (Dp-PER) platform for programmable, enzyme-synergized cascade processes. This strategy involves upstream primer design and T4 PNK-mediated dephosphorylation control, enabling effective coupling with MNAzyme transduction and PER amplification. Unlike conventional downstream primer that causes nonspecific amplification, the Dp-PER is a streamlined design that leverages the intrinsic self-blocking property of the cyclic phosphate moiety to achieve leakage-suppressed and definable signal output. By reprogramming the upstream molecular network, we demonstrate the capability of the Dp-PER to perform Boolean logic operations in response to arbitrary nucleic acid inputs. Furthermore, through the rational design of additional transduction modules, the Dp-PER enables multifunctional sensing of breast cancer-related microRNA (miRNA) and apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1 (APE1). Notably, the system also supports integration with intricate nanostructures for topological regulation and functional augmentation. We envision that this programmable Dp-PER will function as a powerful core module, opening up more novel concepts for biocomputing, molecular diagnostics and nanomedicine.

Results and discussion

Design and principle of dephosphorylation-regulated MNAzyme-PER system

This study presents a dephosphorylation-mediated enzymatic regulation switch that promotes the integration of MNAzyme catalytic transduction with PER amplification to construct versatile molecular logic circuits and sensing platforms (Scheme 1A). In our design (Scheme 1B), the linker is responsible for the assembly of MNAzyme through hybridization, subsequently triggering the cyclic cleavage at the rA site within the substrate. This process generates a substantial amount of cleavage products bearing a 2’,3’-cyclic phosphate terminus at the rA site, which blocks the amplification signal that would otherwise be generated by their 3’ ends acting as primers. Therefore, we rationally designed the primer sequence to be complementary to the MNAzyme substrate arm and positioned it as the upstream primer (the primer sequence is located at the 5’ end of the nucleic acid probe). Subsequently, the 2’,3’-cyclic phosphodiesterase and 3’-phosphatase activities of T4 PNK catalyze the removal of the terminal groups on these cleavage products, acting as a dephosphorylation switch to restore their 3’-end primer function and thereby couple upstream transduction with downstream amplification. The dephosphorylated upstream products then function as primers that efficiently hybridize with amplification hairpin, initiating the PER circuit to synthesize G‑quadruplex (G4) concatemers. Upon incorporation of thioflavin T (ThT) dye, the resulting G4/ThT complex yields significantly amplified fluorescence signals. Notably, the 2’,3’-cyclic phosphate terminus of the upstream cleavage product serves as an intrinsic blocking group, facilitating the strategic activation of downstream amplification via a dephosphorylation-regulated mechanism. Unlike conventional downstream primers, which potentially induce undesired nonspecific amplification due to hybridization with PER amplification hairpins arising from probe breathing, this design effectively mitigates signal leakage in complex cascaded networks. Furthermore, the MNAzyme serves as a programmable transducer, possessing comprehensive multianalyte sensing capability. This feature enables the Dp-PER to support multimodal miRNA logic analysis, versatile magnified detection in nucleic acid and enzyme, and efficient cascading with tetrahedral DNA nanostructure (Scheme 1C).

Construction and evaluation of Dp-PER performance

Based on the design principle delineated in Scheme 1, we selected breast cancer-associated miRNA-210 as the target to validate the constructed Dp-PER. Assembly of MNAzyme, in the presence of Mg²⁺, enables cyclic cleavage of the upstream primer, resulting in the release of two primer species (Fig. 1E, “desired” pathway). The upstream cleavage product bears a 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate terminus, while the downstream primer retains its native 3′-OH terminus. The 2′,3′-cyclic phosphodiesterase and phosphatase activity of T4 PNK catalyzes the dephosphorylation and consequent activation of the upstream product, allowing it to initiate the PER in the presence of Bst DNA polymerase (Dp-PER). The feasibility of Dp-PER was confirmed by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). As shown in Fig. 1J, lanes 1–4 and lane 7 correspond to miR-210, MNAzyme L (Mz-L), MNAzyme R (Mz-R), upstream primer, and the PER hairpin (HP). Upon addition of the miR-210, the band intensity of the upstream primer in lane 5 was conspicuously weaker than that in lane 6 without target, indicating cleavage of the upstream primer specifically recognized by MNAzyme. In the Dp-PER system (lane 8), high molecular weight species with reduced electrophoretic mobility were observed in the presence of target, consistent with those generated in the primer-triggered PER amplification controls (lanes 10 and 12). These results confirm the transduction by MNAzyme and its effective coupling with PER. Furthermore, we utilized a synthesized primer with a 3’-phosphate group, which had the same terminal state as the MNAzyme cleavage product after 2′,3′- cyclic phosphodiesterase hydrolysis. Subsequently, we verified the feasibility of this 3′-P-modified primer in combination with PER, including in the presence and absence of T4 PNK. The results showed that without T4 PNK, the 3’-P-modified primer produced almost no signal above the background. In contrast, after T4 PNK treatment, a significantly enhanced fluorescence signal was observed, which confirmed that dephosphorylation restored the extensibility of primer and successfully triggered PER (Fig. 1I). Additionally, fluorescence spectra of the Dp-PER with dephosphorylation (Fig. 1F) and the Mz-PER without dephosphorylation (Fig. 1D) were compared. The results showed that in the reaction of the upstream primer acting as a transducer without the regulation of dephosphorylation (Fig. 1C), the 2’,3’-cyclic phosphate generated by MNAzyme cleavage in the desired pathway blocks the following PER progression, whereas in the undesired pathway, PER amplification fails due to the mismatched nature of the upstream primer 3’-terminal extension sequence. Consequently, a distinct fluorescence intensity difference was observed in positive groups, indicating the dephosphorylation-regulated activation of the MNAzyme-PER system mediated by T4 PNK.

We next evaluated the performance of the Dp-PER. Among currently developed cascade reactions based on nuclease cleavage products, designs utilizing downstream primer are widely adopted. As demonstrated in Fig. 1A, in the desired pathway, target-induced MNAzyme assembly cleaves a downstream primer, and the resulting downstream product bearing a native 3′-OH terminus can be released to initiate PER for signal enhancement (MB-PER). However, in the absence of target, spontaneous conformational fluctuations of the hairpin also allow it to act as an intact primer, triggering undesired amplification. Both PAGE (Figure S1) and fluorescence analysis (Fig. 1B) confirmed that such nonspecific amplification leads to elevated background signals, which severely compromise the sensitivity for magnified detection. To assess the effectiveness of the proposed Dp-PER, a comparison was made with MB-PER, Mz-PER, DNA-primed PER (Primer-triggered), RNA-primed PER (Primer-rA-triggered), and phosphorylation-primed PER (3’-P-modified primer triggered). Importantly, the upstream and downstream primers were designed with identical base content and secondary structure, differing only in primer position (Figure S2), ensuring that performance differences arise from the dephosphorylation gate rather than from primer structure. Figure 1G, H illustrate the amplification signals from PER, mediated by DNA primer and terminal RNA-containing primer-rA respectively. Fluorescence intensity and signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) results across the six systems (Fig. 1K) revealed that Dp-PER yielded higher fluorescence than Mz-PER, confirming the efficient dephosphorylation regulation of T4 PNK. Moreover, Dp-PER exhibited a higher S/N than MB-PER, indicating that the design of upstream primer significantly reduces nonspecific background in the cascade process. Benefiting from the sequential reaction mechanism, Dp-PER attains optimal analytical performance through enzymatically regulated dynamic control without compromising the efficiency of PER, thus offering distinct advantages for logic analysis and versatile sensing applications.

Investigation of leakage mechanism and refinement of upstream primer

Under optimized buffer conditions (Figure S3), we investigated the effect of primer structure on Dp-PER. To pinpoint the cause of signal leakage in MB-PER, we first designed downstream primers with varying stem lengths (Fig. 2A). Without the target, the downstream primer and PER HP were expected to maintain stable hairpin structures (Fig. 2D). However, NUPACK simulations revealed that hairpins with stem lengths of 6 to 10 nucleotides (nt) exhibited Gibbs free energy changes (ΔG) inadequate for stable duplex formation (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, structural fluctuations of the Dow-Pri inevitably exposed the 3′ terminal trigger sequence, allowing unintended complementarity with PER-HP and thereby forming an undesired Dow-HP duplex (Fig. 2D). The ΔG of this duplex became increasingly favorable with longer complementary stems (Fig. 2E), leading to nonspecific amplification. The analysis of background signals and S/N for downstream primers of varying lengths within the MB-PER (Fig. 2B, C) demonstrated that longer stems effectively minimized signal leakage due to enhanced thermodynamic stability of Dow-Pri and fewer Dow-HP structures formed (Fig. 2F). Nevertheless, excessively long stems led to overly stable duplex structures (Fig. 2E) that remained intact even after cleavage, thereby impeding efficient hybridization with the HP in the subsequent PER circuit and consequently diminishing amplification efficiency (Fig. 2C). Overall, increasing stem length concurrently suppressed both target signals and background noise (Figure S4). This underscores a prevalent challenge in homogeneous assays: the necessity for meticulous probe design to optimize melting temperature and mitigate leakage caused by DNA breathing, which imposes rigorous thermodynamic stability requirements and demands intricate optimization. Conversely, our upstream primer design capitalizes on the benefits of sequential reaction mechanisms. The native cyclic phosphate terminus functions as an intrinsic blocking moiety, effectively preventing leakage without requiring complex probe engineering.

Grounded in the design principle of the upstream primer, the present study systematically evaluated the influence of distinctive hairpin structures on the amplification efficiency in Dp-PER (Fig. 2G). To modulate the structural stability of the hairpin, the length of both the loop and stem regions was varied. Specifically, spacer sequences of 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 nt were inserted at both ends of the loop region, which contains the MNAzyme substrate arm and cleavage site while maintaining a constant stem length. As shown in Fig. 2H and Figure S5, Dp-PER achieved the optimal S/N when the spacer length was 0 nt. Furthermore, the Up-7-nt design provided the best thermodynamic compromise for Dp-PER amplification performance among hairpins with stem lengths ranging from 5 to 10 nt and identical loop lengths (Fig. 2I and Figure S6). Next, we compared the effects of diverse probe architectures on Dp-PER amplification efficiency, including hairpin primer (Up-7nt), linear primer (Up-L-Pri), and linear primers with single or dual cleavage sites (Up-LD-Pri) (Fig. 2J). Fluorescence spectra and S/N analyses (Fig. 2K, L) revealed that the linear primer outperformed the 7 nt hairpin primer in terms of detection performance. The use of a linear primer obviates the necessity for laborious optimization of stem-loop sequences and lengths. Notably, the dual-site linear primer demonstrated a considerably elevated background signal in comparison to the single-site version at equivalent concentrations. This phenomenon may be ascribed to augmented leakage arising from heightened local substrate concentrations during MNAzyme transduction, or alternatively, to intrinsic RNA degradation at the cleavage sites. We then proceeded to assess the concentration selectivity of the Up-L-Pri across a range of target concentrations (Fig. 2M). Fluorescence results indicated that at lower target levels, a primer concentration of 100 nM (Up-L-Pri×2) maintained lower leakage while ensuring higher reaction efficiency and an optimal S/N (Fig. 2N, O, Figures S7 and S8). In conclusion, following the optimization of the primer structure and concentration (100 nM), the subsequent refinement of additional reaction parameters was undertaken. These parameters encompassed the PER primer length, T4 PNK and Bst enzyme concentrations, PNK and G4 buffer compositions, ThT concentration, PER reaction time, and HP concentration (Figures S9-S14).

Fabrication of DNA circuit for logic operations

The molecular programmability of DNA nanotechnology is derived from the Watson-Crick base-pairing rules, a foundational principle that, when implemented through Boolean logic, enables applications in molecular diagnostics [32], engineered biological circuits [33], and intelligent therapeutic systems [34]. As noted previously, the MNAzyme architecture partitions the catalytic core and incorporates sensing arms to decouple the input and output units. Its reprogrammable sequence feature affords design flexibility for implementing logic gates. In this work, we attempted to modulate the upstream molecular network governed by MNAzyme transduction to establish compatibility between Dp-PER and logical computing (Fig. 3A).

The YES and NOT gates represent fundamental logic systems that form the basis of more complex logic architectures. We selected breast cancer-associated miR-210 and miR-21 to implement YES and NOT operations, respectively, demonstrating the capability and flexibility of the Dp-PER in processing arbitrary nucleic acid inputs. The presence of a target was defined as input “1”, while its absence was defined as input “0”. In the output signal, state “1” corresponded to significant fluorescence intensity generated by G4 concatemers, whereas state “0” indicated no significant signal. Figure 3B illustrates the operational principle of the YES gate. The presence of miR-210 activates the MNAzyme-mediated cleavage of upstream primer, which subsequently initiates the Dp-PER. As shown in Fig. 3D, the output conforms to the truth table of the YES gate (Fig. 3C), where input 1 yields output 1, and input 0 yields output 0. While in the NOT gate design based on miR-21 (Fig. 3E), CmiR21 was introduced to facilitate MNAzyme assembly in the absence of the target, producing output “1” when the input is “0”. Conversely, the presence of miR-21 binds to CmiR21 through the toehold-mediated strand displacement, leading to the separation of the MNAzyme and preventing substrate cleavage, thus resulting in output “0”. Furthermore, we characterized the strand displacement efficiency under different toehold lengths and free energy changes by evaluating the cyclic cleavage of the Up-LF-Pri by MNAzyme (Fig. 3F and Figure S15), thereby optimizing the performance of the NOT logic gate (Fig. 3G, H).

To explore the capabilities of Dp-PER, we proposed the OR logic gate, selecting breast cancer-related miR-210 and miR-196 as inputs (Fig. 3I). It is noteworthy that despite being activated by different miRNAs, the MNAzyme cleaves substrates to generate identical upstream primers. Consequently, both miR-210 and miR-196 enable the activation of fluorescence signal generated by the Dp-PER system. In addition, we optimized the sensing arm design for miR-196-recognizing MNAzyme. Considering the 22 nt length of miR-196 and the symmetric or asymmetric recognition of MNAzyme, we designed Mz-L with lengths of 11 nt (L-11), 12 nt (L-12), and 10 nt (L-10). Figure 3J, K and L illustrate the sequence designs for different cleavage sites in MNAzyme and the corresponding real-time fluorescence responses. Figure 3M compares the fluorescence intensity and S/N across the three Mz-L designs. Using the optimized 12 nt Mz-L configuration, we implemented the OR logic gate integrating both miR-210 and miR-196 inputs. Figure 3N presents the truth table for the OR gate. As demonstrated in Fig. 3O, the presence of either or both miR-210 and miR-196 triggers the Dp-PER process, which is evidenced by enhanced fluorescence intensity. No prominent fluorescence peak was observed except when both miR-210 and miR-196 were absent, establishing a canonical OR logic relationship.

Using miR-210 and miR-196 as inputs, we further constructed an AND logic gate (Fig. 3P). Both CmiR210 and CmiR196, which hybridize with miR-210 and miR-196 respectively, are partially complementary to the Linker-AND strand. MiRNAs cause the release of Linker-AND through toehold-mediated strand displacement. This ultimately activates the synthesis of G4 concatemers and generates amplified output. We systematically screened toehold lengths for strand displacement by both miRNAs. Figure 3Q displays the fluorescence responses produced by five duplex designs implementing the AND logic gate under different toehold length combinations. Of these, the ds47 duplex demonstrated superior AND gate performance in both MNAzyme-catalyzed cyclic cleavage and Dp-PER fluorescence assays, outperforming other configurations. (Figures S16 and S17). The truth table and fluorescence results in Fig. 3R, S confirm that the Dp-PER is activated exclusively when both Input 1 and Input 2 are present (1,1), yielding an output of 1. All other input combinations ((0,0), (1,0), and (0,1)) resulted in an output of 0, successfully establishing an AND logical relationship between miR-210 and miR-196.

We also fabricated an INHIBIT logic gate utilizing miR-210 and miR-196 as inputs (Fig. 3T). The MNAzyme assembly triggered by miR-210 was maintained as the conventional upstream switch for Dp-PER. Meanwhile, the substrate arm of the miR-196-responsive MNAzyme was designed to be complementary to that of the miR-210-responsive MNAzyme. In this configuration, miR-196 functions as an inhibitor of the miR-210-responsive unit. Without miR-196, the split fragments of the MNAzyme fail to stably hybridize with the substrate arm of the miR-210-responsive MNAzyme. Introduction of miR-196 enhances thermodynamic stability through elongation of the substrate arm, effectively inhibiting base pairing between the miR-210 transduction unit and upstream primer. This achieves the implementation of the " miR-196 inhibits miR-210” operational mode. Using MNAzyme-catalyzed cleavage of Up-LF-Pri and Dp-PER fluorescence as readouts, we further screened for the optimal inhibitory gate design (Fig. 3U and Figures S18 and S19). Consequently, output 0 was observed for input combinations (0,1), (1,1), and (0,0), while output 1 was exclusively produced for input (1,0) (Fig. 3V). These results are directly corroborated by the fluorescence responses shown in Fig. 3W.

Collectively, across the five logic gates constructed in this study, the S/N obtained under all correct input conditions ranged from 4.2 to 9.6, compared to the corresponding no-input or incorrect-input combinations (Figure S20). These results demonstrate that the Dp-PER reliably performs logic operations and exhibits seamless compatibility within molecular networks, establishing it as a highly versatile platform for broader utility.

Development of multifunctional biosensing for miRNA and APE1 detection

Following the successful demonstration of the compatibility with logic operations in Dp-PER, we explored its versatility as a programmable biosensing platform. The flexible sequence design of the MNAzyme sensing arm permitted the engineering of a universal transduction module. Simply by replacing the upstream recognition units, the module can be programmed to detect distinct classes of biomarkers. For validation, we selected two critical tumor markers: microRNA-210 (miR-210) and APE1. miR-210 is a known key regulator in breast cancer progression, metastasis, and therapy resistance [35]. APE1 plays a pivotal role in tumorigenesis through its core functions in DNA damage repair (the BER pathway) and redox signaling, making it a significant diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target [36]. Capitalizing on the design versatility, high-performance detection of nucleic acid and enzyme was accomplished through specific reconfiguration of the recognition elements.

The detection mechanism for miR-210 follows the operational principle of the YES logic gate described previously (Fig. 4A). We subsequently optimized the sensing arm design of the miR-210-responsive MNAzyme (Fig. 4B). Mz-L was designed with lengths of 11 nt (L-11), 10 nt (L-10), and 12 nt (L-12), respectively. Figure 4C presents the real-time fluorescence kinetics corresponding to the different cleavage site designs. Comparative analysis of fluorescence intensity and S/N for the three arm lengths is shown in Fig. 4D. Using the optimized configuration (10-nt Mz-L arm) and reaction temperature (Figure S21), we measured the fluorescence response across a range of miR-210 concentrations (Fig. 4E). As expected, the fluorescence signal increased proportionally with miR-210 concentration. Figure S22 displays the corresponding fluorescence readout after the transduction of MNAzyme for 60 min. A linear relationship between fluorescence intensity and miR-210 concentration was observed from 0.05 to 1.25 nM (Fig. 4F), with a calculated limit of detection (LOD) of 0.031 nM. The assay demonstrated excellent selectivity, as evidenced by signals generated by miR-210 clearly exceeding those from potential interfering molecules at equivalent concentrations (Fig. 4G, H). Furthermore, recovery tests in 10% serum confirmed the robustness of the system for detecting miR-210 in complex biological matrices (Figure S23). To assess the performance of the biosensor in biological samples, we applied Dp-PER to detect miR-210 in extracts from two human breast cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-231 and SK-BR-3) and one normal breast epithelial cell line (MCF-10 A). Initial RT-qPCR analysis verified obvious upregulation of miR-210 in both cancer cell lines compared to MCF-10 A controls (Fig. 4I, left panel, and Figure S24). Subsequent Dp-PER detection yielded consistent results, clearly distinguishing the elevated miR-210 levels in cancer cells from those in MCF-10 A cells (Fig. 4I, right panel), validating the specificity and reliability of the system in complex sample backgrounds. Linear correlation analysis between the fluorescence intensities from the Dp-PER assay and the relative expression levels from RT-qPCR yielded a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.99, indicating excellent agreement between the two methods (Fig. 4J).

The inherent hydrolytic activity of APE1 at apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) sites endowed the Dp-PER with the potential for APE1 detection. In our design (Fig. 4K), the recognition and cleavage of a double-stranded probe by APE1 releases an AP linker, which subsequently initiates the transduction of MNAzyme, dephosphorylation control, and PER amplification to generate an enhanced signal. We first determined the optimal AP linker length for effective MNAzyme activation (Figure S25). Substrate preference was then investigated using three duplexes (ds22, ds23, ds24) featuring variations in the redundancy surrounding the AP site and different cleavage sites (Fig. 4L). The duplex ds22 with a 12-nt Mz-L sensing arm was selected for subsequent experiments, as it provided efficient APE1 cleavage while minimizing background signal (Fig. 4M and Figure S26). Under optimized PER conditions (Figures S27 and S28), the system detected APE1 concentrations as low as 0.005 U/mL (Fig. 4N). As shown in Figure S29, the fluorescence intensity for varying APE1 concentrations following 90 min of MNAzyme reaction was displayed. A linear correlation existed within the concentration range of 0.005 to 2.5 U/mL (Fig. 4O). Recovery experiments in 10% serum demonstrated reliable APE1 detection in complex media (Figure S30). The assay exhibited high specificity, as signals generated from APE1 were distinctly higher than those from potential interference, including Bst, KF, RNase H, Nb.BsrDI, λ exonuclease, and UDG (Fig. 4P, Q). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Dp-PER exhibits robust compatibility with diverse transduction modules and enables accurate and reliable biosensing of multiple analytes.

Exploration of scalability on Dp-PER with nanostructure cascading

Nanostructures constructed based on DNA nanotechnology such as cubes [37], tetrahedra [38], polyhedrons [39], DNA origami [40], and spherical nucleic acids (SNAs) [41] have attracted intense and broad interest in physiological studies, clinical diagnostics, and cancer therapy [42–44]. This interest stems from their nanoscale precision in organizing and manipulating molecules. Among these, the tetrahedral DNA nanostructure (TDN) [45] has emerged as an ideal scaffold for coupling with sensing nucleic acids and proteins signal output platforms, owing to its excellent mechanical rigidity, structural programmability, and nanoscale positioning capability [46, 47]. After demonstrating multifunctional biosensing of the Dp-PER, we were driven to explore the possibility of coupling this platform with TDN to exploit its topological control and functional augmentation, aiming to enhance the response for low-abundance targets. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 5A, the 5’ end of the screened upstream primer (Up-L-Pri) was extended with a specific sequence enabling its attachment to the apex of TDN-I via base complementary pairing (Figure S31). Consequently, in the presence of the target, the assembled MNAzyme is activated and performs cyclic cleavage of Up-L-SubE. The dephosphorylated upstream product then undergoes elongation directly on the TDN-I scaffold, yielding the TDN-PER product.

The assembly of TDN-I and TDN-A was confirmed by PAGE. As illustrated in Fig. 5B, lanes 1–4 represent the four individual single-stranded components (S1-S4) of the tetrahedral framework. Lanes 5–8 show intermediate hybrids formed from every combination of three strands, exhibiting reduced electrophoretic mobility relative to the single strands. Upon full hybridization of all four strands, a distinct band of higher molecular weight with further retarded mobility was observed in lane 9, confirming the formation of the TDN-I. Lane 10 corresponds to the Up-L-SubE substrate. Incubation of TDN-I with Up-L-SubE at room temperature for 30 min resulted in the appearance of a clearly slower-migrating band in lane 11, verifying the successful assembly of TDN-A. Structural characterization of TDN-A was further performed using atomic force microscopy (AFM). As depicted in Fig. 5C and Figure S32, AFM images revealed well-defined two-dimensional geometries and consistent three-dimensional nanocone architectures. Statistical analysis of individual particles (Fig. 5D) yielded an average lateral size of 34.59 ± 5.87 nm (Mean ± SD, n = 70). This increased size, compared to the theoretical value of naked TDN, is likely attributable to the grafted upstream primer on the vertex of TDN-I. These results collectively indicate that the assembled nanostructures exhibit good dispersity and a uniform size distribution.

With the assembly of nanostructures fully validated, we next evaluated the cascading efficiency of the Dp-PER reaction on the TDN scaffold using fluorescence assays. Three systems were constructed: the first was the free Dp-PER system without any scaffold (designated as S-PER); the second was the pre-assembled tetrahedral structure containing the MNAzyme substrate at its apex (designated as TDN-PER); the third contained nucleic acid strands of identical length, the same primer-binding sites, and the same concentration as those used for tetrahedron assembly, but without undergoing assembly into the tetrahedral structure (designated as F-PER). All three systems were then subjected to the same Dp-PER procedure, and their fluorescence responses in the presence and absence of the target were compared. As shown in Fig. 5E, the TDN-PER system containing the functional TDN generated a fluorescence signal that was 3.11-fold higher than that of the S-PER system, while the background signals remained virtually unchanged. In contrast, the F-PER system (unassembled scaffold) exhibited a slight increase in positive signal compared to S-PER, which may be attributed to nonspecific amplification arising from the disordered structures present in the complex system. Consequently, the TDN-PER system achieved a markedly improved S/N compared to both the S-PER and F-PER systems (Fig. 5F). This enhancement is consistent with the proposed mechanism of spatial confinement and local concentration effects conferred by the TDN architecture. Unlike the free system, where reactants diffuse freely, the TDN scaffold may concentrate reaction components in a confined nanoscale environment, which could increase the effective hybridization frequency and the likelihood of productive molecular collisions, thereby potentially boosting amplification efficiency.

We further assessed the detection performance of the TDN Dp-PER system for miR-210. Fluorescence enhancement was observed across a progressive concentration range of 0.01–2.5 nM (Fig. 5G), with a linear correlation between signal intensity and miR-210 concentration (Fig. 5H). The LOD was determined to be 0.0085 nM. Compared to the Dp-PER system, the TDN-integrated platform demonstrated a lower detection limit and a broader linear range. Figure S33 showed the fluorescence intensity at different concentrations of miR-210 after 60 min of MNAzyme transduction. Selectivity assays revealed minimal signal generation in the presence of non-target miRNA, confirming the high specificity of the TDN Dp-PER system (Fig. 5I, J). Overall, these results demonstrate that the integration of the Dp-PER cascade with a structurally defined TDN has yielded an amplification platform with enhanced sensitivity and precision, demonstrating its potential for biosensing and diagnostic applications.

Conclusion

As described in this study, we constructed a dephosphorylation-regulated enzymatic cascade platform (Dp-PER) that significantly reduces nonspecific signal leakage and supports multitask molecular actuation involving logical operation, multifunctional biosensing, and nanostructure cascading. The core mechanism of Dp-PER comprises the sequential reaction process and chemical moiety confinement of circuitry reactants in synergistic cascade system. Through systematic experimental validation and theoretical elucidation, we analyzed the sources of the leakage and demonstrated that the 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate terminus generated by MNAzyme serves as an intrinsic blocking group, which effectively suppresses background interference while enabling highly controllable signal output of specific molecules compared with conventional downstream primer. This mechanistic understanding enabled further enhancement of the overall performance of Dp-PER through systematic optimization based on the upstream primer stem-loop structure and fine-tuning of binding sites and concentrations, providing valuable insights into the probe design for collaborative amplification system. Leveraging the sequence-programmable MNAzyme sensing arm, we achieved a variety of Boolean logic operations including YES, NOT, OR, AND, and INHIBIT through the rational configuration of the upstream molecular network. This capability highlights the exceptional dynamic programmability of Dp-PER in processing arbitrary nucleic acid inputs. Moreover, the Dp-PER platform was effectively utilized for the highly precise detection of multiple biomolecules, involving breast cancer-associated microRNA and APE1. It demonstrates exceptional stability and reliability in the context of biological sample testing. Importantly, the validated cascading of Dp-PER with tetrahedral DNA nanostructures confirms its expandable capability to accommodate nanoscale topological regulation and enhanced functional integration. Therefore, this MNAzyme-PER converter, combining the diversity of nucleic acid conformational regulation with the versatility of enzymatic dynamic modulation, exhibits comprehensive advantages including straightforward integration, design flexibility, functional expandability, and system compatibility. Further exploration of its integration with other molecular systems is anticipated to yield novel tools and strategies for applications in molecular diagnostics and nanotechnology, demonstrating the potential of this platform as a versatile and expandable system for future research.
