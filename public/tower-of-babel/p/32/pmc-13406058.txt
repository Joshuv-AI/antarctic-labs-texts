Predicting return-to-work outcomes: A comparative analysis of logistic regression and machine learning in occupational mental health
Authors: Adriano Dias, Hélio Rubens de Carvalho Nunes, Carlos Ruiz-Frutos, Juan Gómez-Salgado, Melissa Spröesser Alonso, João Marcos Bernardes, Antonio Castaño-Seiquer, David Ribas-Pérez, Juan Ramon Lacalle-Remigio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This investigation compares the analytical performance of logistic regression (LR) and targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) methodologies for occupational health research using simulated workforce data. The study employs a simulation framework based on empirical observations from 738 Brazilian public university employees, generating a reference population of 10,000 cases with predetermined distributions of 2 critical exposure variables and some covariables. Through systematic sampling across multiple sample sizes (150–5000 observations), the research evaluates the comparative efficacy of traditional regression techniques versus TMLE framework. Key findings revealed important differences between the methods. For analyzing changes in diagnosis chapters, LR consistently produced more accurate estimates, with odds ratios (ORs) closer to the true population value (0.28) compared with TMLE (0.44–0.46). The precision of LR estimates, as measured by root mean squared error, was significantly better, especially in larger samples (0.02 vs 0.16 at n = 5000). The analysis of mental health conditions showed more complex results. While LR estimates were initially unstable in small samples, they converged to the true OR (27.35) as sample size increased. TMLE produced more stable but consistently conservative estimates across all sample sizes (ORs = 15–17). Notably, TMLE confidence intervals only included the true population value in the smallest sample (n = 150), suggesting potential limitations in its application for this type of analysis. These findings carry significant implications for occupational health research methodology. The results challenge prevailing assumptions about TMLE framework automatic superiority, demonstrating that conventional regression techniques can outperform more complex approaches in specific epidemiological contexts. The study highlights the continued relevance of LR for analyzing occupational health outcomes, particularly when investigating well-defined exposure-outcome relationships with adequate sample sizes. The research contributes to methodological literature by providing empirical evidence for analytical decision-making in public health research contexts.

1. Introduction

Just over 12% of the Brazilian workers are public servants, a proportion lower than a lot of countries in Latin America and far from the percentages of developed countries, where the average is over 23%, with many exceeding 25%, such as the Scandinavian countries.[1,2]

Absenteeism holds significant relevance for both workers and corporations. For workers, it reflects their health, well-being, and work-life balance.[3,4] On the corporate side, absenteeism directly impacts productivity levels and costs.[5] High absenteeism rates lead to decreased output, additional expenses for hiring temporary replacements or paying overtime, and disruptions in workflow.

From a health standpoint, whether because of common illnesses or occupational hazards, absences pose health risks for workers, often originating from their work environment, such as unstable relationships or changes in workplace conditions.[6,7] For instance, in 2022, American workers averaged nearly 8 days of lost work,[8] while their English counterparts lost over 5 days.[9] When comparing absences because of illness between workers in the public and private sectors in the United Kingdom, the proportion was statistically higher for public sector employees.[10]

Most of the time, absenteeism occurs because of specific events; however, repeated or long-term absences,[11] resulting from the severity or chronicity of an illness, or from broader health impacts,[12,13] can hinder return to work[14] and increase the complexity of the situation, affecting the worker’s mental health.[11,12] Among public sector workers, mental health conditions are cited as a reason for absenteeism in 15% of cases, statistically higher than the approximately 10% in the private sector.[10]

Studies on absenteeism and return to work among Brazilian public university employees showed that, for 2 investigated exposures (modification of the chapter of International Classification of Diseases [ICD-10] throughout the periods of work absence and diagnoses of illnesses included in Chapter V of ICD-10), with the outcome being return to work with adaptation, adjusted for some covariates, there are no differences in predictive power between a traditional analysis model (multiple logistic regression [LR]) and an alternative one (targeted maximum likelihood estimation [TMLE]).[15]

The frequency and impact of absences on work routines and worker health make it extremely important to quantify them, aiming to provide elements for interventions and modifications in the environments where they occur. Thus, from the perspective of applying statistical analysis techniques to support epidemiological interpretation, predicting an outcome and the ability to generalize results is almost always the main goal. Among the techniques commonly observed to achieve this objective when the outcome is binary (or binarized with Bernoulli distribution), LR are frequently used.[16]

LR assume probabilities of the occurrence of the event of interest to be supposedly equal among the individuals who compose a random sample. One of the reasons for the popularity of the LR model is the ability to interpret the estimates of its regression coefficients in terms of odds ratio (OR), a measure of association of great interest in epidemiological studies. However, its estimates can be biased depending on the number of analysis units per independent variables contained in the regression model, in addition to possible convergence issues of the estimator.[17] In addition, many researchers perceive that LR and other classes of parametric regression models are often specified incorrectly.[18]

In recent years, some new computational techniques have been developed for fitting models. TMLE[18] is one of these methods. TMLE is a semiparametric causal inference framework that combines machine learning algorithms with maximum likelihood estimation to estimate causal effects while minimizing bias because of model misspecification. Unlike conventional predictive algorithms focused exclusively on prediction accuracy, TMLE incorporates both the outcome model and the exposure mechanism (propensity score), generating doubly robust estimators with desirable asymptotic statistical properties.[18–20]

Importantly, TMLE should not be interpreted as a supervised machine learning algorithm itself. Instead, it constitutes a causal estimation framework that may incorporate supervised machine learning methods during nuisance parameter estimation. While supervised machine learning primarily aims to maximize predictive accuracy and classification performance, TMLE focuses on unbiased causal effect estimation under explicit causal assumptions. Consequently, although both approaches may employ similar computational tools, their objectives, interpretability, and inferential frameworks differ substantially.[19,21,22]

In comparison to the TMLE approach, LR exhibits inferior performance, as shown in study using simulated data,[20] that compared LR with various versions of the TMLE approach regarding the estimation of the OR in scenarios of both poorly specified and well-specified models. The authors observed that the TMLE versions provided point estimates of the OR closer to its true value when compared with the estimates obtained by LR.[20]

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown inconsistent evidence regarding the superiority of machine learning approaches over traditional regression models in clinical and epidemiological prediction studies. While some studies report improved predictive flexibility and handling of complex data structures, others demonstrate little or no substantial performance advantage over LR, particularly in observational datasets and structured clinical data.[23,24] Therefore, comparative methodological investigations remain necessary to determine under which epidemiological conditions machine learning approaches may provide meaningful analytical benefits.

It is worth noting that TMLE models are a well-established general alternative approach, although more recent, for causal effects or statistical association measures. They are used to construct an efficient, semiparametric, and doubly robust estimator, that includes a secondary “targeting,” step that optimizes the bias-variance tradeoff for the target parameter and under standard causal assumptions, their estimates can be interpreted as causal effects.[19]

Although not endorsed by a previous study,[15] considering that the literature indicates the superiority of machine learning models adjustments in some situations,[25,26] the objective of this study is, from the generation of simulated databases of different sample sizes, to compare and evaluate 2 analytical approaches[27] in a multiple scenarios.

2. Methods

2.1. Design

This investigation is a simulation study developed to compare whether the TMLE method produces estimates with less sampling error compared with the LR method. The simulation framework was based on empirical data (ED) obtained from a previously published historical cohort study involving 738 Brazilian public university workers.[15] The setting used for this comparison was a causal pathway to return-to-work, focusing on 2 exposure variables: “Changes in the ICD-10 chapter of main complaints” and “Having had one or more absences due to mental health.”

It is important to note that this study was not designed as a systematic review or evidence synthesis study; therefore, no formal database search strategy or predefined search period was applied.

2.3. Population simulated by simulation plan

With the simulation plan described above, a population was generated that simulates 10,000 workers using custom programming in the R language and with the support of R packages “simcausal” (to generate values from a binomial distribution), “FamilyRank” (to normal bimodal distribution), “stats” (to regression logistic), and “clipr” (to export the data to Microsoft Excel).

From the generated population, the next step consisted of extracting samples, to which the TMLE and LR methods were applied, with the aim of evaluating whether the estimates of the effect of the exposure variables obtained by TMLE present less sampling error, that is, whether they are closer to the true effect in the generated population than those produced by the LR method.

2.4. Samples from the simulated population

The samples taken from the population had sizes of 150, 750, 1500, and 5000. The sample size range between 150 and 5000 was defined as such because pilot tests with smaller samples of size 25, 50, and 100 did not adequately represent the levels of categorical variables, produced unstable estimates and wide confidence intervals (CIs), making it difficult to obtain unbiased estimates of minimum variance.[28,29]

Next, 100 simple random samples, without replacement, were generated for each of the sample sizes 150, 750, 1500, and 5000, using the sample function of the R language, available in the base package.

2.5. Comparison between TMLE and LR

In each sample selected, the TMLE and LR methods were applied to estimate the effect of the exposure variables “Changes in the ICD-10 chapter of main complaints” and “Having had one or more absences due to mental health” on the outcome “Return to a distinct work,” using the OR as a measure of association. Thus, in the 100 samples of size 150, 100 OR estimates were obtained by TMLE and 100 by LR, and these values were used to calculate the root mean squared error (RMSE) of each method. The process was repeated for samples of size 750, 1500, and 5000.

The association between exposure variables and the outcome “Return to a distinct work” was tested using the OR because the OR is a fundamental epidemiological measure for estimating and interpreting associations in various study designs.[30] Its wide application stems from both its methodological flexibility and computational simplicity. In predictive modeling, it facilitates the interpretation of the probabilities of occurrence of individual variables and sets of variables within LR structures,[30] allowing evidence-based decision-making, the evaluation of the effects of predictor variables, and the quantification of their impact on specific outcomes.

Importantly, the OR estimates derived from LR seem to approximate the aOR = 0.28 and greater precision. It is always worth emphasizing that this estimator accounts for associations mediated by potential confounding variables. The population-aOR is encompassed within the CIs of all LR-adjusted estimates, regardless of sample size. In contrast, in models adjusted using TMLE, the aOR is included within the 95% CI only at the smallest sample size, which is also the least precise estimate.

As hypothesized, the increase in sample sizes impacts the estimations. A narrowing of the 95% CI is observed as sample sizes increase for both OR and RMSE across both model adjustment techniques, along with greater variations in the upper limits of these intervals.

However, the point estimates of RMSE demonstrate more substantial reductions than those of OR as sample size increases, whereas the OR estimators exhibit less variability because of sample size.

For model 1, the TMLE adjustments showed higher RMSE across all samples, indicating a greater mean error compared with LR, with more pronounced differences as sample sizes increased.

So, the results of the predictive model adjustments using both methods for changes in the ICD-10 chapter and return to work in a new activity indicate that exposure serves as a protective factor during the reintegration process. Specifically, experiencing a change in the type of illness during sick leave was associated with a reduced likelihood of returning to work in a different role, estimated at approximately 70% and 55% by the LR and TMLE model, respectively. The estimates demonstrated good accuracy, particularly in larger sample sizes.

First, for model 2, it is essential to highlight the differences in the magnitude of the ORs when comparing the estimates for ED and SD, as well as their deviation from the CIs obtained for the real data, a pattern markedly distinct from that observed in model 1. Another key point is the substantial increase in the strength of association for the aOR compared with the cOR, which may indicate significant high confounding effects in the associations involving this exposure.

Although in the same direction, the OR estimates for SD suggest a much stronger increase in the strength of association compared with ED than in model 1, for both techniques, with a more pronounced effect in the LR adjustments.

The ORs obtained from LR, while substantially different from the ED (unlike the findings for model 1), exhibit the same pattern observed in that previous model. The population-aORs consistently fall within the CIs of all LR-adjusted estimates across sample sizes, and for TMLE estimates, this inclusion only occurs at the smallest sample size.

The same pattern of narrowing 95% CIs with increasing sample size remains evident in this model. However, these intervals are substantially more stable in the TMLE adjustments, both for the ORs and RMSE values. Yet, despite their increased stability, the adjusted values are significantly closer to the populational cOR in SD (which accounts for bivariate exposure-outcome scenarios). Thus, the values that most closely approximate the adjusted population OR are those generated by LR in the larger sample sizes.

For model 2, the RMSE was considerably high in the smallest sample but decreased sharply as sample size increased when using LR adjustment. With TMLE, the RMSE remained more stable, suggesting potentially more consistent model performance across sample sizes.

The results of the predictive model adjustments using both methods for having had at least one diagnosis of an ICD-10 Chapter V disease and returning to work in a new role indicate that a primary diagnosis related to mental health during sick leaves substantially increases the likelihood of returning to work in a different role, ranging from 27- to 60-fold in the LR model and from 15- to 17-fold in the TMLE model.

As with the 95% CIs, behavioral patterns can also be identified for intervals between smaller and larger percentiles when comparing different adjustments. First, all CIs of the estimators obtained via LR contained the adjusted population OR, regardless of sample size or model specification. This was not the case for TMLE adjustments, where only the smaller samples (n = 150) – and thus those with wider CIs – included the OR. For model 1, intervals showed consistent displacement to the right of the OR, while for model 2 displacement was to the left. These patterns demonstrate distinct estimator behaviors depending on association strength, while also showing that TMLE does not consistently produce weaker effect estimates compared with LR.

4. Discussion

The comparative performance of machine learning algorithms against traditional statistical models in observational versus experimental settings is a topic of ongoing debate.[31] While no definitive consensus exists on artificial intelligence’s superior performance in either paradigm,[23,32] evidence suggests that experimental studies – through stricter variable control and reduced bias – may yield higher-quality data for optimizing nontraditional model calibration.

A quick Public Medical Literature Database search shows that over the past 5 years, observational studies represented 66% of approximately 120,000 publications explicitly mentioning keywords related to experimental or observational study designs in their titles or abstracts – regardless of other methodological characteristics. Given that observational studies have long dominated scientific literature and contributed immensely to advancing health knowledge, this study compared the predictive performance of LR and TMLE models using computationally simulated data. The simulations were grounded in a real historical cohort of Brazilian university workers,[15] as TMLE has emerged in the literature as a modern, adaptable alternative for diverse architectures, offering greater robustness compared to LR models.[18–21]

The study modeled 2 exposures and several covariates in relation to a single outcome (Fig. 1), using 2 analytical approaches. Both methods yielded ORs as measures of association for comparative purposes. At its core, the study contrasted how LR and TMLE simultaneously engage with 4 interrelated dimensions: the nature of the data (whether supervised or exploratory), the analytical objectives (hypothesis-driven prediction or uncovering hidden patterns),[33] the interpretability of relationships (parametric vs nonparametric),[24,34] and the complexity of underlying structures (linear models vs intricate hierarchical architectures).[35] By confronting these dimensions within an observational study framework, where causal inference poses inherent challenges, this comparative analysis revealed critical insights into the strengths and limitations of each approach when handling real-world data constraints.

The OR serves as a fundamental epidemiological measure for estimating and interpreting associations across diverse study designs.[30] Its widespread application stems from both methodological flexibility and computational simplicity. In predictive modeling, it facilitates interpretation of occurrence chances for individual variables and variable sets within LR frameworks.[30] This enables evidence-based decision-making, assessment of predictor variable effects, and quantification of their impact on specified outcomes.

In this study, the ORs estimated through LR and TMLE revealed distinct patterns (in comparison to the populational aOR): for model 1, ORs remained consistently low and stable with increasing sample size. In contrast, model 2 exhibited higher initial OR estimates that diminished in magnitude with larger samples, though showing weaker association strength when measured by TMLE. Thus, for the exposure variable demonstrating stronger outcome association (increasing outcome chances) compared with the protective factor with weaker association, the estimators’ behaviors diverged significantly.

The instability observed in small samples may indicate potential biases or confounding effects, likely because of insufficient representation of variables absent from the proposed causal model that consequently remained unmeasured and uncontrolled.[32] The progressive stabilization with increasing sample sizes supports this interpretation, suggesting that reduced variability may have partially mitigated the influence of these unmeasured variables.

The weaker association strengths produced by TMLE adjustments may suggest superior confounding control in more stable scenarios (e.g., with larger samples), representing a more conservative epidemiological estimate.[36] However, they may simultaneously reveal that – when properly controlling these unmeasured factors – the true association is stronger, as demonstrated by LR results.

In practice, for modifications of the main complaint chapters of ICD-10 among work absences, the ORs, regardless of adjustment type, would, if these were real data, demonstrate worker protection against workplace return in a different activity. This protective association would be stronger (i.e., for SD, in samples approaching or exceeding the size of ED population) than that conferred by ED. However, as in the original study, there are no performance metrics to decisively favor either LR or TMLE approaches.[15]

For the occurrence of at least one illness included in Chapter V of ICD-10 (mental and behavioral disorders), the interpretation follows the same analytical framework, although in the opposite direction. These conditions increased the chance of return-to-work with work readaptation, with stronger associations when adjusted via LR compared with TMLE approaches, substantially exceeding estimates from the original data. Thus, in SD larger samples, the analysis may have controlled unmeasured confounders not accounted for in ED, effectively isolating the mental health issue and demonstrating its significant impact on work reintegration outcomes.[37–39]

While this explanatory framework suggests both LR and TMLE could theoretically show improved performance, neither approach can be definitively preferred without additional evidence about potential confounding sources, particularly those that cannot be estimated through theoretical assumptions or indirect measurements. All potential confounding variables included in the original measurements were specified as a priori within the theoretical framework presented in Figure 1.

Although certain findings suggest that LR may outperform TMLE methods in larger samples (i.e., from n = 750 onwards), had performance been assessed solely through this set of OR estimators, the conclusion would have remained that neither technique demonstrated superiority over the other. This rationale motivated the inclusion of an additional metric pool – RMSE values – to further validate the OR findings under simulated conditions.

RMSE is a widely used metric for evaluating regression model performance.[40] In this study, lower RMSE values indicate that the estimated effect of the exposure on the outcome more closely approximates the true population effect from which the sample was drawn, thereby reducing the potential for systematic error or bias. This implies that, in our analysis, the LR method provided more reliable estimates of the investigated exposures – particularly for sample sizes ≥750 (numerically equivalent to ED). This held true for both exposures: modifications of the main complaint chapters of ICD-10 among absences, and the occurrence of at least one illness included in Chapter V of ICD-10.

Thus, considering RMSE as a measure of estimator precision,[41] the results similarly indicate inconclusive superiority, with LR producing more stable estimates and narrower CIs for exposure 1, while TMLE appears better suited for exposure 2. This pattern mirrors the OR estimator findings, thereby confirming and validating these results.

Consequently, after implementing both techniques, the TMLE estimators failed to demonstrate distinct advantages over LR outputs. While TMLE adjustments may have altered either the statistical significance of exposure-outcome associations (a possible though theoretically unexpected outcome) or the association magnitudes (a more probable and literature-consistent result), the analysis could not conclusively determine whether either method operated more conservatively – even when applied to amplified simulated datasets like those examined in this study.

The evaluation of TMLE versus LR techniques through CI overlap analysis, which properly accounts for variability across samples, demonstrated greater methodological appropriateness than simple comparison of OR ratios between techniques, despite both being available. The considerable overlap observed between CIs indicates that, for this sample, the analytical models produced largely consistent interpretations concerning both the odds of return-to-work following ICD-10 modifications and the impact of having at least one Chapter V mental disorder. This finding does not, however, exclude the possibility that TMLE techniques might offer advantages when applied to different datasets with distinct variable dynamics.

It should be noted, however, that the use of ICD-10 administrative classifications to define disease categories, despite providing standardized diagnostic coding, may be insufficiently sensitive or heterogeneous for some conditions. Thus, potential diagnostic misclassification cannot be completely excluded and may have influenced exposure categorization.[42]

Consequently, when considering the implementation challenges posed by TMLE, including its relative unfamiliarity in the field, limited accessibility outside the R software environment with its inherent operational difficulties, and less straightforward interpretability compared with LR, the findings suggest no apparent advantage to employing the TMLE methodological approach.[23]

The interpretation of results constitutes the most critical aspect of epidemiological analysis. A key distinction lies in the requirement for TMLE methods to rely on a predefined plausible causal theoretical model. In contrast, LR allows researchers to begin with simple regression adjustments using entry and exit criteria for multivariate modeling, which may be less restrictive. This approach enables the model structure to develop organically from individual predictor results without imposing a priori causal assumptions that may not adequately reflect the complex nature of health outcomes.

Furthermore, this intermediate step involving simple models enables researchers to examine association patterns, including identifying potential collinearities among predictor variables. It also helps detect possible confounding factors in exposure-outcome relationships, even when these variables were not originally measured for this specific purpose.

With larger samples, LR estimates approach the aOR, while TMLE estimates converge to the confounder-naive cOR, making LR epidemiologically preferable as it more closely reflects true associations in causal analyses.[43]

Health outcomes rarely result from a single cause; rather, they emerge from a constellation of interacting determinants at multiple levels.[44–47] Under the premise that risk represents probability as sample size approaches infinity, where probability incorporates uncertainty and such uncertainty inherently reflects individual variability because of multicausality, epidemiological investigations of health events, when framed within risk assessment paradigms, fundamentally require the inclusion of identifiable and measurable covariates.

The findings of this investigation support the use of adjusted association measures.[48] Even for large sample sizes, the traditional analytical approach involving multiple LR adjustments remains the preferred and most reliable method for result interpretation.

Therefore, although the findings of this investigation cannot corroborate the literature on TMLE advantages over LR,[23,49] developing alternative methods within predictive analytics remains essential to improve model performance, accommodate complex data structures, and address the limitations of traditional statistical approaches, especially in observational study settings.[50] Such advancements would yield more precise association estimators, particularly in complex research scenarios. Given that TMLE approaches are not yet among the primary analytical choices and their application literature, while extant, remains limited, their potential benefits cannot be dismissed. Consequently, future work should focus on expanded applications and analyses across different architectures, with particular emphasis on comparative model studies, to continually expand analytical options.

This study aimed to advance the methodological debate about different modeling approaches in complex epidemiological scenarios, where unmeasured confounding factors may be present and data collection limitations pose substantial challenges for causal inference, particularly in studies where information was not originally gathered for research purposes, as with the source study used for this simulation database.

Although the study does not claim to provide definitive answers regarding methodological choices, it offers valuable empirical evidence to inform analytical decision-making in observational research, particularly when navigating the inherent tension between predictive accuracy and interpretability of results.[16,22,51]

The innovative approach of this work, which integrates computational simulation with real-world data, enables a rigorous comparative evaluation of methodological performance under conditions that authentically reflect contemporary epidemiological research challenges. The novelty of this study does not concern new diagnostic or therapeutic approaches. Instead, the innovative contribution lies in the comparative methodological evaluation of TMLE and LR using computational simulations derived from real-world occupational health data. To our knowledge, few occupational epidemiology studies have explored these analytical comparisons under simulated causal structures grounded in empirical absenteeism and return-to-work data.

5. Conclusions

The comparison of artificial intelligence algorithm performance in experimental and observational studies remains in its early stages. While there is no consensus on its superiority, experimental studies, by reducing bias, may provide higher-quality data for model calibration.

In this study, the strength of associations varied between LR and TMLE. Larger samples favored estimator stability, suggesting better control of confounding factors. TMLE estimates indicated weaker associations, potentially reflecting a more conservative adjustment.

RMSE values showed that, for the biggest sample sizes, LR provided more precise estimates. The overlap of CIs suggests consistent interpretations between models, with no clear evidence of TMLE superiority over LR.

Despite TMLE’s potential, its application in epidemiology faces challenges in implementation and interpretation. Conversely, LR allows for progressive adjustments and aligns more closely with traditional epidemiological practices.

Although the findings do not support TMLE’s superiority, they highlight the need for further investigation into its applications. Future studies should explore different architectures to enhance association estimation in complex scenarios.

This study contributes to the methodological debate by integrating computational simulations with real-world data, providing evidence for analytical decision-making in observational studies.
