Postoperative Hyponatremia After Pleural Symphysis
Authors: Cheryssa Hislop, David Rekhtman, Karina Bruestle, Sunil Singhal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pleurodesis is the definitive management for recurrent spontaneous pneumothorax. We report the case of a 62-year-old man with Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome who developed recurrent left-sided spontaneous pneumothorax. He initially underwent mechanical pleurodesis, followed by chemical pleurodesis after recurrence. On both occasions, his postoperative course was complicated by severe hyponatremia consistent with the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion. This case highlights both the challenge of recurrence despite pleurodesis and the importance of recognizing the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion as a rare but clinically significant postoperative complication.

Pneumothorax, a potentially life-threatening condition, occurs when air enters the pleural space, resulting in spontaneous collapse of the lung. While observation or chest tube placement may suffice as a form of conservative management, those at higher risk may require definitive management with pleurodesis.1 Birt-Hogg-Dube (BHD) syndrome is a genetic syndrome that predisposes patients to recurrent spontaneous pneumothorax due to cystic lung disease.2 As such, mechanical or chemical obliteration of the pleural space through pleurodesis can serve as an attractive option for patients at higher risk.

A 61-year-old male patient had 2 visits to the emergency department for conservative management of spontaneous pneumothorax. During these episodes, the patient had experienced anterior chest discomfort, dyspnea with exertion, and nonproductive cough. He remained afebrile, hemodynamically stable, and without clinical or radiographic evidence of tension pneumothorax (Figure 1A). His past medical history was significant for BHD syndrome. Family history revealed that his father, a Navy veteran with known asbestos exposure, died of lung cancer. Socially, the patient worked as an environmental and safety specialist in a chemical plant and had never smoked cigarettes. The patient was seen in the outpatient clinic and later taken for an elective left video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery and mechanical pleurodesis. The procedure was uncomplicated. Postoperatively, he developed severe hyponatremia (Na, 120 mEq/L) (Figure 2) requiring transfer to the intensive care unit for close monitoring. The patient’s symptoms included nausea, headache, lightheadedness, as well as numbness and tingling in his hands. The etiology of the hyponatremia was initially not well understood since the urine osmolality (258 mOsmol) and serum osmolality (251 mOsmol) did not fit a specific etiology. Adrenal insufficiency was also considered, but both early morning cortisol (6.3 μg/dL) and adrenocorticotropic hormone (8.8 pg/mL) were within normal range, minimizing concern for endocrine pathology. Given the improvement with fluid restriction, the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was thought to be the underlying cause. Sodium levels subsequently improved with fluid restriction and administration of hypertonic solution. Four years later, the patient re-presented to the emergency department with shortness of breath and subcutaneous emphysema of the chest and neck. Cross-sectional imaging demonstrated a recurrent left pneumothorax (Figure 1B) with associated moderate to large emphysematous changes bilaterally in the chest and neck. A chest tube was placed at bedside, and he was then taken to the operating room for a chemical pleurodesis with talcum powder the following day. The procedure was uncomplicated, but his postoperative course was again notable for severe hyponatremia (Figure 2). As before, this was attributed to SIADH in the context of recent thoracic surgery, and he improved with fluid restriction and salt tabs. The chest tube was kept to –20 suction for 3 days after which it was kept to water seal overnight and removed on postoperative day 4. The patient recovered and was discharged later that day. He was seen in clinic 2 weeks later at which time he was doing well.

Comment

Spontaneous pneumothorax can be a life-threatening event that occurs in patients with or without underlying lung disease. Common risk factors for recurrent spontaneous pneumothoraces include tall stature, smoking status, and presence of pulmonary blebs.1 Certain genetic conditions, such as BHD syndrome, also increase the risk of a pneumothorax and recurrence, as was seen in our patient.2

Pleurodesis is performed as the definitive treatment for pneumothorax and can utilize mechanical abrasion with gauze or surgical pads, or chemical agents including talcum powder, betadine, and tetracyclines, to induce inflammation and obliterate the potential pleural space to minimize recurrence rates. There is mixed evidence regarding which method of pleurodesis is most effective for long-term outcomes. A study comparing mechanical and chemical pleurodesis with tetracycline showed a higher recurrence rate among those with mechanical pleurodesis at 3 years (5% vs 0%, P < .001).3 A review comparing long-term outcomes concluded that recurrence rates were lower with talc pleurodesis when compared with mechanical abrasion, but some of the included studies did find comparable recurrence rates regardless of technique.4 There has been interest in performing pleurodesis with both mechanical and chemical methods, but no analysis of long-term recurrence rates is available.

Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) is a hormone produced in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary in response to hyperosmolarity or hypovolemia. SIADH is commonly associated with paraneoplastic syndromes, as seen with small cell lung cancer, but can also be caused by a variety of psychiatric medications and antibiotics. Pulmonary and pleural diseases, including pneumonia, empyema, asthma, atelectasis, and pleural tumors, have also been associated with SIADH5 In the absence of these conditions and medications as well as the temporal nature of his presentations, the patient described in the case report most likely experienced SIADH as a postoperative complication. It is possible that high degrees of inflammation—such as the pleuritis achieved with thorough pleurodesis—can result in inappropriate ADH release, resulting in hyponatremia.6 This is further corroborated by prior case reports that have linked pleuritis to hyponatremia through a proposed SIADH mechanism after chemical pleurodesis.5,7,8 Higashihara and associates5 also propose a secondary hypothesis that pleurodesis resulting in significant vagal stimulation can cause ADH release. In this case, the patient underwent both mechanical as well as chemical pleurodesis upon recurrence and had notable hyponatremia postoperatively which was likely linked to the procedure as opposed to the method, further suggesting that pleurodesis may be the underlying cause. Of note, BHD syndrome has not previously been shown to cause hyponatremia or SIADH, so his postoperative complication cannot be attributed to the genetic condition.

In conclusion, this case illustrates the clinical challenge of managing recurrent spontaneous pneumothorax and highlights an unusual complication after pleurodesis—severe hyponatremia likely due to SIADH—that occurred after both mechanical and chemical pleurodesis in the same patient. While pleurodesis remains the definitive therapy to reduce recurrence of spontaneous pneumothorax, the choice between mechanical, chemical, or combined approaches continues to be debated. Importantly, surgeons should be aware of hyponatremia as a possible postoperative complication and obtain serology in the postoperative period. Further research is necessary to clarify both the comparative efficacy of pleurodesis techniques and the pathophysiologic mechanisms linking pleural inflammation to hyponatremia.
