Exosomal miR-181d-5p derived from distal airway stem cells inhibit apoptosis of pulmonary vascular endothelial cells in COPD mice by targeting PDAP1
Authors: Qing Song, Cong Liu, Ling Lin, Aiyuan Zhou, Tao Li, Ping Zhang, Yuqin Zeng, Ping Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study was to investigate the role of exosomes derived from distal airway stem cells (DASC) in apoptosis of pulmonary vascular endothelial cells (PVEC) in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and to explore the potential mechanisms.MethodsCigarette smoke extract (CSE) plus cigarette smoke (CS)-induced COPD mice were treated with DASC and exosomes. The microRNA (miR) sequencing of exosomes was performed to explore the underlying mechanisms. We determined the expression of miR-181d-5p and PDAP1 in patients with COPD.ResultsCompared with control mice, DASC and exosomes derived from DASC could alleviate emphysema changes, decrease the mean linear intercept and alveolar destructive index, reduce apoptosis of PVEC in COPD mice. Furthermore, miR-181d-5p was significantly decreased in exosomes derived from COPD mice DASC when compared with exosomes derived from normal mice DASC. The knockdown of miR-181d-5p in exosomes derived from DASC could weaken its effects in alleviating emphysema and apoptosis of PVEC, increase the expression of PDAP1, and decrease the expression of miR-181d-5p. The overexpression of miR-181d-5p and knockdown of PDAP1 could significantly reduce apoptosis in CSE-induced PVEC. Furthermore, the overexpression of PDAP1 could reverse the anti-apoptotic effect of miR-181d-5p on CSE-induced PVEC. Finally, the expression of miR-181d-5p was decreased in patients with COPD and positively related to pulmonary function, while the expression of PDAP1 was increased and negatively related to pulmonary function.ConclusionsExosomes derived from DASC could alleviate lung injury in COPD. The mechanism may be to reduce the apoptosis of PVEC by delivering miR-181d-5p by targeting PDAP1.Graphic Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05118-x.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is the most common chronic respiratory disease characterized by airflow limitation. It has a high morbidity and mortality. It has become the third leading death disease and brings a huge burden on patients and public health [1, 2]. Currently, the treatments for COPD, including bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids, oxygen therapy, vaccines and pulmonary rehabilitation, can only alleviate the symptoms and reduce the risk of exacerbation of the patients [3], but cannot repair the damaged lung tissue structure. Therefore, there is a vital clinical significance to find an effective treatment for COPD.

Smoking is the most significant risk factor for COPD, causing cell death, lung tissue damage and accelerating the progression of the disease [4]. Apoptosis of pulmonary vascular endothelial cells (PVEC) has been confirmed to be involved in the occurrence and development of COPD [5]. Therefore, exploring the mechanisms of reducing apoptosis of PVEC may become an effective treatment measure for COPD.

Stem cells are a type of cells with therapeutic potential. They can differentiate into endothelial cells, fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, etc., and participate in the repair of tissue damage [6]. Distal airway stem cells (DASC) are a type of stem cells located in the distal airways of lung tissue, capable of expressing p63 and Krt5, and having multiple differentiation and regeneration potential, and secretory functions [7]. DASC have been proven to repair the lung injury in COPD and bleomycin induced pulmonary fibrosis [8–9]. However, the detailed mechanisms have not been completely elucidated.

Exosomes are rich in active substances including microRNA (miR) and participate in intercellular communication. In fact, exosomes have numerous advantages, such as non-infusion toxicity, non-immunogenicity, easy access, effortless preservation, and freedom from tumorigenic potential and ethical issues compared to stem cells. In addition, exosomes can inherit similar therapeutic effects from their parental cells [10]. However, the role of exosomes derived from DASC in COPD is unclear.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the role of exosomes derived from DASC in the apoptosis of PVEC in COPD and to explore the potential mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Isolation and culture of mice DASC

week female C57BL/6J mice purchased from the SJA Lab Animals corporation (Hunan, China) were selected to isolate DASC. As previously described [11], all mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, and we isolated the entire mouse lung tissue and placed it in F12 medium (C11765500BT, Gibco, USA) containing 1% Pen/Strep (C0222, Beyotime, China) and 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10100147, Gibco, USA). After separating the trachea and two main bronchi, the lung tissues were cut into small pieces and were digested in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; 8122386, Gibco, USA)/F12 containing 1 mg/mL protease (P5147, Sigma, USA), 0.005% trypsin (15400054, Gibco, USA) and 10 ng/mL DNaseI (AWR0008, Abiowell, China) for 12–14 h with gentle rocking. Then, the liquid was shocked and filtered with a 70 μm filter (352350, Corning, USA) to obtain single cell suspension. After centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the cells were plated onto mitomycin-C (M5353, Sigma, USA) inactivated 3T3 (CL-0171, Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) feeder layer in DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS, Primocin (ant-pm-05, InvivoGen, France), and growth factor cocktail including 0.1 µg/ml cholera toxin (227036, Sigma, USA), 0.4 µg/ml hydrocortisone (H0888, Sigma, USA), ITS-X ( PB180431, Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), and 25ng/ml epidermal growth factor (EGF) [7], at 37 °C with 95% air and 5% CO2. The DASC colonies were picked up by clone cylinder (C2059, Sigma, USA).

This study was performed in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Immunofluorescence staining

The identification of DASC was performed using immunofluorescence staining. Briefly, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeated with 0.2% Triton X-100 (P0096, Beyotime, China), washed with phosphate buffer solution (PBS) and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (ST023, Beyotime, China). The cells were incubated with p63 antibody (AF300657, AIFang Biological, China, 1:200) and Krt5 (66727-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA, 1:500) overnight at 4°C. After being incubated with ABflo® 488-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (AS053, ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China, 1:250) and ABflo® 555-conjugated Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (AS057, ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China, 1:250) secondary antibody for 1 h and then stained with DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2’-phenylindole) (C1002, Beyotime, China). Finally, the cells were viewed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

The DASC were inoculated into 60 mm petri dishes (NEST Biotechnology, China) until they reached 70%-80% confluence. Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Invitrogen, USA) was used to transfect DASC with the miR-181d-5p inhibition and the empty inhibition plasmid (Jikai Gene Technology Co., Ltd., China). After transfection, we collected the culture supernatant of DASC and extracted exosomes (DASC-ExoEmpty inhibition and DASC-ExomiR−181d−5p inhibition).

Extraction and identification of exosomes

The DASC were seeded into 60 mm petri dishes (NEST Biotechnology, China) until the density reached 70%-80%, the lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Invitrogen, USA) was used to transfect DASC with empty inhibition plasmid and miR-181d-5p inhibition plasmid. 48 h after transfection, we collected the culture supernatant of DASC and extracted exosomes (DASC-ExoEmpty inhibition and DASC-ExomiR−181d−5p inhibition).

The culture supernatant of DASC was centrifuged at differential centrifugation, 300 g for 10 min at 4 °C, at 2000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and at 12,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and centrifuged two times at 120,000 g for 75 min at 4 °C to obtain exosomes (Beckman Coulter, USA) and suspend in PBS [12]. The exosomes were identified by nanoparticle tracking analysis, transmission electron microscopy and surface markers including CD9 (ab92726, Abcam, USA), CD63 (ab231975, Abcam, USA), and CD81 (ab109201, Abcam, USA). Flow cytometry analysis was used to identify the surface markers of exosomes. Briefly, the exosomes (10 µg/protein) were incubated with latex beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at room temperature for 15 min, followed by gentle rotation at 4 °C for 2 h. The exosome-bead complexes were then blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin for 30 min at room temperature to prevent non-specific binding. After washing twice with PBS, the beads were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated primary antibodies against exosomal surface markers for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Finally, the samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCanto, USA), and data were processed with FlowJo software.

Animal model

6-week female C57BL/6J mice purchased from the SJA Lab Animals corporation were kept in the experimental animal center of the Second Xiangya Hospital, and divided into the following groups (8 mice/per group): Control group, COPD + PBS group, COPD+DASC group, COPD+DASC-Exo group, COPD+DASC-ExoEmpty inhibition group, and COPD+DASC-ExomiR−181d−5p inhibition group (N = 8 mice per group).

The mice were injected intraperitoneally with cigarette smoke extract (CSE) plus cigarette smoke (CS) exposure to establish COPD model as previously described [13]. Briefly, five cigarettes (Furong, Changde Cigarette Company, China) were burned and bubbled the smoke with 10 mL of PBS to obtain a concentration of CSE and given an intraperitoneal injection (0.01 mL/g body weight) on days 1, 11, and 23. The mice were exposed to total body CS conditions every day except for days 1, 11, and 23 for 4 weeks. After the mice were anesthetized (Pentobarbital sodium 50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection). The mice in COPD+DASC group were given an intratracheal instillation of DASC (2 × 106 cells/50 µL) at day 5 [9]. The mice in COPD+DASC-Exo group, COPD+DASC-ExoEmpty inhibition group, and COPD+DASC-ExomiR−181d−5p inhibition group were given an intratracheal instillation of exosomes derived from DASC (6.35 × 108 particles/50 µL) at days 13 and 26 [5, 14]. On day 28, all mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation to obtain lung tissues. The right lung was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and the left lung was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde.

miR sequencing

The exosomes derived from normal mice DASC and COPD mice DASC were conducted miR sequencing. The miR sequencing and data analysis were performed by Aksomics Inc. (Shanghai, China) as previously described [5]. Differentially expressed miRs were identified through statistical analyses (Fold change ≥ 1.0, and p < 0.05). Subsequent data-mining analyses were conducted including Gene Ontology analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analyses.

Lung function measurements

Lung function of mice was measured using a small animal tester (PLY3211 system, Buxco Electronics, USA) as previously described [15]. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized (Pentobarbital sodium 50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection), tracheostomized, and the cannula was connected to a computer-controlled small animal spirometer. The forced expiratory volume in 300 s (FEV300), forced vital capacity (FVC), peak expiratory Flow (PEF), and inspiratory time/expiratory time (TI/TE) were measured. After finishing measurements, the mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation.

Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) staining of lung tissue

After the left lung was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and then cut into 4 μm sections. Finally, the sections were stained with HE (H3136, Sigma, USA). The mean linear intercept (MLI) and the destructive index (DI) of the lung tissues were measured as described previously [16].

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining for apoptosis

The apoptosis of lung tissues was identified by double staining including TUNEL staining (TUNEL BrightGreen Apoptosis Detection Kit, A112-03, Vazyme, China) and CD31 labeling (SAF005, AIFang Biological, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions, as previously described [5]. A microscope (Olympus, Japan) was used to assess the fluorescence of sections.

The apoptosis of PVEC was identified by TUNEL staining (E-CK-A320, Elabscience Biotechnology Co.,Ltd, China) according to the instructions. Briefly, the cell crawling slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeated with 0.2% Triton X-100 (P0096, Beyotime, China) and washed with PBS. They were incubated with TUNEL staining working solution, and stained with DAPI (C1002, Beyotime, China). Finally, a microscope (Olympus, Japan) was used to assess the fluorescence of sections. The TUNEL staining was performed three times in technical and biological replicates.

Isolation and identification of PVEC

The mice PVEC were isolated from lung tissues by CD31+ magnetic beads (130-097-418, Miltenyi, Germany) as previously described [5]. The cells were grown in 5% CO2 at 37℃ with a specific pulmonary vascular endothelial cell medium (1001, Sciencell, USA). The immunofluorescence staining for von Willebrand factor (vWF) was used to identify the PVEC. The cells were incubated with a vWF antibody (SA00006-4, AIFang Biological, China, 1:200) overnight at 4 °C. They were incubated with a secondary antibody for 1 h and then stained with DAPI (C1002, Beyotime, China). Finally, the cells were viewed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Exosomes captured by PVEC

First, the exosomes were stained with PKH67 (PKH67, Sigma, USA) at room temperature and in the dark for 30 min. Then the exosomes were added to the culture medium and co-cultured with PVEC. DAPI and cytoskeleton staining were performed. Finally, the cells were observed under a microscope (Olympus, Japan).

PVEC transfection and interference

The PVEC were seeded into 6-well plates (NEST Biotechnology, China) until they reached 70%-80% confluence. Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Invitrogen, USA) was used to transfect PVEC with the miR-181d-5p mimic (to achieve miR-181d-5p overexpression) and the NC mimic (negative control) (RiboBio Corporation, China), the PDAP1 overexpression plasmid (p-PDAP1) and empty plasmid (p-Empty) (Hanbio Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China), the knockdown plasmid of PDAP1 (sh-PDAP1) and the empty plasmid (sh-Empty), the si-NC and si- miR-181d-5p (to achieve miR-181d-5p knockdown) (RiboBio Corporation, China). Twenty-four hours after transfection, PVEC were treated with 2.5% CSE (Half a cigarette was burned and bubbled the smoke with 10 ml of PBS. The obtained CSE was filtered using a 0.22 μm filter to obtain a concentration of 100% CSE and then diluted to a concentration of 1%, 2.5% and 4% for the cell experiments) for 24 h.

Western blot

The western blot was conducted as previously described [5]. The primary antibody was described as follows: PDAP1 (15081-1-AP, Proteintech, USA, 1:1000), Bcl-2 (SC-7382, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., USA, 1:500), Bax (14796, CST, USA, 1:1000), cleaved caspase-3 (#9661, CST, USA, 1:1000), GAPDH (10494-1-AP, Proteintech, USA, 1:5000), and β-actin (AC038, ABclonal, China, 1:10000). The secondary antibody was described as follows: goat anti-rabbit IgG conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (SA00001-2, Proteintech, USA, 1:10000) and goat anti-mouse IgG conjugated to HRP (SA00001-1, Proteintech, USA, 1:10000). For cell experiments, western blot experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, and each biological replicate included three technical replicates. For quantitative analysis, the gray values of technical replicates from the same biological sample were first averaged, and the obtained values from the three independent biological replicates were then used to calculate the mean and standard deviation for statistical analysis.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (BS258A, biosharp, China). The reverse transcribed was used the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (K1622, Thermo, USA). Then, RT-qPCR was performed with SYBR green master mix (11184ES08, Yeasen, China) according to instructions. The primers of mouse PDAP1: Forward TGGCACAGACAACTAAGAAGGTC, Reverse CTCTGTCTTCCCAGCCAAATGC. The stem-loop method was used to reverse transcribe miRs. The primers of hsa-miR-181d-5p and mmu- miR-181d-5p, hsa-U6 and mmu-U6, mmu-miR-144-5p, mmu-miR-219a-1-3p, and mmu-miR-483-5p were designed and constructed by RiboBio Corporation (Guangzhou, China). Each reaction was run in triplicate.

Luciferase assay

First, wild-type and mutant PDAP1 (PDAP1-WT and PDAP1-Mut, respectively) plasmids were constructed (WellBio, China). The mmu-miR-181d-5p mimic or the NC mimic, and the PDAP1-WT or PDAP1-Mut plasmid (1 µg/well in 6 well plates) were transfected into PVEC by using lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Invitrogen, USA). Twenty-four hours after transfection, firefly and renilla luciferase activities were measured by using the dual-luciferase assay kit (11402ES60, Yeasen BioTechnologies co.,Ltd., China). The normalized luciferase activity is expressed as the ratio of firefly luciferase to renilla luciferase units.

Proteomic sequencing

PVEC were transfected with PDAP1 knockdown plasmid (sh-PDAP1) and empty control plasmid (sh-Empty). Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells were treated with CSE. After another 24 h, PVEC were harvested for proteomic sequencing. Subsequently, total protein was extracted and subjected to enzymatic digestion to collect peptide fragments. The peptides from each sample were separated using the Vanquish Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). All mass spectrometry raw data were integrated via DIA-NN software, followed by database searching and DIA-based quantitative proteomic analysis.

Study participants

The lung tissues from thirteen COPD patients and fifteen control participants undergoing lung resection in the Second Xiangya Hospital’s Cardiothoracic Surgery Department (Hunan, China). The demographic data were shown in Supplement Table 1.

The peripheral blood samples were obtained from forty-three COPD patients and thirty-three control participants. We collected the plasma and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) and the demographic data were shown in Supplement Table 2. We detected the concentrations of PDAP1 in plasma by enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay. The expression of miR-181d-5p was detected in PBMC by RT-qPCR.

The diagnosis of COPD complies with GOLD 2017 report: the ratio of forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) to FVC was < 0.70 after inhaling a bronchodilator [17].

ELISA

We collected the plasma samples from COPD patients and control participants. The concentration of PDAP1 was detected by ELISA kits (ABIN6962152, antibodies-online, UK) according to the instructions.

GEO database

The GSE38974 RNA-seq data of COPD patients were obtained from the GEO database (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). This data included nine control participants and twenty-three COPD patients. We used R package to conduct differential expression genes analysis (log2 fold change ≥ 1.0 and a p value < 0.05) on the data and the volcano plot was made.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution and homogeneous variances were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared with the t-test or analysis of variance. The least significant difference t-test was used for pairwise comparisons between groups. Otherwise, the data was expressed as interquartile range and compared with non-parametric tests. Pearson analysis was used to analyze the correlation between the expression levels of miR-181d-5p, PDAP1 and pulmonary function results and the correlation coefficient r was calculated. SPSS Statistics Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., USA) was used to perform statistical analysis. GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, USA) was used to draft figures. A p-value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

The effects of DASC in COPD mice

We isolated DASC from normal mice lung tissues. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that the expression of p63 and Krt5 was positive for the cells (Fig. 1A, B). Furthermore, we administered an intratracheal instillation of DASC to mice. Compared with the control group, the decrease in body weight (Fig. 2A) and lung function (Fig. 2B) in mice of COPD group. Mice in the COPD group showed enlarged alveolar spaces, thinner alveolar septa, and destruction of alveolar walls (Fig. 2C). In addition, COPD mice lung tissue had more apoptotic PVEC, and the expression of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 were increased, while the expression of Bcl-2 was decreased in the lung tissue when compared with the control group (Supplement Fig. 1A, B). However, the above changes were reversed after treatment with DASC (Fig. 1 and Supplement Fig. 1).

The exosomes were identified by transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 3A), nanoparticle tracking analysis (Fig. 3B), and flow cytometry for surface markers (Fig. 3C). miR sequencing for exosomes

To identify the underlying mechanisms responsible for the protective effect of exosomes derived from DASC, we conducted miR sequencing between exosomes derived from normal mice DASC and COPD mice DASC. There were sixty-four different miRs. Compared with the exosomes derived from normal mice DASC, thirty-seven miRs were downregulated in exosomes derived from COPD mice DASC (Fig. 5A). The top four downregulated miRs included miR-483-5p, miR-219a-1-3p, miR-144-5p and miR-181d-5p (Fig. 5B). After verification by RT-qPCR, we found that miR-483-5p and miR-181d-5p were downregulated in exosomes derived from COPD mice DASC (Fig. 5C). In addition, we found that the expression of miR-181d-5p was downregulated in COPD mice lung tissue. The expression of miR-181d-5p was increased in the lung tissue after treatment with DASC-Exo when compared with COPD mice (Fig. 5D).

The effects of exosomal miR-181d-5p in COPD mice

To identify the effects of miR-181d-5p in exosomes, we transfected miR-181d-5p inhibition into DASC to achieve the knockdown of miR-181d-5p in exosomes. We found that transfection with miR-181d-5p inhibition could reduce the expression of miR-181d-5p in exosomes (Fig. 6A).

Furthermore, we administered an intratracheal instillation of exosomes to mice. We found that the knockdown of miR-181d-5p weakened the therapeutic effect of exosomes in COPD mice, decreasing the expression of miR-181d-5p in lung tissue (Fig. 6B). In addition, the COPD mice treated with miR-181d-5p knockdown exosome showed enlarged alveolar spaces, thinner alveolar septa, and destruction of alveolar walls (Fig. 6C), and the apoptosis of PVEC were increased (Supplement Fig. 2A, B).

Exosomes derived from DASC attenuate apoptosis of CSE-induced PVEC by delivering miR-181d-5p

We isolated and identified normal mice PVEC and identified them (Supplement Fig. 3A). Then, we treated PVEC with different concentrations of CSE (0%, 1%, 2.5% and 4%), and the apoptotic rate increased gradually (Supplement Fig. 3B). Considering that 4% CSE could induce marked cell death, 2.5% CSE was chosen for the subsequent cell experiments. Furthermore, the exosomes could be captured by PVEC (Supplement Fig. 3C). We found that the treatment of CSE-induced PVEC with exosomes derived from DASC could increase the expression of miR-181d-5p (Fig. 7A) and Bcl-2, while decrease the expression of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 (Fig. 7B), and reduce the apoptosis of CSE-induced PVEC (Fig. 7C). However, the knockdown of miR-181d-5p in exosomes derived from DASC could reverse the above changes (Fig. 7).

The knockdown of PDAP1 attenuates apoptosis of CSE-induced PVEC

We performed in-depth analysis of the GEO RNA-seq dataset (GSE38974). 592 genes were up-regulated in COPD patients compared with control participants. The top ten up-regulated genes included HNRNPA0, PHF5A, DLST, SLC43A2, HRASLS5, TNFRSF1A, PDAP1, TMEM201, CHMP4B and CACUL1. In addition, the PDAP1 mRNA was significantly increased in RNA-seq data of GSE38974 (Fig. 9A). Furthermore, we predicted the target genes of miR-181d-5p by using two online databases: miRanda and TargetScan. Overlap analysis revealed that PDAP1 was a potential target of miR-181d-5p. Luciferase reporter assay demonstrated that miR-181d-5p could directly target the 3’-untranslated region (UTR) of PDAP1 (Fig. 9B). In addition, the expression of PDAP1 increased gradually as the CSE concentration increased (Fig. 9C). Furthermore, the knockdown of PDAP1 could increase the expression of Bcl-2, while decrease the expression of Bax and cleaved caspase-3, and reduce the apoptosis of CSE-induced PVEC (Fig. 9D, E).

miR-181d-5p attenuates apoptosis of CSE-induced PVEC by targeting PDAP1

First, we found that the treatment of exosomes derived from DASC could decrease the expression of PDAP1 in COPD mice lung tissue (Fig. 10A) and CSE-induced PVEC (Fig. 10B). However, the knockdown of miR-181d-5p in exosomes could increase the expression of PDAP1 in COPD mice lung tissue and CSE-induced PVEC (Fig. 10A, B). In addition, the overexpression of miR-181d-5p could decrease the expression of PDAP1 in CSE-induced PVEC (Fig. 10C, D).

Next, we found that the overexpression of PDAP1 significantly increased the expression of Bax and cleaved caspase-3, while decreased the expression of Bcl-2 in PVEC (Fig. 10E). In addition, the overexpression of PDAP1 could significantly change the PVEC morphology and increase cell death (Supplement Fig. 4).

In addition, we transfected PVEC with the miR-181d-5p mimic and the PDAP1 overexpression plasmid. As expected, the overexpression of PDAP1 could reverse the anti-apoptotic effect of miR-181d-5p in CSE-induced PVEC (Fig. 11A, B). Finally, we transfected PVEC with the si-miR-181d-5p and the PDAP1 knockdown plasmid. The knockdown of miR-181d-5p could promote apoptosis in CSE-induced PVEC, whereas PDAP1 knockdown could alleviate this pro-apoptotic effect (Fig. 11C).

The preliminary regulatory function of PDAP1

To investigate the regulatory role of PDAP1, we performed proteomic sequencing in CSE-induced PVEC following transfection with the PDAP1 knockdown plasmid. Compared with the control group, a total of 138 differentially expressed genes were identified in CSE-induced PVEC transfected with the PDAP1 knockdown plasmid. Among these, 83 genes were downregulated, including Dctn4, Acaa1a, Sdf2l1, Pcbp2 and Zfand6, while 55 genes were upregulated, including Myh6, Rtn4, Lpl, Frem1 and Grm1 (Supplement Fig. 5).

The expression of miR-181d-5p and PDAP1 in COPD patients

To evaluate the effect of miR-181d-5p and PDAP1 in humans, we collected lung tissue samples from the control participants and patients with COPD, and the clinical characteristics of the participants are shown in Supplement Table 1. Compared with the control participants, the expression of miR-181d-5p (Fig. 12A) and Bcl-2 were significantly decreased in patients with COPD, while the expression of PDAP1, Bax and cleaved caspase-3 were significantly increased (Fig. 12B).

Furthermore, we collected the plasma and PBMC from the control participants and patients with COPD, and the demographic data were shown in Supplement Table 2. The expression of miR-181d-5p was significantly decreased in COPD patients and was positively related to the FEV1, FEV1%pred and FEV1/FVC (Fig. 12C). In addition, the concentration of PDAP1 in plasma was significantly increased in COPD patients and was negatively related to the FEV1, FEV1%pred and FEV1/FVC (Fig. 12D).

Furthermore, we found that the expression of PDAP1 was negatively related to miR-181d-5p in the peripheral blood (Fig. 12E). In addition, the expression level of miR-181d-5p and PDAP1 in the peripheral blood were positively correlated with their expression in lung tissue of the patients (Fig. 12F, G).

Discussion

Previous studies have confirmed that DASC have a protective effect in COPD and pulmonary fibrosis [8, 9]. However, the underlying mechanism has not been completely elucidated. The apoptosis of PVEC participates in the development of COPD [5]. Inhibiting the apoptosis of PVEC may become one of the effective targets for the treatment of COPD. In this study, we are the first to find that DASC can deliver miR-181d-5p by targeting PDAP1 through exosomes, thereby reducing the apoptosis of PVEC and alleviating COPD.

In this study, we used an intraperitoneal injection of CSE and CS exposure for four weeks to establish a COPD mouse model. Previous studies have confirmed that the CSE plus CS exposure could successfully construct a COPD mouse model and that the apoptosis of PVEC was increased in these mice [13, 18]. In addition, study has shown that COPD established by intraperitoneal injection of CSE plus CS exposure has a similar phenotype to what is induced by exposure to cigarette smoke for six months [19]. In addition, we used a CD31+ magnetic-activated cell sorting to isolate PVEC. PVEC were identified by vWF staining and showed high purity.

Exosomes, as a type of extracellular vesicle, have a diameter of 30–150 nm, and can participate in intercellular communication by delivering active substances including miR, DNA and other proteins. Due to the advantages including non-immunogenicity of exosomes, they are receiving increasing attention. Currently, the role of exosomes derived from stem cells in treating several diseases has been confirmed. Our previous study has confirmed that exosomes derived from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) could improve emphysema and repair damaged lung tissues [5]. Song et al. [20] found that adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes biopotentiated extracellular matrix hydrogels accelerate diabetic wound healing and skin regeneration. In this study, we found that exosomes derived from DASC could reduce the apoptosis of PVEC and repair damaged lung tissues in COPD mice. In fact, DASC are lung-resident stem cells with intrinsic tissue homology to the respiratory tract, which can specifically differentiate into airway and alveolar epithelial cells to repair impaired small airway structure in COPD. Furthermore, DASC are inherently adapted to the pulmonary microenvironment and display strong lung tropism, allowing efficient homing and retention at injured distal airways.

miRs are a type of non-coding RNA with regulatory functions, and its length is 22–25 nucleotides [21]. miRs also play an important role in lung disease. Keller et al. [22] found that low miR-150-5p and miR-320b expression predicts reduced survival of COPD patients. Ong et al. [23] found that differential effects of miR-21, miR-99 and miR-145 on lung regeneration and inflammation during recovery from influenza pneumonia. In this study, we found that the expression of miR-181d-5p was significantly decreased in COPD patients and positively related to the lung function. Furthermore, we found that knocking down the expression of miR-181d-5p in exosomes can weaken its therapeutic effect in COPD mice. It means that exosomes derived from DASC could improve COPD by delivering miR-181d-5p.

PDAP1 is a 28 kDa phosphorylated protein and was initially discovered as a mitotic regulator associated with PDGFA and PDGFB in rat neuroretinal cells. Study has shown that PDAP1 is widely expressed in tissues, with the highest expression in the brain, lungs and intestines, mainly related to cytoplasm and plasma membrane expression. As a dimer isomer, PDGF protein binds to receptor tyrosine kinase and activates cytoplasmic SH2 domain proteins to control multiple signaling pathways, regulate angiogenesis, cell growth, actin recombination, migration and differentiation [24, 25]. Previous studies have confirmed that PDAP1 is associated with different types of cancers and could promote the proliferation of tumor cells [26, 27]. We used GEO database to conduct differential genes analysis and found that PDAP1 was up-regulated in COPD patients. However, the role of PDAP1 in COPD is not clear. In this study, we found that the expression of PDAP1 was significantly increased in COPD patients and was negatively related to the lung function. In addition, we are the first to find that the expression of PDAP1 was significantly increased in CSE-induced PVEC and COPD mice lung tissue, and the overexpression of PDAP1 significantly increased the apoptosis of PVEC. The knockdown of PDAP1 could alleviate the apoptosis of CSE-induced PVEC. Furthermore, we found that miR-181d-5p could reduce the apoptosis in CSE-induced PVEC by targeting PDAP1. Liu et al. [28] found that PDAP1 was significantly upregulated in CS-induced COPD mouse lung tissues and CSE-induced bronchial epithelial cells. Chirichella et al. [25] found that regulatory factor X transcription factors control a miR-150/PDAP1 axis that restrains the proliferation of human T cells. These were similar to our results.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not conduct a head-to-head comparison between exosomes derived from DASC and exosomes derived from BMSCs. Therefore, the protective effect of exosomes derived from DASC may be a common phenomenon shared by other stem cells. Second, this study lacks side-by-side experimental data to validate the superiority of exosomes derived from DASC over synthetic miRNA mimics in stability, targeting capacity and therapeutic efficacy. Third, there are clinical limitations of exosomes derived from DASC, including difficulties in cell isolation, in vitro expansion, large-scale preparation and the corresponding bottlenecks for clinical translation. This work only provides a preliminary exploratory basis, and further rigorous experimental validation is required to support its translational potential. Fourth, although our study confirmed PDAP1 regulates Bax, Bcl-2 and cleaved caspase-3, the exact mechanism remains unclear, including whether PDAP1 acts directly on these apoptotic proteins or indirectly through upstream kinases and transcription factors, which warrants further investigation in future work. Fifth, based on previous different studies, DASC were administered on day 5 [9] and the exosomes were injected on days 13 and 26 [14]. We indeed overlooked the potential confounding effect caused by inconsistent intervention timing.

Conclusions

Exosomes derived from DASC could improve lung injury in COPD. The mechanism may be to reduce the apoptosis of PVEC by delivering miR-181d-5p by targeting PDAP1.
