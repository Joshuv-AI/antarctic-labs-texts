Taking a definitional stance in health professions education scholarship
Authors: Catherine Patocka, Rachel Ellaway
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProblemDefinitions are fundamental to the work of scholarship. Indeed, all scholarship has a definitional stance, even if that stance is not to use definitions. A definitional stance is the position scholars take regarding the use, interpretation or treatment of definitions within their work. In this paper, the authors explore definitional stances that shape inquiry in health professions education (HPE), from the formulation of research questions to the interpretation and dissemination of findings. Despite their ambient presence, definitional stances are rarely acknowledged in scholarly work, nor are they explicitly and consistently examined in peer review processes, critical appraisal, the methodological literature or in graduate education. As a result, definitional ambiguity and misalignment often goes unnoticed, and the coherence of scholarly discourse is undermined.Definitional stancesThe authors describe eight distinct types of definitional stances taken in health professions education. These range from adefinitional (avoiding any definitions) and rhetorical (adopting definitions as tools of persuasion) positions that resist fixed meanings, to realist, construct and pattern‐based stances that embrace definitional coherence while allowing for ongoing inquiry and conceptual evolution. The authors illustrate the utility of this framework through a worked example (using the construct of professionalism), showing how different stances yield different understandings and scholarly pathways.What this paper addsThere is no one ‘right’ definitional stance, but rather to promote thoughtfulness, reflexivity and transparency in how definition stances are taken and the implications thereof. The paper offers practical guidance to help scholars identify, articulate and justify their definitional stances in ways that are aligned with their epistemological commitments and research purposes. By making definitional stances more deliberate, transparent and open to discussion, HPE scholarship can make stronger knowledge claims based on a richer understanding of the kinds of knowledge that different stances afford, which has the potential to advance HPE in both principled and pragmatic ways.

INTRODUCTION

The ‘need to define terms’ is a common belief both within health profession education (HPE) and beyond, 1 although how definitions should be approached has long been a subject of debate across the sciences, including those of HPE. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 There can be significant consequences that flow from how definitions are used. For instance, scholars often discount the ideas or findings of others whose definitions (or ideas of definitions) diverge from their own. 7

Scholars may use definitions to add precision and clarity to their work, to sort and distinguish between concepts of interest, or to avoid the ‘contamination’ of ambiguity. Definitions may also be used to facilitate communication and collaboration or to lend rigour to measurements and analyses. Definitions might be used to do some or even all these things. A perceived lack of definitional clarity in others' work may lead to yet more definitions or it may result in a reluctance to define at all. 8 , 9 , 10 Each approach to using definitions has limitations that may precipitate unintended consequences, such as a narrowing of scholarly exploration, a curtailing of meaningful dialogue, difficulty synthesizing varied bodies of work and, ultimately, conceptual fragmentation and incoherence.

Rather than treating the use of definitions as a binary choice (to define or not to define), we propose that every act of inquiry is shaped by a definitional stance, whether explicitly stated or implicitly held. These stances influence how research questions are framed, how methods are selected and how findings are interpreted. Yet, despite their ubiquity and influence, definitional stances are rarely acknowledged, let alone accounted for, in peer review and critical appraisal, addressed in methodological texts or taught in graduate programs. This gap has significant implications for the coherence, transparency and cumulative authority of all scholarly work. In this paper, we explore what it means to take a definitional stance, we examine different types of definitional stances evident in HPE, and we offer guidance on negotiating and articulating such stances within specific acts of scholarly inquiry and in HPE scholarship more broadly.

WHAT IS A DEFINITIONAL STANCE?

While our defining what we mean by a definitional stance may seem somewhat paradoxical in the context of this paper, yet we nonetheless find it necessary to offer such clarification. A definitional stance is the position scholars take regarding the use, interpretation or treatment of definitions within their work. This may involve asserting a new definition, adopting an existing definition, adapting an existing definition or deliberately avoiding definitions altogether. Each of these is a stance, explicit or implicit, on how definitions are expressed, employed and potentially developed or extended within scholarly inquiry. A definitional stance need not be taken deliberately or consciously to qualify as such, it may emerge unreflectively or it may be shaped by habit, disciplinary norms, ideology, training or simply by following prevailing conventions. Whatever form it takes, a definitional stance inescapably shapes acts of scholarship, and the nature, scope and perceived credibility of the knowledge claims that it generates. For example, the authors of a systematic review on empathy in medical education adopt the stance that a specific, agreed‐upon definition of empathy is essential. This definition guides every stage of the review process, from literature search and article screening to data extraction, analysis and synthesis. A different definition would likely yield lead to entirely different conclusions or require a different methodological approach altogether.

One reasonable expectation of scholarly work is that the definitional stance taken in any given study should align with the underlying philosophy of inquiry its authors espouse. When this alignment is absent, it can lead to confusion, misinterpretation or methodological inconsistency. As an example, Greenhalgh et al (2017) examined how feedback of patient‐reported outcome measures might improve patient care, but they did so without defining what they meant by ‘feedback’, and it is unclear whether this was a deliberate omission or an oversight. 11 Similarly, the scoping review by Bing‐You et al(2017 did not clearly define what was meant by ‘feedback’. 12 While this may be appropriate within a constructivist paradigm, where the aim is to explore meaning‐making and conceptual variation, the authors did not explicitly state their paradigmatic stance. As a result, the rationale for this definitional openness remains unclear, making it difficult to interpret the scope and implications of their findings.

Although all scholarly work adopts a definitional stance, whether explicit or implicit, knowing or recognizing what these stances are becomes especially important when synthesizing bodies of work that are expected to address the same issue or concept. 13 , 14 , 15 We encountered this challenge in our exploration of feedback in medical education, where we found a proliferation of definitions and definitional stances, 16 which made it difficult to synthesize extant research studies, and it made it difficult to build a cohesive program of research without adhering to one definitional stance and rejecting the rest.

DIFFERENT KINDS OF DEFINITIONAL STANCES

Drawing on our work in feedback and in other studies and areas of HPE, we identified (through iterative discussion and model building) eight distinct definitional stances: In a naïve adefinitional stance, no definitions are provided, and there is no clear justification for the stance. It could be inattention, lack of understanding or a belief that a definition is not needed but this is speculative and irresolvable without reference to the authors. To be clear, ‘naïve’ is not a criticism, it refers to a lack of a clearly articulated purpose or clarity regarding definitions.In a naïve definitional stance, definitions are provided but without a rationale for their selection or any consideration of their implications. Again, there may be many reasons for doing this, such as adherence to established practices or aligning with prevailing local culture, but the reader can only speculate as this is not made explicit. The absence of a rationale for a definitional stance generally involves a lack of consideration of competing definitions or stances.An inductive adefinitional stance deliberately avoids any definitional acts as part of a broader methodological stance where definitions should reflect participant perspectives and not be defined a priori. The justification for the stance is made more or less explicit in the methodological description. Examples of this include constructivist grounded theory, phenomenology and discourse analyses. Justification for this stance is grounded in the methodological orientation of the study.A Socratic definitional stance deliberately questions definitions, rather than accepting them as fixed or settled. Work that takes this stance tends to be playful or subversive, using the fluid dissonance of concepts to develop and advance arguments. Again, the justification for this stance is grounded in the methodological orientation of the work, which tends to take the form of thought experiments, logical arguments and manifestos.A rhetorical definitional stance deliberately asserts a definitional position in order to persuade others, set the terms of a debate or stake a claim in setting up a study or in making knowledge claims from a study. The primary focus of a rhetorical definitional stance is on asserting ontological authority, i.e. the power to define what exists, what matters and what counts as legitimate knowledge within the context of a conceptual space.A construct definitional stance is where polysemic concepts are converted to monosemic constructs. This reflects Young's distinction between concepts and constructs (2025) where precision is necessary within the bounded context of a study (particularly one involving measurement) or activity (such as measurement‐based assessment). 17 A signature of this stance is that construct definitions are not taken as binding outside the study context, which in turn reflects Merton's concepts of the middle‐range (1968) even though the findings of the study may well be more broadly generalizable. 18 A realist definitional stance is where definitions are shaped by the causal relationships they aim to explain; they are not fixed but vary depending on context. As such, realist definitions are always middle‐range and acknowledged as such; they are neither purely abstract nor tied to a single case. 18 , 19 The difference between realist and construct stances is that a construct stance is always deductive while a realist stance is abductive, seeking the most plausible explanation for observed regularities. 20 Accordingly, within a realist framing, definitions are contingent and relational and generate explanations rather than acting as constraints on scholarly inquiry.A pattern definitional stance eschews single definitions and focuses instead on definitional spaces and variations within and across them. Epistemologically, a pattern stance focuses on similarities, differences and clustering of variant ideas around one or more common conceptual nexuses rather than on single exclusive definitions. 21 Precision is supported through situating different definitions within a common conceptual landscape.

CHALLENGES IN ADOPTING A DEFINITIONAL STANCE

There are other broader challenges that need to be considered when working with definitions. The first challenge we note flows from the fact that definitions are ontological statements, they specify what kinds of things concepts are, what they are like, and how they relate to other concepts. Of course, those other concepts might also need to be defined, which could lead to other definitions and so on.

For instance, if a scholar defines clinical reasoning as a form of cognitive processing, they are making an ontological claim about what clinical reasoning is. This framing assumes and invokes a particular view of cognition, which may itself require definition (e.g., as information processing, embodied experience or sociocultural interaction). Each of these framings carries different assumptions and leads to different lines of inquiry, analytic tools and forms of interpretation. Thus, the initial definitional stance has ripple effects, requiring further definitional decisions that shape the entire conceptual architecture of a study.

If scholars were to define every concept they use, then they (and everyone else) would be completely overwhelmed by these definitions. It is axiomatic to scholarly efficiency, therefore, that a definitional stance (of whatever kind) is focused on particular terms or concepts, while taking others as given. While not every term in a study needs a definitional stance, one or more of the core concepts typically will. This is because defining key concepts signals their importance, situates them within a particular epistemological frame and establishes the ground rules on which the study is conducted.

A second challenge is in making sure the right concepts are the focus of a definitional stance, something that can be understood in terms of two kinds of error. The first error is when scholars define terms unnecessarily (such as coincidental or peripheral concepts), thereby overcomplicating the study or distracting from its focus. The second error involves failing to define concepts that do require precision, such as constructs central to an experimental study design. Both kinds of error can occur in the same study.

A third challenge involves making sure the definitional stance taken aligns with the methodological orientation of the study. For example, adopting a construct‐based stance in a knowledge synthesis may lead to unjustified exclusion or forced definitional stretching, while using an inductive definitional stance in a quantitative psychometric study would likely be confusing and would render any findings conceptually incoherent. Furthermore, although there are times when adopting an adefinitional stance may be both necessary and appropriate, particularly in exploratory or interpretive work, adefinitional approaches can obscure foundational assumptions, erode conceptual clarity and make scholarly contributions difficult to interpret or build upon.

A fourth challenge lies in how aware scholars are of the complexities involved in taking a definitional stance and how effectively that awareness informs the design of their studies. The first part of this is reflected in the purpose of this paper – to draw attention to the issue of definitional stances, to provide a typology of stances and to guide scholars in their use of or appraisal of such stances. The second part of this is based on understanding which definitional stance(s) relate to the methodological orientation for a study. A fifth challenge emerges from how definitions are expressed. Definitions, like theories, are reductive abstractions; they only work by capturing or expressing key characteristics of a concept and omitting everything else. The challenge here has two dimensions: the first concerns what is included in or excluded from the definition itself (ontological focus), the second concerns how that definition is expressed (epistemic focus). For instance, in defining a ‘failing student’, we might take an objective approach in defining failing in terms of assessment scores and preceptor ratings, or we might take a more subjective approach in terms of how preceptors feel a student has been performing or how the student perceives their own performance. Each approach carries its own definitional implications. Objective definitions may lend themselves to standardization and transparency, but risk overlooking contextual nuances or interpersonal dynamics. Subjective definitions, on the other hand, may capture more holistic or situated understandings of failure, but may introduce variability and bias, potentially undermining the perceived fairness or reliability of the knowledge claims made.

The definitional choices we make, therefore, shape not only how we understand the concept, but also how we act upon it in educational practice and policy. This is a particular concern in HPE as we engage with a great many concepts that can be defined in many different ways (competence, assessment, curriculum, remediation, feedback, professionalism, evaluation, etc.). When the same concept can indeed be defined in very different ways, putting it into practice in educational settings can become a complex and nuanced task. It can also complicate the conduct of knowledge syntheses and other forms of integrative scholarship.

DISCUSSION

We have argued that scholars need to be aware of their definitional stances and the implications of taking particular stances. To that end, we have described a range of definitional stances, their characteristics and applications and the challenges that can arise from suboptimal definitional choices. Ultimately, choosing and sustaining a defensible definitional stance requires epistemic awareness, methodological consistency and careful reflection on both the conceptual and practical implications throughout the arc of a study.

Nothing we describe here is specific to HPE research, but neither are these issues acknowledged in our field or often in the social sciences as a whole. Although there has been some attention paid to definitional stance, it has been somewhat limited. For instance, Ennis (2016) differentiated between stipulated (arbitrary in a particular context), reported (what others' definitions are) and positional (provisionally stating a definition as part of an argument) definitional stances. 22 Others have described particular definitional stances (typically in the work of others) as part of a critical argument but have not considered definitional stance as an issue in its own right.

It is time for that to change. If we are to build knowledge that is both conceptually robust and practically meaningful, we must be more deliberate in how we define, more transparent about why we define and more reflective about what our definitions do. Definitional stance should not be treated as an afterthought—it is a foundational act of scholarship, shaping not just what we study, but how, why and to what end.
