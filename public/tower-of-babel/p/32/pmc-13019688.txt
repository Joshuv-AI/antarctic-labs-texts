The orally available SIK2/SIK3 inhibitor SK-124 increases bone mass in hypogonadal male mice
Authors: Roy B Choi, Sung-Hee Yoon, Parthena E Kotsalidis, Caroline H Houghton, Majd George, Daniel J Brooks, Yingshe Zhao, Mary L Bouxsein, Marc N Wein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAt present, there are no FDA-approved orally-available bone anabolic agents to treat osteoporosis. PTH stimulates bone formation through an intracellular signaling cascade that involves the inhibition of salt-inducible kinase (SIK) isoforms 2 and 3. Therefore, direct small molecule SIK2/SIK3 inhibitors may represent a strategy to mimic PTH actions to treat different forms of osteoporosis. We previously described the synthesis and characterization of SK-124, a pharmacologic SIK2/SIK3 inhibitor that increases trabecular bone formation in eugonadal mice. However, the efficacy of this agent in osteoporosis mouse models remains unknown. Hypogonadism is an important cause of age-related bone loss. In this study, we investigated the therapeutic potential of SK-124 in a male hypogonadal bone loss model (orchiectomy, ORX) in BALB/c mice. Radiographic and histological analyses revealed that SK-124-treated ORX mice showed reduced bone loss compared to the vehicle-treated ORX mice. Serum bone turnover markers demonstrated that SK-124 treatment increased bone turnover, suggesting that SK-124 acts in a PTH-like manner in ORX mice. Bone RNA-sequencing analysis demonstrated novel pathways associated with increased bone formation in response to SK-124 treatment. These findings indicate that SK-124 prevents bone loss in a hypogonadal bone loss model and holds potential as an orally available therapeutic for treating osteoporosis due to testosterone deficiency.Keywords: male osteoporosis, bone anabolic therapies, PTH, signal transduction, histomorphometry

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a prevalent bone disease characterized by low bone mass and increased risk of fractures. Fragility fractures cause pain, morbidity, and mortality in the aging population.1 Hypogonadism is a major cause of osteoporosis in both women and men.2,3 While antiresorptive therapies, such as denosumab and bisphosphonates, are commonly used to treat osteoporosis, their efficacy is limited, and their use is associated with rare side effects.4,5 Currently available bone anabolic agents include PTH receptor agonists (teriparatide and abaloparatide) and the anti-sclerostin antibody romosozumab.6,7 While effective, widespread use of these bone-building medications is limited due to the need for frequent injections and increased risk of cardiovascular events with romosozumab.8,9 Thus, new orally available bone anabolic agents with acceptable safety profiles represent a major unmet need for osteoporosis treatment.

The physiological role of PTH is to protect organisms from hypocalcemia.10 However, this hormone also stimulates bone formation (perhaps to protect the skeleton over time), especially when PTH levels are increased in a pulsatile manner.7 Recently, we demonstrated that PTH signaling in target cells in bone triggers a signal transduction cascade that inhibits the activity of salt-inducible kinase (SIK) isoforms 2 and 3. Salt-inducible kinases are broadly expressed 5′ AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) family kinases. SIK2 and SIK3 activity within cells is activated by kinase domain activation loop phosphorylation by liver kinase B1 (LKB1) and inhibited by protein kinase A (PKA)-dependent C-terminal phosphorylation.11 In mesenchymal-lineage bone cells, PTH signaling inhibits SIK2/SIK3 activity, thereby reducing phosphorylation levels of SIK substrates (class IIa histone deacetylases (HDACs) and CREB-regulated transcription coactivator (CRTCs)). Upon dephosphorylation, SIK substrates translocate from the cytoplasm into the nucleus to regulate gene expression. Nuclear class IIa HDACs suppress sclerostin expression, and nuclear CRTC proteins induce RANKL expression. Genetic SIK2/SIK3 deletion increases trabecular bone mass and mimics the effects of constitutive PTH receptor activation.12 Global/postnatal SIK2/SIK3 deletion increases trabecular bone mass without major systemic health consequences.13

“First generation” small molecule SIK inhibitors such as YKL-05-09914 increase bone formation in healthy and hypogonadal female mice.13,15 However, this compound caused off-target toxicities due to promiscuous targeting of many unrelated kinases. Thus, the development of “next generation” isoform-selective SIK2/SIK3 inhibitors represents a major priority. We reported bone anabolic actions of the SIK2/SIK3 inhibitor SK-124 in eugonadal mice.16 However, the effects of this compound in models of hypogonadal osteoporosis remain unknown. Here, we tested this orally-available small molecule SIK2/SIK3 inhibitor in a mouse model of male osteoporosis due to surgical orchiectomy (ORX). In this model, SK-124 treatment increases bone turnover and bone mass in a PTH-like manner.

Materials and methods

Mice

All animals were housed in the Center of Comparative Medicine at the Massachusetts General Hospital and the Massachusetts General Hospital Subcommittee on Research Animal Care approved all the experiments conducted on the animals (approval number: 2018N000023). All animal procedures were performed by guidelines issued by the IACUC in the Center for Comparative Medicine at the Massachusetts General Hospital and Harvard Medical School. Mice were housed in the Center for Comparative Medicine at the Massachusetts General Hospital (21.9 ± 0.8 °C, 45 ± 15% humidity, and 12-h light cycle 7 am-7 pm). Six-week-old sham- and ORX-operated male BALB/cJ mice were obtained from a commercial vendor (JAX #5759225). To determine the optimal bone loss time point, either sham or ORX surgical groups were sacrificed post 6 or 10 wk after the surgical date. Mice from each surgical group were randomly allocated into 3 drug treatment groups. Drug treatments started 2 wk after ORX surgery for a total of 4 wk and were sacrificed at 12 wk of age. See Figure 1A for an overview of the study.

Drug treatment

Mice in each surgical group were divided into 3 drug treatment groups (vehicle, SK-124, and hPTH (1-34)). SK-124 was dissolved in a vehicle (15% hydroxy-propyl beta-cyclodextrin dissolved in sterile water) and administered orally once daily for 4 wk. The SK-124 dose used (40 mg/kg) was selected based on previous experience with this compound.16 PTH was dissolved in buffer (10 mM citric acid, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween-80, pH 5.0) and injected by s.c. injection once daily for 4 wk.

Miro-CT

Formalin-fixed femora and fifth lumbar vertebrae (L5) were scanned, reconstructed, and analyzed on a benchtop μCT system, (μCT40; Scanco Medical) as previously described.16 Briefly, the femora and vertebrae were dissected, fixed overnight in neutral buffered formalin, and then stored in 70% EtOH until the time of scanning. The L5 vertebral body and distal femoral metaphysis and mid-diaphysis were scanned using 70 kVp peak X-ray tube potential, 114 mAs X-ray tube current, 200 ms integration time, and 10 μm isotropic voxel size. Scanning and analyses adhered to the guidelines for using micro-CT to assess bone architecture in rodents.17 Micro-CT analysis was done on a blinded basis, with all mice assigned to coded sample numbers. Samples from some mice could not be measured due to dissection artifacts. All measured samples were included in subsequent analyses. Cortical thickness of L5 vertebral bodies was analyzed as previously described.18 Briefly, a 10 slice region starting 0.5 mm from caudal growth plate was measured, and region of interest defined by including the outer layer and excluding the inner marrow cavity.

Whole bone mechanical testing

Parameters related to whole bone mechanical and apparent material level properties were measured using 3-point bending tests, as previously described.13 Briefly, femora that were collected fresh and frozen, were mechanically tested in 3-point bending using a materials testing machine (Electroforce 3230, Bose Corporation). The bending fixture had a bottom span length of 8 mm. The test was performed in displacement control, with a displacement rate of 0.03 mm/s, and force and displacement data collected at 50 Hz. All bones were positioned in the same orientation during testing with the cranial surface resting on the supports and being loaded in tension. Bending rigidity (EI, N-mm2), ultimate moment (Mult, N-mm), and apparent modulus of elasticity (Eapp, MPa), were calculated based on the force and displacement data from the tests and the mid-diaphysis bone geometry measured with μCT. Bending rigidity was calculated using the linear portion of the force-displacement curve. The minimum moment of inertia and bone radius measured by μCT were used when calculating the apparent material properties.

Bone histomorphometry analysis

Femurs were processed for histological sectioning and measurements as described previously.16 Briefly, mice were given calcein (20 mg/kg by intraperitoneal injection) on 9 and 2 d before sacrifice, respectively, to label mineralizing bone. Formalin-fixed bones were dehydrated in graded ethanol, then infiltrated and embedded in methyl methacrylate without demineralization. Undecalcified 5- and 10-μm-thick longitudinal sections were obtained using a microtome (RM2255, Leica Biosystems). 5 μm sections were stained with Goldner Trichome and at least 2 nonconsecutive sections per sample were examined for measurement of cellular parameters. 10 μm sections were left unstained for measurement of dynamic parameters, and only double labels were measured, avoiding nonspecific fluorochrome labeling. A standard dynamic bone histomorphometric analysis was done using the Osteomeasure analyzing system (Osteometrics Inc.). Measurements were performed in the area of secondary spongiosa, 200 μm below the proximal growth plate. Structural, dynamic, and cellular parameters were calculated and expressed according to the standardized nomenclature.19

Histological analysis

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded decalcified tibia sections were obtained from experimental mice. H&E staining was performed on paraffin-embedded decalcified tibia sections as previously described.16

For anti-sclerostin immunohistochemistry, antigen retrieval was performed using proteinase K (Dako) for 15 min at room temperature. Endogenous peroxidases were quenched in a 3% H2O2 solution diluted in methanol. Next, slides were blocked in TNB buffer (Perkin-Elmer), stained with anti-sclerostin biotinylated antibody (1:50, R&D Systems, BAF1589) overnight at 4 °C. Sections were washed with Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) 3 times and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-coupled secondary antibodies; signals were amplified using tyramide signal amplification, and HRP detection was performed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB, Vector) for 2 min. Slides were counterstained with 0.02% fast green staining solution. Quantification of sclerostin-positive osteocytes was performed using ImageJ imaging software in a blind test manner.

For tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining, the mouse tibia paraffin section was stained with 0.2 M acetate buffer (0.2 M sodium acetate and 0.05 M sodium tartrate, pH 5.0) and staining solution (naphthol AS-MX phosphate, and fast red TR salt, Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 30 min. Fast green counterstaining was performed for the TRAP staining.

Serum analysis

Mice were fasted 16 h prior to the collection of blood by the retromandibular vein. Serum was isolated, and analyte levels were determined using the following commercially available detection kits: P1NP (IDS Immunodiagnostic Systems, #AC-33F1), CTX from IDS (#AC-06F1), PTH (mouse PTH (1-84) ELISA (Quidel)), and active osteocalcin (Takara, #MK127). All absolute concentrations were determined based on interpolation from standard curves provided by the manufacturer. The DRI-CHEM 700 veterinary chemistry analyzer (Heska) was used for measuring serum analytes, including alkaline phosphatase (ALP), BUN, calcium, cholesterol, creatine kinase, glucose, and phosphorus.

Gene expression analysis

Snap frozen marrow-flushed cortical bone from mouse tibiae were harvested. Total RNA was extracted by tissue blender with TRIzol (Life Technologies) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and further purification was performed with PureLink RNA mini kit column. For RT-qPCR, cDNA was prepared with 500 ng RNA using the Primescript RT kit (Takara Inc.) and analyzed with PerfeCTa SYBR Green FastMix ROX (Quanta bio) in the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using specific primers designed for each targeted gene. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−∆CT method normalized by β-actin.

Bulk-RNA sequencing bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies), an automated electrophoresis platform that reports RNA Integrity Number (RIN) based on ribosomal RNA ratios and fragment distribution. Only RNA samples with RIN ≥ 6 were accepted for downstream processing. RNA concentration was determined using the Qubit Flex Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the RNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit, enabling sensitive fluorescence-based quantification in up to 8 samples simultaneously. Samples meeting both RIN ≥ 6 and ≥200 ng RNA were carried forward for library construction. cDNA libraries were generated using the NEBNext UltraExpress RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs), designed to convert 25-250 ng total RNA into high-quality sequencing libraries with minimal hands-on time. The workflow included mRNA enrichment, fragmentation, random-primed cDNA synthesis, end-repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification with indexed primers. Library size and integrity were confirmed using the Agilent TapeStation with DNA ScreenTape, verifying fragment distribution (~300 bp) and the absence of adapter dimers. Concentrations were measured using the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay and verified by qPCR using the KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Roche). Libraries were normalized to equimolar concentrations using qPCR results and pooled to ensure uniform sequencing depth. The pooled libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X system using a 25B flow cell with paired-end 150 bp reads, generating approximately 20 million reads per sample. Raw base call (BCL) files were converted to FASTQ format and demultiplexed using Illumina’s bcl2fastq v2.20 software. Read quality was assessed using FastQC, and residual adapters and low-quality bases were trimmed using Trim Galore, as implemented in the nf-core RNA-seq pipeline. MultiQC aggregated QC metrics across samples.20 Gene transcripts were quantified by Salmon based DESeq2.21 Briefly, sequencing reads were aligned by Salmon to the GRCm39 reference genome using Ensembl BALB/cJ annotation. Then, mouse reference genome was aligned with Salmon to build Salmon quasi-alignment-based transcriptome index. Each gene transcripts were quantified using Salmon pseudo-align index to read the transcriptome. Salmon outputs were aggregated transcript to gene level with tximport. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 package.22 RNA-seq data will be deposited to GEO upon study publication. Datasets S1 and S2 shows CPM values and differential expression analysis for RNA-seq results. Sequencing files from Sham/Vehicle, ORX/Vehicle, and ORX/SK-124 datasets have been deposited to Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under the accession code GSE310090.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software Inc.). All data were analyzed within each surgical group using 1-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test except body weight data. Figure S1 data were analyzed by Student’s t-test. Figure S2A body weight data were analyzed by repeated measure ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test. Fasted body weight data were analyzed by 2-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test. Statistical significance is indicated in the figures. Outliers were identified using Rout (Q = 1%). The datasets are presented in mean ± SD format with individual mice superimposed as separate data points.

Results

Establishment of orchiectomy-induced bone loss model

Previous reports indicated C57BL6/J male mice rendered hypogonadal by surgical ORX exhibit significant trabecular bone loss in both femur and lumbar vertebrae without the loss of cortical thickness.23 To overcome this limitation, we used BALB/cJ male mice, a strain where ORX-induced cortical bone loss has been reported.24 To begin studies with SK-124 in ORX male mice, we elected to study young mice, using ages similar to our previous report in eugonadal animals.16 To characterize bone loss due to ORX at 6 wk of age, we isolated femur and serum from either sham or ORX males 6- and 10-wk following surgery. In both groups, significant ORX-induced bone loss in trabecular bone volume (Tb.BV/TV(%)) and cortical thickness (Ct.th) compartments were noted (Figure S1A-C) without changes in femur length (Figure S1D). Bone remodeling markers, including P1NP and CTX-1, showed more pronounced increases in post-6 wk groups (Figure S1E and F). Therefore, we used 6 wk post-ORX for treatment studies in this model.

SK-124 treatment improves bone mass and strength in hypogonadal male mice

We previously demonstrated that SK-124 promotes bone anabolism in young eugonadal male mice.16 To assess the effects of SK-124 in hypogonadal male mice, 6-wk-old sham-operated or ORX mice were obtained and, 2 wk later, animals in each surgical group were treated with vehicle, SK-124 (40 mg/kg PO), or PTH (1-34, 80 μg/kg SC) once daily for 4 wk (Figure 1A). As expected, compared with vehicle control, both SK-124 and PTH increased trabecular bone volume fraction (PS.BV/TV: primary spongiosa analyzed due to increased responsiveness to PTH and SK-124 in growing mice) and midshaft cortical thickness (Ct.Th). Notably, these parameters, which were reduced by ORX vs sham surgery were indistinguishable from sham-operated (vehicle-treated) controls (Figure 1B and D) in ORX+PTH or ORX+SK-124 treatment groups. Similar to μCT measurements in the distal femur, cancellous and cortical bone properties of lumbar vertebrae (L5) also exhibited increasing properties with PTH and SK-124 treatment. Additionally, μCT-derived parameters of the distal femur and L5 are reported in Table S1. In summary, 4 wk of SK-124 treatment in young ORX mice increased cortical and trabecular bone mass and microarchitecture to a degree comparable to that of once-daily PTH injections. Next, to assess the relationship between bone mass and strength, we conducted biomechanical testing on femur samples. Structural properties (ultimate moment, work-to-fracture, and work-to-ultimate moment) were assessed by 3-point bending testing showed improvements by SK-124 in ORX groups (Figure 2A and Figure S3A). However, no significant changes were found in apparent material properties (Figure 2B and Figure S3B).

SK-124 stimulates bone anabolism in hypogonadal male mice

Next, we determined the effects of SK-124 on bone remodeling in ORX mice by measuring serum bone turnover markers and performing histomorphometry. Serum analysis showed that PTH and SK-124 treatment increased P1NP levels (bone formation marker): PTH increased serum P1NP in both sham-operated and ORX mice after 2 wk of treatment. SK-124 treatment increased P1NP levels after 4 wk in ORX mice, and tended (p = .08) to increase P1NP after 2 wk of treatment in ORX animals. In contrast, SK-124 had non-significant effects on serum P1NP in sham-operated mice at both time points. Serum CTX levels were not increased by PTH or SK-124 at the time points studied, although a small reduction in CTX was noted in PTH-treated mice at 4 wk (Figure 3A and B). At the tissue level, TRAP staining (Figure 3C) and static histomorphometry-derived parameters (Ob.S/BS, N.Oc/B.Pm, and ES/BS) showed increased osteoblast and osteoclast numbers and activity in both SK-124 and PTH treatment groups (Figure 3D and E). Consistent with micro-CT findings, H&E staining of proximal tibia revealed increased trabecular bone in the primary and secondary spongiosa of PTH- and SK-124-treated mice (Figure 4A). Dynamic histomorphometry-derived bone formation parameters (bone formation rate (BFR), MAR, and mineralizing surface (MS/BS)) in the secondary spongiosa region displayed reduced or no changes in ORX groups (Figure 4B and C). In contrast, mineral apposition rate (MAR) was increased in SK-124-treated ORX mice in the metaphyseal endocortical region, suggesting that SK-124 improves cortical bone mass by promoting bone formation (Figure 4C). Like PTH,15 SIK2/SIK3 inhibitors reduce sclerostin (encoded by the Sost gene) expression in osteocytes.16 Thus, we assessed Sost mRNA and sclerostin protein levels in samples from this study within the ORX treatment group. As shown in Figure 4D-F, both SK-124 and PTH treatment reduced sclerostin protein and Sost mRNA levels in ORX mice. As expected based on its PTH-like effects, Tnfsf11 (Rankl) expression was increased in both SK-124- and PTH-treated mice (Figure 4F). Taken together, these results indicate that SK-124 increases bone mass, in part via stimulating bone formation in ORX mice.

Transcriptomic effects of SK-124 treatment in ORX male mice

While Sost suppression is one mechanism used by the PTH/SIK pathway to influence bone remodeling, it is likely that other gene networks in osteocytes and other cells in bone are regulated by SIK2/SIK3 inhibition. Therefore, we sought to define the global transcriptomic changes induced by SK-124 treatment in cortical bone. We performed RNA-sequencing on marrow-depleted cortical bone RNA from n = 4 mice from the following treatment groups: sham + vehicle, ORX+vehicle, and ORX+SK-124 (Figure 5A, Datasets S1 and S2). Volcano plots comparing the effects of ORX (vs sham) and SK-124 (vs vehicle in ORX mice) indicated that each perturbation was associated with distinct gene expression changes (Figure 5B). Notably, genes that we previously reported as highly PTH-regulated25–28 (Wnt4, Tnfrsf19, Nr4a1, and Kcne4) were also induced by SK-124 treatment (Figure 5B, lower panels). Further gene ontology (GO) pathway analysis indicated that upregulated immune cell regulation pathways (B cell activation and mononuclear cell proliferation) in ORX Vehicle group (Figure 5C, upper panel) were attenuated by SK-124 (Figure 5C, lower panel), suggesting additional anti-inflammatory effects of SK-124 in ORX mice. Taken together, bioinformatic analysis on RNA sequencing further support the notion that SK-124 modulates a network of gene regulatory networks in osteocytes and other cells in the bone microenvironment.

Discussion

In this study, we describe the effects of the SIK2/SIK3 inhibitor SK-124 on hypogonadal-induced bone loss (ORX) in male BALB/cJ mice. Notably, in this strain/age, ORX-induced loss of both cancellous and cortical bone is observed. Our findings showed that SK-124 increases bone mass in both cancellous and cortical bone in a PTH-like manner, as previously reported in eugonadal mice.16 Notably, these skeletal effects of systemic SK-124 treatment occur without overt systemic toxicity (Table S2), further supporting the therapeutic development of this compound.

Age-associated osteoporosis is a major public health problem.29 Therefore, the development of new therapies to treat osteoporosis is a top priority.30,31 Increased risk of skeletal fragility accompanied by reduced bone mass, osteoblast function, and impaired bone remodeling is caused by various factors during aging.32 Among these, hypogonadism is a major risk factor for osteoporosis in young and aged individuals.2,33 Reduced sex hormone levels (primarily testosterone in men and estrogen in women) accelerate bone loss and are associated with dysregulated bone remodeling.34–36 Disrupted bone remodeling by reduced sex steroid levels leads to increased bone resorption and decreased bone formation, resulting in a net loss of bone mass and strength. Alongside the detrimental effects of impaired remodeling caused by reduced estradiol, androgens also contribute to bone metabolism, suggesting specific roles of sex hormones in skeletal health.37

The surgery-induced estrogen-deficient model (ovariectomy, OVX) model is widely used since age-associated osteoporosis commonly affects post-menopausal women.38–40 Studies in female OVX mice led to knowledge gaps regarding the effects of androgens on bone biology; however, recent work suggests that a coordinated relationship exists between estrogen and testosterone to synergistically regulate skeletal homeostasis.41,42 These findings inspired us to use the ORX model here to study the effects of SIK2/SIK3 inhibitors in male osteoporosis. The effects of SK-124 on bone loss due to estrogen deficiency and aging will be an important priority for future research as the focus of this study is on the effects of SK-124 in male mice subjected to ORX surgery.

Many current FDA-approved treatments for post-menopausal osteoporosis in women and male osteoporosis have focused on either elevating sex hormones or inhibiting osteoclast activity. Hormone replacement therapy and selective estrogen receptor modulators for women, and bisphosphonates and denosumab in both men and women have limited potency and efficacy.4,43 Other anabolic-based therapy, PTH-based drugs, must be administered by subcutaneous injection daily, and lifetime use is typically limited to 24 mo for most patients. A PTH-based oral formulation is currently under clinical trial to overcome some of these challenges.44 However, those anabolic agents may require higher doses to induce bone formation in aged individuals, which can be associated with adverse side effects and waning efficacy.45

Recently, the critical role of cortical bone in skeletal fragility has become better appreciated.46 Notably, PTH-based anabolic therapies primarily exert their anabolic actions on trabecular bone, and, in some settings, can cause potentially deleterious effects in cortical bone as manifested by increased cortical porosity.47–50 Therefore, studying cortical bone in hypogondal bone-loss models is crucial to test treatment effects. However, previous studies indicated limitations and challenges of establishing a hypogonadal bone-loss model that displays both trabecular and cortical bone loss due to genetic resistance derived from certain mouse strains or other factors.24,51,52 Our study found that BALB/cJ ORX male mice displayed reduced cortical and trabecular bone with impaired bone remodeling markers 6 wk after ORX, thus providing an acceptable model of hypogonadal bone loss in young mice.

Previously, we reported that directly inhibiting SIK isoforms 2 and 3 (SIK2 and SIK3) mimics the intracellular PTH signaling pathway and promotes bone remodeling in a PTH-like manner.15 A “first generation” small molecule (YKL-05-099) that inhibits all 3 SIK isoforms prevented bone loss in OVX female mice.13 However, this “tool” compound caused toxicities related to off-target kinase effects and potently targets SIK1, a kinase that plays a key role in modulating blood pressure and renal salt clearance.14 We demonstrated that SK-124, a “next generation” SIK2/SIK3 inhibitor, stimulates bone formation in young mice without overt toxicities.16 Similar to our previous study, here, we found that SK-124 increased bone formation and bone mass. Bone formation marker (P1NP) was significantly elevated while bone resorption marker (CTX-1) remained unchanged, though histomorphometry- and histologically-derived osteoclast parameters were increased. Previous studies in eugonadal male mice demonstrated that SK-124 treatment increases RANKL levels, osteoclast numbers in situ, and serum CTX levels.16 Here, we observe that in ORX mice this compound (and PTH) increase RANKL and osteoclast numbers/activity by histomorphometry, but do not observe expected changes in serum CTX. At present, we do not fully understand the cause of this finding, although it is possible that diurnal variation or mouse strain differences may contribute.

Bioinformatic analysis of RNA sequencing data provides further insights into how SK-124 may prevent bone loss in this hypogonadal mouse model. We verified that canonical PTH target genes (Wnt4, Tnfrsf19, Nr4a1, and Kcne4) were upregulated by SK-124 treatment. Along with increased PTH signaling pathway output, many immune cell-related signaling genes, including gene networks founds in B lymphocytes and myeloid cells, were downregulated, suggesting potential anti-inflammatory actions induced by SK-124 that are distinct from PTH.53–55 This result suggests that SK-124 may promote macrophage reprogramming actions related to roles of SIKs in immune cells which are independent of PTH and may be of benefit in treating bone loss due to inflammatory conditions.56,57 Indeed, a prostaglandin-cAMP-PKA-SIK pathway blocks pro-inflammatory cytokine production and promotes expression of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 via SIK-dependent CRTC phosphorylation.54 Further research is needed to gain a better understanding of the effects of potential immunomodulatory effects of SK-124 vs PTH analogs in osteoporosis models associated with inflammation.

We acknowledge limitations to this study. First, although we observed increased trabecular bone at study termination, dynamic histomorphometry at this time point did not detect increased bone formation in this skeletal compartment. This may be due to the relatively “late” time point during drug treatment in which dual calcein labeling was performed. In contrast, SK-124 increased endocortical MAR in both sham and ORX mice, suggesting that SK-124 differentially affects bone formation in a compartment-specific manner. Second, our whole bone mechanical property analysis showed discrepancies between the 2 properties. While structural bone properties showed improvements by SK-124 treatments in ORX groups, apparent material properties showed no changes or only modest improvements. Further investigation, likely with longer treatment durations or different doses, may be needed to understand the effects of SK-124 on bone biomechanical properties in ORX mice. Third, although no observable toxicity was noted during in vivo treatment, trends toward mildly glucose and cholesterol levels were observed in some SK-124-treated mice, although findings were not consistent across surgical groups (Table S2, elevated ALP is likely due to increased bone formation). Specifically, elevated serum glucose levels could be due to actions of SK-124, which induces CRTC/CREB-driven expression of genes involved in gluconeogenesis (PCK1 and G6PC), in hepatocytes.58 Alternatively, SK-124 treatment may lead to increased food intake which may also contribute to trends toward increased blood glucose levels. Further study is needed to better understand whether these mild abnormalities, which were not observed in previous SK-124 treatment studies,16 are reproducible and/or important for the health of the treated mice.

In summary, our study demonstrates the therapeutic potential of orally-administered SK-124 in a male osteoporosis mouse model. These findings suggest that the therapeutic potential of SIK2/SIK3 inhibitors could be expanded to hypogonadal-induced osteoporosis. Further study is warranted to determine is SIK2/SIK3 inhibitors show therapeutic potential for other osteoporosis models such as bone loss due to immobilization, glucocorticoid treatment, or kidney disease.
