Mediating Role of Building Vibration Noise in the Relationship between Housing Type and Mental Health: Evidence from a Taiwanese Online Survey
Authors: Dian-Jeng Li, Cheng-Fang Yen, Yi-Lung Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Prior research has documented associations between environmental noise and mental health; however, the impact of building vibration noise remains unclear. This study aimed to test a mediation model evaluating whether specific noise types mediate the association between housing type and mental health among adults in Taiwan.Methods:Cross-sectional data of 776 participants obtained from an online survey conducted from December 1, 2024 to May 31, 2025 were analyzed. The participants rated subjective levels of road-traffic noise, railway noise, and building vibration noise. Mental health outcomes included sleep quality, depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and suicidality. Mediation analysis with nonparametric bootstrapping (5000 resamples) was used to test mediation.Results:Residing in an apartment/condominium had a significantly indirect mediating effect on poorer mental health (estimates of the indirect effect: 0.225, P < 0.001 for sleep quality, 0.431, P < 0.001 for anxiety, 0.412, P < 0.001 for depression, and 0.062, P = 0.024 for suicide risk), and this association was mediated by higher levels of building vibration noise. However, road-traffic and railway noise did not show significant mediation effects.Conclusions:Building vibration noise appears to be a key pathway linking living in an apartment/condominium to adverse mental health outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Association between Environmental Noise and Mental Health

Noise is a constant feature of daily life and contributes to both auditory and nonauditory health effects. Noise-induced hearing loss remains common in occupational settings and is increasingly associated with environmental noise exposure, leading to physical, mental, social, and economic impacts.[12] A population-based study in Australia demonstrated significant associations between environmental noise and poorer mental health outcomes, and further showed that mental health improved when noise exposure decreased over time.[3] Another longitudinal survey reported that early-life exposure to air and noise pollution was prospectively associated with common mental health problems, including psychotic experiences, depression, and anxiety from adolescence to young adulthood.[4] Specific types of noise, such as from road traffic, can have a pronounced negative impact on mental health.[5] Emerging evidence indicates that traffic noise is associated with poorer sleep, greater anxiety and depression, and impaired cognitive development,[67] and it has also been associated with more severe depressive symptoms and increased suicide risk.[8] Mechanistically, a preclinical study found that chronic exposure (4 weeks) to moderate noise (85 dB, 4 h/day) induced anxiety-like behavior involving excitation of glutamatergic neurons in the medial geniculate body and auditory cortex.[9] Neuroinflammation and cerebral oxidative stress have likewise been implicated in noise-related mental health impairment.[10] To better elucidate these complex etiologic pathways, further research is needed to clarify the factors linking environmental noise to mental health.

Association between Housing Type, Noise, and Mental Health

The type of housing has been associated with environmental exposure relevant to mental health, with noise emerging as a key factor. Compared with detached houses or townhouses, apartments and condominiums typically involve higher residential density and greater exposure to neighbor, traffic, and community noise due to shared walls and floors and proximity to busy roads, all of which can adversely affect psychological well-being.[7] In addition, residents of high-intensity condominium developments may experience wind-induced noise, mechanical noise (e.g., pumps), and building vibration noise associated with dense vertical living.[111213]

The housing environment also plays an important role in mental health, and satisfaction with roof quality, green space, and exercising outdoor indicators has been associated with a higher level of mental well-being.[14] A previous study demonstrated higher psychological distress in occupants of apartments compared with detached houses,[15] and another study reported that residents in multifamily housing units had a higher risk of mental health problems than those living in single-family units.[16] Moreover, chronic neighbor noise in apartments has been linked to increased psychological distress, impaired adaptation, increased stress vulnerability, and even learned helplessness.[1017] Apartments are also often situated in dense urban contexts where road-traffic, railway, and aircraft noise are prevalent, whereas detached homes and townhouses typically buffer both external and internal noise through greater spatial separation. By offering more privacy and control over the acoustic environment, detached housing can reduce exposure associated with high residential intensity. Given the substantial evidence of the association between noise and mental health, further investigations are warranted to compare differences in mental health across different housing types.

Aims of the Current Study

Although the association between noise and mental health is well documented, few studies have directly compared the impacts of different types of noise, such as road-traffic, railway, and building vibration noise, on mental health, and even fewer have examined how these effects may vary by housing type. To address this gap, this study aimed to investigate associations among housing type (apartments/condominiums vs. detached houses/townhouses), subjective noise levels by source, and mental health outcomes (sleep quality, depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and suicidality) in adults in Taiwan. Where associations were present, further analysis was conducted to elucidate whether specific noise types mediated the association between housing type and mental health.

METHODS

Participants and Procedure

This cross-sectional online survey was conducted from December 1, 2024 to May 31, 2025 among adults (≥18 years) residing in Taiwan. Participants were recruited via posts on Facebook and Instagram (Meta Platforms Inc., USA), Dcard (Dcard Holdings Ltd., Taiwan), and the PTT Bulletin Board System of the university where the study was conducted (Taiwan). The inclusion criteria were participants (1) aged ≥18 years at the time of recruitment and (2) willing to participate in the study. Participants with missing data and those who were younger than 18 years were excluded. The study protocol was approved by the Central Regional Research Ethics Committee of China Medical University (IRB No. CRREC-113-004). All participants agreed to initiate the online survey by clicking the “Agree” button on the first page prior to the initiation of the study. The authors identified this as informed consent for the online study.

Questionnaires

Demographic Characteristics

Data on sex, age, educational attainment, and annual income were collected. These variables were included as covariates to adjust for potential confounding.

Level of Nighttime Noise

Following the study by Brink et al.,[18] the participants reported nighttime noise exposure arising from (1) road traffic, (2) railway, and (3) structural/building vibrations within their residence (e.g., floors, ceilings, walls). Noise disturbance was assessed using a five-point verbal scale adapted from the International Commission on Biological Effects of Noise, with responses from “not at all” to “extremely.” The prompt was: “Over the past twelve months at your home, during the nighttime when you wanted to sleep, how much did <noise source> bother, disturb, or annoy you?” A higher score indicated a higher level of noise disturbance from the specific source. This was the first use of the scale, and the estimated Cronbach’s α value was 0.65, indicating acceptable consistency.

Sleep Quality

Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), which evaluates seven domains: (1) subjective sleep quality, (2) sleep latency, (3) sleep duration, (4) habitual sleep efficiency, (5) sleep disturbances, (6) use of sleep medication, and (7) daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored from 0 to 3 for a global score of 0 to 21. A higher global PSQI score indicates a higher level of sleep disturbance. The PSQI has been shown to have strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.83) and good psychometric validity,[1920] and it has also been validated in the Taiwanese population.[21]

Depression

Depressive symptoms were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a nine-item self-report scale based on DSM-IV diagnostic criteria. Items are rated from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”) over the past 2 weeks. A higher total score indicates greater depressive symptom severity. The PHQ-9 has been shown to have high internal consistency in the US and Taiwan, with a Cronbach’s alpha α of 0.89.[222324]

Anxiety

Anxiety symptoms were measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), a seven-item self-report scale scored from 0 to 3 over the past 2 weeks. A higher total score indicates greater anxiety severity. The GAD-7 has been shown to have excellent psychometric properties with high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.92),[25] and it has also been well validated in Taiwan.[26]

Suicide

Suicide risk was assessed using the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale—Self-Report, Recent Version (C-SSRS-SR-Recent), a brief instrument capturing the presence and severity of suicidal ideation and behavior across six yes/no items (from passive death wish to active ideation with plan/intent). A higher total score reflects a greater risk of suicide. The C-SSRS-SR-Recent has been shown to have excellent internal consistency (α = 0.94–0.95), and the Chinese version has been validated.[2728]

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analyses of demographics, nighttime noise exposure (road traffic, railway, and building vibration), sleep quality, and mental health indicators (depression, anxiety, and suicide risk) were conducted. Participant characteristics were compared by housing type (detached house/townhouse vs. apartment/condominium) using Pearson’s χ2 test for categorical variables and Welch’s t test for continuous variables.

To examine associations between housing type, nighttime noise levels, and mental health outcomes, linear regression analysis was conducted adjusting for sex, age, educational attainment, and annual income. For each model, regression coefficients β, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and two-sided P values (α = 0.05) were calculated.

To examine whether continuous variables (noise exposure and mental health) were normally distributed, skewness and kurtosis were calculated. According to Kim,[29] when the sample size is >300, the threshold for normality is a skewness value ±2 and Pearson’s kurtosis value within ±4. The preliminary analysis indicated that road-traffic noise exposure and suicide risk, as measured by the C-SSRS-SR-Recent, were not normally distributed. Accordingly, robust standard errors with heteroskedasticity-consistent covariance matrices (HC3 estimators) were used in subsequent analysis.[30]

When associations among housing type, nighttime noise, and mental health outcomes were identified, mediation analysis was conducted using the mediation package in R,[31] specifying housing type as the exposure (0 = detached/townhouse; 1 = apartment/condominium), nighttime noise level as the mediator, and each mental health measure as the outcome. The conceptual model is shown in Figure 1. It was drawn by PowerPoint (Office Home edition 2021, Microsoft). Robust standard errors were used to obtain percentile-based 95% CIs and P-values. The total effect, natural direct effect, and natural indirect effect were reported. The total effect represented the combined direct and indirect influence of housing type on the outcome; the natural direct effect represented the portion not operating through nighttime noise; and the natural indirect effect represented the portion mediated through nighttime noise. Statistical significance for mediation was inferred based on the P-value of the indirect effect. To control the familywise error rate, Bonferroni correction was applied, and Bonferroni-adjusted P values were reported. Multiple family comparisons were conducted at the level of a single exposure–mediator pair; within each family, associations were tested across four outcomes (sleep quality, anxiety, depression, and suicide; k = 4). Accordingly, the unadjusted P values from the mediation analyses for the total effect, natural direct effect, and natural indirect effect were multiplied by 4 (and truncated at 1, where applicable) to yield Bonferroni-adjusted P values. All analyses were conducted in R, version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025). Indirect effect sizes were examined using κ2, which represented the proportion of the maximum possible indirect effect that could have occurred given the constraints of the study design and data. A κ2 value of 0 indicated that there was no linear indirect effect, whereas a κ2 value of 1 indicated that the indirect effect was as large as it could be under those constraints.[32]

RESULTS

Descriptive Statistics

A total of 781 participants were screened. After excluding individuals who declined to participate (n = 2) and those younger than 18 years (n = 3), 776 participants were included in the final analysis. Of these participants, 215 lived in detached houses or townhouses (132 males, 83 females) and 561 lived in apartments or condominiums (364 males, 197 females). The participants who lived in detached houses/townhouses were significantly older than those in apartments/condominiums (36.9 ± 11.3 vs. 33.3 ± 9.8 years; P < 0.001). Building vibration noise scores were higher among apartment/condominium residents than among detached house/townhouse residents (P < 0.001). The remaining noise and mental health measures did not differ significantly by housing type [Table 1].

Associations between Variables

After adjusting for demographic covariates, higher road-traffic noise was positively associated with all mental health outcomes, including poorer sleep quality (β = 0.777), greater anxiety (β = 1.609), greater depression (β = 1.317), and higher suicide risk (β = 0.164) [Table 2]. Railway noise (β range across outcomes: 0.263–1.357) and building vibration noise (β range: 0.209–1.544) were likewise positively associated with all mental health outcomes. Housing type was associated with the mediator: apartment/condominium living was associated with higher building vibration noise (β = 0.278), whereas associations with road-traffic and railway noise were not observed.

Tests for the Mediation Model

Given the observed associations among housing type, building vibration noise, and mental health, mediation analysis was conducted. Building vibration noise showed significant indirect effects mediating the association between apartment/condominium living and mental health outcomes, including sleep quality (β = 0.225; 95% CI, 0.098–0.372, P < 0.001), anxiety (β = 0.431; 95% CI, 0.168–0.723, P < 0.001), depression (β = 0.412; 95% CI, 0.172–0.694, P < 0.001), and suicide risk (β = 0.062; 95% CI, 0.018–0.115, P = 0.024). The indirect effect sizes were sleep quality (κ2 = 0.030; 95% CI, 0.011–0.051), anxiety (κ2 = 0.036; 95% CI, 0.014–0.059), depression (κ2 = 0.035; 95% CI, 0.013–0.060), and suicide risk (κ2 = 0.018; 95% CI, 0.006–0.035). In contrast, total effects and natural direct effects were not statistically significant; direct-effect estimates suggested small negative associations (β = −0.080 to −0.353), with 95% CIs including zero [Table 3]. No significant indirect effects were detected for road-traffic or railway noise.

DISCUSSION

Main Findings

The results of this study showed that building vibration noise significantly mediated the association between apartment/condominium living and adverse mental health outcomes. Specifically, higher levels of vibration noise partially explained why individuals residing in apartments or condominiums reported poorer sleep quality, along with greater anxiety, depressive symptoms, and suicide risk. However, the total and direct effects of living in apartments or condominiums on mental health outcomes were insignificant, suggesting that there may be other unexplored factors involved in the association between housing type and mental health.

Association between Housing Type and Mental Health

The results showed that the difference in mental health between participants living in apartments/condominiums and those living in detached houses/townhouses was not significant. Although prior studies have linked multifamily housing to greater noise exposure[111213] and consequently potentially negative effects on mental health, several factors may have attenuated a direct association in the present study. First, apartments/condominiums can enhance social contact through shared spaces and closer neighborhood cohesion, which may reduce loneliness and psychological distress and increase perceived support.[3334] Second, apartment/condominium settings often offer greater proximity to health care, recreational areas, cultural amenities, and public transportation, all of which may confer mental health benefits that counteract environmental stressors. Detached homes, particularly in suburban or rural areas, may have fewer nearby amenities, which may then lead to poorer mental health outcomes.[35] Taken together, these factors could help explain the nonsignificant total/direct effect despite a clear indirect effect via vibration noise.

Impact of Building Vibration Noise

Building vibration noise, defined as low-frequency, structure-borne oscillations transmitted through floors, walls, and ceilings, has been associated with poorer mental health among apartment/condominium residents. In multifamily buildings, vibrations can originate from mechanical systems (elevators, pumps, heating, ventilation, and air conditioning systems), neighboring units, and structural transmission across shared elements. Compared with typical airborne noise, low-frequency vibration is more difficult to insulate, may be felt as well as heard, and is frequently described as intrusive and persistent. Such exposure is associated with increased annoyance, sleep fragmentation, fatigue, somatic complaints, and psychological distress,[36] and may exacerbate anxiety and arousal dysregulation.[3738] The results of the present study further extend these findings to depressive symptoms and risk of suicide. An animal study explained the association between chronic noise and mental burden through hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenocortical axis dysregulation.[39] It is possible that the shared walls and vertical stacks characteristic of multifamily structures amplify vibration transmission and thereby increase the likelihood that residents perceive and are disturbed by these low-frequency events compared with those living in detached housing.

Clinical and Policy Implications

Strategies to mitigate vibration noise include improved building design (e.g., resilient structural materials, vibration isolation systems), stricter urban planning policies near railways and highways, and the inclusion of vibration standards in residential building codes. From a public health perspective, addressing vibration noise is crucial given its potential to negatively affect psychological well-being in dense urban living environments. Building vibration noise represents a distinct and underrecognized environmental stressor for residents of apartments and condominiums, contributing to annoyance, sleep disturbance, stress responses, and ultimately poorer mental health outcomes. Future research and housing policy should more explicitly address vibration exposure to ensure healthier residential environments in increasingly urbanized societies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, nighttime noise was assessed at the source level using self-reports rather than objective, in-bedroom acoustic/vibration measurements, which may have introduced exposure misclassification. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference for either the direct or indirect pathways, and therefore the mediation results should be interpreted with caution. Third, all mental health outcomes were self-reported without objective measurements (e.g., sound level meters, vibration sensors), and this may have led to reporting bias. Fourth, selection bias is possible given online recruitment, such as overrepresentation of younger, higher-educated groups, and this may limit the generalizability of the findings. Fifth, residual confounding (e.g., building age, floor level, façade insulation, neighborhood deprivation, and social capital) could not be fully addressed due to the limitations of the study protocol. Moreover, body mass index, marital status, parenthood, and medical comorbidities were not identified due to limitations of the original protocol, all of which may be associated with psychological status. Sixth, although the mediating effect was statistically significant, the proportion of the association between housing type and mental health that could be explained by building vibration noise was limited due to the effect sizes. Finally, generalizability may be limited to Taiwanese adults living in dense urban settings; exposure patterns and housing construction practices may differ in lower-density contexts with greater setbacks and detached housing.

CONCLUSION

In summary, living in an apartment/condominium was indirectly associated with worse mental health through higher building vibration noise, whereas road-traffic and railway noise did not show significant mediation effects. These findings highlight that building vibration noise is an underrecognized pathway linking high-density housing to sleep and mood disturbances and suicide risk. Future longitudinal studies with larger, more diverse cohorts and objective room-level acoustic and vibration assessments are needed to validate these results, investigate causal mechanisms, and identify modifiable building and neighborhood factors.
