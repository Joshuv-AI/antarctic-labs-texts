Motion-preserving direct osteosynthesis for a C2 complex fracture involving the vertebral body and posterior elements: a case report
Authors: Yohei Miyamoto, Ichiro Okano, Yusuke Dodo, Yuta Urabe, Yoshifumi Kudo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical treatment of C2 fractures often involves fixation across C1–2, which restricts atlantoaxial motion. Because the C1–2 segment plays a major role in cervical mobility, motion-preserving strategies may be desirable in selected cases. We report a case of a complex C2 fracture treated with navigation-assisted motion-preserving fixation.Case DescriptionA 54-year-old man sustained multiple injuries in a motor vehicle collision, including a C2 fracture involving the vertebral body and posterior elements. He presented with neck pain but no neurological deficits. Computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a fracture line extending from the left vertebral body to the right lamina with minimal displacement. CT angiography showed no vertebral artery injury. The patient remained hemodynamically stable. Considering his occupational demands and preference for early return to activity, surgical treatment was performed on hospital day 5. Under intraoperative navigation, direct osteosynthesis was achieved. A lag screw was inserted to reduce and compress the displaced laminar component. Bilateral pars screws connected by a rod were placed to augment stability without crossing adjacent motion segments or performing fusion. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was mobilized early with a hard cervical collar and discharged on postoperative day 12. At 13 months, CT confirmed solid bony union. The patient remained symptom-free with preserved cervical range of motion.ConclusionsMotion-preserving fixation using navigation-assisted direct osteosynthesis achieved stable union in a complex C2 fracture involving both anterior and posterior elements. In carefully selected patients, this strategy may serve as a viable alternative to C1–2 fusion.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In a 54-year-old man with a complex second cervical vertebra (C2) fracture extending from the vertebral body to the contralateral lamina, navigation-assisted direct osteosynthesis using a translaminar lag screw and bilateral C2 pedicle screws connected by a rod achieved solid union at 13 months, enabled mobilization from postoperative day 1, and preserved cervical range of motion without fusion across adjacent motion segments.

What is known and what is new?

• Surgical stabilization of complex C2 fractures often requires constructs crossing the atlantoaxial segment, which restrict upper cervical motion, whereas conservative treatment may require prolonged rigid immobilization.

• This report describes a motion-preserving construct tailored to an asymmetric fracture involving both the vertebral body and posterior elements: compression of the laminar component with a lag screw, supplemented by bilateral pedicle screw–rod fixation confined to C2.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• In carefully selected patients without gross instability, discoligamentous injury, or vertebral artery compromise, navigation-assisted direct osteosynthesis may be considered as an alternative to fusion-based fixation.

Introduction

Fractures of the axis (C2) represent a substantial proportion of cervical spine injuries and are increasingly encountered in aging populations. While they are more common in elderly patients after low-energy trauma, C2 fractures in younger individuals typically result from high-energy mechanisms (1). Several classification systems have been established for C2 fractures, including the Anderson and D’Alonzo classification for odontoid fractures (2) and the Levine classification for traumatic spondylolisthesis of the axis (Hangman fracture) (3). However, fractures involving the C2 vertebral body are not fully addressed within these systems and remain less well characterized (4). The optimal management of C2 body fractures is not clearly defined. Conservative treatment is generally considered for minimally displaced fractures, whereas surgical stabilization may be required in displaced or unstable cases (5). When operative treatment is indicated, fusion procedures extending across C1–C3 are commonly performed. Although these constructs provide reliable stability, fusion across the atlantoaxial segment restricts cervical mobility and may affect functional outcomes (6-8). Motion-preserving strategies have been reported for other upper cervical injuries, including direct fixation of C1 Jefferson fractures and screw osteosynthesis for selected Hangman fractures (9,10). To date, however, direct osteosynthesis for C2 body fractures has rarely been described. We present a case of a complex C2 fracture involving the vertebral body and posterior elements treated with navigation-assisted motion-preserving fixation, achieving stable union while maintaining cervical range of motion. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0055/rc).

Case presentation

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report and accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

A 54-year-old male with no significant medical history was involved in a motor vehicle accident in which the taxi he was riding in was struck by an 8-ton truck. He presented with neck pain and was transported to our hospital via ambulance on the same day. On initial examination, he had a 5 cm contusion on the right occipital region and periorbital bruising on the right side. Neurological examination revealed no deficits.

Plain cervical radiographs revealed irregularity of the anterior edge of the C2 vertebral body and anterior soft tissue swelling (Figure 1). Whole-body trauma computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a C2 fracture, multiple right-sided rib fractures, a right pulmonary contusion, and hemothorax. The C2 fracture line extended from the left side of the vertebral body to the right vertebral arch, with approximately 2 mm of displacement (Figure 2). Cervical CT angiography showed no evidence of vertebral artery occlusion. MRI of the cervical spine revealed high T2 signal intensity in the anterior aspects of the C2 and C3 vertebral bodies, as well as in the posterior soft tissues from C1 to C3. No evidence of C2/3 discoligamentous injury, including injury to the intervertebral disc or surrounding ligamentous structures, was identified. Furthermore, no spinal cord compression or intramedullary signal abnormality was detected. The patient was admitted for further evaluation and management. Despite evidence of pulmonary contusion and hemothorax, his respiratory condition improved without the need for chest tube insertion. On the fifth day of hospitalization, the patient underwent cervical spine surgery in response to his request to avoid prolonged bed rest and strict cervical immobilization, and to enable early ambulation and expedited return to work, due to his professional responsibilities.

Lateral cervical radiograph demonstrating irregularity of the anterior margin of the C2 vertebral body with associated prevertebral soft tissue swelling.

CT images demonstrating a fracture line extending from the left side of the C2 vertebral body to the right vertebral arch, with approximately 2 mm of displacement. CT, computed tomography.

A direct osteosynthesis of the C2 vertebra was carried out. Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed prone with the head secured in a Mayfield clamp. No intraoperative repositioning was required. The procedure was performed through an open posterior approach. Using navigation guidance, the displaced right lamina fracture was reduced using manual compression of fractured fragment and a cannulated cancellous screw was inserted across the fracture line using a translaminar trajectory. Bilateral pedicle screws were placed and connected with a rod to augment stability. Adequate reduction was achieved intraoperatively. Immediate postoperative radiographs confirmed satisfactory implant positioning and cervical alignment (Figure 3). No postoperative complications were observed during the early postoperative period. The patient wore a hard cervical collar as a protective adjunct, was mobilized from postoperative day 1, and was discharged on postoperative day 12. At the final follow-up, one year and one month after surgery, CT imaging confirmed complete bony union (Figure 4). The cervical range of motion was as follows: anterior flexion 42°, posterior extension 25°, right and left rotation 45° each, right lateral flexion 7°, and left lateral flexion 12°. Although right lateral flexion was mildly limited, anterior-posterior flexion and rotational movements were well preserved.

Discussion

We report a case of a complex C2 fracture involving both the vertebral body and posterior elements successfully treated with motion-preserving direct osteosynthesis. The fracture pattern was complex, involving both the vertebral body and posterior elements, with a fracture line extending from the vertebral body to the contralateral lamina rather than a typical bilateral pars fracture configuration. Although C2 fractures are relatively common among cervical spine injuries, optimal management of C2 body fractures remains less clearly defined compared with odontoid and Hangman fractures. Surgical indications are generally based on instability, progressive displacement, risk of nonunion, or neurological compromise. When surgery is required, fusion constructs extending across C1–C3 are frequently selected to achieve stability. However, such procedures inevitably restrict upper cervical motion. Moreover, conservative management typically requires long-term rigid cervical immobilization. Although direct evidence is lacking for C2 vertebral body fractures, studies of anterior odontoid screw fixation have demonstrated that direct fracture stabilization can facilitate earlier mobilization without the need for prolonged external fixation (11). Given the similar concept of motion-preserving fracture fixation, comparable advantages may have contributed to the favorable clinical course observed in the present case.

Direct osteosynthesis represents an alternative strategy aimed at restoring anatomical alignment while preserving intersegmental mobility. Favorable outcomes have been reported for C1 Jefferson fractures treated with C1 ring fixation, which stabilizes the atlas without sacrificing occipito-atlantal or atlantoaxial motion (9,12). Similarly, pedicle-based direct osteosynthesis has been described for selected Hangman fractures, achieving reliable union with preserved cervical range of motion (13,14). Reports addressing direct fixation of C2 body fractures remain scarce. Nagashima et al. (10) reported a case in which direct screw fixation was performed in the subacute phase due to progressive fracture displacement. They used two cannulated screws to stabilize the fractured fragments, as the bilateral fracture lines were confined to the vertebral body.

Motion-preserving osteosynthesis has also been reported for selected traumatic spondylolistheses of the axis. Zeden et al. described favorable outcomes using navigation-guided lag screw fixation for traumatic spondylolisthesis of the axis (15). Some fracture configurations in their series shared similarities with the present case. In our patient, a lag screw was inserted into the laminar component to achieve compression and reduction. In addition, because the fracture lines were asymmetrically distributed between the anterior and posterior elements, bilateral pedicle screws connected with a rod were added to reinforce stability. This construct allowed sufficient fixation without crossing adjacent motion segments. Solid union was achieved, and cervical mobility was preserved at follow-up.

Upper cervical instrumentation carries inherent risks due to the proximity of the spinal canal and vertebral artery. Accurate screw placement is therefore critical. Navigation-assisted instrumentation has been shown to improve pedicle screw accuracy in upper cervical surgery, including in traumatic C2 injuries (15-17). Zeden et al. utilized navigation-guided lag screw fixation for traumatic spondylolisthesis of the axis and reported favorable clinical and radiological outcomes (15). Similarly, in the present case, we utilized a navigation system during surgery since the margin for error seemed small for effective screw insertion. For cervical spine, the use of navigation for inserting pedicle screws, not just C2, is recommended to increase the accuracy of insertion (16). There have been reports that the use of navigation has enabled accurate screw insertion in Hangman’s fractures. Singh et al. reported that they used intraoperative O-arm-based navigation in 15 patients with unstable hangman fractures and found that misplacement of C2 pedicle screws occurred in only 6.7% of cases grade 2, resulting in accurate screw placement and good reduction (17).

Taken together, this case suggests that in carefully selected patients without gross instability or vascular compromise, motion-preserving fixation for complex C2 fractures may provide stable union while maintaining cervical function.

Conclusions

Motion-preserving direct osteosynthesis for a complex C2 fracture achieved stable union, enabled early mobilization, and preserved cervical range of motion. In selected cases, this approach may serve as a viable alternative to fusion-based constructs. The use of intraoperative navigation can enhance the safety and accuracy of instrumentation in this anatomically demanding region.
