Phytochemical characterization and biological activities of wild Ceratonia siliqua L. leaves: antioxidant, antibacterial, and cytotoxic effects
Authors: Salah Laaraj, Kaoutar Elfazazi, Chaimaa Jabbari, Ayoub Farihi, AbdelAziz Ed-Dra, Abdellatif Boutagayout, Atman Adiba, Mohammed Al-zharani, Fahd A. Nasr, Ashraf Ahmed Qurtam, Younes Noutfia, Souad Salmaoui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Carob (Ceratonia siliqua L.) is a Mediterranean plant traditionally valued for its nutritional and therapeutic properties. Despite the recognized biological potential of its various parts, the pharmacological value of carob leaves remains underexplored. This study aimed to evaluate the phytochemical content and assess the antioxidant, antibacterial, and cytotoxic properties of wild carob leaves ethanolic extract (CLEE). The total polyphenol, flavonoid, and condensed tannin contents of CLEE were quantified as 163.50 ± 9.25 mg gallic acid equivalent/g dry matter (DM), 22.23 ± 8.61 mg quercetin equivalent/g DM, and 34.35 ± 9.70 mg catechin equivalent/g DM, respectively. HPLC–DAD analysis revealed cinnamic, ferulic, sinapic, rutin, and succinic acids as the dominant phenolic compounds. CLEE exhibited strong antioxidant activity, with IC₅₀ values of 0.293 ± 0.034 mg/mL for DPPH and 19.48 ± 2.47 mg/mL for ABTS assays, alongside a total antioxidant capacity of 189.01 ± 7.18 µg ascorbic acid equivalent/mg. The extract also demonstrated notable antibacterial effects against a range of bacterial strains, as evaluated by disc diffusion and broth microdilution methods. Cytotoxicity assays revealed that CLEE exerted a selective antiproliferative effect against the MCF-7 breast cancer cell line, with an IC₅₀ value of 28.26 ± 3.15 µg/mL. Molecular docking highlighted rutin as a key compound contributing to the antioxidant activity. In silico absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion profiling showed that most detected bioactive compounds possess good oral bioavailability and comply with Lipinski’s rule of five. These findings suggest that CLEE contains pharmacologically relevant constituents and holds promise as a source of natural antioxidants and antimicrobial and anticancer agents. The study supports the valorisation of wild carob leaves, often considered an agricultural by-product, for potential applications in nutraceutical and pharmaceutical development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-09062-3.

Introduction

The carob tree (Ceratonia siliqua L., Fabaceae family) is indigenous to the Mediterranean region1. It is primarily cultivated in countries within the Mediterranean basin, notably in southern Europe, North Africa, and the Middle East. Global annual production of carob pods is estimated at approximately 400,000 tonnes, with European Union (EU) countries accounting for more than 70% of this total. Major producers and exporters within the EU include Spain, Italy, Portugal, Greece, Cyprus, and Malta. In the Middle East and North Africa region, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Egypt, and Lebanon are significant contributors. Notably, Morocco ranks among the top five global producers, alongside Portugal, Italy, Spain, and Turkey. With a cultivated area of approximately 21,202 hectares and an annual yield ranging between 40,000 and 60,000 tonnes of carob pods, Morocco plays a key role in international production2,. Additionally, carob cultivation is expanding in non-traditional regions such as Australia, where commercial interest in this drought-tolerant species is rising3.

The tree has long been used as a healthy food source because of its delicious and nutrient-dense fruits4. The plant is highly qualified due to its longevity, economic worth, and the nutrient-dense makeup of all of its parts (leaves, pods, pulp, and seeds)5, Owing to their bioactive properties, these components are widely utilized in the pharmaceutical, food, and cosmetic industries6,7. The substantial amounts of carbs are carbohydrates, fiber, minerals, and beneficial compounds found in carob pulp and seeds make them ideal for human consumption8,9, also carob pulp has been used to make a variety of culinary products, such as syrup, molasses, and drinks beverages1,10,11. Carob seeds have been used as an alternative to common thickening agents such as guar gum and carrageenan7. Moreover, the increasing demand for natural and sustainable products has positioned carob as a crop of growing interest in scientific research. Numerous recent studies have focused on its nutritional and functional properties, bioactive compounds, and potential health benefits, underlining the relevance of carob in the context of sustainable agriculture and health-promoting food development12–16.

Carob leaves are the principal by-product, which are available in abundance throughout the year17. According to earlier research, the most prevalent substances in leaves, as well as other carob tree parts, are defined as the secondary metabolites, including polyphenols and are rich in condensed tannins18–20; also, leaves and pods contain gallic acid, isoquercetin, kaempferol, quercetin, (-)-epigallocatechin-3-O-gallate, and quercetin21,22. In addition, it was also approved that carob leaves contain a considerable quantity of linoleic (C18:3) and oleic acids (C18:2), these compositions contribute to the leaves having high nutritional value23,24. Owing to their unique qualities and their antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, anti-diabetic, anti-diarrheal, and antioxidant activities, carob leaves have long been utilized in traditional medicine17,25. These beneficial effects are attributed to the presence of various bioactive compounds in carob leaves, including flavonoids, polyphenols, and tannins26,27. Given the well-documented anticancer properties of quercetin and kaempferol present in carob leaves, it is plausible that locust bean leaves also possess potential anticancer activity. However, there is a notable lack of research regarding the possible applications of carob leaves, particularly at the national level, where they are often regarded as a low-value by-product13,28..

This study aimed to investigate the bioactive compounds present in carob leaf ethanolic extract (CLEE), with particular emphasis on flavonoids, polyphenols, and condensed tannins. High-performance liquid chromatography with diode-array detection (HPLC–DAD) was employed to analyze the extract and determine the qualitative profile of phenolic constituents responsible for its bioactivity. Additionally, the antioxidant, antibacterial, and cytotoxic activities of the wild carob leaf extract were assessed. Molecular docking analysis was further conducted to identify the principal bioactive compounds and explore their potential mechanisms of action.

Results and discussion

Bioactive content of carob leaf ethanolic extract

Table 1 summarizes the polyphenols assessment contents of the carob leaf ethanolic extract (CLEE). The total polyphenol content (TPC) of the CLEE was 163.50 ± 9.24 mg gallic acid equivalent (GAE)/g dry matter (DM), and the total flavonoid content (TFC) was 22.32 ± 8.61 mg quercetin equivalent (QE)/g DM, while the total condensed tannins (TCT) was 34.35 ± 9.70 mg catechin equivalents (CE)/g DM. The TPC observed in our investigation was in line with the study conducted by Uysal et al.29, who found a value ranging from 154.72 to 161.95 ± 14.55 mg GAE/g, and higher than those described by Ena Cegledi et al.10, which ranged from 25.87 ± 0.23 to 90.67 ± 0.93 mg GAE/g. Previous research had documented TPC values of 6.28 mg GAE/g30, 21.41 mg GAE/g31, and 45.26 mg GAE/g32. However, a study found a very high value (680 mg GAE/g) compared to our study; this value was achieved by a series of extractions and fractionations using different solvents33. According to the research findings, carob leaves are rich in a diverse array of phenolic compounds. This complex phytochemical profile contributes significantly to the pharmacological potential of carob leaf extracts, which have been shown to exhibit multiple beneficial bioactivities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and anticancer effects. The total flavonoid content (TFC) of CLEE was evaluated, as flavonoids constitute a class of phenolic compounds well recognized for their distinct antibacterial properties35. CLEE exhibited a considerable TFC of 22.32 ± 8.61 mg quercetin equivalents (QE)/g dry matter (DM). These values are notably higher than those reported in previous studies, such as Dallali et al.23, who observed TFC ranging from 3.42 to 7.42 mg QE/g DM, and Custódio et al.18, who reported values between 2.1 and 13.4 mg rutin equivalents (RE)/g DM. Nevertheless, this finding was lower than those found by Uysal et al.29 and Hsouna et al.36, with a value ranging between 42.89 and 64.64 mg RE/g and 75.94 and 193.3 mg QE/g, respectively. In light of growing epidemiological evidence supporting the diverse health benefits of flavonoids, there has been an increasing interest in investigating flavonoids derived from dietary sources. Assessing the flavonoid content in foods is essential, as their presence directly influences the antioxidant intake of individuals. Carob leaves represent a particularly rich source of flavonoids, making them a valuable candidate for further exploration in the context of nutritional and therapeutic applications37.

The total condensed tannin (TCT) content of CLEE was evaluated, with the results presented in Table 1. The measured TCT was 34.35 ± 9.70 mg catechin equivalents (CE)/g dry matter (DM). This value exceeds the range reported by Custódio et al.18, who observed TCT levels between 1.8 and 23.7 mg CE/g DM. However, the findings of Ben Othmen et al.17 reported a higher total condensed tannin content than our study, with a value of 18.52 ± 0.03 g CE/100 g DM. The capacity of tannins, a subclass of plant polyphenols, to precipitate and form complexes with proteins sets them apart from other polyphenols. Animal illnesses associated with parasite infections can be effectively treated by the condensed tannins found in carob leaves38,39. Overall, the findings demonstrate that CLEE contains substantial amounts of bioactive compounds, which may confer significant health benefits. The observed variations in TPC, TFC, and TCT between our study and previous reports, summarized in Table 1, are likely influenced by factors such as genotype, geographic origin, and environmental growth conditions40–42.

HPLC–DAD analysis of phytochemical constituents in carob leaf extract

Phytochemical analysis using HPLC–DAD was used to determine the chemicals present in the ethanol extract of carob leaves. Table 2 displayed some chemical compounds found in the carob leaf ethanolic extract. The major constituents were cinnamic acid (21.09%), followed by Ferulic acid (19.40%), sinapic acid (16.10%), rutin (11.78%), succinic acid (10.10%), quercetin (7.67%), naringin (5.45%), and p-coumaric acid (4.39%). Cinnamic acid is an essential component of the aromatic acid group that is found in high concentrations in plants. The simplicity of its structure is indicative of its diverse biological functions. Consequently, its utilization in the field of drug synthesis is well-documented, as is its employment in the structural modification of natural products with a view to enhancing efficacy, reducing toxic side effects and improving pharmacokinetic properties. Cinnamic acid can also be used as a linking agent to enrich the skeleton of the natural product or introduce pharmacophores43,44. In carob products, a variety of phenolic chemicals from different classes have been detected and identified. Ferulic acid and coumaric acid, p-coumaric acid, a kind of phenolic acid, are the primary categories of phenolic chemicals found in carobs. These phenolic acid metabolites contribute to the characteristics of color, taste, and astringency. They have a role in the distinctive sensory properties of foods43,45. Richane et al.40, and Goulas et al.46 revealed the presence of sinapic acid and rutin in carob powder. Sinapic acid was found in lower concentrations, while rutin was identified as the predominant component. These results suggest that carob may have several health benefits. Naringin is a natural compound known as flavanone glycoside. It is often found in Chinese herbal remedies and citrus fruits. The predominant concentration of naringin is 7.56%. The results are consistent with earlier studies showed by El-Haddad et al.47. Additionally, this investigation discovered a significant abundance of flavonoids and phenolic acids in carob leaves. The therapeutic effects of carob extract are emphasized by the presence of many components, particularly naringin, which has been identified as one of the primary constituents. Naringin has shown numerous health advantages, including anti-inflammatory and antioxidant capabilities48. Furthermore, the presence of quercetin, another bioactive compound, reinforces the extract’s potential anticancer properties, as this flavonol has been extensively associated with antitumor effects in previous studies11. DPPH free radical scavenging is a common antioxidant activity test that measures hydrogen-donating ability. Lower IC50 values indicated stronger radical scavenging activity. The DPPH radical scavenging activity of CLEE was found to be 0.293 ± 0.034 mg/mL, a result that merits comparison with that of the synthetic antioxidant ascorbic acid (0.13 ± 0.03 mg/mL). These findings align with those of Custodio et al.18, who reported considerable antioxidant activity with IC50 values ranging from 0.153 to 0.273 mg/mL. However, the present results were considerably higher than those obtained in the study of Tunisian carob leaves (ranging from 1.80 to 41.01 µg/mL)33. These DPPH free radical scavenging activities of CLEE could be attributed to the bioactive polyphenols present in these extracts in appreciable amounts34. The CLEE exhibited significant ABTS radical scavenging activity, with an IC50 value of 19.48 ± 2.47 mg/mL. This activity was important in comparison to that of ascorbic acid (1.81 ± 0.34 mg/mL). The results obtained are higher than those found by Khelouf et al.49 (70.3 ug/mL). CLEE showed a higher total antioxidant capacity, it was found that CLEE had the highest phosphomolybdenum reduction, at 189.01 ± 7.18 µg ascorbic acid equivalents/mg extract. This study’s findings demonstrate slight improvement over previous research in this field34,50. As demonstrated by DPPH, and ABTS, CLEE exhibited strong antioxidant activity as well as a strong antiradical capacity. The amounts of anthocyanin, flavonoids, condensed tannin, and total phenols may be responsible for this antioxidant action. Additionally, phenolic compounds enhance antioxidant activity in a dose-dependent manner, reaching peak effectiveness based on their distinct properties51. The antioxidant, cytotoxic, and free-radical scavenging properties of carob leaf extracts have all been extensively studied over time. The two main techniques for these purposes are DPPH and ABTS. Because they include ascorbic acid, polyphenols, and carotenoids, carob leaves have demonstrated a high potential to be a source of antioxidant activity in addition to their pod, fruit, and seeds52,53.

β-carotene bleaching assay is considered a way to determine the antioxidant activity of CLEE. The principle of this assay depended on loss of orange color of β-carotene when β-carotene’s conjugation is disrupted by free radicals54. The β-carotene bleaching potentiality of carob leaves was compared BHA with IC50 of 2.11 ± 0.08 and 0.129 ± 0.013, respectively. CLEE exhibited notable efficacy when compared to the reference standard BHA, highlighting its significant capacity to convert free radicals into non-toxic substances. The β-carotene bleaching assay is based on the oxidation reaction between β-carotene and linoleic acid, which measures the ability of antioxidants to neutralize the linoleic acid peroxide radical, this radical is responsible for the oxidation of β-carotene in the emulsion phase. Recent research by Dahmani et al.55 reported that CLEE’s ability to prevent β-carotene bleaching increased progressively, reaching a peak of 67.03 ± 0.47% inhibition at a concentration of 2 mg/mL. Ascorbic acid, used as the standard, offered complete protection against β-carotene oxidation at a concentration of 4 mg/mL. Furthermore, the findings of Elbouzidi et al.34 are also in line with the current results as they also used similar antioxidant assays to evaluate the antioxidant activity of carob leaves extracts. These findings enhance our understanding of the antioxidant capacity of carob leaves extracts, emphasizing their potential role in the prevention or alleviation of disorders associated with oxidative stress. The strong antioxidant effects observed are primarily linked to the substantial levels of polyphenolic compounds within the extracts. Collectively, the results support the value of carob as a promising natural source of antioxidants, which may contribute to health maintenance and disease prevention when included in the diet6.

For the DPPH method, this correlation reached the following values 0.947 with TFC 0.933 with TCT and finally 0.923 with TPC. For the ABTS and TAC methods, the correlation reached the same values with the antioxidant components and is represented by the following values: 0.993 with TPC, 0.820 with TFC and finally 0.796 with TCT (Table S1).

This result suggests that TPC, TFC and TCT in the leaves part of CLEE are mainly responsible for antioxidants’ ability to get rid of harmful free radicals. A study showed that polyphenolic content and antioxidant activity were positively correlated. According to Amponsah-Offeh et al.56, polyphenols can carry out biological functions using processes that neutralize free radicals and suppress ROS56. These results imply that the high quantity of polyphenols in the CLEE is probably what gives it its antioxidant action55.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA), as illustrated in Fig. 1, revealed that the two principal components explain the entire variance in the dataset. Specifically, Component 1 accounts for 54.45% of the variance and is strongly correlated with TPC (r = 0.927), ABTS (r = 0.877), and TAC (r = 0.877), while also showing a moderate correlation with DPPH (r = 0.710). Component 2 accounts for 45.55% of the variance and is highly correlated with TCT (r = 0.913), TFC (r = 0.896), and DPPH (r = 0.701).

These findings indicate that the antioxidant activities observed in CLEE are closely associated with its bioactive composition, particularly the presence of polyphenols, flavonoids, and condensed tannins. The rotated component space allows a clear interpretation of how these bioactives influence the different antioxidant assays. Notably, TPC is most strongly associated with ABTS and TAC, followed by TFC and TCT. In contrast, for DPPH activity, TFC exhibits the highest contribution, followed by TPC and then TCT.

Overall, the PCA results underscore the substantial contribution of phenolic compounds to the antioxidant potential of CLEE. These outcomes align with previous studies, such as Elbouzidi et al.34, which emphasized the richness of carob leaf extracts in antioxidant constituents. More recently, Dahmani et al.6 corroborated these findings by reporting diverse biological activities of carob leaf extracts attributed to their antioxidant content. Therefore, CLEE can be considered a valuable natural source of antioxidant compounds with promising applications in food and pharmaceutical industries.

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of CLEE was assessed by disc diffusion and broth dilution methods, and results were summarized in Table 4 and Fig. 2. Our findings revealed that CLEE creates an inhibitory zone of 15.00 ± 1.00 mm against L. monocytogenes, 14.5 ± 1.5 mm against S. aureus, 14.00 ± 1.00 mm against E. coli, and 6.5 ± 0.5 mm against P. aeruginosa (Fig. 2). While chloramphenicol showed an inhibitory zone of 26.00 ± 1.5 mm against L. monocytogenes, 25.00 ± 0.5 mm against S. aureus, 28.5 ± 1.50 mm against E. coli, and 11.00 ± 0.5 against P. aeruginosa. Additionally, broth dilution method showed that CLEE inhibit bacterial growth at MIC of 0.2 mg/mL for L. monocytogenes, 0.78 mg/mL for both S. aureus and E. coli, and 25 mg/mL for P. aeruginosa (Table 4), while our results recorded MBC values of 0.2 mg/mL for L. monocytogenes, 0.78 mg/mL for both S. aureus, 3.13 mg/mL for E. coli, and 100 mg/mL for P. aeruginosa (Table 4), indicating that CLEE has a bactericidal effect against gram-positive bacteria with an MBC/MIC ratio lower than or equal to 2, and a bacteriostatic effect against gram-negative bacteria with an MBC/MIC ratio of 457. Our findings are aligned with the results published by Awwad et al.58, as the carob leaf extracts were used to derive silver nanoparticles, which showed potent antibacterial activity. Extracts from carob leaves provide a natural and potential substitute for bioconservation and food safety. The antibacterial activity of CLEE can be explained by its richness in secondary metabolites, which may be correlated to the content of polyphenols, flavonoids, and tannins. Previous studies have demonstrated the antibacterial capacity of cinnamic acid and its derivative compounds61,62. Cinnamic acid has been detected by HPLC–DAD as the major phenolic compounds in CLEE. Additionally, ferulic acid has proven efficacity in inhibiting multidrug resistant bacteria63,64. Rutin have also demonstrated great antibacterial activity against various bacterial strains, including Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria65,66. Furthermore, the synergistic, antagonistic, and inhibition phenomenon may be done and affect the total antibacterial activity of plant extracts67,68. In this regard, Arima et al. demonstrated the ability of Rutin to enhance the antibacterial activity of flavonoids against Bacillus cereus and Salmonella69.

In addition, our findings revealed that CLEE is slightly more effective against Gram-positive than Gram-negative bacteria, which agree with previous studies that reported the susceptibility of Gram-positive bacteria to plant-derived compounds compared to Gram-negative bacteria57,70. This difference is due to the difference in the wall structure of bacteria where the plants compounds overcome the cell envelope and cause several damages to bacterial cells, including damage of cell wall, destruction of cell membrane, block ribosomal protein synthesis, inhibit energy production mechanism, inhibit DNA replication, among other71,72.

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of death in the world, contributing to nearly ten million deaths, or around one in six deaths in 2020, and being the second-leading cause of death after cardiovascular disease73,74. There are several side effects that can damage non-cancerous tissue due to different clinical therapies such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy75. Plant-based anti-cancer medications such as epipodophyllotoxins, paclitaxel, vincristine, and vinblastine are used in therapy because cancer patients are becoming less sensitive to chemotherapy drugs. Therefore, plants are playing an increasingly important role in the search for new chemotherapeutic drugs.

In vitro, several carob extracts have been examined for their effects on various tumor cells19. Carob is well-known for its use in traditional medicine and its rich content of bioactive compounds, including polyphenols, which have been scientifically shown to possess therapeutic properties against several human illnesses, notably cancer and metastases30. To investigate the mechanisms by which CLEE eradicates breast cancer cells, the MTT test was conducted using three cell lines: MDA-MB-231, MCF-7, and MDA-MB-436. The results are summarized in Table 5. In this experiment, cisplatin was used as a positive control. As illustrated in Table 5, CLEE demonstrated a range of cytotoxic effects across the diverse cell lines. The MCF-7 cell line demonstrated the greatest sensitivity, as indicated by its IC50 value of 28.26 ± 3.15 µg/mL. In contrast, the MDA-MB-436 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines exhibited lower sensitivity levels, as seen by their IC50 values of 48.74 ± 1.67 µg/mL and 36.04 ± 1.45 µg/mL, respectively. These results appear more robust and reliable than those reported by Custódio, et al.50. Polyphenols are natural substances present in various plant tissues, especially leaves. These compounds are notable for their exceptional ability to interrupt or reverse the process of carcinogenesis. It is crucial to emphasize that they precisely target the chemicals that make up the intracellular signaling network. This network is crucial during all stages of illness genesis and progression. Polyphenols possess the capacity to make a substantial impact in preventing cancer by decelerating, or even reversing, the advancement of the disease as a result of their capability to control these signaling pathways. By selectively focusing on a restricted set of crucial components in the cellular signaling system, it is feasible to induce the initiation of the apoptotic process in cancer cells in a precise manner76. The search for new and safe therapies for cancer treatment is a common and ongoing endeavor, with the majority of these therapies derived from natural resources77. The findings indicate that CLEEs may possess anti-cancer properties with significant potential. This suggests that more research is required to confirm their effectiveness, including mechanistic investigations and in vivo experiments. Their therapeutic utility will be greatly increased by further studies on the possible applications of the investigated extracts in the treatment of cancer, as well as a thorough understanding of their mechanisms of action.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) are among the first normal cells to encounter anticancer agents administered via conventional intravenous chemotherapy. Therefore, minimizing cytotoxic effects on PBMCs is crucial for evaluating the safety of new therapeutic candidates. In this study, the cytotoxicity of carob leaf ethanolic extract (CLEE) on PBMCs was assessed using the MTT assay. CLEE exhibited a high IC₅₀ value (> 720 µg/mL), indicating low toxicity towards normal cells.

In comparison to cisplatin, CLEE showed greater cytotoxicity against cancer cell lines than against PBMCs, highlighting its selective action. Although CLEE demonstrated a higher IC₅₀ (lower potency) than cisplatin across all tested cancer cell lines, it also exhibited higher selectivity index (SI) values. This suggests that CLEE preferentially targets malignant cells while sparing healthy ones.

Such selectivity is a promising pharmacological feature, indicating the potential of CLEE as a safer anticancer agent with reduced side effects relative to conventional chemotherapeutics like cisplatin.

Interactions with human enzyme glutathione reductase (PDB: 3DK9)

Glutathione reductase (GR) is crucial for safeguarding cells against oxidative stress, which is connected to the development of several diseases. Focusing on glutathione (GSH), an essential antioxidant, could offer therapeutic benefits by reducing the harmful oxidative stress linked to various health conditions78. Inhibiting glutathione reductase presents a promising approach for developing targeted cancer therapies and antimicrobial treatments. This strategy leverages the dependency of both bacteria and cancer cells on the glutathione system for their antioxidant defenses, potentially enhancing the precision and efficacy of existing treatment methods79. The results indicated that, with the exception of catechin, naringin, p-coumaric acid, rutin, quercetin, and flavone, all the analyzed molecules displayed an affinity for glutathione reductase that was either equal to or lower than that of xanthine, a standard inhibitor (− 6.2 kcal/mol) (Table S2). Notably, rutin exhibited an exceptional affinity, as the interaction analysis of the rutin-3DK9 complex demonstrated its effective binding with multiple amino acid residues. Precisely, conventional hydrogen bonds are formed between rutin and amino acid residues ASN366, ILE367, SER225, and GLU386. These hydrogen bonds are essential for anchoring the ligand and may play a pivotal role in obstructing the enzyme’s catalytic activity, a mechanism similarly reported for other flavonoid-based GR inhibitors80. Furthermore, additional carbon–hydrogen bonds involving TYR364 and THR379 further enhance the stability of the complex. A noteworthy Pi-Sigma interaction was also identified between the aromatic moiety of rutin and ALA336 (Fig. 3), a contact that contributes to the overall non-covalent stabilization of the complex. Such interactions are often associated with improved binding affinity due to enhanced π-electron cloud overlap with hydrophobic residues, consistent with prior studies on aromatic antioxidants targeting GR81. The combination of these interactions strongly suggests that rutin can effectively bind to glutathione reductase, potentially inhibiting its activity. Notably, several other compounds also displayed strong binding profiles, further highlight in the potential of CLEE as a source of glutathione reductase inhibitors. Guneidy et al.82 demonstrated that these compounds exhibit significant inhibitory activity against glutathione reductase, thereby suggesting their potential as antioxidant agents. These findings support the hypothesis that CLEE may exert antioxidant effects through the inhibition of this enzyme, complementing earlier reports that link polyphenolic compounds with modulation of redox homeostasis and suppression of oxidative stress pathways83.

Interactions with human enzyme dihydrofolate reductase (PDB: 1DRF)

Dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) is a critical enzyme found in various species, fundamental to folate metabolism and the production of nucleotides required for DNA replication84 Inhibitors of DHFR are vital in combating bacterial infections by interfering with the synthesis of DNA and proteins necessary for bacterial survival85. The results demonstrated that, with the exception of catechin, naringin, p-coumaric acid, rutin, quercetin, apigenin, and flavone, all analyzed compounds exhibited binding affinities to dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) that were equal to or lower than that of methotrexate a standard reference inhibitor with a binding energy of -5.1 kcal/mol (Table S2). Among the active molecules, rutin emerged as a particularly promising ligand, displaying the strongest affinity for DHFR. A detailed molecular docking analysis of the rutin–DHFR complex (PDB ID: 1DRF) revealed a network of stabilizing interactions within the enzyme’s active site. Rutin formed several conventional hydrogen bonds with key residues GLN102, GLU104, and ARG91, all located in catalytically important regions of the enzyme. These interactions contribute significantly to the stability of the enzyme–ligand complex and may impair the enzyme’s function, thereby enhancing rutin’s inhibitory potential. Additionally, hydrophobic interactions, such as Pi–Alkyl contacts with LEU89 and LEU99, further anchor rutin within the enzyme’s active pocket, reducing ligand flexibility and strengthening binding affinity. A notable Pi–Sigma interaction with LEU89 suggests strong engagement of rutin’s aromatic core with the surrounding hydrophobic environment (Fig. 3), consistent with binding mechanisms observed for other polyphenolic DHFR inhibitors86. These in silico findings align with previous studies demonstrating the antibacterial activity of rutin through DHFR inhibition. Several studies have reported that rutin significantly inhibits bacterial growth by targeting folate biosynthesis pathways, particularly through suppression of DHFR activity87. Taken together, these data support the hypothesis that CLEE contains bioactive constituents such as rutin capable of inhibiting DHFR, potentially disrupting bacterial folate metabolism. These findings confirm the antibacterial potential of CLEE and suggest that rutin could serve as a natural scaffold for the development of DHFR-targeting antimicrobial agents.

Interactions with human epidermal growth factor receptor protein (PDB: 3W2S)

Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is essential for regulating a range of normal cellular functions, such as growth, differentiation, and survival. However, in the context of cancer, EGFR is often found to be overexpressed or excessively activated, driving the development and progression of various malignancies, including non-small cell lung cancer, colorectal cancer, head and neck cancer, and pancreatic cancer. As a result, targeting EGFR has become a key strategy in cancer therapy88. The results of the study reveal that the majority of the tested compounds exhibit binding affinities for EGFR that are comparable to, or slightly lower than, that of vincristine, a well-established EGFR inhibitor with a binding energy of -8.1 kcal/mol (Table S2). However, two flavonoids stand out: rutin and naringin, which display more negative binding energies, indicating a stronger affinity for the enzymatic target. Among these compounds, naringin emerges with a particularly favorable interaction profile. Analysis of the Naringin-EGFR complex (PDB ID: 3W2S) revealed several stabilizing interactions. Specifically, the molecule forms conventional hydrogen bonds with GLU749 and ASP837, two critical residues located in the catalytic region of the enzyme. These interactions are essential for maintaining the enzyme-ligand complex and are frequently associated with inhibitory effects, as confirmed by several previous studies on flavonoid-type EGFR inhibitors89. In addition, a noteworthy Pi-Anion interaction is observed with PHE723, further enhancing the complex’s stability. The aromatic ring of naringin may play a key role in this interaction, which aligns with stabilization mechanisms reported for other aromatic EGFR inhibitors90. Additional hydrophobic interactions, including Pi-Alkyl contacts with ARG858 and ARG889, contribute to the efficient anchoring of the molecule within the active site, reducing ligand flexibility and enhancing its inhibitory potential. In comparison, although rutin also exhibits strong affinity for EGFR, its interactions are fewer and mainly located in peripheral regions of the active site, which may result in less specific inhibition (Fig. 3). These findings confirm that certain polyphenols found in CLEE, particularly naringin, possess structural features compatible with effective EGFR inhibition. This molecular mode of action could contribute to the anticancer potential of CLEE. Previous studies have already demonstrated the antiproliferative properties of naringin on various cancer cell lines through the inhibition of EGFR-dependent signaling pathways91. In vitro studies have demonstrated that both rutin and naringin exert inhibitory effects on EGFR signaling pathways. Rutin has been reported to inhibit EGFR expression92. Similarly, naringin has been shown to inhibit EGFR activation and modulate associated signaling cascades, thereby inhibiting tumor cell viability91. Therefore, our results are consistent with the literature and support the notion that CLEE may represent a promising natural source of EGFR inhibitors.

Early pharmacokinetic assessment using computational models enables the swift identification of drug candidates and optimizes the development process. These studies play a crucial role in reducing the risk of adverse reactions, lowering the chances of drug development failures, and enhancing clinical success rates93. Lipinski’s Rule of Five offers important guidelines for selecting potential candidates in oral drug development. It suggests that a compound is more likely to be successful if it meets the following criteria: (1) the compound should not have more than 10 hydrogen bond acceptors, which are typically oxygen or nitrogen atoms, (2) its octanol–water partition coefficient (log P or MLogP) should not exceed 5, (3) it should not possess more than 5 hydrogen bond donors, which are oxygen or nitrogen atoms bonded to one or more hydrogen atoms, (4) the molecular weight should be less than 500 daltons, and (5) the polar surface area (PSA) should be below 140 Å2. Compounds that exceed more than one of these thresholds may face challenges in bioavailability and absorption94. In our study, we found that all the phytochemicals identified in the ethanolic extract of carob leaves met Lipinski’s Rule of Five, suggesting their potential for development as oral drugs. The only exceptions were Naringin and Rutin, which had molecular weights exceeding 500 g/mol. Furthermore, our analysis highlighted that several of these compounds, including vanillin (TPSA = 46.5 Å2, WLogP = 1.2), cinnamic acid (TPSA = 37.3 Å2, WLogP = 1.6), ferulic acid (TPSA = 66.7 Å2, WLogP = 1.3), p-coumaric acid (TPSA = 57.3 Å2, WLogP = 1.3), and flavone (TPSA = 30.2 Å2, WLogP = 3.4), possess the ability to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), as shown in Fig. 4. This finding suggests their potential as therapeutic agents capable of affecting the central nervous system. This finding underscores their potential for central nervous system activity, making them promising candidates for the development of therapies targeting neurological disorders95,96. These results are supported by experimental data: vanillin, for example, has demonstrated neuroprotective effects in mouse models of hypoxic brain injury, where it reduced infarcts, strengthened BBB tight junctions, and positively modulated the expression of BDNF and TrkB97. Furthermore, a study in rats showed that it has modest oral bioavailability and has also been identified as a modulator of membrane fluidity, promoting the intestinal absorption of other active ingredients, which reinforces its favorable profile. Cinnamic acid, another compound detected in our extract, demonstrated neuroprotective efficacy in vivo98. Similarly, ferulic acid and p-coumaric acid demonstrated good intestinal permeability in cellular models (Caco-2)99, and antioxidant and neuroprotective effects in Alzheimer’s models, suggesting their ability to act both peripherally and in the central nervous system100.

A molecule with high intestinal absorption provides notable benefits including enhanced bioavailability, therapeutic efficacy, ease of administration, and better patient tolerance, making it a strong candidate for oral drug development101. Importantly, except for naringin and rutin, all the phytochemicals analyzed in this study showed high intestinal absorption (Table S3). This observation is consistent with previous work where compounds such as apigenin, quercetin, or ferulic acid have demonstrated good cell permeability and favorable pharmacokinetic profiles102. Additionally, all the analyzed compounds were identified as P-glycoprotein non-substrates (PGP-), except for catechin (10) (Fig. 4). This suggests that these compounds could avoid premature elimination by P-gp, thereby maintaining more stable and prolonged therapeutic concentrations in the body103. Cytochrome P450, an essential enzyme for detoxification, is predominantly found in the liver104. Our findings revealed that the majority of the compounds tested were not significant inhibitors or substrates for CYP450 enzymes, particularly CYP1A2. However, compounds such as quercetin, apigenin, and flavone were identified as inhibitors of CYP1A2 (Table S3). This observation suggests that these phytochemicals are less likely to interfere with drug metabolism, which may contribute to their favorable safety profile.

Figure 5 shows the bioavailability radar plots of the identified compounds, offering a clear visualization of their oral bioavailability potential. The pink region on the radar represents the ideal zone where a molecule’s graphical profile should fall to be considered "drug-like." This representation is indispensable for assessing the compound’s viability as a therapeutic agent, as it indicates its potential for efficient absorption and systemic distribution within the body103. Remarkably, the bioavailability radar for succinic acid (9) falls within the pink zone, highlighting its promising characteristics as a potential drug candidate. These predictions, already observed for certain simple organic acids in pharmacokinetic tests in rats, highlight the interest of this molecule in various therapeutic applications105. In summary, the results of our study are consistent with numerous data from the scientific literature, confirming that the identified compounds possess not only physicochemical properties compatible with oral administration, but also biological effects demonstrated in in vitro and in vivo models.

Materials and methods

Carob material

Wild Carob leaves were randomly collected from three different areas: Ouaouizerth (32°11′52.9″ N 6°19′52.2″ W), Afourar (32°08′41.1″ N 6°30′04.8″ W), Bin Elouidane (32°32′57.0″ N 6°00′42.9″ W), Elksiba (32°33′18.1″ N 6°01′00.8″ W) and Tagzirt (32°26′09.0″ N 6°11′22.4″ W) of the Beni-Mellal Khenifra region in the center of Morocco in March–April 2024. Leaves (approximately 1 kg fresh weight) of uniform color, size, and shape that were free from any disorders were selected for analysis. The identification of the plant was conducted by Professor Fennane Mohammed, a professional botanist from the Scientific Institute in Rabat, Morocco. Voucher specimens of Ceratonia siliqua L. were found deposited in the Herbarium of the University Mohammed Premier in Oujda, Morocco (HUMPOM74).

Extract preparation and physicochemical analysis

The carob tree leaves were first dried in the shade at ambient temperature, a traditional and widely adopted method that helps preserve the integrity of phytochemical compounds by minimizing thermal degradation. The dried leaves were then thoroughly cleaned and finely ground using an electric grinder to produce a uniform powder with a particle size of less than 1 mm. Fine carob leaf powder (20 g) was subjected to maceration in 200 mL of 80% ethanol (sample-to-solvent ratio of 1:10, w/v) for 24 h at room temperature (25 °C) under continuous agitation (150 rpm). The choice of this hydroethanolic mixture aimed to optimize the solubility of both polar and moderately non-polar compounds, while preserving thermolabile constituents. carob leaves ethanolic extract (CLEE) were centrifuged at 15,000 rpm (2150×g) for 15 min, followed by filtration through a 0.45 μm polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane filter. The ethanolic extract was completely evaporated using a rotary evaporator (IKA RV 10 auto, Germany) to remove the solvent, resulting in 2.365 g of dry extract from 20 g of plant material, corresponding to an extraction yield of approximately 11.83%. The obtained dry extract was concentrated and preserved under appropriate conditions until its use in subsequent in vitro assays. No reconstitution in solvent was performed prior to the biological evaluations.

Quantitative phytochemical analysis

The total phenolic content (TPC) in the CLEE was quantified using the Folin-Ciocalteu (FC) reagent106 with some modifications. The findings were expressed in mg GAE/g DM (milligrams of gallic acid equivalent per gram of dry mass).

The quantification of the total flavonoid content (TFC) and total condensed tannins content (TCT) of the leaves was conducted following the methodology established by Laaraj et al.11 with certain modifications. The results were reported as milligrams of quercetin equivalent (QE) and milligrams of catechin equivalent (CE) per gram of dry leaf mass, respectively.

HPLC–DAD analysis of phenolic compounds

To analyze CLEE, HPLC–DAD (Waters Corporation, USA) technique combining a diode array detector with high-performance liquid chromatography was applied. The empower program was then used to handle and examine the data produced by this investigation107. The prepared samples, measuring 20 µL each, were injected using an automated injection device. An elution gradient of 0–25 min at 20% B, 25–30 min at 100% B, and 30–35 min at 20% B was used. The injection took place on a 250 × 4.6 mm Zorbax XDB-C18 column with a 5 µm porosity. The results were integrated using a 1 mL/min Agilent ChemStation HPLC. Sample elution was done at 40 °C in mobile phases A (water/0.5% phosphoric acid) and B (methanol). Spectrophotometry was done at 280 nm. We compared the UV spectra and retention times of the identified peaks to a genuine reference solution (5 mg/mL) run on the same column under identical conditions to determine the compounds in the ethanolic extract108.

ABTS scavenging assay

The scavenging ability of carob leaves extracts were examined against the 2,2-Azino-bis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) radical, as detailed by Nakyai et al.110, with specific modifications. ABTS solution was combined and incubated with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate at room temperature for 16–18 h in the dark to produce ABTS+. The solution ABTS and a CLEE extract were kept in ethanol till an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 was attained at a wavelength of 750 nm, separately. l-ascorbic acid served as a standard control. The assay was performed by combining 200 µL of diluted ABTS·+ solution with 20 µL of the tested extract. The mixture was maintained in darkness for 10 min at room temperature, then it was measured using a microplate reader at 734 nm (a wavelength). The percentage of ABTS radical cation decolorization activity was assessed, analogous to the previous DPPH test.

β-carotene bleaching assay

The antioxidant capacity of the extract was evaluated using the β-carotene-linoleic acid bleaching method, as described by Tikent et al.106 and Rădulescu et al.111, with slight modifications. Briefly, 2 mg of β-carotene was dissolved in 10 mL of chloroform. To this solution, 20 mg of linoleic acid and 200 mg of Tween-80 were added. After thorough mixing, the chloroform was evaporated at 40 °C using a rotary evaporator. Subsequently, 100 mL of distilled water was added to the residue under vigorous stirring to form a stable emulsion.

Aliquots of the resulting emulsion were transferred to a 96-well microplate in triplicate. Each well received the sample extract, and the plate was incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 470 nm using a spectrophotometer, both immediately after addition of the hydroethanolic extract (t₀) and after 2 h (t₁). A blank control, containing all components except β-carotene, was included. Butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA) served as the positive control.

Antibacterial activity

Four bacterial strains were chosen for the study, consisting of 2 Gram-positive bacteria (L. monocytogenes ATCC 13,932 and S. aureus ATCC 29,213) and 2 Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli ATCC 25922 and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853). Each strain was cultured on Tryptone Soy Agar and was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The disc diffusion method was used to investigate the antibacterial activity of CLEE based on the determination of inhibitory zones, according to previous protocols112. In brief, each microorganism was spread onto Petri dishes containing Mueller–Hinton Agar using a sterile swab to form a suspension with a concentration equivalent to 0.5 McFarland. Following this, 20 µL of the extract was added to filter paper discs and then placed on the agar surface in each dish. Sterile distilled water served as the negative control, while Chloramphenicol (30 µg) was used as the positive control. The inhibition zone diameter, including the disc, was measured in millimeters113.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

MICs and MBCs were determined using the microdilution technique in 96-well microtiter plates with a few modifications mentioned by Ed-Dra et al. (2018). Briefly, 40 μL of CLEE was serially diluted twofold in sterile distilled water. Subsequently, 40 μL of the bacterial strain suspension (108 CFU/mL) was added, followed by 120 μL of Muller-Hinton broth. A well containing sterile distilled water with CLEE served as a negative control, and a bacterial suspension with Muller Hinton broth was used as a positive control. After incubating the microplates at 37 °C for 24 h, 40 μL of 2, 3, 5-triphenyl tetrazolium chloride-TTC (0.2 g/mL) was added to each well, followed by further incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. To assess the Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC), 5 μL from each well with no visible growth was sub-cultured on Muller Hinton Agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. MBC was demarcated as the lowest concentration at which no bacterial colonies formed114.

Cytotoxic activity toward breast cancer cell lines

Cell culture

Two distinct breast cancer cell lines were used in this study: estrogen receptor-positive MCF-7 cells and estrogen receptor-negative MDA-MB-436 and MDA-MB-231 cells, the latter representing models of triple-negative breast cancer. All cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Molsheim, France). Standard cell culture protocols recommended by the supplier were strictly followed to ensure optimal cell viability and maintenance34.

Cell viability by MTT assay

The 3-(4,5- dimethylthiazo-l-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay was used to assess the potential of CLEE to inhibit cancer cell growth, following the methods outlined by Chaudhary et al.115. Exponentially growing MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, and MDA-MB-436 human breast cancer cell lines were seeded into 96-well microplates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 100 µL of culture medium. The cells were incubated for 24 h to allow for attachment. The tested plant extracts were initially dissolved in 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and subsequently diluted with culture medium to achieve a range of final concentrations (6.25 to 300 µg/mL). Cisplatin was included as a positive control and prepared at concentrations ranging from 0.39 to 50 µg/mL.

Following the 24-h incubation, cells were treated with the respective extract or control solutions and incubated for an additional 72 h. The control group received medium containing 0.1% DMSO only. After the treatment period, 200 µL of fresh medium was added to each well, followed by the addition of 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in phosphate-buffered saline, PBS). The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to allow for formazan crystal formation.

After incubation, the medium was carefully removed, and 100 µL of DMSO was added to each well to solubilize the formazan. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm using an HT Multi-Detection Microplate Reader (Bio-Tek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). Cell viability was assessed by calculating the percentage of viable cells relative to the untreated control group, according to the method described by Tikent et al.106\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{Cell viability }\left(\text{\%}\right)=100- \left[\left(\frac{{\text{A}}_{0}-{\text{A}}_{\text{t}}}{{\text{A}}_{0}}\right) \times 100\right]$$\end{document}

A0 represents cells treated with medium containing 0.1% DMSO, while At refers to cells treated with the extracts at varying doses. A solution containing 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in culture medium was used as the negative control. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values were determined using GraphPad Prism software version 8.01, with cisplatin serving as the reference compound. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from human blood samples through density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll-Paque, following the manufacturer’s protocol (Capricorn Scientific).

All procedures involving human participants were approved in advance by the institutional Research Ethics Committee (approval numbers 03/22-LAPABE-10 and 4 March 2022). To evaluate the cytotoxicity of the hydroethanolic extracts derived from carob leaves on PBMCs, the same treatment conditions and concentration ranges applied to the tumor cell lines were employed.

Molecular docking study

Protein, ligand preparation

The following three proteins were recovered from the Protein Data Bank (PDB): human dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) (PDB ID: 1DRF), glutathione reductase (GR) (PDB ID: 3DK9), and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) (PDB ID: 3W2S) (available at www.rcsb.org, accessed on 20 November 2024)116. Each structure was separately treated by removing water molecules, followed by the insertion of Kollman charges and polar hydrogens using AutoDockTools (ADT; version 1.5.7)117. For the molecular docking study, a grid was constructed with a point interval of 0.375 Å and dimensions of 40 × 40 × 40, centered on the x, y, and z coordinates to cover both the active sites and the adjacent regions of the proteins.

Specific phytochemicals were identified using HPLC–DAD in the hydro-ethanolic extract of carob leaves, along with the usual inhibitors in 3D SDF format: xanthene (CID:7107), vincristine (CID: 5978), and methotrexate (CID: 126941) (available online https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/)118. We evaluated the molecular docking of these phytochemicals as potential inhibitors of GR, DHFR, and EGFR. The ligands were transformed into PDB format using PyMOL (version 2.5.3) and subsequently transformed into pdbqt format with AutoDock Tools software (ADT; version 1.5.7, The Scripps Research Institute)117.

ADME studies

Evaluating the pharmacokinetic properties of a compound, including its absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME), is essential for understanding its behavior in the body. This involves examining the ability of a molecule to traverse cell membranes, interact with transporters and enzymes involved in excretion and absorption, and assess its metabolic stability from entry into the system to its eventual clearance using computational tools118. In this study, the SwissADME platform (available online:www.swissadme.ch, accessed on November 20, 2024)117 was used to conduct an inclusive analysis of the pharmacokinetic properties of the phytochemicals found in carob leaves and to investigate their potential as therapeutic agents.

Statistical analyses

Data analysis was executed using one-way ANOVA with GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 and SPSS 21.0 software, and principal component analysis (PCA) and bivariate Pearson correlation analysis were also conducted.

Conclusion and perspectives

This study demonstrates that ethanolic extract of carob leaves (CLEE) from the Béni-Mellal Khenifra region constitutes a rich source of bioactive compounds with promising biological activities. Quantitative analyses revealed high levels of total polyphenols (163.50 ± 9.25 mg GAE/g DM), flavonoids (22.23 ± 8.61 mg QE/g DM), and condensed tannins (34.35 ± 9.70 mg CE/g DM). HPLC–DAD profiling identified several phenolic compounds, including cinnamic, ferulic, sinapic, and succinic acids, as well as rutin a key molecule further confirmed by molecular docking to contribute significantly to antioxidant activity.

CLEE exhibited potent antioxidant activity in vitro, with IC₅₀ values of 0.293 ± 0.034 mg/mL and 19.48 ± 2.47 mg/mL for DPPH and ABTS assays, respectively, and a total antioxidant capacity of 189.01 ± 7.18 µg AAE/mg. The extract also demonstrated strong antibacterial properties against a variety of pathogenic strains, supported by both disc diffusion and microdilution results. Moreover, CLEE showed selective cytotoxic effects on MCF-7 breast cancer cells, with an IC50 value of 28.26 ± 3.15 µg/mL, suggesting its potential role in anticancer therapy. In silico ADME analysis further confirmed the drug-likeness and oral bioavailability of the major detected compounds, in compliance with Lipinski’s rule of five.

These findings substantiate the therapeutic potential of carob leaf extract as a multifunctional agent with antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anticancer properties. The valorisation of wild carob leaves, often regarded as agricultural waste, may represent a sustainable strategy for the development of functional foods, nutraceuticals, and phytopharmaceuticals.

Future research should aim to validate these findings through in vivo studies and clinical investigations to better assess the safety, efficacy, and bioavailability of CLEE in biological systems. Investigating the synergistic interactions among its major bioactives and exploring different formulation strategies could enhance its stability and therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, biotechnological applications such as nanoencapsulation or incorporation into active food packaging may open innovative avenues for industrial exploitation.
