Polygenic risk scores and LDL cholesterol: Improving precision in primary cardiovascular prevention
Authors: Jamal S. Rana, Mahsima Shabani
Published: 2026

Low‑density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‑C) is a fundamental anchor for coronary artery disease (CAD) prevention. LDL‑lowering therapies have been well-proven to reduce incidence of CAD. Concomitantly, it has been long recognized that LDL‑C and traditional risk factors do not fully account for interindividual variability. Many first CAD events occur in individuals with LDL‑C below the treatment thresholds, while others with marked hypercholesterolemia remain event‑free for decades.

Studies on inherited risk of CAD estimated through polygenic risk scores (PRS), have been a substantial addition to the traditional risk factors. PRS quantification initially involved summation of independent common variants, yet these scores are not clinically implemented. Recent study by Iribarren et al [1] and now in current issue study by Harper et al., provide complementary and timely evidence that PRS materially enhance LDL‑C–based risk stratification and are clinically relevant to primary prevention [2].

Joint consideration of LDL‑C and polygenic risk

In a large, statin‑naïve, real‑world cohort with nearly 14 years of follow‑up, Iribarren and colleagues demonstrate that PRS modulated the association of LDL-C with incident CHD such that there was a significantly increased risk even at LDL-C levels of 100 mg/dL and above among individuals with high polygenic risk. Furthermore, we found a remarkably increased risk (HR: >3.5) with the combination of high polygenic risk and LDL ≥190 mg/dL [1]. Harper and colleagues extend these observations using a prevention‑oriented framework that is particularly relevant for clinical practice [2]. Rather than focusing only on relative risk gradients, they benchmark joint LDL‑C × PRS strata against the guideline‑defining LDL‑C ≥190 mg/dL group considered as severe hypercholesterolemia, where treatment decisions are rarely debated. Their findings show that individuals with LDL‑C between 100 and 160 mg/dL and high PRS (14.8% of the study population) have CAD risk that is comparable to this benchmark. These patterns were consistent across age strata, robust to sensitivity analyses, and replicated in an independent U.S. cohort. These studies show that PRS identify clinically important heterogeneity within LDL‑C categories, particularly in ranges where treatment decisions are often uncertain.

PRS and subclinical CAD: Complementary and not competing tools

Coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring is an important adjunct to CAD risk assessment and particularly refining treatment decisions in middle‑aged and older adults. Although subclinical CAD identified by either method reflects aggregated inherited, acquired and environmental risk of CAD, it is highly time dependent. In contrast, PRS can be assessed at any age, including in young adulthood, long before atherosclerosis becomes detectable by imaging. Subclinical CAD primarily refines short‑ to intermediate‑term risk of clinical events, whereas PRS inform lifelong susceptibility and align more naturally with primordial and early primary prevention, providing a stronger risk prediction in young adults [3].

In a genotype-first PROACT sub study, Abou-Karam et al. have shown that about half of patients with low traditional risk score and high PRS —otherwise not treatment-eligible—had subclinical CAD [4]. PRS therefore addresses a gap by determining elevated lifetime risk when traditional risk calculators and imaging are least informative.

Collectively, PRS and CAC should be considered complementary rather than competing tools.

The findings from Iribarren et al. and Harper et al. suggest that PRS estimation could guide screening strategies to begin earlier, particularly in genetically susceptible individuals with deceptively low short‑term risk.

Why this matters for preventive cardiology

From a preventive cardiology perspective, the value of PRS lies not in replacing LDL‑C thresholds, but in improving risk stratification where traditional risk factors are equivocal or discordant. One of the most debatable and yet often seen groups in clinical practice is individuals with moderate LDL‑C elevations (100–160 mg/dL) who fall into gray zones of risk assessment, particularly younger adults, or those with family history of CAD [5]. Importantly, the approach used by Harper et al.—risk equivalence to LDL‑C ≥190 mg/dL—aligns closely with how preventive decisions are made. Identifying individuals whose risk approximates that of a group already considered appropriate for treatment provides a clinically intuitive rationale for earlier or more intensive prevention, even when LDL‑C is below traditional thresholds.

Risk identification versus treatment benefit

As with any risk‑stratification tool, risk identification must be distinguished from treatment benefit. Neither study demonstrates that individuals with moderate LDL‑C and high PRS derive the same benefit from lipid‑lowering therapy as those with LDL‑C ≥190 mg/dL. However, preventive cardiology has long accepted risk‑enhancing tools without requiring randomized trials stratified by each factor. PRS fits this precedent. They identify individuals with substantially elevated lifetime risk not captured by LDL‑C or short‑term risk calculators alone. Post hoc trial analyses further suggest that individuals with higher PRS experience greater absolute benefit from LDL‑lowering therapies, supporting the biological plausibility of earlier intervention.

A key unresolved matter is the clinically justifiable age for PRS assessment. In a CARDIA cohort sub study, it was shown that the incidence and severity of CAD is proportional to cumulative exposure to LDL-C [6]. While lifestyle modification could begin early in individuals with high PRS, further studies are needed to define the earliest plausible age for pharmacologic intervention.

Conclusion

LDL‑C remains central to CAD prevention, but the evidence presented by Iribarren et al. and Harper et al. demonstrates that LDL‑C does not operate in isolation. PRS provide a stable, biologically grounded measure of inherited susceptibility that meaningfully refines LDL‑C–based risk assessment, particularly in individuals with moderate LDL‑C levels whose risk is often underestimated. PRS can be assessed at any age. By identifying elevated lifetime risk early, PRS align with primordial and early primary prevention.

Importantly, improved risk resolution using PRS also coincide with an expanding therapeutic landscape. While statins remain first‑line therapy, potent injectable lipid‑lowering agents, including PCSK9 inhibitors and emerging RNA‑based therapies, enables durable LDL‑C reduction beyond statins alone. In selected individuals with high PRS, long anticipated exposure time, and suboptimal LDL‑C control, earlier combination therapy may reduce cumulative CAD burden.
