The BSRA framework for dual sparse parameter efficient fine tuning with block structured gating and rank adaptation
Authors: Wang Jian, Guorong Chen, Hu Hong, Yixuan Zhang, Chongling Du, Zhang Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Current large-scale language models face severe resource constraints when applied to downstream tasks. While full-fledged fine-tuning enhances model performance, it incurs substantial computational costs. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) techniques address resource limitations while maintaining high model efficiency; for instance, the emergence of low-rank adaptive methods like LoRA demonstrates outstanding parameter efficiency and model performance. However, fixed low-rank approaches struggle to adapt to varying parameter demands across layers, while unstructured sparsity, though capable of further parameter compression, often suffers from training instability and limited practical gains due to irregular shapes. To address this, this paper proposes BSRA, a dual-sparsity parameter-efficient fine-tuning framework integrating block-level structural sparsity with dynamic rank adaptation. BSRA achieves dual sparsity across rank and structural dimensions: at the coarse-grained level, it employs sensitivity-based dynamic rank pruning and non-linear cubic scheduling to smoothly remove low-contribution rank channels; at the fine-grained level, it introduces differentiable evolutionary block-level gating to structurally filter LoRA’s output features. To mitigate training oscillations during sparsification, we construct a unified constrained optimisation framework. This achieves synergistic optimisation of dual sparsity through distributed adjustment of rank scaling coefficients and continuous control of gated sparsity, enabling the model to adaptively converge towards a clear, stable low-rank structure. Experimental results demonstrate that BSRA matches or outperforms strong low-rank baselines with fewer trainable parameters, exhibiting outstanding parameter efficiency and task adaptability.

Introduction

Large pre-trained models based on Transformers have achieved breakthrough progress in natural language processing and multimodal tasks. Bidirectional encoding models exemplified by BERT1 significantly enhanced performance in language comprehension tasks. Auto-regressive generative large models (such as the GPT2 series) further extended the pre-training–fine-tuning paradigm to open-domain generation and complex reasoning scenarios. A succession of subsequent models (including larger-scale, data-rich variants like RoBERTa3) has continuously refined pre-training objectives and data utilisation efficiency. The emergence of open-source large models in recent years (such as the LlaMA4 series) has provided flexible foundational platforms for downstream research and applications. As parameter scales expand to billions or even hundreds of billions, direct full fine-tuning proves prohibitively costly in terms of GPU memory consumption, training duration, and model storage. Moreover, deploying such models across multiple tasks and scenarios necessitates maintaining a complete parameter copy for each task, introducing additional burdens in version management and maintenance. Against this backdrop, a central challenge in current large model research and application is how to achieve efficient adaptation to diverse downstream tasks with minimal incremental parameters, while preserving the general capabilities of the pre-trained model.

To mitigate these conflicts, Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) has gradually emerged as the mainstream approach. The overarching strategy involves freezing as many pre-trained parameters as possible while introducing only a small number of trainable increments within the model’s local structure or low-dimensional subspaces. For instance, the Adapter5 method achieves task specialisation by inserting lightweight bottleneck modules onto selected Transformer layers; Prompt/prefix methods6,7 inject soft prompt vectors into inputs or intermediate layers to modulate model behaviour with minimal parameter adjustments; low-rank adaptation8 reparametrises weight increments from linear transformations as low-rank decompositions, updating only sparse low-rank parameters while preserving frozen weights. In scenarios with relatively abundant computational resources, such methods can already achieve performance approaching or even matching that of full-parameter fine-tuning while significantly reducing GPU memory and storage overhead. However, from the perspective of model internal structure and parameter distribution, mainstream approaches often implicitly make several strong design assumptions: layers are typically treated as homogeneous units, employing a uniform bottleneck dimension or low-rank size; channels, attention heads, or sub-blocks within the same layer are treated as a unified whole, with their importance to the task assumed to be approximately equal; when adjusting low-rank capacity and selecting structural units, reliance is placed on mutually independent hyperparameters and heuristic rules, lacking a unified resource allocation perspective. As model scale and task complexity continue to increase, the limitations of this coarse-grained structural design and near-uniform parameter distribution are becoming increasingly apparent9.

From a spectral perspective, existing efficient parameter fine-tuning methods such as rank adaptation and dynamic rank allocation employ singular value spectra, gradient statistics, or importance metrics during training to allocate varying degrees of low-rank capacity across different layers or even directions within a given overall parameter budget, thereby enhancing parameter utilisation efficiency. From a structural perspective, structured sparsity research employs holistic pruning of network structural units at varying granularities–such as channels, attention heads, blocks, or N:M ratios–to compress model parameters and floating-point operations without significant performance degradation. These approaches are highly complementary in philosophy: the former focuses on the magnitude of rank updates at specific positions, while the latter determines which positions warrant retention and updating10–12. However, in most existing approaches, low-rank adaptation and structural sparsity are either treated as two independent subproblems, optimised separately at different stages or modules, or merely concatenated in a simplistic manner. Rarely are structural choices and rank allocation considered as collaborative decisions under a unified budget constraint within a coherent framework. This approach leads to difficulties in achieving a global balance between the structural and spectral dimensions of the parameter budget. Furthermore, when simultaneously tightening both structural and rank degrees of freedom, it lacks stable contraction paths and reasonable regularisation constraints, readily inducing issues such as gradient oscillations and training instability.

Based on the aforementioned research, this paper proposes a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method grounded in block-level structural sparsity and dynamic rank adaptation. The core idea is to no longer treat the selection of positions for updating and the assignment of rank to these positions as two independent subproblems. Instead, within the same LoRA branch, these two degrees of freedom are unified within a dual sparse framework for collaborative modelling. On the structural dimension, block-level structures are partitioned using output channels as fundamental units. Learnable continuous gating parameters are introduced to screen and reweight blocks via soft gating during early training, progressively hardening within a unified pruning window. On the spectral dimension, the effective rank within each layer and block is dynamically adjusted based on a unified importance metric. Under a fixed overall parameter budget, this enables the finite low-rank capacity to adaptively concentrate on structurally significant regions. To ensure smooth evolution of dual sparsity during training, this paper constructs a joint objective encompassing task loss, rank distribution stabilisation terms, and gated sparsity regularisation. Related work

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) aims to reduce training and storage costs by freezing the parameters of the pre-trained backbone while training only a small number of newly added parameters or a selected subset of parameters to achieve downstream adaptation. Based on the injection method of trainable degrees of freedom, existing approaches can be broadly categorized into: module injection and constrained parameter updates. Each approach strikes different trade-offs between expressiveness, controllability, and deployment convenience.

Module injection approaches achieve adaptation by introducing lightweight trainable structures into the network, such as Adapter5 and its multi-task fusion variant AdapterFusion13, as well as further compression and structural modifications like Compacter14 and Ladder Side-Tuning15. The prompt learning direction guides model behavior by learning a small number of prompt vectors6,7,16,17. This approach offers advantages in minimal intrusion to backbone weights and flexible transferability. However, its key capacity is typically determined by static hyperparameters such as bottleneck dimensions and prompt length. When capacity requirements vary significantly across layers and modules, it often leads to allocation imbalances–insufficient capacity at critical positions and redundant capacity at non-critical ones.

Constrained parameter update paths emphasize updating only a small number of parameters or restricting updates to low-dimensional and sparse subspaces. BitFit18 achieves certain effects by updating only biases ; Diff Pruning enhances sparsity by learning sparse differential increments and introducing approximate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_0$$\end{document} constraints, while the “intrinsic dimension” perspective explains the feasibility of efficient adaptation through low-dimensional subspaces19,20. Among these, LoRA8 freezes original weights via low-rank reparameterization, becoming one of the most widely adopted PEFT schemes. It has spawned derivatives including UniPELT21 (unifying multiple PEFT variants), AdaLoRA9 (budget-adaptive allocation), DePT22, QLoRA23 combining quantization to reduce memory usage, and stability/initialization improvements (LoRA+24, PiSSA25, ResLoRA26). However, these methods often still assume the structure positions are fixed (where to update and how to update the shape) and primarily adjust update strength through continuous variables like rank/magnitude/scaling. When both structural and budget constraints must be satisfied simultaneously, a unified collaborative mechanism remains lacking.

Rank adaptive

After LoRA became the mainstream PEFT paradigm, the core issue of rank adaptation is not whether variable rank is feasible, but how to allocate ranks to truly sensitive layers or dimensions within a fixed budget and redistribute them stably during training. Existing methods have made progress in inter-layer rank allocation, yet significant differences remain in granularity, coupling mechanisms, and stability protocols.

From a methodological perspective, one class of approaches enhances expressive power through decomposition and recomposition, such as breaking down low-rank updates into multiple sub-branches and recombining them during training or inference (e.g., ME-LoRA27, PRILoRA28). Another class explicitly learns rank distributions, dynamically allocating budgets across layers based on importance or sensitivity signals (e.g., dynamic rank allocation in DoRA29). While these strategies improve budget utilization, most still operate at the layer or module level, making it difficult to capture the differential contributions of different channel blocks or feature subspaces within the same layer. Additionally, rank adjustments often rely on heuristic metrics or stage-specific strategies, which can easily conflict with other structural constraints.

To mitigate training instability or further compress parameters, some studies have modified the LoRA branch from a parameterization and optimization perspective: for instance, enhancing trainability and stability through amplitude or direction decomposition30, reducing trainable degrees of freedom by sharing projection and scaling vectors31, or introducing importance evaluators to redistribute rank budgets across modules32. Other work has highlighted the limitations of the low-rank assumption in knowledge-intensive tasks and explored higher-capacity alternative update forms33. Related theoretical and uncertainty analyses provide rationale for “why to layer or vary ranks,” but typically do not directly offer unified training objectives that synergize with structural sparsity34,35.

Furthermore, rank adaptation begins to intersect with gate selection and sparsity or pruning: Mixture of LoRA Experts36 employs gating to achieve sample-level expert composition, while RankAdaptor37 performs hierarchical rank allocation for pruned sparse large models; Methods like LoSA38, SLoPe39, and SoRA40 attempt to link rank adjustment with sparsity constraints, yet many implementations still follow multi-stage, loosely coupled paradigms–lacking unified coordination mechanisms for how rank is adjusted, how structure is pruned, and how both jointly satisfy budget constraints while maintaining training smoothness. Our BSRA addresses this gap by treating rank scheduling as a spectral-dimensional budget allocation while introducing block-level structural gating. By jointly constraining their evolution under a unified objective, we achieve finer-grained update control and more stable training processes within a fixed budget.

Structured sparsity

Another key parallel development alongside rank adaptivity is structured sparsity. Its goal is to selectively zero out portions of weights at structural granularities–such as channels, heads, blocks, or N:M ratios–while preserving accuracy as much as possible. This reduces the number of effective parameters and makes computations more amenable to hardware acceleration. Early research approached this from the perspectives of sparse trainability and recoverable sparse subnetworks. For instance, the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis41 suggested sparse subnetworks within parameterized networks that could be trained independently under the original training schedule; RigL42 continuously explored the structural space under a fixed budget through periodic pruning and connection growth during training; Dynamic Sparse Reparameterization43 redistributes non-zero parameters between layers during training to match varying capacity demands; Movement Pruning44 measures importance by tracking weight update magnitudes during fine-tuning, making it more adaptable to transfer scenarios.

In Transformer scenarios, structured sparsity further manifests as the selection of attention heads/channels/blocks. Pruning methods that select key heads based on attention head importance or multi-head self-attention indicate significant variations in structural unit importance. Head/channel/block-level pruning can partially preserve performance while offering structured acceleration potential45,46; Block Pruning47 reduces inference costs while maintaining accuracy by segmenting and pruning matrices or channels. A common feature of these methods is that structural selection itself often resembles discrete decisions (retaining/removing a specific head, channel, or block). Consequently, they typically rely on heuristic metrics, staged procedures, or additional scheduling strategies to mitigate training oscillations. However, the synergy between structural selection and capacity updates remains unsystematically modeled.

In recent years, the integration of structured sparsity with PEFT has become increasingly prevalent, yet loose coupling issues persist. LoRAPrune combines structured pruning with LoRA low-rank adaptation: it employs LoRA weights or gradients to construct importance metrics guiding pruning of the backbone structure, while simultaneously using LoRA to mitigate performance degradation caused by pruning. This achieves a unified approach to structural compression and efficient parameter fine-tuning48. Additionally, FourierFT49 imposes sparsity constraints on weight increments from a frequency-domain perspective; LoRA-Sparse50 reduces computational load in multimodal models by filtering key attention channels via low-rank projection; LoRA-drop51 elevates pruning granularity to the layer level and performs structured deletion based on the contribution of LoRA module outputs. SHIR52 achieves high-sparsity adaptation without additional inference overhead by sparsifying a minimal proportion of base weights; SuperLoRA53 enhances capture of parameter structural information through tensor decomposition and grouped folding; SVFT54 constructs weight increments using frozen heterovectors and sparse singular value coefficient matrices. These works demonstrate the complementary nature of structural sparsity and low-rank updates. However, most methods still treat the retention of structural blocks as relatively independent binary decisions, while update capacities (e.g., LoRA rank, channel count) remain largely determined by static configuration or empirical rules. When both exist concurrently, common practices involve separate scheduling and separate regularization, leading to inconsistent optimization objectives and potentially conflicting training dynamics.

This paper’s BSRA addresses the aforementioned gaps by employing block-level structural gating as a trainable structural selection mechanism. It synergistically constrains this with dynamic rank allocation under unified budget and unified regularization objectives, enabling structural retention/deletion and capacity scaling to co-evolve within the same optimization process. This achieves dual compression and adaptation with finer granularity, structural interpretability, and smoother training under a fixed budget. Methods

This section details the design of the BSRA framework. Figure 1 presents a comparison between LoRA and BSRA. Unlike standard LoRA, which employs low-rank decomposition with a fixed rank r, BSRA constructs a dual sparse mechanism across two dimensions. On the rank dimension, each rank-1 component (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_i$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_i$$\end{document}) is assigned a scaling factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Lambda _i$$\end{document} to measure its importance; on the structural dimension, a learnable gate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_k$$\end{document} controls the activation state of output blocks. This design enables different layers to autonomously discover their optimal rank configuration, enhancing parameter efficiency while maintaining performance.

Evolutionary block-level gating

Existing PEFT methods typically apply low-rank updates uniformly within a layer, implicitly assuming that output channels (or sub-blocks) contribute equally to downstream adaptation. BSRA relaxes this assumption by introducing block-level gates on the LoRA output dimension: we partition the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{\text {out}}$$\end{document} channels into K contiguous blocks, and assign one learnable scalar gate to each block. During early training, gates remain soft (continuous in (0, 1)) to avoid prematurely suppressing potentially useful blocks; as training progresses, gates are gradually hardened to binary block masks within a pruning window, resulting in a stable block-structured sparse configuration. In this way, block selection becomes a smooth, learnable process rather than an abrupt pruning decision, enabling refined control over LoRA updates while improving training stability.

We implement block gating on the output dimension of the LoRA branch, so that the update is selectively modulated at the block level during training. The standard LoRA is defined as:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} W = W_0 + \Delta W = W_0 + AB \end{aligned}$$\end{document}To maintain consistency with subsequent gating mechanisms, we explicitly introduce the diagonal scaling matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Lambda \in \mathbb {R}^{r \times r}$$\end{document} in the low-rank decomposition, writing it as:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} W = W_0 + \Delta W = W_0 + B\Lambda A \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B \in \mathbb {R}^{d_{out} \times r}, \quad A \in \mathbb {R}^{r \times d_{out}}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_0 \in \mathbb {R}^{d_{out} \times d_{in}}$$\end{document} denote the weights of the baseline linear layer. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Lambda = I_r$$\end{document} (where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_r$$\end{document} represents the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r \times r$$\end{document} identity matrix), this constitutes the standard LoRA format; when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Lambda = diag(s)$$\end{document}, it represents row-wise channel scaling.

To achieve this block-wise scalable approach, we introduce a continuously differentiable gating parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g = \sigma (\theta ) \in [0, 1]$$\end{document} (where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} is a learnable parameter) to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta W$$\end{document} during the training phase. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g = \sigma (\theta )$$\end{document} is generated from the learnable parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} via a Sigmoid function, enabling continuous control over the importance of each channel and thereby implementing soft gating during training. During the early training stages, the gating parameter g is progressively adjusted to converge towards the target sparsity level in later phases, ensuring the model’s sparse structure stabilises smoothly. Specifically, this process gradually controls the network architecture by modulating the sparsity of the gating, thereby guaranteeing structural stability and efficiency throughout training. As training progresses, the gating values progressively harden, i.e., g is fixed to either 0 or 1 through a thresholding operation, thereby yielding a stable and sparse structure upon training completion.

Within each layer of the standard Transformer, the computational sub-layer comprises multi-head attention (incorporating linear projections of q, k, v and the output projection) alongside two feedforward layers (upper projection and lower projection); we inject gated LoRA wrapping at the aforementioned projection points. We introduce an evolutionary block-level gating mechanism into the output layer of the LoRA branch, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Specifically, the output dimension is partitioned into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K = \lceil d_{out} / b \rceil$$\end{document} contiguous blocks, each containing b channels. Each block corresponds to a shared scalar gating value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_k^{blk}$$\end{document}, collectively forming the block-level gating vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g^{blk} = [g_1^{blk}, g_2^{blk}, \dots , g_K^{blk}]$$\end{document}. At the outset of training, its corresponding learnable parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta ^{blk}$$\end{document} is set to zero, marking a neutral starting point for structural evolution. Subsequently, the gating vector is mapped via a Sigmoid function to continuous weights within the (0,1) interval, with an initial output of 0.5. This design prevents excessive suppression of LoRA updates during early training, ensuring all functional blocks possess equal competitive opportunities in the initial evolutionary phase. As training progresses, gradient signals progressively drive the gating values of important channels towards significant differentiation.3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} g^{blk} = \sigma (\theta ^{blk}) \in (0, 1)^K, \quad \theta ^{blk(0)} = 0 \Rightarrow g^{blk(0)} = 0.5 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}During forward propagation, the gating parameters achieve dimensional alignment with the LoRA outputs through tensor broadcasting mechanisms. To simplify the mathematical representation, a gating expansion operator R may be defined, mapping the gating vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g^{blk}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R(g^{blk})$$\end{document}. This enables structured modulation of the LoRA outputs via block-level gating operators, thereby implementing hierarchical sparse control over adaptive updates within the feature space.4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R(g^{blk}) = \begin{bmatrix} \underbrace{g_1^{blk}, \dots , g_1^{blk}}_{b},&\underbrace{g_2^{blk}, \dots , g_2^{blk}}_{b},&\dots ,&\underbrace{g_K^{blk}, \dots , g_K^{blk}}_{b} \end{bmatrix}^T \in \mathbb {R}^{d_{out}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}During training, LoRA was updated as follows:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta W_{blk} = R(g^{blk}) (B \Lambda A), \quad (\Delta W_{blk})_{ij} = g_{k(j)}^{blk} (B \Lambda A)_{ij} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where i denotes the batch index, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$j \in \{1, 2, \dots , d_{out}\}$$\end{document} represents the output dimension index, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k(j) = \lfloor j/b \rfloor$$\end{document} indicates the block index to which the jth output dimension belongs–meaning all channels within the same block share a common gating value. During late training, Top-K hardening converts continuous \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g^{blk}$$\end{document} gates into binary block masks, enabling structured sparsification of LoRA outputs to achieve block-level activation control. This block-level gating mechanism maintains end-to-end learnability while implementing structured sparse control over LoRA feature outputs. Synergising with the dynamic rank adaptation mechanism, it enhances parameter efficiency and compression performance simultaneously at both structural and rank dimensions.

Dynamic rank adaptive

A fixed LoRA rank allocates the same spectral capacity to all layers, even though their contributions to the downstream task can differ substantially. BSRA therefore treats rank as a budgeted resource and gradually removes low-importance rank-1 components during training, so that the remaining capacity concentrates on more informative directions. To avoid unstable decisions caused by noisy single-step gradients, we smooth component importance over time and shrink the target rank with a scheduled contraction rather than abrupt pruning.

Due to the parameter update noise introduced by stochastic gradient descent, instantaneous scores based on single-step weights often exhibit significant fluctuations, potentially leading to misjudgements of rank importance. This mechanism, by integrating historically accumulated contributions, enables the score to smoothly reflect the long-term importance trends of rank components, effectively suppressing the interference of short-term training noise.

Having established stable importance metrics, the subsequent critical issue concerns determining the pruning rhythm. Overly aggressive pruning early in training can degrade optimisation and may destabilise convergence. Therefore, this paper introduces a dynamic scheduling mechanism for target sparsity, employing a cubic schedule within the pruning window to smooth the rank reduction process. Crucially, while this rank-dimensional compression eliminates global spectral redundancy, local structural redundancy may persist within the retained low-rank subspace. This precisely sets the stage for the evolutionary gating introduced in the preceding subsection–enabling further refined structural pruning within the compact subspaces determined by rank adaptation. Their synergistic operation achieves optimal configuration of the model parameters.

Dual sparse joint optimisation

Rank pruning and structural gating are coupled: shrinking rank changes the effective contribution of LoRA components, while gating changes where rank capacity should be allocated. If optimised independently, their interactions can destabilise training–e.g., early fluctuations in rank scaling can bias importance estimation, and overly aggressive gating can prematurely saturate and restrict learning. BSRA therefore introduces a joint optimisation objective that explicitly stabilises rank dynamics and constrains the evolution of gating sparsity, so that both dimensions contract in a coordinated and smooth manner throughout training.

Building on the proposed structural gating and dynamic rank adaptation modules, we formulate a dual-sparsity joint optimisation strategy by augmenting the task loss with two regularisation terms: (i) a rank-variance stabilisation term to prevent abnormal amplification along individual rank channels, and (ii) a gated sparsity regulariser to control the global sparsity level via a scheduled target. The resulting objective is:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}(t) = \mathscr {L}_{task} + \alpha (R_{rank}(t) + R_{gate}(t)) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The rank regularisation is defined as:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R_{rank} = \frac{1}{L} \sum _{\ell =1}^{L} \text {Var}\left( s^{(\ell )}(t)\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where L denotes the number of linear layers to be replaced, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s^{(\ell )}(t) \in \mathbb {R}^{r_{\ell }}$$\end{document} represents the rank scaling vector for the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell$$\end{document}-th layer. The variance is computed along the rank dimension and subsequently averaged across layers. This term suppresses abnormal amplification in individual channels, prevents the model from collapsing into a single low-rank subspace, and enhances separability between channels. The gated regularisation term is expressed as:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} G_{\ell }} \sigma \left( \theta _i^{(\ell )}\right) - \left( 1 - s_{\text {gate}}(t)\right) \right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The gated regularisation term controls the average activation rate of all layer gating parameters via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} The regularisation term acts as a dynamic density controller: imposing penalties to suppress activation when density exceeds thresholds, and conversely providing rewards to encourage exploration. Driven by this mechanism, the gating parameters of each layer gradually converge towards stable 0 or 1 states, ensuring the smooth progression of the sparsification process.

Under the gated regularisation, BSRA coordinates structural gating and rank pruning through a staggered contraction schedule. Structural sparsity starts early (around the first 10% of training) and is implemented as periodic hardening that gradually turns soft gating probabilities into binary block selections. Rank pruning is concentrated within the mid-training window (approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$15\%-50\%$$\end{document}), so that rank contraction and structural selection can adapt to each other while avoiding abrupt changes at the beginning. After this window, rank scaling is frozen (at the 50% mark) to prevent interference with the late-stage convergence of gating. During the warm-up stage, sparsity constraints are kept mild to allow sufficient exploration and to avoid premature saturation. When the hardening trigger is satisfied, structural hardening commences:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {step} \bmod T_{\text {period}} = 0 \quad \text {and} \quad \text {step} > T_{\text {total}} \cdot r_{\text {warmup}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{period}$$\end{document} denotes the number of steps for the hardening phase, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{total}$$\end{document} represents the total training steps, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{warmup}$$\end{document} indicates the warm-up ratio. Subsequently, based on the current target sparsity level, the Top-K strategy is employed to retain the top k channels according to their importance, thereby generating the corresponding binary mask:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} h_j = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1, & j \in \mathscr {K} \\ 0, & \text {otherwise} \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {K}$$\end{document} is the selected set. To prevent residual gradients from affecting the hardening outcome, pruned gates are assigned large negative values so that their activation probabilities remain near zero. During the interval in which structural gating hardens, rank pruning may still update dynamically according to the global threshold; to avoid mutual interference, we freeze rank scaling after the predefined point (50% of training). Subsequently, gating hardening enters its convergence phase, and both sparsity processes gradually stabilise in the later stages of training.

Following the convergence of structural gating, the binarised gating vector is applied to LoRA’s output projection to mask deactivated blocks, yielding the final sparse update representation. This converts the soft sparsity patterns learned during training into a fixed block-wise structure at inference time. As a result, BSRA yields a stable and interpretable dual-sparse configuration with explicit rank allocation and block-level structural selection.

Experiments

Datasets

To comprehensively evaluate the adaptability and generalisation performance of the proposed BSRA framework across multiple natural language processing tasks, this paper conducts experiments on several classic natural language understanding (NLU) and generation (NLG) benchmark datasets. We selected three datasets: the GLUE55 benchmark, SQuAD56, and Xsum57, covering diverse task types including classification, question answering, and generation. This provides a multi-dimensional testing environment for analysing model performance.

Unlike traditional multi-sentence summarisation datasets, XSum emphasises generating highly compressed, semantically complete, and linguistically natural ultra-short summaries. These summaries capture the core essence of the article’s content and often require greater synthesis and rewriting skills than mere content extraction.

SQuAD 1.1 contains over 100,000 question-answer pairs spanning more than 500 articles, where questions were formulated by crowdsourced workers and answers were extracted from textual segments within article paragraphs. SQuAD 2.0 expands upon the SQuAD 1.1 dataset by introducing over 50,000 additional unanswerable questions. These questions were created by adversarial modifications made by crowdsourced workers to answerable questions, making them appear as though they have answers when in fact no valid answers exist within the text. The introduction of SQuAD 2.0 challenges models not only to retrieve answers but also to discern when no valid answer exists within the text, thereby increasing the complexity of model comprehension.

Xsum is a large-scale news corpus designed for extreme summarisation tasks, comprising approximately 227,000 articles from BBC News alongside their corresponding single-sentence human summaries. Each article is accompanied by a highly refined single-sentence overview authored by professional annotators. These summaries typically require substantial condensation, semantic integration, and linguistic rephrasing of the original content, rather than mere extraction of source sentences. Unlike traditional multi-sentence summarisation datasets, XSum emphasises generating highly compressed, semantically complete, and linguistically natural ultra-short summaries. These summaries capture the core essence of the article’s content and often require greater synthesis and rewriting skills than mere content extraction.

Implementations

All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 (24GB) graphics card, with model training and evaluation implemented using PyTorch and HuggingFace Transformers. RoBERTaBase and Bart base models served as foundational models, with fine-tuning configurations independently constructed for all downstream tasks.

Regarding optimisers, AdamW was employed for training with a weight decay of 0.01 and a gradient clipping threshold of 1.0. The learning rate accounted for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$6\%$$\end{document} of total training steps during the warm-up phase, followed by a linear decay strategy. Beyond the main branch parameters, the structural gating parameters within BSRA were grouped separately, with their learning rate set to twice the main learning rate to ensure greater update flexibility for gating during early training stages.

We set the scaling factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} of LoRA to 8, the dropout rate to 0.1, and the block size b to the default value of 8. For adaptive rank allocation, the initial rank is set to 1.5 times the final target rank, progressively contracting to the target rank during training via a predefined pruning window. The final target rank, learning rate, number of epochs, batch size, regularisation coefficient, and pruning interval are independently determined for each task based on validation set performance. Regarding input length, GLUE tasks uniformly employ 128 tokens, SQuAD v1/v2 utilise 384 tokens, and XSum utilise 768 tokens, all processed using HuggingFace’s default tokeniser.

Evaluation metrics

During the evaluation phase, this paper strictly adheres to the experimental setup outlined in DoRA, employing its standardised and widely recognised metrics for different task types. For the GLUE benchmark, we utilise multiple indicators–including Accuracy, F1 score, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and Pearson/Spearman correlation coefficients–in accordance with each sub-task’s official definition. This comprehensive approach measures the model’s performance across classification, sentence matching, semantic similarity, and natural language inference tasks. For the reading comprehension tasks SQuAD v1.1 and SQuAD v2.0, we employed Exact Match (EM) and F1 scores as evaluation metrics. These respectively measure the consistency between answer fragments and standard answers in terms of exact match and word-level overlap. Notably, v2.0 further assesses the model’s ability to provide no-answer responses in unanswerable scenarios. For the summary generation task XSum, we employ three metrics: ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L. These assess the similarity between generated text and reference summaries in terms of lexical coverage and sequence structure. The aforementioned metrics encompass multiple dimensions including classification accuracy, semantic consistency, extraction capability, and generation quality. They comprehensively reflect the model’s overall performance across both natural language understanding and natural language generation aspects.

Dual-sparsity evolution and effective computation distribution

To gain deeper insights into the evolutionary behavior of the proposed method during the compression process and its adaptive allocation strategy for computational resources within the model, this section presents a visual analysis of the model’s rank distribution under specific constraints. All experiments were conducted on the SST-2 dataset, with the target mean rank set to 8.

Fig. 3Evolution of gate distribution and block retention ratio during training.

Evolution of gate distribution and block retention ratio during training.

Figure 3 shows the evolution of gate distribution and block retention ratio during training. Panel (a) illustrates the probability density evolution of the gating signal throughout the training process. By tracking all gating parameters in real time, we observe a three-stage evolution of gating values: from “neutral dispersion” to “exploratory differentiation” and finally to “convergent focus.” In the early training phase, the high concentration of gating values around 0.5 indicates that the model initially maintains equal information flow weights across all output blocks, avoiding premature structural pruning that could suppress learning potential. As training progresses, driven by task loss gradients, the gating distribution begins to spread toward both sides. The heatmap reveals broader distribution bands, reflecting the model’s identification and reinforcement of key feature pathways while weakening redundant connections. Notably, around step 35K, the distribution undergoes a distinct “break” transformation, suggesting the model reached a critical point of structural reorganization at this stage. As training progresses into the late phase, the gating distribution stabilizes within an extremely narrow band near 0.5, with a noticeable accumulation of density in the extremely low-value region (close to 0). This phenomenon indicates that as target sparsity gradually increases, identified redundant blocks are systematically pruned, ultimately forcing the gating values to converge and achieving structural sparsity.

Figure 3b illustrates the spatiotemporal distribution characteristics of the block retention ratio across 12 Transformer layers, using the Query module as an example. Unlike the unidimensional evolution of the gating distribution, the block retention ratio exhibits a more complex non-monotonic dynamic pattern of “initial increase followed by decrease.” At the initial training stage, retention rates across all layers are extremely low, indicating the model rapidly identifies and prunes redundant blocks during sparse initialization. This contrasts with the high concentration observed during the early evolution of gating–though gating itself is “neutral,” regularization constraints drive preliminary structural selection. As training progresses, retention rates rapidly climb to a peak in intermediate layers. This “revival” phenomenon aligns with the mid-stage diffusion of the gate distribution, indicating that under early low-target sparsity constraints, task loss gradients dominate gate optimization, prompting the model to activate more blocks to fully capture downstream task features. In the late training stage, retention rates experience a significant decline, eventually stabilizing within the 30% to 50% range. This late-stage pruning phenomenon validates the core design of dual-sparsity co-optimization proposed in the paper: maintaining sufficient model capacity through loose sparsity constraints early on, followed by efficient compression via dynamically enhanced structural sparsity later. Notably, the color changes across different layers exhibit high synchrony, indicating that block-level gating is driven by a unified global strategy rather than independent evolution at each layer.

When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} is too large (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5 \times 10^{-4}$$\end{document}), excessive sparsity pressure erroneously suppresses useful feature channels, leading to significant performance degradation. Notably, the CoLA task (MCC metric) exhibits higher sensitivity to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} than SST-2. This may stem from the language acceptability judgment task requiring finer-grained feature encoding, where excessive sparsity regularization undermines the model’s ability to capture subtle syntactic nuances. Furthermore, model performance remains relatively stable within the range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 10^{-6}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 10^{-4}$$\end{document}, indicating that BSRA exhibits strong robustness within this interval and provides ample parameter tuning space for practical applications.

To validate the efficacy of each core component within the proposed BSRA framework, we conducted a comprehensive ablation analysis across two representative datasets: SST-2 (semantic task) and CoLA (syntactic task), with results presented in Table 6. First, we evaluated a baseline model incorporating only dynamic rank adaptation. This configuration already demonstrated commendable performance under high-budget settings. However, this coarse-grained compression solely at the spectral dimension failed to address the fine-grained structural redundancy within retained ranks, limiting the model’s potential for further breakthroughs on complex syntactic tasks. Building upon this foundation, we introduced evolutionary block-level gating, which steadily improved model performance. SST-2 accuracy increased to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$95.28\%$$\end{document}, while CoLA MCC surged to 65.65. Notably, this substantial improvement was achieved with a significant reduction in effective parameter count. This robustly demonstrates that, in high-budget scenarios, eliminating redundant structural blocks via gating mechanisms proves more efficient than merely preserving high-dimensional parameter spaces. Finally, by constructing a comprehensive BSRA framework through a further designed joint regularisation term, the model achieved optimal performance (SST-2 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$95.35\%$$\end{document} / CoLA \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$65.89\%$$\end{document}). Particularly on the CoLA task, this regularisation term yielded an additional + 0.24 point gain, indicating that explicit constraints on structural evolution are crucial for capturing complex syntactic features within high-dimensional parameter spaces. BSRA successfully achieves efficiency gains through parameter reduction, surpassing larger purely dynamic rank-based baselines while significantly compressing its parameter size.

Discussion and limitations

Although our experiments mainly cover encoder-based and encoder–decoder Transformers, BSRA is architecture-agnostic in that it operates exclusively on the LoRA update branch; therefore, it can be extended to decoder-only (GPT-like) Transformers without modifying the backbone computation graph. Under the unified notation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta W = B\Lambda A$$\end{document}, the spectral dimension (dynamic rank adaptation) can be retained unchanged: the importance of rank-1 components is computed from the low-rank factors and can be aggregated across layers to realise budget-constrained global rank contraction. On the structural dimension, the proposed evolutionary block-level gating is likewise directly applicable: output channels are partitioned into blocks with learnable block gates, which are progressively hardened into binary block masks within the hardening period. Notably, decoder-only specifics such as causal attention masking do not affect BSRA, since both rank contraction and block gating are defined on the parameter-update branch rather than on attention connectivity patterns.

From an optimisation perspective, decoder-only models often involve longer contexts and larger activation footprints, which may amplify gradient noise and make overly aggressive sparsification more prone to oscillations; hence, a more conservative and smoother contraction-and-hardening schedule can be adopted. We leave a systematic empirical validation on large-scale decoder-only LLMs to future work.

Conclusions

This paper proposes BSRA, an efficient fine-tuning framework that integrates block-level structural sparsity with dynamic rank adaptation for dual sparsity. The method employs sensitivity-based dynamic rank adaptation for coarse-grained spectral dimension reduction, whilst introducing an evolutionary block-level structural gating mechanism to achieve fine-grained spatial dimension pruning. To ensure robust convergence during the joint evolution of these dual sparsity modes, we introduce variance stabilisation terms and synchronised cubic scheduling strategies. Extensive experiments on GLUE, SQuAD, and XSum benchmarks demonstrate that BSRA consistently outperforms existing baseline methods. By leveraging synergistic effects between spectral and spatial pruning, BSRA achieves diminishing returns with enhanced efficiency, attaining superior performance with significantly reduced parameter counts. Future work will investigate BSRA’s scalability on models with several hundred billion parameters and explore its potential applications in domains such as multimodal learning.
