A Nanopore Direct RNA Sequencing Atlas of RNA Modifications in pig (Sus scrofa domesticus) Testes across Sexual Maturation
Authors: Yiting Yang, Zhijuan Yan, Ziling Hao, Junxiang Mao, Yong Du, Yan Wang, Xiaofeng Zhou, Lei Chen, Ye Zhao, Linyuan Shen, Li Zhu, Mailin Gan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RNA modifications represent a crucial layer of post-transcriptional regulation, yet their dynamic landscape during testicular maturation remains largely uncharted. In this study, we employed Nanopore Direct RNA Sequencing to generate a comprehensive epitranscriptomic dataset for porcine testicular tissue at pre-sexual and post-sexual maturation stages. This dataset captures the profiles of four RNA modifications (m⁶A, m⁵C, Ψ, and inosine) across these critical developmental time points. We further characterized the global abundance, distribution, and motif features of these modifications. This resource provides a valuable foundation for elucidating the epitranscriptomic mechanisms governing mammalian testicular development and spermatogenesis.

Background & Summary

As early as the 1960s, a large number of modifications were discovered on RNA nucleotides1,2. Over the past 60 years, researchers have continuously identified a variety of modification types, such as m⁶A, m⁵C, Ψ and inosine3,4, constituting a dynamic post-transcriptional regulatory layer that regulates nearly every step of RNA metabolism, from transcription and splicing to translation efficiency and degradation. Although eukaryotic RNA modifications have been known since the 1960s, it was not until the past decade that they were identified and characterized on mRNA and various non-coding RNAs5,6. These RNA modifications, primarily found on transfer RNA and ribosomal RNA, have been shown to influence various pathways and are considered promising targets in human cancers7. In the realm of mammalian reproduction, these modifications have emerged as critical regulators of germ cell development, gonadal differentiation, and the acquisition of reproductive competence, with dysregulation linked to infertility and reproductive disorders in both humans and model organisms8,9. Building upon the well-established roles of RNA modifications in somatic tissues and early embryogenesis, there is a compelling impetus to explore their specific functions in the maturation of male reproductive organs, with the testis representing a critical and dynamic context for such epitranscriptomic regulation.

The testis serves as the primary site of spermatogenesis, a highly orchestrated process that transforms diploid spermatogonia into haploid, functionally competent spermatozoa10. Sexual maturation represents a pivotal developmental transition in males, marking the shift from immature testes incapable of supporting full spermatogenesis to mature organs that sustain continuous sperm production11. This transition is driven by profound transcriptional and post-transcriptional reprogramming, including changes in hormone signaling, cell cycle regulation, and the expression of germ cell-specific genes12,13. A comprehensive understanding of testicular maturation therefore necessitates moving beyond the transcriptome alone to integrate the dynamic layer of epitranscriptomic regulation, particularly the systematic mapping of how RNA modification landscapes are remodeled across the pre-sexual to sexual maturation continuum.

Direct RNA Sequencing (DRS) refers to direct RNA sequencing based on the third-generation sequencing platform of Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT). DRS can solve the methodological problem of simultaneously detecting multiple RNA modifications on a transcriptome scale while preserving the native RNA structure14. Conventional short-read sequencing methods require cDNA amplification, which can introduce biases and obscure modification signatures, and often rely on antibody-based enrichment that limits the number of modifications analyzed in a single experiment. In contrast, DRS overcomes these limitations by enabling amplification-free, long-read sequencing of native RNA molecules14. This technology captures both sequence information and the unique electrical signal perturbations induced by RNA modifications, allowing for the simultaneous detection and quantification of multiple epitranscriptomic marks across the entire transcriptome.

Pigs represent a valuable agricultural and biomedical model, with testicular development and maturation pathways that share significant homology to humans, enhancing the translational relevance of the resulting data. In this study, we applied nanopore Direct RNA Sequencing to comprehensively profile the epitranscriptome of testicular tissue from a Tibetan × Yorkshire hybrid pig population across pre- and post-sexual maturation stages. We constructed a high-resolution atlas capturing the abundance, distribution, and sequence motifs of four RNA modifications (m⁶A, m⁵C, Ψ, and inosine) (Fig. 1). Methods

Animals and sample collection

This study used 6 boars from F1 hybrid population of Tibetan and Yorkshire pigs. They were grouped by age: 3 TY hybrid pigs approximately 28 days were designated pre-sexual maturity (PSM), and 3 TY hybrid pigs approximately 210 days were designated sexual maturity (SM). The animals used in this study complied with the regulations of the Sichuan Agricultural University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and have been approved by the committee (Chengdu, People’s Republic of China; Approval No. 20240638). The individuals were sourced from commercial breeding farms. Pigs of appropriate age were randomly selected for slaughter, which involved stunning the animals with electricity followed by bleeding to minimize animal suffering and comply with ethical guidelines. Following the collection of male gonadal samples, the epididymis was removed, and phenotypic measurements were performed. Subsequently, the tunica albuginea was removed, and the central parenchymal region of the testis was collected. Phenotypic results showed that the pre-sexually mature testicular tissue was significantly smaller in weight, length, and width than the sexually mature testicular tissue (Fig. 2A-E). RNA isolation

Total RNA was extracted from animal soft tissues using the R6834 Total RNA Kit I. Briefly, 15–30 mg of tissue was pulverized in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in TRK Lysis Buffer. The lysate was then passed through a homogenization column by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 2 min at room temperature to remove insoluble debris. An equal volume of 70% ethanol was added to the filtrate, and the mixture was applied to an RNA binding column. The column was washed sequentially with 500 µL of RNA Wash Buffer I and twice with 500 µL of RNA Wash Buffer II, with each wash step followed by centrifugation. After a final 2-minute centrifugation to dry the column matrix, total RNA was eluted with 30–70 µL of Nuclease-free Water. RNA quality and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer with the Qubit RNA Assay Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), respectively.

DRS Library preparation and sequencing

The Direct RNA Sequencing (DRS) library was prepared using the SQK-RNA004 kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). Briefly, 25 µg of total RNA was used as input, and poly(A) + mRNA was enriched. The enriched mRNA was then ligated to a reverse transcription adapter using T4 DNA Ligase. Subsequently, reverse transcription was performed to synthesize the first-strand cDNA using reverse transcriptase. The resulting cDNA product was purified using Agencourt RNAClean XP beads. A sequencing adapter was ligated to the purified cDNA in a second T4 DNA Ligase reaction, followed by another bead-based purification step. The final library was prepared by mixing the adapter-ligated product with Sequencing Buffer and Library Solution. The prepared library was loaded onto a FLO-PRO004RA flow cell and sequenced on a PromethION sequencer (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) for 48 to 72 hours.

Sequencing data pre-processing

The raw data from the Nanopore sequencer were in the pod5/fast5 format, containing all raw sequencing signals. Base calling analysis was performed using Dorado (version 0.9.0) (parameter: --estimate-poly-a) software and converted into fastq sequence files. Because raw sequencing data may contain low-quality sequences, to ensure the reliability of information analysis results, the raw sequencing data were classified into pass (Q > = 10) and fail (Q < 10) based on the average quality of the sequencing reads during base calling (Table 1). Pass data were considered clean reads and were used for subsequent analysis. Filtered sequences were aligned to the reference genome(Sus_scrofa.Sscrofa11.1.114) using minimap215 (version 2.17-r941; parameters: -ax splice -uf -k14). Alignment statistics were generated using samtools16 (version 1.11; parameters: flagstat). Quality control and comparison information between samples indicated that the dataset was of high quality (Fig. 3A-B), exhibited high correlation between samples within groups (Fig. 3C-D), and a mapping rate of >95% (Fig. 3E). Transcript expression was quantified using Salmon17 (version 1.4.0). TPM calculations were performed by dividing the read count of each gene/transcript by its length (in kilobases) to obtain the reads per kilobase (RPK) of the gene/transcript. All RPK values were calculated for each sample and then divided by 1,000,000 to obtain a scaling factor per million. RNA modification analysis

Dorado’s latest model (rna004_130bps_sup@v5.1.0) was used to identify m6A, m5c, Ψ, inosine sites. This new model achieves ultra-high single-base accuracy (94.5%)18, enabling deeper understanding of RNA biological functions. The software calculates a statistic for each read, which is used to determine the methylation status of the read at that site. Map the modification sites to the reference genome, extract the corresponding site information for mapping, count the number of methylated sites in a 50 kb window, and draw a distribution map of the modified sites.

Differential RNA modification loci (DML) analysis and differential transcript analysis were performed using modkit (v0.4.1). “Effect size” was calculated as Group 1_pct_modified minus Group 2_pct_modified. The threshold for screening differential loci was |effect_size| > = 0.1 and p-value < 0.05. The threshold for screening differential transcripts was |log2fc| > = 1 and score > 3.

The m6A, m5C, and inosine modifications extend the RNA modification site by 2 bases upstream and downstream, respectively, to obtain a motif consisting of 5 bases. The Ψ modification site extends 1 base upstream and 2 bases downstream, to obtain a motif consisting of 4 bases. Based on the aforementioned motif extraction and analysis strategy, the characteristic sequences and genomic distribution densities of 4 classes of RNA modification motifs in immature and mature testicular tissues were systematically visualized. Technical Validation

The number and distribution of 4 RNA modifications

In recent years, nanopore technology and RNA modification detection algorithms have developed rapidly. Oxford Nanopore’s SQK-RNA004, a novel flow cell chemical reagent, achieves differentiated signal characteristics, higher signal resolution, and improved base recognition accuracy by replacing motor proteins and increasing molecular translocation speed. Dorado’s pseudouridine model achieved an accuracy of 96–98%. Similarly, Dorado’s N6-methyladenosine model achieved an accuracy of 94–98%21. Significant differences in RNA modification with Fraction > 0.722 were observed in testicular tissue before and after sexual maturity. Analysis of high-confidence modification sites showed that all four RNA modifications were detected across different transcript regions, including 5′ UTR, CDS, and 3′ UTR (Fig. 5A–H). The distribution patterns of m⁶A, m⁵C, Ψ, and inosine are distinct and reproducible, demonstrating that the workflow can reliably capture the positional landscapes of multiple modification types within the same dataset. Association between RNA modification and transcript expression

Differential analyses of RNA modification sites and transcript expression levels, including calculation of log2 fold changes and statistical significance, were conducted as a technical validation to evaluate the sensitivity and dynamic range of the sequencing workflow (Fig. 6A–B). Furthermore, correlation analysis between modification signals and transcript expression consistently revealed characteristic associations between RNA modifications and their corresponding transcripts (Fig. 6C–F). These results demonstrate that the sequencing workflow reliably captures diverse RNA modification signals. Usage Notes

This work profiles RNA modifications in porcine testes using bulk direct RNA sequencing. A key limitation is that bulk data cannot fully disentangle the contributions of cell-type composition changes from cell-intrinsic modification dynamics during sexual maturation. The observed epitranscriptomic differences likely represent a combined effect of cellular proportion shifts and stage-specific regulatory programs. Future integration of single-cell nanopore direct RNA sequencing will help resolve cell-type-specific RNA modification patterns in the developing testis.

This dataset is derived from testicular tissue of Tibetan pig × Yorkshire F1 hybrid pigs and focuses on the dynamic changes in RNA modifications before and after sexual maturity. The current data are intended to investigate epitranscriptome changes related to sexual maturity; caution should be exercised when directly extrapolating to purebred pig populations.
