Health related quality of life of patients with breast cancer in Malaysia: evidence from EQ-5D-5L and EORTC QLQ-C30
Authors: Siew Chin Ong, Sook Pin Goh, Siew Wei Yeong, Chow Kyn Ng, Yi Jing Tan, Muhammad Daoud Butt, Kian Keong Kong, Sin Dee Cheah, Boon Seang Tan, Jen Ven Lee, Ros Suzanna Ahmad Bustamam, Wei Wern Khor, Liyana Ahamad Fouzi, Aina Shafiza Abd Aziz, Su Yiu Chieng, Sarahfarina Abd Rahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeHealth-related quality of life (HRQoL) data among patients with breast cancer (BC) in Malaysia remain limited. This study aimed to describe HRQoL and identify its key predictors to inform future health policy planning.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted between April 2023 and January 2025 among BC outpatients in eight public specialist hospitals in Malaysia. Sociodemographic and clinical data were collected alongside patient-reported outcome measures, including the EuroQol-5 Dimension 5-Level questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L), a preference-based health utility measure, and the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire-Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30), a cancer-specific HRQoL questionnaire. Multivariable linear regression models were used to identify independent predictors of HRQoL for each instrument.ResultsThe mean (standard deviation) EQ-5D-5L health state utility value (HSUV) was 0.853 (0.179), the mean EQ-VAS score was 79.2 (19.1), and the mean (standard deviation) EORTC QLQ-C30 summary score was 83.3 (15.3). The EQ-5D-5L dimensions with the highest prevalence of reported problems were pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression. The EQ-5D-5L HSUV and VAS score demonstrated moderate to strong positive correlations with the EORTC QLQ-C30 summary score (ρ = 0.503 to 0.672, p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, advanced cancer stage, Chinese ethnicity, and lower education were independently associated with poorer EQ-5D-5L HSUV. EQ-VAS scores were associated with Indian ethnicity and completion of the questionnaire in English. For EORTC QLQ-C30, advanced cancer stages and Chinese ethnicity remained significant predictors.ConclusionHRQoL among patients with BC in Malaysia was primarily influenced by cancer stage, ethnicity, and education level. Advanced cancer stage was consistently associated with poorer patient-reported outcomes. The complementary use of EQ-5D-5L and EORTC QLQ-C30 provides more comprehensive estimates to inform survivorship care and future economic evaluations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-026-04334-5.Keywords: Health-related quality of life, Breast cancer, EQ-5D-5L, EORTC QLQ-C30

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is a malignant disease characterized by the uncontrolled growth of abnormal cells in breast tissue. It can be fatal if left untreated. BC continues to pose a significant global burden, particularly in terms of morbidity, mortality, socioeconomic impact, and escalating healthcare costs [1–3]. Studies have shown a substantial increase in new cases, prevalence, and disability-adjusted life years lost, especially in countries with low and lower-middle sociodemographic indices [4], where healthcare systems often face resource constraints. The global age‑standardized incidence rate of BC rose from 16.42 per 100,000 in 1990 to 26.88 per 100,000 in 2021 [4], an observed increase that likely reflects a combination of improved screening, greater health awareness, and evolving risk factors, representing a growing public health concern.

In Malaysia, BC was reported as the most commonly diagnosed cancer between 2017 and 2021 according to the National Cancer Registry Report [5]. The report indicated that up to 50.5% of the cases presented at late stages (Stage III and IV) [5]. Late-stage presentation of BC is often associated with poorer prognosis, higher treatment burden, and substantial psychosocial distress, all of which may adversely affect patients’ health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Therefore, there is an urgent need to assess the HRQoL of patients with BC as a key outcome in disease management and supportive care planning.

A robust HRQoL measurement tool is needed to accurately capture the multidimensional burden of BC. Instruments for measuring HRQoL can be categorised into generic preference-based measures (PBMs) and disease-specific tools. PBMs are particularly important in health economic evaluation, as they are widely used to generate health state utility values (HSUVs) for cost-effectiveness analyses and health technology assessments (HTA) [6]. The EuroQol-5 Dimension 5-Level questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) is considered one of the most established generic PBMs. It is often recommended in health economic evaluations due to its simplicity and the availability of country-specific value sets [7, 8]. However, concerns remain regarding its sensitivity to fully capture the complex health states of patients with cancer, as it is prone to ceiling effects and has limited sensitivity to psychosocial dimensions [9]. In contrast, European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) questionnaires, especially the Quality of Life Questionnaire - Core 30 (QLQ-C30) provide a detailed assessment of cancer-related symptoms and functional domains but do not generate utility values for economic modelling. These two instruments measure structurally distinct domains, and previous research generally reported only moderate statistical correlations between them [10]. Since no single instrument is considered a gold standard for HRQoL measurement and both generic and condition-specific instruments cannot be used interchangeably [11], the concurrent use of EQ-5D-5L and EORTC QLQ-C30 was adopted in this study to enable both health economic analyses and clinically meaningful HRQoL assessment among patients with BC.

Globally, patient-reported outcomes (PROs) are essential for evaluating the impact of BC and its treatments. Several studies have used EQ-5D-5L to measure and derive HSUVs for HRQoL of patients with BC worldwide [12–14]. However, uncertainty remains regarding the construct validity and discriminatory performance of EQ-5D-5L in newly diagnosed BC populations, particularly in middle-income Asian settings. HSUVs derived from other countries may not be transferable to Malaysia due to differences in sociocultural context, healthcare access, and stage distribution at diagnosis.

In Malaysia, HRQoL of patients with BC has mainly been explored using disease-specific tools such as the EORTC QLQ-C30 [15–19] and FACT-B [20–22], while the application of generic PBMs such as the EQ-5D-5L remained scarce. The few available studies utilizing the EQ-5D-5L have primarily relied on either single-centre settings [23] or specific non-governmental organizations [24, 25], thus limiting the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, none of these studies provided HSUVs that reflect newly diagnosed patients with BC recruited across multiple public hospitals, and multicentre assessments integrating both the EQ-5D-5L and the EORTC QLQ- C30 have not been conducted. This gap limits the availability of locally derived HSUV inputs for economic evaluation and constrains assessment of measurement validity in the Malaysian oncology context.

Therefore, this study aimed to address these gaps by using a dual-instrument approach to provide a comprehensive assessment of HRQoL among Malaysian patients with BC who were diagnosed within the past two years to capture both acute treatment-related effects and early survivorship outcomes. The specific objectives were:

To evaluate the construct validity of the EQ-5D-5L by examining its associations with conceptually related domains of the EORTC QLQ-C30, and to assess its discriminatory performance across clinical stages.

To evaluate the construct validity of the EQ-5D-5L by examining its associations with conceptually related domains of the EORTC QLQ-C30, and to assess its discriminatory performance across clinical stages.

To derive HSUVs for newly diagnosed patients with BC across multicentre settings to inform Malaysian health economic modelling.

To derive HSUVs for newly diagnosed patients with BC across multicentre settings to inform Malaysian health economic modelling.

To identify socio-demographic and clinical predictors associated with HRQoL outcomes.

To identify socio-demographic and clinical predictors associated with HRQoL outcomes.

Based on these objectives, we established three a priori hypotheses: (1) the EQ-5D-5L will correlate strongly with conceptually related EORTC QLQ-C30 domains; (2) advanced-stage disease will be associated with significantly lower HRQoL compared to early-stage disease; and (3) lower socioeconomic status and higher clinical burden (e.g., radiotherapy, chemotherapy) will significantly predict poorer HRQoL outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This is a cross-sectional, multi-centre, and convenience-sampling survey on the HRQoL of women with BC in Malaysia. The study was conducted from April 2023 to January 2025. Outpatients with BC were recruited from eight major public hospitals across Malaysia The detailed list of participating hospitals is presented in Supplementary Material Table S1.2.

Study population

Participants were female patients aged 40 years and above with a confirmed diagnosis of BC. This age threshold aligns with current screening guidelines, which recommend annual BC screening starting at age 40 [26]. Additional inclusion criteria required participants to have been diagnosed with BC within the past 24 months. This time window was selected to capture both short- and long-term quality-of-life outcomes. Early-stage treatments usually last less than 6 months, during which the resulting side effects (e.g., fatigue, pain, emotional distress) are known to impact HRQoL for up to 18 months after diagnosis [27]. Participants were required to be able to read and understand at least one of the three languages: Malay, Chinese, or English to ensure effective self-reported data collection. Women with a history of other cancers, concurrent malignancies, or who were involved in another clinical trial were excluded from the study.

Data collection

HRQoL was assessed using the EQ-5D-5L and EORTC QLQ-C30 depend on participants' preferred language (Malay, English, or Chinese). Locally validated versions were employed to minimize cross-cultural bias. Studies confirm strong measurement equivalence and convergent validity for the EQ-5D-5L across these three languages in Malaysia [32, 33]. Similarly, the EORTC QLQ-C30 language versions demonstrate robust construct validity and acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α ≥ 0.70) in local and regional oncology cohorts [19, 34–36].

The EQ-5D-5L consists of five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension has five response levels, with higher levels indicating greater severity. It also includes the EQ visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS), on which patients rate their current health from 0 (worst imaginable health) to 100 (best imaginable health) [37]. For this study, all language versions were obtained through official licensing, and HSUVs were derived using the Malaysian EQ-5D-5L value set developed by Shafie et al. [38].

The EORTC QLQ-C30 consists of 30-item cancer-specific questions [39]. The questionnaire is used to assess global health status/quality of life, functional domains, symptom burden, and financial impact of disease. Most items are rated on four-point response scale from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very much), except for global health status, which uses a seven-point response scale. The questionnaire is organised into several scales: one global health status scale, five functional scales, and nine symptom scales, including fatigue, pain, nausea and vomiting, and financial impact. The EORTC QLQ-C30 Summary Score was calculated by averaging the scores of 13 of its 15 scales (excluding the global health status and financial impact scales), after reversing the symptom scale scores [40]. The summary score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better HRQoL.

Participants were recruited during outpatient clinic visits. They were asked to complete a self-administered survey that included sociodemographic information, clinical data, and the EQ-5D-5L and EORTC QLQ-C30 questionnaires after providing written informed consent. Participants were encouraged to complete the forms independently, with the support of trained data collectors if needed. All data were de-identified to ensure confidentiality.

Potential predictors variables

Potential predictor variables were selected based on their clinical relevance and documented impact on BC outcomes in Malaysia setting. These variables included age [16, 41–43], employment status [41], ethnicity [42], cancer stage [43], and treatment modalities [16, 42] such as mastectomy, lumpectomy, breast reconstruction, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and hormonal therapy.

Statistical analysis

Several variables were grouped to ensure clinical relevance and statistical stability to prevent sparse data bias in the regression models. Cancer stage was categorized as Stage 0-I (early disease), Stage II (intermediate), and Stage III-IV (advanced). Education was classified as ‘no formal/primary education’ versus ‘secondary or higher education’, due to its strong association with health literacy and HRQoL outcomes in Malaysian populations [44].

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sociodemographic characteristics, clinical variables, and HRQoL outcomes. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations (SD) and, when appropriate, as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages.

Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha [45] for EQ-5D-5L dimensions and the EORTC QLQ-C30 summary score. A coefficient over 0.70 was considered as acceptable [46]. Construct validity of the EQ-5D-5L was evaluated by examining its associations with conceptually related domains of the EORTC QLQ-C30. The normality of HRQoL scores was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Given the non-normal distribution of HRQoL measures, the non-parametric Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was used instead of Pearson's correlation to assess correlations between the EQ-5D-5L HSUVs, EQ-VAS scores, and the EORTC QLQ-C30 summary score. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as negligible (< 0.20), weak (0.20–0.34), moderate (0.35–0.49) and strong (≥ 0.50) [47].

Known-group validity was assessed by comparing HRQoL scores across clinical subgroups, particularly to test the a priori hypothesis that patients with advanced-stage disease experience poorer HRQoL than those with early-stage disease. Due to the skewed distribution of the data, the differences in HRQoL scores across sociodemographic and clinical groups were assessed using non-parametric tests such as Mann–Whitney U was applied for variables comprising two independent categories, whereas Kruskal–Wallis H tests were utilized for variables comprising three or more independent categories [48]. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d, with values ranging from 0.2 to < 0.5 indicating a small effect, 0.5 to < 0.8 a medium effect, and values ≥ 0.8 a large effect [49].

A two-step linear regression approach was employed to identify sociodemographic and clinical factors independently associated with EQ-5D-5L HSUVs, EQ-VAS score and EORTC QLQ-C30 summary score. Variables with p < 0.25 in univariable analyses, as well as clinically relevant variables identified from prior literature, were included in multivariable ordinary least squares regression models using this purposeful selection strategy [50, 51]. Adjusted regression coefficients (B), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and robust standard errors were reported to account for potential heteroskedasticity.

Missing data patterns and mechanisms were examined and summarized by variable and by case. Little’s Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) test was used to assess whether data were MCAR, with a p-value < 0.05 indicating evidence against the MCAR assumption. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equation (MICE) via the mice package in R (version 4.5.1). Five imputed datasets were generated with ten iterations per imputation, including all study variables in the prediction model [52]. The first completed dataset was used for the primary analyses when the overall missingness was below 10% [53, 54].All analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel and R with two-tailed statistical significance set at p-values < 0.05.

Guidelines and ethics approval

This study was conducted and reported in compliance with current guidelines, following recommendations by the ISPOR-Professional Society for Health Economics and Outcomes Research for estimating HSUVs [55], and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist [56] for cross-sectional studies. The study was registered with the National Medical Research Register of Malaysia (NMRR-20-2202-56545 [IIR]) and approved by the Medical Research and Ethics Committee of the Ministry of Health Malaysia.

Results

A total of 280 respondents were recruited in the study. However, only 275 respondents met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis. Of these, complete data were available for 265 respondents (96.4%). The variable with the highest proportion of missing data was history of lumpectomy, with 1.1% missing responses (Supplementary Material Section 1). Little’s MCAR test was non-significant (p = 0.263).

Descriptive and summary statistics

As summarized in Table 1, respondents were predominantly from the West coast region (67.6%). Most respondents opted to complete questionnaires in Malay (74.9%), with smaller proportions completing them in English (14.2%) or Mandarin Chinese (10.9%). The mean (SD) age of respondents was 58.96 (11.34) years. The majority were Malay (47.3%), married (79.6%), and had at least secondary education and above (67.3%). Nearly half of the respondents (45.8%) were recruited within six months of BC diagnosis. Most respondents were diagnosed with stage III-IV BC (42.9%). Regarding clinical management, 57.1% had undergone mastectomy, while 32.4% had received lumpectomy. However, the majority did not undergo breast reconstruction (97.5%). Chemotherapy was administered to 45.1% of respondents, radiation therapy to 25.8%, and hormonal therapy to 32.0%.

Distribution of EQ-5D-5L dimensions and HSUV

Most respondents reported no problems in the self-care (88.0%) and mobility (73.5%) dimensions, while 30.5% reported at least slight problems in usual activities. Pain/discomfort was the most frequently reported problem, with over half of the respondents (51.6%) indicating at least slight problems in this dimension. Similarly, anxiety/depression was common, with 42.9% reporting at least slight psychological distress. The frequency distribution of responses across the EQ-5D-5L dimensions is presented in Table S3 in Supplementary Materials.

The mean (SD) EQ-5D-5L HSUV was 0.853 (0.179), with a median (IQR) of 0.92 (IQR 0.80–1.00). A ceiling effect was observed in 27.6% of the respondents, while no floor effect was detected. The mean (SD) EQ-VAS score was 79.2 (19.1), with a median (IQR) of 80.0 (70.0–90.0). Ceiling effects for the EQ-VAS were less pronounced, with only 17.5% of respondents reporting the maximum score, and no floor effects were observed. Summary statistics for EQ-5D-5L HSUV and EQ-VAS scores are presented in Table 2.

Distribution of disease-specific HRQoL based on EORTC QLQ-C30

The frequency distribution and summary statistics of the EORTC QLQ-C30 questionnaire are presented in Table 3. The mean (SD) global health status score was 76.4 (18.4), with 20.0% of the respondents reporting the most favourable health state. Mean scores for all functional scales were consistently high, exceeding 80 points. Social functioning demonstrated the highest mean (SD) score 86.6 (20.4), followed by cognitive functioning 82.7 (19.7) and physical functioning 81.2 (20.1). In addition, symptom burden was generally low across all the scales. The lowest mean (SD) scores were for nausea and vomiting 7.7 (19.4), diarrhoea 5.3 (14.7), and dyspnoea 9.6 (20.2). Fatigue 29.1 (23.0) and insomnia 27.4 (30.4) were the most commonly reported symptoms, with only 16.0% and 46.5% of respondents, respectively, reporting no problems. Overall, the mean (SD) summary score for EORTC QLQ-C30 was 83.3 (15.3), with only 2.2% of respondents reporting the highest possible score.

Reliability

Internal consistency was acceptable for both the five EQ-5D-5L dimensions and the items contributing to the EORTC QLQ-C30 summary score, with Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.80 and 0.94 respectively.

Correlation analysis

A strong positive correlation was observed between the EQ-5D-5L HSUV and EORTC QLQ-C30 (ρ = 0.672, p < 0.001). EQ-VAS scores also demonstrated moderate positive correlations with both EQ-5D-5L HSUV (ρ = 0.503, p < 0.001) and EORTC QLQ-C30 summary score (ρ = 0.509, p < 0.001) respectively. The correlation relationship between EQ-5D-5L HSUV, EQ-5D VAS score and EORTC QLQ-C30 summary scores are presented in Supplementary Material Table S2.3.

Univariable linear regression analyses further identified cancer stage, ethnicity, and education level as variables significantly associated with HSUV, and these variables were therefore considered for inclusion in the multivariable model (Table 4).

After multivariable adjustment, cancer stage, ethnicity, and education level remained significant predictors of HSUV. Respondents with stage III-IV BC reported significantly lower HSUV compared to those diagnosed at stage 0-I BC (adjusted B = − 0.072, p = 0.018). In contrast, no significant difference in HSUV was observed between respondents with stage II BC and those with stage 0-I BC (p = 0.773). In addition, respondents with at least secondary education were independently associated with higher HSUV compared to those with no formal or only primary education (adjusted B = 0.055, p = 0.034). Chinese ethnicity was also independently associated with higher HSUV compared to Malay ethnicity (adjusted B = 0.066, p = 0.020).

Predictors of EQ-VAS

Unadjusted analyses demonstrated statistically significant differences in EQ-VAS scores across education level, employment status, and time since diagnosis (Supplementary Materials Section 3). In univariable linear regression analyses, ethnicity, employment status, education level, and time since diagnosis were significantly associated with EQ-VAS scores. In the multivariable model (Table 5), only questionnaire language and ethnicity remained significantly associated with EQ-VAS score. Respondents who completed the questionnaire in English reported significantly higher EQ-VAS scores compared with those who completed it in Malay (adjusted B = 7.518, p = 0.035). Conversely, respondents of Indian ethnicity reported significantly lower EQ-VAS scores compared with those of Malay ethnicity (adjusted B = − 9.192, p = 0.029).

Predictors of EORTC QLQ-C30

In bivariate analyses, region, ethnicity, menopause status, family history, and cancer stage were significantly associated with EORTC QLQ-C30 Summary Scores (SupplementaryMaterials Section 3). Univariable linear regression analyses further identified region, ethnicity, family history, relationship to affected family members, and cancer stage as significantly associated with the summary score. In the multivariable model (Supplementary Material Section 4), only ethnicity and cancer stage remained independently associated with the EORTC QLQ-C30 summary scores. Respondents of Chinese ethnicity reported significantly higher summary scores compared with those of Malay ethnicity (adjusted B = 5.621, p = 0.017). In contrast, respondents with stage III-IV BC reported significantly lower summary scores compared with those diagnosed at stage 0-I BC (adjusted B = − 5.234, p = 0.043).

Model performance

The final multivariable models were statistically significant for all three outcomes based on the overall F-test (p < 0.05). The models explained 12.2% of the variance in EQ-5D-5L HSUV, 11.9% in EQ-VAS score, and 13.1% in EORTC QLQ-C30 summary score. Further details on model fit are presented in Supplementary Materials Section 5.

Discussion

This study assessed the HRQoL among patients with BC in Malaysia using concurrent generic and disease-specific instruments. Overall, our findings largely supported our a priori hypotheses.

The study analysis revealed that the mean (SD) of HSUV was 0.853 (0.179), together with the mean (SD) EQ-VAS score of 79.2 (19.1) and the mean (SD) of QLQ-C30 summary score was 83.3 (15.3). These findings align with international studies. For instance, a study from Toronto reported a mean (SD) HSUV of 0.830 (0.130) and a VAS score of 75.7 (17.5) among patients with BC, although most participants had been diagnosed more than two years [13]. Similarly, research from mainland China also reported comparable utility values across four different country-specific value sets [57], despite a longer mean disease duration of 38.2 months. Miret et al. further reported EQ-5D-based utilities ranging from 0.840 to 0.860 and QLQ-C30 summary score between 84.7 and 86.6 among women with non-metastatic BC at diagnosis and at two years post-diagnosis [58]. These values fall within a range similar to those observed in the current study, and therefore suggesting relative consistency in HRQoL outcomes across diverse settings and time horizons.

In comparison to local data, this study reported a higher mean HSUV compared to the earlier Malaysian data from a tertiary referral university hospital, which reported a mean HSUV of 0.780, although the VAS score of 79.7 was comparable to that observed in our study [23]. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in study setting, clinical setting, and recruitment period. The prior data were collected from a single study site during an earlier study period (2017), before potential advancements in BC treatment. In addition, over 60% of respondents in the study had received treatments such as hormonal therapy, chemotherapy, radiotherapy and/or surgery. These differences may explain why their HSUV scores were lower than those observed in the current study. Compared with Puteh et al. [25], who assessed HRQoL in a broader Malaysian cancer population, the current study reported higher mean HSUV (0.853 vs 0.760) but slightly lower EQ-VAS scores (79.2 vs 81.06).

The relatively high score of HRQoL observed in the study may reflect improvements in the access to cancer treatment and survivorship care within the Malaysian public health system, as well as the predominance of the respondents recruited from the West Coast of Peninsular Malaysia, where healthcare resources are more concentrated [59]. Nevertheless, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression still emerged as the most frequently reported problems, despite relatively high overall HRQoL scores. These findings align with previous research indicating that severe or extreme problems are most commonly reported in these two dimensions among patients with BC and survivors [60]. Previous studies have shown that patients with BC often experience physical pain and reduced emotional functioning following treatment with influencing factors such as type of surgery and adjuvant therapies [61, 62].

Furthermore, the interpretation of these high scores must account for a notable ceiling effect presented in EQ-5D-5L HSUV (27.6%). While this value observed here is not unusual as previous studies typically reported ceiling effects vary between 10–35% [63–65], it highlights the generic instrument's limited sensitivity in detecting milder impairments. In our study, this phenomenon is closely linked with patients' clinical and sociodemographic profiles. Clinically, the majority presented with early/intermediate disease (57.1% Stage 0-II) and did not undergo chemotherapy (54.9%), thereby preserving the mobility and self-care functions that dominate EQ-5D-5L scoring. Besides, strong familial support networks (79.6% married) likely provided better coping mechanisms with the psychosocial impact of the disease. As a result, the true prevalence of reported problems may be underestimated, and therefore the findings should be interpreted cautiously. The concurrent use of the disease-specific EORTC QLQ-C30 likely enhanced sensitivity to cancer-related functional limitations and symptom burden. These findings consistent with prior local [28, 66, 67] and international studies [60, 68, 69]. The high prevalence of psychosocial distress and pain underscores the need for integrated psycho-oncological support within standard clinical management [70].

Consistent with our first hypothesis, the strong correlations between EQ-5D-5L HSUV and the EORTC QLQ-C30 summary score indicate that both instruments capture overlapping constructs of health status and supporting their complementary use. The EQ-VAS also demonstrated moderate correlations with EQ-5D-5L HSUV and the EORTC QLQ-C30 summary score. This highlights the role of EQ-VAS in measuring subjective global health perception rather than serving as a purely preference-based measure. These findings align with previous validation studies demonstrating that EQ-VAS captures broader psychosocial and contextual health perceptions that may not be fully captured in utility-based measures [71]. The correlation analysis further suggests that the distinct strengths of generic preference-based measures and cancer-specific tools offer complementary insights for comprehensive outcome evaluation even though these instruments assess overlapping domains of health status.

Furthermore, supporting our second hypothesis, cancer stage consistently emerged as a key determinant of HRQoL across all instruments. Respondents diagnosed with stage III-IV BC reported significantly lower EQ-5D-5L HSUV and EORTC QLQ-C30 summary scores compared with those diagnosed at stage 0-I, even after adjusting for sociodemographic and treatment-related factors. These results highlight the sustained impact of disease burden, which extends beyond the effects of treatment alone. These results are consistent with international evidences, which identified metastatic disease as a significant driver of both physical and emotional deterioration [72, 73]. The findings emphasize the importance of early detection and timely intervention, not only for improving survival outcome but also for preserving patients’ HRQoL.

Regarding our third hypothesis, the predictive roles of socioeconomic status and clinical burden were nuanced. Respondents with secondary or tertiary education showed better HRQoL compared to those with no formal education or only primary education which supported the socioeconomic aspect of this hypothesis. The persistence of this effect after adjustment suggests that educational influences perceived health status beyond disease severity alone. This association is likely because individuals with higher education tend to have better health literacy, coping capacity, access to supportive resources, and treatment adherence [24]. Quantitative evidence also indicates that patients with secondary or tertiary education have significantly higher odds of better quality of life compared with those with only primary education, with up to 4.90 fold greater likelihood of improved outcomes [74]. Furthermore, this educational gradient in health outcomes is often mediated by socioeconomic factors. For example, higher education correlates with better employment opportunities and financial stability, which are pivotal for optimizing quality of life among cancer survivors [75].

Ethnicity also demonstrated a complex relationship with HRQoL outcome. While Indian ethnicity was associated with a statistically significant lower EQ-VAS score, Chinese ethnicity emerged as a significant predictor for a higher EQ-5D-5L HSUVs and EORTC QLQ-C30 summary score compared with Malay respondents. This finding is consistent with the study by Ganesh et al. [41] in which Chinese patients with BC reported the best HRQoL. These disparities are likely due to a multifactorial interplay involving cultural beliefs about illness [66], differences in social support structures [66], and potential variations in socio-economic status [76], all of which are often associated with ethnicity in Malaysia. The results highlight the importance of culturally competent care, in which healthcare providers should consider how different communities perceive and report quality of life. However, interpretation of these findings should be approached with caution due to the uneven distribution of ethnic groups within the study population.

This study also revealed that respondents who completed the questionnaire in English reported higher EQ-VAS scores. This association was not observed for EQ-5D-5L HSUVs or EORTC QLQ-C30 scores. Therefore, this finding suggests that language primarily affects subjective health perception rather than functional or symptom-based domains. The observed association is more likely to reflect underlying differences in socioeconomic status, educational background, or urban residence rather than a true language effect [77]. Individuals with higher socioeconomic status and literacy levels may interpret VAS differently than those with lower literacy [78]. Previous research has cautioned that EQ-VAS responses can vary substantially between individuals with similar health states, and thus results should therefore be interpreted carefully [71, 77].

Conversely, the clinical burden aspect of our third hypothesis was not supported in the final models. Treatment modalities variables such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and hormonal therapy, were not identified as independent predictors of HRQoL after multivariable adjustment. This finding aligns with previous studies [23, 43, 79] where the treatment variables were diminished after adjusting for potential confounders. This may due to the close relationship between treatment modalities and disease severity. For instance, patients receiving chemotherapy are more likely to be diagnosed with advanced-stage disease, which itself is associated with lower HRQoL. Additionally, the analysis did not differentiate between patients undergoing active treatment and those who had completed treatment may have attenuated treatment-specific effects.

Although regional differences were observed in bivariate and univariable analysis for EORTC QLQ-C30 summary scores, region did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment. This implies that regional disparities in HRQoL may be explained by compositional differences in disease stage, ethnicity, or other sociodemographic factors rather than by geography itself. Caution is warranted in interpreting this finding, as the small sample size in certain regions, such as the East coast, was relatively small. In addition, other pre-specified predictors, including age and employment status, were not associated with HRQoL, likely due to confounding or mediation by more proximal determinants such as cancer stage and socioeconomic factors. These results are consistent with evidence from Asian populations [80], which found that ethnicity, educational level, and cancer stage are the primary determinants of HRQoL in this population.

Strengths and limitations

The key strength of this study is its multicentre design, public-hospital-based derivation of HSUVs specifically for newly diagnosed patients with BC in Malaysia, ensuring a representative sample that captures the acute treatment and early survivorship phases (within two years of diagnosis). Another strength is the use of two complementary HRQoL instruments, one generic and one cancer-specific, which improves construct validity by capturing both overall and disease-related HRQoL dimensions. It also provided a comprehensive analysis of predictors, providing valuable insights into sociodemographic and clinical determinants of HRQoL. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional study design limits causal inference, and future research should consider longitudinal design to monitor HRQoL over time. Recruitment was limited to public hospitals and predominantly on the West Coast of Peninsular Malaysia, which may limit representativeness and introduce selection bias. Similarly, the exclusion of patients under 40 years of age restricts the generalizability of our findings. Younger patients with BC below this threshold are clinically distinct, often characterized by more aggressive tumour biology and unique psychosocial challenges, such as fertility concerns and premature menopause [81]. Consequently, our HRQoL profiles may not reflect the specific burdens experienced by this younger demographic. Self-reported clinical data are also subject to recall or response bias. Additionally, important factors such as financial toxicity, social support, and coping strategies such as lifestyle or complementary medicines used were not measured in this study and may account for some of the unexplained variance in HRQoL. While the adjusted R2 values may appear modest, they are consistent with HRQoL researches [82, 83], where outcomes are influenced by a wide range of often unmeasured factors. The analyses involved multiple statistical tests, which may increase the risk of Type I error, although the overall patterns of association were consistent with prior literature [80].

Implications for future research

This study’s findings highlight the influence of cancer stage and socioeconomic factors in shaping HRQoL among Malaysian patients with BC. Therefore, efforts to promote early detection, improve health literacy, and provide targeted supportive care for advanced-stage patients are likely to yield meaningful improvements in quality of life. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs and broader psychosocial measures to better capture the variation in HRQoL among Malaysian patients with BC and explore mechanisms underlying ethnic and language-related differences in patient-reported outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identifies both clinical severity and sociodemographic factors, particularly ethnicity, education level, and cancer stage, as the key determinants of HRQoL among Malaysian women with BC. The strong agreement between the EQ-5D-5L and EORTC QLQ-C30 supports their complementary use in outcomes research and future economic evaluation. These findings have direct implications for survivorship care planning, HTA, and policy development aimed at reducing healthcare inequities and improve the long-term quality of life for patients with BC in Malaysia.
