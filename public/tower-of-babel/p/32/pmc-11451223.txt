Clinic transfers and engagement in HIV care during the perinatal period across a network of healthcare centers in Lilongwe, Malawi
Authors: Angela M Bengtson, Wiza Kumwenda, Madelyn Frey, Shaphil Waille, Yu Li, Sophie Lazar, Denzel Matiya, William C Miller, Mina C Hosseinipour
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDisengagement from HIV care during the perinatal period remains a challenge. Improving engagement in HIV care requires monitoring engagement across multiple indicators, including retention in HIV care, visit adherence, clinic transfers, and viral suppression to support improved clinical and programmatic outcomes.MethodsWe enrolled a prospective cohort of pregnant WHIV across a network of five urban clinics in Lilongwe, Malawi from February 2020-February 2021. WHIV were followed from their first antenatal care visit through 9 months postpartum across all study sites using biometric fingerprint scanning. Study visits occurred at enrollment into antenatal care, 6 weeks’, 6 months, and 9 months postpartum. In addition, all usual care HIV visits were captured via medical records. Participants who missed a study visit or usual care visit were traced. We evaluated determinants of multiple indicators of engagement in care, including retention in HIV care (attending a scheduled visit or self-reported recent visit when traced), HIV visit adherence (missed scheduled HIV visits and HIV visit coverage), clinic transfers, and viral load suppression (< 1000 copies/mL) using modified Poisson regression and sub-distributional hazard ratios to account for competing events of death and loss-to-follow-up. Associations between clinic transfer and subsequent indicators of engagement in HIV care were evaluated using generalized estimating equations.ResultsAmong 399 participants, 81% were on ART at baseline. Retention in HIV care was 87% at 6 weeks postpartum, 77% at 6 months postpartum and 89% at 9 months postpartum. At 9 months postpartum, 91% of participants were virally suppressed, 81% had missed a scheduled HIV visit, and 19% had transferred clinics. WHIV who transferred clinics were most likely to miss their subsequent scheduled HIV visit by ≥ 30 days. Transferring clinics was not associated with unsuppressed viral load or non-retention at 9 months postpartum.ConclusionsIn a cohort of WHIV, retention and viral load suppression were high in the perinatal period, but missed HIV visits and clinic transfers were common. Transferring clinics was associated with an increased likelihood of missing a subsequent HIV visit. Clinic transfers may be important indicators of disruptions in clinical care for WHIV in the perinatal period.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-024-06865-6.Keywords: Option B+; Engagement in HIV care; Malawi, Retention in HIV care; Viral load suppression; HIV care continuum

Background

In 2011, Malawi became the first country to provide lifelong antiretroviral therapy (ART) for pregnant and breastfeeding women with HIV (WHIV) under Option B+ [1, 2]. Option B + has led to declines in vertical HIV transmission, as well as improvements in maternal and infant health and viral suppression [3–9]. However, sustained engagement in HIV care has emerged as a challenge to the success of Option B+. To continue making progress towards the UNAIDS 2025 goals [10], an improved understanding of determinants of engagement in HIV care during the perinatal period is needed.

In Malawi, up to 30% of WHIV who are identified as lost to follow-up from an Option B + program have transferred their HIV care to another facility [11]. In non-pregnant populations with HIV, transferring clinics has been associated with gaps in ART treatment and may influence viral load suppression over time [12]. HIV appointment adherence is particularly important during the perinatal period to support the health of WHIV and to reduce the risk of vertical HIV transmission during pregnancy and breastfeeding, but is difficult to monitor across clinics [13]. Because of these limitations, few longitudinal studies have characterized engagement in HIV care across multiple indicators, including retention in HIV care, HIV appointment adherence, clinic transfers and viral suppression, during the perinatal period or evaluated the impact of transferring clinics on subsequent engagement in HIV care outcomes for WHIV.

To address these gaps, we conducted a prospective cohort study of WHIV enrolled in the Option B + program during antenatal care and followed through 9 months postpartum across a network of large, urban clinics and used biometric fingerprint scanning to uniquely identify participants across clinics. We describe multiple indicators of engagement in HIV care during the perinatal period, evaluate determinants of suboptimal engagement in HIV care, and investigate how switching clinics influences subsequent engagement in HIV care.

Methods

Study setting

The study took place in Lilongwe, Malawi, where HIV and ART services are integrated into routine antenatal care (ANC) services and are free of charge at government clinics [14]. HIV testing during pregnancy is standard of care and women diagnosed with HIV infection are initiated on ART on the same day. Since 2011 Malawi has offered universal ART for all pregnant WHIV through Option B+, thus over time more WHIV are entering ANC already taking ART [15]. ART is available in pregnancy as part of routine ANC or through ART clinics. In Malawi, no model of care is recommended for delivering ART postpartum. In practice, some WHIV receive ART from their original ART clinic throughout pregnancy and postpartum, while others receive ART as part of ANC care and then transition to an ART clinic, typically between 6 weeks and 1 year postpartum [16]. Frequency of routine HIV care visits and ART dispensing differs based on time since HIV diagnosis and adherence to treatment, but is typically every 1 to 3 months [17]. During the early part of the COVID-19 pandemic, the Malawi Ministry of Health recommended dispensing ART in 6 months supplies to reduce the need for clinic visits [18].

Study design and population

We conducted a prospective cohort study across five large, urban clinics in Lilongwe District between February 2020 and August 2022. The primary goal of the study was to evaluate the use of biometric fingerprint scanning to monitor engagement in HIV care during the perinatal period. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were living with HIV (based on medical records or an HIV test in the index pregnancy), ≥ 18 years of age, residing in Lilongwe District, and presenting for their first ANC visit. After the start of the COVID-19 pandemic in March 2020, we additionally required participants to have a phone to conduct study visits by phone, if necessary.

Data collection

Participants were enrolled at their first ANC visit and followed through 9 months postpartum. Information on routine HIV visits was collected prospectively and from medical records (clinic registers, electronic medical records, and patient Mastercard’s) by trained study staff fluent in English and Chichewa, the local language, to ensure complete coverage. In addition, study visits were conducted at enrollment (baseline), 6 weeks-, 6 months-, and 9 months-postpartum. Study visits were scheduled to coincide with routine HIV care visits whenever possible. At both routine HIV visits and study visits biometric fingerprint scanning was used to identify participants. Biometric fingerprint scanning information was uploaded to a cloud-based server and shared across study sites, facilitating the identification of study participants across study sites.

Information on HIV appointment adherence and clinic transfers was collected at routine HIV visits and study visits. At 9 months postpartum, HIV viral load was collected via dried blood spot. Participants received a phone call if they missed a scheduled study visit and were traced if they were late by > 30 days. Tracing information was used to determine retention in HIV care status for participants who missed a study visit. From March to June 2023 study enrollment was paused and in-person study visits were not feasible due to the COVID-19 pandemic. During this time, study visits were conducted by phone and regularly scheduled HIV visits were captured retrospectively using medical records. All participants provided written informed consent and ethical approval was provided by the National Health Sciences Research Committee in Malawi (NHSRC). Participants were reimbursed 7300 Kwacha (~$10USD) per study visit, per NHSRC policy.

Outcomes

We evaluated multiple indicators of engagement in HIV care during the perinatal period including: [1] retention in HIV clinic, retention in HIV care, and viral load suppression to evaluate the Option B + program across a network of clinics, and [2] HIV appointment adherence, HIV visit coverage, and clinic transfers as potential indicators of disruptions to HIV clinical care, in the perinatal period.

Programmatic outcomes

We defined retention in HIV clinic at 6 weeks, 6 months, and 9 months postpartum as attending a scheduled visit at the respective time point, prior to any tracing efforts. We defined retention in HIV care at the same time point as retained in HIV clinic, but taking into account HIV visits self-reported upon tracing. Participants who were traced and reported no recent HIV visits or who had died were considered not retained in HIV care. Participants who were traced but could not be located were considered to have their retention status unknown. Viral load suppression at 9 months postpartum was defined as < 1000 copies/mL.

Clinical care outcomes

HIV appointment adherence was evaluated as missing a scheduled HIV visit (defined as missed by > 30 days). We defined HIV visit coverage in two ways to account for routine variation in length of time between usual care HIV visits. Time-varying HIV visit coverage was defined as having attended ≥ 1 HIV visit within the previous a 3-month period [19]. Cumulative HIV coverage at 9 months postpartum was defined as having attended ≥ 1 HIV visit in the previous 3-months, for at least 75% of the available coverage windows during a person’s follow-up time. A clinic transfer was defined as attending a study clinic that differed from the study clinic attended at the previous visit and was determined using both biometric fingerprint scanning data and self-reported information on clinic transfers.

Covariates

Sociodemographic and behavior characteristics were assessed at baseline by trained research assistants in a private clinic room. We measured common mental disorders (CMD) using the WHO’s Self Reporting Questionnaire (SRQ-20) [20]. The SRQ-20 ranges from 0 to 20 and a cut-point of ≥ 8 indicates probable common mental health disorders, such as depressive or anxiety symptoms [20]. We assessed functional social support using the 8-item Duke-UNC Functional Social Support Questionnaire (range 1–5) [21]. Responses were summed, averaged and dichotomized at the study population median value [21]. Participants scoring ≥ 3 on the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT-C) were considered to have hazardous alcohol use [22]. Recognizing the COVID-19 pandemic led to disruptions in HIV care, we categorized participant’s enrollment period as pre-COVID-19 (Feb-March 2020), early COVID-19 pandemic (June-December 2020), and later COVID-19 pandemic (January-February 2021). We dichotomized income level based on the mean reported household income per month: ≤ 30,000 or > 30,000 Malawi Kwacha. Information on gestational age and ART regimen was collected from participant’s medical record.

Statistical analysis

The primary goals of the study were to describe multiple indicators of engagement in HIV care across the perinatal period, identify determinants of suboptimal HIV care engagement, and examine if clinic transfers influenced subsequent engagement in HIV care outcomes.

Determinants of suboptimal HIV care engagement

We identified determinants of HIV care engagement at enrollment into ANC, when most WHIV are targeted for retention interventions, for three key engagement outcomes at 9-months postpartum: viral load suppression, retention in HIV care, and cumulative HIV visit coverage (all time-fixed outcomes). Participants with ‘unknown’ retention status (n = 32, 8%) were considered not retained in HIV care at 9 months postpartum. We fit generalized linear models with a log link, Poisson distribution, and robust variance estimator to estimate bivariable and multivariable risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals [23]. To be inclusive in identifying potential determinants, baseline covariates with at least one category with a p-value < 0.10 for each outcome in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable models.

Because HIV appointment adherence and clinic transfers may be indicators of disruptions in HIV care, we evaluated determinants of a first missed HIV visit and first clinic transfer using multivariable sub-distributional proportional hazard models to account for competing events of lost to follow-up (LTFU, defined as > 6 months with no HIV visit) or death (n = 2). Participants were followed from baseline until the first of the following events: (1) the outcome of interest (2) a censoring event of LTFU or death, or (3) administrative censoring at 9 months postpartum.

Clinic transfers and subsequent engagement in HIV care

To investigate whether clinic transfers were associated with subsequent suboptimal engagement in HIV care, we used multivariable modified Poisson models with generalized estimating equations (GEE) [24] to examine if clinic transfers were associated with HIV appointment adherence, HIV visits coverage (time-varying and cumulative), retention in HIV care and viral load suppression at 9 months postpartum (time-fixed).

Missing data were rare (< 5% except for income (11%)) and thus all analyses were complete case. Statistical analyses were completed using Stata, version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas).

Results

Of 627 WHIV screened, 597 (95%) were eligible for inclusion and 402 (67%) enrolled in the study. The most common reasons for not enrolling (n = 195) were not being interested in research (51%), wanting more time and not returning (24%) and not having a phone (12%). Of the 402 participants enrolled, 2 withdrew prior to baseline data collection and 1 died for a total of 399 participants (Fig. 1). Over half the cohort was ≥ 30 years of age and the median gestational age was 20 weeks at the first ANC visit (Table 1). Most participants were already on ART (81%) and taking a regimen of TDF + 3TC + DTG (76%). Most participants had disclosed their HIV status to their partner (87%) and family and friends (76%). Male partner attendance at their first ANC visit was low (12%).

Engagement in HIV care

Programmatic outcomes: Retention and viral load suppression

Without accounting for tracing information, 71% of women were retained at an HIV clinic at 6 weeks postpartum, 60% at 6 months postpartum, and 68% at 9 months postpartum (Table 2). After incorporating tracing information, retention rose to 87% at 6 weeks postpartum, 77% at 6 months postpartum and 89% at 9 months postpartum. At 9 months postpartum, 91% of participants were virally suppressed.

Clinical outcomes: HIV appointment adherence, HIV visit coverage, and clinic transfers

By 6 weeks-postpartum two-thirds of participants missed at least one scheduled HIV visit by ≥ 30 days and this increased to 81% by 9 months postpartum (median 2 missed HIV visits (IQR 1–2; Table 2)). The frequency of missed HIV visits peaked in February 2021, when total study visits and usual care visits were also highest (Figure S1). Missed HIV visits were most common at the 6-week postpartum visit (Figure S2). When examining HIV visit coverage, 19-27% of participants across the study period missed a visit in the previous 3 months and 21% had cumulative HIV coverage with an HIV visit in < 75% of available 3-month coverage windows at 9 months postpartum (Table 2).

By 9 months postpartum, 19% of participants had transferred clinics at least once (median transfers 2, IQR 1–2), with nearly half (12%) of those clinic transfers occurring by 6 weeks postpartum (Table 2). The frequency of clinic transfers did not differ appreciably over time (Figure S1) and clinic transfers were relatively evenly distributed across study visits and usual care HIV visits (Figure S2).

Determinants of suboptimal engagement in HIV care

Determinants of engagement in care at enrollment into ANC

In multivariable analyses, being 35 years or older was protective against viral load > 1000 copies/mL (RR 0.09, 95% CI 0.01, 0.65), while living in an informal settlement was associated with an increased risk of viral load > 1000 copies/mL (RR 2.72, 95% CI 1.05, 7.03; Table 3). Enrollment at the Area 25 clinic (RR 0.36, 95% CI 0.18, 0.72), enrollment early (RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.32, 0.66) and later in the COVID-19 pandemic (RR 0.42, 95% CI 0.19, 0.91), relative to enrollment prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, was associated with improved cumulative HIV visit coverage at 9-months postpartum. No significant baseline predictors of non-retention in HIV care at 9 months were observed.

Determinants of disruptions in HIV clinical care

Participants contributed 2,253 person months to the analysis of determinants of HIV appointment adherence, during which 315 missed a first HIV visit and 8 competing events of death or LTFU occurred. The rate of first missed HIV visits was 13.75 (95% CI 12.27, 15.35) per 100 person months. In multivariable models accounting for age, clinic, new ART initiator status, and several other determinants of engagement in care, only enrollment early (HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.50, 0.89) or later in the COVID-19 pandemic (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.29, 0.71), compared to pre COVID-19, was associated with lower probability of a first missed HIV visit (Table S1).

Participants contributed 3,473 person months to the analysis of determinants of clinic transfers, during which 75 first clinic switches and 54 competing events occurred. Overall, the rate of first clinic transfers was 2.16 (95% CI 1.70, 2.71) per 100 person months. No covariates significantly predicted first clinic transfer, but there was a trend for enrollment early (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.38, 1.26) or later in the COVID-19 pandemic (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.20, 1.63) and a lower probability of first clinic transfer. Enrollment at the Likuni clinic, the most rural of the five clinics, was borderline associated with increased probability of first clinic transfer (HR 2.17, 95% CI 0.97, 4.84; Table S1).

Clinic transfers and subsequent engagement in HIV care

In multivariable analyses, transferring clinics was associated with an increased risk of missing one’s next scheduled HIV visit by ≥ 30 days (RR 1.38, 95% CI 1.06, 1.80; Table 4). Transferring clinics over time was not associated with suboptimal cumulative HIV visit coverage (RR 1.19, 95% CI 0.80, 1.76), non-retention in HIV care (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.44, 2.12) or unsuppressed viral load suppression (RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.19, 2.24) at 9 months postpartum.

Discussion

In this longitudinal study of WHIV enrolled in Option B + during the COVID-19 pandemic, we observed high levels of viral load suppression and retention in HIV care through 9 months postpartum. However, suboptimal HIV visit adherence, HIV visit coverage, and clinic transfers were common. The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted HIV clinical care, leading to an increased likelihood suboptimal HIV visit adherence and cumulative HIV visit coverage, as well as clinic transfers. Transferring clinics in the perinatal period was associated with missing a subsequent HIV visit. but was not associated with an increased risk of unsuppressed viral load and non-retention in HIV care at 9-months postpartum.

In Malawi, and many other low- and middle-income countries, ART is typically delivered at the clinic where a person with HIV registers for treatment, until they request a formal transfer letter to a different clinic [25, 26]. The need for an official clinic transfer has been associated with disruptions to ART treatment and WHIV registering as a new patient in order to access ART [27]. In this study, we used biometric fingerprint scanning to monitor engagement in HIV care across a network of five large urban ANC/ART clinics, which allowed us to identify HIV visits and clinic transfers across sites. Despite improved monitoring, > 25% of participants were traced at each study visit and of those traced and found, 13–38% reported being in care at a non-study clinic, which aligns with previous estimates from the literature [28–30]. Our findings highlight the high level of movement between clinics for HIV care and the ongoing need for and flexible approaches, such as biometric fingerprint scanning, to providing and monitoring engagement in HIV care.

In this cohort, retention in HIV care was close to 80% through 9 months postpartum. We observed higher levels of retention than other estimates from Malawi prior to the COVID-19 pandemic [31–33] but similar to recent estimates among Kenyan WHIV in Option B+ [19, 34]. Differences in retention in HIV care in this population may reflect higher levels of engagement in this population due to active follow-up by study staff, programmatic improvements in Option B+, or the fact that over time WHIV are increasingly entering pregnancy on stable ART and may be less likely to disengage from HIV care [35].

Despite high retention in HIV care during the perinatal period, suboptimal HIV visit adherence, coverage, and clinic transfers were common. Transferring clinics is a risk factor for future missed HIV visits, as observed in this study and elsewhere [12]. In this study, transferring clinic was not associated with an increased risk of unsuppressed viral load, but we may have had limited power to detect an association due to high viral load suppression overall. In the absence of more frequent viral load monitoring, clinic transferring may be important indicators of disruptions in clinical care for WHIV during the perinatal period.

Younger age, initiating ART for the first time, and lack of partner involvement have been noted in the literature to be risk factors for disengagement from HIV care [36–41]. While we observed some similar trends in this study, the COVID-19 pandemic also appeared to play a role in engagement in HIV care. Across multiple indicators, enrollment just prior to the start of the COVID-19 pandemic was strongly associated with disruptions in HIV care. This finding likely reflects that participants enrolled in February and March 2020 were most likely to be affected by disruptions in clinical care [42], which adversely influenced their engagement in HIV care. While the COVID-19 pandemic caused disruptions on an unprecedented scale, Malawi has experienced several recent local emergencies, including cyclones and mudslides that have displaced populations and impacted access to clinical care [43]. Our findings highlight the importance of ensuring ongoing and flexible access to HIV care services during emergencies.

Our study has several important strengths and limitations. First, we combined biometric, clinical, programmatic, and tracing data across a network of five large ANC/ART clinics to comprehensively monitor engagement in HIV care during the perinatal period. In addition to evaluating programmatic indicators of the Option B + program (retention in HIV care and viral load suppression), we described clinical indicators of engagement, including HIV appointment adherence, HIV visit coverage, and clinic transfers. By considering those with ‘unknown’ retention status to be not retained in care at 9 months in statistical analyses, we may have introduced some misclassification of the retention outcome. However, only 8% of the population had an ‘unknown’ retention in HIV care, suggesting any bias is likely to be minimal Our study took place across five urban clinics in Lilongwe during the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, patterns of engagement in HIV care may not be representative of WHIV in rural areas and may reflect changes in behavior due to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Conclusions

In a large cohort of WHIV in Lilongwe, Malawi we observed high levels of retention in HIV care and viral suppression through 9 months postpartum, but frequent missed HIV visits and clinic transfers. Disruptions in care due to the COVID-19 pandemic were evident across multiple indicators of engagement in HIV care and associated with an increased likelihood of clinic transfers, missed HIV visits, and suboptimal HIV visit coverage. Transferring clinics was associated with an increased risk of missing the next scheduled HIV visit. In settings of high retention in HIV care and viral load suppression, clinic transfers may be an important indicator of disruptions in HIV care during the perinatal period. Future intervention should consider focusing retention support on WHIV experiencing clinic to continue Malawi’s progress towards UNAIDS 2025 goals.
