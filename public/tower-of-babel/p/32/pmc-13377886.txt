Clinical Relevance

This case highlights the need for routine prolactin screening in patients receiving sulpiride for irritable bowel syndrome, especially in populations where off-label use is common. Early detection can prevent misdiagnosis and unnecessary imaging or hormonal therapy.

Introduction

Hyperprolactinemia, defined by serum prolactin above the normal range (<25 μg/L in women),1,2 can result from physiological, pathologic, or pharmacologic factors.3,4 While prolactin levels >500 μg/L are highly indicative of a prolactinoma, it is crucial to note that certain medications can also induce marked elevations exceeding 200 μg/L in the absence of an adenoma.5,6

Hyperprolactinemia can be induced by several drug classes, most notably antipsychotics, antidepressants, prokinetics, antihypertensives, and certain gastrointestinal and hormonal therapies. First-generation antipsychotics such as butaperazine, chlorpromazine, fluphenazine, haloperidol, veralipride, and sulpiride,7, 8, 9, 10, 11 and second-generation agents including amisulpiride, levosulpiride, and risperidone,12, 13, 14 are frequent contributors, along with neuroleptic-like drugs such as domperidone and metoclopramide.15 Antidepressants, particularly clomipramine, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors like duloxetine, and monoamine oxidase inhibitors such as pargyline,16, 17, 18 as well as atypical antidepressants (bupropion, mirtazapine), serotonin modulators (indoramine), and selective noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors (reboxetine),18, 19, 20 have also been associated with elevated prolactin levels. Additional implicated agents include H2 receptor antagonists (cimetidine), proton pump inhibitors (lansoprazole), opioids (heroin, morphine),21, 22, 23 and other medications such as methyldopa, estradiol, and carbamazepine.18,24,25

Even minor elevations in prolactin should be considered, as they may indicate a prolactinoma, and microadenomas can also cause marked increases in prolactin levels.1

Common causes of hyperprolactinemia include prolactinoma, hypothyroidism, chronic kidney disease, and dopamine receptor antagonist use, and guidelines recommend evaluating symptomatic patients for medications, kidney disease, hypothyroidism, and parasellar tumors.1,7 Symptoms include galactorrhea, menstrual irregularities, infertility, and hypogonadism in women, and erectile dysfunction, reduced libido, and gynecomastia in men, with chronic elevations affecting bone density and other complications. Treatment targets the underlying cause, with dopamine agonists, particularly bromocriptine and cabergoline, being first-line options for prolactinomas. Approaches vary by etiology, severity, and patient factors.8,9

Materials and Methods

In this case, serum prolactin was measured using a standardized immunoassay. The normal range for women in our laboratory is < 25 μg/L (ng/mL), with women showing higher levels than men. According to the World Health Organization Standard 84/500 formula, a laboratory reading of 1 μg/L (ng/mL) equals 21.2 mIU/L. For diagnosing hyperprolactinemia, dynamic assays of prolactin secretion using thyrotropin-releasing hormone, L-dopa, nomifensine, and domperidone have not proven more effective than a single serum prolactin sample.

Case Presentation

A 38-year-old married woman with a known history of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) presented to the endocrinology clinic with a 1-month history of intermittent milky discharge from both breasts (galactorrhea) and breast tenderness. She reported regular menstrual cycles, with her last menstrual period occurring 1 month before the visit. She denied any symptoms of pituitary compression, such as headaches or visual disturbances.

The patient reported a 2-year history of IBS, managed with lifestyle and dietary modifications. She had previously used mebeverine for symptom control but discontinued it several months before starting Sulpiride.

At presentation, she had stable clinical observations, with a body mass index of 27.78 kg/m2. She had no documented history of psychiatric illness. Her obstetric history included 2 prior full-term pregnancies, with 2 children currently aged 7 and 10 years, having breastfed each child for approximately 1 year. She was not currently pregnant or lactating.

One month before presentation, for the management of IBS symptoms, the patient was prescribed Sulpiride 50 mg once daily at a private hospital.

Following the onset of galactorrhea, she was referred for endocrine evaluation. A comprehensive hormonal panel was obtained. Key laboratory findings at this time are summarized in Table 1. Notably, her serum prolactin level was markedly elevated at 339.88 ng/mL, and a pregnancy test was negative.

To exclude a pituitary adenoma as the cause of the severe hyperprolactinemia, a pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed. The scan was entirely normal, showing no evidence of a pituitary mass, abnormal enhancement, or structural abnormalities in the suprasellar region, optic chiasm, or cavernous sinuses.

Based on the temporal relationship between starting sulpiride and symptom onset, the markedly elevated prolactin level, and the normal pituitary MRI, a diagnosis of Sulpiride-induced severe hyperprolactinemia was made. The intervention was to discontinue sulpiride immediately.

The patient was reassessed 2 weeks after stopping the medication. At this follow-up, her symptoms of galactorrhea and breast tenderness had completely resolved. A repeat serum prolactin level had decreased to 7.96 ng/mL, well within the normal range. The trend in prolactin levels is illustrated in Table 2.

Discussion

This case reports a rare instance of severe hyperprolactinemia (>300 ng/mL) induced by a low dose (50 mg/d) of sulpiride in a patient with IBS, in the absence of a pituitary adenoma. Sulpiride functions as an atypical antipsychotic by blocking dopamine D2/D3 receptors, thereby decreasing dopaminergic suppression of prolactin release and leading to elevated prolactin secretion.11,26,27

The severity of the elevation in this patient can be explained by sulpiride's pharmacological properties. Antipsychotics with a reduced ability to cross the blood–brain barrier, such as those in the amisulpride/sulpiride group, tend to have a more significant impact on the pituitary gland, which lies outside the blood–brain barrier.28 This results in higher drug concentrations in the pituitary and a more pronounced hyperprolactinemic effect compared to other antipsychotics.29,12 While sulpiride is often associated with elevated prolactin, there are currently few published reports of hyperprolactinemia of this magnitude at such a low dose (50 mg/d).11 In this instance, the prolactin level rose above 300 ng/mL, which is typically a level that might raise suspicion for a prolactinoma.5

This case underscores 2 critical clinical points. First, sulpiride can induce severe hyperprolactinemia even at low, nonpsychiatric doses used for gastrointestinal indications. Second, while levels above 250 μg/L often suggest a prolactinoma, drug-induced etiologies must be aggressively pursued, as demonstrated here by the normal pituitary MRI and rapid normalization of prolactin upon drug cessation.5 Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for this adverse effect and monitor patients on sulpiride for symptoms of galactorrhea or menstrual irregularity, regardless of the prescribed dose.

Conclusion

The use of medication often results in hyperprolactinemia, highlighting the importance of differentiating it from pathologic causes such as prolactinomas. Sulpiride should be initiated with caution due to its potential to induce galactorrhea, even at small doses, and in sensitive groups. Clinicians should assess patients for galactorrhea during follow-up appointments. Our study has found cases of severe hyperprolactinemia (over 300 μg/L), with prolactin levels above 250 μg/L typically indicating a possible prolactinoma. Additionally, it is vital to consider non–prolactin-secreting tumors initially, as even slight increases in prolactin levels could suggest the presence of a prolactinoma.
