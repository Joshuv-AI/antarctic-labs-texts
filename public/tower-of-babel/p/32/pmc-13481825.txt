Improving the Patient Experience in Pediatric Cystometrography: A Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Assia Comella, Amy Bodley, Hayley Nugara, Edwina Mcdonald, Neil Price, Elizabeth Dally, Chris Kimber, Kiarash Taghavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCystometrography, despite its invasive nature, is a crucial investigation for understanding and managing the bladder in children with neurogenic, non‐neurogenic and congenital bladder conditions. Results are influenced by child behaviors and artefacts, but results can be critical in developing patient‐specific management plans. This study aims to assess the child's anxiety level and distress levels prior to testing, how their behaviors affect test outcomes, and the overall experience of the child and their families.MethodsThis is a prospective observational study conducted at Monash Children's Hospital (MCH) between January 2023 and January 2025. Patients requiring cystometrography completed a Visual Analog Scale for Anxiety (VAS‐A) score prior to the procedure to assess their anxiety. During the appointment, the Brief Behavioral Distress Scale (BBDS) score was completed by the attending consultant to objectively assess patients' behavior and its impact on test results. Finally, the Urodynamics Experience Questionnaire was completed by patients to provide feedback on their experience.ResultsTwenty patients were enrolled in the study. Eight patients were under 4 years of age (seven on clean intermittent catheters prior to the study, CICs) and 12 patients were 5–17 years old (two on CICs prior to the study). The median VAS‐A score was 4.3. BBDS results showed that in four patients (20%) behavior significantly compromised the test results. Notably, the four patients with catheters inserted under general anesthetic did not exhibit behaviors that interfered with the test results. The Urodynamics Experience Questionnaire found that 17 patients (85%) experienced some level of distress, with 16 (80%) reporting discomfort.DiscussionMost patients undergoing cystometrography are not adequately prepared, leading to higher anxiety prior to the procedure than afterward. Patients using CICs prior to the test demonstrated fewer disruptive behaviors during the test. In children not performing CIC, catheter placement under general anesthetic resulted in fewer disruptive behaviors.ConclusionEnhancing the patient and family experience in pediatric urodynamics is essential. Future efforts should focus on multimodal preparation specific to the child's age and stage. Furthermore, managing anxiety and minimizing discomfort will improve the overall quality and outcomes of cystometrography in children.

Introduction

In pediatrics, balancing the need for accurate diagnostic information with the child and family experience is a daily tension. Cystometrography (CMG), unlike many invasive procedures in children, generally requires patient collaboration and is not performed under general anesthesia (Figure 1). However, the child's experience during CMG can negatively impact the accuracy of the results. These results are vital for informing subsequent clinical decisions and management [1, 2].

Photo of room where cystometrography is performed.

CMG offers valuable insight into bladder function, guiding management, yet presenting unique challenges [3, 4]. However, the insertion of urethral and rectal catheters, necessary for assessing detrusor pressure, is expectedly distressing for children. This can compromise data quality, even when distraction techniques and support are in place. Spin‐offs of inaccurate data capture and interpretation have clinical impacts. Adding to these investigation‐specific challenges, there is also lack of age‐appropriate pediatric‐validated behavioral and anxiety level questionnaires that can be used to objectively study the distress level that children experience while undertaking these investigations. It is crucial to be able to study these aspects with validated tools in order to develop measures targeting the most distressing steps of the investigations in pediatrics.

Previous research, has highlighted catheter insertion as a particularly distressing aspect of urodynamics [5]. Tucker et al. [6] established the reliability and validity of the Brief Behavioral Distress Scale (BBDS) to evaluate children's procedure‐related distress behaviors and provide clinicians with a valuable assessment tool. This was validated for patients aged between 2 and 10 years old (Figure 2).

The Brief Behavioral Distress Scale (BBDS).

The aim of this study was to analyze child and family experiences of CMG, aiming to identify barriers and opportunities for improvement in the current paradigm to gain the most accurate results.

Materials & Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted at Monash Children's Hospital (MCH, Melbourne) between January 2023 and January 2025, following approval from Monash Health Ethics (Reference number: RES‐23‐0000‐132A). Pediatric urodynamic studies at MCH occur bi‐weekly with one or two patients being booked per session. The surgeon booking the child for CMG will explain to the family and child why the test is being recommended and what this practically involved, specifically urethral and rectal catheters. Further practicalities on the day are reviewed, and the need for possible procedural sedation (midazolam and/or nitrous oxide) for catheter insertion is assessed. When this was needed a dose of 0.3 mg/kg of oral midazolam was used prior to the procedure according to hospital sedation policy. In some instances, if the patient was still in distress and insertion of the catheters was otherwise impossible, nitrous oxide (N2O) was also used in addition to the previously given dose of midazolam. The information provided is augmented by a patient information sheet (Appendix 1 and 2). On the day of the procedure the child is admitted to the day ward, and brought to the CMG suite (Figure 1). Team members present are pediatric stoma therapists, pediatric urology fellow, pediatric urologist and radiographer. The suite has TV screens and is fitted with speakers and music or videos are played as per the child or young adults preference. If the study proves impossible then occasionally the rectal catheter is omitted (all of the study patients completed the investigation with urethral and rectal catheters).

At the time of booking (typically weeks in advance), one of the investigators (AC) contacted families to obtain verbal consent and emailed a participant information sheet. Written consent was confirmed on the day of CMG.

Data Collection Involved the Following

Visual Analog Scale for Anxiety (VAS‐A): Completed upon arrival to assess patient anxiety related to CMG (Figure 3). The VAS‐A is a visual, 10‐point scale (0 = “not worried at all,” 10 = “very worried”). Older children (≥ 5 years) completed the scale themselves; parents/caregivers completed it for younger children (< 5 years). Brief Behavioral Distress Scale (BBDS): Completed by the attending consultant pediatric urologist during the CMG to assess disruptive behaviors and their potential impact on study results (Figure 2). This is a tool validated by Tucket et al. for patients between 2 and 10 years of age [6]. Our cohort of patients was between 7 months old and 11 years old, with six patients < 2 years old and three patients aged 11 years old. We do acknowledge that this assessment tool was not validated by Tacker et al. in their study for these patients, but as previously mentioned, there is lack of pediatric validated behavioral distress scales and the authors decided to use the same validated tool for all ages in order to provide a comparable analysis in amongst all the patients [6]. Urodynamics Experience Questionnaire: Administered to the patient or parent/guardian post‐CMG to gather feedback (Figure 4). Patients were all 6 years old or younger. This validated, standardized 11‐item tool, adapted from Nelson et al., [7] assesses pain, anxiety, time perception, and satisfaction. Again, we do acknowledge that Nelson et al. only surveyed patients 6 years old or younger and our cohort of patient has children also older than 6 years old, but as previously stated there is lack in the literature of validated questionnaires [6]. In addition Nelson et al. validated a simple and clear tool in a pediatric population, specifically looking at genitourinary investigations, therefore it was deemed to be the most reliable questionnaire to eb used to explore children's experience for our study [6].

Results

During the study period, 24 urodynamics studies were performed. Twenty patients were recruited; four were ineligible (two families non‐English speaking, two residing outside the catchment area). The final cohort (Table 1) included patients between 7 months old and 13 years old. Fourteen had not undergone prior CMG, and 11 were not on clean intermitted catheters (CICs). Eight patients were between 0 and 4 years old (seven on CICs); and 12 patients were 5–16 years old (two on CICs). In terms of required sedation: one patient required oral midazolam only to undertake the procedure successfully; one patient required N2O only as this and trained staff were readily available and it has a shorter‐lasting effect; two patients required both midazolam and N2O. Regarding these latter two patients; one was a 13 year‐old female with end‐stage renal failure and undertaking urodynamics prior to renal transplant, and one a 6 year‐old male for overactive bladder requiring previous botox treatment. Both patients were not performing CICs and both having never undertaken urodynamics before. The patient that received only oral midazolam was a 17 months old female that had undergone a myelomeningocele repair and a vesicostomy formation in the past; while the patient that required only N2O was a 5 year‐old patient that had posterior urethral valves resected at birth.

Discussion

Cystometrography is an invaluable tool for informing critical management decisions in pediatric urology by assessing bladder function and identifying at‐risk bladders [8]. However, the invasive nature of the procedure presents challenges, particularly in the pediatric population, where patient experience directly impacts data quality [8]. Our study at Monash Children's Hospital (MCH) highlights this tension, revealing that despite being conducted in a tertiary center with experienced staff, a significant portion of patients experienced discomfort.

A key finding was the associated between CIC use and reduced anxiety. Patients on CICs prior to CMG had significantly lower VAS‐A scores, potentially because they were accustomed to catheterization. This is further supported by the fact that all patients whose results were compromised by behavior were not performing CICs prior to the study, suggesting catheter insertion may be a key course of distress. Interestingly, younger patients were more likely to be on CICs, reflecting the MCH's model of care for children with spina bifida. This may imply that children on CICs having undergone education and preparation specific to this treatment, are less distressed by catheterization (particularly in an unfamiliar environment).

Another point to consider is the difference between reported distress by the patients, compared to their families perspective of their distress. A significantly higher number of patients reported they were “very distressed,” compared to their parents who reported they were “not distressed at all.” This difference needs to be taken into consideration when planning and performing CMG with children, in regards to expectation setting and management of anxiety.

Our findings align with previous research highlighting the importance of preparation and education. Finkelstein et al. [5] found that patients were more anxious before urodynamics, which is consistent with our observation that 11 patients anticipated greater anxiety than they experienced. Similarly, Thacker et al. emphasized the role of adequate information in managing distress, which supports the need for interventions to improve patient understanding and expectations [9].

These findings should be interpreted in light of our study's limitations. As an observational study with a relatively small sample size (n = 20), our results may not be generalizable to all pediatric populations and settings. However, the 100% response rate strengthens the validity of our data. Another limitation of the study was the use of sedation (midazolam and/or N2O) in some of the studied patients that might have impacted on the results and patients' anxiety evaluation. The use of anxiolytics can alter prefrontal cortex activity and therefore the neural signaling system. This can therefore potentially alter communication and experiences [10, 11, 12]. The authors acknowledge this, but as this was an observational study, no deviation from the decisions made by the clinician undertaking the investigation was allowed.

These research findings highlight challenges in identifying child anxiety during invasive investigations, such as CMG. There is clear need for evidence‐based age‐appropriate questionnaires and tools to precisely evaluate this. Strategies can then be employed to improve patient and family experience, as well as improve investigation fidelity. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating age‐specific interventions to minimize anxiety and distress relating to CMG. Future studies may identify best practices for patient and family preparation, distraction techniques, and child‐life specialist involvement. Exploring the impact of these interventions on CMG outcomes is warranted. As alternative technologies are also developed, the role and methods of the current CMG paradigm also need to be re‐evaluated [13]. The study group is planning an interventional study subsequent to this baseline data, borrowing philosophies developed to prepare children for MRI (e.g., Mock‐MRI) and burns dressing changes.

Conclusions

Ultimately, this study underscores the need to prioritize patient and family experience in pediatric urodynamics. Health professional need to be alert to potential differences in the interpretation of distress between patient experience and caregiver reports, as these discrepancies can impact communication and management. By implementing targeted strategies to reduce anxiety and distress, we can improve data quality, enhance patient satisfaction, and optimize clinical decision‐making.
