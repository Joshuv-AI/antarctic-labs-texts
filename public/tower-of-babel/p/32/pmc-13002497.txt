Nonsurgical Management of Postinfarction Left Ventricular Free Wall Rupture With a Percutaneous Ventricular Assist Device
Authors: Yu Ando, Tetsuji Morishita, Hiroaki Matsuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft ventricular free wall rupture (LVFWR) is a fatal complication of acute myocardial infarction. Successful nonsurgical management using the IMPELLA (ABIOMED) has not been previously reported.Case SummaryA 68-year-old man presented with anterior ST-elevation myocardial infarction underwent emergency percutaneous coronary intervention. History of Presentation

A 68-year-old man presented to a local clinic with acute-onset chest pain. Based on the clinical presentation and electrocardiographic findings consistent with ST-elevation myocardial infarction, he was urgently transferred to our tertiary care facility for primary percutaneous coronary intervention.

Past Medical History

The patient had been taking esomeprazole for chronic gastritis. Other than that, there was no notable past medical history or medication use.

Differential Diagnosis

Acute coronary syndrome, myocarditis, acute aortic dissection, pulmonary artery embolism, and Takotsubo cardiomyopathy were considered among the differential diagnoses.

Investigations

On admission, the patient was alert and oriented. He was 170.0 cm tall and weighed 66.6 kg (body mass index, 23.0 kg/m2), with blood pressure of 140/85 mm Hg and heart rate of 80 beats/min. Cardiopulmonary examination was unremarkable. The electrocardiogram showed ST-segment elevation in multiple precordial leads, suggestive of extensive anterior wall acute myocardial infarction (AMI), with Q waves indicating a delay since symptom onset (Figure 1A). Chest x-ray showed mild pulmonary congestion. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe hypokinesis of the anterior septum and apical segments, with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 15%. No pericardial effusion was detected. The patient was diagnosed with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and considered a candidate for emergency coronary angiography.

Management and Outcome

Emergent coronary angiography revealed complete thrombotic occlusion of the proximal left anterior descending artery #7 segment. Given the persistence of chest pain and ST-segment elevation, emergent revascularization was indicated. A drug-eluting stent (Xience Sierra 2.75 mm/38 mm; Abbott) was deployed. After intervention, coronary flow was assessed (Figure 1B) as TIMI flow grade 2, with evidence of a to-and-flo pattern in the left anterior descending artery. Due to the extensive infarct area and suspected myocardial edema with increased left ventricle (LV) filling pressures, intra-aortic balloon pumping was initiated, and the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit.

Approximately 4 hours after admission, the patient experienced abrupt hemodynamic deterioration: blood pressure dropped to 73/45 mm Hg, heart rate increased to 102 beats/min, and he exhibited diaphoresis and peripheral coldness. These findings were consistent with Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions shock stage D. Echocardiography showed new pericardial effusion with right atrial and right ventricular collapse, consistent with cardiac tamponade.

Emergency pericardiocentesis yielded a large volume of hemorrhagic effusion, raising suspicion of oozing-type left ventricular free wall rupture (LVFWR). Due to the severely impaired LV function, catecholamines were administered intravenously, and an IMPELLA CP device (ABIOMED) was inserted via the femoral artery to provide mechanical circulatory support. Heparin was titrated to maintain an activated clotting time (Figures 1C and 1D) of 160-180 s and an activated partial thromboplastin time of 1.5-2.0× baseline.

Pericardial drainage progressively decreased, and serial echocardiograms showed no further accumulation. Surgical intervention was deferred, and the patient remained under close observation in the intensive care unit. By hospital day 2, drainage had decreased to <100 mL/day and became serosanguinous. With improving LV function, IMPELLA weaning was initiated.

On days 3 and 4, drainage further declined to 60 mL/day and became light brown. No pericardial reaccumulation was observed, so the pericardial drain was removed. By hospital day 6, the IMPELLA device was successfully explanted. Echocardiography showed LV ejection fraction improved to 31% without significant pericardial effusion. Catecholamine support was withdrawn on day 12 (Figure 1E), and cardiac rehabilitation was initiated. He was discharged home in stable condition on day 16.

Successful Nonsurgical Management of Oozing-Type LVFWR Following STEMI Using a Percutaneous Left Ventricular Assist Device

A patient with anterior STEMI due to LAD occlusion underwent primary PCI with a drug-eluting stent followed by intraaortic balloon pumping support. Four hours later, he developed cardiac tamponade caused by oozing-type LVFWR. After IMPELLA CP insertion, pericardial drainage decreased without recurrence. The device was removed on day 6, and he was discharged uneventfully on day 16. Discussion

While several reports have described successful surgical management of LVFWR following AMI utilizing IMPELLA support, cases of survival without surgical intervention remain exceedingly rare.1,2 This case illustrates successful nonsurgical management of oozing-type LVFWR using the IMPELLA device following extensive AMI. LVFWR occurs in approximately 2% of AMI cases and contributes to around 10% of acute-phase mortality.3 LVFWR still accounts for 24%-61% of in-hospital disease-related mortality and requires prompt diagnosis and treatment.4

Clinically, LVFWR is classified into 3 types: 1) blow-out type, characterized by sudden rupture and circulatory collapse; 2) oozing type, involving slow, continuous bleeding from the infarcted myocardium; and 3) pseudoaneurysm type, where the rupture site adheres to the pericardium and forms a pseudoaneurysm.5 All these complications typically demand emergent surgical repair.

In the present case, the gradual accumulation of pericardial fluid and clinical progression were consistent with oozing-type rupture. The IMPELLA device was used to unload the LV, reducing intracavitary pressure and promoting hemostasis.

However, IMPELLA support necessitates continuous anticoagulation, in turn increasing the risk of bleeding and hemolysis. A delicate balance between maintaining device patency and minimizing bleeding is thus critical. When bleeding is uncontrolled or pericardial effusion recurs, surgical intervention should not be delayed. Surgical repair must be emphasized as the definitive treatment for LVFWR, and surgical intervention should never be withheld or delayed when clinically indicated. Nevertheless, it should be acknowledged as a limitation that this report describes a single case, and whether this concept can be generalized or influenced by survival bias remains uncertain.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this case suggests that IMPELLA support may represent a potential nonsurgical option for managing select cases of LVFWR. With careful monitoring and patient selection, percutaneous ventricular assist devices might serve as a temporary bridge to recovery in high-risk situations.
