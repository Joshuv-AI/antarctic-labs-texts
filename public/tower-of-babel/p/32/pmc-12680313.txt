Eosinophilic esophagitis: Examining the impact of disease
Authors: Thomas L Offerle, Nidhi R Talasani, Emily C McGowan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is an increasingly common allergic and gastrointestinal condition affecting both children and adults. The objective of this review was to summarize the existing literature on the impact of EoE on quality of life, physical well-being, psychosocial functioning, and healthcare utilization.Methods:Literature searches were performed using PubMed. Keyword combinations included those representing eosinophilic esophagitis and quality of life (QOL), anxiety, depression, sleep disturbance, financial impact, and healthcare utilization.Results:EoE impacts multiple facets of patients’ lives, and this does not always correlate with endoscopic and histologic findings. QOL appears to be significantly associated with symptom burden, which can be influenced by symptom-specific anxiety and esophageal hypervigiliance. Patients with EoE also have increased health-care costs, which are primarily due to provider visits, diagnostic procedures, medications, disease complications, and “hidden costs.”Conclusion:Providers caring for patients with EoE should be aware of the complex interplay between the emotional, mental, social, and financial impacts of this disease and consider them carefully during shared decision-making.Keywords: eosinophilic esophagitis, quality of life, burden of disease

Over the past 30 years, eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) has transformed from a case-reportable disease to a major cause of upper-gastrointestinal morbidity. EoE is characterized by symptoms of esophageal dysfunction and eosinophil-predominant inflammation in the esophageal tissue in the absence of other causes of eosinophilic inflammation.1 Symptoms of EoE vary by the age of presentation and often include feeding aversion, abdominal pain, and vomiting in children, and dysphagia or food impactions in adolescents and adults. Patients with EoE can have symptoms for years before diagnosis, and untreated disease can lead to complications, e.g., esophageal strictures and narrowing, food impactions, and increased resistance to available therapies. Research over the past decade has improved our understanding of the impact of EoE on patient-centered measures. In this review, we summarized the existing literature on the impact of EoE on quality of life (QOL), psychosocial domains, health-care utilization, and financial cost.

QOL

QOL refers to an individual’s perception of his or her overall well-being and satisfaction across multiple life domains.2 It is a complex function with significant variability among groups and individuals, and includes a person’s physical health, social and psychological functioning, and relationship to his or her environment.3 Given such a broad, all-encompassing measure, the concept of health-related QOL (HR-QOL) has also been established to better understand how specific disease entities and their therapies affect QOL. In measuring HR-QOL, both generic- or disease-specific tools may be used, depending on the study objective. Generic instruments, e.g., the 36-item Short Form, are multipurpose surveys commonly used in comparisons between diseases or to evaluate effects of different treatment regimens.4 Alternatively, disease-specific tools allow for a more focused appraisal of the disease burden and clinical outcomes.

The first QOL survey developed specifically for EoE was the Adult Eosinophilic Esophagitis Quality of Life Questionnaire, which was developed and validated by Taft et al.,5 in 2011. This survey scores patients according to five domains: eating/diet impact, social impact, emotional impact, disease anxiety, and choking anxiety.5 Around the same time, Franciosi et al.6 performed semistructured interviews and identified themes that affect QOL in pediatric patients with EoE, including feelings of being different than their family and peers, concerns about diet and medication adherence, difficulties eating food, and worries about their symptoms and illness. This led to the development and validation of the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory™ EoE module, a pediatric EoE-specific survey.7 The development and validation of these surveys enabled subsequent studies that assessed specific disease and patient factors that affect QOL in adults and children with EoE.

Over the past decade, much research has been performed to establish determinant factors that affect HR-QOL and to evaluate the implications of treatment. The most up-to-date review was published by van Klink and Bredenoord2 in 2024 and aggregates results from 16 studies that measured generic- and disease-specific HR-QOL in adult patients with EoE. Whereas some results vary based on study design and instrument used, it is clear that increased symptom severity and disease status are correlated with adverse patient perceptions of their EoE HR-QOL.8–15 Similarly, longer disease duration has been reported to negatively impact HR-QOL.10,16 Other factors, such as higher educational level, the presence of specific symptoms (dysphagia, recurrent food impaction), perceived disease stigma, and certain treatments (i.e., food restrictions), are also implicated in lower HR-QOL scores.11,17,18 Interestingly, there are conflicting data on the effect of gender on HR-QOL.17,19,20

Because symptom severity is a key driver of adverse HR-QOL in patients with EoE, it is not surprising that successful treatment of EoE improves QOL measurements. Swallowed topical steroids have been shown to improve the social and emotional impacts of EoE and decrease swallowing anxiety.21,22 Furthermore, treatment with dupilumab has been shown to improve HR-QOL scores when compared with placebo.23,24 Conversely, results of several studies have shown that multifood elimination diets may lead to increased diet-related anxiety, mealtime social anxiety, and difficulty with planning meals.12,25,26 Interestingly, this was not seen in milk-only elimination diets, and, in fact, a subset of patients reported improved QOL with this less-restrictive dietary option.27 Whether this improvement in QOL persists long-term with these various treatment options is unclear and warrants further study.

PSYCHOSOCIAL IMPACT

QOL is a functional measurement that is related to, but distinct from, psychosocial assessments, which include additional characteristics such as anxiety, depression, sleep quality, somatization, social difficulties, and work and/or school impairment. In 2013, Harris et al.28 performed a retrospective review of 64 children with EoE who had undergone a behavioral health assessment and found that 69% of patients reported some form of psychosocial concern. In younger children, sleep disturbances and feeding problems were most common, whereas older children more often experienced anxious behavior and depressive feelings.28 Since this time, additional studies have assessed the impact of an EoE diagnosis on anxiety, depression, sleep quality, and social functioning, which will be reviewed below.

Anxiety and Depression

Analysis of emerging data suggests that there may be an association between EoE and anxiety and depression. In 2017, Chehade et al.29 characterized a large population of patients with EoE (N = 705) and found a high prevalence of anxiety and/or depression, which increased with age (≤11 years old: 9.3%; 11–18 years: 19%; ≥18 years: 24%). Subsequent studies found that 26–28% of patients with EoE have a diagnosis of anxiety or depression, which is higher than in the general population, and this seems to be more prevalent in adults (33%) than in children (15%).30,31 In a cross-sectional study with longitudinal follow-up in Sweden, Röjler et al.32 found that having a diagnosis of EoE was associated with mood disorders, anxiety disorders, and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder. Furthermore, subjects diagnosed with EoE had a higher risk for developing a psychiatric disorder in the 5 years after their EoE diagnosis than matched controls, and this risk was most pronounced among those diagnosed with EoE in childhood.32 Analysis of emerging data also suggests that children who were recently diagnosed with EoE have more anxiety than those with a longer diagnosis,33 which may be attributed to concerns about the new diagnosis, diagnostic tests, and initiation of treatment. These findings are likely clinically relevant because depression has been associated with poorer medication adherence in eosinophilic gastrointestinal disease,34 including EoE,35 and anxiety may impact reported symptoms and treatment response.

In addition to assessments of overall anxiety, recent work has examined EoE-specific measures of anxiety, including esophageal hypervigilance (the tendency to overly focus attention on physical sensations in the esophagus) and symptom-specific anxiety. In 2021, Taft et al.36 administered the esophageal hypervigilance and anxiety scale to 103 adults with EoE undergoing clinical care and assessed its association with histologic and endoscopic measurements of EoE disease activity, symptoms, and QOL. They found that 46% of patients with EoE had elevated hypervigilance and symptom-specific anxiety, and these factors were the most important predictors of symptoms and decreased QOL.36 The researchers concluded that hypervigilance and symptom-specific anxiety should be assessed in patients with persistent symptoms despite histologic and endoscopic remission.

Sleep Quality

In recent years, measures of sleep disturbance have been assessed in children with EoE. In a cross-sectional study of 55 children with EoE who had completed 12 consecutive nights of ambulatory sleep monitoring, Lynch et al.37 found that children with EoE who had persistent epigastric pain had greater sleep disturbances, lower sleep efficiency, and greater episodes of waking after sleep onset. Two years later, Siriwat et al.38 performed a retrospective study of children with EoE who were referred to sleep clinics and found that children with EoE frequently reported snoring, restless sleep, leg jerking and/or discomfort, and daytime sleepiness. Compared with controls, children with EoE had significantly lower rapid eye movement sleep, increased periodic leg movements index, and increased arousal index.38 Most recently, Jensen et al.33 surveyed 87 children with EoE by using the pediatric sleep questionnaire and found that children with concomitant neurodevelopmental conditions had higher scores for sleep disordered breathing. A high proportion of adults with EoE have also been shown to have sleep disturbance (50%),39 but further research in this population is warranted.

Social and/or Emotional Impact

The social and emotional impacts of EoE have been well described by patients and caregivers, who report feelings of isolation and frustration, and the negative impacts on school, work, and social activities.5,6 Before diagnosis, patients and caregivers report feeling confused, frustrated, anxious, lonely, and misunderstood.40 After diagnosis, concerns remain about the cost of medications, diagnostic procedures, and dietary substitutions.40 The social impact of EoE may be further heightened in patients who are treated with extensive elimination diets, which can lead to further social limitations, diet-related anxiety, and maladaptive feeding.25,41 When examining disease-specific factors, it seems that symptoms of food impaction and epigastric pain contribute to lower social impact dimensions on QOL assessments in adults8,17 and children,15 respectively.

EoE Impact Questionnaire

To further assess how treatment affects the psychosocial impact of EoE, McCann et al.42 developed and validated the. This tool comprises 11 questions that assess the impact of EoE on emotional, social, work and school, and sleep domains. This survey was administered during the phase III study of dupilumab in adolescent and adult EoE, and a significant improvement was seen in EoE impact questionnaire scores after treatment with dupilumab 300 mg weekly.24 In particular, significant improvements were noted in patients feeling less bothered by symptoms, having fewer worries about swallowing, choking, or swallowing in public, and having less disturbed sleep.24 Whether similar improvements are also seen with other treatments, such as proton pump inhibitors, swallowed steroids, or elimination diets, is currently unknown and warrants further study.

FINANCIAL IMPACT

In addition to the impact on QOL and psychosocial domains, the diagnosis of EoE comes with an increase in health-care resource utilization (HCRU) and financial cost to the patient in the United States.43,44 Before the diagnosis, patients experience costs attributed to consultations from multiple specialists and diagnostic procedures. After the diagnosis, patients accrue costs from outpatient follow-up visits, medications, and additional procedures. If complications from the disease occur, then there are further costs associated with emergency department visits and potentially hospitalizations. In 2015, Jensen et al.43 examined these direct medical costs among 8135 cases of patients with EoE and 32,540 matched controls, and found that the median annual cost per EoE case was $3304 compared with $1001 per matched control. These costs included outpatient visits ($2508), endoscopies ($157), and medications ($325), which result in an annual estimated health-care cost of $1.4 billion.43 In comparison, the per-person direct medical cost estimates for asthma, food allergy, and Crohn disease are $3266,45 $2081,46 and $12,000,47 respectively.

Since this time, results of studies have shown that the prevalence of EoE, the volume of EoE-associated emergency department visits48 and the number of EoE-related hospitalizations49 continues to increase, so it is likely that the annual HCRU and costs are now even higher. A recent case-cohort study of claims data estimated that the total costs associated with EoE care could now reach up to $3 billion annually.44 Interestingly, most of the HCRU and costs in this study were accrued from outpatient visits.44 It will be interesting to see, moving forward, if these cost estimates shift with the recent U.S. Food and Drug Administration approvals of dupilumab and budesonide oral suspension as well as with the emergence of less-invasive esophageal sampling methodologies such as transnasal endoscopy50 and the esophageal string test.51

In addition to these measured costs, there are “hidden” costs associated with EoE care that are not included in the above analyses. These include costs attributed to the delay in EoE diagnosis and psychosocial impacts of the disease. Patients also have costs associated with time missed from work and school, traveling to centers that have expertise in EoE, and increased life insurance policies.52 In addition, patients on elimination diets have increased costs associated with obtaining food substitutes and traveling to additional grocery stores to find specialty items, which may be reduced when pursuing a less-extensive elimination diet (i.e., two foods versus four or six foods).53,54 Given these high costs outlined above, strategies and guidelines for providing cost-effective care for patients with EoE are needed.55

CLINICAL APPROACH TO ASSESSING THE IMPACT OF EOE

Providers can help improve the influence of EoE on a patient’s QOL, psychosocial domains, and financial cost by assessing for these factors during clinical care and by using this information in shared decision-making discussions. For example, at all visits, providers should ask patients about the presence of EoE symptoms, including the IMPACT (imbibe fluids with meals; modify food; prolong meal times; avoid hard texture foods; chew excessively; turn away tablets/pills) adaptive behaviors.56 Providers should, in addition, ask questions about the patient’s adherence to his or her treatment regimen as well as financial and social factors that could be contributing to noncompliance. In addition, providers can assess whether the patient is experiencing anxiety about his or her symptoms or treatment regimen, and whether these factors are affecting the patient’s activities of daily life (social limitations, financial restrictions, absence from work and/or school). Based on these factors, as well as the patient’s endoscopic and histologic disease activity, providers and patients and /or families can then participate in shared-decision making to identify the best management strategy, at that time, for the patient.

CONCLUSION

Over the past decade, it has become increasingly recognized that EoE impacts multiple facets of patients’ lives and that this does not always correlate with endoscopic and histologic disease end points. QOL seems to be significantly impacted by symptom burden, which, in turn, can be influenced by symptom-specific anxiety and esophageal hypervigilance. Furthermore, patients with EoE are faced with increased health-care utilization and costs that stem from outpatient visits, diagnostic procedures, medications, and disease complications. Providers should be aware of these emotional, mental, social, and financial impacts, and assess for these factors while caring for patients with EoE.
