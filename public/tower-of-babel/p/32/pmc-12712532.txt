A World Without Dialysis or a World With Dialysis for All Who Need It?
Authors: Massimo Torreggiani, Giorgina Barbara Piccoli
Published: 2025

To the Editor

We read with great interest the study by Tangri et al.,1 which foresees a 3.2% to 23.2% reduction in the prevalence of kidney replacement therapy (KRT) over the next 10 years through the application of guideline-directed medical therapies. Although evidence-based guidelines are aimed at ultimately reducing the need for KRT, excessive optimism and overreliance risk conveying misleading messages. The World Health Organization recently acknowledged chronic kidney disease as a priority.2,3 Increased life expectancy and decrease in competitive mortality play a role in the growing demand for KRT in high-income as well as in low to medium-income countries.4, 5 The optimism elicited by the availability of new drugs echoes the unmet expectations for renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors in the 1990s, which, by improving cardiovascular health, paradoxically contributed to increasing the need for KRT.S1

If lifespan were to increase in low to medium-income countries, where life expectancy is 20 years lower than in high income countries, and if dialysis were to become universally accessible, the need for KRT will rise. In their brilliant paper, the authors acknowledge only the limitation of underestimating the effects of guideline-directed medical therapies, thus implying that their magnitude could only be greater, without considering different scenarios.

Although the World Health Organization urges its member stating “to integrate prevention, early detection, and management of kidney disease (…) by an adequately trained health workforce, to all individuals without any discrimination (…),” screening programs are not integrated in clinical practice, and guideline-based indications fail to consider early nephrology referral.S2

As Sackett pointed out, evidence-based medicine is the “conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patient.”S3

We hope that the growing burden of chronic kidney disease highlighted by this paper, will foster screening programs for early chronic kidney disease detection, where the benefit of timely care is expected to be the highest. Overly optimistic projections may limit resources allocated to kidney care; whereas early referral, the judicious use of evidence and personalized care require a reinforced nephrology taskforce. Hence, we suggest that before and while dreaming of a world without dialysis, we should invest for a world where dialysis is available for all those who can benefit from it.
