Diagnostic Performance of Fibrosis‐4 Index, Nonalcoholic Fatty Liver Disease Fibrosis Score, AST‐To‐Platelet Ratio Index, and BARD Score Among Young and Older Adults for the Diagnosis of Advanced MASLD Fibrosis: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Frank Lin, Ayah Obeid, Mehak Sharma, Parampreet Kaur, Kimberly Chaput, Hammad Liaquat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis study aims to compare the diagnostic performance and accuracy of non‐invasive fibrosis scoring tools, including the Fibrosis‐4 index (Fib‐4), Nonalcoholic Fatty Liver Disease Fibrosis Score (NFS), AST‐to‐Platelet Ratio Index (APRI), and BARD score among patients with biopsy‐proven MASLD or MASH, to either diagnose or exclude advanced fibrosis.Methods and ResultsA retrospective cohort of patients with biopsy‐proven MASLD or MASH was analyzed. Inclusion criteria for the study included patients over the age of 18, liver biopsy‐proven MASLD or MASH, and availability of laboratory findings prior to the biopsy to perform calculations for the non‐invasive liver fibrosis scoring tools. Patients were excluded based on a history of alcohol use and evidence of another or coexisting cause of chronic liver disease based on laboratory or pathology findings. Data were collected on patient demographics, comorbidities, and liver biopsy findings. The stage of fibrosis was determined using the Metavir Scoring System (F0–F4) categorized into mild to moderate (score: 1–2) and advanced fibrosis (score: 3–4). The statistical analysis of the four non‐invasive fibrosis scoring tools in this study resulted in a higher negative predictive value for all patients, particularly in the young adult population. There was significant variability and limitations regarding sensitivity, specificity, and AUROC for all four scores.ConclusionsThe study suggests that the Fib‐4, NFS, APRI, and BARD scores are valuable biomarkers for excluding advanced fibrosis in patients with MASLD or MASH. These four biomarkers are precluded as confirmatory tests; thus, further research and risk stratification with other non‐invasive scoring modalities or imaging are needed.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most common liver disease worldwide and affects 20%–30% of the general adult population [1]. As of 2024, 38% of adults and 7%–14% of children and adolescents have been diagnosed with MASLD [2]. MASLD prevalence worldwide is estimated at 32%, with patients who are obese having a threefold higher likelihood of developing liver disease. A model simulation predicted the prevalence of MASLD from 33.7% in 2020 to 41.4% by 2050 [3]. Higher rates of diagnosis are related to the rise in obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and metabolic conditions. MASLD places patients at risk of developing other health conditions, especially when the condition progresses to an advanced stage of fibrosis. These include metabolic dysfunction associated with steatohepatitis (MASH), which can lead to decompensated liver cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and death [4, 5]. Therefore, MASLD is diagnosed when there is the presence of at least one cardiometabolic risk factor and its hallmark of hepatic steatosis [4]. Diagnostic liver biopsy is reserved for cases when the diagnosis is uncertain in a patient with suspected MASLD [3].

MASLD stratification is essential for risk assessment and guiding surveillance and treatment. The pathogenesis of MASLD‐related hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is multifactorial, yet it is understood that hepatic steatosis contributes to increased tumorigenic potential. With the rising prevalence of MASLD, the incidence of MASLD‐associated HCC is also increasing, making it a fast‐growing cause of HCC and an indication for orthotopic liver transplantation [6]. Although cirrhosis remains the strongest risk factor for HCC development, emerging evidence reports that MASLD‐related HCC can occur at earlier stages of fibrosis. Notably, 25%–50% of MASLD‐HCC cases have been reported in the absence of cirrhosis [7]. Regarding prognosis, in a study by Shimose et al., the impact of MASLD on the efficacy of Lenvatinib was evaluated. Patients with MASLD had significantly longer median overall survival than those without. These findings suggest that although MASLD increases the risk of HCC, it may serve as a favorable prognostic factor in the setting of HCC treated with Lenvatinib [7].

This invasive procedure carries a risk of complications, such as bleeding and variability in sampling, potentially limiting its effectiveness. Non‐invasive methods can be used by primary care specialists, in addition to hepatologists. Specifically, biomarkers such as the Fibrosis‐4 score (Fib‐4) and NAFLD Fibrosis score (NFS) are safer to utilize and are easy to obtain to not only diagnose but monitor clinical disease progression [8]. These markers can serve as inexpensive methods to determine the severity of fibrosis. The Fib‐4 score uses age, liver enzyme levels, and platelet count and in one study found it to be superior to other markers, such as the NAFLD fibrosis score, Goteburg University Cirrhosis Index, AST/ALT ratio, Cirrhosis discriminant score, and BARD score [9]. In another study, the NAFLD fibrosis score was tested in a cohort with liver biopsy‐proven MASLD, which includes criteria used in the Fib‐4 score, in addition to age, BMI, and albumin. The results concluded the score could have a 90% positive predictive value, supporting the high accuracy that only 25% of patients with indeterminate scores required a liver biopsy to confirm fibrosis, avoiding 75% of participants from requiring an expensive and invasive method for diagnosis [10]. Another simple scoring system using the AST/ALT ratio, BMI, and the presence of diabetes mellitus is the BARD score, which has been reported to have a high negative predictive value of 96% for MASLD [11]. Lastly, the APRI score uses AST and platelet counts. It has also been reported to have consistent and reliable prognostic information to determine future liver‐related problems [3].

Hence, biomarkers such as Fib‐4, BARD, APRI, and the NAFLD fibrosis scores can stratify those with MASLD and are often comparable to the more invasive and complicated liver biopsy. The markers are all non‐invasive, inexpensive, easy to interpret, and consist of variables that are often readily available to help decrease healthcare costs and improve the quality of life for patients.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 254 patients with biopsy‐proven MASLD or MASH between 2020 and 2023. Data were collected on patient demographics, comorbidities, and liver biopsy findings. The stage of fibrosis was determined using the Metavir Scoring System (F0–F4) categorized into mild to moderate (score: 1–2) and advanced fibrosis (score: 3–4). Inclusion criteria for the study included patients over the age of 18, liver biopsy proven MASLD or MASH, and availability of laboratory findings prior to the biopsy to perform calculations for the non‐invasive liver fibrosis scoring tools. Patients were excluded based on a history of alcohol use and evidence of another or coexisting cause of chronic liver disease based on laboratory or pathology findings, such as chronic hepatitis, autoimmune disease, or hereditary diseases contributing to advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis. Diabetes, obesity, hypertension, and hypercholesterolemia, which encompass the full spectrum of metabolic syndrome, were all factors considered in the inclusion of MASLD or MASH patients for the study.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Stata (version 18.1). Metrics used for comparison for the Fib‐4 index, NFS, APRI, and BARD scores are the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and area under the receiver operating curve (AUROC). Qualitative variables were described using frequency and percent. Fibrosis risk from the liver biopsy was categorized into mild to moderate (score: 1–2) and advanced fibrosis (score: 3–4). Similarly, the overall BARD score was categorized as low risk of advanced fibrosis (score: 0–1) and high risk of advanced fibrosis (score: 2–4). Comparison across these two variables was conducted using an unpaired t‐test, a Mann–Whitney test, a Chi‐square test, and Fisher's exact test. The accuracy of these tests was compared to the gold standard test for detecting advanced fibrosis (liver biopsy).

Results

The primary aim of this study was to compare the diagnostic accuracy of non‐invasive liver fibrosis scoring tools, including the Fib‐4 index, NFS, APRI, and BARD score. A total of 254 patients were included, with a mean age of 53.1 years, 62.2% females, and 80.3% Caucasians. The majority of patients had metabolic syndrome (50.8%), obesity (75.2%), hypertension (62.6%), and dyslipidemia (64.9%) (Table 1). For the Fib‐4 index, results were interpretable in 171 out of 254 patients, out of which 28.7% were predicted to have advanced fibrosis. The Fib‐4 index was indeterminate in the remaining 83 patients, of whom 6% had advanced fibrosis on liver biopsy. Overall, the Fib‐4 index had low sensitivity (10%) and higher specificity (65.7%) in predicting advanced fibrosis, with the best outcomes seen in the young age group (Table 2). Area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curve analysis showed unsatisfactory performance of the Fib‐4 index in the overall sample and older age group, with AUROC of 52.6% and 40.9%, respectively. There was good accuracy for the young age group, with AUROC of 81.6%. For AUROC, cut‐off values of greater than 80%, 70%–80%, and less than 60% were used to indicate excellent performance, acceptable performance, and limited or poor performance, respectively.

For the BARD score, advanced fibrosis was present in 4.4% of patients. The BARD score demonstrated a sensitivity of 72.73% and specificity of 37.21% in identifying advanced fibrosis (Table 5). Discussion

As MASLD continues to rise globally, identifying reliable, accessible, and non‐invasive tools for staging liver fibrosis is increasingly critical to avoid the risks of liver biopsy. This retrospective study aimed to evaluate and compare the diagnostic accuracy of four widely used non‐invasive fibrosis scoring systems: Fib‐4, NFS, APRI, and BARD score, in identifying advanced fibrosis in biopsy‐confirmed MASLD or MASH patients. An age cutoff of 45 years was used throughout the study for the two age groups, as literature review identified mid‐40s as a turning point for fibrosis risk. In a biopsy‐based analysis of MASH, Angulo et al. showed that age was an independent predictor of fibrosis and that the optimal age threshold by ROC analysis was 45 years. Patients < 45 had a markedly lower prevalence of severe fibrosis than those ≥ 45, and those 45 years or older with obesity and diabetes mellitus had a greater prevalence of severe fibrosis on liver biopsy [12]. Another review by Alkhouri et al. identified factors that predict the development of progressive fibrosis and cirrhosis, with age older than 45 years being one of them [13]. One retrospective study utilized multivariate analysis and found age at liver biopsy ≥ 45 years as an independent and significant variable for septal fibrosis [14].

Among the evaluated markers, Fib‐4 exhibited the highest AUROC in the younger age group at 81.6%, indicating excellent performance in younger individuals. It also had a high NPV of 97.5% in this group, making it a strong test for ruling out advanced fibrosis in younger patients. However, its performance decreased in the overall cohort (AUROC 52.6%) and in older adults (AUROC 40.9%), with an overall sensitivity of only 10%. These results suggest limited utility for Fib‐4 in positively identifying advanced fibrosis, especially in older populations. Notably, in the older adult subgroup, the AUROC values for both Fib‐4 (40.9%) and NFS (45.3%) were only slightly better than chance. These findings raise concerns regarding their diagnostic reliability in older patients. One possible explanation is age‐related inflation of the Fib‐4 and NFS scores, which include age as a component and may thus overestimate fibrosis risk in the absence of true disease. Additionally, older adults may exhibit a narrower biochemical range due to comorbidities or altered baseline lab values, reducing the discriminatory power of these tests. From a clinical perspective, this limitation underscores the need for alternative strategies—such as age‐adjusted cutoffs or integration with imaging tools—in the evaluation of advanced fibrosis in older populations. Other studies have similarly reported variability in Fib‐4's performance across age groups and emphasized its prognostic implications in predicting liver fibrosis and mortality, particularly at thresholds > 2.67 [15, 16, 17, 18].

Notably, a large multicenter biopsy‐confirmed MASLD study demonstrated that Fib‐4 specificity dropped to 35% in patients ≥ 65 years, contributing to high false‐positive rates. Adjusting the cutoff to > 2.0 restored specificity to 70% without compromising sensitivity [19]. This aligns with our own findings, where specificity in the younger age group was markedly higher (82%) compared to older adults (55%), reinforcing the need for age‐stratified thresholds [19, 20, 21, 22]. In a meta‐analysis, Fib‐4 with a cutoff of 1.30 achieved a sensitivity of 84.4%, specificity of 68.5%, and AUROC of 0.85 [20]. Another review found its AUROC ranged from 0.67 to 0.93, depending on population and fibrosis staging system [23].

In our cohort, NFS showed similarly poor overall sensitivity, aligning closely with Fib‐4 in terms of diagnostic performance. However, it yielded a high NPV of 94.4% and 98% in younger and older patients, respectively, supporting its use for ruling out fibrosis. Our data also showed a notable proportion of indeterminate scores (36.6%), consistent with other studies that emphasize the limitations of NFS in real‐world populations [16, 24]. In a systematic review of 13 studies, NFS demonstrated an AUROC between 0.70 and 0.83 for predicting all‐cause and liver‐related mortality, outperforming APRI (AUROC 0.52–0.73) but performing similarly to Fib‐4 [25]. Another meta‐analysis reported NFS AUROC of 0.82 with low sensitivity of 30%, closely mirroring our own findings [23].

Moreover, in a direct comparison of non‐invasive scores, NFS demonstrated a sensitivity of 66.7%, specificity of 71.8%, and AUROC of 0.76, performing comparably to Fib‐4 and BARD [26]. However, it also exhibited the highest rate of false negatives in younger patients, a limitation observed in MASLD‐specific populations [27].

In contrast, APRI presented a balanced sensitivity of 72.7% and a high NPV of 97.6%, though its specificity of 50.2% and overall (AUROC 51.2%) were low. Interestingly, APRI performed slightly better in older adults (AUROC 53.7%). These findings are in line with prior studies that suggest APRI is best suited as an initial screening tool to rule out advanced fibrosis but lacks power to confirm disease, particularly in low‐prevalence populations [16, 18]. APRI has also been linked to prognostic value in predicting liver‐related events and even malignancy development in advanced fibrosis [28]. Compared to our study's AUROC of 51.2%, APRI demonstrated notably better diagnostic performance in other cohorts, with reported AUROCs of 0.80 in one validation cohort and 0.79 in a MASLD population, suggesting consistent performance across diverse populations [27, 29].

Another prospective analysis found that while APRI had high specificity (93.2%), its sensitivity was only 25%, with an AUROC of 0.769 again underscoring its role in ruling out but not confirming advanced fibrosis [30]. A comprehensive meta‐analysis further confirmed that APRI AUROC ranges from 0.65–0.85 [25].

The BARD score displayed a sensitivity of 72.7% and an excellent NPV of 95.5%, reinforcing its utility as a rule‐out tool. However, it showed the lowest specificity of 37.2% and a low PPV of 6.9%, which restricts its diagnostic confirmation ability. With a modest AUROC of 54.97%, its independent diagnostic performance remains suboptimal. Several studies have supported BARD's prognostic role in liver‐related outcomes and extrahepatic complications such as cardiovascular events [16, 18, 26]. In a comparative analysis, BARD showed an AUROC of 0.76 with a sensitivity of 74% and a specificity of 66% [31].

Other cohorts have reported similar findings, with AUROCs around 0.76–0.82 and NPVs ranging from 95.5% to 96%, reinforcing BARD's utility for ruling out advanced fibrosis. However, several studies also noted that BARD missed up to 26% of patients with advanced fibrosis and performed best only when adjusted thresholds were applied, highlighting its limited role in confirming disease [32, 33]. These findings are in partial agreement with our results. In contrast to our results, other studies in morbidly obese patients reported substantially poorer performance, with AUROCs as low as 0.44 and non‐significant predictive value [34, 35].

The comparative effectiveness of these non‐invasive tools reveals a common trend: while their ability to rule out advanced fibrosis is promising, their ability to confirm it remains poor. This is largely due to the high NPVs and low PPVs observed across the board, as well as generally poor AUROC values in the total population. These trends underscore the need for a tiered approach in clinical workflows, where non‐invasive scores serve as initial screening tools, followed by more advanced imaging modalities or biopsy in high‐risk or indeterminate cases.

A major strength of this study is its use of biopsy‐proven fibrosis as the reference standard, ensuring high diagnostic accuracy. The inclusion of a diverse age range allowed for age‐stratified analysis, revealing significant performance variability across younger and older populations. Furthermore, the side‐by‐side comparison of four major non‐invasive tools under consistent conditions strengthens the internal validity of the findings.

Limitations include the low prevalence of advanced fibrosis in our cohort (~4%–6%) likely contributing to the overall poor PPV and AUROC values observed in the study. PPV is highly dependent on disease prevalence; in low‐prevalence settings, even tests with good sensitivity and specificity will yield a high number of false positives, thus reducing PPV. While AUROC is typically considered prevalence‐independent, its interpretability can still be affected by disease rarity and spectrum bias. These limitations not only impact the perceived diagnostic performance but also constrain the generalizability of our findings. In referral hepatology centers where the burden of advanced fibrosis is higher, these non‐invasive tools may demonstrate improved PPV and AUROC performance, reinforcing the importance of contextualizing predictive metrics to the underlying population. Additionally, the rate of indeterminate results was high for NFS and Fib‐4, particularly in older patients, which limits their clinical interpretability. The retrospective, single‐center design may reduce external validity, and the study did not evaluate combinations of tools or incorporate imaging modalities such as elastography for secondary stratification.

In summary, Fib‐4, NFS, APRI, and BARD scores offer valuable utility as rule‐out tools for advanced fibrosis in MASLD/MASH patients, especially in younger populations. However, their limitations in sensitivity, specificity, and AUROC performance preclude them from being used as a confirmatory test. APRI and BARD appear most sensitive, while Fib‐4 and NFS offer higher NPVs in younger patients. Indeterminate results present a significant clinical challenge, often creating ambiguity in decision‐making. For instance, clinicians may be uncertain whether to pursue further diagnostics, refer to hepatology, or observe over time. In practice, such results typically prompt follow‐up with more advanced modalities—either blood‐based biomarkers like the Enhanced Liver Fibrosis (ELF) test or composite scores such as Agile 3+ and 4, which integrate non‐invasive scoring with imaging data. Transient elastography (FibroScan) and MR elastography are also commonly employed to clarify fibrosis risk in indeterminate cases. These tiered strategies enable more accurate patient stratification, potentially avoiding unnecessary biopsies while ensuring that high‐risk individuals are not overlooked. Incorporating such follow‐up tools into care algorithms may help optimize management in settings where non‐invasive results are inconclusive.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces the potential for selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Second, while we reported diagnostic performance characteristics (e.g., sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and AUROC) for each scoring tool, direct head‐to‐head statistical comparisons were not feasible given the constraints of our dataset, and thus our findings should be interpreted as descriptive rather than conclusive. Finally, because the study population was derived from a single tertiary health network, the results may not be fully generalizable to all practice settings. Despite these limitations, the use of a large cohort, standardized data collection, and concordance of our findings with prior literature strengthen the validity and clinical relevance of our conclusions.

Conclusion

The study suggests that the Fib‐4, NFS, APRI, and BARD scores are valuable biomarkers for excluding advanced fibrosis in patients with MASLD or MASH. All four biomarkers demonstrated excellent negative predictive value, albeit tests such as the Fib‐4 score and NFS yielded a portion of indeterminate scores. Further research and risk stratification with other non‐invasive scoring modalities such as enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) and Agile 3+ and 4 scores to identify advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis may be warranted.
