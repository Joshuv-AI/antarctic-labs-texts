Children’s Height and Injury Risk With Child Restraint Systems
Authors: Hsuan-Fan Tung, Jen-Fu Huang, Jhih-Ming Tang, Chia-Hsiang Hu, Shion Wei Chai, Tsai-Heng Hsieh, Shu-Jung Huang, Chi-Tung Cheng, Chien-Hung Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionIs children’s height associated with variations in injury patterns among those using child restraint systems (CRSs) or seat belts (SBs)?FindingsIn this cohort study of 6503 children involved in motor vehicle crashes and evaluated at trauma centers, CRS use was associated with a lower prevalence of abdominal injuries in children measuring 110 to 140 cm; however, these children had a higher frequency of serious spinal and thoracic injuries compared with SB users.MeaningThese findings suggest a nonlinear association between pediatric stature and injury anatomy.

Introduction

Motor vehicle crashes (MVCs) remain a leading cause of mortality within the pediatric population younger than 14 years.1 In the United States, children accounted for 2% of traffic fatalities, translating to 3 child fatalities and 442 injuries every day.2 Age-appropriate child restraint systems (CRSs) and robust legislation are associated with lower pediatric injury and mortality rates, with protective system use associated with a 45% to 74% reduction in motor vehicle–related mortality.3,4,5,6 In jurisdictions with mandating CRS laws, children aged 4 to 8 years were 4 times more likely to be appropriately restrained. This legislative environment is associated with a 17% reduction in the prevalence of fatal or incapacitating injuries and a 20% decrease in mortality risk.7,8

The American Academy of Pediatrics advises that belt-positioning booster seats be used once children outgrow forward-facing seats until a proper belt fit is achieved—typically at a stature of 145 cm (4 ft, 9 in), corresponding to 8 to 12 years of age.9 The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration similarly recommend booster seats during this transitional growth phase to ensure appropriate vehicle belt fit.10,11

Although research on children younger than 8 years is well established, data characterizing regional injury patterns during the transition from CRS to seat belt (SB) use among children aged 6 to 11 years remain scarce.12,13,14 Consequently, empirical evidence regarding how specific restraint types correlate with injury anatomy across varying body sizes is inconsistent.15,16,17 Because children in this age group exhibit significant anthropometric diversity, height-based variations in injury distribution may be more clinically relevant than age alone. Furthermore, current transition criteria are primarily derived from manufacturer-based measurements and simulation studies, with a lack of validation using injury pattern data from clinical settings.18,19

Appropriate restraint use is associated with variations in pediatric morbidity and mortality following MVCs. Therefore, evidence-based recommendations regarding the transition from CRS to SB are essential for caregivers. Given the limited empirical evidence pertaining to children in the transitional age range, this study used data from the American College of Surgeons–Trauma Quality Improvement Program (ACS-TQIP) to characterize the association between a child’s height and regional injury patterns among children evaluated at trauma centers. We hypothesized that injury anatomy among children using CRSs or SBs is height dependent. The objective of this study was to examine this association to better understand the height-dependent variations in clinical outcomes.

Methods

This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Jen-Ai Medical Foundation. The requirement for informed consent was waived because this study involved a retrospective analysis of a fully deidentified national database (ACS-TQIP), posing no more than minimal risk to the subjects. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies.20

Data Source and Study Population

A retrospective analysis was conducted using data from the ACS-TQIP database (2017-2020). The ACS-TQIP database aggregates clinical data from verified Level I to IV trauma centers across the United States. These participating facilities adhere to standardized data collection protocols mandated by the American College of Surgeons, ensuring consistency for patients with significant traumatic injuries. The database has demonstrated robust validity for multicenter trauma benchmarking, with external audits reporting substantial improvements in data accuracy (error rates decreasing from 8.0% to 3.2%) and strong interrater reliability across key clinical variables.21

Using International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) external cause codes V40 to V59, we identified children aged 6 to 11 years involved in MVCs who had used either CRSs or SBs and were evaluated at a trauma center participating in the ACS-TQIP data program. Data on participant age and restraint type were extracted from standardized ACS-TQIP data fields, with age verified through the “age” and “age units” elements. Restraint use was categorized via the “protective devices” field to specifically identify children using either a child restraint (encompassing safety and booster seats) or a lap and shoulder belt. Patients with an unknown Injury Severity Score (ISS) or those assessed using the 1998 or 2015 ISS versions; those with missing data on age, sex, body height, or body weight; those with incomplete documentation regarding protective devices or an unknown emergency department discharge status; those who had been transferred from the emergency department to another hospital; and those with missing data on clinical outcomes were excluded from the analysis. Two groups were categorized according to protective device type: SB and CRS.

Covariates

The variables collected included demographic and anthropometric characteristics (such as age, sex, body weight, body height, and body mass index). To address potential confounding related to crash severity, we incorporated these crash-related high-risk factors—including crash intrusion (>30.5 cm [>12 inches] at the occupant site or >45.7 cm [>18 inches] at any site), crash ejection (partial or complete) from the automobile, crash-related mortality in the same passenger compartment, high-risk injury based on the judgement of the emergency medical services professional, and Automated Crash Notification vehicle telemetry data consistent with high-risk injury.

Outcomes

The main outcomes of intertest were severe regional injuries, defined as an Abbreviated Injury Scale score ≥3 in the head and neck, spinal cord, thorax, or abdomen. These were specifically analyzed to characterize their height-dependent associations with restraint type. Secondary clinical outcomes included the ISS, major trauma (ISS ≥16), hospital admission (including general ward, observation, step-down, or intensive care units), and in-hospital mortality. In accordance with National Trauma Data Bank reporting conventions, both “mortality” and “transfer to hospice” were classified as mortality events, whereas discharge statuses recorded as “not known/not recorded” were treated as missing data.

Statistical Analysis

Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores was used to balance baseline covariates between the 2 groups. The primary goal of this weighting was to derive an average treatment effect for the comparative analysis of restraint types. Conceptually, IPTW aims to create a “pseudopopulation” where the baseline characteristics of CRS and SB users are balanced, minimizing selection bias. The propensity scores were calculated using a gradient boosted model with 10 000 trees. The propensity score model incorporated demographic and anthropometric characteristics—age specified in 1-year increments, sex, body weight, body height, and body mass index—along with crash-related high-risk factors. Balance between groups was assessed using the standardized difference (STD), with absolute values of less than 0.2 considered acceptable.

Associations between restraint types and clinical outcomes were evaluated within the IPTW-adjusted weighted cohort. Univariate logistic and linear regression analyses were performed to examine associations between restraint type and binary and continuous outcomes, respectively. The generalized estimating equation with an exchangeable working correlation matrix and the robust standard errors were used to account for the nonindependence of observations within hospitals. To assess whether the association between restraint type and outcome risk varied by height, we used a logistic regression model that included an interaction between restraint type and height, with height modeled using a natural cubic spline. Height was treated as a continuous variable and incorporated as a restricted cubic spline with 3 knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles. We chose this approach to capture potential nonlinear associations, allowing the injury risk to fluctuate smoothly across the height spectrum rather than assuming a fixed, linear association. This enabled the estimation of the odds ratio (OR) for the use of CRSs vs SBs, along with corresponding 95% CIs, across the full range of observed heights.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure the robustness of our findings. First, we included all participants with missing discharge information (n = 106) by applying multiple imputation with 10 datasets. Subsequently, a multivariable covariate adjustment was conducted for all variables listed in Table 1. In addition, we performed a sensitivity analysis ignoring the potential clustering effect by hospital. This involved using a model-based estimator while maintaining the original IPTW weights to conduct the same analysis. Finally, we compared outcomes between children who had used any safety restraint and those who had not, using multivariable covariate adjustment.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Participants

Of 6503 children aged 6 to 11 years (mean [SD] age, 8.48 [1.69] years; 3251 male [50.0%], 3252 female [50.0%]) identified, 1066 (16.4%) were classified as belonging to the CRS group and 5437 (83.6%) to the SB group. Compared with the SB group, the CRS group was significantly younger (mean [SD] age, 6.96 [1.17] vs 8.77 [1.62] years; STD, −1.28 years), had a shorter stature (mean [SD] height, 123.1 [14.3] vs 135.1 [17.9] cm; STD, −0.74 cm), and had a lower weight (mean [SD] weight, 26.9 [9.1] vs 38.1 [14.4] kg; STD, −0.93 kg). After IPTW adjustment, covariates were well balanced between the 2 groups (STD <0.2) (Table 1). Notably, more than 50% of children aged 6 to 11 years were unrestrained in this clinical cohort at the time of the crash (Figure 1).

Clinical Outcomes

Table 2 summarizes the clinical outcomes of the study population before and after IPTW adjustment. Following adjustment, hospital admission rates were 83.9% in the CRS group and 83.7% in the SB group, whereas in-hospital mortality was 1.6% and 1.0%, respectively. The CRS group was associated with a higher mean (SD) ISS compared with the SB group (10.1 [9.7] vs 9.0 [8.7]). Regarding regional injury patterns, CRS use was associated with a higher prevalence of severe head and neck injuries (13.9% vs 10.5%; OR, 1.41; 95% CI, 1.06-1.88; P = .02) and severe spinal cord injuries (2.9% vs 1.8%; OR, 1.70; 95% CI, 1.02-2.84; P = .04). No significant differences were observed between the CRS and SB groups for severe thoracic injuries (12.3% vs 11.2%; OR, 1.14; 95% CI, 0.80-1.61; P = .48) or severe abdominal injuries (11.6% vs 12.7%; OR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.62-1.34; P = .63).

Height-Dependent Association

Restricted cubic spline analyses revealed that body height was associated with a shifting association between restraint type and clinical outcomes within this study population. Among the children in this cohort, the OR for mortality was 0.63 (95% CI, 0.39-1.03) at approximately 90 cm of height, whereas an OR of 1.30 (95% CI, 1.00-1.69) was observed in children measuring 120 cm or taller. (Figure 2A). For hospital admission, the spline model demonstrated an OR of 0.77 (95% CI, 0.59-0.99) among children approximately 90 cm tall, which characterizes the association between body height and the likelihood of admission within our clinical data (Figure 2B). A higher prevalence of severe head and neck injuries (Abbreviated Injury Scale score ≥3) was associated with CRS use across the majority of the height spectrum (Figure 2C). Severe spinal cord and thoracic injuries were more frequently observed among CRS users between 110 and 140 cm in height. However, at a height of approximately 150 cm, the observed associations favored the CRS group (spinal cord OR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.19-0.37 and thoracic OR, 0.96, 95% CI, 0.86-1.08). (Figure 2D and E). In contrast, among children approximately 110 to 140 cm tall, those using CRSs were found to have a lower prevalence of severe abdominal injury (Figure 2F).

Sensitivity Analyses

Multiple imputation results for missing discharge information were consistent with the primary analysis (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). Models ignoring hospital clustering demonstrated higher statistical significance (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). This suggests that accounting for clustering provides a more appropriate and conservative approach for these data.

Compared with restrained peers, unrestrained individuals in this cohort were slightly older (mean [SD] age, 8.69 [1.71] vs 8.48 [1.69] years; STD, −0.13 years), taller (mean [SD] height, 134.2 [19.4] vs 133.1 [18.1] cm; STD, −0.06 cm), and more likely to be male (63.4% [5005 of 7896] vs 50.0% [3251 of 6503]; STD, −0.27%), although they were involved in incidents with fewer documented crash-related high-risk factors (14.4% [1140 of 7896] vs 19.8% [1285 of 6503]; STD, 0.14%) (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). Restraint use was associated with a lower ISS (mean [SD] score, 9.16 [8.88] vs 10.94 [10.40]), lower incidences of major trauma (17.4% [1132 of 6503] vs 22.0% [1736 of 7896]) and mortality (1.1% [70 of 6503] vs 2.4% [188 of 7896]), as well as fewer severe injuries in the head and neck (10.9% [711of 6503] vs 17.3% [1364 of 7896]; OR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.50-0.62; P < .001) and chest (11.3% [737 of 6503] vs 12.5% [985 of 7896]; OR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.76-0.94; P = .002) regions (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). Conversely, higher prevalence of severe abdominal (12.4% [806 of 6503] vs 9.1% [715 of 7896]; OR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.22-1.56; P < .001) and spinal cord injuries (2.0% [131 of 6503] vs 1.2% [97 of 7896]; OR, 1.57; 95% CI, 1.18-2.07; P = .002) were observed among the restrained group.

Discussion

In this cohort of pediatric patients evaluated at trauma centers following MVCs, we observed that restraint use was suboptimal and decreased progressively with age. Furthermore, regional injury patterns were closely associated with height; for children measuring 110 to 140 cm, CRS use was associated with a lower prevalence of abdominal injuries but a higher frequency of spinal and thoracic injuries, indicating that clinical associations between safety equipment and injury anatomy vary nonlinearly across the pediatric height spectrum.

Previous studies examining mortality rates for CRS and SB users have reported inconsistent results, primarily focusing on younger children prior to the transitional growth phase.13,16,22,23,24 Consequently, there is limited evidence regarding the height thresholds and criteria associated with the safe use of adult SBs in this population. Our study demonstrates an analytical approach to evaluating the associations between height and mortality within a trauma center–based cohort. Although these findings are not population based and cannot estimate absolute preventive efficacy, they illustrate that clinical associations between protective systems and injury anatomy are nonlinear.

Conversely, several studies have consistently associated belt-positioning boosters with a lower prevalence of nonfatal severe injury and hospital admission among children aged 4 to 8 years compared with SBs alone, with reported injury odds 45% to 77% lower.17,25,26,27 Durbin et al25 reported that CRS use was associated with a lower prevalence of abdominal, spinal, and lower-limb injuries compared with SB use alone. However, other analyses have indicated an association with higher adjusted odds for neck and thoracic injuries.13,28 Our height-specific data clarify the nonlinear association between height and regional injury patterns among transitioning children. By characterizing the height bands associated with variations in the prevalence of spinal, thoracic, and abdominal injuries, these findings support current safety guidelines and provide a clinical framework for understanding occupant safety system performance.

Global safety standards are shifting from age- to height-based criteria. Although the European UN R129 (i-Size) and Australian guidelines recommend children using booster seats until reaching a height of 145 cm, many regional laws (such as in Taiwan) still rely on age thresholds. This reliance overlooks the 6- to 11-year “transition cohort,” where growth variations often correlate with premature SB use and distinct injury pattern. The low observed use of child restraints, particularly among children aged 6 to 11 years presenting to an emergency department after MVCs, underscores the need for enhanced government regulation and public awareness campaigns that target older, transitioning children. Our findings bridge automotive safety theory and clinical trauma management by providing empirical evidence for height-dependent injury patterns in this vulnerable group. Clinicians should be aware of the specific trauma patterns associated with the use of restraints, including the risk of severe head, cervical spine, and thoracic injuries among restrained children, particularly those measuring between 110 and 140 cm in height.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective observational design identified associations rather than causal relationships. Because the cohort includes only children evaluated at trauma centers, the true population-level primary prevention effect cannot be determined. Nevertheless, the TQIP registry encompasses a large and diverse trauma population and provides significant insights into injury patterns among children sustaining clinically significant injuries. Second, the accuracy of the “restraint type” variable specifically has not been independently validated. Third, the small sample size of older children (9-11 years) using CRSs precluded a robust interaction analysis between height and age, leaving the potential for residual confounding regarding age-relative stature. Fourth, the ACS-TQIP registry lacks several granular variables, which may introduce residual confounding. Key limitations include (1) the absence of detailed crash descriptors (eg, impact direction, delta-V); (2) a lack of restraint design specifics, such as harness-style vs belt-positioning safety seats, or high-back vs backless boosters; (3) the database does not capture biomechanical variables such as booster seat stiffness or child seating posture (eg, slouching), both of which are significant predictors of submarining risk29; and (4) the absence of direct socioeconomic factors and social determinants of health variables; both can significantly influence restraint compliance and clinical outcomes.30,31 Collectively, these limitations may influence the observed associations between height and regional injury patterns.

To address these gaps, future research involving the linkage of prospective transportation crash databases and trauma registries may further clarify the associations between restraint use, mechanisms of injury, and clinical outcomes within the pediatric population. Enhanced reporting protocols that incorporate standardized documentation of restraint configuration and belt fit at the crash scene could improve data fidelity and reduce the reliance on retrospective proxies. In addition, further investigation using biomechanical crash simulations with anthropomorphic test devices representative of transitional pediatric body sizes could provide valuable insights into injury loading patterns. Finally, evaluating clinical booster seat fit, restraint-use compliance, and the effectiveness of caregiver education strategies remains essential for identifying the behavioral and implementation barriers that contribute to the injury associations observed in this study.

Conclusions

In this cohort study of 6503 pediatric patients evaluated at trauma centers following MVCs, the association between restraint use and regional injury patterns was height dependent and nonlinear. Our findings indicate that although CRS use was associated with a lower prevalence of abdominal injuries in specific height cohorts, it was also associated with a shift in spinal and thoracic injury risks as children transition in body size. These observations suggest that anatomical growth stages may result in varying vulnerabilities that a uniform height threshold may not adequately capture.
