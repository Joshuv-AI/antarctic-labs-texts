Background

Aspiration pneumonia represents a clinically important subtype of pneumonia characterized by the entry of oropharyngeal or gastric contents into the lower respiratory tract, particularly affecting older adults and individuals with neurological diseases. Its incidence has increased alongside population aging, and it is associated with prolonged hospitalization, recurrent events, and substantial mortality [1–3]. Risk estimation remains highly variable in practice, relying largely on clinical judgment rather than standardized or validated tools applicable to general populations [4, 5]. Despite its substantial burden, aspiration pneumonia has received far less epidemiological scrutiny than community-acquired or hospital-acquired pneumonia.

Existing evidence remains limited in several aspects. First, large-scale population-based risk factor analyses are sparse; most available studies rely on small cohorts or specific high-risk groups such as post-stroke or long-term-care populations [6, 7], limiting broader interpretability. Second, prediction models for aspiration pneumonia are underdeveloped. Current clinical scoring systems emphasize bedside suspicion but have rarely been evaluated in extensive, deeply phenotyped cohorts such as the UK Biobank [8]. Third, although neurological vulnerability—particularly dementia, Parkinson’s disease, and impaired swallowing mechanics—is known to increase aspiration risk, the underlying genetic susceptibility remains poorly understood. While APOE-related neurodegeneration has been linked to both dysphagia and pneumonia risk [9], genome-wide evidence for aspiration pneumonia specifically is still scarce.

The UK Biobank (UKB) provides an opportunity to address these gaps [8]. Its scale, standardized phenotype collection, biomarker profiling, and genome-wide genotyping allow for comprehensive, multidimensional evaluation [10]. A matched cohort nested within UKB enables assessment of epidemiological and biomarker-based risk factors [11]. Machine-learning approaches can be used to test whether non-linear patterns materially improve prediction over conventional logistic regression while maintaining clinical interpretability [12]. In parallel, genome-wide association analysis offers the ability to detect susceptibility loci and explore biological pathways related to neurodegeneration, swallowing regulation, and immune responses [13].

Accordingly, this study aimed to: (1) quantify demographic, lifestyle, clinical, and biomarker-based risk factors for aspiration pneumonia in a large general population; (2) develop and compare multiple prediction models, emphasizing the relative performance and interpretability of logistic regression versus non-linear machine-learning methods; and (3) conduct a genome-wide association analysis to investigate genetic susceptibility and identify biological pathways potentially contributing to aspiration pneumonia. Together, these complementary analyses provide an integrated view of epidemiological, predictive, and genetic determinants of aspiration pneumonia.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a nested case–control study within the UK Biobank (n = 501,968), a large-scale prospective cohort predominantly comprising middle-aged and older adults (aged 40–70 years at recruitment). Aspiration pneumonia cases (ICD-10 J69.0) were identified, and an initial pool of controls was selected by exact matching on age (± 1 year), sex, and assessment center (Fig. 1).

Study workflow UKB, UK Biobank; ICD-10, International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision; PSM, propensity score matching; GWAS, genome-wide association study; REGENIE, whole-genome regression implemented in REGENIE; OR, odds ratio; CI, confidence interval; AUC, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve; SHAP, SHapley Additive exPlanations

To achieve covariate balance, propensity scores were estimated using demographic, lifestyle, functional, biomarker, and comorbidity variables. A 1:4 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching without replacement was then performed using a caliper of 0.2 SD of the logit of the propensity score, following established recommendations for observational studies [14]. The 1:4 ratio was selected to retain all available aspiration pneumonia cases while improving precision through a larger matched control sample; additional controls beyond this ratio were not used to avoid worsening covariate balance and increasing computational burden. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (target < 0.1). Participants with missing data in variables required for a given analysis were excluded from that specific analysis. Cases were defined as participants with recorded aspiration pneumonia (ICD-10 J69.0) during follow-up, whereas controls were selected from participants without a recorded diagnosis of aspiration pneumonia in the available linked health records.

Comorbidity analysis

Associations between clinical factors and aspiration pneumonia were evaluated in the matched sample using logistic regression. Robust standard errors were applied to account for residual correlation introduced by the matching procedure. Effect estimates were presented as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. To assess whether large comorbidity associations reflected clustering of neurological conditions, we additionally fitted a joint multivariable comorbidity model including dysphagia, stroke, Parkinson’s disease, dementia, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, chronic kidney disease, cancer, malnutrition, psychiatric disorders, and gastroesophageal reflux disease simultaneously. A clinical sensitivity analysis was also repeated after excluding participants with dementia.

Machine-learning prediction models

Four prediction algorithms were developed: logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost [15], and LightGBM [16]. Models were implemented in Python 3.10 using scikit-learn [17], with pandas [18], SciPy [19], and statsmodels [20] for data handling and statistical computation. The matched dataset was randomly split into a training set (80%) and a testing set (20%). Hyperparameters were tuned using five-fold cross-validation within the training set.

Model performance was assessed in the test set using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and the Brier score. 95% confidence intervals for AUCs were calculated using the DeLong method. No additional class-imbalance handling was applied because the matched cohort preserved a fixed 1:4 case–control ratio.

Model interpretability

Model interpretability was assessed using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [21]. Tree-based models were interpreted using TreeSHAP, and logistic regression was interpreted using KernelSHAP. SHAP summary plots were used to quantify the magnitude and direction of each variable’s contribution to model predictions. Visualizations were generated using matplotlib [22] and seaborn [23].

Genome-wide association analysis

Genome-wide association analyses were conducted using REGENIE v4.1 [24]. Step 1 used whole-genome ridge regression to generate polygenic predictions, and step 2 applied binary-trait association testing with Firth correction, assuming an additive genetic model.

Quality-control filters excluded variants with minor allele frequency < 1%, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium P < 1e-6, imputation INFO < 0.8, or missingness > 2%. GWAS analyses used genotyped UK Biobank participants with complete phenotype and covariate data (n = 435,936; 3,679 cases and 432,257 controls). Population structure was controlled using the first 10 genetic principal components; the analyzed GWAS set was restricted to the European-ancestry analytic subset available in the UK Biobank genetic data. Association results were reported as effect sizes (beta), standard errors, odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and P-values.

Software and reproducibility

Analyses were performed using R 4.3 and Python 3.10. The specific software tools used for machine-learning, statistical analysis, visualization, model interpretation, and genome-wide analyses are cited at their first mention in the relevant Methods subsections above. All preprocessing, modeling, and visualization scripts followed standardized, reproducible workflows.

Ethical compliance statement

This study was conducted under UK Biobank Application Number 778,067. UK Biobank obtained ethics approval from the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (REC reference 11/NW/0382), and all participants provided written informed consent. The present analysis used de-identified data only.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 3,659 aspiration pneumonia cases and 14,214 matched controls were included in the final analytic cohort. The study population comprised middle-aged and older adults, with ages at recruitment ranging from 40 to 72 years. The median age was 64.0 years (IQR: 59.0–67.0) for cases and 63.0 years (IQR: 59.0–67.0) for controls (Mean age: 61.9 and 61.8 years, respectively). Notably, the majority of the participants were in their seventh decade of life, aligning with the peak incidence period for age-related aspiration risks and neurological decline.

Cases had a higher prevalence of neurological comorbidities, including dysphagia, dementia, Parkinson’s disease, and stroke. Functional and lifestyle measures also differed: cases exhibited slower walking pace, poorer self-rated health, and higher frailty-related indicators. Biomarker profiles showed higher inflammatory markers (e.g., C-reactive protein and neutrophil count) and lower albumin and hemoglobin levels. Comorbidity associations

In logistic regression, neurological conditions were the strongest predictors of aspiration pneumonia (Fig. 2). Dysphagia (OR 11.71, 95% CI 10.52–13.03; P < 0.001), Parkinson’s disease (OR 10.94, 95% CI 9.20-13.02; P < 0.001), and dementia (OR 9.45, 95% CI 8.35–10.69; P < 0.001) showed the largest effect sizes. Additional associations were observed for stroke, psychiatric disorders, heart failure, COPD, malnutrition, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, cancer, and gastroesophageal reflux disease.

The table columns show the number and percentage of affected participants among controls and cases. P-values are from logistic regression models; all displayed associations met P < 0.001

In the joint comorbidity model, dysphagia (adjusted OR 7.36, 95% CI 6.50–8.33; P = 1.12e-218), stroke (adjusted OR 7.03, 95% CI 6.13–8.07; P = 5.87e-171), Parkinson’s disease (adjusted OR 5.79, 95% CI 4.70–7.13; P = 1.19e-61), and dementia (adjusted OR 5.73, 95% CI 4.95–6.65; P = 9.61e-119) remained independently associated with aspiration pneumonia. In addition, 1367 of 3659 cases (37.4%) had none of the four major neurological risk conditions (dysphagia, stroke, Parkinson’s disease, or dementia). After excluding participants with dementia (n = 16,610; 2,826 cases and 13,784 controls), dysphagia (adjusted OR 7.71, 95% CI 6.75–8.81; P = 1.97e-199), stroke (adjusted OR 8.02, 95% CI 6.94–9.26; P = 3.94e-175), and Parkinson’s disease (adjusted OR 7.07, 95% CI 5.52–9.05; P = 1.96e-54) remained strongly associated with aspiration pneumonia.

Machine-learning model performance

All evaluated machine-learning algorithms demonstrated similar discriminative performance in the testing set. LightGBM showed the highest AUC (0.867; 95% CI 0.854–0.880), followed closely by XGBoost (AUC 0.865), random forest (AUC 0.864), and logistic regression (AUC 0.862). Logistic regression and LightGBM performed nearly identically (DeLong P = 0.913), and sensitivity and specificity remained well balanced across models. Model interpretability

Model-interpretation analyses consistently identified neurological vulnerability as a dominant contributor to model predictions. Dysphagia, dementia, Parkinson’s disease, and stroke had strong positive contributions, while functional measures such as walking pace and falls history also contributed substantially, followed by inflammatory biomarkers and frailty-related indicators. The SHAP summary plot and mean absolute SHAP ranking are shown in Fig. 3C and D, respectively.

Genome-wide association analysis

The genome-wide association analysis identified a single robust genome-wide significant locus at the APOE/TOMM40/APOC1 region on chromosome 19 (Fig. 4A). The lead variant, rs429358, showed the strongest association with aspiration pneumonia (beta 0.79746, SE 0.03087, OR 2.22, 95% CI 2.09–2.36; P = 2.29e-13). The top associated variants, including standard errors and 95% confidence intervals, are summarized in Table 2. Although several additional variants on chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 11, 13, 14, 17, 18 and 20 reached P < 5e-8 in the unfiltered output, all of these had observed effect-allele frequencies exceeding 99.96%, extremely large standard errors (SE > 0.39), and wide and unstable odds-ratio 95% confidence intervals (e.g., 0.45–2.61 for rs563468372; 0.24–4.32 for 18:51031383_CT_C); these signals therefore represent unstable Firth-corrected associations driven by a very small number of minor-allele carriers and were not considered credible loci. Discussion

This population-based analysis provides an integrated clinical, functional, and genomic characterization of aspiration pneumonia in community-dwelling adults. Neurological vulnerability, including dysphagia, dementia, and Parkinsonian syndromes, emerged as the strongest determinants, consistent with recent evidence highlighting impaired airway protection and neurodegenerative pathways as major contributors to aspiration-related respiratory events [25–29]. Functional measures such as slower walking pace, reduced self-rated health, and frailty indicators further differentiated cases from matched controls, reflecting systemic vulnerability frequently observed in older adults at elevated aspiration risk [30–32].

Prior studies on aspiration pneumonia have focused predominantly on post-stroke cohorts, nursing home residents, or hospitalized populations [33, 34], limiting generalizability to broader populations. By leveraging deeply phenotyped data within the UK Biobank—including lifestyle factors, objective functional measures, inflammatory biomarkers, and genome-wide genotyping—our findings extend the current evidence base and illustrate the multidimensional nature of aspiration pneumonia susceptibility.

From a public health perspective, the identification of functional indicators—specifically walking pace and frailty—as independent risk factors offers a pragmatic window for prevention. Unlike genetic susceptibility or established neurodegenerative diagnoses, functional decline is often reversible or manageable. These findings suggest that functional markers such as walking pace may be useful for risk stratification in community settings and warrant further evaluation in prospective studies. Whether combined functional and genetic risk profiling can improve targeted prevention strategies requires further prospective validation. This shift from reactive treatment to proactive functional screening is crucial for reducing the burden of aspiration pneumonia in aging populations.

Our finding that complex machine-learning algorithms (e.g., LightGBM) did not significantly outperform logistic regression has important implications for clinical implementation. The comparable performance of machine-learning models and logistic regression suggests that the available predictors in this dataset may not provide substantial additional non-linear predictive gain beyond conventional regression. This ‘simplicity’ is advantageous for public health: it supports the development of transparent, nomogram-based risk scores derived from logistic regression that are easy to interpret and implement in primary care systems. Unlike ‘black-box’ models, which can face barriers to adoption due to lack of interpretability, linear models provide clear insights into which specific factors (e.g., dysphagia vs. frailty) are driving a patient’s risk, thereby facilitating personalized patient education [35, 36].

Genome-wide association analysis identified a single significant locus at APOE/TOMM40/APOC1. The lead variant rs429358, which defines the APOE epsilon4 allele, has been increasingly implicated in neurodegeneration, impaired swallowing coordination, decreased laryngeal sensory responsiveness, and pneumonia susceptibility in aging populations [9, 37–40]. Our findings are consistent with this literature and support a shared pathway linking neurodegeneration, dysphagia, and respiratory vulnerability. However, because dementia-excluded and comorbidity-stratified genotype-level analyses were not performed, the present results cannot determine whether the APOE signal is fully independent of dementia-related pathways. Larger multi-ancestry studies, dementia-excluded GWAS, comorbidity-stratified GWAS, and meta-analyses will be essential to refine the genetic architecture of aspiration-related phenotypes.

Strengths of this study include its large sample size, detailed functional characterization, integration of biomarker and genomic data, and the use of propensity-score matching to improve covariate balance. Interpretation analyses enhance transparency and underscore clinically coherent determinants. Limitations include potential misclassification of aspiration pneumonia using administrative codes, residual confounding inherent to observational data, restriction of GWAS findings to individuals of European ancestry, and conservative GWAS calibration as reflected by λGC < 0.8. Low albumin and elevated C-reactive protein should be interpreted as associated clinical markers rather than proven causal determinants; Mendelian randomization or other causal-inference designs would be needed to test causality. Additional genotype-level sensitivity analyses excluding dementia or stratifying by major comorbidity status were not performed in the present analysis and should be prioritized when individual-level genotype access is available. External validation and studies incorporating instrumental swallowing assessments, respiratory imaging, and longitudinal functional trajectories will be necessary to advance mechanistic understanding and clinical translation.

Overall, these findings outline a coherent multidimensional profile of aspiration pneumonia risk shaped by neurological impairment, frailty-related functional decline, inflammatory and nutritional markers, and an APOE-centered genetic signal. This integrated framework may support future development of risk-stratification tools and guide identification of high-risk individuals within population settings.

Conclusions

This population-based analysis delineates a multidimensional risk profile for aspiration pneumonia, shaped by neurological vulnerability, functional decline, frailty-associated features, inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers, and an APOE-related genetic signal. Machine-learning and epidemiological analyses converged on broadly overlapping determinants, underscoring the central role of neurofunctional impairment and systemic vulnerability in defining individual risk. The APOE-centered locus provides genetic support for pathways linking neurodegeneration, swallowing dysfunction, and respiratory vulnerability, but further dementia-excluded and comorbidity-stratified GWAS will be required to test independence from major neurological comorbidities.

Together, these findings offer an integrated framework for understanding aspiration pneumonia risk in community-dwelling adults and establish a foundation for future work aimed at refining risk stratification, external validation, and mechanistic investigation across diverse populations.
