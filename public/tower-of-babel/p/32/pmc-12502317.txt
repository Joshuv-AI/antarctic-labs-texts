The effect of a plant- and dairy-based protein diet on serum levels of inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers in patients with liver cirrhosis: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Banafshe Khalese Ranjbar, Mahdiyeh Taghizadeh, Soraiya Ebrahimpour-Koujan, Mir Saeed Yekaninejad, Amir Anushiravani, Mehdi Saberifiroozi, Somayyeh Asghari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have confirmed that plant and dairy proteins help with hepatic encephalopathy; yet, the impact on oxidative stress and inflammation in liver cirrhosis remains unexplored. This is the first interventional clinical trial investigating the impact of a plant and dairy protein diet on oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers in cirrhotic patients.MethodsIn this controlled randomized clinical trial, 46 patients with cirrhosis were divided into two groups to receive a prescribed diet based on vegetable and dairy proteins (intervention group, n = 23) or a standard isocaloric diet (control group, n = 23) for 12 weeks. Anthropometric indicators, physical activity level, inflammatory biomarkers including serum levels of high sensitive C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin 6 (IL-6), as well as oxidative stress status including serum malondialdehyde (MDA), total antioxidant capacity (TAC), and total oxidant status (TOS) were evaluated before and after the intervention. Dietary intake was also assessed before, during, and after the intervention.ResultsBased on the findings of this study, consuming a diet based on plant and dairy protein significantly reduced the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score (P < 0.05). No significant changes were observed in anthropometric parameters, including body mass index, mid-arm muscle circumference, and triceps skinfold thickness, as well as serum levels of inflammatory biomarkers (hs-CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6) and oxidative stress biomarkers (MDA, TOS, and TAC).ConclusionFollowing a diet based on plant and dairy proteins for 12 weeks in patients with cirrhosis caused a reduction in MELD score, which may indicate meaningful clinical improvement in disease severity even without significant changes in inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers.Trial registration numberIRCT20220426054667N3, this trial was registered on July 2, 2023.Keywords: Cirrhosis, Vegetable protein, Dairy protein, Inflammation, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Cirrhosis is the final result of all types of liver diseases. Regardless of its cause, the liver undergoes gradual damage, which is characterized by the restructuring of its lobular architecture into regenerative nodules and liver dysfunction [1–4]. Each year, approximately one million deaths are attributed to complications of cirrhosis, making cirrhosis the 11th most common cause of death globally [5]. The spectrum of cirrhosis manifestations ranges from asymptomatic and compensated stages to symptomatic and decompensated stages, resulting in different clinical symptoms and complications, especially malnutrition [6–10].

Cirrhosis disrupts glycogen metabolism and glucose supply, causing fatty acid oxidation, proteolysis, and gluconeogenesis. This catabolic state compromises nutritional status and leads to protein-energy malnutrition, which is associated with a decrease in skeletal muscle mass and sarcopenia [11–14]. Sarcopenia is one of the most common complications of cirrhosis, with a prevalence of 40–70% and is significantly associated with more serious complications and high mortality. It has been shown that there is a strong relationship between inflammation and sarcopenia. In cirrhosis, the levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin 1 (IL-1), and interleukin 6 (IL-6) increase, which are considered the main catabolic mediators in skeletal muscles and are associated with a decrease in muscle mass.

Oxidative stress is another factor involved in the pathogenesis of chronic diseases associated with muscle wasting. Evidence suggests that oxidative stress can reduce antioxidant defense mechanisms in liver cells by stimulating the formation of free radicals and leading to lipid peroxidation, neuromuscular degeneration, and, as a result, loss of fibers and muscle function.

Elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) have been shown in cirrhosis as a result of liver cell injury [15, 16].

Therefore, considering the catabolic nature of cirrhosis and the existence of inflammatory conditions and oxidative stress, it is important to consider and carry out appropriate interventions to control complications and prevent the progression of the disease and mortality rate.

Currently, according to the existing guidelines, it is recommended that these patients receive high-energy and high-protein diets and avoid long-term starvation. However, the type of protein consumed may be more important [17, 18]. Dairy and vegetable proteins improve hepatic encephalopathy in cirrhosis, largely due to their high branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) content [19]. BCAAs reduce oxidative stress by increasing the expression of proteins involved in antioxidant defense, such as glutathione and superoxide dismutase [20]. Recently, a wide range of BCAA effects in reducing oxidative stress and enhancing liver regeneration have been confirmed by clinical studies in patients with liver cirrhosis [21–23]. Besides, a diet rich in plant foods, due to the high content of polyphenols and antioxidants, can be very helpful in alleviating oxidative stress and inflammation caused by cirrhosis [24]. In addition, fiber and isoflavones contents of plant protein sources, such as soybeans and legumes can reduce systemic inflammation and oxidative stress through their effects on cell signaling, gene expression, and modulation of the gut microbiome [25–27]. Fiber-rich diets also increase fecal ammonia excretion which can suppress inflammatory signaling pathways, as well as decreasing the levels of lipid peroxidation markers [28–33]. Dairy products, besides being a rich source of BCAAs, are also a source of high biological value protein [34]. Therefore, following a diet containing plant and dairy proteins may have more desirable results for these patients. Considering that most of the interventional studies investigated the effects of vegetable and dairy proteins in cirrhosis were of animal type, and the human interventional studies are limited and mostly focused on the management and prevention of hepatic encephalopathy, there is no data that specifically evaluates the benefits of this type of diet compared to an isocaloric standard diet on inflammation and oxidative stress status in patients with cirrhosis. Thus, the present randomized controlled clinical trial study was designed to investigate the effect of a diet based on plant and dairy proteins on the serum biomarkers of inflammation and oxidative stress in patients with liver cirrhosis.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Forty-six patients with cirrhosis, diagnosed for at least six months by a gastroenterologist and aged between 30 and 60 were recruited from the specialized gastroenterology clinics at Shariati and Imam Khomeini hospitals affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences from September 2023 to July 2024. Subjects were not included in the study who had a MELD score greater than 18, Child class C, active gastrointestinal bleeding, serum creatinine higher than 1.5 mg/dl during the last month, consumed alcohol and tobacco in the last six months, or were pregnant or lactating women. Those suffering from diabetes, lung issues, heart failure, mental disorders, liver carcinoma or other cancers, acute viral hepatitis, cholestatic cirrhosis, autoimmune cirrhosis, various autoimmune diseases, hyperuricemia, musculoskeletal disorders, as well as corticosteroid and immunosuppressive consumers were also not included. In the cases of the occurrence of serious complications during the study and the need for hospitalization or liver transplant, changing the patient’s medication protocol, and non-compliance with the study protocol (less than 70% compliance), patients were supposed to be excluded from the study.

Due to the lack of similar studies, the sample size was calculated using G-Power software, equal to 21 people in each group, taking into account the first type error of 5%, the test power of 80%, and the effect size of 0.8 (Cohen’s d), which was increased to 23 people in each group considering the 10% chance of attrition.

Study design

This randomized, placebo-controlled trial, designed according to the CONSORT guidelines, was conducted on 46 individuals with cirrhosis after obtaining written informed consent.

Before the intervention, participants underwent a one-week “run-in” period during which we gathered a range of data such as general demographic information, socioeconomic status, disease history, and the use of drugs, supplements, alcohol, and tobacco using a general questionnaire. Dietary intake was evaluated using a 24-hour food record for three days. The severity of malnutrition in patients was determined using the RFH-SGA questionnaire. At the end of the run-in period and after performing the aforementioned evaluations, the patients were randomly allocated into two groups using stratified block randomization with size two by stratifying age (30–45 and 46–60), sex (male or female), and malnutrition status (adequately nourished, moderately malnourished, or severely malnourished). Stratified randomization was achieved by generating a separate block for each combination of covariates (age, sex, and malnutrition status), and participants were assigned to the appropriate block. Random sequence was generated using the random allocation software (RAS). Malnutrition status was determined by the Royal Free Hospital-Global Assessment (RFH-GA) algorithm [35]. The intervention group received a prescribed diet based on vegetable and dairy protein, and the control group received a prescribed standard isocaloric diet for 12 weeks. The protocol of this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (ethics number: IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1402.048) and was registered at the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (ID: IRCT20220426054667N3).

Intervention

According to the latest guidelines, both the intervention and control groups were given the same diet in terms of energy and macronutrients (1.3 times REE energy using the Harris-Benedict formula, 1.2 g of protein per kilogram of body weight, 30% of total calories from fat, and 50–55% of carbohydrates from total calories) but differed in the type of protein consumed. The number of servings of bread, grains, vegetables, fruits, and oils were the same in both groups depending on the estimated energy intake according to the healthy eating pyramid. However, the intervention group was instructed to consume vegetable protein including legumes, soybeans, soy milk, and nuts, whereas, the control group was asked to consume all sources of protein such as legumes, meat, poultry, fish, eggs, and nuts.

Dairy foods that were recommended to consume in both groups included low fat (1.5%) milk or yogurt and low fat, low salt cheese. The control group were instructed to consume two servings as recommended in a standard diet based on the healthy eating pyramid. The intervention group were asked to consume 3–4 servings depending on the required energy intake. The diets were prescribed for 12 weeks in the form of a menu consisting of three meals and four snacks.

Anthropometric measurements

For each patient who participated in the study, anthropometric assessments, including weight, height, BMI, skinfold thickness in the area of the triceps muscle (TSF), mid-arm circumference (MAC), and mid-arm muscle circumference (MAMC), were conducted at the beginning and end of the study. Weight was measured with minimal clothing and no shoes using a digital scale with an accuracy of 100 g. The standing height of the subjects was measured without shoes using a standard height meter with an accuracy of 0.5 cm. The BMI of individuals was also determined by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. A caliper was used to measure TSF. First, using the thumb and index finger, a vertical fold of skin and subcutaneous fat was taken in the posterior-middle position of the left arm while standing, and then it was pulled up to separate it from the underlying muscle tissue. The opening of the caliper was placed in contact with the two layers of skin, and 2–4 s after applying full pressure of the caliper, the thickness of the skin was measured in millimeters. For a more accurate measurement, the average of three measurements with an interval of at least 15 s was included. MAC was measured using a tape measure at the midpoint of the left arm, at the distance between the elbow and the tip of the shoulder, with an accuracy of 0.1 cm, while in a standing position, and the person’s hand was free, parallel to the body. Assessment of malnutrition severity

The severity of malnutrition in patients was determined using the RFH-SGA algorithm. This assessment tool is designed for the population of cirrhosis patients, and the severity of malnutrition is determined based on BMI, MAMC, and food intake adequacy.

Nutritional intake assessment

Participants’ dietary intake was evaluated using a three-day food record (including two normal days and one day off) at the beginning and the end of the study. A one-day food record was also taken from the patients at the third, sixth, and ninth weeks to evaluate the compliance with the prescribed diets. If a participant’s energy intake was less than 70% of their total energy expenditure (TEE), they were considered noncompliant. Additionally, for the intervention group specifically, noncompliance also occurred if the ratio of protein intake from vegetable and dairy sources to total protein intake was less than 70%.

To determine the amount of macronutrients and micronutrients received by patients based on food records, Nutritionist IV software (First Databank, Hearst Corp., San Bruno, CA, USA) was utilized according to Iranian foods.

Physical activity assessment

The physical activity of study subjects was recorded at the beginning and end of the study, including one weekday and one day off. The level of physical activity in MET-h/day was calculated based on the activities performed, using the standard MET table.

Biochemical measurements

Blood samples (10 cc) were collected after a 12-h overnight fasting at the beginning and end of the intervention. The blood sample was poured into tubes without an anticoagulant and left for 30 min at the laboratory temperature until the blood clotted. Then the tube was placed in a centrifuge at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate the serum. After this step, the serum samples were transferred into 200-microliter micro tubes and stored in −80 °C until the analyses were performed. It is worth noting that a serum hemolysis check was performed to assess serum quality. Serum concentrations of inflammatory biomarkers, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin 6 (IL-6) were measured using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) test with the commercial kits (Karmania Pars Gene Co., Rafsanjan, Iran) with a sensitivity of 2 pg/ml, inter-assay precision of <%8–10, and intra-assay precision of <%3–4. High sensitive C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was determined using immunoturbidimetry. Serum concentrations of oxidative stress indicators were measured using the enzymatic colorimetric method (spectrophotometry) by commercial kits (Navand Salamat Co., Urmia, West Azerbaijan Province, Iran) including malondialdehyde (MDA) with the sensitivity of 0.1 nmol/ml, inter assay precision of %5.5, and intra assay precision of %6.7; total antioxidant capacity (TAC)) with the sensitivity of 2 µmol Fe2+, inter assay precision of %2.5, and intra assay precision of %3.7; and total oxidant status (TOS) with the sensitivity of 23 µmol/ml, inter assay precision of %3.6, and intra assay precision of %4.5.

The concentration of serum creatinine was measured using the colorimetric/Jaaffe method (Biorex Fars, Shiraz, Iran). Serum total bilirubin concentration was also measured using the Jendrassik-Grof method (Biorex Fars, Shiraz, Iran). To measure prothrombin time (PT), tissue thromboplastin and calcium ions were added to the patient’s plasma, and the time taken for plasma coagulation was measured. Since the patient’s PT test was performed with the reference tissue reagent, the ratio of the patient’s PT to the control is equal to the INR.

Statistical analysis

SPSS software version 26, was applied for all analyses. Normal distribution of the data was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The Paired-Samples t-test or its non-parametric equivalent, the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test, was used to assess the differences in each group. For comparing quantitative variables between the study groups, the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was applied, while the chi-square and Mann-Whitney U test were applied for nominal and ordinal variables, respectively. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was applied to determine the impact of the intervention when adjusted for baseline values for normally distributed variables and Quade Nonparametric ANCOVA for non-normally distributed variables. Quantitative variables with normal distribution were represented as mean ± standard error (SE), while median (25-75th interquartile range) was reported for those with non-normal distribution. Also, the qualitative data were reported as frequency and percentage. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The flowchart of this clinical trial is shown in Fig. 1. As indicated, 320 subjects were initially screened for eligibility. Of these, 274 individuals were excluded due to not meeting the inclusion criteria or their own refusal to participate; ultimately, forty-six patients agreed to participate. Stratified blocked randomization was used to randomly assign these patients to intervention and control groups, with 23 subjects in each group. After the measurements and tests of the first stage, two patients in both the intervention and control groups dropped out. Thus, 42 patients (21 in the intervention group and 21 in the control group) completed the trial and were included in the analyses.

As expected, the intervention group exhibited a significant change in mean intake of plant, dairy, and animal proteins before and after the study (p < 0.001). In contrast, the standard diet group showed no significant change in mean protein intake from each of these sources over the same period (p > 0.05). Furthermore, the between-group differences in plant and animal protein intakes were statistically significant at the end of the study (p < 0.01).

Participants in the intervention group demonstrated relatively acceptable compliance with the prescribed diet plan, achieving an 83% adherence rate for the ratio of plant and dairy protein sources to total protein intake. The estimated adherence to prescribed energy intake was 96% in the standard diet versus 100% in the plant and dairy protein-based diet.

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the effect of a diet based on plant and dairy protein in patients with cirrhosis. Although the benefits of this diet on encephalopathy are well documented, to our knowledge, this is the first interventional trial to evaluate its impact on inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers in cirrhosis, compared to a standard diet.

According to the findings of the current clinical trial study, serum levels of inflammatory (hs-CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6) and oxidative stress (MDA, TOS, and TAC) biomarkers did not show significant differences between the two groups. Consistent with these findings, Hashemilar et al. showed that whey protein supplementation with a dose of 20 g/day via oral gavage for three weeks did not cause any significant changes in serum levels of MDA and TAC in patients with acute ischemic stroke [36]. A systematic review and meta-analysis also concluded that whey protein supplementation did not produce a significant difference in serum levels of the oxidative stress factors, including MDA, TAC, and GSH [37]. In a cross-sectional study, no significant association were observed in consumption of a low-fat dairy protein-based diet and the plasma inflammatory compounds, including hs-CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6 in patients with metabolic syndrome [38]. Markova et al. also showed that consuming a diet based on plant protein did not cause significant changes in serum levels of IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, and MCP-1 but led to a significant decrease in serum levels of TNF-α in patients with type 2 diabetes and NAFLD during six weeks [39].

Contrary to our findings, in an animal study, it was shown that supplementation with BCAA for 16 weeks in rats with advanced liver cirrhosis reduced iron accumulation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis, and improved glucose metabolism in the liver and cumulative survival [22]. Besides, consuming a diet containing adequate dairy protein (more than 3.5 servings of dairy per day) for 12 weeks in obese or overweight adults with metabolic syndrome resulted in significant reductions in serum levels of inflammatory and oxidative stress markers, such as TNF-α, IL-6, and MDA [40]. According to the results of a clinical trial conducted by Binita et al., a significant reduction in serum hs-CRP levels was observed following the consumption of a vegetarian diet in patients with stable coronary artery disease over eight weeks [41]. Moreover, in a systematic review and meta-analysis focusing on clinical trials it has been demonstrated that soy protein supplementation caused significant reduction in serum levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and CRP [42]. It has been shown that fiber and isoflavones contents of plant protein sources, such as soybeans and legumes can reduce systemic inflammation and oxidative stress through their effects on cell signaling, gene expression, and modulation of the gut microbiome [25–27].

It should be noted that studies investigating plant based proteins on inflammation or oxidative stress are so limited and no one was done in patients with liver cirrhosis. So, comparing the results of these studies with the present finding may not show the similarities or discrepancies. On the other hand, different study durations and in some cases short intervention periods may affect the results. Different nature and design of the studies and the administration of various amounts and forms of vegetable and dairy proteins also may affect the outcomes.

A number of limitations should be taken into consideration while interpreting the findings of this study. First, a lack of blinding for both the researcher and the patients due to the diet intervention. Second, adhering to the prescribed diet in the intervention group was difficult due to the nature of the diet, which was based on vegetable and dairy proteins. Third, the duration of the intervention may have been insufficient to detect significant changes in inflammatory and oxidative stress measurements. The fourth limitation is the relatively small sample size, which may make the study underpowered to detect moderate changes in the study outcomes.

Conclusion

Generally, our results indicated that consumption of a diet based on plant and dairy protein for 12 weeks in patients with liver cirrhosis caused no significant changes in serum levels of inflammatory biomarkers, including hs-CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6, as well as oxidative stress biomarkers, including MDA, TOS, and TAC. It is noteworthy that the aforementioned changes occurred only in a short intervention period and, therefore, continued consumption over longer periods may have more remarkable effects. However, further long-term studies are needed to confirm these findings and to determine whether this type of diet, compared to a standard diet, can improve inflammation and oxidative stress in patients with cirrhosis or if strict dietary restrictions are unnecessary.

It is suggested to conduct future studies with a prolonged duration of the intervention to examine molecular signaling pathways to better understand the direct impact of dietary protein types on inflammation and oxidative stress at the cellular level.
