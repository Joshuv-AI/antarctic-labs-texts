Rehabilitation assessment approaches and technologies to measure gaze behaviour during walking in older adults and central neurological populations: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Nicole Cesca, Damian M Manzone, Kristin E Musselman, José Zariffa, Emilia Main, Thomas P Walden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFalls are a frequent event among older adults and individuals with central neurological conditions. Gaze behaviour (i.e. how individuals use visual input to guide movement) plays a critical role in motor control while walking and likely contributes to fall prevention. However, existing research in this area remains fragmented. This scoping review will aim to identify how gaze behaviour is measured and utilized during gait rehabilitation among older adults and individuals with central neurological conditions. Findings will inform standardized assessment approaches and the development of interventions targeting gaze behaviour to improve walking and reduce fall risk.Methods and analysisThis review will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute scoping review guidelines and is registered with the Open Science Framework. Studies will be included if they involve older adults (≥ 65 years) or individuals with central neurological conditions and measure gaze behaviour during walking. A comprehensive search strategy was developed in collaboration with a University Health Network Librarian and refined iteratively by the research team. Eight databases (Medline, Embase, CENTRAL, CDSR, Emcare Nursing, APA PsycINFO, Web of Science Core Collection, and CINAHL) were searched from inception to August 2025. Title and abstract screening will be followed by a full-text review to identify articles that meet the inclusion criteria. Data related to study/participant characteristics, methods for eye tracking, intervention, equipment, outcome measures, and key findings will be extracted. Methodological quality of studies will be assessed using the appropriate tool based on study design. Results will be reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews checklist.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12938-026-01597-4.

Introduction

Falls are a frequent event among older adults (> 65 years) and individuals with central neurological conditions (e.g., stroke, spinal cord injury (SCI), Parkinson’s disease), particularly while walking in the community [1–3]. Among older adults, falls are a leading cause of disability, and for those with central neurological conditions, they can be a setback in the overall health and functional independence of the individual [4–6]. In addition to physical injuries (e.g., fractures, disability), the impact of falls can lead to psychological consequences (e.g., depression, fear of falling), while also placing significant financial strain on healthcare systems [5, 7]. In response, healthcare professionals and researchers have been increasingly focused on identifying the underlying causes of falls and developing effective prevention strategies [5, 8].

One area of growing interest is gaze behaviour (i.e. where an individual looks, for how long, and how rapidly they move their eye gaze between focal points). Visual input is essential for guiding foot placement, maintaining balance, and navigating complex environments during walking [9, 10]. As individuals walk, they continuously scan their surroundings, using visual cues to inform the central nervous and motor systems to coordinate real-time adjustments, helping to avoid obstacles, maintain stability, and support smooth movement [10, 11]. Visual information provides both immediate feedback on body position and anticipatory (feedforward) cues that support proactive adjustments, making it essential for all aspects of purposeful walking, from stepping and postural control to navigating complex terrain [9, 10, 12].

In recent years, there has been growing interest in understanding gaze behaviour in the context of walking and fall risk, particularly among at-risk populations (e.g., older adults, individuals with central neurological injury/disease). In young individuals without injury/disease, gaze behaviour is well-characterized. The research suggests eye movements and stepping are closely coordinated, indicating gaze behaviours are incorporated into gait motor plans [8, 9]. Fixation duration, gaze direction, and shifts in eye position (e.g., saccades) are common measures used to better understand this relationship [13]. To date, much of the research exploring gaze behaviour has focused on older adults, who are at increased risk of falls and experience mobility impairments [8]. Recent studies have identified maladaptive gaze strategies, including excessive downward gazing, premature gaze shifts, and reduced environmental scanning as potential indicators of compromised sensorimotor control in both older adults and individuals with central neurological conditions [8, 14]. However, there is a growing need to synthesize research on how gaze behaviour is affected in these populations in order to identify clinically meaningful patterns that can inform rehabilitation and mobility interventions [14–16].

Although the link exists between altered gaze behaviour and unsteady gait [8], the potential to train gaze behaviour as a means to improve walking stability and reduce fall risk remains underexplored. As interest in this area grows, it is important to synthesize the current body of research to better understand how gaze behaviour is being measured, targeted, and integrated into rehabilitation interventions. Additionally, in recent years, advances in eye-tracking technology have further expanded opportunities to study and train gaze behaviour [17, 18]. Given these developments, conducting a systematic scoping review to compile and synthesize the current evidence in this area is both timely and valuable [19].

A systematic scoping review is appropriate for broad research questions, especially in emerging fields where evidence is limited or fragmented. It is also valuable for synthesizing diverse methodologies and outcomes, identifying knowledge gaps, and informing future research and clinical practice [19, 20]. Therefore, this review aims to identify the current evidence on how gaze behaviour is being measured, and the implementation strategies currently adopted during gait rehabilitation, with the goal of guiding future interventions and supporting the development of standardized assessment approaches. Hence, this manuscript outlines a systematic scoping review protocol aimed at identifying the technologies and methods used to measure gaze behaviour during walking in older adults and individuals with central neurological conditions, specifically those primarily affecting the central nervous system (e.g., SCI, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s, stroke, traumatic brain injury, concussion). The findings from the review will guide the development of future interventions and assessment strategies aimed at effectively evaluating and potentially training gaze behaviour in older adults and individuals with central neurological conditions, with the long-term goal of enhancing walking ability and informing fall prevention efforts.

Methods and analysis

The proposed scoping review will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Guidelines [19, 21] and the methodological framework outlined by Arksey and O’Malley [20]. The scoping review protocol was registered with the Open Science Framework in March 2025 (https://osf.io/qv8xj/). All revisions to the proposed scoping review protocol will be noted on this webpage. Results of the scoping review will be prepared for dissemination in a peer-reviewed scientific journal and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRIS-MA-ScR) checklist [22].

Search strategy process

An initial Medline search strategy was developed by three researchers (NC, DM and TW) under the direction and guidance of a University Health Network (UHN) Librarian (EM) in June 2025, following the Population, Concept, Context (PCC) framework [19, 21, 22]. The population included SCI and Brain Injury and Central Neurological Injuries (high level) and Older Adults. The Concept included Gaze Behaviour and Walking, and a Context section was not specified in the search to cover all settings and environments. Three researchers (NC, DM and TW) reviewed and provided feedback on the initial search. The search returned a small response, so the strategy was updated to include stroke, cauda equine syndrome, and tremor, while terms related to facial and eye injuries were removed. The UHN Librarian (EM) then executed the updated search strategy in Medline. After the updated search also returned a finite number of responses, the search strategy was further refined, removing the population aspect to reduce the likelihood of missing eligible publications. The Medline search strategy is included in Appendix 1 and was adapted as needed for use across seven additional databases. Lastly, to ensure a comprehensive search, three researchers (NC, DM and TW) will review the reference lists of the included studies to identify any relevant publications that may have been missed. Secondary searches of citations and authors will be conducted using Google Scholar.

Information sources

The following eight databases were searched from their inception to August 5, 2025: Ovid MEDLINE ALL, Embase Classic + Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (CDSR), Ovid Emcare Nursing, APA PsycINFO (all via Ovid), Web of Science Core Collections and CINAHL Ultimate (EBSCOhost Research Databases). Databases searched also include pre-prints. Furthermore, searches for grey literature were not conducted as this review aims to assess and report on the methodological quality of the studies included. Grey literature sources typically do not undergo formal peer review and often lack sufficient methodological detail to allow for effective appraisal. No language or date limits were applied. If necessary (i.e. the initial search ends up being more than 6 months from the date of submission of the scoping review for publication), a follow-up search will be performed. Specifically, the follow-up search will be conducted one month before submission to ensure all eligible studies are captured and to reflect the most current evidence available at the time of the scoping review’s publication.

Scoping review objectives and questions

The primary objective of the scoping review is to identify the methods and technologies used to measure gaze behaviour during walking (Concept) in older adults and individuals with central neurological conditions (Population) across all settings and environments (Context). Our secondary objective will examine how these approaches are implemented within gait rehabilitation. Additional exploration of the Population and Concept elements will compare methods of measuring gaze behaviour across each of the different populations (e.g., older adults compared to each central neurological population). This work will examine whether gaze behaviour is predictive or relates to the likelihood of a fall.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were developed by all authors and finalized based on preliminary database search outcomes and feedback from the UofT Librarian (ES). Inclusion criteria are as follows: a) participants are older adults (≥ 65 years) or adults (≥ 18 years) with central neurological conditions (e.g., SCI, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s, stroke, traumatic brain injury, concussion); b) implemented eye tracking or measured gaze behaviour during walking; and c) occurred in a laboratory or real-world setting. Studies will be excluded if they do not use quantitative methodologies (i.e. qualitative studies), as these designs will not provide sufficient details on how gaze behaviour is measured and are therefore not suitable for the objectives of this review. Mixed‑methods studies will be included if they meet the inclusion criteria; however, only the quantitative components will be extracted and reported on. Additionally, studies that involve solely animal research, focus on facial or eye injuries, are published in languages other than English, or are presented as conference proceedings, abstracts, textbook chapters, notes, reviews, or unpublished literature will be excluded.

Study screening process

All articles identified through the database searches will be imported into Covidence, a web-based systematic review management platform (Online—Veritas Health Innovation) [23], where the study screening process will be conducted. Duplicates will automatically be removed in Covidence [23]. Titles and abstracts will be screened by three researchers (NC, DM and TW) to identify studies that meet the inclusion criteria. Initially, the three researchers (NC, DM and TW) will participate in a pilot screening trial, during which 10 randomly selected abstracts will be independently assessed for inclusion. Once 80% agreement between the three researchers is achieved, the select team members will move on to the formal screening stage [24]. Articles that pass the title and abstract screening will undergo full-text review by the three researchers (NC, DM, and TW), independently. Each article will be randomly assigned to two of the three reviewing authors. Any discrepancies in study inclusions will be resolved by consensus. If reviewing authors cannot come to consensus, a fourth reviewer (KEM) will settle inter-rater discrepancies.

Data extraction and charting

The three researchers (NC, DM and TW) will develop a charting table in Microsoft Excel that aligns with the review’s objectives, prior to the extraction process [19, 21]. A singular article will be randomly selected and independently reviewed by the selected three team members to assess the suitability of the charting table. Any amendments that are deemed required by at least two of the three reviewing authors will be made to the charting table prior to the extraction process. Data extraction fields may include: citation details, study location, study design, aim and purpose, population and participant characteristics such as sample size, study setting, methods for eye-tracking, intervention (if applicable), equipment, description of control group (if applicable), outcome measures, and key findings. The data extraction process will first start with a discussion between NC, DM and TW to ensure a shared level of understanding of the required detail for extraction. Then, all three researchers will divide up the included studies, and each will independently extract the necessary data. Upon completion of the data extraction process, one author will review the full data extraction table to ensure consistency in how the data are presented and that all details are captured. If there is any disagreement or if the data are not presented in a consistent manner, the selected researchers (NC, DM, and TW) will meet to discuss, resolve the issue, and determine how the data should be corrected and presented.

Quality appraisal

Post data extraction, the selected researchers (NC, DM, and TW) will be randomly assigned to the included articles to assess the methodological quality via the Modified Downs and Black Checklist [25]. The Modified Downs and Black Checklist can be used to assess the methodological quality of both randomized and non-randomized studies in healthcare research. The checklist includes 27 items across 5 primary categories (i.e. reporting, external validity, internal validity [bias], internal validity [confounding], and power). The maximum achievable score for a study is 32. Studies will be categorized as either excellent (26–28), good (20–25), fair (15–19), or poor (≤ 14) [26], with higher scores reflecting greater methodological robustness [25]. First, the selected researchers (NC, DM, TW) will review the same three studies independently. Results will be compared, and any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion. Once each researcher is confident in using the appraisal tool, the remaining articles will be distributed individually to NC, DM, or TW for assessment. We anticipate reviewed studies will include randomized control trials, cross-sectional studies, case–control studies, and case studies/series, all of which can be appropriately assessed with the Modified Downs and Black Checklist [25]. Studies will not be excluded based on quality; however, methodological quality will be charted in Microsoft Excel and reported for each study. When summarizing the results, the methodological quality (i.e. measure of reliability and rigour) of each study will be described and reflected in the narrative synthesis and further discussed in the Discussion section of the review.

Synthesis of results

The methodological decision process of including or excluding studies will be displayed using a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram [19, 22]. Descriptive data from the eligible full-text articles will be displayed in tables, for ease of interpretation. A narrative synthesis will be conducted to identify patterns across the populations within the literature. These analyses will identify technology and implementation strategies commonly used within each population for the measurement of gaze behaviour, allowing the identification of methodologies that are having positive impacts in a specific population which may have applicability and benefit to another target population. Specifically, we will compare and contrast the methodological approaches used in older adult and central neurological populations to determine whether similar methods have been applied across groups and whether gaze-tracking approaches common to older adults could inform future research direction for central neurological populations. The analyses will be visualized using concept maps and frequency plots. The quality assessment of each included study will be colour coded and displayed on a Cochrane Risk of Bias style table using Microsoft Excel [27].

Ethics and dissemination

The proposed scoping review does not require ethical approval from the University Health Network’s or the University of Toronto’s research ethics committees. The search strategy will be made publicly available on Open Science Framework and as an attached appendix to the published document.

The findings of the scoping review will be disseminated in academic and public venues. The authors will pursue publication in a tier-one scientific journal in the field of central neurological rehabilitation. The results will also be prepared for presentation at a scientific conference, specific to the populations identified within the review. Any significant clustering of key concepts or populations may be further explored via a systematic review, implementing a narrower focused research question, rigorous appraisal of study quality, and a statistical synthesis of the published evidence (meta-analysis). The findings will possibly bring to light a new field of study, bridging aspects of rehabilitation science currently studied in isolation, increasing the current understanding of gaze behaviour, its influence on fall prediction/prevention and refining techniques for the implementation of gaze rehabilitation in clinical practice. Furthermore, by identifying how existing research measures gaze behaviour during walking, this review may help inform the development of new methodological approaches for assessing this aspect of walking in clinical practice.
