Micromechanical characteristics of compression densification of spent mushroom residue biomass based on DEM
Authors: Zhen Li, Hongqiang Wang, Chao Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To fill the research gap regarding the influence of particle breakage on biomass densification, waste mushroom substrate (SMS) was used as the raw material. A Tavares breakage model was established, and EDEM simulations together with experimental analyses were carried out. The results show that particle breakage exhibits a staged evolution during compression. The relative breakage rate increases slowly, then rapidly, then slowly again before stopping. The voidage decreases nonlinearly with compression. After compression, the voidage along the compression direction shows spatial inhomogeneity, increasing from top to bottom, which is consistent with the BET pore volume measurements. Force response analysis reveals that a pronounced arching effect is observed, which is one of the reasons for the decrease of force chains along the compression direction. A quantitative analysis of force chain evolution was also performed, and the results are in good agreement with the trend of experimental particle microhardness changes. SEM analysis further indicates that fine particles generated by breakage fill the pores and, together with lignin softening, form a bonding structure, which corroborates the simulation results. This study provides a basis for understanding particle breakage during biomass densification and its impact on fuel quality.

Introduction

At present, with the continuous escalation of industrialisation and the rapid economic and social development of mankind, global energy consumption continues to rise 1. According to the BP Statistical Review of World Energy 2024, the total consumption of primary energy in the world has been increasing every year for the past 20 years, with fossil fuels accounting for more than 80 per cent of the total. Global coal consumption also continues to increase, with 164.03 EJ of coal consumed globally in 2023, an increase of 0.6 per cent from the previous year and the highest level of coal consumption since 2014. Coal continues to be the main fuel for global power generation, with a share of 35.4 per cent, and Asia is the world’s dominant coal consumer, accounting for more than three quarters of global coal consumption 2. However, the emergence of the energy crisis and environmental pollution has made the development of green and sustainable clean energy a strategic goal for countries around the world 3.

Biomass energy has the advantages of being renewable, widely distributed, with large reserves and low pollution 4. Increasing the rational development and utilisation of biomass energy can alleviate the energy crisis, and at the same time, it is beneficial to promote the transition of human society’s energy use to clean and sustainable development. As a large agricultural country and a populous country, China not only has huge agricultural and forestry biomass resources, but also produces a large amount of edible mushroom residue waste every year 5, however, its utilization rate still needs improvement. The most common way of utilising biomass energy refers to its compression moulding into solid pellet fuel with high density, high calorific value and easy to transport 6, in which the main influencing factors include moulding pressure, feedstock particle size, moisture content and temperature 7. Based on the significance of biomass energy development and utilisation, scholars at home and abroad have conducted a large number of experimental studies. Y. Zhang 8 employed the Discrete Element Method (DEM) to investigate the hot pressing process of pine waste and demonstrated that higher forming pressure significantly alters contact force transmission and particle displacement behavior. S. Mani 9 reported that biomass pellet quality is strongly influenced by feedstock particle size and moisture content. Z. Li 10 further showed that appropriate moisture content can enhance interparticle bonding strength and improve pellet integrity during densification. In addition, DEM-based studies have revealed the evolution of stress transfer, heat generation, and particle rearrangement during biomass compression 11.

These studies demonstrate that, although DEM has been widely applied to investigate the mesoscopic mechanical behavior of biomass particle densification, the coupling mechanisms among particle breakage, force transmission, and pore evolution during compression remain insufficiently understood. Because biomass particles are subjected to continuous loading, progressive fragmentation, and dynamic force-chain reconstruction during compression, conventional bonding models and empirical breakage models are unable to fully capture their mesoscopic evolutionary behavior. In contrast, the Tavares breakage model, based on local contact energy accumulation, introduces damage accumulation and dynamic daughter-particle generation mechanisms, enabling effective representation of progressive particle breakage and contact network evolution. With the rapid development of China’s edible mushroom industry, its annual production accounts for more than 75% of the global total, generating a large amount of spent mushroom substrate (SMS) that requires high-value utilization 12. As a typical heterogeneous biomass mixture, SMS exhibits pronounced particle fragmentation and structural reconfiguration during densification; however, the coupling mechanisms among particle breakage, force-chain structure, and pore evolution during compression have not yet been systematically studied. Therefore, this study combines DEM simulations based on the Tavares model with experimental analysis to investigate the coupled evolution of particle breakage, force-chain structure, and pore distribution during SMS compression, providing theoretical insight for mesoscopic mechanical understanding of biomass densification considering particle breakage effects. Tavares model and theory

The Tavares model is a breakage energy-based contact model written on the basis of the Hertz-Mindline (no slip) model. The particle substitution method used in the model is derived from collaborative research between the Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro and the University of Edinburgh 13. During the simulation, the energy acting on a particle in each loading (or collision) event is evaluated within the EDEM framework and compared with its breakage energy threshold. If the applied energy is lower than the particle’s breakage energy, no macroscopic breakage occurs. However, damage and microcracks may accumulate within the particle, reducing its structural integrity and making it more susceptible to breakage in subsequent loading events. This effect of subsequent impacts with energies less than the original particle breakage energy that may lead to breakage is called particle damage accumulation 14. Implementation of the Tavares breakage model in EDEM

In EDEM, the Tavares breakage model was coupled with the Hertz–Mindlin contact model to simulate particle breakage during biomass compression. The Hertz–Mindlin model was first used to calculate particle contact forces and contact energies. Subsequently, the corresponding contact energy was evaluated and transferred to the Tavares model for damage accumulation and breakage determination. Under the Tavares breakage model, the particle contact energy, characteristic breakage energy, and cumulative particle damage during the contact process are described by Eqs. (1), (11), and (12), respectively.11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{50} = E_{\infty } (1 + (\frac{{d_{0} }}{d})^{\Phi } )$$\end{document}

(11), E50 represents the characteristic breakage energy corresponding to a 50% breakage probability, E∞ is the asymptotic breakage energy, d0 is the reference particle size for breakage energy scaling, d is the current minimum particle size of the crushed particles, and Φ is the size dependence coefficient of breakage energy. Once the accumulated damage exceeds the break criterion, the parent particle is replaced by daughter particles according to the Tavares breakage algorithm. The breakage size distribution is controlled by parameter b, while the breakage probability dispersion is governed by parameter σ.

Simulation and experiments

Compared with the traditional Bonding contact model, the Tavares model fully takes into account the impact wear process of particles in the breakage simulation process 18. Although the Tavares breakage model is originally formulated based on impact energy, its implementation in EDEM evaluates breakage using the energy accumulated at particle contacts. This implies that particle breakage is governed by local contact energy, regardless of whether it arises from impact or quasi-static compression. While the model does not explicitly distinguish between different loading paths, it captures the progressive accumulation of energy leading to breakage. Therefore, it remains suitable for simulating particle fragmentation in biomass compression processes dominated by quasi-static loading. If the contact energy acting on a particle exceeds its limit contact energy during the simulation, the particle undergoes breakage and is replaced by daughter particles, as shown in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Particle breakage process.

Particle breakage process.

Additionally, spherical dummy particles with diameters of 1.2–2.4 mm were introduced to ensure numerical stability. These dummy-particles do not represent real material and do not participate in mechanical interactions. Analysis of results

Analysis of particle breakage

From the perspective of energy conservation, the energy consumed for particle breakage under the same confined system is numerically equal to the energy released in the breakage process, and the energy conversion of biomass particle breakage process in the numerical simulation system of this study complies with this law. The energy required for the particle specimen to withstand load to crack extension to breakage is an important indicator of the mechanical properties of the material. In the research process, this energy is defined as the breakage energy, which is expressed as the energy released by the particle loaded breakage process. The data obtained from the calculation is shown in Fig. 4, which shows that the overall breakage energy shows a rapidly increasing trend in the early stage, reaching a peak maximum value of 54.47 J near 39.6 s, and not increasing thereafter. This phenomenon indicates that there is no more new breakage energy generated in the system after 39.6 s, i.e., the particles are no longer crushed. Meanwhile, it can be observed from the figure that the breakage energy curve exhibits fluctuations within a certain range. This is mainly attributed to the intermittent accumulation and release of contact energy within the force-chain network during particle breakage and the destabilization of arch.Fig. 4Breakage energy–time curve.

Breakage energy–time curve.

Analysis of voidage evolution and particle characteristic

From a fine-grained point of view, biomass formed pellets are porous media, and the transport of small particles inside the pores changes the macroscopic properties of the material 23. In order to investigate the effect of breakage on the quality of formed pellet, the concept of voidage is introduced. During EDEM post-processing, the calculation of voidage requires the dynamic creation of geometry bins based on changes in particle volume within the mold, as shown in Fig. 9, or the placement of geometry bins at specified locations near the bottom of the mold, as shown in Fig. 10. The evolution of voidage in the formed pellet during compression under different compression displacements was extracted from the simulation data, and the corresponding variation trend is shown in Fig. 9. The results show that the porosity decreases slowly during the initial compression stage. After the compression time approaches 40 s, the porosity exhibits a nearly linear and rapid decline, reaching a minimum value of 10.09%. Since particle breakage has already ceased at 40 s, these changes indicate a transition from compaction dominated by particle breakage and rearrangement to densification governed primarily by pore collapse.

By analysing the change rule of voidage shown in Fig. 9, the change of voidage during the whole compression process showed a nonlinear trend. To investigate the possible heterogeneity in voidage distribution, five voidage detection elements were installed at fixed positions near the bottom of the die to dynamically collect voidage data at different locations during the forming process, as shown in Fig. 10. It was found that the changes in voidage of each part showed the same trend and a similar distribution to the changes in voidage of the overall formed pellet in Fig. 9, which also indicates the reliability of the above conclusion. In addition, the images show that the voidage of the top part L1 is the largest before the compression starts, which is presumed to be mainly due to the fact that the voidage of the particles at the other lower positions is somewhat reduced by the gravity of the upper particles. However, this change does not show a single linear trend, and it is assumed that the main reason is that the complex mechanical structure such as friction lap between particles in the mould weakens the gravity effect of the upper particles. After the compression started, the voidage of the formed pellet changed significantly, and the voidage of the lower part of the pellet was larger than that of the upper part, and the final voidage of the formed pellet was 4.78%, 8.28%, 10.87%, 12.06%, and 14.5% in the order from L1 to L5. It is inferred that the voidage reason for the non-uniform porosity lies in the arching structures, which redistribute the vertical stress to the surrounding particles and the die wall, thereby reducing the effective compressive stress transmitted to the lower region of the particles. It is also found that the voidage at the upper positions (L1, L2, and L3) exhibits larger fluctuations compared with the lower positions (L4 and L5). This is likely due to the influence of the contact structure and mechanical behavior of particles in the nearby undetected upper region.

Samples were taken from the center of each segment for BET analysis to determine the total mesopore volume. The BET results were then compared with the simulation results, as shown in Fig. 11. It was found that the BET measurements exhibited a trend consistent with the simulation data. Combining Fig. 12 with Fig. 13, it was found that the number of particles at the L5 (P5) position before the compression time of 30 s was relatively small compared to the other positions, but after 30 s there was a sharp increase. To investigate this phenomenon, the average particle size at each location was analyzed using the bottom measurement approach shown in Fig. 14. Subsequently, the particle sizes at L2, L3, L5 and L4 positions decreased rapidly in turn. At the same time, as the small particles continued to move to the lower layer, it would make the average particle size at L2 appear to have a transient increase in the phenomenon. Similarly, it is inferred that the increase in the average particle size at the L1 position at 55 s is mainly attributed to the downward migration of fine particles.

The average particle size at the L5 position continues to decrease significantly after 30 s of compression and reaches a minimum value of 0.88 mm at 43 s. After approximately 40 s, the average particle size at L5 begins to increase slowly, which is attributed to the cessation of particle breakage, allowing some larger particles from the upper layers to migrate into this region as compression continues. This migration is driven by percolation effects 24, while compression-induced size segregation and transient pore channels formed by particle breakage also contribute. Based on the analysis of particle number and particle size evolution shown in Figs. 13 and 14, particle breakage during the forming process predominantly progresses downward along the direction of the applied compressive force and gradually ceases once particles reach a certain size. In addition, due to the arching structure and the influence of the mold wall at the bottom, particles in the lower layer require the longest time to complete fragmentation.

Analysis of force response

The compression and forming process of SMS particles in mould has complex mechanical properties 25, and the study of the mechanical behaviour of particles in this process of compression and breakage and forming is of great significance for improving the quality of formed pellets and the improvement of the forming process. When the particles are compressed in the mould, a strong contact force chain network will be formed, and at the same time, due to the boundary conditions, the particles will self-assemble to form a stable force-transferring arch structure 26, and produce the arch effect. The application of the Discrete Element Method can reveal the spatial distribution, directionality and stability of the force chain, and can also determine the formation and collapse of the arch structure by the compression force or stress jumps.

Analysis of arch structure

Compression force data were extracted from both the simulation and experimental processes, as shown in Fig. 15, and the two exhibit good agreement with an absolute error of less than 4 kN. The results show that the compression force rises slowly and uniformly between 0 and 20 s. The compression force rises faster and jumps several times between 20 and 40 s. After 40 s, the compression force rises sharply and jumps significantly only once. The main reason for analysing it lies in the internal looseness of the particles between 0 and 20 s, and the pressure is mainly used to overcome the loose lap friction and voidage between the particles, as well as the breakage of a small part of the particles. Between 20 and 40 s the particles are in close contact, and at the same time, the complex laps will produce an arch structure, and the destruction process is accompanied by fluctuations in the force. After 40 s, the particles become highly compacted, and the pressure and stress fluctuations associated with the collapse of arching structures become more pronounced.Fig. 15Compression force–time curve.

Analysis of force chain

The contact force chains were analysed for the formed pellet and the results are shown in Fig. 19. In the diagram, red represents strong chains and blue represents weak chains. Strong chains indicate that the contact force between particles in that group is greater than the average contact force, while weak chains indicate that the contact force is less than the average contact force. As can be seen in Fig. 19, the strong force chain mainly appeared at the bottom and middle of the mould in the natural stacking state before the compression force was applied. Subsequently, under the action of the compression force, the strong force chain moved up to the top and continued to shift to the lower part, and the phenomenon of weakening of the particle contact force chain along the direction of the compression force appeared. The main reason is that the complex interlocking friction between particles and the resulting arching effect weaken the stress borne by the lower particles, while the downward migration and accumulation of fine particles generated after breakage also play a role. This phenomenon differs from the conclusion that the force chain is transmitted from the top and bottom ends to the middle as calculated by the discrete element Bonding model in literature 28. The main reason is that the bonding model represents interparticle cohesion and bond breakage through virtual bonds. In contrast, the Tavares breakage model directly describes particle fragmentation based on an energy threshold. This process continuously modifies the contact network, alters the force chain distribution, and redistributes the load among newly generated particles, thereby reducing the persistence of load-bearing chains. Compared with the bonding model, the force chain structure in the Tavares model is more dispersed and exhibits a more dynamic evolution. By analyzing the EDEM post-processing data, the total particle contact forces and the total number of particle contacts were calculated to quantify the average contact force between particles. The results are shown in Fig. 20, where the average contact force exhibits an approximately linear increase.Fig. 19Force chain-time relationship.Fig. 20Average contact force between particles.

Microstructure analysis

Based on the actual compression test, cross-section slicing samples were made with formed pellets, and the breakage surface was observed and analyzed for micro-morphology by SEM. The experimental procedure is shown in Fig. 25. First, slices were cut from the formed pellets. The cut specimens were then placed in an intelligent vacuum drying oven for 24 h. After drying was completed, the specimens were placed into a vacuum ion sputtering instrument to uniformly spray 10–20 nm of conductive gold. This process used a 20 mA current and continued spraying for 90 s. Finally, the prepared specimen was put into the sample chamber and vacuumed, and the scanning test was carried out after vacuuming. Microstructural Analysis: Sample Preparation Process and Testing.

Micro-morphology of cross-section.

Figure 26 shows the microstructural morphology of the formed pellet detected in sections (a), (b) and (c), where location c is near the bottom of the formed pellet. From all three sets of figures, it can be seen that there are complex morphological distributions and contact structures between the particles, including breakage lines, contact pores, overlapping structures and embedding, and so on. The difference is that the liquid solidified tissue is clearly observed in Figs. (a) and (b), accompanied by a disordered indefinite bubble-like pore structure. As can be seen from the figures, this liquid phase solidified tissue acts well as a natural binder. By integrating the softening temperature of lignin in biomass 29–31 with the frictional temperature generated during compression 31,32, it can be determined that this liquid-phase solidified tissue is an amorphous glassy structure formed by the melting of lignin during compression, and an amorphous thin film formed by the melting of lignin and laying flat on the surface of the solid component. The natural breakage surfaces in Fig. (c) mainly show amorphous lap, embedded and contact pore structures between the particles, with no obvious liquid-phase cured organisation. It is assumed that this difference in the distribution of liquid-phase cured tissues is mainly caused by the difference in pressure and frictional heat applied to the particles at each site.

Analysed in terms of contact form, the particles embedded around the pores in Fig. (a) tend to have a flat distribution in the cross-section layer, the particles embedded around the pores in Fig. (b) have a flat with a slight concavity in the cross-section layer, and the concavity of the small particles in Fig. (c) is slightly more prominent. Based on such phenomena, it was judged that the contact between particles in the upper and lower layers at position (a) was dominated by surface contact, but point contact appeared in the upper and lower layers at positions (b) and (c). This phenomenon is associated with a decrease in the pressure exerted on particles from (a) to (c), and may also be related to the large compression displacement of the upper particles in (a), which results in higher frictional heat and facilitates compression. In addition, the contact compactness between particles show a tendency to weaken sequentially along the pressure direction. This conclusion is consistent with the previous simulation study which found that the pressure on the bottom particles is weakened by the arch structure.

Research limitations and future work

The Tavares model parameters used in this study were not obtained directly; instead, they were derived from the parameter ranges of similar biomass materials. After repeated adjustments through multiple simulations and experiments, good agreement was achieved between the model results and the experimental data. Nevertheless, we believe that directly calibrating each set of parameters in the Tavares model for different biomass materials would be more convincing, and this constitutes the research work we plan to carry out in the future.
