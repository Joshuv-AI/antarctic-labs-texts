Crystal‐Structure‐Dependent Water Adsorption Governs Water Oxidation Activity of FeOOH Polymorphs: Insights from Electrochemical and Bias‐Free Photochemical Systems
Authors: Megumi Okazaki, Kenta Ikegami, Seira Mori, Zi Lang Goo, Naoki Tarutani, Takeshi Watanabe, Shunsuke Nozawa, Yuta Tsuji, Kunihisa Sugimoto, Kiyofumi Katagiri, Kazuhiko Maeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
FeOOH has emerged as a promising earth‐abundant catalyst for water oxidation, with activity comparable to that of precious‐metal oxides such as IrO2 and RuO2 under optimal conditions. Herein, we systematically investigate the effect of crystal structure on water oxidation activity using four FeOOH polymorphs (α, β, γ, and δ) under identical conditions. We demonstrate that the catalytic performance strongly depends on the crystal structure, with higher surface hydrophilicity leading to enhanced water oxidation activity. The same structure‐dependent trend is observed under both electrochemical and photochemical conditions, indicating that the intrinsic catalytic properties of FeOOH govern the reaction irrespective of the reaction mode. Density functional theory calculations reveal that the adsorption energy for H2O on FeOOH surfaces correlates well with the experimentally observed activity, with δ‐FeOOH exhibiting the highest performance. These findings identify interfacial hydrophilicity as a key descriptor for water oxidation on FeOOH polymorphs and provide design guidelines for the development of efficient, earth‐abundant water oxidation catalysts.

Introduction

Electrochemical and photocatalytic water splitting have attracted considerable attention as promising routes for the sustainable production of hydrogen fuel from renewable energy sources. In both systems, the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), which accompanies H2 generation, is generally recognized as the kinetically most demanding half‐reaction and thus largely determines the overall efficiency of water splitting [1, 2]. Consequently, extensive efforts have been devoted to the development of highly active OER catalysts, among which precious‐metal oxides, including IrO2 and RuO2, represent the state‐of‐the‐art materials [3, 4, 5]. However, the scarcity and high cost of these noble metals impose serious limitations on their large‐scale deployment, highlighting the urgent need for alternative OER catalysts composed of earth‐abundant elements.

In this context, Fe‐based oxyhydroxides (FeOOH) have emerged as promising candidates for sustainable water oxidation catalysis. Fe is the fourth‐most‐abundant metal element in the Earth's crust, and FeOOH exhibits multiple polymorphs with distinct crystal structures, conventionally denoted as α‐, β‐, γ‐, and δ‐FeOOH (Figure 1). Notably, Morikawa et al. have demonstrated that a Ni‐doped β‐FeOOH electrode exhibits electrocatalytic OER performance comparable to that of IrO2 [6]. In addition, β‐ and δ‐FeOOH have recently been independently reported as efficient OER electrocatalysts by several research groups [7, 8]. Beyond purely electrochemical systems, Mullins et al. [9] have reported that photoelectrochemical OER activity of BiVO4 is enhanced by depositing an amorphous FeOOH film. Morikawa et al. [10] have demonstrated that the deposition of an amorphous FeOOH onto N‐doped TiO2 photoelectrodes substantially enhances photoelectrochemical water splitting. These reports show that FeOOH can also function as an effective water oxidation cocatalyst in light‐driven systems, and collectively suggest that FeOOH is a versatile and sustainable OER material applicable to both electrochemical and photochemical water splitting.

Crystal structures of α‐, β, γ‐, and δ‐FeOOH.

Despite these promising reports, the relationship between the crystal structure of FeOOH and its catalytic activity for water oxidation remains insufficiently understood. From a practical perspective, the presence of multiple FeOOH polymorphs offers a unique opportunity to establish structure–activity relationships that could guide the rational design of Fe‐based OER catalysts. However, most previous related studies have investigated FeOOH catalysts with different crystal structures prepared on distinct electrode substrates or under disparate experimental conditions. As a result, a systematic comparison of water oxidation activity across all four FeOOH polymorphs (α–δ) under unified conditions has not yet been reported, particularly with respect to both electrochemical and photochemical reaction systems.

In this work, we present a comprehensive investigation of crystal‐structure‐dependent water oxidation activity across all four FeOOH polymorphs (α–δ) under identical experimental conditions in both electrochemical and photochemical systems. As shown in Figure 1, all FeOOH polymorphs are composed of FeO6 octahedra but differ in crystal system and space group: α‐FeOOH (orthorhombic, Pbnm), β‐FeOOH (tetragonal, I4/m), γ‐FeOOH (orthorhombic, Cmcm), and δ‐FeOOH (trigonal, P3¯m1). For electrochemical OER, previous comparative studies focusing on limited pairs of polymorphs have suggested that factors such as the Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio play a decisive role in determining catalytic performance [11, 12, 13]. By contrast, our systematic analysis reveals that, when all four polymorphs are considered, the OER overpotential correlates more strongly with the degree of surface hydrophilicity of FeOOH. In addition, in photochemical water oxidation using a Ru(II) complex as a photosensitizer, the OER rate exhibits a pronounced dependence on the FeOOH crystal structure even under electrochemically unbiased conditions. To elucidate the origin of these trends, we carried out density functional theory (DFT) calculations to evaluate the adsorption energies for H2O molecules on representative FeOOH surfaces. The results demonstrate that more hydrophilic polymorphs exhibit lower water adsorption energies, providing a unified mechanistic rationale for the observed structure‐dependent activity in both electrochemical and photochemical water oxidation. These findings offer fundamental insights into the role of crystal structure in FeOOH‐based OER catalysis and establish design principles for sustainable, earth‐abundant water oxidation catalysts.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Synthesized FeOOH

The four FeOOH polymorphs (α–δ) were synthesized separately; the detailed synthetic procedures are provided in the Experimental section. Powder X‐ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Figure S1) confirm the successful formation of single‐phase FeOOH with crystal structures consistent with those previously reported for each polymorph [14, 15, 16], indicating that no crystalline impurities were present within the detection limit.

The oxidation state and local coordination environment of Fe were examined by X‐ray absorption spectroscopy. As shown in the X‐ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra (Figure S2a), all of the FeOOH samples exhibit absorption edge positions characteristic of Fe3+ and closely resembling the edge position of Fe2O3, confirming that Fe is present exclusively in the trivalent state, irrespective of the crystal structure. By contrast, the extended X‐ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra (Figure S2b,c) clearly differ from those of Fe2O3, reflecting the distinct local coordination environments of Fe in FeOOH. Subtle but discernible differences among the α–δ polymorphs are also observed in the EXAFS region, which is attributable to variations in Fe—O bond lengths and coordination geometry arising from their different crystal structures [17, 18, 19]. These results demonstrate that all of the samples share the same oxidation state while retaining crystal‐structure‐dependent local environments.

Surface‐sensitive XPS measurements further corroborate the Fe3+ state at the surface of FeOOH. The Fe 2p3/2 peaks appear in the binding‐energy range 711–714 eV for all of the polymorphs (Figure S3), in agreement with previously reported values for Fe3+ oxyhydroxides [20, 21].

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Figure 2) reveal morphological differences among the FeOOH polymorphs. Rod‐like particles are observed for α‐, β‐, and γ‐FeOOH, whereas δ‐FeOOH consists of round‐shaped aggregates composed of primary nanoparticles. The particle sizes follow the trend β > α > γ > δ.

SEM images of α‐, β, γ‐, and δ‐FeOOH polymorphs.

Thermogravimetric (TG) analysis was performed to evaluate the thermal stability and surface hydroxyl (–OH) content of the FeOOH polymorphs (Figure S4). All of the samples exhibit gradual weight loss when heated in the temperature range 300–680 K. For α‐, γ‐, and δ‐FeOOH, the total weight loss converges to approximately 11%, which is close to the theoretical value (10.2%) expected for the phase transformation from FeOOH to Fe2O3. By contrast, β‐FeOOH shows additional weight loss at higher temperatures (>550 K), which is plausibly attributed to the gradual release of Cl− ions incorporated in the crystal lattice for structural stabilization. In addition, a distinct weight decrease observed between 318 and 398 K for all of the samples is assigned to the desorption of surface –OH groups [22]. The crystal‐structure‐dependent surface hydroxylation was further corroborated by water vapor adsorption measurements. Figure 3a shows the water‐vapor adsorption isotherms for the FeOOH polymorphs. Surface oxygen‐containing functional groups, such as –OH, serve as primary adsorption sites for H2O molecules. At low relative pressures (p/p 0 < 0.2), water molecules preferentially adsorb onto these sites, followed by the formation of water clusters around the initially adsorbed molecules [23]. Accordingly, a larger water uptake in this pressure region directly reflects higher surface hydrophilicity. Consistent with the TG results, δ‐FeOOH shows the highest water adsorption capacity, indicating that it exhibits the most hydrophilic surface among the four polymorphs. The calculated Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface areas based on water adsorption (SBET,H2O) are summarized in Table 1.

(a) H2O and (b) N2 absorption/desorption isotherms, which were acquired at room temperature and 77 K, respectively.

For comparison, we recorded N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms to assess the textural properties of the FeOOH samples (Figure 3b). α‐, β‐, and γ‐FeOOH display type‐II isotherms, characteristic of nonporous or macroporous materials, whereas δ‐FeOOH exhibits a type‐IV isotherm, indicating mesoporous features. Notably, the adsorption capacities derived from N2 adsorption do not follow the same trend as those obtained from water‐vapor adsorption. This discrepancy highlights that physisorption behavior probed by nonpolar molecules such as N2 does not necessarily correlate with surface hydrophilicity governed by polar interactions with H2O molecules. These results emphasize that surface –OH density, rather than surface area alone, is a key descriptor for evaluating the interaction between FeOOH surfaces and water molecules.

Overall, our comprehensive characterization confirms that the synthesized FeOOH polymorphs are comparable in terms of phase purity and Fe oxidation state, while exhibiting pronounced differences in surface hydroxylation and hydrophilicity depending on the crystal structure. These well‐defined variations provide a solid basis for systematically elucidating the crystal‐structure‐dependent catalytic activity of FeOOH in electrochemical and photochemical water oxidation reactions discussed in the following sections.

Electrochemical Water Oxidation by FeOOH Catalysts

Electrochemical water oxidation was evaluated using FeOOH catalysts immobilized onto carbon paper (CP) substrates. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements were conducted under identical experimental conditions; the resultant polarization curves are shown in Figure 4a. A pronounced dependence of the OER activity on the crystal structure of FeOOH is evident, as reflected in the onset potential and current response associated with O2 evolution.

(a) Linear sweep voltammograms of FeOOH/CP electrodes and overpotential determined for water oxidation. The inset shows the overpotentials required to reach a current density of 200 µA cm–2. (b) Chronoamperometry of FeOOH/CP electrodes.

To quantitatively compare the intrinsic electrocatalytic performance of the FeOOH polymorphs, the overpotential (η OER) for water oxidation was defined as the potential difference between the thermodynamic OER potential (1.23 V vs. reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE)) and the applied potential required to reach a current density of 200 µA cm–2. This relatively low current density was chosen to minimize the influence of mass transport limitations and to focus on the intrinsic catalytic properties of the FeOOH surfaces. Among the four polymorphs, δ‐FeOOH exhibited the smallest overpotential, whereas α‐FeOOH showed the largest value. The difference in overpotential between these two extremes amounts to approximately 300 mV, clearly demonstrating a strong crystal‐structure dependence of the electrochemical water oxidation activity.

The trend observed in the LSV measurements is further supported by chronoamperometric (CA) responses recorded at a fixed potential of +1.8 V vs. RHE (Figure 4b). Consistent with the overpotential analysis, δ‐FeOOH delivers the highest and most stable anodic current, whereas α‐FeOOH shows the lowest activity under identical conditions. These results confirm that the relative OER performance of the FeOOH polymorphs is robust and reproducible across different electrochemical evaluation methods.

To examine the structural stability of the FeOOH catalysts during electrochemical operation, XRD patterns were collected before and after the OER measurements (Figure S5). No discernible changes in peak positions or relative intensities are observed for any of the polymorphs. Although XRD is not sufficiently surface‐sensitive to completely exclude subtle surface reconstruction at the catalyst surface, the absence of detectable crystallographic changes indicates the crystal structures of α‐, β‐, γ‐, and δ‐FeOOH are preserved throughout the electrochemical tests. This structural stability demonstrates that all four FeOOH polymorphs function as genuine water oxidation electrocatalysts without undergoing detectable phase transformations under the applied reaction conditions.

The electrochemical double‐layer capacitance (C dl) for the FeOOH catalysts was evaluated by cyclic voltammetry (CV) in a non‐Faradaic potential window (1.10–1.20 V vs. RHE) using the same electrolyte as that used for the OER measurements (Figure S6). For all of the FeOOH polymorphs, the capacitive current exhibits a linear dependence on the scan rate, confirming that the measured currents originate predominantly from double‐layer charging rather than from Faradaic processes. To isolate the contribution from the FeOOH catalysts, the C dl values for the bare CP substrate were subtracted from those for the FeOOH/CP electrodes.

The obtained C dl values (Table 1) reveal a clear inverse correlation with the η OER values determined from LSV measurements (Figure 5a). Specifically, δ‐FeOOH, which exhibits the smallest overpotential, shows the largest C dl, whereas α‐FeOOH displays the smallest C dl and the highest overpotential. Rather than simply reflecting the electrochemically active surface area, this trend suggests that C dl serves as an effective descriptor of the interfacial accessibility of water molecules to the FeOOH surface. A higher C dl indicates an enhanced ability to accumulate water molecules at the catalyst/electrolyte interface, thereby facilitating the water oxidation reaction.

Relationship between (a) overpotential at 200 µA cm−2 and electrochemical double layer capacitance and (b) overpotential at 200 µA cm−2 and SBET,H2O.

This interpretation is strongly supported by independent water‐vapor adsorption measurements. Among the four polymorphs, δ‐FeOOH exhibits the highest water adsorption capacity, whereas α‐FeOOH shows the lowest (Figure 3), in excellent agreement with the C dl trend. In addition, a quantitative correlation between the SBET,H2O and η OER values reveals a nearly linear decrease in overpotential with increasing SBET,H2O (Figure 5b); furthermore, the current density recorded at +1.9 V vs. RHE showed a good correlation with SBET,H2O (Figure S7b). By contrast, no meaningful correlation is observed between the overpotential and the specific surface area obtained from N2 adsorption (SBET,N2) (Figure S7a). This clear divergence demonstrates that the electrocatalytic performance of FeOOH is governed predominantly by surface hydrophilicity and water‐adsorption behavior rather than by the total surface area probed by nonpolar molecules.

Together with the XANES and XPS results, these findings conclusively indicate that the observed differences in electrochemical OER activity cannot be attributed to variations in the electronic state of Fe. Instead, the crystal‐structure‐dependent interfacial hydrophilicity of FeOOH, which determines the local availability of water molecules at the active surface, emerges as the key factor controlling the water oxidation performance.

Photochemical Water Oxidation

To further investigate the intrinsic catalytic activity of FeOOH for water oxidation, we conducted photochemical OERs under electrochemistry‐free conditions using a Ru(II) complex photosensitizer [13]. In this system, FeOOH catalysts were suspended in aqueous solution without the use of any electrochemical apparatus, allowing assessment of their catalytic performance independent of electrode effects.

Under irradiation with visible light (λ = 480 nm), the Ru(II) tris–bipyridine complex ([Ru(bpy)3]2+) is photoexcited and subsequently undergoes oxidative quenching by persulfate (S2O8 2–) ions, which serve as a sacrificial electron acceptor, to generate the one‐electron‐oxidized species [Ru(bpy)3]3+. Electron transfer from the suspended FeOOH particles to [Ru(bpy)3]3+ subsequently regenerates the ground‐state [Ru(bpy)3]2+, while oxidative equivalents (holes) accumulate on the FeOOH surface to drive water oxidation catalysis.

The time course of O2 evolution is summarized in Figure 6. Among the tested polymorphs, δ‐FeOOH exhibited the highest O2‐evolution activity. The observed O2 evolution rate (r OER) followed the order δ > γ > β > α, which is fully consistent with the trend observed in electrochemical water oxidation measurements, indicating that the intrinsic catalytic properties of FeOOH dominate the activity irrespective of the reaction mode. Regarding the relationship between electrochemical and photochemical water oxidation system, the normalized O2 evolution activity by C dl values (r OER/C dl listed in Table 1) did not follow the same trend as the apparent r OER, although C dl is often used as a proxy for electrochemically accessible surface area (ECSA) descriptor.

Schematic of photochemical water oxidation mechanism and time courses of O2 evolution over FeOOH polymorphs.

Control experiments excluding individual components of the photochemical system (FeOOH catalyst, [Ru(bpy)3]2+, persulfate, or light irradiation) resulted in negligible O2 evolution (Table S1), confirming that all of the components are essential for catalytic water oxidation. The apparent quantum yield for δ‐FeOOH reached 11% at 450 nm, and the action spectrum closely followed the absorption profile for [Ru(bpy)3]2+ rather than that for FeOOH (Figure S8a), supporting the proposed photosensitized reaction mechanism. In addition, catalyst durability was examined through recycling experiments. As shown in Figure S8b, δ‐FeOOH maintained stable O2‐evolution activity over three consecutive cycles. In these tests, only the aqueous solution containing the [Ru(bpy)3]2+ photosensitizer was refreshed every 3 h, demonstrating that the FeOOH catalysts themselves are robust and reusable under photochemical water oxidation conditions.

To confirm the structural stability of the four FeOOH polymorphs after photochemical water oxidation (3 h), we compared their XRD patterns with those acquired before the reaction (Figure S9). This comparison confirms that the crystal structures were preserved. In addition, the oxidation state of surface Fe was observed by XPS as almost 3+ after the reaction, even for δ‐FeOOH, which exhibited a slightly lower binding energy before the reaction (Figure S10). The binding energy maxima of 2p3/2 after reaction for all of the polymorphs were approximately consistent with each other. The XANES spectra show the same tendency of oxidation states before and after the reaction, indicating that the oxidation states of Fe did not substantially change before/after the reaction (Figure S11). Hence, the oxidation states of Fe in the four FeOOH polymorphs used in this study were maintained as 3+ during the water oxidation reaction, not only on the surface but in the bulk. These results therefore strongly suggest that the valence state of Fe itself not only on the surface of FeOOH but also in the bulk does not substantially affect the O2‐evolution ability among the polymorphs. In addition, as confirmed by the EXAFS spectra in Figure S12, the coordination environment around Fe was not changed between before and after the reaction. EXAFS also revealed that the spectra of the four FeOOH polymorphs differ completely from that of α‐Fe2O3. This result indicates that all of the FeOOH polymorphs retain their original crystal structures during photochemical reaction.

To examine the behavior of the photosensitizer, we acquired UV–vis absorption spectra of the reaction solutions before and after the photochemical reaction. The spectra of all of the solutions after reaction with the different FeOOH polymorphs show diminished intensities of the absorption bands at 400–550 nm and 260–350 nm (Figure S13), which are assigned to singlet metal‐to‐ligand charge transfer (1MLCT) and to the π–π* band of [Ru(bpy)3]2+, respectively. In addition, a new broad band was observed at approximately 600–700 nm in all cases after reaction, indicating the generation of the one‐electron‐oxidized species of the photosensitizer, [Ru(bpy)3]3+ [24]. Therefore, the photochemical water oxidation using the FeOOH catalysts is consistent with an oxidative quenching mechanism involving electron transfer from the photoexcited Ru(II) complex to the persulfate acceptor, followed by hole accumulation on FeOOH.

Adsorption Energy for H2O Molecule on FeOOH Surface by DFT Calculations

To clarify the relationship between hydrophilicity and water oxidation activity, we estimated the water adsorption energy using DFT calculations to confirm the hydrophilicity trend of FeOOH polymorphs quantitatively. The crystal structures used in the DFT calculations were those for three phases: α [25], which showed the lowest activity; γ, which showed moderate activity; and δ, which exhibited the highest activity in the photochemical water oxidation reaction. The surface facets used for the calculations were determined on the basis of previously reported studies [7, 26, 27, 28]. For the α phase, the (021) and (110) facets have been suggested as possible water adsorption surfaces, and studies have reported that water molecules are more strongly bound on the (110) facet under room‐temperature conditions [26]. For the γ phase, enhanced reactivity has been observed on the (001) facet [28], while for the δ phase, HRTEM analyses have indicated the exposure of the (001) facet [7]. Antiferromagnetic ordering was adopted for α‐ and γ‐FeOOH on the basis of previous studies [27, 28]; for δ‐FeOOH, three possible antiferromagnetic configurations were examined and the most stable one was selected. The resultant surface models were subsequently optimized (Figure S14). A water molecule was then placed on the OH‐terminated, coordinatively saturated surfaces in 30 randomly generated initial configurations, which were subsequently optimized. The configuration with the lowest energy was regarded as the most stable adsorption structure for water (Figure 7), and the water adsorption energy was calculated as −0.737, −0.967, and −1.285 eV for the α, γ, and δ phases, respectively.

Optimized structures of FeOOH polymorphs α(110), γ(001), and δ(001) are shown in top row of figures. Correspondingly, FeOOH polymorphs with adsorbed water molecule (black solid circle) are shown in bottom row of figures. Yellow‐green and brown: Fe, red: O, white: H. Fe atoms are shown in yellow‐green and brown, where the two colors indicate opposite spin orientations.

The calculated water adsorption energies were found to be most stable in the order δ, γ, and α, consistent with the trend in hydrophilicity (Figure 3). The relationship between the estimated water adsorption energy and the O2 evolution rate (Figure 8) strongly suggests a linear correlation with each other. Therefore, the constructed surface models reasonably reflect the experimental environment, confirming that the DFT calculations appropriately evaluate the observed trend in hydrophilicity.

Relationship between water adsorption energy and O2 evolution rate in photochemical water oxidation.

Conclusion

Four FeOOH polymorphs (α, β, γ, and δ) were systematically evaluated as water oxidation catalysts under both electrochemical and photochemical conditions to elucidate the effect of crystal structure on catalytic activity. Among the polymorphs, δ‐FeOOH, which exhibited the highest surface hydrophilicity, showed the lowest overpotential for water oxidation under electrochemical conditions. The same trend was consistently observed in photochemical water oxidation, where δ‐FeOOH demonstrated the highest activity. XPS and XAFS analyses revealed that the oxidation state of Fe remained predominantly Fe3+ for all of the polymorphs during the reaction, indicating that differences in O2‐evolution activity cannot be attributed to variations in the valence state of Fe. Instead, the results indicate that surface hydrophilicity plays a decisive role in determining the water oxidation performance of FeOOH. In addition, DFT calculations showed that the adsorption energy for H2O on the FeOOH surfaces correlates well with the experimentally observed hydrophilicity, highlighting the importance of water adsorption processes in governing catalytic activity. These findings establish surface hydrophilicity as a key descriptor for water oxidation on FeOOH polymorphs and provide design insights for the development of efficient, earth‐abundant water oxidation catalysts.

Experimental Section

Materials

Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (Wako Pure Chemicals, ≥99.9%), FeCl3·6H2O (Kanto Chemicals, ≥99.0%), FeCl2·4H2O (Kanto Chemicals, ≥99.0), NaOH (Kanto Chemicals, ≥97.0), H2O2 (Kanto Chemicals, 30.0%–35.5%), Na2S2O8 (Wako Pure Chemicals, ≥97.0%), tris(2,2′‐bipyridyl)dichloro ruthenium(II) hexahydrate (Sigma–Aldrich, 99.95% trace metal basis), BN (Wako Pure Chemicals, ≥99.0%), and C2H5OH (Kanto Chemicals, ≥99.5%) were used without further purification. Aqueous phosphate solutions containing NaH2PO4·2H2O (Wako Pure Chemicals, ≥99.0%) and Na2HPO4·12H2O (Wako Pure Chemicals, ≥98.0%) were prepared as neutral pH buffers.

Synthesis of FeOOH Polymorphs

α‐FeOOH was synthesized by a hydrothermal method according to a previously reported procedure [15]. To 25 mL of an aqueous solution containing 0.16 M Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, 9 mL of 5 M NaOH was added under vigorous stirring, followed by 4 h of additional stirring. The resultant suspension was transferred into a 100 mL Teflon‐lined container and heated at 393 K for 12 h using a stainless‐steel autoclave. The obtained product was separated by centrifugation, washed three times with deionized water, and then dried overnight under vacuum to obtain the final product.

β‐FeOOH was synthesized by a hydrothermal method according to a previously reported procedure [15]. An aqueous solution (50 mL) containing 0.5 M FeCl3·6H2O was transferred into a 100 mL Teflon‐lined container and heated at 353 K for 16 h using a stainless‐steel autoclave. The obtained product was separated by centrifugation, washed three times with deionized water and then dried overnight under vacuum to obtain the final product.

γ‐FeOOH was synthesized according to a previously reported method [14]. An aqueous solution (50 mL) containing 0.1 M FeCl2·4H2O was prepared and then slowly oxidized by O2 in air under an air flow rate of 0.8 L min−1. During the oxidation process, 5 M NaOH was added dropwise to maintain the pH in the range of 8.0–8.5. Completion of the oxidation reaction was confirmed by stabilization of the pH. The resultant product was separated by centrifugation, washed three times with deionized water, and dried overnight under vacuum to obtain the final product.

δ‐FeOOH was synthesized according to a previously reported method [16]. An aqueous solution (50 mL) containing 0.1 M FeCl2·4H2O was prepared, and its pH was adjusted to 8.0–8.5 by dropwise addition of 5 M NaOH. Rapid oxidation was then induced by injecting 15 mL of H2O2 solution under vigorous stirring. The resultant product was separated by centrifugation, washed three times with deionized water, and dried overnight under vacuum to obtain the final product.

Characterization

The details of the instruments used for characterization are described below. XRD patterns were recorded using a MiniFlex 600 powder diffractometer (Rigaku) equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source operated at 40 kV and 15 mA. Peak assignments were performed using ICSD entries #239322, #31136, #1545, and #38299.

Gas adsorption–desorption isotherms were recorded using a BELSORP‐max II instrument (BEL Japan). Prior to the measurements, samples were degassed overnight at 343 K. N2 and H2O adsorption–desorption measurements were carried out at 77 and 298 K, respectively. The measurements were conducted under standard conditions commonly employed for the respective adsorbates, and all experiments were performed at temperatures where the FeOOH crystal structures remained stable without phase transformation. The specific surface area was calculated using the BET method with the BELMaster software.

Thermogravimetry–differential thermal analysis (TG–DTA) was performed using a Thermo Mass Photo analyzer (Rigaku) under a He atmosphere, with a heating rate of 10 K min−1 to 673 K. Fe K‐edge XANES spectra were collected in transmission mode at the BL33XU beamline of SPring‐8 and the BL12C beamline of KEK. X‐ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) data were analyzed using the Athena software package.

X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out using an ESCA‐3400 (Kratos Analytical, Japan) equipped with a monochromatic Mg Kα source (150 W). The C 1s peak of adventitious hydrocarbons at 284.8 eV was used as an internal standard for binding‐energy calibration.

Electrochemical Measurements

Electrochemical measurements were carried out at room temperature using a potentiostat (HZ‐Pro, Hokuto Denko) and a single‐compartment electrochemical cell. The cell was made of Pyrex and configured in a three‐electrode system with a Pt‐wire counter electrode and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The potentials measured against the Ag/AgCl reference electrode (saturated KCl aqueous solution) were converted to the RHE scale using the following equation

ERHE = EAg/AgCl + 0.059 pH + 0.197 at 298 K

In this study, the electrochemical activity was evaluated on the basis of the current density normalized by the catalyst powder loading area. A 0.05 M phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.6) was used as the electrolyte. Prior to the measurements, the electrolyte solution was purged with Ar gas for at least 30 min to ensure sufficient deaeration. CV was performed over the potential range from +0.35 to +2.00 V (vs. RHE) for two cycles, and the anodic sweep of the second cycle was used as LSV. Chronoamperometric measurements were conducted at +1.8 V (vs. RHE) for 30 min.

Photochemical Water Oxidation Reactions

Photochemical water oxidation reactions were carried out using [Ru(bpy)3]SO4 as a photosensitizer. All experiments were performed in a closed circulation system equipped with a top‐irradiation‐type reaction cell. [Ru(bpy)3]SO4 was prepared by adding one equivalent of Ag2SO4 to an aqueous solution of [Ru(bpy)3]Cl2, followed by precipitation and removal of AgCl.

The reaction solution was prepared by dissolving a phosphate buffer (0.5 mM), [Ru(bpy)3]SO4 (0.25 mM), and Na2S2O8 (0.5 mM) in 20 mL of deionized water, and 10 mg of FeOOH was suspended as a catalyst. Light irradiation was performed using a Xe lamp (MAX303) equipped with a 480 nm band‐pass filter under an incident light intensity of approximately 1.0 mW cm−2. The evolved gases were quantified by gas chromatography.

The apparent quantum yield (AQY) was determined by monochromatic light irradiation at wavelengths of 400, 420, 450, 480, 520, and 580 nm using a Xe lamp (MAX303) equipped with the corresponding band‐pass filters. The AQY values were calculated using the following equation

AQY (%) = (A × R) / I × 100 where A is the reaction coefficient (for oxygen evolution, A = 4; however, in the present system, A = 2 because two electrons are involved in the reduction of Na2S2O8, which acts as an electron acceptor), R is the rate of O2 evolution, and I is the number of incident photons per unit time.

DFT Calculations

DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) version 6.4.3 [29, 30]. Core electrons were described using the projector augmented‐wave (PAW) method [31], and valence electrons were expanded in a plane‐wave basis set with a cutoff energy of 520 eV. The exchange–correlation interactions were treated using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional [32] within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA). Spin‐polarized calculations were performed, and the on‐site Coulomb interaction for the Fe 3d electrons was described by the GGA + U method within the Dudarev formalism [33], with U eff = 6.0 eV (U = 7.0 eV, J = 1.0 eV), as adopted in a previous study [27]. Dispersion interactions were treated using Grimme's D3 correction method with Becke–Johnson damping (D3‐BJ) [34].

The convergence criteria were set to 0.05 eV Å−1 for atomic forces and 1 × 10−5 eV for the total energy. Brillouin‐zone sampling was performed using a Γ‐centered k‐point mesh with a spacing of 2π × 0.05 Å−1. Surface supercells (α‐(110): 2 × 1, γ‐(001): 2 × 1, and δ‐(001): 2 × 2) were constructed on the basis of reported crystallographic data, using the corresponding unit cells and initial atomic positions, followed by structural and energy optimizations.

The unit cells and initial atomic positions for the FeOOH polymorphs were obtained from previously reported crystallographic data [7, 26, 27, 28]. A vacuum layer of at least 20 Å was introduced along the c‐axis to avoid interactions between periodic images. Dipole corrections were also applied along the c‐axis to eliminate artificial electrostatic interactions between periodically repeated slabs. An isolated H2O molecule was calculated in a 20 × 20 × 20 Å3 box. The optimized structures presented in this work were rendered using VESTA. The adsorption energy of water was calculated using the following equation

Eads = Esurf/water − Esurf − Ewater where E surf/water is the total energy of the surface slab with an adsorbed water molecule, E surf is the total energy of the clean surface slab, and E water is the total energy of an isolated water molecule.
