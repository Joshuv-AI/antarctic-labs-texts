Distinct proteomic profiles of clinical isolates show diversity of Pseudomonas aeruginosa colistin-resistance
Authors: Telma de Sousa, Thierry Sayd, Didier Viala, Christophe Chambon, Manuela Caniça, Miguel J N Ramos, Patrícia Poeta, Michel Hébraud, Gilberto Igrejas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPseudomonas aeruginosa is a multidrug-resistant pathogenic bacterium that poses a substantial threat to global public health because of its resistance to antibiotics, and especially to last-resort colistin. The aim of this study is to perform a comparative analysis between the antibiotic-sensitive isolate A and the resistant isolate C (8 µg/mL for isolate A and 128 µg/mL for isolate C), with the intent of elucidating the discrete molecular mechanisms underpinning resistance. This investigation seeks to distinguish between pathways inherently expressed in the absence of antibiotic exposure (acquired resistance) and those activated in response to antibiotic challenge (induced resistance), thereby providing deeper insights into the multifaceted nature of antimicrobial resistance in P. aeruginosa.ResultsProteomic analysis, performed under basal conditions and after exposure to increasing doses of colistin, revealed that, although both isolates have intrinsic resistance to several antimicrobials, the mechanisms underlying colistin resistance diverge significantly. While isolate A showed a stable proteomic response, characterized by the overexpression of proteins related to membrane remodeling and efflux systems, isolate C demonstrated a more dynamic response, evidenced by metabolic adaptations and oxidative stress mitigation mechanisms. These differences suggest that each isolate employs specific strategies to cope with antimicrobial pressure, which has direct implications for the choice of alternative therapies and the development of optimized dosing regimens.ConclusionsThe findings have direct implications for the choice of alternative therapies and the development of optimized dosing regimen. In summary, the results reinforce the complexity of resistance mechanisms in P. aeruginosa and highlight the importance of personalized therapeutic approaches for the management of infections caused by multidrug-resistant isolates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12014-026-09592-6.Keywords: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Antimicrobial resistance, Colistin resistance, Proteomic analysis, Induced resistance, Acquired resistance

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a pathogenic bacterium that opportunistically infects immunocompromised humans and animals leading to sepsis and death. The bacterium poses a serious threat to global health as it exhibits a high propensity to develop resistance to antibiotics [1]. Antibiotic resistance in P. aeruginosa can occur through various mechanisms, including de novo development of resistance following antibiotic exposure, patient-to-patient transfer of resistant bacteria, and cross-resistance between different classes of antibiotics. These combined mechanisms contribute to the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) P. aeruginosa isolates, which are associated with significantly higher mortality rates [2, 3]. In this context, it remains a critical global health threat due to its vast array of virulence factors and its ability to develop resistance to multiple antibiotic classes, requiring contemporary strategies to combat these MDR pathogens [4–6].

Polymyxins, specifically polymyxin B or colistin (polymyxin E), serve as a last-resort treatment option for P. aeruginosa infections induced by MDR and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) isolates due to their effectiveness against these highly resistant isolates. The utilization of polymyxins is typically reserved for situations where other antibiotics have failed, primarily due to their associated toxicity [7–9]. Changes in the composition of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) have been associated with P. aeruginosa resistance, notably the addition of 4-amino-4-deoxy-L-arabinose (L-Ara4N) or phosphoethanolamine (pEtN) to Lipid A. These modifications are regulated by two-component regulatory systems (TCS) such as PhoPQ, PmrAB, ParRS, and CprRS. Alterations in these regulatory networks have been implicated in P. aeruginosa resistance to polymyxin, although the underlying mechanisms have not been fully elucidated [10, 11]. ArnC is involved in adding L-Ara4N groups to LPS, while ArnT catalyzes the transfer of phosphate from phosphatidylglycerol lipid to L-Ara4N bound to LPS [12]. These modifications in LPS alter its electric charge and structure, thereby reducing the affinity of colistin for the bacterial membrane and consequently conferring resistance to the antibiotic [13–15].

The outer membrane of P. aeruginosa has low permeability. Efflux pumps, a common feature of this species, play a crucial role in determining resistance to various antibiotics, for example, Mex-type efflux pumps and AmpC cephalosporinase [16–18]. Molecular analysis has revealed that colistin-resistant isolates overexpress efflux pump genes, while those with intermediate resistance showed lower expression of these genes.

Colistin heteroresistance in P. aeruginosa refers to a phenomenon where bacterial subpopulations within a isolate exhibit varying levels of resistance to colistin. In other words, while the majority of bacterial cells may be susceptible to colistin, some cells within the same isolate may develop resistance to the antibiotic. Colistin heteroresistance is a concerning phenomenon as it can lead to failed treatment of bacterial infections and additional clinical complications [8, 19].

Understanding the proteins associated with colistin resistance in P. aeruginosa is of paramount importance, both in terms of elucidating the underlying resistance mechanisms and the potential for heteroresistance. Any insights gained would be valuable in developing effective therapeutic strategies against infections caused by MDR bacterial isolates. The aim is to compare the more sensitive A isolate with the resistant C isolate, and to decipher the different molecular mechanisms potentially expressed without the addition of antibiotics (acquired resistance) and after the addition of antibiotics (induced resistance).

Results and discussion

Characterization of antimicrobial resistance

The two isolates exhibit intrinsic resistance to certain classes of antibiotics, notably to colistin in the polymyxin category. Plasmid-mediated resistance was not observed in any of the isolates, as previously determined through analysis of the whole genome sequences. It is noteworthy that colistin resistance is attributed to genetic mutations and the presence of specific endogenous proteins in each bacterium [20]. The isolates were classified as MDR (multidrug-resistant) if they displayed non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories, and as XDR (extensively drug-resistant) if they remained susceptible to agents in only two or fewer categories [21].

However, the resistance profiles of isolates to other antibiotics differ, due to associated antibiotic resistance mechanisms, to which the epidemiological contexts from which they were isolated will have an important contribution isolate. Isolate A demonstrates resistance phenotypes to multiple antibiotics, including piperacillin/tazobactam (PIP/TZP), ceftazidime (CAZ), ciprofloxacin (CIP), doripenem (DOR), imipenem (IMP), and others. This broad spectrum of resistance presents potential challenges in treating urinary tract infections caused by this isolate, requiring careful consideration of alternative antibiotics and infection control measures. Of particular interest is isolate 4098 (C), sourced from urinary tract infections, which displays an elevated MIC of 128 µg/mL, also indicating resistance to colistin, but at a higher level. This observation suggests isolate C has an adaptation mechanism or higher positive election contributing to its heightened resistance phenotype. There are several other hypotheses explaining why isolate C is more colistin-resistant than other isolates. One possibility is that this isolate may be expressing resistance proteins more effectively than other isolates. These proteins could have the ability to neutralize the effects of colistin or increase the bacterium’s ability to expel the antibiotic from the cell. Another possibility is that isolate C may have undergone an evolutionary adaptation process in an environment with frequent exposure to colistin. This adaptation may have occurred through genetic changes that confer greater survival capacity in the presence of the antibiotic, making the isolate more resilient compared to others. Over time, this could have led to the development of more sophisticated and broader resistance mechanisms as this heightened resistance is accompanied by a diminished susceptibility to a broad array of antibiotics, including meropenem (MEM). The emergence of such multidrug-resistant isolates limits treatment options so infections become increasingly difficult to manage. While colistin has historically served as a last-resort treatment option for severe infections, its efficacy is increasingly compromised by the rise in resistant isolates. All the studies examining colistin resistance mechanisms have shed light on the multifaceted nature of this phenomenon. Differential resistance profiles observed among P. aeruginosa isolates reflect the diversity of the antibiotic exposure, is part of selective pressures, and bacterial adaptation in distinct clinical contexts. The study conducted by Van Thi Khanh Nguyen et al. evaluated the effectiveness of different colistin dosing regimens for treating severe infections in critically ill patients, using Monte Carlo simulations to model colistin exposure and predict therapeutic success based on MIC [22]. The results showed that high-dose regimens, such as 9 million international units per day in three divided doses, were significantly more effective than the standard dosing regimen of 2.5 million units twice daily, achieving a therapeutic success probability of 70–80% against higher MICs compared to 30–50% with the standard dose. The simulation demonstrated that the efficacy of colistin is highly dependent on the pathogen’s MIC, high-dose regimens are more effective especially when the MIC is in the range of 2 to 4 µg/mL or higher, where the standard dosing regimen often fails to reach the minimum effective concentration required to eradicate the infection [22].

The comparison of the studies reveals that the results of our study are consistent with the findings of Van Thi Khanh Nguyen et al. and Katy Jeannot et al. our study demonstrates significant variability in the colistin MIC among isolates, with isolate 4098 showing notable resistance. Van Thi Khanh Nguyen’s study confirms that high dose colistin regimens are more effective against high MICs, while Katy Jeannot’s study validates the importance of precise methods for detecting colistin resistance, such as the MALDIxin test. Several studies have investigated colistin resistance in a clinical context. One study aimed to evaluate various colistin dosing regimens for treating P. aeruginosa infections in critically ill patients and proposed an appropriate regimen based on microbiological data [22]. A Monte Carlo simulation was conducted using pharmacokinetic parameters of colistin in critically ill patients, pharmacodynamic targets from a mouse thigh infection model, and MIC results from a Vietnamese hospital. An analysis of 121 P. aeruginosa laboratory datasets revealed carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa rates of 29.8% and colistin-resistant P. aeruginosa rates of 0.8%. Based on these findings, the study recommends three regimens for P. aeruginosa infection at MICs of 0.5 µg/mL, 1 µg/mL, and 2 µg/mL, excluding lung infections. In conclusion, increased total daily doses and fractionated colistin dosing regimens may be required for difficult cases, whereas a less intensive dose may be adequate for empirical treatment in environments with low MIC [22]. Another study evaluated the susceptibility to colistin of 131 bacterial isolates from patients with bloodstream infections. Out of the 131 isolates, 100 (76%) were classified as susceptible to colistin, while 31 (24%) were categorized as resistant. The results demonstrated a wide variation in colistin MIC, ranging from 0.25 to 512 µg/mL. However, most susceptible isolates showed MIC concentrations predominantly around 1 µg/mL and 2 µg/mL [23]. Another study investigated antibiotic resistance and the prevalence of colistin resistance across various sample origins, including urine, wounds, lower respiratory tract, upper respiratory tract, and others. Among the 100 patients analyzed, 68% presented MDR isolates, while only 2% presented isolates with resistance to colistin. Notably, colistin resistance was solely observed in isolates from the lower respiratory tract and MIC values were below 4 µg/mL [23].

Following this selection, the normalized abundance of these proteins was analysed by RFLOMICS, a shiny package. To each dose of colistin (0, 2 and 4 µg/ml), a comparison of abundance between the two isolates was performed using a volcano plot. Isolate A’s reliance on stable membrane modifications (e.g., LPS remodeling) aligns with high-risk clones like ST235, which prioritize structural defenses [31, 32]. This rigidity is quantitatively supported by proteomic data: isolate C exhibited 93 upregulated proteins compared to isolate A (Supplementary Data 01), highlighting its dynamic response. Materials and methods

The experimental workflow of this study is summarized in Fig. 8. Briefly, it encompasses the selection of P. aeruginosa isolates A and C based on their distinct colistin MICs (8 µg/mL and 128 µg/mL, respectively), followed by bacterial growth under controlled colistin exposure (0, 2, and 4 µg/mL). The procedure details the subproteome extraction via sonication, protein quantification using the 2-D Quant kit, and subsequent tryptic digestion. The resulting peptide mixtures were analyzed by nano-LC-MS/MS. Finally, the data underwent bioinformatic processing using Proteome Discoverer and Mascot, followed by differential expression and functional enrichment analysis via RFLOMICS and STRING.

Experimental and bioinformatic workflow for the proteomic characterization of colistin resistance in P. aeruginosa

Bacterial isolates and minimum inhibitory concentration assays

The isolates HU121, and 4098 were utilized in this study, designated as isolates A and C, respectively. The choice of these isolates was based on their different origins, as isolate HU121 originated from urinary tract infections and 4098 from urinary tract infections, noting that the latter is from Twain (Chang Gung Memorial Hospital) while the former two are from Portugal. The colistin resistance phenotype was also considered as a criterion.

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of colistin was determined for each isolate using the broth microdilution method in Mueller-Hinton broth (Frilabo, Portugal). The isolates were cultured in Mueller-Hinton broth for 24 h at of 37 °C, and after dilution to a concentration of 5 × 105 cells/mL, were exposed to a range of colistin concentrations (up to 512 µg/mL) in 96-well microtitration plates. After a 24 h incubation period at 37 °C, MIC were determined. The standards established by EUCAST (European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing) were used to interpret, isolates with MIC values exceeding 4 µg/mL were classified as resistant. Each isolate was tested in at least three independent replicates.

Protein extraction from cellular fractionation

Intracellular and cellular debris proteins were isolated from the two isolates, starting from 50 mL of bacterial cultures containing 0 µg/mL, 2 µg/mL, or 4 µg/mL of colistin. Each sample was centrifuged at 7500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the bacterial pellet washed with TE buffer, then resuspended. The wash cycle was repeated, the supernatant carefully removed. The bacterial cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of TE buffer by gentle shaking. Bacterial cells were lysed using a sonicator (single-shot cell disruptor, 1–8 mL, 2.7 kBar max), applying a pressure of 2.5 kBar for 10 s. This procedure was repeated three times, and the resulting lysate quickly transferred to a chilled surface to prevent protein denaturation. The lysate was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant, containing intracellular and membrane proteins, was carefully aspirated and kept on ice. The resulting pellet, enriched in cellular debris proteins, was stored at -20 °C for further analysis. At least three independent replicates of protein extracts were prepared from each isolate cultured with the three colistin concentrations.

Protein quantification

The protein concentration of the two subproteomes from isolates grown at different colistin concentrations was determined using the 2-D Quant kit (GE Healthcare, Buckinghamshire, UK), following the manufacturer’s instructions. This method involves the quantitative precipitation of proteins, leaving other substances in solution. The precipitated proteins are subsequently resuspended in a copper-containing solution, with the unbound copper measured using a colorimetric agent. The color density (absorbance at 480 nm) is thus inversely proportional to the protein concentration of the sample.

Protein concentration and digestion

Protein extracts were then migrated on a 1D-SDS PAGE gel to obtain protein bands as described previously [42] .Briefly, short electrophoresis was performed on 12% SDS polyacrylamide electrophoresis gels to concentrate protein extracts in the first millimetres of the resolution gel. The concentrated protein bands were manually excised with a sterile scalpel blade and transferred to Eppendorf tubes. Then, 100 µL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate/50% ethanol buffer was added to each band and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. This was followed by a dehydration step with 100 µL ethanol for 15 min. To reduce the proteins, 200 µL of 10 mM DTT buffer was added and the sample incubated at 56 °C for 30 min. Following this step, proteins were alkylated with 200 µL of 55 mM iodoacetamide buffer, followed by incubation in the dark for 30 min. The samples were then washed with 200 µL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Coomassie Blue dye was removed in two steps by agitating the sample in 200 µL of 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer with 5% then 50% acetonitrile (ACN) for 15 and 30 min, respectively. Finally, the samples were dehydrated with 100% ACN. Samples were hydrolyzed overnight at 37 °C using 48 µl of trypsin solution (Promega) prepared at a concentration of 12.5 ng/µl in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer. After hydrolysis, peptides were extracted from the gel bands under ultrasonic conditions for 10 min using 38.4 µl of 100% acetonitrile, approximately 80% of the digestion volume. Samples were then transferred to high performance liquid chromatography (LC) vials. If necessary the volume of the sample was adjusted to 15 µl by adding a solution comprising 2% acetonitrile/0.05% TFA.

Nano-liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (nano-LC-MS/MS)

All peptide mixtures were analyzed by nano-LC-MS/MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using an Ultimate 3000 system coupled to a QExactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a nanoelectrospray ion source. A LC-MS/MS Peptide Reference mixture (V7495, Promega) consisting of 6 peptides isotopologues was used to assess the performance of the LC column and the parameters of the MS instrument, including sensitivity and dynamic range. For each sample, a volume of 4 µL of peptide mixture was first pre-concentrated and desalted on a C18 pre-column (Acclaim PepMap 100 C18, 5 μm, 100 A nanoViper − 5 mm length x 300 μm) equilibrated with 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid in water at a flow rate of 30 µL/min. In a second step, after 6 min, the concentration column was switched online with a nanoflow analytical C18 column (Acclaim PepMap 100 - C18-75 μm inner diameter × 25 cm length − 3 μm -100Å) equilibrated with solvent A (99.9% water, 0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 400 nL/min. Peptides were eluted according to their hydrophobicity using a gradient of solvent B (99.9% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) rising from 4% to 25% over 70 min. For MS analysis, the eluates were electrosprayed in positive ion mode at 1.6 kV through a nanoelectrospray ionization source heated to 250 °C. The MS HFX operated in top 18 CID mode (i.e., 1 full scan MS and the 18 most intense peaks in the full scan were selected for MS/MS). The parameters of mass spectrometry analysis were as follow: full enhanced-scan MS spectra performed in FTMS orbitrap with a resolution of 60,000 acquired with scan range 375–1600, AGC target 3e6, maximum inject time of 50 ms and dynamic exclusion of 20s. For MS/MS, resolution was fixed at 15,000, isolation width for ion precursor fixed at 1,4 m/z, AGC target 1e5, single and > 8 charged species were rejected and maximum inject time of 100 ms.

Bioinformatic and statistical analysis

To process raw data of the two subproteomes of each isolate, MS/MS ion search was used to identify and quantify peptides using Proteome Discoverer Software v2.4 and Mascot Daemon v2.8.0 for database searches in a custom database containing distinct entries corresponding to the raw protein sequences and various predicted mature proteins of Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 (GCF_000006765.1). The following search parameters were considered for the searches: a precursor mass tolerance of 10 ppm and a fragment mass tolerance of 0.02 Da, allowing a maximum of two missed cleavage sites of trypsin; carbamidomethylation (C) and oxidation (M) were defined as variable modifications; protein identification was considered valid when at least 2 unique peptides of this protein have a Mascot score giving a FDR < 1%. For this study, only proteins identified with at least two peptides and meeting the quantification criteria (see below) are discussed.

For quantification, the normalized abundance (Quantile Normalization) of each protein, based on the sum of the intensities of the three most intense validating peptides, is then calculated. The quantification of each protein is considered in the study only if the coefficient of variation (CV) of the abundances per group (Isolate-Dose) is lower than 20% in at least 4 out of the 6 groups studied.

The differential analysis was realized, using RFLOMICS (https://forgemia.inra.fr/flomics/rflomics), an R package and Shiny interface. The analysis was performed in single omics (proteomic) using the Proteomic Discoverer results (Normalized abundance). Three simple selected contrast were used: strainC-strainA_in_dosed0, strainC-strainA_in_dosed2 and strainC-strainA_in_dosed4. The data were Log2 transformed and normalized by median. To each contrast, a volcano plot analysis was performed using the following setting: Method = lmfit (Limma), the adjusted pvalue cutoff = 0.05 and the Log2FC cutoff = 1. The volcano plot results were summarized in the Supplementary-Data-01. A protein-protein interaction networks associated with a functional enrichment analysis was realized using the web application String (version:12.0). The minimum required interaction score was 0.7. We have chosen to hide the disconnected nodes in the network. The integrated k-means clustering from string was used to define subgroup (subnetwork) in the principal network created.

Conclusion

Comparative analysis of P. aeruginosa isolates A (MIC 8 µg/mL) and C (MIC 128 µg/mL) revealed that, although both exhibit intrinsic resistance to colistin, each employs distinct molecular strategies under antimicrobial pressure. Isolate A maintains a high basal expression of proteins responsible for outer‑membrane remodeling, such as LPS components, porins, and efflux systems (MexAB–OprM, AmpC), and displays a relatively stable proteomic profile even as colistin concentration increases. In contrast, isolate C mounts a dynamic response: at 2 µg/mL it induces proteins involved in resistance to antimicrobial peptides (ArnB, PA3559, PA4775, ArnA) and in protein‑stress control (ClpB, DnaK, Tig, GrpE), while at 4 µg/mL it further reinforces LPS modifications (ArnT, ArnC) and activates metabolic pathways that support membrane repair and energy supply. These findings suggest that isolate A relies on “preconfigured” defenses, whereas isolate C continually adapts its metabolism to neutralize the antibiotic. Therapeutically, this implies that successful treatment of multidrug‑resistant P. aeruginosa may require flexible dosing regimens and combination therapies: pairing colistin with efflux inhibitors or membrane‑destabilizing agents may be most effective against profiles like isolate A, while profiles like isolate C may demand interventions targeting metabolic and stress‑response pathways to curb adaptive plasticity. Thus, clinical management should consider not only the MIC but also the predominant molecular mechanisms in each isolate, enabling a truly personalized approach. Regarding the scope of this work, the main strength of the current study lies in the comprehensive comparative proteomic analysis of clinical isolates with distinct resistance profiles (acquired vs. induced), providing a detailed view of the dynamic protein changes under different colistin pressures. By using advanced nano-LC-MS/MS and robust bioinformatic pipelines, we were able to pinpoint specific pathways, such as the arnBCADTEF operon and metabolic shifts, that characterize different survival strategies in P. aeruginosa. However, this study also has limitations, including the focus on only two clinical isolates, which may not fully represent the vast genetic and phenotypic diversity of the species. Additionally, while the proteomic data provide strong evidence of resistance mechanisms, further transcriptomic integration and functional gene knockout studies would be beneficial to fully validate the roles of the newly identified candidate proteins.
