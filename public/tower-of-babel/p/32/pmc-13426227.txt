Incidental discovery of a non-secreting bladder paraganglioma: A case report
Authors: Jose Delgado Moreno, Diana Tejera, Gabriella Papineau, Armando De Gracia, Mario Morales Fletcher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Paragangliomas are rare neuroendocrine tumors derived from extra-adrenal autonomic paraganglia. Bladder paragangliomas are exceptionally uncommon, accounting for only 0.06% of bladder tumors, with about 200 reported cases worldwide. Their presentation varies from asymptomatic disease to hematuria, hypertension, palpitations, or micturition-induced syncope, making diagnosis challenging. This case describes a 54-year-old woman with macroscopic hematuria in whom bladder paraganglioma was incidentally discovered during transurethral resection. Histopathology and immunohistochemistry confirmed classic neuroendocrine features without catecholamine secretion. The report highlights the diagnostic difficulty of non-secreting bladder paragangliomas and the need to include them in the differential diagnosis of bladder masses.

Introduction

Paragangliomas are uncommon neuroendocrine neoplasms arising from chromaffin tissue associated with the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems.1 When located outside the adrenal gland, they are classified as extra-adrenal paragangliomas.1,2 Bladder paragangliomas comprise less than 0.1% of all bladder tumors and typically present in individuals between 20 and 50 years, with a slight female predominance.3

The pathogenesis of paragangliomas has been closely linked to mutations in the succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) complex, a critical component of the Krebs cycle and electron transport chain.4 SDH dysfunction results in succinate accumulation and stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF), contributing to tumorigenesis.4 This molecular background is clinically relevant because bladder paragangliomas may vary substantially in both hereditary risk and catecholamine-secreting behavior.

Bladder paragangliomas can be broadly classified according to metanephrine production into functional and nonfunctional tumors. Functional bladder paragangliomas classically present with catecholamine-related “micturition attacks,” including hypertension, palpitations, headache, diaphoresis, or syncope during or after urination, which should raise clinical suspicion. In contrast, nonfunctional tumors often present with nonspecific symptoms such as hematuria, mimicking more common bladder pathologies.5

This overlap in presentation, together with the rarity of bladder paragangliomas, makes preoperative diagnosis challenging and may delay recognition, which is clinically important because unrecognized functional tumors may carry perioperative hemodynamic risk. Herein, we describe an incidentally discovered non-secreting bladder paraganglioma and review key diagnostic and management considerations.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old woman presented with a two-year history of intermittent macroscopic hematuria accompanied by dysuria and suprapubic discomfort. She described episodes of bladder fullness and recurrent urinary retention requiring evaluation in primary care settings. At the presentation, she exhibited active macroscopic hematuria with visible blood clots.

Her medical history included well-controlled hypertension managed with amlodipine and a 21-year history of passive smoking exposure. Family history was notable for a maternal grandmother who died of cancer, although the specific type was unknown. She denied headaches, palpitations, diaphoresis, or syncope during micturition.

Initial laboratory tests confirmed gross hematuria without evidence of urinary tract infection. Ultrasonography followed by contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated a lesion located on the anterior bladder wall with endoluminal growth and measured 4.5 × 3.2 × 4.4 cm (Fig. 1). It appeared as a vascularized, well-defined submucosal bladder wall lesion concerning for neoplasia. Based on these findings, the patient underwent transurethral resection of the bladder tumor (TURBT). The procedure was completed without hemodynamic instability. Cystoscopic evaluation revealed a highly vascular, submucosal mass. Histopathologic analysis of the transurethral resection biopsy of the bladder tumor and deep muscle showed polygonal chief cells arranged in well-defined nests (Zellballen pattern), surrounded by spindle-shaped sustentacular cells and a delicate capillary network (Fig. 2). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated positivity for chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and CD56, with strong S-100 staining in sustentacular cells, while cytokeratin was negative—findings consistent with bladder paraganglioma. Given the high hereditary burden of paragangliomas, genetic testing was considered and planned. Postoperative plasma and 24-h urine metanephrines were within normal limits (52.2 μg/24 h; reference range 0–350 μg/24 h), confirming a non-secreting tumor. Complete resection by TURBT could not be definitively confirmed because the tumor was submucosal/intramural, measured 4.5 cm, and the extent of bladder wall involvement could not be fully determined.

Following multidisciplinary evaluation, partial cystectomy was recommended to achieve definitive local excision. The patient underwent partial cystectomy without intraoperative complications and was discharged 9 days postoperatively, with satisfactory recovery. Final pathology after partial cystectomy with bilateral pelvic lymph node dissection and perivesical fat resection showed no residual tumor and no evidence of malignancy in the lymph nodes, perivesical fat, or urachus.

The patient has remained asymptomatic over 3 years of follow-up. Surveillance has consisted of contrast-enhanced thoracoabdominopelvic CT imaging, including two follow-up CT scans, both showing no evidence of recurrence or malignancy. No new complications have been identified during follow-up.

Discussion

In our case, the bladder paraganglioma was identified incidentally during evaluation of persistent hematuria in a patient without catecholamine-related symptoms. Normal plasma and urinary metanephrines and normetanephrines suggested a non-secreting tumor, which substantially reduced clinical suspicion, as biochemical screening is traditionally relied upon to detect functional paragangliomas.6 However, up to one-third of bladder paragangliomas are non-functional, and normal metanephrine values do not exclude the diagnosis.1,3 This highlights the limitation of biochemical testing, particularly when adrenergic symptoms are minimal or absent.

Although bladder paragangliomas are considered exceptionally rare representing a very small fraction of bladder tumors their clinical presentation is broad and often overlaps with more common urothelial pathology.1,3 Early case series emphasized functional tumors with pronounced adrenergic manifestations, but more recent reports describe an increasing number of biochemically silent lesions presenting solely with hematuria.7,8 Imaging may suggest a vascular or submucosal mass, yet these findings lack specificity and frequently mimic urothelial carcinoma.6,8,9 Consequently, definitive diagnosis relies on histopathology. In our patient, the classic Zellballen pattern (nests or “cell balls” of chief cells surrounded by sustentacular cells, Fig. 2), supported by chromogranin, synaptophysin, CD56, and sustentacular S-100 positivity, confirmed the neuroendocrine nature of the lesion.6,9,10

This diagnostic overlap makes urothelial carcinoma the main differential diagnosis. Histologically, the nested variant of urothelial carcinoma is an important mimic; however, immunohistochemistry helps distinguish it by its cytokeratin positivity, in contrast to the neuroendocrine profile of paraganglioma. Other less common differentials include leiomyoma, hemangioma, and inflammatory lesions.11

Once bladder paraganglioma is recognized, the diagnosis has implications beyond local tumor characterization, particularly for hereditary risk assessment. Bladder paragangliomas have a strong hereditary association, with pathogenic variants reported in more than half of cases, most commonly involving SDHx genes, particularly SDHB. For this reason, current guidelines recommend germline genetic testing in all patients, even in the absence of a clear family history, as hereditary causes may be clinically occult.12

The diagnosis also directly influences management, especially according to tumor functionality.6 Functional paragangliomas warrant preoperative alpha-adrenergic blockade to prevent hypertensive crises during surgical manipulation, whereas this step is unnecessary in non-secreting tumors such as ours.6,9 Surgical resection remains the standard treatment, for both functional and non-functional tumors as histopathology cannot reliably distinguish benign from malignant behavior. Although transurethral resection may be appropriate for selected small, localized, non-functional tumors, partial cystectomy is generally preferred when there is concern for muscular involvement, as these tumors often arise from the bladder wall musculature.9 In our case, the decision to proceed with partial cystectomy was supported by the tumor's bladder wall location and the need to achieve definitive local excision.

Long-term follow-up is essential because malignant potential cannot be determined by histology alone. Malignancy is defined only by the presence of metastases to non-chromaffin tissues. Although bladder paragangliomas have historically been considered mostly benign, a multicenter cohort reported an overall metastatic rate of approximately 30%, including both synchronous and metachronous disease.6 Prognosis after metastasis is variable, with reported 5-year mortality ranging from 18% to 37% depending on genetic background and surveillance strategies.13 Consequently, long-term surveillance is recommended regardless of biochemical activity.6,9,10

Our case adds to the expanding literature on asymptomatic, biochemically silent bladder paragangliomas. By presenting exclusively with hematuria and maintaining entirely normal metanephrine levels, this case reinforces the need for clinicians to consider paraganglioma in the differential diagnosis of highly vascular bladder masses, even when biochemical and imaging findings appear unremarkable.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of considering bladder paraganglioma in patients presenting with hematuria, even in the absence of catecholamine-related symptoms. Non-functional lesions may remain clinically silent until incidentally discovered. Histopathologic and immunohistochemical confirmation is essential for diagnosis, and surgical management with vigilant follow-up is recommended due to the potential for recurrence or metastasis.
