Multilevel modified Poisson regression analysis of barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women in high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries: insights from demographic and health surveys data
Authors: Tesfahun Zemene Tafere, Moges Tadesse Abebe, Amare Molla Zelelew, Gashaw Mekete Adal, Chalie Tadie Tsehay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSub-Saharan Africa has the highest maternal death rate worldwide. Access to quality healthcare for pregnant women can significantly reduce the number of maternal deaths. There is limited evidence on barriers to healthcare access, particularly among pregnant women in countries with a high burden of maternal mortality. Therefore, this study intended to examine the magnitude of barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women in high and very high maternal mortality countries in sub-Saharan Africa.MethodsThis study employed a population-based cross-sectional study design. Data was gathered from the latest Demographic and Health Surveys data of the top 15 high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries. A total weighted sample of 21,079 pregnant women were participated. A multilevel modified poison regression analysis model was fitted. Adjusted Prevalence Ratio (APR) with a 95% confidence interval and a P-value of less than 0.05 was used to declare statistical significance.ResultsThis study revealed that 65.08% (95% CI: 64.43–65.72) of pregnant women in high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries have barriers to healthcare access. In this study, age 15–24 (adjusted prevalence ratio [APR] = 1.12, 95%CI: 1.06–1.18), no education (APR = 1.53, 95%CI: 1.37–1.73), primary education (APR = 1.35, 95%CI: 1.49–1.82), secondary educational (APR = 1.48, 95%CI: 1.36–1.62), poor wealth quantile (APR = 1.76, 95%CI: 1.65–1.88), middle wealth quantile (APR = 1.30, 95%CI: 1.23–1.41), partner’s no education (APR = 1.18, 95%CI: 1.06–1.30), partner’s primary education (APR = 1.16, 95%CI: 1.04–1.34), unmarried (APR = 1.13, 95%CI: 1.08–1.21), no health insurance (APR = 1.59, 95%CI: 1.24–1.94), not autonomous in household decision making (APR = 2.12, 95%CI: 1.32–2.88), unwanted pregnancy (APR = 1.20, 95%CI: 1.06–1.40), rural resident (APR = 1.25, 95%CI: 1.17–1.33), community level of women’s education (APR = 1.31, 95%CI: 1.16–1.51), low poverty status (APR = 1.86, 95%CI:1.20–2.52) were significantly associated with barriers to healthcare access.ConclusionsOur findings concluded that two-thirds of women in high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries have barriers to healthcare access. Women’s barrier to healthcare access was affected by individual and community-level factors. Our findings recommend that empowering women by enhancing their education and reducing poverty can help mitigate women’s barriers to healthcare access. Moreover, healthcare policymakers and stakeholders should improve healthcare infrastructure and the availability of healthcare services in rural areas.Keywords: Healthcare access, Barrier, Poisson regression, Pregnant women, Maternal mortality

Background

Healthcare access is defined as the opportunity to reach and obtain appropriate healthcare services in situations of perceived need for care [1]. It can be broadly characterized by availability, affordability, accessibility, and acceptability, and deficiencies in these areas can negatively impact an individual’s physical and mental health, as well as their overall quality of life [2, 3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) states that healthcare services are a fundamental human right for all citizens, and it is the responsibility of the nation to ensure that these services are acceptable, accessible, and provided in a timely manner [4]. In 2015, world leaders endorsed the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and pledged to achieve the universal health coverage (UHC) target of 3.8, which aims for everyone to have access to quality health services when and where they need them, without any hardship [5]. Although there is an agreement that healthcare access should be universal and equitable for all, pregnant women, especially in low-income countries, still encounter substantial challenges in accessing and utilizing healthcare services [6].

Globally, around 400 million people lack access to healthcare, resulting in eight million deaths from preventable health issues, which again leads to an estimated economic loss of about six trillion USD in low- and middle-income countries [7]. In Africa, only half of the population has access to healthcare services [8, 9]. Pregnant women, in particular, have greater healthcare needs throughout their pregnancy period, requiring significantly more frequent interactions with medical providers than any other population group [10]. Health system factors such as long distances to health facilities, poor transportation networks, weak referral systems, a shortage of skilled health professionals, and inadequate facility readiness continue to hinder access to healthcare among pregnant women in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [11]. Low education, poverty and restricted decision-making autonomy significantly reduce timely care-seeking behavior among pregnant women [12]. Sociocultural norms, gender inequalities, traditional beliefs, and stigma related to adolescent or unmarried pregnancy further hinder access to healthcare among pregnant women in the region [13].

The United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.1 aims to reduce the global maternal mortality ratio (MMR) to below 70 deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030 [14]. Despite considerable reductions in maternal mortality over recent decades, the rate of maternal deaths remains high in low- and middle-income countries [15, 16]. In 2023, SSA is the only region with a high MMR, estimated at 454 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births [17]. Most of these maternal deaths could be prevented through better access to quality medical care at the time of pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period [18].

According to the 2025 joint report by WHO, United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), the World Bank Group, and United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs (UNDESA), eight countries in SSA: Nigeria, Chad, Central African Republic, South Sudan, Liberia, Somalia, Benin, and Guinea-Bissau have very high MMR (500 to 999 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births). Additionally, 13 countries in SSA: Burundi, Côte d’Ivoire, Gambia, Guinea, Kenya, Lesotho, Madagascar, Mali, Mauritania, Niger, Sierra Leone, Togo and Zimbabwe were categorized as having high MMR in SSA (300 to 499 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births) [17].

Evidence shows that in resource-limited African countries, maternal deaths are often linked to barriers to healthcare access during the antenatal period [19, 20]. Optimal healthcare access for pregnant women is vital for achieving and sustaining good health, preventing and managing adverse pregnancy outcomes [21]. In most countries with good healthcare access to pregnant women, the MMR is below 200 per 100,000 live births [15]. Sub-Governments and development partners in SSA have implemented multiple initiatives to reduce barriers to healthcare access for pregnant women through community engagement, financial incentives, digital health innovations, and health system strengthening [22]. However, in SSA, pregnant women often do not access these essential services to the necessary extent [23]. The inability to access timely and adequate healthcare services during pregnancy significantly affects the health and well-being of both women and their babies [24, 25]. It is crucial to identify and tackle factors that hinder access to healthcare services among pregnant women to effectively reduce maternal mortality and morbidity in resource-limited settings [26]. Although some studies have examined barriers to healthcare access among women of reproductive age [6, 27–31], the present study makes a distinct contribution in several important ways. First, unlike previous studies that focused broadly on women of reproductive age, this study concentrated exclusively on pregnant women in countries classified as having high and very high maternal mortality, thereby targeting the population at greatest risk. Second, the study utilized the most recent demographic and health survey (DHS) datasets available. Third, the study analyzed data from multiple countries, enabling cross-national comparison and improving the generalizability of the findings. Accordingly, this study was conducted to examine the magnitude of barriers to healthcare access and its determinants among pregnant women in high and very high maternal mortality SSA countries using updated nationally representative DHS data. The findings of this study will help healthcare policymakers identify barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women and design effective interventions to reduce maternal mortality, thereby supporting SSA’s progress toward achieving SDG target 3.1.

Methods

Study settings and data source

This study used the most recent DHS data (2014–2023) from the 15 SSA countries with high and very high MMR to obtain up-to-date evidence. The countries identified as having high and very high MMR were selected based on the most recent (2025) maternal mortality estimation report by WHO, UNICEF, UNFPA, the World Bank Group, and UNDESA [17]. According to the report, SSA countries; Nigeria, Chad, Central African Republic, South Sudan, Liberia, Somalia, Benin, Guinea-Bissau were identified as having very high MMR with a value of 500 to 999 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births and, Burundi, Côte d’Ivoire, Gambia, Guinea, Kenya, Lesotho, Madagascar, Mali, Mauritania, Niger, Sierra Leone, Togo and Zimbabwe were identified as countries with high MMR with a value of 300 to 499 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births. South Sudan, Somalia and Guinea-Bissau were not included in the study due to the lack of a DHS dataset. Central Africa, Togo and Niger were also excluded because the data were collected before 2014, which is outdated. Finally, a total of 15 SSA countries having high and very high MMR (Nigeria, Chad, Liberia, Benin, Burundi, Côte d’Ivoire, Gambia, Guinea, Kenya, Lesotho, Madagascar, Mali, Mauritania, Sera Lion and Zimbabwe) were included in this study.

DHS adhere to standardized procedures across all countries, including sampling, questionnaire design, data collection, cleaning, coding, and analysis, enabling cross-country comparisons [32].

The DHS is a comprehensive survey designed to gather nationally representative data on fundamental health indicators, including morbidity, mortality, and the use of maternal and child health services. The primary aim of DHS surveys is to offer high-quality data for monitoring and evaluating population health programs and to support evidence-based health policy development. The survey employed a multi-stage (two-stage) stratified sampling approach to select participants. Initially, enumeration areas (EAs) were randomly selected, and in the second stage, households within those areas were randomly selected. The study focused on women who were pregnant at the time of the survey as the primary unit of analysis. The data used in this analysis were weighted to adjust for non-response and variations in the probability of selection. A total weighted sample of 21,079 pregnant women were included in this study. Variables and measurements

Dependent variable

The outcome variable in this study was “barriers to healthcare access”. It is a composite variable constructed from four questions assessing challenges in healthcare access. During the DHS survey, the women were asked whether they were pregnant or not. Only women who responded that they were pregnant were included in our study. Moreover, in this survey, pregnant women were questioned whether each of the following four factors was a problem when seeking medical advice or treatment when they were ill: (a) getting permission for treatment, (b) getting money for advice or treatment, (c) distance to the health facility, and (d) going alone to the health facility. The response options for each question were categorized as “big problem” and “not a big problem. The outcome variable was coded as “yes” if a pregnant woman encountered at least one of the aforementioned barriers to healthcare access. Those pregnant women who did not have any problems were considered not to have barriers to healthcare access and coded as “no” [27, 33].

Explanatory variables

The predictor variables analyzed in this study included factors from both individual and community levels. The variables were chosen based on their strong theoretical grounding and practical importance in influencing maternal healthcare utilization [34]. The individual-level variables examined in this study included age (15–24, 25–34, 35–49), marital status (unmarried, married), educational status (no education, primary, secondary, higher), women’s occupation (not working, working), family size (< 3, 3–5, 5+), wealth index (poor, middle, rich), mass media exposure (no, yes), sex of the household head (male, female), partner’s education (no formal education, primary, secondary, higher), health insurance (no, yes), household decision making autonomy (not autonomous, autonomous), parity (1, 2–4, 5+), current pregnancy wantedness (wanted, unwanted) and ever-terminated pregnancy (no, yes) [35].

The community-level variables considered were type of residence (urban, rural), community-level women’s education (low, high), community-level media exposure (low, high), and community-level poverty (low, high) [35–37]. In the DHS data, all variables, except for residence and region, were collected at the individual level. Therefore, three community-level variables were derived by aggregating individual-level data at the cluster level: community-level media exposure, community-level poverty, and community level of women’s education. These aggregated variables were then categorized as ‘high’ or ‘low’ based on their median values. The median value was utilized as a cut-off point for the categorization because the aggregated variable was not normally distributed [38]. Media exposure was measured by aggregating TV watching, radio listening, and newspaper reading. Thus, women who watched TV or listened to the radio or read a newspaper less than once a week and at least once a week were considered as having media exposure, while those who did not watch TV or listen to the radio or read a newspaper at all were categorized as not having media exposure. Accordingly, the variable was categorized as ‘high’ if the proportion of women exposed to at least one form of media less than once a week and at least once a week was greater than 50%, and ‘low’ otherwise [39]. Moreover, the wealth index was constructed using data on various household assets to evaluate overall household wealth status. In the dataset, the wealth index was presented with five groups: poorest, poorer, middle, richer and richest. For this study, a new variable was created by merging poorest with poorer and richest with richer, resulting in three categories: ‘poor’, ‘middle’ and ‘rich’ categories to simplify our analysis and interpretation of the findings [40]. Community-level poverty was classified as ‘high’ if the proportion of women in the lowest wealth quintiles was above the median value, and as ‘low’ if it was below the median. Similarly, community level of women’s education was considered as high if the proportion of women with at least primary level education was higher than the median cut-off value (50%) and low if the proportion was 50% or less [41].

Data management and analysis

We pooled data from 15 high and very high maternal mortality SSA countries after extracting variables based on the literature. Data analysis was performed using Stata version 17 software. Before analysis, the data were weighted to account for the complex survey design. After appending the datasets, the data were weighted by using the DHS individual sampling weights (v005/1,000,000) to ensure the pooled sample was representative and to produce accurate estimates and standard errors.

A multilevel modified Poisson model regression analysis was performed to identify significant factors at both the individual and community levels. In cross-sectional studies with common outcomes (prevalence > 10%), odds ratios (OR) can overestimate associations compared with prevalence or risk ratios, potentially leading to misleading interpretations. Therefore, prevalence ratios (PR) are the most appropriate measures of association for the present study [42]. Variables with a p-value less than 0.2 in the bivariable analysis were selected for the multivariable analysis. In the final analysis, adjusted prevalence ratio (APR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and a p-value less than 0.05 were deemed statistically significant. Before fitting the final model, we assessed multicollinearity among the independent variables using the variance inflation factor (VIF), which showed no significant collinearity (VIF = 2.10). According to previous studies, VIF values below 10 are considered acceptable [43, 44]. Moreover, Cronbach’s alpha of 0.92 confirmed the internal consistency of the items used in the study. Data were summarized using frequencies, percentages, and mean, and the findings were presented through narratives, tables, and figures.

Model building

This study employed four models: the null model (with no independent variables), model I (incorporating individual-level factors), model II (incorporating community-level factors), and model III (incorporating both individual and community-level factors). The model’s fit was evaluated based on the lowest deviance. We evaluated and compared the fitness of nested models using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), median risk ratio (MRR), proportional change in variance (PCV), and deviance (-2 LLR). In a multilevel modified Poisson regression model, the ICC quantifies the proportion of total variance in the outcome that is due to differences between clusters. ICC was calculated for each model as [38, 45], where σ²u0 = Variance of the random intercept (between-cluster variance), Var (residual) = the within-cluster variance. Moreover, the MRR was calculated for each model to quantify unexplained cluster-level heterogeneity on the risk ratio scale, which is MRR = exp (0.95√VA) [46], where VA = variance of the random effect at the cluster level. PCV was also computed for each model, [47–49], where Vnull = variance in the Null model, Vm1= variance in Model I, Vm2= variance in Model II, Vm3= variance in Model III.

Results

Individual-level and community-level factors

In this study, a total of 21,079 pregnant women were included in the analysis. The majority (45.34%) of the women were in an age range of 25–34 years, and the mean age of the respondents was 28.80 (SD: ±0.026) years. About four in five (81.10%) were married, and 45.27% were with no education. The results further showed that 84.84% of the respondents were with a household head of the male sex. Moreover, 95.00% had no health insurance, and 55.03% were not autonomous in household decision-making. Regarding their obstetrics characteristics, 77.26% of the current pregnancy was wanted, and 83.39% of respondents had ever had a history of a terminated pregnancy. Of the respondents, two-thirds (66.60%) of the study participants were rural dwellers. About 54.56% of the women were from communities with a high proportion of community level of women’s education. About 53.10% were from a low community of media exposure. Moreover, 53.25% of the respondents were in a high poverty status (Table 2).

Random effect (measures of variation)

The ICC for the null model was 17.09%, indicating substantial clustering of barriers to healthcare access within communities. This suggests that 17.09% of the total variation in barriers to healthcare access is attributable to between-community (contextual) differences, while the remaining 82.91% was attributable to individual differences. However, after adjusting for both individual- and community-level predictors in Model III, the ICC decreased to 15.48%. This reduction suggests that the predictors accounted for a significant portion of the between-cluster variability in barriers to healthcare access. In the null model, the MRR for problems of accessing healthcare was 2.06, suggesting that the pregnant women’s barriers to healthcare access differed between clusters. This means that if we randomly selected the pregnant women from different clusters, those in a cluster with higher barriers to healthcare access would be 2.06 times more likely to face barriers to healthcare access compared to pregnant women in a cluster with lower barriers to healthcare access. Model III, which included both individual-and community-level variables, demonstrated an increase in PCV and explained 29.31% of the between-community variance in barriers to healthcare access. Model III is the most precise in explaining the variability of pregnant women’s barriers to healthcare access and was identified as the best-fitting model because it had the lowest deviance (73,219.76) (Table 3).

Magnitude of barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women in high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries

This study revealed that 65.08% (95% CI: 64.43–65.72) of pregnant women in high and very high maternal mortality SSA countries have barriers to healthcare access. The most reported barrier (53.72%) was money. Nearly one-third (36.33%), 22.24%, and 20.55% of respondents reported that distance to a health facility, going alone, and not receiving permission to go to the facility were barriers to healthcare access, respectively. Barriers to healthcare access extensively varied across countries, ranging from the highest, 94.55% in Chad, to the lowest 37.55% in Gambia (Fig. 1).

Determinants of barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women in high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries

In the final model, women’s age, education status, occupation status, wealth index, partners’ education status, health insurance, household decision-making autonomy, current pregnancy wantonness, residency, community level of women’s education and poverty status appeared to be significantly associated with barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women.

In this study, the likelihood of barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women aged 15–24 was (APR = 1.12, 95%CI: 1.06–1.18). Women with no education (APR = 1.53; 95% CI: 1.37–1.73), primary education (APR = 1.35; 95% CI: 1.49–1.82), and secondary education (APR = 1.48; 95% CI: 1.36–1.62) had significantly higher prevalence of the outcome compared with women who had higher education. Moreover, the likelihood of facing barriers to healthcare access among women in the poor and middle wealth quantiles was 1.76 (APR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.65–1.88) and 1.30 (APR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.23–1.41) times higher as compared to women in the rich wealth quantile, respectively. In this study, barriers to healthcare access was 1.18 (APR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.06–1.30) and 1.16 (APR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.04–1.34) times higher for women whose partner had no education and completed primary level education than for women whose partner had a higher education status.

The likelihood of barriers to healthcare access among unmarried women was 1.13 (APR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.08–1.21) higher than that of those who were autonomous in household decision making.

Women who had no health insurance coverage were 1.59 (APR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.24–1.94) more likely to face barriers to healthcare access compared to those women who had health insurance coverage. Pregnant women who had no autonomy for household decision making had a higher prevalence of barriers to healthcare access (APR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.32–2.88). Moreover, women with unwanted pregnancy had 20% higher likelihood of barriers to healthcare access as compared to those women who wanted their pregnancy (APR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.06–1.40). In this study, pregnant women who resided in rural areas were more likely to face barriers to healthcare access as compared to women in urban dwellers 1.25 (APR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.17–1.33). Moreover, the likelihood of barriers to healthcare access among women from low community level of women’s education was higher by 31%, 1.31 (APR = 1.31, 95%CI: 1.16–1.51) as compared to their high community education level counterparts. The likelihood of barriers to healthcare access among women with low poverty status was 1.86 (APR = 1.86, 95%CI: 1.20–2.52) times higher as compared to those women with high poverty status (Table 4).

Discussion

This study sought to examine the magnitude of barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women in high and very high maternal mortality SSA countries. The study revealed that two in three pregnant women have barriers to healthcare access. This implies that the current maternal health programs are insufficiently reaching vulnerable populations, indicating a critical gap in maternal health service delivery in the region. This highlights the need for health system strengthening, including improvements in service availability and affordability. The magnitude of barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women in this study was higher than the study conducted in SSA (57.46%) [50], East African Countries (57.09%) [51], Myanmar (52%) [52], south Africa (43.8%) [53], Egypt (29%) [54], Portugal (21%) [55], Uganda (23%) [56], Bangladesh (66.3%) [57], Tanzania (65.53%) [58], and Rwanda (64%) [59]. This discrepancy might result from variations in healthcare infrastructure, availability of services, and the effectiveness of healthcare policies across the countries. Moreover, differences in socioeconomic conditions, such as income levels, employment status, and overall economic stability between the nations, might contribute to varying experiences in accessing healthcare. However, this finding was slightly consistent with findings from prior DHS studies done in Ethiopia (69.9%) [60] and emerging regions of Ethiopia (71.0%) [61].

The study has identified several individual-level and community-level factors that were significantly associated with barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women. In this study, the likelihood of barriers to healthcare access was found to be decreased as the education status of the study participants increased. Compared to women with higher education, those with no education, primary and secondary education were 1.53, 1.35 and 1.48 times, respectively, more likely to face barriers to healthcare access, which is supported by previous studies in Benin [12], Tanzania [62] and low and middle-income countries [63]. This observed decrease in barriers to healthcare access with increasing education status could be that higher education often leads to better health education, improved knowledge of available health services, and enhanced ability to navigate the healthcare system. Educated individuals are more likely to understand the importance of seeking medical care, recognize available resources, and advocate for their own health needs, thereby reducing barriers to healthcare access.

In this study, we found that pregnant adolescent women had a higher likelihood of barriers to healthcare access as compared to women in other age groups. A possible explanation is that adolescents often face such barriers due to limited autonomy, lower health education, and financial dependency [64]. Furthermore, social stigma, fear of discrimination, and the absence of adolescent-friendly health services may discourage care-seeking behavior, thereby increasing their vulnerability compared to older women [65].

The household economic status was another significant determinant factor. The prevalence of barriers to healthcare access was higher among women from poor and middle-income households compared to those from high-income households. This observation aligns with studies conducted in SSA [50], Egypt [54], Tanzania [58], East African countries [51] and Benin [12] in which women from poor households were more likely to face barriers to healthcare access than the rich. This might be because lower-income households often face financial constraints, which can limit their ability to afford healthcare services. They may also experience greater challenges related to transportation, insurance coverage, and accessing quality care, all of which can contribute to difficulties in obtaining necessary medical care.

In this study, we found that there was a significantly negative association between women’s partner’s education status and barriers to healthcare access. Women whose partners were educated were found to have a lower likelihood of barriers to healthcare access. This finding was in line with the study conducted in Malawi [66] and SSA [67], which reported that women whose husbands were educated were less likely to face barriers to healthcare access compared to those whose husbands had no education. This might be because educated partners have better health education and a greater understanding of the importance of healthcare. This implies that interventions aimed at enhancing health education and education within families could be beneficial. Programs focusing on increasing educational opportunities for partners and promoting joint health education could help address barriers to healthcare access and improve overall health outcomes for pregnant women and their families.

We found an increased likelihood of barriers to healthcare access among women who had no health insurance coverage. In this study, uninsured women were 1.59 times more likely to face barriers to healthcare access compared to insured women. A similar finding was reported in Ghana [68, 69], which showed that uninsured women had a higher odds of barriers to healthcare access. This could be attributed to the lack of financial protection among uninsured women. This implies that expanding health insurance schemes could be an effective strategy to reduce financial barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women.

In this study, there was a negative association between barriers to healthcare access and household decision-making autonomy. The likelihood of barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women who had no autonomy for household decision making was 2.12 times higher than that of their counterparts. This finding aligns with a study conducted in Ethiopia [70]. This might be because autonomous women are more empowered to make timely decisions about their health, including seeking care, which reduces delays and barriers in accessing healthcare services.

Moreover, women who did not want their current pregnancy were 20% higher to face barriers to healthcare access as compared to those women who wanted their pregnancy. This finding was in agreement with the study conducted in Tanzania [71] and the United Kingdom [72]. This might be because unwanted pregnancies may lead to emotional distress, reduced health-seeking behavior, and a lack of support, increasing the likelihood of encountering barriers to healthcare access. This implies that addressing unintended pregnancies through family planning and counseling services could help reduce barriers to healthcare access among affected women.

We further observed that there was residency heterogeneity in barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women in high and very high maternal mortality SSA countries. Pregnant women in rural settings are more likely to have barriers to healthcare access compared to women living in urban areas. This finding is in agreement with results from previous studies conducted in Ethiopia [61], Gambia [33], and Kenya [73]. This implies that targeted interventions through expansion of health facilities, transport, and referral systems are needed to address healthcare access disparities between rural and urban areas.

In this study, it was observed that there was a significantly negative association between community level of women’s education and barriers to healthcare access. The prevalence of barriers to healthcare access among women from low community level of women’s education was higher by 31% as compared to their high community level of women’s education counterparts. A similar finding was observed in a study done in Benin [12]. This implies that improving education level could enhance women’s healthcare access. Addressing education gaps may therefore be a key strategy in reducing health disparities and ensuring equitable healthcare for all pregnant women.

Moreover, the likelihood of barriers to healthcare access among women with low poverty status was higher by 86% as compared to those women with high poverty status. This finding was supported by the study conducted in Gambia and Ethiopia, where women from high poverty status had lower odds of barriers to healthcare access [33, 74]. A plausible explanation is that women living in low-wealth communities often face multiple structural barriers, such as limited health facilities, transportation challenges, and financial constraints, which collectively reduce their ability to access healthcare services. This suggests that improving community-level economic conditions could enhance pregnant women’s access to healthcare, as increasing wealth and economic stability may help reduce barriers faced by pregnant women.

Policy implications

Our study found that a significant proportion of pregnant women have barriers to healthcare access. There should be context-specific, equity-focused, and multisectoral policies to improve women’s healthcare access. Efforts should focus on improving women’s educational status. Moreover, enhancing women’s economic empowerment should be strengthened through social protection frameworks, microfinance initiatives, and livelihood programs targeting economically disadvantaged households. The expansion of community-based health insurance schemes is also critical to reduce financial obstacles to healthcare access. Gender-transformative policies that promote women’s decision-making autonomy, supported by community engagement programs involving men, religious leaders, and traditional authorities, should be scaled up to challenge harmful gender norms and foster shared responsibility for maternal health. Additionally, strengthening rural healthcare infrastructure must remain a top priority through improving physical facilities, ensuring the consistent availability of skilled health workers, medicines, and essential equipment, and expanding outreach services such as mobile clinics and community health workers to effectively reach remote populations.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The main strength of this study is its reliance on a large sample size, with weighted nationally representative data from 15 SSA countries. Additionally, the study utilized a multilevel analysis, which accounts for both individual and community-level factors. The DHS dataset is bolstered by validated instruments, a large sample size, and well-established procedures. Nevertheless, there are some limitations. The cross-sectional nature of the DHS data prevents the establishment of causal relationships. Furthermore, the use of data from different time periods might have impacted the comparability of the results, although the data used were the most recent available for these countries. The analysis was limited by the scope and availability of variables within the DHS datasets.

Conclusion

This study concluded that two in three pregnant women experience at least one barrier to healthcare access. The study identified age 15–24 years, no education, primary education, secondary education, poor wealth quantile, middle wealth quantile, partner’s no education, partner’s primary education, having no health insurance, not autonomous in household decision making, and unwanted pregnancy as individual-level factors associated with barriers to healthcare access. Moreover, rural residency, low community level of women’s education, and low poverty status were identified as community-level factors associated with barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women. Therefore, our findings recommend that empowering women by enhancing their educational and economic status can help mitigate pregnant women’s barriers to healthcare access. Furthermore, healthcare policymakers and stakeholders of the health systems and policy in the studied countries should improve healthcare infrastructure and the availability of healthcare services in rural areas. We also recommend that future researchers interested in this area conduct qualitative studies to further explore barriers to healthcare access among pregnant women.
