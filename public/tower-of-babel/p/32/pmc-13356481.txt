Adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with type 1 diabetes: A nationwide, population‐based study, 1999–2021
Authors: Astrid Melteig Stalheim, Roy Miodini Nilsen, Siri Vangen, Eline Skirnisdottir Vik, Patji Alnæs‐Katjavivi, Marjolein Memelink Iversen, Tore Julsrud Berg, Ragnhild Bjarkøy Strandberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFor the total population and for groups of non‐immigrant and immigrant women, we examined the associations of maternal type 1 diabetes with adverse pregnancy outcomes among women who gave birth in Norway from 1999 to 2021. Further, we examined the associations between the two time periods 1999–2014 and 2015–2021 and adverse pregnancy outcomes, and whether these associations differed in women with or without type 1 diabetes.Material and MethodsIn this nationwide population‐based observational study, with data from the Medical Birth Registry of Norway and Statistics Norway 1999–2021, we used logistic regression to examine the associations between type 1 diabetes and adverse pregnancy outcomes. Moreover, differences in ORs for adverse pregnancy outcomes between those with and without type 1 diabetes were compared for the two time periods 1999–2014 and 2015–2021 using interaction analyses. Analyses were performed for the overall population, as well as separately for non‐immigrant (n = 964 542) and immigrant women (n = 257 409).ResultsWomen with type 1 diabetes (non‐immigrant women n = 5067 and immigrant women n = 619) had higher ORs for almost all adverse pregnancy outcomes (OR 1.41–11.04) compared to women without diabetes in the period 1999–2021. In the time period 2015–2021, compared to 1999–2014, women with type 1 diabetes had a notably reduced OR of congenital malformations compared to those without diabetes (p interaction: 0.006). Immigrant women with type 1 diabetes had a higher OR in 2015–2021 vs. 1999–2014 of having large for gestational age infants (p interaction: 0.003).ConclusionsWomen with type 1 diabetes had higher odds of most of the examined adverse pregnancy outcomes, though improvements were observed from 2015 to 2021 compared to 1999–2014, particularly a reduction in congenital malformations. In contrast, immigrants had higher odds of large for gestational age. These findings highlight the continued need for targeted interventions to reduce the burden of adverse outcomes in all women with type 1 diabetes.

INTRODUCTION

The incidence of type 1 diabetes is increasing worldwide, with regional variations observed, 1 and Norway is among the four countries worldwide with the highest incidence of type 1 diabetes in children. 1 , 2 Consequently, there has also been an increase in pregnancies in women with type 1 diabetes. 3 Previous studies have shown that the offspring of women with type 1 diabetes have a 2 to 5‐fold higher risk of congenital malformations compared to the background populations. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Type 1 diabetes also complicates pregnancies and labor due to higher risk of macrosomia, 7 , 8 preterm birth, 7 , 10 pre‐eclampsia 7 , 10 as well as stillbirth and perinatal mortality. 10 , 11 , 12 In Scotland, the rates of stillbirth and perinatal mortality remained stable between 1998 and 2013 among women with type 1 diabetes, while birthweight increased. 13 Similar results have also been observed in the UK, as rates of large for gestational age (LGA) infant and preterm birth in women with type 1 diabetes increased between 2014 and 2018. 14 To reduce the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, women with type 1 diabetes are advised to plan their pregnancies carefully and ensure optimal glucose management before and during pregnancy. 15 In the last decade, advances in the treatment of diabetes have contributed to improved glycaemic control. 16 , 17 Whether this has resulted in improved pregnancy outcomes remains unclear. 18 , 19 , 20 Accordingly, more knowledge about the prevalences of adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with and without type 1 diabetes through a more recent time period is warranted.

During a ten‐year period from 2008, the proportion of the pregnant women in Norway with an immigrant background increased from 20% to 30%. 21 Two systematic reviews 22 , 23 reported that women with an immigrant background, compared to non‐immigrant women, are generally more susceptible to adverse pregnancy outcomes, though this varies by outcomes. Previous studies examining the association between adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with pre‐existing diabetes and immigrant background have often been limited by small sample sizes, 24 use of older data, or a lack of distinction between type 1 and type 2 diabetes. 25 , 26 There is therefore a need for updated knowledge on adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with type 1 diabetes, in both immigrant and non‐immigrant populations.

The aims of this study were, for the total population and for groups of non‐immigrant and immigrant women, to examine the associations of maternal type 1 diabetes with adverse pregnancy outcomes among women who gave birth in Norway from 1999 to 2021. Further, the aim was to examine the associations between the two time periods 1999–2014 and 2015–2021 and adverse pregnancy outcomes, and whether these associations differed in women with or without type 1 diabetes. This study was designed to examine immigrant and non‐immigrant women in parallel, rather than to directly compare the two groups. This subgroup‐specific approach was chosen to make the estimates more directly applicable to each subgroup and to improve the generalizability of our findings.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design

This was a nationwide population‐based study with observational data from the Medical Birth Registry of Norway (MBRN) and Statistics Norway (1999–2021).

Study sample

The MBRN is a large perinatal database with compulsory reporting on all births in Norway since 1967. 27 As the information in MBRN was not sufficient to classify pregestational diabetes as type 1‐ or type 2 diabetes before 1999, 28 we restricted the study period to 1999–2021. Further, we included only singleton births to ensure a homogeneous sample and reduce potential confounding associated with multiple gestations (n = 1 310 231) (Figure 1). In addition, we excluded all registered births where the birthweight was less than 500 g or if the pregnancy ended before 22 weeks of gestation (n = 9677). We further excluded extreme misclassifications defined as birthweight below −4 standard deviation (SD) or above 4 SD from the mean by gestational age (n = 7402). 29 We also excluded all cases with diabetes diagnoses (type 2 diabetes, MODY, gestational diabetes) other than type 1 diabetes (n = 36 650), births of Norwegian‐born women with foreign‐born parents (n = 12 750), foreign‐born women with at least one Norwegian‐born parent (n = 20 524), and all births with missing data on maternal country of birth (n = 1277). Type 1 diabetes was diagnosed before pregnancy and registered in MBRN. 28 , 30 Information about the mother's background and sociodemographic data were obtained from Statistics Norway and linked to the data from MBRN, using the national identity number for each individual. We used information from Statistics Norway to group the women as immigrant or non‐immigrant. The immigrant group consisted of foreign‐born women with foreign‐born parents, and the non‐immigrant women were born in Norway with at least one Norwegian‐born parent. Among the non‐immigrant women, there were 964 542 singleton births, of which 5067 had type 1 diabetes. Among the immigrant women, there were 257 409 singleton births, of which 619 had type 1 diabetes.

Flow chart of study sample according to immigration from 1999 to 2021.

Outcome variables

The estimated date of birth was based upon ultrasound assessment performed at approximately gestation week 18 (following the National guidelines for antenatal care). In the absence of an ultrasound dating scan, the 1st day of the last menstruation was used for calculating the woman's expected date of birth. Preterm birth was defined as birth prior to 37 completed weeks of gestation. Low birthweight was defined as <2500 g at birth. We defined small for gestational age (SGA) as infants born with birthweight below ‐ 2 SD, while LGA infants had birthweight above +2 SD, using z‐score based on the child's weight, gestational length, and gender. Pre‐eclampsia, including mild and severe, was diagnosed according to criteria from the Norwegian Society of Gynecology and Obstetrics, which was maternal proteinuria combined with a blood pressure of 140/90 mmHg or higher after gestational week 20 on at least 2 occasions. 31 These measures were reported by a checkbox in the MBRN and have been reported to correlate well with medical records. 32 Congenital malformations include all malformations reported to MBRN. Stillbirth was defined as antenatal or intrapartum deaths after 22 weeks of gestation, while perinatal mortality included antenatal deaths, intrapartum deaths, and deaths in the first week of life. 33 Assisted vaginal birth included both forceps and vacuum extraction. Brachial plexus injury was used as a proxy for severe shoulder dystocia. All outcomes were categorized as dichotomous variables (yes/no).

Other variables

Demographic and clinical data used in the description of the study sample and regression analyses were year of childbirth (1999–2002/2003–2006/2007–2010/2011–2014/2015–2018/2019–2021), parity (number of previous births 0/1/2/3/≥4), marital status (married or registered partner/cohabitant/unmarried, single, divorced, separated, widowed, and other), education level (no education or primary school/secondary school/university, college), mother's income (Q1/Q2/Q3/Q4), and smoking (not smoking/smoking).

Maternal age was reported as both a continuous variable (mean, SD) and as a categorical variable (intervals of five years). The body mass index (BMI) was based on the first measured weight and height in pregnancy and was reported as both a continuous variable (mean, SD) and as categorical variables underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight/pre‐obese (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), obese class 1 (30.0–34.9 kg/m2), and obese class 2&3 (≥35 kg/m2). 34

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to quantify sample characteristics. Logistic regression models were used to examine the association between type 1 diabetes and adverse pregnancy outcomes. Because an increasing proportion of births in Norway occur in individuals with an immigrant background and since information regarding pregnancy outcomes in immigrant women with type 1 diabetes is limited, we analyzed immigrant and non‐immigrant groups separately. This subgroup‐specific approach, with no between‐group statistical test being performed, was selected to ensure that estimates would be more directly applicable to each subgroup and enhance the generalizability of our findings. The associations were reported as both crude and adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

The following variables were considered potential confounding factors and adjusted for in the multiple regression models: marital status, maternal education, maternal income, smoking, immigration background, year of childbirth, maternal age, and parity. Information on BMI was not available before 2006, and the variable further contained a large proportion of missing data before 2010 (77.4% missing). Therefore, additional analyses with adjustment for BMI were done in a subset of the data sample for the time period 2010–2021.

Adjusted regression analyses were only conducted where we had sufficient numbers of individuals with both type 1 diabetes and the specific outcome under study. These were preterm birth, pre‐eclampsia, LGA infant, elective caesarean section, and emergency caesarean section. For the other outcomes: perinatal mortality, stillbirth, congenital malformations, brachial plexus injury, SGA, low birthweight, and assisted vaginal birth, we present crude OR estimates to mitigate the risk of overfitting.

Robust standard errors were further used to account for the potential dependency arising when women had more than one pregnancy during the study period. Additionally, a multiple imputation technique was performed to replace missing values in marital status, education, mother's income, smoking, and BMI. This included a multivariate normal imputation model with five imputations. Further, we used Rubin's combination rules, adjusted for the variability between the imputation sets, to obtain pooled ORs with 95% CI across the five imputed datasets.

To investigate whether there has been a reduction in the occurrence of adverse pregnancy outcomes among people with type 1 diabetes in the context of the advances of diabetes treatment in recent years, we compared odds for adverse outcomes in the period between 2015 and 2021, relative to odds for these outcomes in the period between 1999 and 2014. We further compared the estimated ORs among the type 1 diabetes group with the same ORs among the non‐diabetes group using interaction analysis. In these analyses, we included a time period‐by‐type 1 diabetes interaction term in the aforementioned regression models to formally test if the association between time period and the adverse pregnancy outcomes was different between those with and without type 1 diabetes. The p‐value for interaction was evaluated using the Wald test (for crude analyses) and the F‐test (for analyses based on multiply imputed data). The analyses were performed for the total population and stratified by non‐immigrant and immigrant women. As described above, only the outcomes preterm birth, pre‐eclampsia, LGA infant, elective caesarean section, and emergency caesarean section were analyzed in multiple regression analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed in Stata IC version 18.0 (Stata Statistical Software, College Station, Texas, USA).

RESULTS

Women with type 1 diabetes had higher mean pre‐pregnancy BMI compared to women without diabetes (26.0 vs. 24.2), and 51% of women with type 1 diabetes were classified as overweight or obese (Table 1). Comparable findings were observed after stratification for non‐immigrant and immigrant women. Non‐immigrant women with type 1 diabetes did not differ in age at birth from non‐immigrant women without diabetes (mean maternal age at birth: 29.8 vs. 29.7). Among immigrant women, those with type 1 diabetes were slightly older than those without diabetes (mean age at birth: 31.9 vs. 30.1).

Further, the stratified analyses showed that the non‐immigrant women with type 1 diabetes had higher adjusted odds for LGA infant (OR 11.09; 95% CI 10.27, 11.99), preterm birth (OR 6.31; 95% CI 5.86, 6.79), pre‐eclampsia (OR 4.93; 95% CI 4.51, 5.39), elective caesarean section (OR 4.50; 95% CI 4.09, 4.95), and emergency caesarean section (OR 5.26; 95% CI 4.88, 5.67), compared with non‐immigrant women without type 1 diabetes (Table 2). Similar associations were also observed for immigrant women for all outcomes, although without direct statistical comparisons, the strength of the associations was somewhat weaker. Including covariates in the regression model had only little impact on the associations in either group (Table 2). When performing additional adjustment with BMI in the restricted sample from 2010 to 2021, the ORs remained essentially the same for both non‐immigrant women and immigrant women (Table S3).

When examining adverse pregnancy outcomes in 2015–2021 compared to 1999–2014, in the total study population with type 1 diabetes, we found reduced ORs for perinatal mortality, stillbirth, pre‐eclampsia, SGA infant, and low birthweight. Further, there was a notable reduction in congenital malformations for women with type 1 diabetes compared to women without (crude OR 0.64; 95% CI 0.51, 0.80 vs. crude OR 0.87; 95% CI 0.86, 0.89, p interaction 0.006). Only the OR for assisted vaginal birth increased (crude OR 1.38; 95% CI 1.16, 1.63) (Table 3).

The stratified analyses showed similar results for the non‐immigrant women with type 1 diabetes. There was a reduction in OR for adverse pregnancy outcomes in 2015–2021, except for assisted vaginal birth (crude OR 1.37; 95% CI 1.15, 1.63). When comparing the OR for adverse outcomes among non‐immigrant women with type 1 diabetes in 2015–2021 relative to 1999–2014, there was a lower OR for SGA infant (p interaction 0.039) and congenital malformations (p interaction 0.041), and a higher OR for assisted vaginal birth (p interaction 0.047), compared to non‐immigrant women without diabetes (Table 3).

Among the immigrant women with type 1 diabetes, we also found a reduction in most outcomes in the latest period, with only the LGA infant increasing (adjusted OR 1.82; 95% CI 1.05, 3.14). Further, when comparing the OR among the immigrant women with type 1 diabetes, we found a lower OR for congenital malformations (p interaction 0.028) and a higher adjusted OR for LGA infant (p interaction 0.003), compared with immigrant women without diabetes (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

Compared to 1999–2014, women with type 1 diabetes giving birth in the period 2015–2021 had notably reduced odds of congenital malformations compared to those without diabetes. Among immigrant women, the odds of LGA infants were higher in women with type 1 diabetes giving birth in the period 2015–2021. Regardless of diabetes status, the odds for most of the other adverse pregnancy outcomes declined. Throughout the entire study period (1999–2021), women with type 1 diabetes compared to women without diabetes had higher ORs of all adverse pregnancy outcomes except for small for SGA infants. Without direct statistical comparisons, we observed generally weaker associations for the immigrant women.

One of the main strengths of this study is its large sample size from a high‐quality population‐based national registry, 35 spanning a long study period. However, some limitations should be considered. Although the data in the MBRN are registered by healthcare professionals, the possibility of information bias cannot be entirely ruled out. Second, to maintain a sufficient number of cases with both type 1 diabetes and pregnancy outcomes for analyses, we did not stratify the group of women with immigrant background by their country or region of maternal birth. As a result, immigrant women were addressed as a single, heterogeneous group, which may have masked important differences in risk profiles and health outcomes between subgroups. Third, BMI is a highly relevant variable when examining adverse pregnancy outcomes such as LGA infants. 36 However, the lack of sufficient information about BMI before 2010 represents a limitation. Nevertheless, because BMI data were available from 2010 to 2021, we conducted a sensitivity analysis with additional adjustment of BMI, which showed that the ORs remained relatively stable for both the non‐immigrant women and the immigrant women. Despite these limitations, we consider that our population‐based study, including nearly all births in Norway, provides a reasonable representation of adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with type 1 diabetes in Norway. In addition, this comprehensive coverage, combined with the established validity of the diabetes diagnosis in the MBRN, 28 supports the generalizability of our findings to women giving birth in Norway.

We found a reduction in congenital malformations in the time period 2015–2021 compared to 1999–2014 for all births in Norway, but the reduction was more pronounced for women with type 1 diabetes. In recent years, the proportion of fetuses that are diagnosed with serious malformations of the heart is unchanged in Norway, but a larger proportion of pregnancies end in early termination, possibly due to improved follow up in early pregnancy. 37 There has been an improvement in diabetes management and simultaneously in the use of continuous glucose monitoring among individuals with type 1 diabetes. In 2021, the national average reached 80%, up from 67% in 2020, while there was only a small increase in insulin pump use, from 34% to 35%. 16 Whether the reduction in congenital malformation could be due to improved general diabetes management, an increase in the proportions of pregnancy terminations, or improved pre‐pregnancy care,5 is yet to be examined.

The majority of perinatal mortality cases in our study population occurred during pregnancy or labor, defined as stillbirths. Our results showed that the ORs of experiencing stillbirth declined in the period 2015–2021 compared to the period 1999–2014, and for non‐immigrant women, it declined even more in the group of women with type 1 diabetes. In contrast, a Scottish study found that the rates of stillbirth and perinatal mortality appeared to be stable between 1998 and 2013 for women with diabetes. 13 The underlying cause for this improvement in adverse pregnancy outcomes in Norway remains unknown, but it may be attributable to factors such as improved glycaemic control or an increase (9 percentage points from 2013 to 2022) 3 in labor induction occurring at a lower gestational age.

The increased frequency of labor inductions at a lower gestational age is thought to contribute to a reduction in the incidence of LGA infants. In our study, we found a reduction in the OR for LGA infant among women without diabetes in 2015–2021 relative to 1999–2014. However, our findings further revealed an elevated odds for LGA infants among immigrant women with type 1 diabetes. This finding is similar to studies of pregnancies in women with type 1 diabetes in the UK. 14 In Norway, the odds for experiencing LGA in infants of women with type 1 diabetes seem to be dependent on both diabetes and immigrant status. In contrast to our result regarding LGA infants among immigrant women, a recent meta‐analysis indicated that immigrant women have a lower risk of LGA infants compared to non‐immigrant women. 38 These findings highlight the complexity and multifaceted nature of the field studied, with factors such as elevated maternal BMI, suboptimal glucose control, and differences in diet, physical activity, or utilization of prenatal care potentially contributing to the observed outcomes. Moreover, demographic shifts within immigrant populations may also play a role.

While most of the studied outcomes decreased in 2015–2021 compared to 1999–2014, assisted vaginal births for women with and without type 1 diabetes increased. This might also be understood in relation to the increased proportions of labor induction. Furthermore, our results indicate a reduction in the odds ratio for experiencing pre‐eclampsia among non‐immigrant women. In this context, the increased rates of labor induction in Norway may be regarded positively, as they may have interrupted the pregnancy before the onset of pre‐eclampsia, and thus prevented this condition. With no stratification for diabetes, similar results have been shown in a recent study from Norway. 31 The authors suggested that an increase in acetylsalicylic acid prescriptions and labor inductions may partly explain the reduction in pre‐eclampsia prevalence among pregnancies in Norway. Previous research has shown a positive association between elevated BMI and the risk of pre‐eclampsia. 39 In line with this, our study found that women with type 1 diabetes had higher BMI and an increased odds ratio for pre‐eclampsia, compared to women without diabetes. The relationship between the rising prevalence of obesity and the reduction in pre‐eclampsia is, on the other hand, still unclear.

Throughout the total study period (1999–2021), women with type 1 diabetes in Norway had higher ORs for adverse pregnancy outcomes than women without diabetes, indicating high‐risk pregnancies in this population. The result is consistent with findings in other studies. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 In particular, two previous studies among women with type 1 diabetes in Norway (covering the periods 1985–2004 and 1999–2004) found increased odds for stillbirth, perinatal death, preterm births, 10 and congenital malformations. 9 In contrast, our study from a more recent sample period (1999–2021) showed a lower prevalence and OR compared with the studies regarding fatal outcomes like stillbirths and perinatal death. On the other hand, the OR for congenital malformations was higher in our study. We included both minor and major malformations, and some of these differences in the OR for congenital malformation might be explained by the lack of including minor malformations in the previous study. The rationale for this exclusion was not reported.

When stratifying the adverse pregnancy outcomes in non‐immigrant women and immigrant women with type 1 diabetes, we observed notable variations in ORs and prevalences. In our study, several outcomes, including LGA infant, preterm birth, and pre‐eclampsia, were more prevalent in non‐immigrant women. This is consistent with previous research, not primarily focusing on diabetes, showing a higher pre‐eclampsia prevalence in Norwegian women than in immigrant groups. 40 Nevertheless, given the projected increase in migration, 21 it is crucial to ensure the quality of care for immigrants with type 1 diabetes. The combination of language barriers, socioeconomic inequalities, and differing cultural concepts of care may place immigrant women at greater risk of some adverse outcomes. 38 Furthermore, as new diabetes technologies continue to improve the standards of care, and previous research has demonstrated that the use of such technologies varies across ethnic groups, 41 a close follow‐up of immigrant women becomes particularly important. Culturally tailored and standardized approaches are therefore needed to promote equal opportunities to obtain and benefit from diabetes technology. 41 Lastly, it is important to emphasize that the immigrant group in this study was highly heterogeneous. Therefore, further studies are needed to examine pregnancy outcomes among immigrant women with type 1 diabetes in greater detail.

Although our findings demonstrated a reduction in the OR for adverse pregnancy outcomes in 2015–2021 compared to 1999–2014, women with type 1 diabetes had a higher OR for pregnancy complications compared to women without diabetes. This highlights the ongoing importance of providing comprehensive and closely monitored pre‐pregnancy and pregnancy care for this population. Moreover, without direct statistical comparisons between non‐immigrant and immigrant women with type 1 diabetes, we observed some variations: non‐immigrant women with type 1 diabetes were more likely to experience pre‐eclampsia, preterm birth, and LGA infants. This finding underscores the necessity of adopting tailored approaches to antenatal care that address the unique risk profiles, especially focusing on preventing these adverse outcomes.

Future research should examine the relationship between glycemic control, increased use of acetylsalicylic acid, labor induction, and adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with type 1 diabetes. Additionally, considering the observed reduction in congenital malformations, it is necessary for upcoming studies to explore the underlying causes of this trend. Further research is also needed to gain a deeper understanding of immigrant women with type 1 diabetes and their unique challenges and risks, enabling more effective interventions and support.

CONCLUSION

Women with type 1 diabetes face higher odds for adverse pregnancy outcomes compared to women without diabetes, regardless of whether they have an immigrant background or not. In the time period 2015–2021, compared to 1999‐2014, there were improvements in most of the studied pregnancy outcomes, including an important reduction in congenital malformations for women with type 1 diabetes compared to the background population. Whether this reduction was due to an increase in pregnancy terminations, improved pre‐pregnancy care, or improved general diabetes management needs further examination. The current study highlights the importance of providing comprehensive and closely monitored pre‐pregnancy and pregnancy care for this population.
