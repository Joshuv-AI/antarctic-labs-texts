Quality of care and long-term survival after ST-elevation myocardial infarction in adults with cancer
Authors: Mohamed Dafaalla, Jan Walter Dhillon Shanmuganathan, Muhammad Rashid, Harindra C Wijeysundera, Derek J Roberts, Safi U Khan, Ayman Elbadawi, Islam Y Elgendy, Phillip Freeman, Christian Mallen, Mamas A Mamas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile current evidence suggests that the clinical outcomes of ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) are worse among patients with cancer, it is unknown what role the quality of care received during admission plays. We aimed to evaluate the association between care quality and patient survival after discharge.Methods and resultsA nationally-linked cohort of STEMI patients (January 2005–March 2019) were obtained from the UK Myocardial Infarction National Audit Project and UK national Hospital Episode Statistics Admitted Patient Care registries. We used the composite opportunity-based quality indicator to measure overall care quality. Survival outcomes were assessed using Cox proportional hazard models and Kaplan–Meier and cumulative survival curves. In total, 6787 STEMI indexed admissions with cancer were identified. Of those, 4340 (63.9%) patients received optimum care, 1320 (19.5%) intermediate care, and 1127(25.2%) low care quality. Patients with low care quality were older [optimum quality median (IQR) = 72.8 (65.1, 79.6), intermediate quality 75.5 (67.9, 82.1), low quality 78.2 (69.2, 84.7)] and more frequently women (optimum quality 21.6%, intermediate quality 27.3%, low quality 35.5%). Compared to patients with optimum care, patients with low care quality had a higher risk of death at 30 days [hazard ratio (HR) 7.0, 95% confidence interval (CI) 5.7–8.7], 1 year (HR 4.0, 95% CI 3.6–4.4), and 5 years (HR 2.6, 95% CI 2.4–2.8). Relative survival analysis revealed that the number of patients who would survive nationally if they received optimal care is 84 (95% CI 67–102), 508 (95% CI 468–548), and 1096 (95% CI 1034–1158) at 30 days, 1 year, and 5 years, respectively. The association between care quality and survival was more profound in the Northwest and Northeast regions.ConclusionQuality of care is closely associated with short- and long-term survival among STEMI patients with cancer. Improving quality of care may save hundreds to thousands of lives in the shorter and longer term.

Introduction

Cancer and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading causes of mortality and hospitalization in developed countries.1 It is projected that more than 21 million patients with cancer will survive in the next decade as a result of earlier detection and newer cancer treatments.2 Cancer and CVDs share common risk factors such as old age, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and smoking. Moreover, patients with cancer have an increased risk of future cardiovascular events because of chemotherapy, radiation, and immunotherapy-related side-effects and cardiotoxicity. CVD is an important cause of mortality in patients with cancer as well. Indeed, CVD overtakes the primary cancer itself within 3–7 years following diagnosis as the principle cause of death,3,4 with ischaemic heart disease accounting for around 60% of all CVD related deaths.5

There is growing evidence that outcomes of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) are worse in patients with instead of without cancer.5–7 Patents with cancer admitted with an AMI have a higher risk of inpatient bleeding and mortality,8 as well as long-term cardiovascular mortality.6 Despite this, it is not known what role the quality of care received during admission plays. Evidence from observational studies suggest that patients with cancer are less likely to receive percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or guideline-indicated mediations like dual antiplatelet therapy possibly because of concerns related to the patients’ prognosis or the increased risk of complications like bleeding.6,9 Therefore, it is important to determine whether improving the quality of care of AMI patients with cancer is associated with better outcomes in this vulnerable group. While measuring the overall care quality is challenging, the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) developed and validated standardized quality indicators (QIs) based on contemporary guideline-based recommendations for AMI management to allow investigators to assess AMI care quality.10

We used the prospective UK Myocardial Infarction National Audit Project (MINAP) heart attack registry to assess the association between the quality of care and clinical outcomes following admission with an ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) in patients with cancer and determine whether there are regional differences.

Methods

Study design

This is a population-based cohort study in patients admitted with STEMI in England and Wales between January 2005 and March 2019. Data were obtained via linkages between the MINAP Registry, hospital admission data from the Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) registry, and the National Deaths Registry from the Office for National Statistics (ONS).11–13 MINAP is a national AMI registry that routinely collects information on the characteristics and clinical care of patients hospitalized with the diagnosis of AMI in England, Wales, and Northern Ireland, which is used to audit the quality of care and for academic research.11,14,15 The database contains information about admission time and method, patient demographics, comorbidities, clinical characteristics, relevant investigations, inpatient pharmacological and interventional treatments, in-hospital outcomes, and discharge treatments.16–19 The Hospital Episode Statistics Admitted Patient Care Registry (HES APC) is a national registry of all admissions to National Health Service (NHS) hospitals in England.12 The ONS is the largest independent producer of official statistics which collects and publish statistics related to the population, economy, and society at the national and local levels.13 ONS includes all certified deaths in England and Wales recorded in the Civil Registration Deaths Data of the ONS of England and Wales.20 We used the ONS database to obtain information about the date of death as stated on the medical certificate of cause of death. We used a unique identifier for each patient (NHS identifier) to link the databases.

Study population

We identified all STEMI patients with cancer from the MINAP database linked with HES-APC. Cancer is defined as presence of cancer symptoms and signs at time of admission with STEMI. Patients with active cancer at date of admission were identified from the HES-APC database by using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10-CM). Population-based studies from the national British registry showed that HES database is a reliable source of information on the diagnosis of cancer, heart failure (HF), and AMI diagnosis.21,22 The cancer conditions included and the ICD-10 codes used in this study are listed in Supplementary material online, Table 1.

Quality of care assessment

TheESC recommends using the composite quality scores to evaluate hospital care of AMI patients.23 There are two composite indicators: the all-or-nothing composite score and the opportunity-based quality indicator (OBQI). Evidence showed that the OBQI construction, utility, and performance is superior to the all-or-nothing composite score.24 It also offers an important advantage over the all-or-nothing composite score as it accommodates aspects of the care contraindicated or declined by patients. In the recent update of the QIs for AMI, the ESC considers the OBQI as the main composite indicator.23 Therefore, we used the composite OBQI to measure the overall care quality in this study.

The OBQI reflects the number of care opportunities fulfilled in each hospital (numerator) divided by the number of care opportunities to provide care (denominator).24 The score consisted of six evidence-based care processes: the prescription of aspirin, thienopyridine inhibitor, β-blocker, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI/ARB), HMG CoA reductase enzyme inhibitor (statin), and enrolment into a cardiac rehabilitation program at discharge.24 Excluded from both numerator and denominator were care processes that were contraindicated, not applicable, not indicated in, or declined by individual patients. STEMI patients were then divided into three cohorts based on the OBQI: optimum care (OBQI = 100), intermediate care (OBQI 75–99), and low care quality (OBQI < 75). Supplementary material online, Figure 1 shows the histogram of the OBQI values in the study population.

Ethical approval

The study underwent formal ethical approval for the data linkages of MINAP, HES, and ONS registries. Ethical approval was provided by the Health and Care Research Wales and the Health Research Authority (REC reference 20/WA/0312).25

Clinical outcomes

The primary clinical outcome was all-cause mortality at 30 days, 1-year, and 5-years post discharge. The secondary outcome was to identify the geographic variation and predictors of care quality.

Geographic variation

We created ‘heat maps’ according to the proportion of patients receiving optimum care according to 2022 England Integrated Care Boards (ICBs) maps, clustering hospitals together by 2022 ICBS geographical boundaries. An ICB is a statutory NHS organization which is responsible for developing a plan for meeting the health needs of the population, managing the NHS budget, and arranging for the provision of health services in a geographical area which replaced the Clinical Commission Groups system implemented until 2021.26 The Integrated Care Boards (Establishment) Order 2022 legally established 42 ICBs with effect from 1 July 2022 across 7 regions in England: East of England (6 ICBs), London (5 ICBs), Midlands (11 ICBs), Northeast and Yorkshire (4 ICBs), Northwest (3 ICBs), Southeast (6 ICBs), and Southwest (7 ICBs).27

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation if normally distributed or as median and interquartile range (IQR) if they were skewed. Categorical variables were expressed using percentages. The chi-square test and t-test were used to test for statistical significance between categorical and continuous variables, respectively. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for skewed data. We used the multiple imputations with chained equations algorithm to impute missing data (Supplementary material online, Table 3). It was assumed that the missing data were missing at random, and ten imputed data sets were generated and the subsequent analyses were performed on the imputed dataset.28–31 We used the Cox proportional hazard models to assess the association between care quality and the risk of death post discharge. We used competing risk models to assess the association between care quality and the risk of cardiovascular death post discharge. The variables we adjusted for in the models were age, sex, ethnicity, cardiac arrest, cardiogenic shock, left ventricular ejection fraction, history of angina, previous MI, HF, diabetes (DM), hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, peripheral vascular disease, stroke, family history (FH) of CAD, smoking, chronic kidney disease, asthma/chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and previous PCI or coronary aortic bypass graft (CABG). Model estimates were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). As patients who received PCI may tend to receive better care quality, we completed an interaction term analysis of care quality and PCI to confirm that any association between care quality and clinical outcomes is not related to receipt of PCI. As patients with advanced cancer are less likely to receive aggressive therapy, we did a sensitivity analysis by excluding patients with metastatic cancer. We calculated the number of patients who would potentially survive by multiplying the HR of death in patients who received optimum care (low or intermediate care as a reference) time the death rate of patients who didn't receive optimum care. STATA V17 software was used to complete statistical analyses.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

A total of 6787 STEMI indexed admissions with cancer who survived to discharge were identified between 1st January 2005 and 30th March 2019. Based on OBQI, 4340 (63.9%) patients received optimum care, 1320 (19.5%) received intermediate care, and 1127(25.2%) received low care quality. The highest proportion of optimum care was reported in the Coventry and Warwickshire ICB (85.2%); Herefordshire and Worcestershire ICBs (87.8%); Milton Keynes, Bedfordshire and Luton ICB (76.6%), and Southeast London ICB (76.0%) (Figure 1A). The highest proportion of suboptimal care (low/intermediate care quality) were reported in Hertfordshire and West Essex (56.3%); Shropshire and Telford and Wrekin (48.2%); and Suffolk and Northeast Essex (46.8%).

Patients’ characteristics and care quality

Approximately 75% of STEMI patients with cancer were admitted under a cardiology consultant. Patients who received optimum care were more likely to be admitted under a cardiologist (optimum quality 80.8%, intermediate quality 71.0%, and low quality 54.4%) and receive coronary revascularization (optimum quality 68.2%, intermediate quality 51.6%, and low quality 28.6%) (Table 2). Achievement of call-to-balloon time less than 2 h was significantly lower in patients with low care quality (optimum quality 68.3%, intermediate quality 62.1%, and low quality 52.1%). However, the difference in achieving door-to ballon time <60 min was more modest (optimum quality 72.1%, intermediate quality 67.8%, and low quality 67.9%) compared to optimum care quality. The best ICBs in terms of achieving DTBT within 1 h in STEMI patients with cancer were Mid and South Essex ICB; Cheshire and Merseyside ICB; Cambridgeshire and Peterborough ICB; and Northeast and North Cumbria ICB. Inpatient management and care quality

Temporal trends

The proportion of patients with cancer who received low care quality in 2005 was 14.6% which decreased slightly to 12.9% in 2019. In contrast, the proportion of patients with cancer who received optimum care quality improved remarkably between 2005 (57.8%) and 2019 (71.5%). Supplementary material online, Figure 3 shows the temporal trends of care quality in patients with cancer.

Care quality and mortality

Compared to patients with optimum care, patients with low care quality had higher risk of death at 30 days (HR 7.0, 95% CI 5.7–8.7), 1 year (HR 4.0, 95% CI 3.6–4.4), and 5 years (HR 2.6, 95% CI 2.4–2.8). Likewise, patients with intermediate care had higher risk of death at 30 days (HR 2.1, CI 1.6–2.8), 1 year (HR 1.9, 95% CI 1.7–2.1), and 5 years (HR 1.5, 95% CI 1.4–1.6). Patients who received low care quality (OR 1.4, 95% CI 1.2–1.6) and intermediate care quality (OR 1.4, 95% CI 1.2–1.7) had a higher risk of cardiovascular death at 5 years. Figure 2 shows 5-year Kaplan–Meier survival estimates according to care quality. Compared to patients with optimum care, patients with low care quality had higher risk of death even after adjusting for the possible interaction between quality of care and receipt of PCI (Supplementary material online, Table 4). Since aggressive management is less likely to be indicated in patients with advanced cancer, we did a sensitivity analysis focussing on patients without metastatic disease. In cancer patients without metastasis, patients with low care quality had higher risk of death at 30 days (HR 3.7, 95% CI 3.2–4.1), 1 year (HR 3.1, 95% CI 2.7–3.7), and 5 years (HR 2.3, 95% CI 2.1–2.5).

Improving care quality is expected to patient survival particularly in the Northeast and Northwest regions. Care quality and mortality in patients with cancer vs. patients without cancer

Since the impact of quality of care on patients’ survival may not be limited to patients with cancer, we assessed the association of quality of care with patients’ survival in a group of patients who didn't have cancer at the time of admission using data from MINAP registry. A total of 319 461 STEMI patients without cancer were included. Around 81% of patients without cancer received optimum care, 12% received intermediate care, and 7% received low care quality. We observed that the quality of care is an important predictor of mortality in patients without cancer as well. However, after adjusting for patients’ characteristics and comorbidities we found out that the impact of low quality of care is more profound in patients with cancer at 30 days (HR 7.0, 95% CI 5.7–8.7 vs. HR 4.52, 95% CI 4.25–4.80), 1 year (HR 4.0, 95% CI 3.6–4.4 vs. HR 2.87, 95%CI 2.77–2.97), and 5 years (HR 2.6, 95% CI 2.4–2.8 vs. HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.55–1.62). Supplementary material online, Table 5 shows the characteristics of STEMI patients without cancer.

Care quality and cancer type

Patients with lung cancer (optimum quality 51.9%, intermediate quality 24.1%, and low quality 24.0%) and colon cancer (optimum quality 56.1%, intermediate quality 23.3%, and low quality 20.6%) were more likely to receive low care quality whereas patients with prostate cancer (optimum quality 71.8%, intermediate quality 17.7%, and low quality 10.5%) and haematologic malignancies (optimum quality 72.6, intermediate quality 16.9%, and low quality 10.6%) were more likely to receive optimum care (Figure 5). Low care quality was associated with higher risk of death at 5 years in patients with lung cancer (HR 2.3, 95% CI 1.9–2.7), colon cancer (HR 2.1, 95%CI 1.6–2.8), prostate cancer (HR1.9, 95% CI 1.2–3.1), breast cancer (HR 2.6, 95% CI 1.7–4.0), and haematologic malignancies (HR 1.9, 95% CI 1.5–2.5) (Supplementary material online, Figure 4).

Predictors of care quality

Women (OR 1.6, 95% CI 1.4–1.8), black asian and minority ethnic (BAME) ethnicity (OR 1.4, 95% CI 1.1–1.8), previous AMI (OR 1.2, 95% CI 1.1–1.5), DM (OR 1.2, 95% CI 1.0–1.4), stroke (OR 1.4, 95% CI 1.2–1.7), CKD (OR 1.9, 95% CI 1.6–2.4), and obstructive lung disease (OR 1.1, 95% CI 1.1–1.5) were the main predictors of low care quality in patients with cancer (Figure 6).

Discussion

This national cohort study of STEMI care and clinical outcomes in cancer patients has several important findings. Less than two thirds of STEMI patients with cancer received optimal care, and around one in six patients received low care quality with significant regional variation reported. Patients with low care quality were older and more likely to be women or from minority ethnic populations and are less likely to receive coronary revascularization compared to patients with optimum care quality. Compared to patients with optimum care, patients with active cancer who had low care quality had higher risk of death at 30 days, 1 year, and 5 years even after excluding patients with metastatic cancer. Patients with lung cancer and colon cancer were more likely to receive low care quality. We expect that more than 500 lives could be saved at 1 year if care becomes optimum, particularly in the Northwest and Northeast regions of England. The geographic variation in care quality and is more profound in patients with cancer than patients without cancer.

The co-occurrence of cancer and coronary artery disease has increased as the number of cancer survivors continues to increase due to the improvements in cancer detection and management.2,32 There is increasing evidence that AMI in patients with cancer have higher risk of death than patients without cancer.6,7 AMI patients with cancer are less likely to receive guideline-indicated medications or receive invasive coronary revascularization.6,8,9 While previous studies showed that the quality of care of AMI patients with cancer is lower than AMI patients without cancer, little is known about the impact of care quality on long term survival. The composite quality indicators (OBQI) developed and validated by theESC provides a unique opportunity to assess the association between quality of care and long-term clinical outcomes.10

We observed that more than one third of STEMI patients with cancer do not receive optimum care. The lower attainment of the QIs in patients with cancer could be attributed to multiple factors. Many STEMI patients with cancer are not admitted under the direct care of a cardiologist. AMI patients admitted to a cardiac ward were more likely to attain the ESC QIs and had better clinical outcomes33 Moreover, there are multiple challenges that face cardiologists when they treat coronary artery disease in patients with cancer. Most cardiovascular clinical trials excluded patients with cancer along with paucity of data regarding AMI outcomes in patients with cancer.34 In fact, the first ESC guidelines dedicated for AMI patients with cancer were published in 2022 and used data from many cohort studies due to lack of availability of randomized controlled trials.35 Subsequently, cardiologists may have had to adopt a more conservative approach in management of patients with cancer due to lack of evidence-based guidelines. Therefore, establishing a local multidisciplinary cardio-oncology team may improve the decision making process and quality of care received by STEMI patients with cancer.35 Our study also shows that care quality of care improved gradually over the last decade possibly because of increased awareness about challenges of management of AMI with cancer which stimulated clinicians to study the clinical outcomes of this vulnerable group. Further improvement in care quality can be achieved by production of robust evidence from clinical trials that compare the results of invasive therapy compared to conservative treatment and develop cancer-specific risk stratification scores in AMI patient with cancer should be encouraged to help cardiologists make more informed decisions.

A recent study by Teece et al. revealed that AMI patients with prior cancer had lower survival and higher in-hospital mortality, but the impact of improving care quality on mortality in patients with cancer compared to non-cancer patients was modest.36 It also showed that the lower quality of AMI care for patients with cancer was primarily driven by differences in age and comorbidities. In contrast, this study revealed that patients with active cancer who had low care quality had higher risk of death even after excluding patients with metastatic cancer and adjusting for patients’ characteristics and comorbidities. Furthermore, quality of care has a greater impact on outcomes in patients with cancer compared to patients without cancer. The key difference between the studies is that Teece et al. study included both STEMI and non-STEMI patients, and the cancer diagnosis could have occurred within the last 15 years, meaning that cancer was not necessarily active at time of presentation with AMI. Finally, the study of Teece et al. focused on in-hospital survival whereas our study focused on long-term survival after discharge which may contribute to the differences reported.

The reasons behind geographic variation of care quality are numerous and can be complex. These include differences in clinicians’ experience, patients’ characteristics, or the impact of incentives in healthcare financing.37 Furthermore, there are geographic variations in the provision of cardio-oncology services in the UK which is probably a major factor behind the variations in care quality. In UK, treatments and management protocols are determined by the local contracting of specialist services, including ambulances, emergency departments, and acute medical care including primary PCI programmes. For the NHS of England, this is the responsibility of the ICBs who work in partnership with hospitals, providers, and stakeholders to improve patients’ outcomes and experience. Our study showed that the impact of care quality on long term survival persist even after adjusting for patients’ characteristics and comorbidities which suggest that regional and hospital-related factors could be at play. It is worth noting that the impact of care quality on patients’ survival is more evident in some of the regions with the best DTBT, namely the Northeast and Northwest regions, which suggest that hospital-related factors are of paramount importance in these areas. Regional ICBs are encouraged to review their local infrastructure, specialists’ availability and training, hospital protocols, and encourage establishment of cardio-oncology services to improve care quality and clinical outcomes particularly in the Northeast and Northwest regions.

The quality of care received by STEMI patients with cancer varied according to the cancer type. While lung cancer and colon cancer are known to be associated with higher risk of death and bleeding respectively,6,7 they received low care quality more frequently than other cancer types. Low care quality was associated with higher risk of death at 5 years in all cancer types as well. Patients’ characteristics also have an impact on the quality of care received. Women and BAME ethnicity are important predictors of quality of care received. While the sex and ethnic disparities in management and outcomes of AMI are well studied and disparities were attributed to variations in symptoms or clinician bias, the causes of these disparities in AMI patients with cancer are not well studied and further studies are recommended.

The large sample size and availability of long-term mortality data are the main strengths of this study. It is also the first study—to the best of our knowledge—to assess the impact of the quality of care on the short- and long-term mortality of STEMI patients with cancer. However, there are few limitations to be considered when interpreting the results of this study. The study design is observational which limits causality conclusions. The exact staging of the cancer, data on cancer treatment received by patients, details of the culprit lesions, and number of vessels treated for patients who had PCI were not available as they were not routinely collected in the databases.

In conclusion, more than one in three STEMI patients with cancer receive suboptimal care, especially patients with lung or colon cancer. Patients with low care quality had higher risk of death at the short- and long-term compared to patients with optimum care. Further academic research is recommended to produce robust evidence-based guidelines to enable clinicians to make objective decisions and improve quality of care, and to identify method to mitigate variations in quality of care provided.
