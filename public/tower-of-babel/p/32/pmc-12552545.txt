Multimodal mechanoregulation strategies towards tissue regeneration
Authors: Qifan Yu, Yudong Duan, Zhuang Zhu, Wei Ji, Caihong Zhu, Bin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mechanical microenvironment of each tissue plays an important role in regulating its special cellular behaviors, such as morphology, proliferation, differentiation, and migration. Mechanical signals can direct lineage specification or promote cell migration towards injury sites and facilitate tissue repair. During tissue regeneration, mechanoregulation is also important due to the ability of providing an extracellular microenvironment that closely resembles the physiological state for cells. Currently, mechanoregulation strategies have been usually applied to promote tissue regeneration. However, the in vivo mechanical environment is highly complex, these single mechanical conditioning strategies cannot comprehensively replicate the mechanical microenvironment experienced by cells or tissues in the body, thereby hindering the achievement of efficient tissue regeneration. The proposal of multimodal mechanoregulation strategies offers promising avenues to address this limitation. Herein, we summarize the critical role of mechanical factors in promoting tissue regeneration and the current development of different multimodal mechanoregulation approaches. Furthermore, the complex mechanical microenvironment of various tissues such as bone, intervertebral disc and cardiac. Afterwards, the recent successful applications of multimodal mechanical strategies in regenerative therapies were reviewed. And we delineate the persisting challenges, potential resolutions, and emerging translational prospects for multimodal mechanoregulation strategies in regenerative medicine, providing a reference for further development of multimodal mechanoregulation approaches.

Introduction

Cells and tissues in living organisms reside in intricate mechanical environments.1 The unique mechanical microenvironment of each tissue can regulate its special cellular behaviors, including morphology, proliferation, differentiation, and migration.2 Mechanosignaling is a crucial factor enabling cells to sense and respond to changes in their external environment.3 Distinct mechanical signals can direct lineage specification or promote cell migration towards injury sites and facilitate tissue repair.4, 5, 6 During tissue regeneration, mechanoregulation is proven to be equally important. It enables simulation of native tissue mechanical properties, thereby providing cells with an extracellular microenvironment that closely resembles the physiological state. This promotes normal cell growth, differentiation, and functional development.7, 8, 9, 10

At the beginning, the mechanical regulation strategy was generally regarded as referring to the externally applied mechanical stimuli.11 In this regard, researchers initially focused on the roles of tensile strain and compressive stress. As the research progressed, shear stress has gradually become a research hotspot, particularly in the field of cardiovascular regeneration.12 The extracellular matrix (ECM) composed of functional proteins and polysaccharides with a network structure can transfer external mechanical stimuli to cells, causing them to remodel their cytoskeleton and the composition structure of ECM, thereby leading to significant differences in tissue development, physiological activities, disease progression, and tissue reconstruction.13 Therefore, ECM with unique mechanical properties in different tissues, including elastic and viscoelastic characteristics, diverse nano/micrometer-scale topological morphologies. In the last decades, how to fabricate substrates to mimic these unique ECM mechanical characteristics have also begun to attract attention and are regarded as important mechanical regulatory strategies.14 Researchers first focused on the impact of stiffness on cell behavior. In recent years, as researchers have discovered that most biological tissues are not purely elastic but viscoelastic, exhibiting significant stress dissipation phenomena, the influence of viscoelasticity on cells has gradually garnered increasing attention from researchers.15 Substrate surface topography (e.g., roughness, micro/nano-structures, and pore dimensions) have also been explored and found to modulate various cell behaviors and tissue regeneration.16,17

The various mechanical regulation strategies mentioned above can all have significant effects on cell behavior and have been proven to play a crucial regulatory role in tissue regeneration. However, sometimes contradictory results occur, which may be due to the fact that a single mode of regulation cannot accurately reproduce the true mechanical microenvironment of the tissue, leading the one-sided results. Fortunately, in recent years, the proposal of multimodal mechanoregulation strategies could address this limitation. Tailoring combinations of mechanical stimuli to replicate the in vivo mechanical milieu of specific tissues and cells, thereby synergistically orchestrating tissue regeneration, represents an emerging paradigm shift.18, 19, 20, 21, 22 However, a comprehensive review synthesizing this field remains currently lacking. This article first summarizes the critical role of mechanical factors in the regeneration of diverse tissues and the current development of different mechanoregulation approaches. Subsequently, it reviews the complex mechanical microenvironment of various tissues and outlines the recent successful applications of multimodal mechanical strategies in regenerative therapies (specifically for cardiac, osseous, and intervertebral disc regeneration), highlighting the key insights derived from these studies. Finally, we delineate the persisting challenges, potential resolutions, and emerging translational prospects for multimodal mechanoregulation strategies in regenerative medicine (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic illustration of mechanoregulation strategies towards tissue regeneration, including external mechanical stimuli, ECM mechanical properties, and substrate topology, all of which cooperatively regulate and precisely control various cellular behaviors.Fig. 1

The complex mechanical microenvironment of biological tissues

Human tissues are embedded in a highly intricate mechanical environment,23 primarily characterized by three interrelated dimensions: the diversity of mechanical loading, heterogeneity of tissue mechanical properties, and variability in topological structure. These three dimensions are intricately intertwined, and together, they govern the mechanical behavior and functional performance of tissues under both physiological and pathological conditions.24

Diversity of mechanical loading in tissues

Force plays a crucial role in the processes of tissue development, regeneration, and functional realization.25,26 However, because of significant differences in the composition and function of various tissues, the nature, magnitude, and frequency of the mechanical loads experienced by different tissues vary considerably. In terms of their nature, mechanical loads are primarily classified into three categories: compression, tension, and shear.24 Among these, bone tissue, primarily responsible for supporting body weight, is predominantly subjected to compressive stimuli. As a mechanosensitive tissue, moderate compressive stimulation helps maintain the homeostasis between osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis in bone tissue.27 Furthermore, recent studies have also shown that moderate compressive stimulation holds potential for promoting bone tissue regeneration. For instance, Liu et al. found that treating a 5-day compression and a 5-day distraction at a rate of 0.25 mm/12 ​h for 10 days could promote bone regeneration and vascularization.28 However, the mechanical environment of the intervertebral disc (IVD), another important mechanosensitive tissue, is far more complex.29 The central nucleus pulposus tissue is primarily subjected to compressive stress. Subsequently, the compressed nucleus pulposus bulges outward, exerting pressure on the surrounding annulus fibrosus tissue and thereby imposing tensile stress upon it. Additionally, as IVD maintains spinal mobility, rotational movements of the human body cause torsion within the disc, generating torsional forces that stimulate the tissue.30 Considering this complexity, mechanical stimulation is a critical factor in the onset of intervertebral disc disorders. Numerous studies have reported that imbalanced mechanical homeostasis is a significant contributor to the development of IVD degeneration (IVDD).31 In addition to compression and tension, shear forces play a critical role in maintaining tissue homeostasis in the human body. A disturbed flow with a low and reciprocating shear stress in arterial branches and curvatures could upregulate pro-inflammatory molecules in vascular endothelial cells (ECs) and damage endothelial function, leading to atherosclerosis. In contrast, laminar flow with a high and unidirectional shear stress induces EC alignment with the direction of flow and elicits an anti-inflammatory and atheroprotective effect on the vasculature.32

The heterogeneity of tissue mechanical properties

Different tissues serve specific functions within the body to maintain homeostasis and enable physiological processes, which results in distinct ECM compositions, consequently conferring varying mechanical properties such as stiffness and viscoelasticity (e.g., loss tangent, stress relaxation rate).33 For example, the storage modulus (G’) of various tissues, which is usually used to determine the elastic properties ranges from approximately 200 ​Pa in skin tissue to about 109 ​Pa in bone tissue, and the loss tangent (Tan ​δ) which is often used to determine the viscoelastic properties ranges from approximately 0.38 in adipose tissue to about 0.01 in ligament tissue (Fig. 2).34 These distinct mechanical properties enable tissues to fulfill their specific functions: for instance, the high stiffness of bone facilitates load-bearing under complex physiological conditions35; the pronounced viscoelasticity of intervertebral disc tissues allows for stress dissipation and cushioning36; and the viscoelastic properties of blood vessels may prolong their fatigue life.37 Altered mechanical properties play a critical role in the pathogenesis of numerous diseases. The changes in mechanical signals can induce various cellular responses in vivo and regulate multiple signaling cascades, thereby controlling disease development and influencing tissue regeneration (Fig. 3). For instance, reduced bone stiffness not only results from osteoporosis but also reciprocally activates osteoclasts, establishing a vicious cycle.38 During IVDD progression, aberrantly elevated stiffness of nucleus pulposus tissue can induce cellular senescence and inflammation, thereby accelerating degenerative progression.39 Similarly, in cardiovascular diseases, abnormal increases in vascular stiffness may trigger TGF pathway activation, leading to vascular fibrosis.40Fig. 2Different biological tissues show various dynamic properties (G′ and G″ at approximately 1 ​Hz34.Fig. 2Fig. 3Schematic diagram of signaling cascades and their cross-talk involved in cellular responses induced by changes in mechanical properties.Fig. 3

Variations in the topological structure of tissues

The topological structure of tissues refers to the spatial configuration characteristics of fiber networks, pore connectivity, and microscale geometric arrangements within the ECM. This microstructure determines the mechanical properties of tissues and regulates cellular behaviors, such as adhesion, migration, and differentiation through physical signaling, thereby influencing tissue development, homeostasis maintenance, and pathological processes.22,41,42 Different tissues in the human body exhibit distinct microstructural topologies owing to their unique properties and physiological functions.43

Bone tissue possesses a specialized topological structure; the collagen fibers in the bone tissue form periodically spaced arrays, with hydroxyapatite crystals deposited along the fiber axis within the gaps, forming the nanoscale “brick-and-mortar” architecture.44 Moreover, 5–20 concentric lamellar layers radiate around central Haversian canals. Within individual lamellae, collagen fibers align in parallel, whereas adjacent layers exhibit helical staggering (∼30°).45,46 These unique structural features synergistically enhance fracture resistance, enabling bone to withstand compressive stress up to ∼180 ​MPa through crack deflection and energy dissipation mechanisms.47,48 Conversely, cartilage tissue also possesses a distinctive topological structure. As reported, cartilage, especially articular cartilage, comprises superficial, intermediate, and deep layers, each exhibiting distinct collagen fiber and cytoplasmic matrix distributions. In the superficial layer, densely packed collagen fibers cross perpendicularly and align parallel to the cartilage surface. This organization resists shear forces during joint motion while providing a low-friction gliding interface. Conversely, deep-layer collagen fibers orient perpendicularly to the articular surface, conferring natural cartilage with exceptional mechanical strength and efficient energy dissipation. These two distinct structures give rise to marked mechanical disparities between bone and cartilage, enabling them to optimally fulfill their respective physiological functions.49

Intervertebral disc tissue also possesses a unique topological structure. Its annulus fibrosus (AF) features a collagen-rich fibrous lamellar architecture comprising 15–25 layers. Within these layers, highly aligned collagen fibers are oriented at 30° to the horizontal plane. This angle-ply laminate configuration enables the spine to withstand complex multidirectional loading, including compression, torsion, and shear forces through efficient distribution of stresses across alternating fiber orientations. Recent research has revealed that during IVDD, collagen fibers in the annulus fibrosus progressively lose their organized alignment, and the matrix composition also changes.50,51 The structural disorganization and composition change disrupt the mechanical microenvironment, ultimately leading to annular tears. Such ruptures permit herniation of the nucleus pulposus, which compresses nerve roots and causes clinical manifestations including low back pain.52

Mechanoregulation approaches towards tissue regeneration

Over the past few decades, several studies have been conducted to clarify the influence of mechanical signals from different aspects of biological tissues (including external mechanical stimuli, ECM mechanical properties, and ECM topography characteristics) on cell behavior, and to emphasize their importance in the regeneration of various tissues.

External mechanical stimulation

Cells and tissues in vivo are constantly subjected to dynamic mechanical forces arising from muscle contraction, blood flow, breathing, and movement. Simulating these forces (e.g., tensile stress, compression stress, and fluid shear stress) in vitro through external mechanical stimulation is crucial for promoting tissue regeneration.26,53,54

Researchers can apply tensile forces to cells in order to simulate the stretching stimuli experienced by cells or tissues in vivo. For example, Wang et al. utilized a loaded cell culture system to provide adjustable longitudinal cyclic stretching to annulus fibrosus cells (AFCs), mimicking the tensile forces sustained by annulus fibrosus tissue in vivo. They discovered that stretching at 5 ​% strain and 0.5 ​Hz for 24 ​h induced a significant anti-inflammatory effect, characterized by the activation of Yes-associated ​protein (YAP) activity and inactivation of NF-κB signaling in AFCs (Fig. 4a).55 Custom bioreactors can also be employed to apply tensile forces to cells or tissues. For instance, Maghin et al. used custom bioreactors to prove that both ECM remodeling and fibroblast overgrowth were positively influenced by tensile force. Mechanically stimulated constructs also promote tissue maturation, with the formation of new oriented and aligned muscle fibers, thereby accelerating muscle injury repair in congenital diaphragmatic hernia.56Fig. 4a) Excessive stretching induces inflammation in AFCs through inactivation of YAP and activation of NF-κB55. b) DDR1 is required for vascular endothelial cell responses to shear flow.58Fig. 4

Further, researchers have developed a series of strategies for in vitro compression of cells and tissues; among these, the bioreactor is the most used method to apply compressive stimulation. For example, Zhou et al. applied cyclic mechanical loading of cell-scaffold composites using a custom-designed bioreactor system. The cell-scaffold composites in the bioreactor chamber were biofabricated by a linear wave of cyclic mechanical compression at a frequency of 1 ​Hz and a magnitude of 0.06 MPa–0.94 ​MPa for 15 ​min/day over 7, 14, or 21 days to investigate the effect of mechanical loading on the inflammatory response of mesenchymal stem cells seeded on scaffolds.57

Shear force is also a curial mechanical stimulus in the body; Liu et al. used a parallel-plate flow apparatus to simulate pulsatile shear (PS), laminar shear (LS) and oscillatory shear (OS) in vivo and explored whether discoidin domain receptor 1 (DDR1) is required for vascular endothelial cell responses to shear flow (Fig. 4b).58

These studies systematically elucidate the effects of various forms of external mechanical stimulation on cellular and tissue regeneration. However, the mechanical microenvironment within the human body is highly complex. A single form of mechanical stimulation cannot fully replicate the mechanical environmental changes occurring during disease pathogenesis or tissue regeneration. Moreover, both the intensity and frequency of mechanical stimulation exert decisive influences on its biological effects. Excessive or insufficient mechanical stimulation not only serves as a significant causative factor in disease development but also represents a major impediment to tissue regeneration. Future research should integrate multiple mechanical stimuli, explore their appropriate intensities and frequencies, and further investigate the interactions between various forms of mechanical stimulation.

Mechanical properties of substrates

Mechanical properties, one of the most important aspects of ECM, have been found to regulate various cell behaviors and tissue functions.17,59,60 With the development of tissue engineering, researchers have developed several tissue engineering materials with mechanical properties matching those of native tissues to promote tissue regeneration.61, 62, 63

Stiffness is one of the most important properties of substrates. It is defined as the degree of deformation of an object under stress, and has been observed to undergo significant alterations during the development of multiple tissues and the progression of various diseases.63, 64, 65 By modulating the crosslinking density and molecular chain length, researchers have engineered a series of materials and systems with tunable stiffness to investigate the feasibility of precisely directing cellular behavior and enhancing tissue regeneration using tissue engineering materials with biomechanically matched properties.66,67 For example, Chiang et al. controlled the stiffness of polyacrylamide gels from 0.9 ​kPa to 74.6 ​kPa by changing the concentration of acrylamide and N, N′-methylenebisacrylamide to investigate whether the biomechanical properties of skeletal muscle directly impact the myogenic program, providing a potential target for improving muscle regeneration in sarcopenia (Fig. 5a).68Fig. 5a) ECM stiffness regulates myogenic differentiation depending on kinesin-1 activity.68 b) ECM viscoelasticity regulates BMSCs differentiation depending on YAP activity.34Fig. 5

However, ECM in the human body often exhibits a viscoelastic rather than purely elastic behavior, which is characterized by stress relaxation, i.e. a time-dependent decrease in stress under a constant strain.69,70 Based on this, an artificial ECM with tunable viscoelasticity has been developed through dynamic covalent bonding strategies, host-guest interaction strategies and ionic coordination strategies to explore the effects of ECM viscoelasticity and promote tissue regeneration.71 For instance, Liu et al. proposed a strategy in which a small molecule, 3,4-dihydroxybenzaldehyde (DB), was used as a fast “dynamic bridge” to construct viscoelastic gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA)-based hydrogels. By adjusting the viscoelastic properties of hydrogels through altering the number of dynamic bonds, ECM viscoelasticity was found to regulate BMSC spread, proliferation, osteogenesis, and chondrogenesis as well as promote osteochondral regeneration, which was related to YAP activation via stabilization of integrin β1; further, cartilage regeneration could be promoted using hydrogels with viscoelasticity similar to that of native cartilage (Fig. 5b).34

These studies emphasize the regulatory role of substrate mechanical properties in cellular behavior and tissue regeneration. However, current researches remain certain limitations. Firstly, multiple mechanical parameters undergo changes during disease progression. For instance, IVDD progression involves simultaneous elevation of stiffness and reduction of viscoelasticity,72 while tumor development demonstrates concurrent increases in both stiffness and viscoelasticity.73 The determinative influence of whether stiffness alterations or viscoelasticity changes dominate remains unelucidated. Secondly, the mechanical environment required during tissue regeneration is inherently dynamic.59 The materials employed in existing studies lack capacity for real-time modulation of mechanical parameters. Future work necessitates developing dynamically adjustable systems to accommodate the evolving demands of tissue regeneration and simulate dynamic alterations in mechanical environments during disease pathogenesis.

Topology of substrates

The surface topology of substrates, such as roughness, micropatterns, and pore size, plays a critical role in regulating cellular behaviors.74, 75, 76 Current mainstream strategies for modulating substrate topology primarily include photolithography,77 microcontact printing,78 and 3D printing technologies.79, 80, 81, 82 By engineering substrates with specific surface topologies, tissue regeneration can be enhanced via biological mechanisms including improved cell adhesion, activation of relevant mechanotransduction pathways, and modulation of cellular morphology. However, the native ECM is far from being a static repository of signals, as it changes in time and space in response to or as a part of diseases progression and tissue regeneration. Hence, due to the advances in engineering technology, several dynamic culture platforms have been developed to precisely control the spatial and temporal representation of adhesion sites through the response of materials to user-directed stimuli such as light, temperature, or other external fields.83 For instance, various Biocompatible shape memory polymers (SMPs) which could recover shapes upon stimulation of temporary including heat, light, and solvent exposure have been applied to realize the dynamic regulation of substrates topology.83 Moreover, Cimmino et al., developed a confocal laser-based technique on a light-responsive azopolymer displaying micropatterns. When irradiated by a laser beam with an adequate wavelength, the azopolymer undergoes a conformational change that cause a microscopic mass migration, leading to the dynamic regulation of substrates topology.84

Each of these topology-modulating techniques possesses its own advantages and limitations. For instance, photolithography is widely employed to construct substrates with specific topological structures owing to its advantages of high resolution and mature processing technology.77 However, its application is constrained by limitations such as expensive equipment and inability to achieve complex 3D structures. Multimodal mechanoregulation toward tissue regeneration

The mechanical microenvironment of tissues is very complex, usually including external mechanical stimuli, dynamically altered biomechanical properties, and evolving surface topographies. In recent years, to promote tissue regeneration and repair, the focus mechanical signal regulation has been changed from a single factor to a multi-modal manner. Construction of multiple mechanical factor environments has proven to enhance mutual coordination and cross-talk, synergistically promoting tissue regeneration and repair.

Application of multimodal mechanoregulation strategies in bone regeneration

As the primary mechanosensitive tissue in the human body, the skeletal response to mechanical stimuli is highly orchestrated, involving specialized interactions among various bone cells through multiple signaling pathways87,88; therefore, the skeletal system has witnessed the most extensive exploration of multimodal mechano-regulation strategies. Yu et al. prepared biomimetic multi-layer cell-collagen constructs with an angle-ply structural feature by assembling micropatterned collagen membranes, cultured MC3T3-E1 cells on them and applied cyclic stretching at a frequency of 0.5 ​% and elongation of 5 ​%. This multimodal mechanoregulation strategy combined with surface topology and external mechanical stimulation could guide the alignment of cells, promote cell differentiation, and ECM synthesis, as well as promote bone regeneration in mouse calvarial defect models.22

Further, with the recent advances in piezoelectric materials, the importance of electrical signals in bone repair has gained increasing recognition. Thus, increasing researchers have applied multimodal mechanoregulation strategies of combining electrical stimulation with other mechanoregulation strategies.87 For instance, Sun et al. developed a micropillar array of BaTiO3, the topology of which could be combined with electrical stimulation to promote the spread, cytoskeletal reorganization, focal adhesion maturation, and osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) by enhancing the clustering of the mechanosensing integrin α5 in BMSCs, promoting a novel multimodal mechano-regulation strategies for bone regeneration.89

Applications of multimodal mechano-regulation strategies in intervertebral disc regeneration

Intervertebral discs, serving as an important load-bearing tissue, comprises three distinct regions: the nucleus pulposus (NP), AF and cartilaginous endplates (CEPs).90 During the progression of IVDD, alterations in ECM composition and fibrotic tissue formation induce significant changes in the mechanical microenvironment.91,92 Consequently, many studies have designed various multimodal mechano-regulation strategies for IVDD treatment, and have demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy. For instance, Cai et al. developed a kind of viscoelastic-adapted dual-network hydrogel. Combined with cyclic compression this hydrogel could effectively reduce the degenerative microenvironment and promote NP regeneration by inhibiting the IL-17 signaling pathway during the progression of IVDD, thus providing a multimodal mechanoregulation strategy combining ECM mechanical properties regulation and external mechanical stimulation for IVDD treatment.19

Additionally, as an important component of the IVD, AF tissue regeneration is crucial for IVDD treatment. Owing to the complex structure of the annulus fibrosus, which can be divided into the inner, middle, and outer zones, both the tissue architecture and cellular subpopulations exhibit significant differences across these regions.93, 94, 95 Therefore, various multimodal mechanoregulation strategies have been used to modulate the differentiation of annulus fibrosus-derived stem cells, offering a potential therapeutic solution for annulus fibrosus repair in IVDD treatment. For instance, Chu et al. developed a kind of poly(ether carbonate urethane)urea nanofibrous scaffold with tunable stiffness and topography. Through a multimodal mechanoregulation strategy of regulating substrate stiffness and topography the researchers successfully induced the annulus fibrosus-derived stem cells to express outer and inner AF phenotypic marker genes.42 Building on this foundation, researchers have further utilized nanofibrous scaffolds engineered to match the biomechanical properties required for annulus fibrosus tissue repair. By loading these scaffolds with therapeutic agents capable of modulating the microenvironment of damaged AF tissue, multimodal mechanoregulation strategies integrating both physical and biochemical cues were implemented. This approach effectively mitigated inflammation and oxidative stress in IVDD while achieving successful annulus fibrosus regeneration, thereby providing a promising strategy for treating IVDD.96, 97, 98

Applications of multimodal mechanoregulation strategies in cardiac regeneration

Beyond the musculoskeletal system, mechanosignaling plays a central role in maintenance and development of cardiac function, spanning from embryonic morphogenesis to adult functional homeostasis.99 Mechanical stresses such as stretching, shearing, and periodic contractions endured by the heart are used to optimize intercellular connections, promote valve formation, and regulate calcium ion flux and membrane receptor expression. Meanwhile, the collagen and elastin secreted by cardiac fibroblasts directly affect the stiffness and viscoelasticity of cardiac ECM.100, 101, 102, 103 Owing to the crucial role of mechanotransduction in the cardiac system, multimodal mechano-regulation strategies have become increasingly prominent in recent studies related to cardiac regeneration. For example, Han et al. developed a multimodal mechano-regulation strategy with external compression stimulation and electrical stimulation by printing a serpentine polyvinylidene fluoride piezoelectric fiber with deposited magnetic PCL/Fe3O4, which promoted the growth and maturation of cardiac tissue, and provided an approach to generate scaffolds with a tailored topological structure and mechanical properties for cardiac functional recovery.104 Similarly, Zhang et al. developed another multimodal mechanoregulation strategy of regulating scaffold stiffness and replicating the natural corrugated structure of native leaflets, thus providing great potential for tissue-engineered heart valves as well.105

In addition to cardiac tissue itself, multimodal mechanoregulation strategies have also advanced for cardiovascular artificial grafts. Previously, autologous vascular transplantation was the mainstream treatment method in cardiovascular surgery, with obvious limited availability and difficult extraction procedures.106 Fahad et al. designed a bilayer small-diameter tissue-engineered vascular graft with a tensile strength like that of the native artery (4.3 ​± ​1.8 ​MPa) using a cardiac extracellular matrix. With a multimodal mechanoregulation strategy, this graft allowed penetration of smooth muscle cells, improved endothelialization, and maintenance of patency for 10 weeks.107

Various tissues within the human body possess distinct characteristics and exhibit differing sensitivities to mechanical stimuli due to their diverse functions. For instance, in intervertebral disc tissue, its unique surface topography enables the “stiffness ​+ ​topography” multimodal mechanical regulation strategy to achieve significant therapeutic effects. Conversely, cardiovascular tissue primarily experiences shear forces from blood flow during daily activities, making the "stiffness ​+ ​shear force" multimodal mechanical regulation strategy more effective for treatment. In the future, researchers will need to utilize more high-throughput and artificial intelligence technologies to screen for optimal multimodal mechanical regulation combinations to achieve superior tissue regeneration. Clinical translation of multimodal mechanoregulation strategies

As research on tissue repair materials based on multimodal mechanical regulation strategies deepens, many materials have now entered preclinical studies, with piezoelectric materials emerging as a prime example. In 2022, Liu et al. pioneered the development of a biodegradable piezoelectric poly(L-lactic acid) (PLLA) nanofiber scaffold. They discovered that this scaffold can generate controllable piezoelectric charges under normal joint loading, thereby promoting cell migration and recruitment. Additionally, it induces endogenous TGF-β production through calcium signaling pathways, facilitating cartilage formation and regeneration both in vitro and in vivo.26 However, this material also presents certain limitations. The degradation of polylactic acid leads to lactic acid accumulation at the implantation site, creating a localized acidic microenvironment that may compromise therapeutic efficacy and hinder further clinical translation. To address this, researchers have turned to constructing novel piezoelectric materials using diphenylalanine crystals, which offer superior biocompatibility compared to polylactic acid. Diphenylalanine is a naturally occurring amino acid in the human body. Studies have further revealed that diphenylalanine-based piezoelectric materials can effectively achieve antibacterial effects and repair infectious bone defects in large animal models, demonstrating significant potential for clinical translation.

However, current piezoelectric materials still face several limitations that need to be addressed for further clinical translation.108 First, during tissue repair processes, tissues exhibit dynamic demands for electrical signals, yet we still lack materials with dynamically adjustable piezoelectric properties. Second, existing piezoelectric materials generate randomly oriented electric fields; future research should explore whether oriented electric fields could enhance therapeutic efficacy. Finally, although current piezoelectric materials demonstrate promising therapeutic potential in large animal models, data from primate studies and clinical trials remain scarce. Considerable journey lies ahead before clinical application, requiring persistent efforts from everyone.

Summary and prospects

Mechanical factors play a crucial role in both normal physiological activities and disease development. Human tissues are embedded in a highly intricate mechanical microenvironment; this unique mechanical microenvironment maintains the normal physiological functions of various tissues and undergoes significant alterations in the progression of related diseases. Advancing research has increasingly demonstrated that biological processes within cells or tissues are regulated by multimodal mechanical stimuli over both short-term and long-term timescales. This recognition has given rise to the concept of multimodal mechanoregulation strategies. Recently, multimodal mechanoregulation strategies have demonstrated significant efficacy in promoting the regeneration of various tissues, including bone, IVD, and heart. However, challenges requiring further investigation remain. For instance, alterations in mechanical properties during the onset and progression of many diseases remain poorly characterized. Furthermore, significant variations exist in the responses of different tissues and diseases to diverse mechanical stimuli, posing substantial challenges for designing relevant multimodal mechanoregulation strategies. Although rapidly advancing artificial intelligence (AI) can partially address this challenge, data scarcity for training models currently limits its applicability. Future efforts should integrate AI with high-throughput screening technologies to provide reliable data and training models. This integration represents a promising approach to further optimize the methodologies and parameters of multimodal mechanoregulation strategies.109,110 Further, combining such approaches with organoid systems may enhance tissue maturation through multimodal mechanoregulation, potentially offering new therapeutic avenues for related diseases.111,112
