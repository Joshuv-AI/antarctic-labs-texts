Assessing the efficacy of a community-based COVID-19 program with vulnerable and underserved Latino communities
Authors: Flavio F. Marsiglia, Sun-Kyung Lee, Hyunsung Oh, Stephanie Ayers Vollrath, Kate De La Rosa, Ammaar Zindani, Stephen Kulis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Latinos with limited English proficiency (LEP) faced disproportionate barriers to COVID-19 testing and prevention in the United States. The present study evaluates the RAPID model, implemented by community health workers (CHWs) who have established ties with Latinos with LEP, aimed at expanding access to culturally and linguistically congruent COVID-19 testing and other services. Guided by the cross-sector alignment theory of change, we assessed the impact of the RAPID model on behaviors, knowledge, and beliefs related to COVID-19, as well as on perceived challenges and trust in information sources. Using a quasi-experimental design, survey data analyzed 410 Latino adults who received free COVID-19 testing at either RAPID events or traditional sites. Participants were matched using propensity score matching to control for differences in sociodemographic characteristics. Regression analyses revealed that RAPID participants reported significantly higher knowledge of COVID-19 mitigating behaviors and increased trust in official sources compared to matched controls. Interestingly, Spanish-speaking RAPID participants demonstrated significantly lower engagement in COVID-19 risk behaviors and higher knowledge than their Spanish-speaking control counterparts, suggesting a moderating effect of language. Findings support the critical role of CHWs in reducing health disparities, particularly among linguistically underserved populations. The study underscores the value of cross-sector collaboration and CHW integration in public health systems and highlights the need for sustainable funding and support for CHWs to maintain community readiness in future health crises.

Introduction

Latinos in the United States, particularly those with limited English proficiency (LEP), faced disproportionate barriers to COVID-19 testing throughout the pandemic [1–3]. A national study found that Latinos had significantly higher odds of testing positive across eight U.S. states [4], underscoring systemic disparities in access to prevention and care. Structural inequities, such as limited access to healthcare, fewer testing sites in Latino neighborhoods, a lack of culturally and linguistically tailored information, fear related immigration status, and mistrust of institutions created significant barriers for many Latino communities [5–9]. Language barriers further amplified these access disparities, as patients who spoke languages other than English had significantly higher COVID-19 positivity rates, even after adjusting for race, socioeconomic status, and other social factors [10].

Healthcare systems and academic institutions were largely unprepared to meet the urgent need for culturally and linguistically competent services during the pandemic, particularly for Latinos who relied on under-resourced community testing infrastructures, that struggled to keep pace with demand [1, 11, 12]. The intersection of LEP status with socioeconomic disadvantage increased vulnerability, particularly for Spanish-speaking households along the US-Mexico border regions and rural areas [6, 9]. These gaps in access not only delayed diagnosis and isolation but also exacerbated community transmission, contributing to disproportionate COVID-19 morbidity and mortality among Latinos [2]. Therefore, the growing public health emergency required a fast and effective response to prevent further casualties among Latinos.

In this context, Community Health Workers (CHWs) emerged as key paraprofessionals, serving as a liaison between the formal health care system and underserved communities [13]. A systematic review identified 19 CHW programs during the pandemic where CHWs disseminated COVID-19 information, distributed personal protective equipment, and provided psychoeducation to promote testing and vaccination for populations underserved by existing systems [14]. CHWs reached diverse underserved communities by leveraging shared identities and lived experiences that promoted trust and cultural embeddedness from target population [14–16].

For Latino populations, early evidence suggests that CHWs effectively increased testing uptake. For example, CHW-staffed testing sites in Oregon reported nearly four times higher testing rates for Latinos per event [17], and CHW assistance for an at-home testing program showed increased test completion rates among Latinos in rural areas [18]. However, while these efforts showed strong reach, evidence of downstream behavioral or attitudinal changes remains limited. A related study by De Anda et al. [19] evaluated a CHW-led health education intervention using a cluster-randomized trial design. While CHWs decreased psychological distress, the intervention did not significantly improve COVID-19 preventive behaviors or attitudes relative to a control group. These findings underscore the need for further research to better understand how CHWs can most effectively support Latino communities during public health crises.

The RAPID model

To address these urgent access barriers in Arizona, the RAPID (Respectful, Action-oriented, Proactive, Inclusive, and Direct-resources) model was developed. The RAPID intervention centered CHWs as the primary drivers of outreach, including educational information on COVID-19 prevention strategies, assistance with COVID-19 vaccine registration, and saliva-based COVID-19 testing in underserved, predominantly Latino communities [20]. More than 6,000 individuals were served through the initiative.

Unlike traditional healthcare-led testing models, RAPID empowered CHWs to undertake critical roles previously held by laboratory technicians like conducting saliva-based COVID-19 testing, especially in underserved communities with high LEP rates [20]. The CHWs developed long-standing connections with Latinos with LEP while assisting them in enrolling in federally subsidized health insurance programs (often referred to as “Obamacare”). Their experiences in helping Latinos with LEP navigate barriers to care equipped CHWs with essential interpersonal skills and positioned them to encourage this underserved group to access needed COVID-19 testing. In addition to administering tests, CHWs conducted follow-up calls to ensure all participants had accessed and understood their test results, provided guidance on self-isolation, addressed healthcare concerns, and assisted with unmet social needs. During these follow up calls, CHWs also offered referrals to community health centers that provide sliding-scale or free care tailored to insurance and income status.

Theoretical approach and hypotheses

The present study is guided by the cross-sector alignment theory of change, which emphasizes the strategic coordination of health care, public health, and social service sectors to better meet the needs of the communities they serve through sustainable, system-level change [21, 22]. According to this framework, the most effective outcomes occur when the unique expertise of each institutional partner is strategically aligned with the specific needs of the targeted population [23, 24]. This theory calls for the active engagement and horizontal collaboration among diverse stakeholders, including providers, community coalitions, and academic partners, with a focus on overcoming power differentials [21].

The RAPID model exemplifies this cross-sector approach by aligning community-based organizations, academic partners, and CHWs to meet the urgent COVID-19 testing and prevention needs among underserved Latino populations. By embedding trust, cultural congruence, linguistically appropriate services and wraparound support into testing events, the intervention sought to expand access while also shifting knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors around COVID-19. Guided by this framework, the present study tested four hypotheses:

H1 Participants attending RAPID model sites will report greater adherence to recommended COVID-19 prevention behaviors, higher levels of knowledge, and stronger belief in the effectiveness of preventive strategies compared to those in the control group.

H2 Participants attending RAPID model sites will report fewer COVID-19-related challenges in meeting essential needs due to information shared during follow-up calls compared to the control group.

H3 Participants attending RAPID model sites will report greater perceived credibility of official COVID-19 information sources than those in the control group.

H4 The impact of the RAPID model on key outcomes will be greater for Latinos who received linguistically appropriate (Spanish-language) services compared to those who did not.

Methods

Study design

Data for the study came from surveys administered between June 2021 to November 2023 to adults who received free COVID-19 testing offered in central and southern Arizona. This study was funded by two grants under the National Institutes of Health (NIH)-funded Rapid Acceleration of Diagnostics for Underserved Populations (RADx-UP) initiative [20]. RADx-UP was enacted by the U.S. Congress in 2020 to support community-based projects that improved access to COVID-19 testing in underserved and vulnerable communities, as well as to understand the underlying mechanisms that contributed to the disproportionate impact of the pandemic on these communities [25]. This study had two groups who received free COVID-19 testing: (1) The RAPID intervention group and (2) control group.

For the RAPID intervention group, 70 free COVID-19 testing events were offered in 22 zip codes located in vulnerable and underserved communities across central and southern Arizona. Where data were available, zip codes were classified as vulnerable and underserved if there were high COVID-19 case rates but limited testing sites, using a linear regression model that predicted the number of COVID-19 cases per 1,000 residents during the past two weeks with the number of testing sites in 5 miles radius per 1,000 residents. Biweekly case counts were documented from the Arizona Department of Health Services (ADHS) Dashboard [26]. This approach allowed us to identify ZIP codes with more cases than expected given the existing testing resources that residents of each ZIP code could access. Specifically, ZIP codes where the case rate exceeded the upper limit of the 95% confidence interval based on the number of testing sites within a five-mile radius were flagged for deployment of RAPID model testing sites. These ZIP codes were considered to have a disproportionately high number of cases relative to available testing sites. The list of ZIP codes flagged as underserved, along with demographic and socioeconomic attributes of those communities from the 2019 five-year estimates [27], was presented to a Community and Scientific Advisory Board led by a community educator who has advocated for improved access to health equity for the Latino community. The board included six members with expertise in working with historically underserved communities and four members with expertise in medical, health care systems and biological engineering, including COVID-19 and vaccinations. The committee prioritized communities by considering the ZIP code and five-year estimate data along with factors such as long-standing barriers to healthcare access and the broader social, economic, and historical context.

The RAPID events were held in easily accessible community locations, such as schools, parks, churches, and supermarket parking lots. Each event was tailored to meet the specific needs of the community and included drive-thru and/or walk-up testing, in-person registration, bilingual personnel and services (English and Spanish), and door-to-door outreach efforts. At each testing event, CHWs provided culturally and linguistically congruent public health education that included informational pamphlets adapted for Spanish-speaking Latinos, which CHWs used to provide information on how to mitigate infection risks and offer evidence-based responses to participants’ questions and concerns while they waited for on-site testing. In total, 6,311 individuals received free COVID-19 testing at a RAPID event. The free COVID-19 testing events also provided individuals with an opportunity to consent to participate in a larger RAPID study investigating factors influencing differences in COVID-19 behaviors, attitudes, and awareness among communities in Arizona. Although the primary target population was Latino individuals, the testing events were open to all community members regardless of race or ethnicity.

For the control group, free COVID-19 testing was provided by [redacted] in partnership with the state of Arizona. Testing was primarily conducted through drive-thru sites, offered only in English, and required online preregistration and online access to test results. Between late 2020 and June 2024, more than 1.5 million tests were conducted across Arizona. During the online registration process, individuals had the option to agree to be contacted for future research studies; those who opted in formed the basis of the control group.

Following the testing event, participants from both the RAPID intervention and control groups were invited to take part in a survey. Because participants could be feeling ill from COVID-19 at the time of testing, the study team chose to conduct the survey after the event to allow time for recovery. The average time between receiving a test and completing the survey was 3.1 months. Invitations were sent via email or conducted over the phone, depending on each participant’s preference (e.g., for individuals without internet access, the survey was conducted by phone to ensure inclusivity and accessibility). Participants who completed the survey received a $25 incentive as a token of appreciation for their time. The survey was available in both English and Spanish. On average, the survey took approximately 45 min to complete and covered a range of topics related to participants’ COVID-19 behaviors, attitudes, and awareness, as well as social determinants of health. Data were entered and managed through the RedCap data management platform [28]. All study procedures were approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board. The CONSORT diagram (Fig. 1) details the flow of participants through each stage of the study – from testing, participant eligibility, consent, survey completion, and analytic sample.

Participant eligibility

Participants in the RAPID intervention group were eligible to participate in the study if they were 18 years and older, attended a RAPID testing event between November 2020 - April 2023, and consented at the testing event to participate in the larger RADx-UP study. Of the 6,311 participants who received free RAPID-sponsored testing, 2,175 (34% of those tested) consented to participate in the future survey, and 503 (23% of those consented) completed the survey. Control group participants were eligible for the study if they were 18 years or older, received a COVID-19 test between November 2020 – November 2023, were tested in one of the 22 zip codes where a RAPID testing event also took place, and opted in to be contacted for future research studies. Of those eligible in the control group, 229 (23% of those eligible) consented to participate in the survey and 227 (99% of those consented) completed the survey. It should be noted that the RAPID testing events did not coincide with the control group’s testing in any ZIP code.

Measures

All measures used in the analysis were drawn from the PhenX Toolkit [29]. These measures were part of the nationwide RADx-UP initiative.

COVID-19 risk behaviors assessed participants’ adherence (“yes” or “no”) to five recommended behavioral mitigation strategies during the pandemic: (a) Gone out to eat inside a restaurant; (b) Attended a gathering with 10 or more people; (c) Gone to a friend, neighbor, or relatives house or residence; (d) Had visitors such as friends, neighbors or relatives at your house or residence; and (e) Had close contact (within 6 feet) with people who do not live with you [30]. These five questions were summed to create a total number of COVID-19 risk behaviors a participant had engaged in over the past seven days, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 5. The internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.68).

Knowledge of COVID-19 mitigating behaviors measured individual’s knowledge toward how safe (“extremely”, “somewhat”) or unsafe (“somewhat”, “extremely”) the following actions are for avoiding exposure to COVID-19: (a) Visiting relatives or friends; (b) Spending time in public spaces, being in crowds, or being part of large gatherings; and (c) Dining in at restaurants. A mean scale was created ranging from (1) “Extremely safe” to (4) “Extremely unsafe,” with a higher score indicating a greater recognition of behaviors that elevate the risk of COVID-19 infection. The internal consistency of the 3-item scale was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.65).

Perceived mask effectiveness was assessed with a single item: “Face masks protect against COVID-19.” Participants rated their agreement on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from (1) “Strongly disagree” to (6) “Strongly agree,” with higher scores indicating greater belief in the protective efficacy of masks.

Facing COVID-19 challenges measured the extent to which they experienced difficulties meeting six essential needs during the pandemic: (1) Getting the medicine they needed; (2) Getting health care (including mental health care); (3) Having a place to live; (4) Getting enough food to eat; (5) Getting to where they needed to go; and (6) Having clean water to drink. Each item was rated on a 3-point scale: (0) “Not a challenge,” (1) “Minor challenge,” and (2) “Major challenge”. Items were averaged to create a composite score, with higher scores indicating greater overall COVID-19–related hardship. Internal consistency for the scale was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.86).

Trust in COVID-19 sources assessed participants’ trust in various sources of COVID-19 information using nine items that asked, “How much do you trust each of these sources to provide correct information about COVID-19?” Responses were rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from (0) “Not at all” to (3) “A great deal”. Items were categorized into two subscales: Trust in Official Sources, which included the U.S. Coronavirus Task Force, Dr. Anthony Fauci, news media (radio, TV, online, or newspapers), the U.S. government, and participants’ doctor or health care provider (Cronbach’s α = 0.86); and Trust in Unofficial Sources, which included coworkers/classmates or other acquaintances, close friends and family members, faith leaders, and contacts on social media (Cronbach’s α = 0.76). Subscale scores were computed by averaging items within each category, with higher scores indicating greater trust in the respective source type.

Language was assessed by the language in which participants completed the survey, either (0) “English” or (1) “Spanish”. Language was included in analyses as a covariate to examine its association with outcomes, and as a moderator to test potential differential effects across language delivery.

Matching covariates used for propensity score matching were selected to represent key social determinants of health indicators, including age, sex, education, employment status, and health insurance coverage. Age was calculated as the years between date of the survey and date of birth and ranged between 18 and 86. Female was dummy coded as (0) “Male” and (1) “Female”. It should be noted that participants had the option of selecting “non-binary.” However, because of the small number choosing that response (n = 8), none of whom identified as Hispanic, they were not included in the analyses. Education was measured by asking about their highest level of education achieved. Responses were treated as an ordinal variable, ranging from (0) “Never attended school” to (7) “Graduate degree,” with higher scores indicating higher educational attainment. Employment status was dummy coded as (1) Currently employed if they responded, “working now,” and (0) Not employed if they selected any other option, including being “temporarily laid off,” “on sick leave,” “looking for work,” “retired,” “disabled” (temporarily or permanently), “keeping house,” or a “student”. This reflects current labor force participation (57%) and was used to keep statistical power given small sizes in several non-employed categories (ranging from < 1% to 7%), except for “keeping house” representing the largest non-employed category (16%). Health insurance was dummy coded into two binary variables: public insurance (1 = “yes”, 0 = “no”), and private insurance (1 = “yes”, 0 = “no”), with no insurance serving as the reference group. Participants were asked to select their current primary type of health insurance or health care plan, ensuring that insurance categories were mutually exclusive. In addition, Region of data collection was dummy coded to account for the location of free COVID-19 testing events: (1) Central Arizona, and (0) Southern Arizona. Year of assessment (2021, 2022, or 2023) was extracted from the survey completion date.

Data analysis

Given that participants were not randomly assigned to the intervention, propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to reduce selection bias and improve covariate balance between groups. Because the participants in the study were overwhelmingly Latino/Hispanic (86.9% of RAPID and 83.8% of Control), the PSM was restricted to the Latino/Hispanic sample. PSM was used to create comparable groups based on observed covariates and to estimate the effects of RAPID intervention group participation more accurately. PSM was conducted in R (version 4.4.1) using the MatchIt package [31]. The matching model included the following covariates: region, year of assessment, age, sex, education, employment status, and health insurance coverage (public, private, versus none). Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression, and nearest neighbor matching was applied with a 2:1 ratio and a caliper of 0.2. Covariate balance was assessed by comparing standardized mean differences before and after matching, confirming acceptable balance across covariates in the matched sample. Region and year of assessment were included to improve covariate balance, but not in the post-matching outcome analyses.

Using the matched PSM sample, we conducted mean difference tests and regression analyses using maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors (MLR) in Mplus Version 8.11. The core social determinant covariates (age, sex, education, employment, and insurance status) were retained in all outcome models to enhance analytic rigor and control for residual confounding. Six primary outcomes were assessed: (1) COVID-19 Risk Behaviors, (2) Knowledge of COVID-19 Mitigating Behaviors, (3) Perceived Mask Effectiveness, (4) Facing COVID-19 Challenges, (5) Trust in Official Sources, and (6) Trust in Unofficial Sources. Lastly, to examine potential differences in program effects by linguistic context, language was included as a moderator in regression models, given that language-specific service delivery was a central feature of the RAPID intervention delivered by CHWs.

Results

Analytic sample

The final analytic sample included those eligible participants who self-identified as Hispanic/Latino and were matched using PSM. The matched sample (N = 410) included 254 participants from the RAPID intervention and 156 participants in the control group (see Table 1 for demographic information). Overall, 67.3% of the participants were female, 72.7% completed the survey in Spanish, and 58.0% were currently working. Regarding health insurance, 19.3% were uninsured, 30.7% had public insurance, and 32.2% had private insurance. Education levels varied: 14.3% had less than a high school diploma, 32.0% had a high school diploma or GED, 18.3% had some college or a technical/vocational degree, 20.2% had a bachelor’s degree, and 6.6% had a graduate degree. The average age of participants was 40.8 years old (SD = 13.6). Post-matching comparisons using t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables revealed no significant differences in demographic characteristics between the RAPID and control groups, indicating that the matching procedure was achieving balance across observed covariates.

Mean differences

Comparisons of means between matched groups (Table 2) showed that participants in the RAPID intervention group reported significantly greater knowledge of COVID-19 mitigating behaviors (M = 2.71, SD = 0.58) than the control group (M = 2.30, SD = 0.60), t = 6.80, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.69, indicating a moderate-to-large effect. Compared to the control group, the RAPID group also reported significantly stronger belief in mask protection (M = 4.91 vs. 4.44), t = 2.33, p = .020, d = 0.34, and faced more COVID-related challenges (M = 0.31 vs. 0.21), t = 2.29, p = .020, d = 0.23, both reflecting small-to-moderate effects. RAPID participants also showed higher trust in unofficial sources (M = 1.58, SD = 0.71) than controls (M = 1.17, SD = 0.80), though the difference was marginally significant, t = 1.81, p = .070, with a moderately large effect size (d = 0.53). Mean differences in trust in official sources were not statistically significant (p = .116), although the effect size was also moderately large (d = 0.52). It is important to note that the trust variables had more missing data compared to other outcomes due to their introduction at a later point in the study as public health messaging evolved during the pandemic. No significant group differences emerged in COVID-19 risk behaviors (M = 3.33 vs. 3.57), t = − 1.54, p = .122.

Regression analyses

Regression models using MLR are presented in Table 3. All models included age, language, sex, education, employment status, and health insurance coverage (public and private) as covariates. RAPID participants reported significantly greater trust in official COVID-19 sources (B = 0.42, SE = 0.13, p = .002) and more knowledge of COVID-19 mitigating behaviors (B = 0.40, SE = 0.06, p < .001) than the control group. They also showed stronger belief in mask protection (B = 0.32, SE = 0.17), and greater COVID-related challenges (B = 0.07, SE = 0.04), with both effects approaching significance (p = .058 and 0.064, respectively). Trust in unofficial sources was marginally higher among RAPID intervention participants (B = 0.23, SE = 0.14, p = .090) compared to the control group. No significant differences between groups were found for COVID-19 risk behaviors.

Moderation by language

Moderation analyses by language (Table 4) tested whether the effects of RAPID participation varied by the language in which participants completed the survey. Two significant interaction effects emerged. Among participants who completed the Spanish survey in Spanish, RAPID participation was associated with fewer COVID-19 risk behaviors (B = − 0.68, SE = 0.32, p = .036) and greater knowledge of mitigating behaviors (B = 0.32, SE = 0.15, p = .038) compared to Spanish-speaking controls. No significant language × intervention condition interactions were found for belief in mask protection, COVID-related challenges, or sources of trust (official or unofficial).

Discussion

The RAPID intervention was associated with improved outcomes in several domains related to COVID-19 prevention. Participants in the RAPID intervention reported higher levels of accurate knowledge about preventive behaviors and greater perceived efficacy of mask-wearing compared to the control group. Once models controlled for key variables, H1, which predicted greater adherence to public health recommendations among RAPID participants, was only partially supported. Although participation in the RAPID intervention significantly improved knowledge of COVID-19 mitigating strategies, it did not lead to significantly stronger beliefs in mask efficacy. One possible interpretation of this finding can be found in the RAPID participants reporting higher COVID-19–related challenges in meeting essential needs. Although they possessed accurate prevention knowledge and attitudes, structural barriers such as limited access to resources may have limited their ability to translate this knowledge into practice [6, 9]. For example, many of them were frontline or essential workers who could not work from home.

Regarding H2, predicting fewer socioeconomic challenges among RAPID participants, was not supported. Although not reaching significance, the differences were in the desired direction. While RAPID participants initially reported more social and economic challenges than those in the control group, these differences were no longer significant once key covariates were accounted for by PSM. Importantly, greater challenges were reported among Spanish-speaking participants and those who were unemployed, regardless of group assignment. In contrast, those with private insurance experienced fewer COVID-19–related challenges. These findings suggest that the brief information-sharing and one-time referrals provided by CHWs may not have been sufficient to meaningfully reduce structural burdens among the most vulnerable. More sustained or intensive forms of support may be needed to ensure that basic needs are met and that preventive behaviors can be implemented consistently.

The findings supported H3, predicting higher levels of trust on official sources of health information. RAPID participants reported higher levels of trust in official sources of COVID-19 information than their control group counterparts. Based on previous findings [20], we were expecting to find a clearer difference between the groups regarding trust in officials and the information they shared regarding COVID-19. While we expected a more pronounced group difference, the lack of a stronger effect may be partially explained by high levels of missing data for this item. Nonetheless, the result underscores the potential of CHWs to counter misinformation and direct individuals toward credible, evidence-based sources of health information.

Importantly, H4, predicting greater desired effects with RAPID participants of LEP was supported. Language moderated the effects of the RAPID model. Spanish-speaking participants in the intervention group demonstrated significantly greater knowledge and lower engagement in COVID-19 risk behaviors compared to Spanish-speaking controls. This aligns with previous qualitative research [13] demonstrating that CHWs using the RAPID model were particularly effective with Spanish-speaking Latinos. These results highlight the power of cultural and linguistically congruent services in improving health behaviors and reducing disparities.

From a cross-sector alignment theory perspective [22], these findings support the importance of forming partnerships between academic and community-based health providers [23] to provide linguistically and culturally tailored public health outreach delivered by CHWs, particularly for Spanish-speaking populations. The present study is confirmatory of previous research studies and contributes new insights into the role of CHWs with LEP Latinos [13, 14, 17]. Most likely without the RAPID-sponsored testing events, many of the underserved Latino individuals would not have been tested, accessed or understood their test results, or received the additional services offered by the CHWs. The program was effective in engaging a vulnerable and underserved portion of the population through services that were linguistically and culturally congruent. The findings also documented that the Spanish-speaking participants in the RAPID program had stronger pro-public health attitudes and behaviors than their counterparts in the control group. More research is needed to better understand the genesis of those differences. The type of traditional media (e.g., television, printed media, and radio) and social media that Spanish speaking community members consume seems to be a factor to consider. Spanish language media may be producing a buffering effect from “fake news” and mainstream public health misinformation. In addition, it is important to highlight the trust community members have in CHWs as a source of accurate public health guidance. The findings from this study confirm the vital role CHWs can play in encouraging prosocial health behaviors during public health emergencies.

Limitations of the study

Although these findings make an important contribution to the existing public health literature, they are not without some limitations. Due to the need to immediately deploy the program to address the urgent health needs of the targeted population, the team encountered several challenges in assessing the efficacy of the different components of the RAPID model [20]. For example, the team did not conduct a random assignment of individuals to experimental and control groups but rather served all individuals in the identified high-risk zip codes. The control group was drawn from testing sites without CHWs and without Spanish language services, which made the groups different. Applying propensity score matching (PSM) reduced selection bias and improved covariate balance between groups. Although innovative and practical, the chosen alternative methods present some shortcomings in ensuring equivalence between the intervention and control groups.

Collecting baseline data on behaviors and attitudes toward mitigation before CHWs delivered public health messages at testing sites proved unfeasible. Stakeholders and CHWs argued that individuals would not remain at a COVID-19 testing site longer than necessary after completing their test, making a 45-minute survey particularly challenging. Similar barriers were reported in a study conducted in Latino-majority counties in California, where insufficient time or the inability to leave work was cited as a key reason for not getting tested for COVID-19 [32]. These types of less conventional methodological choices were made to be as responsible as possible for the health crisis and to help reduce the risk of infection and mortality of as many individuals as possible; however, given the three-month gap between COVID-19 testing and survey completion, participants’ responses may be subject to recall bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of self-reported information. This potential limitation could be minimized in the future by administering baseline surveys using tablets on the day of the event, as participants wait in their cars.

Implications for practice, policy and future research

The CHWs’ built-in trust and embeddedness in communities, their broad skill set, openness to perform a variety of roles, and their commitment to public health and the well-being of communities demonstrated once again the vital role CHWs play within underserved and vulnerable communities. Because many of the CHWs do not have a regular position or salary, the findings of this study shine a light on their important role and precarious status. For CHWs to be ready and available to respond to the next health crisis, policies are needed to formalize their role and increase their sustainability. More research is needed to further document the cost-effectiveness of their role and the return on investment when their position is funded and becomes more predictable. Future research utilizing the RCT gold standard will further solidify the existing evidence about the effectiveness of CHWs.

Conclusion

This study makes an important contribution to the existing knowledge about community-based interventions during major health crises. It captures the essential role CHWs can play in such times and uncovered their effectiveness in engaging and serving vulnerable and underserved communities, specially LEP Latinos. The findings provide a counternarrative to more fatalistic viewpoints about non-English speaking community members. Although not fluent in English, the findings confirm, these community members have strong public health beliefs and attitudes. Access to trusted and culturally and linguistically congruent services is critical for promoting high levels of wellbeing.

In addition, the innovative study design provides alternatives when the urgency of the crisis does not allow for the implementation of more traditional RCT designs. In conclusion, this study demonstrates the importance of having CHWs already embedded in communities as a necessary first step for a rapid response during a major health crisis. CHWs working in partnership with communities, public health agencies and academia attained extraordinary public health results rooted in trust, respect and competence.
