Impact of remnant cholesterol on arterial stiffness and mediating role of systolic blood pressure in chinese hypertensive adults
Authors: Chao Yu, Tao Wang, Lingjuan Zhu, Wei Zhou, Huihui Bao, Xiaoshu Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the association between remnant cholesterol (RC) levels and arterial stiffness in hypertensive patients and to explore potential effect modifiers.MethodsA cohort of 18,152 individuals diagnosed with hypertension was analyzed. RC was calculated using the Martin-Hopkins method, i.e., RC = total cholesterol (TC)—high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C)—low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). Multivariate logistic regression models were employed to mitigate the influence of potential confounding factors and assess the association between RC and arterial stiffness (baPWV ≥ 1800 cm/s). Mediating analysis was conducted to determine the extent to which systolic blood pressure (SBP) mediates the correlation between RC and arterial stiffness.ResultsAmong 18152 study participants, 50.0% of males had an RC < 0.78 mmol/L, with a mean (SD) age of 59.6 (9.6) years. A significant positive correlation was observed between elevated RC levels and an increased risk of arterial stiffness (OR = 1.31; 95% CI: 1.14, 1.50). This positive correlation was also evident in hypertensive populations with SBP ≥ 140 mmHg (OR = 1.45; 95% CI: 1.21, 1.70). However, in populations with SBP < 140 mmHg, the association between RC and arterial stiffness appeared to require additional research and validation (OR = 1. 11; 95% CI: 0.86, 1.43). The results of the mediation analysis indicated that SBP mediated 21.54% of the correlation between RC and arterial stiffness.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated a positive association between RC and an increased risk of arterial stiffness among Chinese adults diagnosed with hypertension, particularly those with inadequate blood pressure control. SBP plays a mediating role in the association between RC and arterial stiffness.Key PointsRC is positively associated with arterial stiffness in hypertensive adults, particularly those with poor blood pressure control.Independent of other traditional lipid markers, RC may serve as a target for the prevention of developing arterial stiffness in hypertensive patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24635-7.

Introduction

Arterial stiffness, a marker of reduced vascular elasticity and subclinical organ damage, is independently associated with cardiovascular disease (CVD) morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Brachial-ankle PWV (baPWV), a noninvasive substitute measure of arterial stiffness, is widely utilized in population studies due to its reproducibility, ease of use, and correlation with central and peripheral arterial stiffness [3]. While carotid-femoral PWV (cfPWV) is the gold standard for detecting arterial stiffness [4], the American Heart Association endorses baPWV as an applicable method for assessing clinical arterial stiffness in Asian populations (Class Ib, Level B evidence) [5].Most studies have shown that baPWVis an independent risk factor for CVD [3, 6]. Arterial stiffness has a bidirectional relationship with hypertension [7], diabetes [8], cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease [9], and all-cause mortality [10], creating a vicious cycle that heightens cardiovascular risk and highlights the importance of early detection.

Abnormal lipid metabolism is recognized as one of the major factors contributing to arterial stiffness [11]. While statins remain first-line therapy for ASCVD [12], residual cardiovascular risk persists despite low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) reduction [12], prompting focus on triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRLs) and remnant cholesterol (RC) [13]. Unlike triglycerides (TG), which are metabolized efficiently, RC-the cholesterol content of TRLs (e.g. intermediate-density lipoprotein, very low-density lipoprotein,) [14]-accumulates in vascular walls, directly promoting atherosclerosis [15–18]. RC levels correlate more strongly with CVD risk than LDL-C [19] or HDL-C [20], and can be estimated via the formula calculation method, which is also recommended by the American Heart Association guidelines and has been implemented globally in several clinical studies [21–23].

However, the correlation between arterial stiffness and some unconventional lipid markers, such as RC, remains to be explored. Although some previous studies have indicated that elevated RC levels may promote arterial stiffness [24–26], there is limited research available examining the relationship between RC and arterial stiffness in real-world populations. This study investigates the RC–arterial stiffness relationship in a multicenter Chinese hypertensive cohort and explores systolic blood pressure (SBP) as a potential mediator, addressing critical gaps in risk stratification and therapeutic targeting. The following article is presented in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies Quality Assessment Tool.

Methods

Study population

Data for this study were obtained from the China H-type Hypertension Registry Study (Registration number: ChiCTR1800017274, Date: July 20, 2018, the URL: https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=28262). This was an ongoing real-world observational study conducted in Wuyuan (Jiangxi Province) and Yuexi (Anhui Province), China, from March 2018 to August 2018. The study design and methods have been described in detail in previous publications [27–29]. In brief, This ongoing multicenter real-world study included participants of both genders aged 18 years and older with hypertension (≥ 140/90 mmHg), or the use of antihypertensive medications at baseline. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Biomedical Research Institute of Anhui Medical University (Ethics number: CH1059) and the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Ethics number: 2018019). Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and consent was obtained from the legal guardians in case of any illiterate participant.

From 28,684 enrolled hypertensive patients, we excluded 57 with missing remnant cholesterol (RC) data, 9,061 with missing baPWV measurements, 543 on Lipid-lowering medications, 418 with peripheral artery disease, and 453 with extreme RC values (± 1.5% of the distribution). The final analysis included 18,152 participants (See Figure S1 for details).

The sample size was estimated using the Kish Leslie formula for cross-sectional studies [30]. Based on Literature, the prevalence of arterial stiffness in hypertensive patients is 48.3% [27]. With a conservative P value of 0.483, a 95% confidence level, and 99% power, and accounting for a design effect of 1.5 and a 10% non-response rate, the required sample size was calculated to be 15821. The actual survey included 18,152 participants, achieving 99% power.

Data collection

Trained researchers administered standardized questionnaires covering demographic, clinical, and therapeutic information (see Supplementary Material). Venous blood samples were obtained from all participants following an overnight fast of at least 8 h. Collected blood samples were immediately frozen and transported to the central laboratory for analysis using a Beckman Coulter fully automated clinical analyzer. All laboratory tests adhered to standardized certification procedures. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation [31]. Diabetes was defined as fasting blood glucose levels ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, the use of hypoglycemic drugs, or a self-reported history of diabetes [31]. Lipid-lowering drugs include the use of statins or Bates. RC was calculated as TC—HDL-C—LDL-C (Martin-Hopkins formula) [32].

Definition of arterial stiffness

BaPWV was measured using an Omron BP-203RPEIII device. After a minimum of 5 min of rest in the supine position, four cuffs were attached to the upper arms and ankles of each participant. The lower edge of the arm cuff was positioned at a distance of 2–3 cm above the transverse grain of the elbow fossae, and the lower edge of the ankle cuff was positioned 1–2 cm above the internal ankle joint. Measurements were automatically recorded by the computer using a high-precision double-layer cuff sensor. Additionally, a heart sound detector was positioned on the left margin of the sternum. Each measurement was performed twice, and the result of the second measurement was considered final. The formula used to calculate baPWV was (La-Lb)/Tba; La represents the distance between the heart and the ankle, Lb represents the distance between the heart and the upper arm, and Tba represents the time difference between the waveform and the upper arm. Data analysis used mean baPWV values derived from bilateral limb measurements. baPWV ≥ 1800 cm/s was defined as arterial stiffness in hypertensive individuals [6].

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests were two-sided tests, and a P-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Statistical analyses and chart generation were performed using the statistical package R, version 4.2.0 (The R Foundation, http://www.r-project.org).

An RC concentration ≥ 0.78 mmol/L was classified as elevated RC, and participants were divided into two groups based on this threshold [22, 23]. Normality of variables was assessed using the one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Quantitative data with normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), non-normally distributed data as median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as number (percentage). Population characteristics stratified by the clinical cutoff of RC were compared using the Student t test or Mann–Whitney U test for quantitative variables (based on distribution normality) and the χ2 test for categorical variables. The dose–response correlation among RC, baPWV, and arterial stiffness was analyzed using a generalized additive model (GAM) and smoothed curve fitting (penalized spline method). baPWV was analyzed as a continuous variable using multivariate linear regression to preserve measurement granularity and maximize statistical power, while arterial stiffness (defined dichotomously as baPWV ≥ 1,800 cm/s) was assessed via logistic regression models to align with risk stratification guidelines. This dual approach enabled both dose–response evaluation of RC’s association with arterial stiffness and clinically actionable risk interpretation. The covariates included traditional and underlying risk factors for arterial stiffness and potential confounding factors, which were selected based on their clinical importance, statistical significance in the univariable analysis, and the potential confounders effect estimates individually changed by at least 10%. Adjustments for the residuals of several conventional lipid indicators were incorporated into various models to mitigate the influence of multicollinearity on the regression coefficients [34]. Additionally, potential modifiers for the association between RC and arterial stiffness were assessed using stratified analyses and interaction testing. These factors included sex, age, BMI, SBP, DBP, current smoking, current drinking, homocysteine (Hcy), uric acid (UA), eGFR, history of stroke, coronary heart disease (CHD), diabetes, antihypertensive drugs, and antiplatelet drugs. Subsequently, a mediating effect analysis was conducted using the product of coefficients method to determine whether SBP could account for the effect of RC on arterial stiffness. This process involved quantification of the aggregate effect (the association between the independent and dependent variables), direct effect (the overall effect not influenced by the intermediary), and indirect effect (the influence of the independent variable on the dependent variable attributed to the intermediary). The proportion of the mediating effect was calculated by dividing the indirect effects by the total effects [35, 36]. To assess potential attrition bias, we conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing baseline characteristics of included versus excluded participants (Table S1).

As shown in Table 2, in the fully adjusted model (model 3), each 1-unit increase in RC was associated with an increase of 30.12 cm/s in baPWV (95% CI: 13.42–46.82), and the corresponding increase in the risk of arterial stiffness was 1.31 (95% CI: 1.14–1.50). Stratified analyses using additional factors

A stratified analysis was conducted to further examine the correlation between RC (< 0.78 vs. ≥ 0.78) and arterial stiffness among the different subgroups (Fig. 3). The results revealed that factors such as sex, age, BMI, DBP, current smoking, current drinking, Hcy, eGFR, history of stroke, CHD, diabetes, antihypertensive drugs, and antiplatelet drugs did not significantly modify the association between RC and arterial stiffness (P-values for all interactions > 0.05), except in the SBP subgroups (P-interaction = 0.025). A significant positive correlation was observed between RC and arterial stiffness (OR, 1.34; 95% CI: 1.16, 1.54) in participants with SBP ≥ 140 mmHg. However, the relationship between RC and arterial stiffness in participants with SBP < 140 mmHg requires further investigation (OR, 1.11; 95% CI: 0.86, 1.43) (Fig. 2). To determine the rationale behind this outcome, we presented baseline data for the study population grouped according to SBP (Table S2). Relationships among RC, SBP, and baPWV and their mediating effects

As shown in Table S4, RC is positively associated with both SBP and baPWV. In the fully adjusted model, per 1-unit increase in RC causes baPWV to increase by 37.92 cm/s (P < 0.05), and SBP increases by 1.18 mmHg (P < 0.05). With SBP as the independent variable and baPWV as the dependent variable, the analysis revealed a positive correlation between the two; specifically, baPWV increased by 6.66 cm/s for every 1-unit increase in SBP (P < 0.05).

A mediation effect analysis was conducted to further elucidate the relationship between RC, SBP, and baPWV. The total effect of increased RC levels on baPWV was determined to be 12.51. Sensitivity analyses

Compared with the 18,152 included participants, the 10,532 excluded individuals were older (mean age + 3.4 years), had higher SBP (+ 2.7 mmHg), higher rates of antihypertensive medication use (+ 3.8%), and displayed adverse metabolic markers including elevated TG, Hcy, and UA (all P < 0.05). Greater standard deviations across variables indicated lower data quality. These differences may have led to an underestimation of the RC effect size (Table S1).

Discussion

This multicenter study aimed to investigate the association between RC and arterial stiffness in Chinese adults with hypertension, with particular focus on the mediating role of SBP. We observed a significant positive association between RC and arterial stiffness, which remained robust in patients with poorly controlled blood pressure even after covariate adjustment. This association persisted after further adjustment for residuals of conventional lipid markers exhibiting collinearity with RC. Furthermore, SBP was identified as a key mediator in the association between RC and arterial stiffness.

Previous studies have demonstrated that RC is an effective predictor of CVD [37]. The PREDIMED study revealed that the risk of major cardiovascular adverse events escalated by 21% for every 10 mg/dL increase in RC levels [33]. Additionally, participants with RC ≥ 0.78 mmol/L had a greater risk of major cardiovascular adverse events than those with lower RC, regardless of LDL-C ≤ 2.59 mmol/L [31]. Quispe et al. demonstrated that the risk of ASCVD associated with RC is independent of traditional risk factors such as LDL-C and apolipoprotein B [13]. Notably, RC’s atherogenicity stems from its role in TRLs: unlike TG, which cells can degrade, RC accumulates in vascular walls, driving foam cell formation and endothelial dysfunction [15–17]. Moreover, RC has been associated with conditions including peripheral artery disease [38], aortic stenosis [39], stroke [38, 40], non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [41], diabetic complications [42], and renal dysfunction [43].

Although a few additional studies have demonstrated that RC may promote arterial stiffness, most of these were cytological in nature [24–26], with a limited number of clinical studies involving actual populations. Large-scale epidemiological evidence remains sparse, despite the clinical feasibility of formula-derived RC measurements [22, 24]. Genetic studies have also identified genes that influence RC levels and increase the risk of CVD [37, 44]. Qian et al. utilized common carotid intima-media thickness as a marker for subclinical atherosclerosis and found that fasting levels of RC in patients who had suffered an ischemic stroke were positively correlated with the mean intima-media thickness of the bilateral common carotid arteries and the maximum intima-media thickness of a unilateral common carotid artery [45]. Two single-center cross-sectional studies from China established an association between elevated RC levels and increased arterial stiffness using the Martin-Hopkins method to calculate RC [46, 47]. These findings align with those of a prior secondary data analysis from a cross-sectional study in Japan [48], and our study further substantiated this view.

To date, additional research is required to definitively confirm the precise mechanism through which elevated RC levels induce arterial stiffness; however, the following hypotheses may be used to explain the underlying mechanism. First, similar to LDL-C, RC particles traverse endothelial cells and accumulate in the vascular intima, where they are ingested by macrophages and smooth muscle cells, leading to the formation of foam cells and eventually helping in the development of atherosclerotic plaques [14]. RC is more readily engulfed and phagocytosed by macrophages compared to LDL-C due to the smaller volume of LDL particles than that of lipoproteins carrying remnant cholesterol, thereby potentially accelerating the formation of atherosclerotic plaque [49]. Second, elevated RC levels have been recognized as a risk factor for vascular endothelial dysfunction, a key contributor to the formation of atherosclerotic plaques [50]. Finally, genetic studies have indicated that elevated RC levels are associated with mild inflammation, which is a well-established risk factor for arterial stiffness [51].

Multivariate linear regression analysis identified that both RC and SBP were positively associated with baPWV, whereas RC was also positively associated with SBP as well. Hypertension is a generally accepted risk factor for the development of arterial stiffness. Apart from its impact on arterial stiffness, dyslipidemia has the potential to elevate blood pressure by affecting the bioavailability of nitric oxide [52], increasing blood viscosity [53], and influencing other relevant factors. The outcomes of the subgroup analysis showed that SBP may partially mediate the relationship between RC and baPWV. The finding was further validated by conducting additional mediation effect analysis, and SBP was identified to mediate the association between RC and arterial stiffness (mediator effect value 2.69, mediator effect ratio 21.54%).

This multi-center study, encompassing a large sample of hypertensive adults from Central and South China, adjusted for key confounders and first identified SBP as a mediator of the RC-arterial stiffness relationship, accounting for 21.5% of the total effect. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, this study is based on a cross-sectional design, which limits detection of causal relationships between RC and arterial stiffness. Second, despite robust adjustments, residual confounding may persist, particularly from unmeasured inflammatory markers such as highly sensitive C-reactive protein [54], which RC is known to elevate through endothelial pro-inflammatory activation [51]. Finally, reliance on fasting RC measurements may underestimate clinical relevance, given evidence that nonfasting lipids (e.g., nonfasting TG in women and nonfasting cholesterol in men) better predict cardiovascular risk in some populations [55]. Future investigations should explore potential differences between RC and arterial stiffness during fasting and nonfasting states.

Conclusions

In this study, a significant positive association was identified between RC and arterial stiffness among Chinese adults diagnosed with hypertension, particularly those with poorly controlled blood pressure. Independent of other traditional lipid markers, RC may serve as an assessment tool for the risk of developing arterial stiffness in hypertensive patients. Furthermore, our findings suggest that SBP acts as a mediator in the association between RC and arterial stiffness.

This study provides a new approach for formulating a lipid-lowering treatment plan for healthcare professionals. Monitoring RC levels may help identify hypertensive patients at elevated risk of arterial stiffness, particularly those with suboptimal blood pressure control.
