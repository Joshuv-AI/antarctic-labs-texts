Pathogenic diversity of Cryptococcus in Galleria mellonella extends beyond classical virulence factors
Authors: Bianca AG Sena, Marlon DM Santos, Cassia M Souza, Amanda C Camillo-Andrade, Haroldo C de Oliveira, Flavia CG Dos Reis, Rafael F Castelli, Diogo Kuczera, Henrique RM Antoniolli, Hellen GG Santos, Charley C Staats, Guilherme L Sassaki, Paulo C Carvalho, Marcio L Rodrigues
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathogenic determinants in the Cryptococcus genus have been extensively studied, often using standard laboratory isolates. Here, we analyzed the virulence of ten Cryptococcus isolates from diverse sources, species, and genotypes. These isolates exhibited marked differences in their ability to colonize and kill the invertebrate host Galleria mellonella, as well as in their interactions with hemocytes. Capsule formation also varied widely among isolates, with no clear correlation between virulence in G. mellonella and source of isolation, species, fungal burden, capsule size, or interaction with larval hemocytes. To further investigate the basis of this pathogenic diversity, we selected two hypervirulent isolates (C. deuterogattii and C. neoformans) and two hypovirulent isolates (C. gattii and C. neoformans) for in-depth analysis. Differences in the induction of antimicrobial peptides during infection did not account for the observed variation in virulence. Genomic analysis of capsule-related genes, scanning electron microscopy of capsule morphology in vitro and in vivo, and nuclear magnetic resonance of the major capsular polysaccharide all revealed high variability among isolates, but none of these features correlated with virulence. Proteomic profiling of cellular extracts showed that virulent strains were enriched in proteins associated with oxidative processes. Supplementation with an antioxidant during infection increased the virulence of hypovirulent isolates in G. mellonella. These results reveal a high pathogenic diversity in Cryptococcus that goes beyond its classical virulence factors.Keywords: Capsule, Cryptococcus, Proteomics, Pathogenic diversity

Introduction

Species within the Cryptococcus gattii/neoformans complex are the causative agents of cryptococcosis, a potentially fatal fungal disease in humans (Meya and Williamson, 2024). While C. gattii and related species can affect both immunocompetent and immunocompromised individuals, C. neoformans and C. deneoformans primarily cause disease in immunocompromised hosts. Significant phenotypic differences exist between the C. gattii and C. neoformans species complexes, highlighting the need to study their distinct pathogenic mechanisms (Saidykhan et al., 2022).

Two well-characterized virulence factors in Cryptococcus are its polysaccharide capsule and melanin production, both of which contribute to immune evasion and host damage (Zaragoza, 2019). However, the extent of melanization and capsule formation varies widely among different isolates (Maufrais et al., 2021). Melanin synthesis depends on the enzyme laccase, and laccase-mediated melanization is one of the few virulence traits linked to clinical outcomes in humans (Sabiiti et al., 2014).

Diversity in fungi arises from multiple mechanisms, including mutation (Hilbert et al., 2023), gene gain or loss (Muñoz et al., 2018), chromosomal rearrangements (Coelho et al., 2024), ploidy changes (Todd et al., 2017), and sexual reproduction (Ni et al., 2013). These processes contribute to genome plasticity, which influences virulence, antifungal resistance, and the pathogen's success in diverse environments (Freese and Beyhan, 2023). In Cryptococcus, population heterogeneity enhances the pathogen's ability to adapt to shifting environmental and host-related conditions, such as the transition from the external environment to the lung, from extracellular to intracellular niches, and from immunosuppressed to drug-exposed settings (Agrawal et al., 2024).

Our recent analysis of 53 Cryptococcus isolates from Brazil revealed considerable phenotypic and genotypic diversity (Maufrais et al., 2021). Classical virulence traits, such as capsule morphology and serological properties, and melanization capacity, varied greatly among strains, with no clear lineage-specific patterns (Maufrais et al., 2021). These findings underscore the complexity of virulence within the genus. To investigate this further, we examined the virulence profiles of ten diverse isolates from the C. gattii/neoformans complex. Although we observed marked variability in virulence, this did not correlate with classical pathogenic traits. Instead, increased antioxidant defenses were reasonably associated with higher virulence, suggesting that non-classical mechanisms play a critical role in pathogenesis. These findings reveal a high and still poorly understood diversity in the pathogenicity of Cryptococcus.

Results

Ten cryptococcal isolates manifest variable virulence profiles in G. mellonella

We initially asked whether the ability of the ten cryptococcal strains selected for this study (Table 1) and the reference strains H99 and R265 would show different efficacies to kill G. mellonella as a consequence of fungal virulence. To assess how lethal the different strains were, we evaluated the survival of G. mellonella larvae infected with each isolate over a period of 10 days. Killing of 100 % of the larvae occurred between days 5 and 8 for most isolates, except for isolate Cg198, which caused only 50 % mortality after day 10 (Fig. 1A). The strains H99, Cn271, Cn161 (both C. neoformans, clinical sources), and R265 (C. deuterogattii, clinical source) were the most virulent, killing 50 % of the population between the third and fourth days, and achieving 100 % mortality of the larval population by the fifth day of the experiment. The strains Cg366 (C. neoformans, environmental) and Cg198 (C. gattii, environmental) were the least virulent strains, with 70 % of the larvae still alive on the sixth day of the experiment for Cg366, and the total larval population of the experimental group only dying by the tenth day. Of note, although the Cg366 nomenclature suggests a C. gattii isolate, this strain was misclassified at the time of its isolation and was later identified as C. neoformans by genome sequencing (Maufrais et al., 2021). For Cg198, we observed 90 % survival on the eighth day, with the survival curve ending on the tenth day with 50 % survival of the larval population in the experimental group. These results suggested an increased pathogenicity in clinical strains. However, strain Cn216 (C. neoformans, environmental) was an efficient killer of G. mellonella. We also evaluated the ability of the strains to survive and replicate within G. mellonella after 72 h hours of infection (Fig. 1B). No clear differences were found between the strains in the growth of colony-forming units (Fig. 1B).

Variation in capsule size during infection of G. mellonella

The capsule is the most extensively characterized virulence factor in the Cryptococcus genus (Casadevall et al., 2019; Kozel, 1995; Zaragoza et al., 2009). Due to the variability in the virulence profile of our group of ten isolates, we asked whether capsule formation would be responsible for the virulence differences and measured capsular dimensions of each isolate before infection and recovered fungal cells after 72 h of infection in G. mellonella for a second round of capsule measurements. All strains showed an increase in capsule size after passing through G. mellonella (Fig. 2; p < 0.0001). However, some isolates exhibited greater changes when pre- and post-infection were compared. The strain Cg198 showed the highest difference in capsular growth, increasing from an average capsule size of 5 μm to 13 μm, followed by Cn186, which grew from 1.6 μm to 9.7 μm. The strains that exhibited the smallest difference in average capsule size were Cn161 (1.9 to 4.8 μm), Cn271 (2.0 to 4.3 μm), and H99 (1.0 to 2.2 μm). All comparisons showed p < 0.0001. Of note, the highly virulent strain Cn271 showed moderate capsule increase, while the hypovirulent strain Cg366 was highly efficient in increasing the capsular dimensions.

Phagocytosis is essential for the control of cryptococcal infection (Feldmesser et al., 2000; Shao et al., 2005), and the capsule is the most important antiphagocytic structure in the Cryptococcus genus. Therefore, we evaluated the indices of infection of hemocytes, the phagocytic cells of G. mellonella, by the isolates under investigation in this study. After 3 h of incubation, using flow cytometry analysis, we quantified the infection indices G. mellonella hemocytes (Fig. 3). The proportion of infected hemocytes ranged from 2 % to 15 %, with the H99 strain (reduced capsule size) showing the highest value. However, strain Cn271, which also had reduced capsular dimensions, infected the hemocytes at a low efficacy, suggesting that capsule size is not necessarily determinant for interactions between cryptococci and hemocytes.

Due to the high variability in the parameters evaluated in our set of strains, we measured the correlations between survival rates, capsule size, fungal loads in vivo, and proportion of hemocyte infection. Specifically, we calculated the correlation values for the following paired systems: G. mellonella survival versus capsule size (average values), G. mellonella survival (average) versus hemocyte infection (percentage), and G. mellonella survival versus fungal load (colony-forming units). All comparisons were performed at days 3 and 5 post-infection (Fig. 4). We found no significant correlation in any of the systems after correction for multiple tests. These results, when collectively analyzed, suggest no clear associations between virulence and capsule size, fungal loads, or indices of hemocyte infection.

At this point, it was impossible to identify conventional cryptococcal determinants associated with high or low virulence in G. mellonella. Of note, all strains had showed similar urease activity (data not shown). Therefore, we then included additional parameters in our analysis, selecting 4 isolates based on profiles of high and low virulence. These strains were Cn271 (C. neoformans) and R265 (C. deuterogattii), classified as hypervirulent, and Cg366 (C. neoformans) and Cg198 (C. gattii) classified as hypovirulent. From this point, our results were mostly focused on this group of isolates, and we adopted the nomenclature in reference to their virulence as R265hyper, Cn271hyper, Cg198hypo, and Cg366hypo.

Characterization of hypo and hypervirulent isolates

The first cryptococcal virulence factor considered in our analysis of the group of four isolates was melanin, given its pathogenic role in murine (Salas et al., 1996) and in human (Sabiiti et al., 2014) diseases caused by Cryptococcus. However, an association between melanin and the pathogenic potential of our isolates was promptly ruled out, based on their pigment production as determined in a previous study (Maufrais et al., 2021). Cg366hypo was an efficient melanin producer, in contrast to Cn271hyper and R265hyper (which showed moderate melanin formation) and Cg198hypo (which showed low melanin formation). These results led us to discard melanin and urease production in our analysis.

We also measured the growth rates of the four strains over 48 h in the typical fungal medium YPD, in addition to the Schneider's medium (ideal for cultivation of G. mellonella hemocytes) at 37 °C. We observed that in YPD, the strains showed similar growth levels, except for strain Cg198hypo, which exhibited reduced growth (Fig. 5A). In the Schneider's medium, strains Cg198hypo, and Cg366hypo grew less efficiently than the hypervirulent isolates R265hyper and Cn271hyper (Fig. 5B). Indeed, both hypovirulent strains gave lower CFU values during G. mellonella infection (Fig. 1B), suggesting either a reduced ability to proliferate in vivo or higher abilities to induce antimicrobial peptides during infection. We then questioned whether the expression of the genes coding for galiomicin and gallerimycin, the primary antifungal peptides produced by G. mellonella (Lee et al., 2004; Schuhmann et al., 2003) would be altered in response to the different cryptococci. qPCR measurements showed that strains Cn271hyper and Cg198hypo were the most efficient inducers of gallerimycin, while isolates Cg366hypo and R265hyper were characterized as poor inducers of the expression of this peptide (Fig. 5C). When the expression of galiomicin was tested, strain Cg198hypo stood out as the most efficient inducer, but the isolates Cn271hyper and R265hyper also induced relatively high levels of galiomicin expression (Fig. 5D). Strain Cg366hypo produced negligible levels of galiomicin expression. In summary, our results suggested that the hypovirulent strains had reduced abilities to growth depending on the media, but no correlation between virulence and expression of antimicrobial peptides was found.

Capsule structure and virulence in Cryptococcus

We were unable to correlate capsular dimensions with virulence, but at this point we could not rule out the possibility that the capsular structure was related to the pathogenicity of strains R265hyper, Cn271hyper, Cg198hypo, and Cg366hypo. In addition to the quantitative measurements of capsule dimensions, we included three other parameters in our capsular analysis: morphological characteristics, assessed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) on several genes related to capsule formation, and structural features of its main polysaccharide component, determined by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), as follows.

The morphological features of the capsule produced by each strain were analyzed by SEM before and after infection of G. mellonella (Fig. 6). The morphological patterns were highly variable. While strains R265hyper, Cn271hyper and Cg366hypo all had reduced capsules before infection, the R265hyper strain clearly manifested a denser fiber network. Strain Cg198hypo had apparently larger fibers than the other isolates. After 72 h of infection, a clear increase in fiber length was observed in the R265hyper strain. Capsular fibers also had different morphological aspects in response to G. mellonella infection in strains Cn271hyper, and Cg366hypo but, once again, we could not establish a pattern common to hyper and hypovirulent strains.

Ultrastructural analysis of the capsules of strains R265hyper, Cn271hyper, Cg198hypo, and Cg366hypobefore (0 h) and after (72 h) infection of G. mellonella. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used for analysis of capsule fibers. The results indicated that passage through the larvae induces dramatic changes in the capsular morphology, but no clear correlation with virulence.

In an additional effort to characterize the capsular characteristics of each of the four strains, we conducted a comparative genomic analysis utilizing the previously determined sequences for strains Cn271hyper, Cg198hypo, and Cg366hypo (Maufrais et al., 2021), with the respective references genomes. For Cg198hypo, the comparison was made with the highly curated reference strain R265hyper (Ferrareze et al., 2021). As strains Cn271hyper and Cg366hypo do not have curated annotation, we compared such genome sequences against the highly curated reference strain C. neoformans H99 (Janbon et al., 2014). A comprehensive analysis of SNP detection revealed significant disparities in both the quantity and anticipated impact of variants across the three strains (Supplementary Table 1). The C. gattii strain Cg198hypo exhibited the highest total number of SNPs (472,122), although the vast majority were predicted to have low or modifier effects (38.95 % and 44.25 %, respectively). In contrast, C. neoformans strains Cn271hyper and Cg366hypo, both compared to the H99 reference, exhibited fewer total SNPs (174,805 and 123,147, respectively). However, they exhibited a higher relative proportion of variants predicted to have functional consequences. Notably, strain Cg366hypo harbored 4671 high-impact SNPs, which is substantially higher than the 2141 observed in strain Cn271hyper (z-score: 16.79; p-value <0.0001). This suggests a greater burden of potentially deleterious mutations in strain Cg366hypo, which could potentially explain the numerous observed altered phenotypes compared to strain Cn271hyper.

We also evaluated the SNPs on several genes whose orthologs were previously associated with capsule homeostasis in C. neoformans (Almeida et al., 2015; Hicks et al., 2005; Kozubowski et al., 2013; Moyrand and Janbon, 2004). The complete set of genes analyzed is available in Supplementary Table 2. For the analysis of SNPs on these genes, we only considered SNPs that were classified by the SnpEff tool (Cingolani et al., 2012) as of high impact, which would cause protein truncation, loss of function or nonsense mediated decay. From the 43 genes associated with capsule metabolism, a comparison between the gene sequences of Cn271hyper and H99 identified 11 SNPs in 8 genes. Similarly, a comparison between Cg366hypo and H99 revealed 40 SNPs in 28 genes. Strain - Cg366hypo exhibited a significantly higher number of high-impact SNPs within genes related to capsule metabolism, accumulating nearly four times as many mutations in a broader range of genes compared to the Cn271hyper strain (z-score 4.00, p < 0.0001). This indicates a strong correlation between the accumulation of potentially deleterious mutations in this pathway and the capsule phenotype. We conducted an analysis of the comparison of genes associated with capsule homeostasis between C. deuterogattii Cg198hypo and R265hyper strains. Our findings revealed 14 SNPs with significant impact in 9 genes (Table 3), thereby confirming the capsular diversity observed in these strains. However, it is important to note that we cannot entirely exclude the possibility that SNPs observed in other genes may also contribute to the observed phenotypes.

The above-described diversity reflected into structural variations, as confirmed by the analysis of the structural properties of GXM, the major capsular polysaccharide in Cryptococcus, in each strain by NMR; Fig. 7. Monosaccharide composition analysis, performed via NMR (Sassaki et al., 2014), identified Man, GlcA, and Xyl, the typical components of GXM (Cherniak et al., 1980), as the exclusive components. These findings were corroborated by TLC and GC–MS analysis of glycoside acetates, (Kochetkov and Chizhov, 1967). The anomeric region (Fig. 7) revealed 15 distinct signals, reflecting the complex distribution of Xyl and GlcA residues along the α-Manp-(1 → 3) backbone. Cross-peak integration highlighted subtle differences, potentially associated with morphological variations between hypo- and hypervirulent isolates. The NMR data revealed critical features of the polysaccharide architecture, the α-Manp-(1 → 3) unit (A2) of the GXM-A triad (δ 5.30/100.8 ppm) was identified as O-2-substituted by β-Xylp, while the A1 (δ 5.25/99.6) and A3 (δ 5.13/99.5) units corresponded to C2-substituted α-Manp-(1 → 3)[β-GlcpA] and α-Manp-(1 → 3)[β-Xylp], respectively (Cherniak et al., 1995; Cherniak et al., 1988). Unsubstituted α-Manp-(1 → 3) triads were detected at δ 4.997/98.9 and δ 5.007/100.4, demonstrating structural heterogeneity. Acetylation patterns were confirmed through characteristic downfield shifts of H-6 signals (δ 4.427; 4.357/64.8), diagnostic of 6-O-acetylated Manp residues (Previato et al., 2017). Notably, a minor but distinct signal at δ 5.36/69.8 ppm suggested additional O-2-acetyl substitution in the Cg198 hypo variant, coexisting with the predominant 6-O-acetylation. These modifications, combined with the observed complex xylosyl branching pattern, underscore the structural diversity of the GXM polysaccharide framework. Notably, the HSQC spectrum of Cg198 hypo exhibited a minor signal (δ 5.36/69.8 ppm), suggesting O-2-acetyl substitution on Manp alongside the predominant 6-O-acetylation. The α-Manp-(1 → 3) backbone also displayed a complex xylosyl pattern, with at least four non-reducing β-Xylp termini exhibiting enhanced mobility in solution, as evidenced by intensified C2-C4 cross-peaks in the HSQC spectrum.

Partial HSQC-edited spectra of cryptococcal capsular polysaccharides (anomeric region), revealing the complex distribution of β-Xylp and β-GlcAp residues along the α-Manp-(1→3) backbone. Fifteen cross peaks were distinguished and integrated. Data acquired at 600 MHz (343 K) in D2O.

Strain-specific differences were observed in the integration of a β-Xylp signal (δ 4.292/104.1 ppm). Hypovirulent isolates (Cg198: 9.7 %; Cg366: 7.1 %) showed higher signal intensities compared to hypervirulent strains (Cn271: 4.0 %; R265: 2.8 %), reinforcing the notion that the cryptococcal capsule is highly diverse in structure.

Analysis of cellular proteomes

The high inter strain diversity associated with the general lack of correlation between the cryptococcal capsule and virulence in our model led us to investigate other structures differentially expressed in strains R265hyper, Cn271hyper, Cg198hypo, and Cg366hypo. The strains exhibited highly diverse proteomes, as initially inferred from our analysis of protein networks in each of the four strains using the String tool (executed on August 24, 2025). Protein classes are summarized in Fig. 8, and the comprehensive list of proteins for each of the four isolates is available as Supplemental File 1, Table 1, Table 2, Table 3, Table 4. These findings substantiate at the molecular level the substantial diversity within the Cryptococcus genus.

For a qualitative analysis of protein composition, we performed paired analysis between the C. neoformans (Cg366hypo and Cn271hyper) and C. gattii/deuterogattii (Cg198hypo and R265hyper) strains, which allowed comparisons between hyper and hypovirulent isolates. The hypervirulent strains showed a higher number of exclusive proteins (201 proteins in Cn271hyper; 312 proteins in R265hyper, in contrast to 32 proteins in Cg366hypo 55 proteins in Cg198hypo). Additionally, we observed that the neoformans strains (Cn271hyper and Cg366hypo) share 1.319 proteins, while the gattii/deuterogattii strains (R265hyper and Cg198hypo) share 1.616 proteins (Fig. 9A). We also searched for the most abundant proteins in the hypervirulent strains as an attempt to identify candidates for pathogenic markers. For identification of these proteins, we once again performed paired analyses within the neoformans and gattii/deuterogattii groups (Cn271hyper vs. Cg366 hypo; Cg198 hypo vs. R265 hyper) (Fig. 9B). In the neoformans group, we found 179 proteins more abundant in Cn271hyper compared to Cg366 hypo, whereas 46 proteins were more abundant in Cg366 hypo. In the C. deuterogattii/gattii group, R265 hyper had 166 more abundant proteins, while Cg198hypo had 63 proteins in higher abundance. For further functional studies and identification of major proteins, we selected the five most abundant proteins in the virulent strains. Protein classes are summarized in Fig. 9C. Of note, 6 out of the 10 proteins with the highest absolute abundance in the two hyper virulent strains were related to oxidative processes (D-arabinitol 2-dehydrogenase, glutathione peroxidase GPX1, acyl-CoA oxidase, multifunctional beta-oxidation protein MFE2, formate dehydrogenase, and aldehyde dehydrogenase). We then asked whether these proteins could confer resistance of the virulent strains against the oxidative response in G. mellonella. We initially tested whether exogenously added ascorbic acid concomitant with the infection by hypervirulent isolates (Cn271hyper and R265 hyper) would alter their virulence profile (Fig. 10). No significant differences were found between survival rates in the absence or in the presence of ascorbic acid (Fig. 10A and B). However, when the hypovirulent strains (Cg366hypo and Cg198hypo) were tested, the presence of ascorbic acid significantly increased their ability to kill G. mellonella (Fig. 10C and D). In fact, ascorbic acid caused an important profile shift, advancing the beginning of the decline in the survival curve by four days for Cg366hypo and two days for Cg198 hypo. By the sixth day of the experiment, the Cg366 hypo and Cg198 hypo strains injected together with ascorbic acid had killed 50 % and 70 % of the population, respectively, whereas those injected with PBS only began to decline their survival curve on that day and still had 80 % of the population alive. These results suggested that the ability to neutralize oxidant agents was important for the pathogenicity of our cryptococcal isolates. To substantiate this conclusion, our analysis encompassed isolates exhibiting intermediate pathogenic potential. These isolates demonstrated a slower rate of mortality against G. mellonella compared to isolates Cn271hyper and R265hyper, while they exhibited a faster rate of mortality against Cg366hypo and Cg198hypo strains, as illustrated in Fig. 1. These isolates were Cg188 and Cg221, representing the gattii/deuterogattii group (100 % mortality by day 8 post infection), and Cn115 and Cn216, representing the neoformans group (100 % mortality by day 7 post infection). In contrast to the virulent isolates, where the presence of ascorbic acid was inconsequential, and to the hypovirulent isolates, where this antioxidant accelerated mortality, effects over the moderately virulent group of isolates were intermediate (Fig. 10E-G). Ascorbic acid generally accelerated the mortality of G. mellonella in all instances, but no statistically significant differences were observed.

We next evaluated whether the differences in virulence observed upon antioxidant supplementation could be linked to variations in key genes associated with redox metabolism. We concentrated on genes annotated with the Gene Ontology term “response to oxidative stress” (GO:0006979), retrieved from FungiDB (Basenko et al., 2024). A comparison of gene sequences between strains Cn271hyper and H99 revealed 10 SNPs, while the comparison between Cg366hypo and H99 identified 17 SNPs in the analyzed genes (Table 4). Although no statistical differences were detected using a two-proportion Z-test (z-score = 0.56, p-value = 0.575), key proteins involved in the detoxification of reactive oxidation species, such as catalase and glutathione S-transferase, accumulate a higher number of deleterious SNPs in Cg366hypo compared to Cn271hyper (Table 5). We also examined C. deuterogattii strains Cg198hypo and R265hyper, focusing on genes involved in oxidative stress, and identified 11 high-impact SNPs, as defined by SnpEff (Table 5). The complete set of genes analyzed is available in Supplementary Table 3. These findings further support the role of redox metabolism in cryptococcal pathogenicity.

Discussion

Diversity at multiple levels has been consistently reported in the Cryptococcus genus, both in small and large collections of fungal isolates. For example, members of the C. neoformans/C. gattii species complexes produce extracellular vesicles with highly variable properties, as demonstrated in a study of 15 isolates from diverse origins, including the four hyper- and hypovirulent strains analyzed in the present work (Reis et al., 2021). In broader survey, a collection of 300C. neoformans isolates from 237 patients in the Netherlands was analyzed for mating type, serotype, genotype, and susceptibility to seven antifungal agents, revealing high genetic diversity among serotype A isolates, and lower diversity within the serotype D group (Hagen et al., 2012). Similarly, analysis of three serotype C C. gattii isolates showed marked variation in capsule dimensions, gene expression, cell wall architecture, pigment production, induction of host chitinase activity, capsule antibody reactivity, and pathogenicity (Rodrigues et al., 2015). Comparative analysis of the well-characterized C. deuterogattii R265 VGIIa strain and Brazilian VGII isolates revealed differences in capsular dimensions and shedding, melanin production, and urease activity, traits that directly influenced virulence (Barcellos et al., 2018).

Whole-genome sequencing of 372 clinical Cryptococcus isolates from 341 HIV-associated cryptococcal meningitis patients in Malawi and Cameroon identified distinct patterns of disruptive mutations in genes related to DNA binding and energy metabolism. Some mutations, including one in a transcription factor regulating phagocytosis, emerged during the course of infection (Sephton-Clark et al., 2023).

Several of our findings contradicted the common sense that capsule and melanin govern cryptococcal virulence. For example, our less virulent strain, Cg366hypo, produced melanin more efficiently than the hypervirulent strains R265hyper and Cn271hyper (Maufrais et al., 2021). A similar pattern emerged in capsule analysis: Cg366hypo formed large capsules during infection, whereas Cn271hyper failed to do so in vivo. These observations are supported by a molecular epidemiological study of 228C. neoformans/C. gattii complex isolates collected before 1975 (Pharkjaksu et al., 2020). In that study, within the C. gattii complex, VGI isolates showed greater nucleotide diversity than VGIII, and high-virulence genotypes (ST5/VNI and VGIIIa/serotype B) did not exhibit elevated levels of classical virulence traits. Among C. neoformans isolates, the VNIV genotype displayed the highest genetic diversity, and clinical isolates were less diverse than environmental and veterinary ones.

Hyper- and hypovirulent isolates did not display consistent patterns in their interaction with hemocytes, in vivo proliferation, or induction of antimicrobial peptides. These findings suggest that the observed variability in pathogenic profiles may arise from strain-specific intrinsic properties, rather than being solely attributable to classical phenotypic traits or genotype-based characteristics. In addition, it is worth noting that some virulence traits, including the capsule, may play dual roles when present at unusually low or high levels, or depending on the distribution of their components. For instance, the loss of the vacuolar Ca2+ exchanger Vcx1 did not affect capsule size but led to decreased secretion of GXM, which was associated with a hypovirulent profile (Kmetzsch et al., 2010).

While our results highlight that the intrinsic complexity of Cryptococcus virulence undoubtedly influences the pathogenic process, the contribution of host factors cannot be ignored. In this sense, it is important to interpret our data in the context of a host model (G. mellonella) that lacks adaptive immunity (Wojda, 2017). This type of immune response is essential for the control of cryptococcosis, but it is not the only one involved in combating Cryptococcus (Rohatgi and Pirofski, 2015). Therefore, under our experimental conditions, the host's role in the pathogenic process was not fully evaluated. Nevertheless, since all isolates were investigated under the same conditions and comparatively analyzed according to their pathogenic potential, we believe it is experimentally valid to conclude that they display variable and diverse levels of virulence.

In our study, capsular architecture and molecular structure varied among the four isolates examined in greater detail, yet no clear correlation with virulence could be established. However, the hypovirulent isolates manifested higher xylose signals compared to hypervirulent strains. The role of xylosylation in cryptococcal virulence is not clear. In a recent study, the Uxt1 and Uxt2 proteins were characterized as transporters specific for the xylose donor, UDP-xylose (Li et al., 2018). Both proteins were required for xylose incorporation into the capsule and cryptococcal virulence. These results and those described in the present study do not correlate clearly, but they point to a key role of xylose in cryptococcal pathogenesis, which requires further experimental exploration. Urease activity, another well-established Cryptococcus virulence factor (Cox et al., 2000), was similar across all isolates (data not shown), leading us to rule out the hypothesis that classical virulence factors were responsible for the pathogenic diversity observed. We then shifted our focus to a broader molecular perspective and analyzed the cellular proteomes of strains R265hyper, Cn271hyper, Cg198hypo, and Cg366hypo. The proteomic profiles were highly diverse, but notably, virulent strains exhibited an enrichment of proteins involved in oxidative processes. As with other pathogens, Cryptococcus survival within the host depends on the ability to withstand oxidative stress (Zaragoza, 2019). Based on this, we hypothesized that enhanced resistance to oxidative responses might contribute to virulence. Supporting this idea, infections with the hypovirulent strains in the presence of ascorbic acid (an antioxidant) partially restored virulence. These findings reinforce the concept that Cryptococcus pathogenesis involves mechanisms beyond the classical virulence factors. However, the neutralization of oxidative responses cannot be universally attributed as a general pathogenic mechanism in the Cryptococcus genus. Our observation that infection of G. mellonella in the presence of ascorbic acid did not lead to increased virulence for all isolates tested supports this notion.

Altogether, our findings underscore the importance of studying Cryptococcus pathogenesis through the use of isolate collections rather than relying solely on standard reference strains. While reference isolates have been instrumental in advancing our understanding of fungal biology, they cannot capture the full extent of phenotypic and molecular diversity present within the genus. The use of a broader panel of clinical and environmental isolates revealed significant variability in virulence, classical virulence factor expression, proteomic profiles, and host interactions, none of which would have been apparent through the study of a single strain. As such, by embracing the natural diversity of Cryptococcus, to unravel the complex and multifactorial nature of its pathogenicity will be more likely, which will have direct implications for treatment strategies, and our broader understanding of fungal disease.

Materials and methods

Strains

For the present study, a total of six isolates of C. neoformans (Cn115, Cn161, Cn186, Cn271, Cn216, Cg366), three isolates of C. deuterogattii (Cg158, Cg188, Cg221), and one C. gattii strain (Cg198) were used. Their general properties are listed in Table 1. Additionally, the reference strains H99 (C. neoformans) and R265 (C. deuterogattii) were included for comparative purposes.

Virulence profiles in G. mellonella

Larvae maintained in our laboratory at 28 °C were divided into groups of 10, with a body weight ranging from 0.150 g to 0.250 g. For each experiment, the larvae were acclimated for 24 h before infection at 37 °C without food. On the day of the experiment, the larvae's prolegs were disinfected using a swab with 70 % alcohol, and one of the lower prolegs was infected with 1 × 106 fungal cells/larva (10 μL) of fungal suspension in PBS using a Hamilton syringe. As a control, a group of 10 larvae was injected with PBS only. The larvae were monitored daily for 10 days. Larvae were considered dead when they failed to respond to touch. Following the same procedures for grouping, acclimation, and disinfection, the larvae were infected with 1 × 106 cells/larva (10 μL) and then injected with either 10 μL of 0.5 M ascorbic acid or 10 μL of PBS. Control groups included larvae injected only with 0.5 M ascorbic acid or only with PBS. Monitoring was conducted as described in the previous section.

Capsule size

For in vitro assays, the isolates were cultured in YPD medium for 24 h, washed in PBS, and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde. For slide preparation, 3 μL of cells and 2 μL of India ink were placed on the slide. Images were captured using an optical microscope at 40× magnification. The capsule was measured using the ImageJ (https://imagej.net/ij/) software. For capsule determination in vivo, G. mellonella (three larvae per group) was infected as described before in this section. After 72 h of infection, an incision was made on the lateral side of each larva, and a drop of hemolymph was collected in an anticoagulant solution (26 mM sodium citrate, 30 mM citric acid, 100 mM glucose, 140 mM NaCl, pH adjusted to 4.11). The cells were collected by centrifugation and washed in PBS.

Evaluation of hemocyte-fungus interaction

After adjusting the isolates to an inoculum of 1 × 107 cells/mL, they were resuspended with 50 μL of Calcofluor White 25 μM (Invitrogen) and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. The cells were washed three times with PBS. Then, three larvae were infected with 1 × 106 cells/larva (10 μL) of fungus previously stained with Calcofluor White. The larvae were placed in an incubator at 37 °C for 3 h. Afterward, 1 mL of anticoagulant solution (Sodium citrate 26 mM, citric acid 30 mM, glucose 100 mM, NaCl 140 mM, pH adjusted to 4.11) (Li et al., 2021) was added to microtubes on ice, and a cut was made on the side of the larvae. One to four drops of hemolymph were collected. The microtubes were centrifuged at 400 ×g for 4 min. The cells were washed twice with anticoagulant buffer and then resuspended in 50 μL of a solution containing 4 % paraformaldehyde, 0.25 % Triton, and 0.165 μM 488 phalloidin for actin staining. After 20 min, 950 μL of PBS was added, and the samples were analyzed using a FACS Canto II for interaction quantification, controls were used, including hemocytes without labeling, hemocytes labeled with phalloidin, fungal cells without labeling, and fungal cells labeled with Calcofluor. Interactions were considered in the quadrant containing dual labeling.

Colony-Forming Unit (C.F.U.) evaluation

Following the infection protocol described in survival curve, two larvae from each group were incubated at 37 °C for 72 h. After this period, they were homogenized using a glass slide. The collected material was transferred to a 100 μm nylon filter and washed with 1 mL of PBS. Next, the cells were centrifuged and resuspended in 1 mL of PBS. Serial dilutions of 1:1000 and 1:2000 were prepared, and 100 μL of each dilution was plated onto Sabouraud agar plates containing 1× Penicillin-Streptomycin. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 48 h, followed by colony counting.

Determination of growth curves

The isolates were pre-cultured for 24 h in YPD (Yeast Peptone Dextrose) medium. Subsequently, the cells were washed with PBS and adjusted to a cell density of 2.5 × 105 cells/well (200 μL) in different media (YPD and Schneider). Optical density (OD) was measured at a wavelength of 530 nm every hour for 48 h at 37 °C using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1M2F).

Expression of antimicrobial peptides

Larvae were infected as described above, after 72 h, and one larva from each group was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen using a pestle. RNA was extracted by the TRIzol-chloroform method (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA was quantified and quality assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ThermoScientific, Loughborough, UK). RNA was normalized to 200 ng/μL. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Primers for β-actin, gallerimycin, and galiomicin were used as described before (Rossoni et al., 2017) (Bergin et al., 2006). Each sample was analyzed in duplicate by real-time PCR using a QuantStudio 3 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). The reaction was performed utilizing PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) with a final volume of 20 μL. The cycling parameters were set as follows: UDG activation at 50 °C for 2 min (1 cycle), dual-lock DNA polymerase activation at 90 °C for 2 min (1 cycle), followed by 40 amplification cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 58 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. At the end of the last cycle, a dissociation curve analysis was performed with the following parameters: 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and 95 °C for 15 s. Gene expression levels were normalized to the β-actin housekeeping gene and calculated relative to the expression of larvae inoculated with PBS solution, which was used as the calibrator. The relative gene expression analysis was performed using the 2^−ΔΔCT method, as described before (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Student's t-test was applied to compare gene expression in paired systems.

Capsule morphology analysis by scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

After larval infection, as described in section survival curve, the larvae were incubated for 48 h. Following this period, hemolymph was collected as detailed in section (In vivo assay). The cells were washed with PBS and centrifuged at 400 ×g. Next, the cells were resuspended in 1 mL of a fixative solution containing 2.5 % glutaraldehyde and 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.2) and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were then washed three times with a post-fixation solution (0.1 M sodium cacodylate, 0.2 M sucrose, and 2 mM MgCl2). Subsequently, the cells were adhered to 0.01 % poly-l-lysine-functionalized coverslips at room temperature for 30 min. The cells then underwent a dehydration series in ethanol: 30 %, 50 %, and 70 % for 5 min each, followed by 90 % and 100 % (twice) for 10 min each. Critical point drying was performed in a Leica EM CPD300 using 100 % ethanol and 10 exchanges with liquid CO2, followed by gold particles metallization and imaging under the electron microscope JEOL JSM-6010 Plus/LA with 5 kV acceleration.

Determination of glucuronoxylomannan (GXM) by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)

The GXM polysaccharide structure was determined using a combination of 2D NMR techniques (COSY, TOCSY, and NOESY), followed by an edited HSQC 1H/13C correlation map. The cryptococcal isolates were grown in YPD medium for 24 h. The cells were washed with PBS and adjusted to a concentration of 1×109 cells in 400 mL of minimal medium. The inoculum was incubated for 72 h at 30 °C and 200 rpm. After incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 15,000 ×g for 15 min. For GXM enrichment, the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.8 μm membrane (Nimrichter et al., 2007). Next, the supernatant was transferred to an Amicon system and filtered using 1, 10, and 100 kDa cutoff membranes. The polysaccharide retained on the membrane was collected in sterile H₂O and stored at −20 °C. GXM polysaccharides (5.0–2.5 mg) were dissolved in 0.5 mL of D2O and chemical shifts were referenced with TMSP-d4 (2,2,3,3-tetradeuterium-3-trimethylilsilylpropionate, δ = 0). Structural characterization was performed by NMR using the Bruker spectrometer AVANCE III 600 MHz TCI 5 mm N2 Prodigy. 1D and 2D NMR chemical shifts of 1H and 13C were determined at 343 K. The homonuclear correlation was determined by COSY, NOESY and TOCSY (mixing time 150 ms). 2D heteronuclear edited-HSQC were carried out to complete linkage analysis and total 1H/13C correlation map. 2D NMR experiments were recorded for quadrature detection in the indirect dimension and acquired using 8–64 scans per series of 1024 × 320 data points, with no zero filling in F1 (1024) prior to Fourier transformation (Sassaki et al., 2013). The 2D HSQC-ed NMR spectra of native capsular polysaccharides could only be acquired at a concentration of 5 mg/mL in D₂O at 343 K using a TCI-N2 Prodigy cryoprobe, due to the high viscosity and molecular weight of the GXM polysaccharides. These conditions were necessary to meet the NMR dynamic requirements for observing 1H signals, overcoming challenges from strong anisotropic effects and short T₂ relaxation times. To optimize sensitivity, adiabatic pulses and a sensitivity-edited HSQC were employed, enabling the characterization of the higher-order structure (HOS) of the polysaccharides in the liquid state.

Preparation of samples for proteomics

The hypervirulent and hypovirulent strains were cultured in YPD overnight at 30 °C with agitation at 200 rpm. YPD was chosen based on our recent experience of obtaining better yields of protein extraction after fungal growth in this medium (Castelli et al., 2025). The inocula were adjusted to a concentration of 1×107 cells/mL. The protocol was performed in biological triplicates. Cultures were then lyophilized for 18 h at 0.04 mBar and −50 °C. In the next step, fungal cells were subjected to lysis by adding 0.1 mm zirconium beads, followed by 10 vortex agitation cycles of 1 min each, interspersed with 1-min incubation on ice. Subsequently, 100 μL of 8 M urea was added to each pellet, followed by five additional cycles under the same conditions. After complete lysis samples were centrifuged at 18,000 g at 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatants were collected, and protein concentration was determined using Qubit fluorometric quantification (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). A total of 100 μg of protein extract was transferred to a new tube and reduced with Dithiothreitol (DTT) at a final concentration of 10 mM for 30 min at 60 °C. Samples were then cooled to room temperature and subjected to alkylation with 15 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) for 25 min in the dark at room temperature. To quench excess IAA, samples were incubated again in the dark with 5 mM DTT for 15 min. Proteins were digested overnight at 37 °C by adding trypsin at 0.5 μg/μL, in a 1:50 (w/w) enzyme-to-protein ratio.Following digestion, 0.5 % trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) (pH < 2) was added to each sample. Digested peptides were again quantified using Qubit fluorometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). The resulting peptides were desalted and concentrated using StageTips (Rappsilber et al., 2007).

Mass Spectrometry analysis

The methodology described in this section was performed by the Mass Spectrometry Platform at the Instituto Carlos Chagas - FIOCRUZ/PR and adapted from a previous study by our group (de Oliveira et al., 2024). Peptide separation and detection were carried out using a nano-liquid chromatography system (Ultimate 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Desalted peptide samples, analyzed in duplicates, were loaded into reverse-phase columns (30 cm long and 75 μm internal diameter), packed with ReprosilPur C18 Acqua (Dr. Maisch) as the stationary phase, featuring 3 μm diameter beads and 120 Å pores. Peptide elution was performed using a chromatographic gradient, gradually increasing phase B (95 % acetonitrile in 0.1 % formic acid) from 5 % to 40 % over 120 minutesThe eluate was directed coupled to Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Chromatography gradient and acquisition parameters were managed using Xcalibur 4 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Instrument settings included a spray voltage of 2.6 kV, capillary temperature maintained at 250 °C, zero flow of carrier or support gases, automatic gain control (AGC) enabled, and radiofrequency level on the S-lens set at 70 %. Mass spectra acquisition was performed in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, allowing for a full MS scan alternated with MS/MS (MS2) acquisition, with dynamic ion exclusion for 60 s. The first scan was conducted with a resolution of 60,000 at 200 m/z for 2-s, where the most intense ions were selected, and singly charged ions (1+) were excluded. Subsequently, the selected ions underwent fragmentation via High-Energy Collision Dissociation (HCD) with normalized energy set to 30 and an analysis resolution of 15,000 at 200 m/z.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using the PatternLab for Proteomics V software [available at http://www.patternlabforproteomics.org/; (Santos et al., 2022)]. The data was preprocessed with the Y.A.D.A. 3.0 deconvolution algorithm to enable multiplexed spectra identification (Clasen et al., 2023; Clasen et al., 2022). Comet 2021 search engine (Eng et al., 2015), which is embedded into PLV, was used for identifying the mass spectra. At this stage, organism-specific databases were loaded into the program. For the C. neoformans group, the database was obtained from the UniProt platform (http://www.uniprot.org), using data from C. neoformans var. neoformans serotype D (strain JEC21/ATCC MYA-565) - organism ID: 214684. For the C. deuterogattii/gattii group, protein data were retrieved from the FungiDB platform (https://fungidb.org/fungidb/app) using data from C. gattii VGII R265. Validation was conducted using the Search Engine Processor (SEPro)(Carvalho et al., 2012), and quantification was performed based on Extracted Ion Chromatograms (XIC). The analyses focused on proteins identified with at least two unique peptides, excluding contaminants and decoys. The final data were processed using two modules available in PatternLab for Proteomics V: the Venn Diagram (Approximately Area-Proportional Venn Diagram) and T-Fold, both filtered to include a minimum of two technical replicates. Additional analyses of protein interactions were conducted using STRING software (https://string-db.org/). The 2,000 most abundant proteins were sorted from the highest to the lowest total signal.

Data availability

The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository (Perez-Riverol et al., 2025) with the dataset identifier PXD065863.

Comparative genomics

Raw whole genome sequencing data was downloaded from the Sequence Read Archive (NCBI) for the isolate Cg198 of C. gattii (accession code SRR13742189), and two isolates of C. neoformans, Cn271 and Cg366 (accession codes SRR13742177 and SRR13742185, respectively). Reads were quality trimmed and adapters were removed with fastp (Chen et al., 2018) using default parameters. After processing, filtered paired-end reads were assembled with SPAdes (Prjibelski et al., 2020) with default parameters. BUSCO 5 (Manni et al., 2021) was used for assessing assembly completeness, with the Basidiomycota orthologs dataset (basidiomycota_odb10). Genome sequences were then annotated with BRAKER3 (Gabriel et al., 2024) using as extrinsic evidence the proteome of C. neoformans (strain JEC21) available at GenBank (accession code GCA_000091045.1).

OrthoFinder (Emms and Kelly, 2019) was then employed for comparing the predicted protein sequences for the three isolates and C. deuterogattii R265, the latter obtained from GenBank (accession code GCA_003011995.1). This pipeline infers orthologs and determines their respective orthogroups, as well as exclusive proteins, among proteomes. The proteome of C. depauperatus (isolate CBS 7855 – accession code GCA_001720195.2) was added to serve as outgroup for this analysis. The proteomes were also submitted to the eggNOG-mapper tool using default parameters (Cantalapiedra et al., 2021) for domain prediction and annotation of Gene Ontologies. Functional enrichment analysis was performed for exclusive genes with topGO package (Alexa and Rahnenfuhrer, 2021) in R 4.1.2 (R Core Team 2021). Lastly, the filtered reads of Cg198 were aligned to the genome of C. deuterogattii R265 (accession no. GCF_002954075.1), while Cn271 and Cg366 were aligned to C. neoformans H99 (GCF_000149245.1) with BWA-MEM (Li 2013). Alignments were filtered with samtools (Danecek et al., 2021) for a minimum quality of 30 (flag –q). Genetic variants were then annotated with freebayes (Garrison and Marth, 2012) and snpEff (Cingolani et al., 2012) for predicting putative effects of single nucleotide polymorphisms on coding genes. The two-proportion Z-test was applied for statistical comparisons at the genome levels.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Infected hemocytes (%) were compared among strains by one-way ANOVA; when the ANOVA F test for the overall effect of strain was significant, pairwise comparisons were assessed using Tukey's post hoc multiple comparisons test. For each strain, the mean capsule size before and after 72 h of infection were compared using a Student's t-test. Multiple comparison correction was performed using the Bonferroni method, with a p-value set at 0.0041. Spearman correlation coefficient (ρ) was estimated at 3- and 5-days post-infection to quantify associations between virulence (survival rate) and capsule size (μm; average), fungal viability (C.F.U./ml) and infected hemocytes (%); p-values were obtained from Spearman's test and were corrected for multiple comparison using the Bonferroni method. Data collected for correlation analysis included average values of G.mellonella survival, capsule size and fungal load (C.F.U./mL). Survival curves were compared using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test.
