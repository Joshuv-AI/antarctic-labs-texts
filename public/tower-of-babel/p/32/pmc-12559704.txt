Machine learning analysis of carbon rebound effect dynamics and drivers in Chinese prefecture-level cities
Authors: Shunyi Li, Xingxing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As global attention to climate change intensifies, China, as the world’s largest energy consumer and carbon emitter, faces the dual challenge of improving energy efficiency while the carbon rebound effect (CRE) counteracts efforts to reduce emissions. Based on panel data from Chinese prefecture-level cities from 2010 to 2021. It combines multiple linear regression with machine learning (ensemble and deep learning) to uncover the nonlinear drivers of CRE. Machine learning models capture high-dimensional interactions and threshold effects. SHAP values and ALE plots are employed to quantify the impact pathways of key variables, overcoming the limitations of traditional models. This study employs multiple linear regression, ensemble learning, and deep learning methods, combined with interpretable SHAP values and ALE charts, to systematically identify the core driving mechanisms behind the carbon rebound effect. The main conclusions of this paper are as follows: (1) The CRE exhibits an overall “M”-shaped fluctuation trend, with a spatial pattern of “East > Central > West” and “North > South”; (2) Machine learning models accurately identify DEI, HTD, ER, Water, and UIS2 as the key drivers of CRE. These factors also exhibit significant and consistent threshold effects. This study provides a scientific basis for the formulation of differentiated ecological policies.

Introduction

Global climate change is intensifying, and countries are prioritizing the achievement of carbon neutrality targets as core strategies to address the climate crisis1. In this pursuit, the carbon rebound effect (CRE) has become a key obstacle. CRE refers to the phenomenon in which, after energy efficiency improvements, carbon emissions rebound due to increased economic activity, thus undermining the reduction in emissions achieved through efficiency gains2. According to the International Energy Agency3, developing countries, due to technological lag and weak policy enforcement, exhibit higher CRE intensity than developed nations, with some regions caught in a vicious cycle of “efficiency improvement-demand rebound.” As the world’s largest emitter of carbon, China exhibits significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity in CRE across prefecture-level cities. The CRE evolution paths in the northeastern heavy industrial regions and the eastern digital economy belt are distinctly different4,5, highlighting the urgent need for a systematic analysis of the driving mechanisms behind CRE. The “14th Five-Year Plan” in China sets a binding target of reducing CO2 emissions per unit of GDP by 18%. It aims to strengthen emission reduction pathways through national carbon market pilots and industrial restructuring. However, current policies focus primarily on carbon intensity control, overlooking the driving mechanisms behind CRE. Factors such as extreme weather events and the nonlinear threshold effects of the digital economy on CRE are not adequately considered, undermining the effectiveness of these policies. This paper addresses the following research questions: What are the patterns of spatiotemporal evolution of CRE in Chinese prefecture-level cities? How do key driving factors such as the digital economy, extreme climate, and environmental regulations affect CRE, and do they exhibit significant threshold effects?

Extensive research on CRE has been conducted both domestically and internationally. Existing studies on the spatiotemporal evolution of CRE indicate that regional differences are mainly driven by factors such as industrial and energy structures, with clear spatial agglomeration characteristics6,7. However, there is limited research at the prefecture level. Existing research on the drivers of CRE has primarily focused on single-factor analyses, such as energy prices and technological advancements8–10. There has been insufficient consideration of the interplay between new factors, such as the digital economy and extreme weather conditions, and traditional environmental regulations, with limited exploration of potential nonlinear and threshold effects on CRE. Traditional econometric models face several limitations in addressing the complex mechanisms of CRE, particularly in handling numerous nonlinear relationships and intricate interactions11,12. Given the gaps in existing research on prefecture-level analysis, multiple-factor considerations, and nonlinear threshold effects, this paper employs machine learning and the TOE framework to systematically explore the spatiotemporal evolution of CRE and the complex interactions between its driving factors.

The innovations and contributions of this paper are as follows: First, it refines the framework for identifying the drivers of CRE and enhances the scientific basis for ecological policymaking. Previous studies often suffered from fragmented or incomplete identification of influencing factors, making it difficult to accurately understand the underlying mechanisms of CRE. This study systematically examines the structure of urban systems to identify key drivers. It then further explores their mechanisms of influence, aiming to construct a more comprehensive and dynamic analytical framework. This framework provides a solid theoretical and empirical foundation for formulating sound and effective carbon reduction policies. Second, the study clarifies the interaction between CRE and multiple socioeconomic–environmental subsystems. It investigates the combined impacts of digital transformation, environmental regulation, and climate pressure on CRE. Previous research rarely incorporated these key variables into a unified analytical system, which often led to fragmented policy recommendations. Using a sample of 278 prefecture-level cities in China, this study employs machine learning-based quantitative methods to uncover the nonlinear and interactive effects of major drivers. In parallel, it conducts qualitative analysis to explain spatial heterogeneity in CRE and to identify contextual factors and differentiated mechanisms. It identifies policy-sensitive pathways. The quantitative analysis reveals the structural causes of CRE, while the qualitative findings propose differentiated policy strategies tailored to regional development contexts. This paper consists of six sections. The first introduces the background, significance, and research questions related to CRE. The second reviews the literature, summarizing the spatiotemporal differentiation, influencing factors, and research methods related to CRE. The third outlines the research methodology, introducing a method for quantifying CRE based on changes in carbon emission efficiency, utilizing the TOE framework to select feature variables, and applying machine learning techniques such as random forests and GBRT to build the model. It further employs SHAP and ALE plots to interpret the model and examine nonlinear relationships between variables. The fourth presents the results and analysis, exploring the spatiotemporal evolution of CRE across Chinese prefecture-level cities, its regional differentiation, and the nonlinear mechanisms and threshold effects of key driving factors. The fifth discusses the advantages of machine learning methods in overcoming the limitations of traditional econometric models and suggests directions for future research. The sixth concludes the paper and offers targeted policy recommendations. As shown in Fig. 1.

Literature review

Spatiotemporal differentiation of CRE

Existing studies on the spatiotemporal differentiation of the CRE have mainly focused on the national or provincial scale, highlighting the impacts of industrial structure, energy transitions, and policy cycles on CRE13. For example, Chen and14 note that CRE in China exhibits a “core-periphery” spatial pattern, with lower CRE values in eastern regions due to faster industrial upgrading15. International comparisons show that in developed countries, CRE is primarily driven by energy price elasticity, while in developing countries, technological lag and fragmented policy implementation result in higher CRE intensity16. However, existing studies have notable limitations: first, they rely heavily on provincial data, neglecting heterogeneity at the prefecture level. For instance17, identify periodic fluctuations in provincial-level CRE, but fail to capture the “M-shaped” temporal pattern at the prefecture level. Second, there has been insufficient attention to the interaction between climate factors and the digital economy, particularly the amplifying effect of extreme weather (e.g., extreme cold) on CRE in resource-based cities in northern China18. Third, there is a lack of systematic analysis on how local policy dynamics, such as carbon market pilot programs, reshape CRE through technological adoption pathways, limiting the practical implications of policy insights.

In summary, while existing studies have provided a preliminary depiction of the spatiotemporal differentiation of CRE, their reliance on coarse data granularity and limited integration of variables makes it difficult to uncover the fine-grained mechanisms at the prefecture level.

Drivers of CRE

The mechanism driving CRE is multidimensional and complex, with key drivers spanning technology, organization, and the environment. Existing studies show that improvements in energy efficiency, followed by rebounds in economic activity, are closely related to the “Jevons Paradox”19–21. At the technological level, both clean energy innovation and digital technologies have bidirectional effects on CRE: green patents may indirectly increase carbon emissions through stimulating economic growth22,23, while the digital economy index significantly reduces CRE by optimizing energy management24. The structure of energy23 sources is also critical: a higher share of fossil fuels exacerbates CRE rebound intensity, while an increase in the share of renewable energy can effectively reduce CRE by lowering carbon intensity25,26. At the organizational level, corporate ESG practices and government environmental regulations can mitigate the rebound effect of technological innovation through policy synergies27,28, though excessive reliance on fiscal investment may increase pressure on carbon emissions29. At the economic environment level, financial agglomeration and openness to trade significantly affect CRE: financial concentration may accelerate the expansion of high-carbon industries30,31, while an open economy can suppress CRE through technology spillover effects32,33. At the social environment level, urbanization and population size drive CRE through scale effects on energy demand34,35, but increased public environmental awareness (e.g., the pollution search index) can partially offset the rebound effect through low-carbon behavioral choices36,37. At the environmental level, extreme climate events significantly drive CRE by affecting energy demand and ecological carbon sequestration capacity38,39, while the spatial distribution of land use types further shapes the dynamic response of regional carbon emissions40. At the industrial structure level, industrial upgrading reduces CRE by increasing the share of services41, but industrial relocation may shift carbon emission pressures to less developed regions42,43. The study also reveals significant threshold effects in these factors, such as a sharp increase in CRE when number of extreme low-temperature days exceeds a critical value, suggesting that nonlinear relationships are key to understanding CRE dynamics44.

In summary, while previous studies have identified multiple drivers of CRE, they are limited by isolated analyses that fail to fully integrate the Technology-Organization-Environment (TOE) framework and overlook the threshold effects of key variables.

CRE research methods

The analytical framework for CRE has shifted from traditional econometric models to a blend of machine learning and interpretability techniques. Early studies relied on dynamic spatial Durbin models45,46 and LMDI decomposition47,48, but their linear assumptions failed to capture the nonlinear effects of the multidimensional technology-organization-environment (TOE) factors49,50. In recent years, machine learning methods have significantly improved modeling accuracy. Random forests and XGBoost, through ensemble learning, effectively capture complex interactions in high-dimensional data51,52, with R2 values reaching 0.98, far surpassing traditional regression models53. Deep learning (e.g., FNN) further optimizes the forecasting ability for non-stationary time series54,55. The introduction of interpretability techniques (e.g., SHAP values, ALE plots) has quantified the threshold effects and contributions of key variables, such as Digital Economy Index and the Number of Extreme Low-Temperature Days56,57, revealing the dual effects of environmental regulation: its incentivizing role at low intensity and its suppressive effect at high intensity58. This methodological innovation not only addresses the limitations of traditional models but also provides dynamic decision-making support for differentiated ecological policy development59. Recent studies have shown that machine learning is increasingly applied in energy policy, carbon emissions modeling, and environmental regulation. For example60,61, used long short-term memory (LSTM) models to predict carbon emissions and found that these models outperformed traditional approaches such as backpropagation neural networks (BPNN) and Gaussian process regression (GPR). In addition62,63, proposed a novel grey forecasting model, V-GM(1, N), to estimate carbon emissions in China’s cement industry, achieving an accuracy rate of 97.29%. These studies suggest that machine learning can not only improve predictive performance but also provide strong support for evidence-based policymaking.

In summary, traditional methods are limited by their linear assumptions and limited capacity to handle high-dimensional data, making them inadequate for analyzing the complex mechanisms of CRE.

Selection of features using the TOE framework

To systematically identify the complex drivers of CRE, this study adopts the Technology–Organization–Environment (TOE) framework proposed by Tornatzky et al. (1990). This framework has been widely applied in areas such as technology adoption and sustainable transitions25,67. It emphasizes the joint influence of technological advancement, organizational behavior, and the external environment on systemic transformation. Compared with traditional linear approaches to identifying CRE drivers, the TOE framework offers a multidimensional and interactive paradigm. It enables a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms and impact pathways behind CRE.

① Technical Factors.

The technical dimension focuses on the dual impact of technological progress and digital transformation on the CRE. On one hand, digital technologies can reduce carbon emission intensity by optimizing energy allocation and improving utilization efficiency68 showing significant potential for emission reduction. This study introduces the Digital Economy Index as a representative indicator to measure the penetration of urban digitalization in energy management. On the other hand, following the “Jevons Paradox,” technological advancements may also lead to carbon rebound by enhancing economic efficiency and stimulating production and consumption activities. Energy consumption intensity, measured by energy use per unit of GDP, is a core indicator of technological efficiency, while the number of green patent applications reflects the level of green technological innovation and its industrialization. Both have indirect effects on either mitigating or amplifying the CRE.

② Organizational Dimension.

The organizational dimension focuses on the behavioral responses of enterprises, governments, and social organizations in carbon governance. Environmental regulation intensity is a key variable. It imposes pressure on firms through policy instruments such as carbon taxes and emission trading schemes. These policy effects often exhibit threshold effects and nonlinear characteristics69. ESG performance scores reflect firm-level commitment to environmental, social, and governance responsibilities. The intensity of fiscal investment reflects government support for green technologies and low-carbon industries. However, it may also generate negative effects by unintentionally supporting high-carbon industries due to misallocated investments. The Public Environmental Participation Index, as an indicator of environmental awareness, can enhance policy feedback mechanisms and impose indirect pressure on firms and governments through public oversight.

③ Environmental Dimension.

The environmental dimension covers multiple aspects, including natural, economic, social, and industrial structure factors. Extreme climate conditions, such as the number of very hot or cold days, can significantly alter energy demand patterns and thus affect carbon emission levels. Land use types affect the region’s carbon sink capacity and act as indirect moderators of CRE. The level of urbanization and population size affect energy consumption through agglomeration and scale effects. These factors alter both structure and total consumption. In addition, the degree of financial agglomeration and trade openness indicates the openness and capacity for innovation diffusion in the external urban environment. The share of high-carbon industries (e.g., the proportion of secondary industries) indicates path dependence in the urban industrial structure and is positively associated with CRE70,71.

④ Interaction Mechanisms Among the Three Dimensions.

Unlike the dominant “single-variable–single-path” paradigm in existing literature, this study emphasizes the joint influence of TOE interactions on CRE. Regarding the interaction between technology and organization, the effective implementation of digital technologies depends on firms’ ESG governance capacity and the enforcement strength of government environmental regulations. If firms lack the willingness to transition, technological gains may not lead to energy savings. Instead, they may drive capacity expansion and intensify CRE.Regarding the interaction between organization and environment, the effectiveness of environmental regulation is significantly constrained by the region’s level of economic development and industrial structure. For example, in regions with a high concentration of high-carbon industries, stringent regulations may trigger industrial relocation, leading to regional spillover effects of CRE. Regarding the interaction between technology and environment, the marginal effect of technological efficiency is moderated by both natural and economic conditions. For instance, in high-temperature areas, the greater sensitivity to energy-saving technologies may amplify the extent of their influence on CRE.

In summary, the TOE framework not only provides a theoretical basis for systematically identifying the drivers of CRE, but also highlights the dynamic interactions among the three dimensions. This approach expands the theoretical boundaries of existing CRE research and enhances the coherence and relevance of policy recommendations.

Ensemble learning

① Random Forest and GBRT.

To identify the optimal model, this study evaluates model performance using R2, mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean square error (RMSE), following the approach of53. The dataset was split into 80% for training and 20% for testing. Five-fold cross-validation was used to assess model robustness and generalization capacity, helping reduce the risk of overfitting due to random splits. Ensemble learning offers significant theoretical advantages. RF reduces variance and overfitting risk by aggregating multiple decision trees, making it suitable for high-dimensional data and nonlinear relationships72. GBRT iteratively minimizes residuals through gradient boosting, capturing threshold effects and variable interactions73.RF and GBRT achieved the highest R2 values (RF: 0.946; GBRT: 0.919) and the lowest error metrics (RF RMSE: 0.215; MAE: 0.022), outperforming both traditional linear models and other nonlinear models such as Support Vector Machine (SVM) and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN). Although neural networks and XGBoost also showed strong predictive performance (e.g., XGBoost: R2 = 0.947), they are computationally intensive and less interpretable due to their “black-box” nature, limiting their policy relevance.In contrast, RF and GBRT offer a strong balance between predictive accuracy, generalization ability, and interpretability, and were therefore selected as the core models in this study.

② Model Training and Hyperparameter Optimization.

All models in this study were trained and evaluated using Python 3.12.4 and the scikit-learn 1.4 framework. To further improve model performance, hyperparameter tuning was conducted for the core models, RF and GBRT.For Random Forest, grid search was used to optimize hyperparameters. The final configuration set the number of decision trees at 100 and the maximum depth at 44, balancing model complexity with generalization ability.For GBRT, Bayesian optimization was applied. The final settings selected a learning rate of 0.1 and 200 boosting iterations.

Under these configurations, RF and GBRT performed well in cross-validation and showed strong predictive accuracy and stability. These results highlight their effectiveness in handling nonlinear relationships and high-dimensional data, supporting their selection as the primary models in this study.

Results and discussion

Spatiotemporal pattern analysis results

In terms of temporal dynamics, the CRE across Chinese prefecture-level cities ranged from 0.013 to 0.35, with an average value of approximately 0.187. This suggests that a portion of the emission reductions achieved through improved carbon efficiency were offset, leaving a significant gap from the national carbon reduction targets. As shown in Fig. 2, the CRE exhibits cyclical fluctuations aligned with carbon intensity constraint policies, exhibiting a distinct “M-shaped” pattern. This pattern can be divided into four phases: Phase 1 (2010–2013): In 2011, China introduced its first binding carbon intensity targets in the “12th Five-Year Plan for Controlling Greenhouse Gas Emissions.” However, this period represented the early stages of economic restructuring. High-emission industries still prevailed, market demand remained strong, and market mechanisms were underdeveloped. Consequently, firms lacked motivation for energy conservation and emissions reduction. The CRE was relatively high—0.03 in 2010, rising to 0.36 in 2011, slightly declining to 0.22 in 2012, and climbing again to 0.33 in 2013Phase 2 (2013–2015): The State Council released the “Action Plan for Energy Conservation, Emission Reduction, and Low-Carbon Development (2014–2015).” This strengthened policy enforcement and regulatory oversight, prompting firms to invest more in low-carbon technologies and to improve energy efficiency. As a result, CRE dropped rapidly—from 0.33 in 2013 to 0.07 in 2014, and further to 0.03 in 2015. Phase 3 (2015–2018): The “13th Five-Year Plan” introduced stricter binding targets and piloted a national carbon trading system. Early policies mainly targeted high-emission industries. As implementation deepened, reasonable carbon demands from enterprises gradually emerged, and emerging industries fostered new growth. Consequently, the CRE rebounded from 0.03 in 2015 to 0.19 in 2016, 0.21 in 2017, and peaked at 0.33 in 2018. Phase 4 (2018–2021): The State Council issued the “13th Five-Year Plan for Controlling Greenhouse Gas Emissions” to reinforce policies, encouraging firms to adopt low-carbon technologies and optimize energy structures. CRE was 0.33 in 2018 and declined to 0.08 in 2019, slightly rose to 0.12 in 2020 due to economic recovery and demand fluctuations induced by the pandemic, and dropped sharply to 0.01 in 2021. During the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, lockdowns and containment measures temporarily reduced emissions. However, these reductions were not driven by structural changes or technological progress. As economic activity resumed, carbon demand rebounded. The sharp drop in CRE in 2021 reflects not only the continued effect of policy enforcement but also increased adaptability among enterprises. The pandemic also heightened awareness of sustainability and green transformation, accelerating the implementation of related policy measures.

In terms of the spatial pattern of CRE, Fig. 3 illustrates the dynamic changes in the CRE levels of Chinese prefecture-level cities from 2010 to 2021 using a color gradient ranging from dark green to dark blue. The levels are categorized as negative rebound (< 0), partial rebound (0–1), mild backfire (1–2), moderate backfire (2–5), and severe backfire (> 5)74,75. In 2010, the negative rebound effects were prominent in traditional industrial cities in Northeast China, such as Qinhuangdao (−1.868) and Handan (−1.174), due to post-crisis demand contraction in steel and chemical sectors, which led to a passive reduction in carbon emissions. From 2013 to 2015, partial rebound effects emerged in central and western cities like Chengdu (−1.276) and Chongqing (0.375), where the introduction of energy-saving equipment coincided with industrial expansion, causing emissions to grow more slowly than economic output. Between 2015 and 2018, coastal high-tech zones experienced mild backfire effects. Cities like Shenzhen (−1.735 → 0.331) and Dongguan (1.178) saw emissions increase to more than 1.5 times the expected reductions, mainly due to reliance on traditional power grids by data centers and similar industries. From 2018 to 2021, resource-based cities showed moderate backfire effects. For instance, in Lüliang (0.062), expansion of coal chemical capacity combined with low coverage of clean technology led to emission growth that exceeded expected reductions by 3.2 times. Special cases of severe backfire effects include Zhangzhou (2010: 8.662) and Fushun (2012: 32.671), where carbon emissions surged due to the relocation of high-energy industries or commissioning of petrochemical projects.

From a spatial evolutionary perspective, a core-periphery gradient shift is observed. Core cities in the Yangtze River Delta maintained low CRE through upgrading of the service sector, while surrounding cities that received relocated industries experienced CRE peaks. Geographic constraints and policy interventions also played significant roles. For example, Lijiang in the mountainous Southwest reduced CRE through hydropower development, while Yichun in the Northeast maintained stable levels through forestry transformation. In post-disaster reconstruction cases, such as Quanzhou in Fujian, the use of green building materials helped avoid a severe backfire, indicating the effectiveness of policy measures.

Figure 3 shows pronounced regional disparities. Economically developed areas sustained low CRE through technological upgrading, while resource-dependent cities and cities receiving industrial relocation faced more serious rebound effects. In terms of temporal dynamics, the national average CRE declined from 0.187 in 2010 to 0.115 in 2021. However, coal-dependent cities in Shanxi and Inner Mongolia remain a key challenge for emission reduction. Therefore, stronger regional coordination mechanisms are needed, alongside capacity constraints and clean technology subsidies targeted at cities with high backfire effects.

To identify and eliminate multicollinear variables and avoid information redundancy or interpretive bias during model fitting, a systematic Pearson correlation analysis was conducted for all candidate features (see Fig. 3). This approach reduces the risk of multicollinearity while preserving the theoretical and empirical dimensions necessary for interpretation, thus improving model robustness and explanatory power (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Discussion on the spatiotemporal distribution of CRE

Based on a detailed analysis of temporal trends, this study finds a dynamic offset between emission reductions driven by intensity decline and increases driven by economic growth. This offset follows a downward but fluctuating trend. Compared with the findings of Wu et al. (2018), the CRE patterns identified in this study are more stable overall but also exhibit greater short-term volatility.From a spatial perspective, the distribution of CRE varies significantly over time across urban agglomerations. Some cities exhibit weak or strong rebound effects, while others show no rebound or even suppressive effects. The relationship between efficiency gains and CRE is not uniform across regions. External shocks, such as the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, caused deviations in the carbon intensity trend. These disruptions led to substantial heterogeneity in the eventual rebound effects across urban clusters, further highlighting the complexity of CRE.Future research should investigate how regional differences in industrial structure, energy consumption, and growth models influence the spatiotemporal patterns of CRE. This would help deepen our understanding of how regional economic characteristics shape CRE outcomes. Comparative studies across regions should also explore intra-urban cluster variation and its underlying drivers. These insights can support the development of more targeted regional emission reduction policies.In addition, long-term tracking of external shocks is needed to evaluate both the short- and long-term impacts of such disruptions on CRE. This would help improve policy responses to uncertainty. However, while this study identifies the spatiotemporal features of CRE, it does not fully explain the underlying mechanisms. It lacks a systematic analysis of interregional interactions and feedback effects. Moreover, the external shock analysis mainly focuses on the 2020 pandemic. Future research should expand to include a broader range of case studies to better assess the effects of various unexpected events on CRE.

Discussion on the drivers of CRE

This study uses machine learning models to explore the relationship between efficiency improvements and CRE, indicating a potential decoupling between the two. DEI, HTD, ER, Water, and UIS2 are identified as the main drivers of CRE. These factors show significant threshold effects. Compared to traditional regression models, machine learning techniques such as Random Forest and XGBoost better capture nonlinear relationships among variables. These models uncover the complex mechanisms of key drivers and offer more precise tools for dynamic CRE analysis.

Future research can be extended in the following directions: First, it is important to deepen the analysis of the pathways and mechanisms through which core variables—particularly DEI and ER—affect CRE. This includes clarifying both direct and indirect effects, as well as examining heterogeneous impacts across different regions and industrial contexts to support more targeted regional emission reduction strategies.Second, based on the threshold features identified by machine learning, future studies could explore how the marginal effects of drivers on CRE vary across different threshold intervals. This would help identify ways to optimize variable configurations through policy adjustments to mitigate CRE.Third, the combination of modeling approaches could be expanded by exploring the applicability of various machine learning algorithms (e.g., LSTM, CatBoost) in CRE analysis. It is also worth considering the integration of these models with causal inference methods (e.g., structural equation models, causal forests) to enhance both explanatory power and policy relevance.

Moreover, although this study identifies key variables, some potential influencing factors may still be omitted. Future research should appropriately expand the set of variables to include socio-economic factors such as financial agglomeration, trade openness, and urbanization level. This would allow for a systematic assessment of their roles in the CRE formation mechanism. Notably, CRE exhibits significant variation across regions. Future studies could establish a multi-regional comparative analysis framework to explore the causes of regional differences and carbon emission transfer pathways, thereby revealing the potential and constraints for coordinated regional emission reductions.

Finally, to promote more comprehensive research development, it is recommended to further analyze the interactions between CRE and other environmental or socio-economic variables, such as air quality, economic development, and social equity. This would provide empirical support for the formulation of multi-objective environmental governance policies. In terms of research design, a machine learning-based CRE prediction system could be developed. Combined with simulation techniques, this system would evaluate the effects of regional emission reduction policies and support local governments in achieving more scientific, dynamic, and precise carbon management.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the analysis is based on data from Chinese prefecture-level cities between 2010 and 2021, which may not fully capture longer-term trends or recent structural shifts in energy and digital policy. Second, while machine learning models offer strong predictive performance, they remain inherently correlational and do not establish causal relationships. Although interpretability tools (SHAP and ALE) are employed to illuminate variable contributions, the mechanisms identified should be further validated through causal methods or experimental designs. Third, the variable selection, though grounded in the TOE framework, may not encompass all relevant factors—such as behavioral elements, international market fluctuations, or sub-city-level heterogeneity—that could influence CRE. Future research could extend this work by incorporating dynamic temporal modeling (e.g., LSTMs or transformer-based approaches), integrating more granular data, or applying causal machine learning techniques to better identify policy-sensitive pathways.

In the comparative modeling exercise, ensemble learning models demonstrated higher predictive accuracy for CRE compared to ordinary linear regression on the available dataset. Among the models tested, the GBRT algorithm showed the strongest performance, with a cross-validated R2 of 0.98, compared to 0.85 for the linear regression model. This suggests that machine learning approaches can effectively capture complex, non-linear patterns in CRE data.

The findings of this study provide evidence and support for the formulation of China’s carbon reduction policies, particularly in addressing the rebound effect. The methodology and insights may also offer valuable perspectives for other economies facing similar challenges, especially those undergoing rapid digital transformation and industrialization. The differential patterns observed between developed and developing regions highlight the potential value of international cooperation and technology transfer in achieving global carbon reduction goals.

Policy recommendation

First, region-specific emission reduction mechanisms should be implemented. In eastern regions, the integration of the digital economy with green technologies should be strengthened. For instance, in Hangzhou, promoting a “Digital Energy Efficiency Management Platform” is essential. This platform enables real-time monitoring of the CRE in high-energy-consuming firms and provides data support for dynamic energy strategy adjustments, thereby reducing carbon emissions. In northern heavy-industry cities, the implementation of a “Low-Carbon Winter Heating Plan” is a key strategy. For example, in Fushun, the government should mandate the use of geothermal heat pumps and industrial waste heat recovery systems. A dedicated subsidy fund should be established to cover 40% of installation costs for eligible enterprises or communities adopting geothermal systems. Additionally, firms utilizing waste heat recovery technologies should receive financial rewards based on the amount of recovered heat. This measure could help replace traditional coal-based heating and reduce emissions. In the southwestern mountainous areas, expanding hydropower subsidies is particularly important. In Lijiang, the government may provide a 30% construction subsidy for rural small hydropower projects. The subsidy criteria and application procedures must be clearly defined to ensure that eligible projects receive one-time support. At the same time, a strict negative list for industrial access should be enforced to prohibit the expansion of energy-intensive industries in the region, thereby protecting local ecosystems.

Second, promote data-driven policy optimization. Establishing a “CRE Dynamic Monitoring Platform” is an essential foundation for targeted policy optimization. The platform’s construction involves four steps: First, the government should take the lead in setting data standards and regulatory rules.Second, universities and research institutes should develop machine learning models, such as random forests, for CRE forecasting.Third, enterprises should deploy IoT devices to collect key variables such as DEI, HTD, and ER, and upload the data in real time.Fourth, based on the model’s predictions, the platform should dynamically adjust carbon quota policies. For example, if a carbon rebound is predicted, the carbon quota for the relevant city should be reduced by 5%.The necessary investment in hardware, software, and personnel training can be supported by a dedicated fund.The platform should also be seamlessly integrated with existing carbon markets and energy consumption monitoring systems to form a closed-loop mechanism of “monitoring–early warning–response–evaluation,” thereby enhancing policy foresight and flexibility.

Third, address extreme climate risks (HTD).In cold cities (e.g., Harbin, Greater Khingan Mountains), a coordinated “climate warning–emergency emission reduction” mechanism should be established.During extreme cold events, production limits should be imposed on enterprises.Smart grids should prioritize residential energy use to reduce the risk of sudden carbon emission spikes.

Fourth, implement dynamic environmental regulation.In low-ER cities (e.g., Lüliang), policies such as environmental tax reductions and green credit incentives should be used to promote emission-reduction investments.In high-ER cities (e.g., Shenzhen), carbon allowance auctions can be introduced, with the revenues allocated to a decarbonization subsidy fund to support industrial technological upgrades.

Fifth, implement zoned management based on water-related characteristics.In low-water regions such as Inner Mongolia, efforts should focus on promoting the transition toward water-saving industries.In Henan, wetland restoration and water resource monitoring should be strengthened.In Zhejiang, the ecological protection redline should be strictly enforced, ensuring a balance between hydropower generation and ecological flow maintenance.

Sixth, promote industrial collaboration and transformation.A low-carbon supply chain collaboration platform should be established in the Yangtze River Delta to promote direct green power supply and interregional cooperation.Resource-based cities (e.g., Lüliang and Fushun) can attract low-carbon industries through “capacity replacement and technology compensation” policies and provide equipment purchase subsidies to support green transformation.
