Developing Synthetic Orthopantomogram Datasets Through Generative Models
Authors: Niha Adnan, Syed Muhammad Faizan Ahmed, Fahad Umer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo generate synthetic Orthopantomograms (OPGs) using generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) models and to evaluate the realism of synthetic images through assessments by humans as well as AI models.MethodologyThis study conducted at Aga Khan University Hospital (AKUH) aimed to generate synthetic OPGs using anonymized images from the existing database at AKUH dental clinics. Additionally, OPGs from the Tufts Dental Dataset and an open-sourced dataset from Kaggle were incorporated to enhance image diversity. After resizing all images to 512 × 512 pixels, a total of 5,383 OPGs were utilized for AI model training. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) were initially employed but yielded poor results. Subsequently, a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM) was trained on Google Colab, generating 2,500 synthetic images. The model's performance was evaluated using the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID). To assess image quality, a set of 20 synthetic and 20 original images was examined by dentists and two AI models; MesoNet (MN) and Vision Transformer (ViT).ResultsThe DDPM achieved an FID score of 26.90, markedly superior to 118.49 achieved by GANs. Dental experts exhibited suboptimal performance in distinguishing real from synthetic images, as evidenced by low Area Under the Curve (AUC) scores, indicating the high realism of the DDPM generated images. In contrast, both MN and ViT models achieved perfect classification accuracy with high AUC scores. GradCAM was the explainable AI technique applied to MN to elucidate AI performance.ConclusionsInability to differentiate synthetic OPGs highlights the realism of the generated images, suggesting their high quality. The authors propose using diffusion models to create annotated synthetic datasets for diverse AI training in healthcare diagnostics.Keywords: Generative artificial intelligence, Orthopantomography, Dental digital radiography, Image processing

Introduction

The Royal Society and the Alan Turing institute define synthetic data, as ‘data that has been generated using a purpose-built mathematical model or algorithm, with the aim of solving data science tasks’.1 This definition highlights the functional and intentional essence of synthetic data which is the use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) to tackle complex scientific challenges.1 In image-based domains, synthetic data is not merely a replication of statistical properties but a mechanism for generating task-specific visual representations that support robust model training and validation.2,3

The concept of synthetic data can be traced back to the 1940s, with the advent of Monte Carlo simulation methods designed to predict future outcomes based on historical trends.4 These early approaches laid the foundation for contemporary image-based synthetic data generation, which now relies on advanced Deep Learning architectures. Models such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), and more recently Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs), have demonstrated strong capability in generating high-fidelity medical images that preserve anatomical realism while avoiding direct replication of patient data.4,5

Within medical imaging, synthetic data has emerged as a particularly valuable tool for addressing data scarcity, class imbalance, and privacy constraints.5 Synthetic medical images have been successfully applied to augment datasets for radiology, pathology, and ophthalmology, leading to improved performance of AI-based diagnostic systems.5 By expanding the diversity of training samples, synthetic image generation enhances generalizability and reduces overfitting, especially in supervised learning tasks involving rare pathologies.5,6 Furthermore, synthetic data can strengthen public health models by improving the prediction of disease trends and outbreak dynamics.6

In dental imaging, these challenges are especially pronounced. Large, well-annotated datasets of Orthopantomograms (OPGs) are difficult to obtain due to ethical considerations, institutional data silos, and variability in imaging protocols.5,7 Limited data availability directly restricts the development and validation of AI-driven diagnostic and treatment-planning tools in dentistry.5 Beyond tailored data quality, fully synthetic data can overcome the issue of data scarcity, especially in the development of large language models trained on domain-specific information.8 Synthetic OPG generation therefore represents a targeted solution, enabling the creation of diverse and privacy-preserving datasets that reflect clinically relevant anatomical and pathological variations.9

This study specifically addresses data scarcity and ethical constraints in dental imaging through the generation of synthetic OPG datasets. The realism and clinical plausibility of the generated images are evaluated using a twofold approach: first, by assessing human observers’ ability to distinguish between real and synthetic OPGs; and second, by comparing these findings with the performance of two AI-based classification models. This focused evaluation aims to establish the fidelity and applicability of synthetic OPGs for advancing AI-driven dental diagnostics and decision-support systems.

Materials and methods

Data collection

This study was conducted at Aga Khan University Hospital (AKUH) after receiving approval from the institution's ethical review committee. This experiment was carried out adhering to the MI-CLAIM checklist, specific to generative AI models.10 Initially, OPGs were sourced from the dental clinics database at AKUH. These radiographs were captured using an Orthophos XG 3-D scanner (Dentsply Sirona GmbH, Bensheim, Deutschland), operated at 60-90 kV and 3-16 mA, and with dimensions of 2440 × 1292 pixels in gray scale. The OPGs were downloaded in Tag Image File Format (TIFF) from the SIDEXIS XG 2.63 software. All images were anonymized, and no patient metadata was accessible. The OPGs were stored in a password-protected database, accessible only to the authors of this study. Additionally, OPGs from the TUFTS Dental Dataset (TDD) and an open-source dataset from Kaggle were used to increase the diversity of the images.9, 10, 11 A total of 5,383 OPGs of permanent dentition were included in the model training. The images were resized to 512 × 512 pixels for uniformity using Python code as well as to reduce computational load.

Model training

The authors initially experimented with GANs for generating synthetic OPGs. A U-Net GAN was trained on Google Colab using a Colab L4 Graphics Processing Unit (GPU), the same training images were resized to 128 × 128 pixels using Python code.12,13 The model underwent training for 400 epochs, with a batch size of 128, and the training was discontinued due to suboptimal performance assessed using the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) score (118.49), along with consistent poor quality synthetic images evaluated visually after every 5 epochs as shown in Figure 1.

A summary of the workflow of the generation of OPGs as well as the evaluation by dentists and AI models.

Subsequently, a DDPM, as referenced in the study by Ho et al., was downloaded from GitHub.14 Training of this DDPM was conducted on Google Colab utilizing a 40 GB A100 GPU.13 The resized images (512 × 512 pixels) were used for training the DDPM. This model underwent training for 120 epochs, with batch size of 4 after which training was terminated upon the generation of optimal synthetic images/ OPGs, assessed using the FID score as well as visual assessment at every 5 epochs.

A total of 2,500 synthetic images were generated using the trained DDPM model. The model's performance was evaluated using the FID score (26.90), which was calculated by comparing the entire original dataset with the entire synthetic dataset.15

Model evaluation

To evaluate the quality of the synthetic images, a set of 40 manually selected images was utilized. Manual selection was performed to ensure that only high-quality synthetic images, free of distortions, blurriness, or artifacts, were included in the assessment. The dataset comprised a random mix of 20 synthetic and 20 original images which were reverted to their original resolution for clarity. Two cohorts of dentists with different levels of clinical expertise, with seven evaluators in each cohort, were asked to distinguish between real and synthetic images. The images were displayed on a projector screen under standardized viewing conditions to ensure a consistent visual experience across all evaluators, minimizing potential bias due to variations in display quality. Additionally, the same assessment was also done by two AI models; a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based algorithm known as MesoNet (MN) as well as a Vision Transformer (ViT) to determine their performances in identifying synthetic images for a head-to-head comparison of AI models with humans. These models were specifically trained to perform this task, with detailed training procedures and parameters provided in the supplementary paper. A summary of the methodology is explained in Figure 2. To further clarify how the AI model identified synthetic OPGs, GradCAM technique was applied.16

Results

After training the DDPM on 5,383 real images (ground truth), the FID score for the 2,500 generated images was 26.90. In contrast, the FID score for GANs, based on 5,000 generated images, was 118.49, which was deemed unacceptable for further evaluation. Consequently, images generated from the DDPM (980 × 512 pixels) were used for the continuation of this experiment (Figure 3).

Synthetic OPGs generated by DDPM.

To assess the realism and distinguishability of the synthetic images, a dataset of 40 images, comprising an equal 1:1 ratio of original and synthetic images, undisclosed to the participants, was presented to two groups: faculty members with over 5 years of clinical experience and postgraduate residents currently undergoing clinical training. The results, detailed in Figure 4, include true predictions, false predictions and Area Under the Curve (AUC) values reported separately for each individual, as well as mean AUCs for both groups. The findings indicate that both residents and faculty demonstrated poor ability to distinguish between original and synthetic OPGs. The Receiver Operating Curves (ROCs) are also shown in Figure 4.

Results of the evaluation by dental faculty, dental resident as well as two AI models, MN and ViT.

Additionally, the same dataset of 40 images was used to test two AI algorithms: a CNN-based MN and a ViT (the training details are presented in supplementary document). The results, presented in Figure 3, indicate that both AI algorithms outperformed humans, achieving perfect classification performance.

MN was selected for further experimentation on explainability because of its compatibility with Grad-CAM, an explainable AI technique that visualizes the regions of an image contributing most to the model’s predictions, thereby clarifying the rationale behind the algorithm’s performance and decision-making process.17 The GradCAM results are shown in Figure 5.

Discussion

The creation of artificial datasets can significantly accelerate healthcare research by providing large-scale, heterogeneous data while mitigating ethical and privacy concerns. Since these artificial images are not linked to patient metadata, they can be shared across institutions without violating regulations. Furthermore, because this data is synthetically generated, rare 'edge cases' can be included in the dataset, covering a broader range of variables.5 By exposing the AI to a wide range of variations in image features, anatomical structures, and pathological presentations, the model can learn robust patterns rather than memorizing specific examples, improving its performance on unseen data and making it more reliable for real-world applications.5

This study aimed to generate a synthetic OPG dataset encompassing all possible anatomical variations and diseases. A DDPM was utilized in this paper; the detailed workings of diffusion models are beyond the scope of this paper and can be found in other literature.5 While GANs was also explored, it yielded a higher FID score (118.49), consistent with known limitations such as mode collapse and challenges in generating high-resolution images.18,19 A study by Pedersen et al. generated OPGs using GANs.20 Although the reported metrics of synthetic quality in that paper differ from those in the present study, the visual assessment of the images indicates that their quality was below average.20 In comparison, DDPMs showed improved stability and produced higher-quality images, with an FID score of 26.90 in the present study. Their effectiveness in image generation is also corroborated by the results reported by Jain et al.5,21

A total of 2,500 synthetic images were generated using the trained DDPM model. The model's performance was evaluated using the FID score, which measures the similarity between real and generated images. The FID score is calculated by comparing the feature distributions of the real and synthetic datasets.22 A lower FID score indicates a closer resemblance between synthetic and real images, reflecting higher model performance.22 In this study, the FID of the DDPM was found to be 26.90. Typically, a lower FID score indicates higher quality and greater similarity between generated images and real training examples. Exceptional generative models often achieve FID scores below 10, with 0 representing perfect performance.23

Additionally, the authenticity of the synthetic images was assessed by two distinct cohorts of dentists: postgraduate residents and dental faculty. For the classification of real and synthetic images, AUC was reported as the metric for evaluation. AUC is particularly valuable for evaluating binary classifiers, as it directly assesses the classifier's ability to prioritize positive instances over negative ones.22 This attribute holds significance in numerous real-world scenarios where precise ranking or scoring is critical for decision-making and outcome assessment.24 A value of 1.0 indicates perfect discrimination. AUC values above 0.80 are generally considered clinically useful, while values below 0.80 are considered of limited clinical utility.24 In this experiment, both postgraduate residents and the faculty were poor classifiers, achieving overall AUC values of 0.55 and 0.51, respectively. The overall AUC values as well as False Positive Rate (FPRs) and True Positive Rate (TPRs) of both groups are presented in Figure 4. Overall, the human classifiers ineffective classification abilities since an AUC of 0.5 may be considered random classification.

A critical factor in assessing the human classification capability was the ratio of original to synthetic images in the testing dataset. To ensure a balanced representation of both image types, a 50:50 ratio was utilized. This approach aimed to facilitate a fair evaluation of experts' proficiency in distinguishing between original and synthetic images. However, it may not fully mirror real-world scenarios where the prevalence of certain features varies and the classifiers might become accustomed to the equal mix, potentially making it easier to identify discrepancies. Nevertheless, employing an equal mix enables a comprehensive assessment of experts' discrimination abilities across various image types and characteristics. It ensures that the evaluation captures the variability and complexity present in both original and synthetic images. With an equal number of images from both categories, the statistical power of analysis is optimized, enhancing result reliability and confidence in this experiment's conclusions.

The authors acknowledge that the overall evaluation dataset was relatively small. However, this was an intentional choice for a pilot-level validation, consistent with early-stage synthetic image assessment studies aimed at identifying major perceptual discrepancies before scaling larger and more resource-intensive experiments. The use of 40 OPG images was also deliberate to ensure that dentists could confidently complete the full self-administered test without fatigue, thereby maximizing response rates and minimizing incomplete evaluations. This controlled design supports the internal validity of the findings while allowing efficient exploration of feasibility.

For a direct comparison between AI and humans, 2 algorithms were evaluated using the same dataset of 40 images. A CNN-based MN and a ViT were employed to distinguish between original and synthetic images. The mean AUC for these algorithms was 0.97, indicating perfect performance. Notably, the ViT outperformed the MN, demonstrating superior discriminatory capabilities. To further understand the rationale behind the AI's ability to distinguish between original and synthetic images, GradCAM was employed to the results of MN.17 This explainable AI technique highlights the areas that influenced the MN’s decision-making process by overlaying heatmaps onto the image. Using this technique, the final layer of the MN and ViT models is replaced with a Grad-CAM layer. This modification enables the last layer to assign importance values to neurons based on a specific decision of interest, which are then visualized as heatmaps overlaid on the images.18 However, in this paper the heatmaps of the GradCAM are focused on the periphery of the OPGs rather than the anatomical features in the image as shown in Figure 5. The rationale behind the AI correctly identifying synthetic and original OPGs was not the tooth morphology or the normal anatomical structures according to these results. This is an area that still needs further clarification and the reason for AI models correctly identifying synthetic images from original ones remains unclear.

The strengths of this study include the use of diverse datasets, incorporating images from the AKUH dataset, TDD, as well as Kaggle, adding to the diversity of the generated OPGs. Additionally, the performance of the DDPM was evaluated at two levels, human as well as AI, to assess the realism of the synthetic images. It should be emphasized, however, that FID scores of the study as well as human/expert evaluations reflect perceptual realism but do not directly indicate clinical usefulness. This study represents a pilot-level assessment with a small human cohort and a limited image set, so the findings should be viewed as preliminary evidence of image quality, rather than definitive validation for diagnostic applications. Future research will involve training a conditional diffusion model to generate tailored synthetic images for use in downstream dental diagnostic tasks.25, 26

Conclusion

The inability of humans to distinguish synthetic OPGs in this study underscores the realism and high quality of the generated images. Within the limitations of this study, the authors propose that synthetic images can be generated using DDPMs to create a synthetic heterogeneous dataset. This dataset can be annotated and used to train various AI models for different diagnostic tasks, particularly in healthcare.
