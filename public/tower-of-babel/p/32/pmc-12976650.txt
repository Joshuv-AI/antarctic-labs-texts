Phenotypic and Genotypic Characterization of Antimicrobial Resistance in Thermophilic Campylobacter Species Isolated From Faecal Samples of Chicken and Turkey
Authors: Muazzez Yeşilyurt, Özgül Gülaydın, Aliye Gülmez Sağlam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThermophilic Campylobacter species cause significant economic losses in cases of enteritis in poultry.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to isolate and identify Campylobacter jejuni, Campylobacter coli and Campylobacter lari from chicken and turkey faeces samples using bacteriological and molecular methods. Also, to determine the antibiotic susceptibilities and antibiotic resistance genes of the isolates.Materials and MethodsWithin the scope of the study, 201 cloacal swab samples were collected from 132 chickens and 69 turkeys. Phenotypic and genotypic examinations were performed on the isolates obtained for resistance to various antimicrobial agents.Results Campylobacter spp. was isolated from 38.30% of the samples. PCR (polymerase chain reaction) analysis identified 63.63% of the isolates as C. jejuni and 23.37% as C. coli. The highest resistance rates of the isolates were determined against cephalothin (81.81%), nalidixic acid (72.72%) and aztreonam (61.03%). Multidrug resistance was found in 50% of Campylobacter spp. strains. The highest susceptibility in C. jejuni and C. coli strains was observed against gentamicin (95.91% and 100%). Statistical analyses demonstrated no significant differences between C. jejuni and C. coli with respect to most of the antibiotics tested (p > 0.05). However, a statistically significant difference was observed for nalidixic acid (p = 0.009). The blaOXA‐61 gene tested positive in 79.71% of the phenotypically ampicillin‐resistant isolates. The tet(O) gene was identified in 72.2% of tetracycline‐resistant isolates and the aphA‐3‐1 gene in 5.8% of aminoglycoside‐resistant isolates. The ERY2074 and ERY2075 genes were identified in 50% and 78.57% of the 28 isolates with erythromycin resistance genes, respectively. The cmeA, cmeB and cmeC genes were identified in 51.28%, 79.48% and 25.6% of the strains that developed multidrug resistance, respectively. The Thr‐86‐Ile point mutation was found in 92% of the isolates that carried the gyrA gene responsible for quinolone resistance.ConclusionThe high susceptibility of the isolates to gentamicin in vitro suggests that gentamicin could be a potential agent for limiting Campylobacter colonization in poultry.Keywords: antimicrobial resistance, Campylobacter, chicken, resistance genes, turkey

Introduction

The importance of poultry farming has been growing worldwide as they are suitable for industrial production and meet the protein needs of human beings at lower prices (Popa et al. 2022). However, digestive system diseases caused by Campylobacter species in poultry lead to considerable economic losses (Çakmak and Erol 2010; Ghaffoori Kanaan et al. 2022). Campylobacteriosis is a significant zoonotic disease, accounting for approximately 80% of human cases, and is primarily transmitted through the consumption of undercooked or raw poultry meat (Ghaffoori Kanaan et al. 2022). Although poultry represents a major source of protein, particularly in developing countries (El‐Wadawe et al. 2019), it also serves as the main reservoir for the transmission of Campylobacter species to humans (Barakat et al. 2020). These pathogens are naturally present in the intestinal microbiota of animals and can be transmitted to humans through contamination of meat and other tissues during slaughter (Mingo et al. 2014; El‐Wadawe et al. 2019). Furthermore, the body temperature of poultry, which is close to the optimal growth temperature of Campylobacter species, contributes to the high prevalence of infection (Çakmak and Erol 2010). Water, the environment, and other food sources are also reported in many cases as sources of infection. Therefore, campylobacteriosis is recognized as a zoonotic disease of considerable importance to both animal and public health (Li et al. 2019; Popa et al. 2025).

Campylobacteriosis causes diarrhoea cases in approximately 166 million people and more than 37,000 deaths annually (Ghaffoori Kanaan et al. 2022). Moreover, it is associated with Guillain–Barré syndrome, which causes neurological symptoms in humans (Poropatich et al. 2010; Finsterer 2022). Studies conducted in Türkiye have reported that the prevalence of Campylobacter spp. varies between 25.5% and 99.1% (Cokal et al. 2009; Aslantaş 2017; Adiguzel et al. 2018; Hizlisoy et al. 2024). This indicates that the pathogen is significantly and persistently present in poultry‐related sources.

For both human medicine and poultry farming, infection caused by campylobacteriosis is usually treated with erythromycin (macrolide), ciprofloxacin (fluoroquinolone), aminoglycosides and tetracyclines (Chen et al. 2010; Silva et al. 2011; El‐Adawy et al. 2012; Adiguzel et al. 2018; Gharbi et al. 2022). However, overuse of these drugs leads to a rise in antibiotic‐resistant Campylobacter species. Resistance develops against macrolides, usually due to mutations at positions A2074C or A2075G in the 23S rRNA gene. Furthermore, the Cme ABC multidrug resistance pump, which leads to resistance to many antibiotic groups, ß‐lactam resistance (blaOXA‐61 ) and aminoglycoside resistance (aphA‐3), is associated with the development of multidrug resistance in Campylobacter species. Fluoroquinolone resistance is caused by a point mutation in the region of the DNA gyrase gene gyrA that determines quinolone resistance. The tet(O) gene, which encodes a ribosomal protein, leads to high rates of resistance to tetracycline (Gharbi et al. 2022). Furthermore, the CmeABC multidrug resistance pump, which confers resistance to many antibiotic groups, β‐lactam resistance (blaOXA‐61 ) and aminoglycoside resistance (aphA‐3), is associated with the development of multidrug resistance in Campylobacter species. CmeABC is particularly effective in the efflux of antimicrobial agents and has the ability to efflux antimicrobial agents such as macrolides, fluoroquinolones and tetracyclines. This clearly indicates its resistance to many antibiotic groups (Yao et al. 2021).

The transmission of campylobacteriosis from poultry to humans through food raises the importance of these resistant strains (El‐Adawy et al. 2012; El‐Wadawe et al. 2019). The World Health Organization (WHO) considers antibiotic resistance in Campylobacter species as a public health concern, and antimicrobial resistance is regarded as a global problem (WHO 2024).

This study was conducted to isolate and identify Campylobacter jejuni, Campylobacter coli and Campylobacter lari from cloacal swab samples taken from chickens and turkeys with diarrhoea in Siirt, Batman, Mardin and Bitlis provinces and districts by bacteriological conventional methods and molecular methods. Furthermore, the susceptibility of the isolates to various antimicrobial agents and antimicrobial resistance genes was determined, and effective treatment options for campylobacteriosis cases were evaluated.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Area

The study area encompasses the provinces of Siirt, Batman, Mardin and Bitlis in the Southeastern and Eastern Anatolia regions of Türkiye (Table 1). These provinces were selected to address a significant regional gap in the literature, as there is currently no documented data regarding the prevalence of Campylobacter spp. in poultry faeces within this specific geography. The study material consisted of 201 cloacal swab samples collected from turkeys and chickens that exclusively exhibited clinical signs of diarrhoea. Clinically normal poultry from the same flocks were not sampled. The collected swab samples were immediately placed in Cary‐Blair transport medium (Cary‐Blair, 104054, Türkiye) and transported under cold chain conditions to the laboratory of the Microbiology Department of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Siirt University.

Methods

Campylobacter spp. Isolation

Swab samples were inoculated on blood agar containing 5%–10% sheep blood (Oxoid, CM0271, UK), MacConkey agar (HiMedia, MH081, India) and modified charcoal cefoperazone deoxycholate (mCCDA) agar (Biolife, NL2403, Italy) containing 5%–10% sheep blood and Campylobacter mCCDA selective supplement (Oxoid, SR0069E, UK). After the inoculation process was completed, the petri dishes were incubated at 42°C for 48 h in a microaerophilic medium (Shakir et al. 2021). Following the incubation period, a single presumptive Campylobacter colony was selected and subcultured onto blood agar plates in accordance with International Organization for standardization (ISO 10272‐1:2017). The plates were then incubated under microaerophilic conditions at 42°C for 48 h. After incubation, the colony morphology was re‐examined to verify the purity of the culture.

The colonies were Gram‐stained and examined for their microscopic morphology, growth on MacConkey agar, motility, oxidase, catalase and indole reactions (Baali et al. 2020).

The suspected isolates were confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using species‐specific primers. Table 2 shows information about the specific primers used for this purpose.

DNA Extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from suspected isolates using a commercial DNA isolation kit (GeneAll, Exgene Clinic SV Mini, 108.101, Korea).

PCR Amplification

A commercial mastermix (A.B.T., 2X PCR Mastermix, P01‐02‐01, Türkiye) was used to prepare the PCR mixture, and the manufacturer's recommendations were taken into consideration. For optimization of the mixture, 5 µL of genomic DNA and 1.5 µL of each primer (10 µM) were added to 12.5 µL of mastermix, and the total volume was brought to 25 µL with PCR water. During the amplification process, the binding temperature was optimized according to the recommendations of the company where the primers were synthesized. The amplicons that were made by PCR were electrophoresed in agarose gel and analysed in a gel imaging system (Gen‐Box ImagER, Ankara, Türkiye).

Determination of Antimicrobial Susceptibility

The susceptibility of the isolates to various antimicrobial agents was determined by the disc diffusion method. The CLSI (Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute) (2007) criteria as well as the criteria established by Miflin et al. (2007) and Luangtongkum et al. (2007) were taken into account while carrying out and assessing the test.

Ciprofloxacin (5 µg, Bioanalyse, Türkiye), gentamicin (10 µg, Bioanalyse, Türkiye), cephalothin (30 µg, Bioanalyse, Türkiye), chloramphenicol (30 µg, Bioanalyse, Türkiye), aztreonam (30 µg, Bioanalyse, Türkiye), nalidixic acid (30 µg, Bioanalyse, Türkiye), amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (20/10 µg, Bioanalyse, Türkiye), erythromycin (15 µg, Liofilchem, Italy), tetracycline (30 µg, Bioanalyse, Türkiye) and streptomycin (10 µg, Bioanalyse, Türkiye) antibiotic discs were used.

As a result of the test, the isolates were evaluated as susceptible, moderately susceptible or resistant to the antimicrobial agents used. Moreover, isolates resistant to three or more antibiotic groups were considered as multidrug‐resistant strains (Magiorakos et al. 2012).

Identification of Antimicrobial Resistance Genes

Phenotypically resistant Campylobacter isolates were analysed for quinolone and erythromycin resistance genes, tet(O) (tetracycline), aph‐3‐1 (aminoglycoside), blaOXA‐61 (ampicillin) and cmeABC (efflux system) antimicrobial resistance genes as identified by Obeng et al. Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Minitab software (version 21). Pearson's chi‐square (χ 2) test was applied to compare the antibiotic resistance profiles of C. jejuni and C. coli isolates. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05.

Results

Isolation and Identification

Suspected Campylobacter spp. isolates (chicken = 114, turkey = 69) were obtained from 201 cloacal swab samples collected in the study by bacteriological conventional methods at a rate of 91.04%. PCR identified 77 (42.07%) of the suspected isolates (turkey = 44, chicken = 33) as Campylobacter spp.

Although 28 (63.63%) of 44 Campylobacter spp. isolates obtained from cloacal swab samples collected from turkeys were identified as C. jejuni, 16 (36.36%) isolates were identified as C. coli. Of the 33 Campylobacter spp. isolates obtained from chicken cloacal swab samples, C. jejuni was the most prevalent species (n = 21, 63.64%), followed by C. coli (n = 2, 6.06%) (Figure 1). On the other hand, 10 of the isolates could not be identified at the species level. Furthermore, C. lari was not isolated from cloacal swab samples collected from both turkeys and chickens.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility

Resistance to nalidixic acid was found in 82.14%, to cephalothin in 75%, to aztreonam and ciprofloxacin in 57.14%, to tetracycline in 39.28% and to amoxicillin–clavulanic acid in 35.71% of C. jejuni isolates obtained from cloacal swab samples collected from turkeys. Of the C. coli isolates obtained from turkeys, the highest resistance rates were observed against cephalothin (93.75%), erythromycin (75%), tetracycline (68.75%) and amoxicillin‐clavulanic acid (68.75%). Furthermore, resistance was also noted for streptomycin (43.75%), nalidixic acid (43.75%), ciprofloxacin (37.5%), aztreonam (37.5%) and chloramphenicol (31.25%). Multidrug resistance development in C. jejuni and C. coli isolates was determined to be 39.28% and 81.25%, respectively.

More than 80% of C. jejuni isolates obtained from cloacal swab samples collected from chickens were resistant to aztreonam, cephalothin and nalidixic acid; 61.9% to ciprofloxacin; 52.38% to amoxicillin–clavulanic acid; 33.33% to tetracycline and 28.57% to multidrug. On the other hand, all C. coli isolates (100%) were resistant to cephalothin and nalidixic acid; 50% were resistant to streptomycin, tetracycline, erythromycin, aztreonam and ciprofloxacin; and 50% developed multidrug resistance (Table 4).

Antimicrobial Resistance Genes

The tet(O) resistance gene was identified in 72.2% (26/36), blaOXA‐61 in 79.71% (55/69) and aphA‐3‐1 in 5.88% (1/17) of the isolates. ERY2074 and ERY2075 resistance genes were identified in 50% and 78.57% of the 28 (96.5%) isolates in which the erythromycin resistance gene was found. The CmeA resistance gene was identified in 20 (51.28%), the cmeB resistance gene in 31 (79.48%), and the cmeC resistance gene in 10 (25.6%) of 39 Campylobacter spp. isolates in which development of multidrug resistance was observed. Furthermore, the PCR analysis showed the presence of the Thr‐86‐Ile point mutation in 92.85% (n = 39) of 42 C. jejuni isolates and 90% (n = 9) of 10 C. coli isolates, which were observed to have quinolone resistance and the gyrA gene.

Statistical Analysis

The findings indicate that there is no significant association between antibiotic resistance to gentamicin, cephalothin, streptomycin, tetracycline, erythromycin, ciprofloxacin and amoxicillin–clavulanic acid in C. jejuni and C. coli isolates (p > 0.05). However, a statistically significant difference was observed in antimicrobial resistance to nalidixic acid between C. jejuni and C. coli isolates (p = 0.009, p < 0.05).

Discussion

Campylobacteriosis, which is caused by Campylobacter species and can be transmitted to humans through food, leads to significant economic losses in poultry farming (Gülmez Sağlam et al. 2019; Gahamanyi et al. 2021). Approximately 50%–90% of poultry are colonized with thermophilic Campylobacter species at 2–3 weeks of age. In particular, C. jejuni, an important thermophilic Campylobacter species, is responsible for 80%–90% of campylobacteriosis cases in humans (Ljupco et al. 2011).

This study aimed to isolate and identify Campylobacter spp. species from chicken and turkey flocks raised in Siirt, Batman, Mardin and Bitlis cities by bacteriological conventional and molecular methods. It also aimed to demonstrate the existence of phenotypic and genotypic resistance antimicrobial agents and the presence of antimicrobial resistance genes in the isolates obtained under this study.

Several national and international studies have been conducted on the subject. In this context, a study conducted by Aslantaş (2017) in the Adana and Hatay provinces of Turkey reported that the isolation rate of Campylobacter spp. was 99.1%. C. jejuni (n = 194) and C. coli (n = 24) were identified as 89.1% and 10.9%, respectively. Similarly, Adiguzel et al. (2018) reported an 82.3% prevalance of Campylobacter spp. in Istanbul, Izmir, and Sakarya, comprising 44.6% C. jejuni and 37.7% C. coli. In a study conducted by Kagambèga et al. (2021) in West Africa, they reported that they isolated 18 (34.61%) Campylobacter spp. from 52 stool samples and identified 17 (94.45%) of the strains obtained as C. coli and 1 (5.55%) as C. jejuni. A study conducted in Canada reported that researchers obtained 1063 Campylobacter spp. isolates, and although the isolation rate of C. jejuni was 61.24% (n = 651), the isolation rate of C. coli was 31.61% (n = 336) (Shrestha et al. 2023).

The related studies have shown that the isolation rate of Campylobacter spp. from turkey and chicken faecal samples varied between 34.61% and 99.1%, the isolation rate of C. jejuni between 5.5% and 89.1%, and the isolation rate of C. coli between 10% and 94.45% (Aslantaş 2017; Adiguzel et al. 2018; Kagambèga et al. 2021; Shrestha et al. 2023). This study revealed that the identification rate of chicken and turkey flocks with thermophilic Campylobacter spp. was 42%, C. jejuni 63% and C. coli 23.37%. The study resulted in no positive results for C. lari in chicken and turkey cloacal swab samples. It was thought that the differences between the study results may be due to geographical conditions, animal rearing conditions, sampling methods and sample size. From an epidemiological perspective, the detection of Campylobacter spp. in almost half of the flocks studied indicates that poultry in this region serves as a significant reservoir for zoonotic transmission. As C. jejuni is the primary cause of human campylobacteriosis, its dominance among isolates raises concerns about foodborne transmission through the processing or consumption of undercooked poultry products. Therefore, strengthening farm‐level biological safety measures and improving hygiene practices are crucial to reduce bacterial load and prevent transmission to humans. In Türkiye, epidemiological data on thermophilic Campylobacter species other than C. jejuni and C. coli are quite limited. This study aimed to provide a more holistic perspective to determine the distribution of thermophilic Campylobacter species. However, the analysis results did not yield any positive results for C. lari. This suggests that C. lari may have a very low prevalence in Türkiye or may reside within a specific environmental niche that does not overlap with our sampling framework. Our study will provide an important basis for empirical evidence regarding the absence of this previously unstudied species.

The use of antibiotics in poultry production can directly contribute to the emergence of antimicrobial resistance within bacterial populations, posing a serious threat to both animal and human health and compromising the efficacy of existing therapeutic agents (Ljupco et al. 2011). In this regard, it is important to monitor the antimicrobial resistance profiles of the agents that cause infections.

In the present study, the phenotypic resistance of C. jejuni isolates to ciprofloxacin was determined to be 57%. This rate is lower than those reported by Aslantaş (2017), Adiguzel et al. (2018) and Kardoğan and Müştak (2023) (86%, 87% and 100%, respectively), but higher than the rates reported by Ljupco et al. (2011) and El‐Wadawe et al. (2019) (42.4% and 20%, respectively). C. coli isolates exhibited a ciprofloxacin resistance rate of 27.7%, which is lower than the rates previously reported by Ljupco et al. (2011), Aslantaş (2017), Adiguzel et al. (2018), El‐Wadawe et al. (2019) and Kardoğan and Müştak (2023) (62.3%, 100%, 98%, 30% and 100%, respectively). The tetracycline resistance rates of C. jejuni and C. coli isolates were 36.7% and 66.6%, respectively, which are lower than the ranges reported by Ljupco et al. (2011) (39.8%–57.9%), Aslantaş (2017) (43%–50%) and El‐Wadawe et al. (2019) (60%–70%). Furthermore, resistance to nalidixic acid was observed in 81.6% of C. jejuni isolates. This finding aligns closely with the 81% resistance reported by Adiguzel et al. (2018) but is lower than the rates reported by Aslantaş (2017) (86%) and by El‐Wadawe et al. (2019) and Kardoğan and Müştak (2023) (100%). The rate of nalidixic acid resistance (50%) in C. coli isolates was lower than the results of the studies by Aslantaş (2017) (100%), Adiguzel et al. (2018) (63%), El‐Wadawe et al. (2019) (90%) and Kardoğan and Müştak (2023) (100%). It was thought that the differences in the antimicrobial resistance rates may be associated with regional conditions, unconscious use of antibiotics, different rearing conditions and different methods used in the studies. However, it was concluded that rational use of antimicrobials and continuous monitoring of resistance patterns are still necessary to reduce the risk of cross‐resistance between animal and human pathogens.

The use of antibiotics in bacterial populations can contribute to the development of resistance, in which resistance genes play a critical role. Identifying the presence of these genes through genotyping is therefore essential to prevent treatment failure (Gharbi et al. 2022).

Several national and international studies have investigated this subject. In their study, El‐Adawy et al. (2012) reported that they found no erythromycin resistance gene and 2074 and 2075 mutations in 15 C. jejuni strains resistant to erythromycin, whereas they found the mutation in gyrA (Thr‐86‐Ile) in 96% of 53 ciprofloxacin‐resistant strains. The researchers reported that they found the tet(O) resistance gene in 97.6% of 42 phenotypically tetracycline‐resistant isolates. In their study, Woźniak‐Biel et al. (2018) found that all C. jejuni and C. coli strains were resistant to ciprofloxacin. The researchers reported that they found mutation in the gyrA gene (Thr‐86‐Ile) in all of the strains (100%). They also found the tet(O) gene in strains obtained from turkeys and chickens as 71%–100%, respectively. In another study, the researchers reported that they identified tet(O), blaOXA‐61 and cmeB resistance genes in C. jejuni and C. coli isolates with the rates of 100%–80%, 81%–93% and 80%–100%, respectively, but they found no aphA‐3 gene in any isolate. They reported that the identification rates of Thr‐86‐Ile, 2075 and 2074 mutation genes in C. jejuni and C. coli isolates were 90%–80%, 86%–61% and 14%–27%, respectively (Gharbi et al. 2022). A study conducted by Hizlisoy et al. in Kayseri in 2024 reported that they identified no ERY2074 and ERY2075 mutations in the gyrA protein in 51 C. jejuni isolates, whereas tet(O) and blaOXA resistance genes were identified in 44 (86%) and 42 (82.3%) of the isolates, respectively.

The present study differed from the previous studies with respect to the mutation of genes responsible for erythromycin resistance and the absence of aphA‐3 resistance gene positivity in some studies (El‐Adawy et al. 2012; Hizlisoy et al. 2024), whereas the presence of Thr‐86‐Ile mutation in the gyrA gene yielded similar results to the previous studies (El‐Adawy et al. 2012; Gharbi et al. 2022). The presence of tetracycline‐resistant genes in the present study was found to be low compared to the results of other studies (El‐Adawy et al. 2012; Gharbi et al. 2022; Woźniak‐Biel et al. 2018; Hizlisoy et al. 2024).

Furthermore, this study observed some inconsistencies between genotypic markers and phenotypic resistance profiles. Specifically, 14 isolates showed phenotypic resistance to ampicillin despite being negative for the blaOXA‐61 gene. Similarly, several isolates resistant to tetracycline (n = 10) and aminoglycosides (n = 16) did not carry target resistance genes. These findings suggest that antimicrobial resistance in Campylobacter species is multifactorial and not solely dependent on the presence of acquired resistance genes. This is primarily thought to stem from the CmeABC multidrug excretion system, which can actively leak antibiotics out of the cell, conferring resistance to multiple antibiotic classes. In addition, the erythromycin‐resistant isolate lacking target resistance genes was found to carry the A2075G (ERY2075) point mutation in 23S rRNA. This study concludes that ribosomal point mutations are critical factors in macrolide resistance and are generally independent of acquired genes.

Campylobacteriosis cases in poultry are particularly significant from a ‘One Health’ perspective. Resistant strains, which pose a risk to both human and animal health, can easily enter the food chain and cause difficult‐to‐treat infections. Therefore, implementing strict regulations regarding antibiotic use in livestock farming and implementing biosecurity measures on farms will reduce the risk of campylobacteriosis for both animal and human health.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that C. jejuni and C. coli isolates are important causative agents of enteritis in chickens and turkeys. Although the findings show high in vitro susceptibility to gentamicin, its low oral bioavailability suggests that its clinical use in poultry will likely be limited to reducing intestinal colonization and faecal excretion rather than systemic treatment. Furthermore, monitoring the development of antimicrobial resistance in Campylobacter species is crucial for both animal and public health. In this context, regular regional monitoring of resistance profiles using phenotypic and genotypic methods, in line with the ‘One Health’ approach, will play a critical role in preventing the spread of resistant strains and developing effective control strategies.
