Longitudinal changes in coagulation-fibrinolysis in patients with traumatic brain injury within seven days after injury
Authors: Xinlong Dong, Nanyu Yao, Limin Zhang, Guanghui Zheng, Yutong Zhao, Weiming Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTraumatic brain injury (TBI)-induced coagulopathy is associated with poor outcomes of patients, but its progression remains poorly understood. We longitudinally collected peripheral venous blood samples from patients with TBI 24 h, 3 days, and 7 days after injury, and analyzed the samples for conventional blood and conventional coagulation tests, thromboelastography, coagulation factor activity, platelet activity, and levels of extracellular vesicles through laboratory tests. By detecting longitudinal changes in the coagulation-fibrinolysis, we found that TBI patients were in a state of hyperfibrinolysis within 24 h after injury, but transitioned to a hypercoagulable state on the 7th day after injury. And, the concentrations of extracellular vesicles continued to increase from 24 h to 7 days after injury. We identified the early changes in TBI-induced coagulopathy, namely that TBI patients undergo a transition from a hyperfibrinolytic state to a hypercoagulable state within 24 h to 7 days post-injury.

Introduction

Because of its high morbidity and mortality rates, traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a major public health challenge. It is an acute disease with signficantly long-term consequences.1,2 TBI-induced coagulopathy (TBI-IC) is a common complication of TBI and associated with poor prognosis of patients.3 TBI-IC typically presents as a hypercoagulable state in the early post injury period but gradually transitioning to a consumptive hypocoagulable state with the massive consumption of platelets and coagulation factors. Therefore, TBI-IC can cause serious thrombotic events4 or delayed rebleeding.5 Unlike disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)-like coagulopathy found in patients with hemorrhagic shock,6 patients with isolated TBI do not sustain significant blood loss, receive significant fluid resuscitation that results in hemodilution, and hypothermia seen in trauma patients with hemorrhagic shock.7 This indicates that TBI-IC has a unique pathogenesis and common causal factors for trauma and hemorrhagic shock-induced coagulopathy3,6,8 may not fully explain TBI-IC.

We and other researchers have reported that extracellular vesicles (EVs), especially brain-derived extracellular vesicles (BDEVs) are key causal factors for TBI-IC,9,10 by triggering extrinsic coagulation pathways,11 activating platelets12 and converting the anti-coagulant endothelium to a procoagulant one.13 EVs are procoagulant because they express the anionic phospholipid phosphatidylserine (PS) and tissue factors (TF), thus allowing the assembly of extrinsic tenase complex to form on their surface. Because PS-expressing EVs cause systemic intravascular coagulation, we found that a fusion chimeric protein linking the PS-binding annexin V to a peptide anti-coagulant (ANV-6L15) is effective in reducing TBI-IC in mouse models.11 Furthermore, lactadherin, which serves as a scavenging factor for PS positive EVs also protects mice against the development of coagulopathy after TBI.14

To address the question as whether findings made in mouse models are applicable to TBI patients, we designed a study to comprehensively investigate longitudinal changes in the hemostatic state of TBI patient.

Results

Information on patients and controls

We recruited 40 healthy volunteers. A total of 223 TBI patients were hospitalized and received treatment in the TBI department of Beijing Tiantan Hospital from October 2023 to February 2025. We included a total of 157 eligible TBI patients and excluded 66 patients, including 42 patients who received oral anticoagulants before injury, 13 patients with coagulation disorders, or severe liver and kidney diseases, or cancer before injury, and 11 patients with extracranial injury. Due to omissions, discharge, and death, a patient may not be able to undergo every test. Therefore, we recruited 157 TBI patients to ensure that each test included 40 samples (Ly30% and Cl testing included 30 samples). The average age of TBI patients is 48.0 years, while the average age of healthy volunteers is 47.4 years, with the majority being male (Table 1). Time from admission to injury varied significantly between 1 and 72 h post injury, with an average of 14.4 h. All TBI patients had intracranial hematoma defined by CT scan, including epidural hematoma, subdural hematoma, intracerebral hematoma, cerebral contusion, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and mixed hematoma, with or without cerebral hernia and skull fracture. 119 patients (75.8% of the total) received surgical treatment, 32 patients (20.4% of the total) received blood transfusion treatment, and 100 patients (63.7% of the total) received procoagulant therapy during hospitalization. It is worth noting that 52 patients (33.1% of the total) experienced thrombotic events (ultrasound showed lower limb venous thrombosis) during hospitalization, of which 9 patients (5.7% of the total) experienced secondary intracranial hemorrhage. We divided patients into three groups based on glasgow coma scale (GCS): mild TBI(GCS:13–15), moderate TBI(GCS:9–12), and severe TBI(GCS:3–8), with males still having a higher proportion than females in each group. Although the proportion of patients with severe TBI receiving surgery and blood transfusion treatment is higher than that of mild and moderate TBI, the proportion of secondary intracranial hemorrhage and thrombotic events is higher in severe TBI, and the prognosis is worse (Table 1).

Longitudinal changes in the rate of fibrinolysis in the first 7 days after TBI

FIB is a platelet ligand that aggregates platelets and is converted to crosslinked fibrin network to stabilize a platelet clot, whereas DD and FDP are degradation products of fibrin and serve as markers for fibrinolysis. We found that both DD and FDP significantly increased in samples collected in the first 24 h after TBI (Figures 1 and 1B), indicating a high rate of fibrinolysis. However, DD and FDP decreased gradually in samples collected on the 3rd and 7th days after injury but remains elevated over the baseline in samples collected on the 7th day (Figures 1A and 1B). Consistent with these observations, Ly30% measured using thromboelastography, which represents the rate of clot dissolution 30 min after the formation of the strongest blood clot, also showed a significant increase in samples collected in the first 24 h and 3rd days of TBI followed by significantly reduced but remained higher than baseline on day 7(Figure 1D). International normalized ratio (INR) is the ratio of PT in healthy references and PT in a given patients, thus reducing or eliminating variations among different laboratories. In addition to conventional clotting assays measured in plasma, we also analyze whole blood samples from these patients using thromboelastogram with several key measurements. First, the K value reflects platelet function and fibrinogen activity. Second, the αangle is closely related to the K value, indicating the rate of blood clot formation and reinforcement with MA indicating the maximal strength and stability of blood clot. Third, Ly30% represents the rate of clot dissolution 30 min after the formation of the strongest blood clot. Finally, Cl reflects the overall coagulation status. We found that the K values increased in the first 24 h after injury and then decreased on the 3rd and 7th day (Figure 2D). In contrast, the α angle, MA, and Cl all decreased in the first 24 h after injury, followed by gradual increase on the 3rd and 7th day (Figures 2E–2G). The results from thromboelastography showed a high rate of coagulation on 7th day than on the 3rd after injury (Figure 2).

In addition to testing the coagulation state of patients, we also measured the activation of coagulation factor VII, X, and II from their zymogen state. Longitudinal changes of CBC in the first 7 days after TBI

We conducted complete blood count (CBC) (including erythrocyte, hemoglobin, and platelet concentrations) on healthy individuals and TBI patients, and used platelet aggregation assays to detect platelet activity by adding activators such as ADP, collagen, and AA. We found a significant decrease in erythrocyte counts, hemoglobin levels, and platelet counts in the first 24 h after TBI (Figures 3A–3C). However, on the 3rd and 7th days, erythrocyte counts and hemoglobin continued to decrease whereas platelet counts increased (Figures 3A–3C). Platelet aggregation was poor in response to arachidonic acid, ADP, or collagen stimulations in the first 24 h, followed by gradual restoration in the 3rd and 7th day after injury (Figures 3D–3F).

The proportion of eEVs (as the second largest EV among the four EVs) showed a significant increase on the third day after TBI and still maintained a high proportion on the seventh day after TBI. Discussion

We provide clinical data that reveals the progression of TBI-IC within 7 days after injury. The aim of this study is to elucidate the changes in coagulation and fibrinolysis in TBI patients within 7 days after injury, as well as the relationship between these changes and adverse outcomes such as secondary intracranial hemorrhage and thrombotic events. We have obtained the following novel and important experimental results.

Firstly, we collected 157 TBI patients, most of whom were middle-aged and young males (Table 1), which may be related to the higher risk of acute injury among middle-aged and young males.15 Most of the enrolled TBI patients underwent surgery (75.8%), and the proportion of patients receiving anticoagulant and transfusion therapy is 63.7% and 20.4%, respectively (Table 1). Patients with both TBI and other organ injuries have been excluded to prevent interference from other organ injuries. Some mild TBI patients who were observed in the emergency department but not hospitalized were also not included in the group. The results of DD, FDP, and Ly30% all indicate that TBI patients are in a state of hyperfibrinolysis within 24 h of injury (Figure 1); The patient transitioned from an early hypocoagulable state to hypercoagulable state 3rd days after injury, and this hypercoagulable state continued until 7th days after injury. The previous findings are consistent with our and other’s reports,9,11,13,16,17 however, our results show that TBI patients mainly exhibit hyperfibrinolysis 24 h after injury (Figure 1), which should be due to the activation of the fibrinolytic system in the body after secondary hypercoagulability in TBI.18,19 But we did not detect the first hypercoagulable state like in animal experiments.11 This is also easy to explain, as patients take an average of 14.4 h from injury to hospitalization (Table 1), including patient transportation time, emergency observation time, and hospitalization processing time.15 Tian Y et al. previously reported that mice reached their peak of hypercoagulability after 3 h of injury.9 Based on this, we speculate that the patient’s hypercoagulability reached its peak shortly after injury, such as within a few hours. When the patient is hospitalized, within 24 h after injury, the body has already activated the fibrinolytic system.20 Our results showed that 5.7% of TBI patients experienced secondary intracranial hemorrhage within 3 days after injury. There have been literature reports that secondary intracranial hemorrhage may be related to early hyperfibrinolytic state after injury,21 although we did not have positive results (No data available). Therefore, this early occurrence of hyperfibrinolysis provides a theoretical basis for clinical use of anti-fibrinolytic drugs such as tranexamic acid to suppress secondary intracranial hemorrhage after TBI.21,22 It is interesting that TBI patients gradually transition from a high fibrinolysis state to a low fibrinolysis state, while also transitioning from a low coagulation state to a high coagulation state after 3 days of injury(Figures 1 and 2). We are not sure if this transformation is the result of clinical treatment or the progression of the disease. Our results showed that 33.1% of TBI patients developed lower limb venous thrombosis 3 days after injury, and this proportion was higher in severe TBI patients (54.8%). Thrombotic events caused by hypercoagulability 3 days after injury may be the important reason for the worst prognosis in severe patients (Table 1).It is necessary to monitor the coagulation status of patients for a long time to clarify the relationship between the previous transformations and secondary intracranial hemorrhage and thrombotic events.4,18

Thromboelastography (K,α, MA, and Cl) provides more obvious indications than CCTs (PT, INR, and TT) (Figure 2). CCTs have long been popular due to their simplicity, affordability, and ability to reflect the fibrinolytic state well.23 Unlike CCTs that only detect plasma or platelets, thromboelastography is a real-time detection of the entire venous blood.24 We believe that thromboelastography can not only provide additional important coagulation information, but also better reflect the true coagulation status of patients after injury. The incidence rate of TBI-IC reported in the current literature varies greatly, ranging from 10% to 97.2%.25,26,27 Some of the reasons may be related to the insensitivity of CCTs to TBI-IC,28,29 thereby overlooking the diagnosis and treatment of TBI-IC. As a necessary supplement to CCTs, we advocate for the rapid popularization of thromboelastography, which may be more helpful for timely diagnosis and effective treatment of TBI-IC.30 However, the process of popularization is not easy, not only because thromboelastography will increase additional hospitalization costs, but also because the advantages of thromboelastography compared to CCTs are still controversial.31 Therefore, more research is needed to demonstrate the unique value of thromboelastography.

Secondly, our results showed that the excessive consumption of coagulation factors such as fibrinogen, thrombin, factor VII, and factor X within 24 h after TBI injury led to early hypocoagulable state (Figure 3). The decrease in platelet concentration and activity is also a cause of low coagulation within 24 h after injury (Figure 3). There are literature reports that platelet dysfunction that occurs immediately after TBI is closely related to the severity of TBI and in-hospital mortality.32 This high fibrinolysis or low coagulation state increases the risk of secondary intracranial hemorrhage in TBI patients,21 and clinical interventions typically involve inhibiting fibrinolysis or promoting coagulation to reduce this risk of bleeding.22 The increase in coagulation factor activity, platelet concentration, and activity from 3 days after injury is the cause of hypercoagulability, although some of their values still fluctuate within the “normal range” (Figure 3). It is worth noting that the definitions of TBI-IC related parameters vary greatly in different reported literature, and even overlap with the “normal range” of healthy individuals. Therefore, it is necessary to redefine the threshold values of TBI-IC related parameters to facilitate the comparison of different research results, thereby facilitating the early diagnosis and treatment of TBI-IC.3 In addition, early high fibrinolysis and late low fibrinolysis after injury may be related to the regulation of endogenous tissue-type plasminogen activator (tPA), urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA), and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1(PAI-1),33,34 and their mechanisms still require further research.

In addition, we analyzed the statistical differences in coagulation parameters between different GCS groups (see Table S1). Unfortunately, we did not find any statistical differences in coagulation parameters between the mild, moderate, and severe TBI groups except for the DD, factor VII, and COL groups (see Table S1). Whether DD, factor VII, and COL groups can distinguish between mild, moderate, and severe TBI still needs to be verified by future multicenter studies. Interestingly, our results suggest a significant difference in DD between severe TBI and moderate or mild TBI. The previous results are consistent with the reported conclusion that the elevation of D-dimer suggests poor prognosis in TBI patients.35,36,37 Although our results showed no significant statistical difference in INR between different GCS groups, Julia K et al. reported that an increase in INR indicates a more severe condition (for example, the activity of coagulation factors V, VIII, IX, and XIII decreases, the amount of fibrinogen monomers and D-dimer significantly increases, and the incidence of progressive intracranial hemorrhage increases) and poorer prognosis in TBI patients.38 In addition to INR, their other study reported that significant deterioration of some routine coagulation parameters, such as lower levels of fibrinogen and lower platelet concentration, also suggests more severe brain damage.39

Thirdly, we hypothesize that changes in EVs (including PS positive EVs, BDEVs, pEVs, and eEVs) after TBI may be an important cause of changes in coagulation factors and platelets (Figure 4). This hypothesis is based on previous reports that injured brain tissue in TBI mice releases EVs (especially BDEVs) through the blood-brain barrier(BBB), causing systemic hypercoagulability and further developing into later stages of consumptive coagulation disease9,13,16,17;However, targeted inhibition of EV’s procoagulant activity11 or mediation of macrophage clearance of EVs14 can inhibit the occurrence and progression of TBI-IC, thereby improving the prognosis of mice. And, we previously reported that brain cells release EVs rich in PS and TF (especially BDEVs) through the damaged BBB into peripheral blood; PS on the EV membrane provides a reaction platform for the assembly of Tenase complexes (TF, FVII, and Ca2+), key factors involved in the initiation and dissemination of exogenous coagulation pathways, in the early post injury period.10,11 At the same time, the assembly of Tenase resulted in significant consumption of VII, X, thrombin, and fibrinogen.11 EVs also adhere to and activate platelets. On the one hand, activated platelets directly participate in coagulation and form strong thromboemboli with fibrin; On the other hand, platelets activated by BDEVs release more pEVs, further spreading the coagulation cascade.11 This is consistent with our data, that is, the proportion of BDEVs was 8.029 ± 3.451 (%) 24 h after TBI, and decreased to 5.252 ± 2.188 (%) 7 days after TBI, while the proportions of eEVs and pEVs all increased (Figure 4F).12 It is interesting that the proportion of PS positive EVs has also decreased, which may be due to the decrease in the proportion of BDEVs. Because we previously reported that BDEVs contains much more PS than pEVs and eEVs.11 The concentration and activity of platelets significantly decreased 24 h after injury as platelets were consumed, although the platelet concentration still fluctuated within the normal range at this time (Figure 3C). The important reasons for platelet consumption and endothelial dysfunction in the early stage of TBI are the activation of platelets and endothelial cells, which generate a large amount of pEVs and eEVs.12,13 We believe this is the reason for the increase in the proportion of pEVs and eEVs. The concentration of EVs in patients gradually increased 3 and 7 days after injury, indicating that EVs still plays a role in promoting coagulation in the later stage of TBI (Figure 4). However, we are still unclear about the regulatory mechanisms underlying the increase in EVs concentration and proportion at 3 and 7 days after TBI.

We supplemented some data and analyzed the relationship between thrombosis and several core coagulation parameters (see Table 3). Our results indicate that abnormalities in PT, MA, Cl, DD, and ADP suggest the occurrence of thrombotic events; factor II, BDEV, and PS positive EV may play a key role in the occurrence of thrombotic events. More reports are needed on whether they can serve as biomarkers for thrombotic events after TBI. In addition, we found no statistically significant differences in coagulation parameters between patients with secondary intracranial hemorrhage and those without (no data available). Therefore, we are unsure whether there is a correlation between these coagulation parameters and secondary intracranial hemorrhage in TBI. Although literature has reported that D-dimer and Ly30% may be potential biomarkers for secondary cerebral hemorrhage in TBI patients,40,41 our data do not provide relevant evidence, which may be related to the small number of cases we have collected.

In summary, we found that TBI patients developed a hyperfibrinolysis within 24 h after injury and gradually progressed to a hypercoagulable state 7th days after injury, and platelets, coagulation factors, and EVs may play important role in the occurrence and development of TBI-IC. We presented data on thromboelastography and coagulation factor activity of TBI patients at different time points; more importantly, we link EVs with the mechanisms underlying the occurrence and development of clinical TBI-IC. Our report describes the evolution of TBI-IC in patients within 7 days after injury, providing references for interventions in hyperfibrinolysis and hypercoagulable state after clinical TBI.

Limitations of the study

There are five limitations to this study. Firstly, we did not capture the patient’s first hypercoagulable state after injury, making it difficult to provide more information and reference for the timing of the patient’s first anticoagulation. Secondly, we did not display the continuous changes of patients from day 1st to day 7th after injury, and whether day 3th after injury is the boundary point between high fibrinolysis and high coagulation is still a debatable issue. Thirdly, we do not have sufficient cases to provide coagulation data one week after injury as most patients have already left the hospital. Fourthly, this report is an observational study and we did not conduct any intervention or inhibition trials on EVs. Fifthly, as an observational study, we did not elaborate on or discuss in detail the correlations between these abnormal coagulation parameters and the occurrence of thrombotic events as well as secondary intracranial hemorrhage events. We intend to fully explore the correlations between them in future research based on the findings of the present study.

Experimental model and study participant details

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for patients and volunteers

Inclusion criteria for TBI patients: 1) Adult, male or female, 18 – 80 years old; 2) clear history of traumatic brain injury; 3) craniocerebral CT shows epidural hematoma, subdural hematoma, intracerebral hematoma, cerebral contusion, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and mixed hematoma, with or without cerebral hernia and skull fracture; 4) informed consent was obtained. Exclusion criteria for TBI patients: 1) history of oral anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs; 2) infectious diseases before injury; 3) patients with combined dysfunction of important organs (such as liver and kidney) or coagulation diseases before injury; 4) complex trauma with extracranial injuries; or 5) cancer.

Selection and exclusion criteria for healthy volunteers: 1) Adult, male or female, 18 - 80 years old; 2) No history of trauma, surgery, or blood transfusion; 3) No history of oral anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs; 4) No no serious illness; 5) the informed consent was obtained. Exclusion criteria for healthy volunteers: 1) history of oral anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs; 2) history of trauma, surgery, and blood transfusion in previous 3 months; 3) coagulation disorder, heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, cerebrovascular diseases and cancer.

Ethics approval and consent

This study has been approved and consented by Institutional Review Board for Ethics of Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University on December 10, 2023 (registration number: KY2023-246-01). All TBI patients or their relatives and healthy volunteers have signed informed consent forms.

Method details

Peripheral venous blood sampling

Venous blood samples (12 ml) were collected from all TBI patients within 24 hours, on the 3rd and 7th day after TBI. One blood samples were collected from each healthy volunteer. The tube containing anticoagulant ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid are used for Complete Blood Count (CBC), with each tube collecting 2ml of whole blood (containing 1.8mg ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid per milliliter of blood). The tube containing anticoagulant sodium citrate is used for experiments other than CBC, with a volume (blood): volume (sodium citrate) =9:1. The blood samples are collected for CBC and thromboelastography (TEG). The plasma samples are collected for conventional coagulation tests (CCTs), platelet activity (Platelet Aggregation Test, PAgT), coagulation factor activity (factor VII, factor X, factor II) and EVs counts and proportion (flow cytometry experiment).

CCTs

The fully automated coagulation analyzer (Werfen ACL-TOP700) and commercial reagent kit are used for CCTs including prothrombin time (PT), thrombin time (TT), fibrin degradation products (FDP), Fibrinogen (FIB) and plasma D-dimer (DD).

TEG

Mix 1ml of blood (volume of sodium citrate: volume of blood=1:9) with 20ul of kaolin reagent at room temperature to obtain a sample. Add 20 μL of calcium chloride solution to the TEG (Haemoscope Corporation, TEG5000) reaction cup. Take 340μl of sample and add it to the reaction cup, then start the detection.

Detection of factor II, factor X, and factor VII activity

The fully automated coagulation analyzer (Werfen ACL-TOP700) is used to detect coagulation factor activity. Mix the plasma of the sample with plasma without factors II/X/VII. And the activity (%) of factors II/X/VII was calculated by comparing the clotting time with the standard curve.

CBC

The fully automatic blood analyzer (Mindray BC6900) and its reagents are used to detect the concentrations of hemoglobin, erythrocyte, and platelets in samples.

Detection of platelet activity

The fully automatic platelet aggregation analyzer (Techlink Biomedical) and accompanying inducer reagents are used for the detection of platelet activity. The sample (volume of sodium citrate: volume of blood=1:9) was centrifuged at 200g for 5 minutes to obtain platelet rich plasma (PRP). PRP was centrifuged at 2000g for 10 minutes to obtain platelet poor plasma (PPP). Adenosine Diphosphate (ADP) powder was dissolved in 0.9% NaCl to 150 μ mol/L and stabilized at room temperature for 5 hours to obtain ADP reagent; Collagen powder was dissolved in 0.9% NaCl to 100μg/mL and stabilized at room temperature to obtain collagen reagent; Sodium arachidonate was dissolved in 0.9% NaCl to 5 mg/mL and stabilized at room temperature for 5 hours to obtain arachidonic acid (AA) reagent. Mix ADP, collagen, or AA reagents with PRP separately and place the mixture in a platelet aggregation analyzer for detection. The transmittance of PPP is set to 100%, and the transmittance of PRP is set to 0%. Calculate the aggregation rate based on the change in transmittance(A) after adding the activator: Platelet aggregation rate = (A - 0%) / (100% - 0 %) × 100 %.

Extract EVs

Centrifuge at 120 g for 20 min at room temperature after collect fresh venous blood to remove large cells such as red blood cells and white blood cells, and obtain PRP; PRP was centrifuged at 1500 g for 20 minutes at room temperature to remove platelets and obtain PPP; PPP was centrifuged at 13000 g for 2 minutes at 4°C to completely remove cellular components and large cell debris to obtain cell free plasma(CFP). CFP rich in EVs can be used for subsequent flow cytometry detection.

Flow cytometry

We used flow cytometry (BD Accuri C6 Plus) to detect EVs in the peripheral blood of healthy volunteers and TBI patients. In brief, mix 100 μ l CFP with antibody at room temperature and incubate for 20 minutes, then dilute with Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) buffer (CFP volume: buffer volume=1:4). The diluted CFP was detected by flow cytometry. Firstly, EVs smaller than 1μm were screened by using standard microbeads (Spherotech, United States) of 0.88, and 1.35μm, as shown in Figures S1 and S2 (data not shown). Then, the position of the flow cytometer gate was set based on the expression of specific cell markers, as shown in Figures S3–S7 (data not shown). The following antibodies are used to detect specific cell markers: (1) FITC conjugated glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) antibody (1μg/ml, Thermo Fisher) was used for the detection of BDEVs; (2) PE conjugated CD42b antibody (1μg/ml, biolegend) for detecting platelet-derived EVs (pEVs); (3) PE conjugated CD144 antibody(2μg/ml, biolegend) is used for detecting endothelial cell-derived EVs (eEVs); (4) APC conjugated Annexin V antibody (5 μl/test, BD Biosciences) was used to bind phosphatidylserine (PS) on the surface of EVs. IgG of the same type as the antibody is used as a negative control. All buffer solutions were filtered through a 0.1μm filter(EDM Millipore, Billerica, MA)before use to reduce interference from small particles.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Continuous variable data is presented in the form of mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variable data is presented in the form of percentages. We used GraphPad Prism (V. 10.4.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, U.S.) for statistical analysis and drawing Figures 1, 2, 3, and 4. First, the normality of the quantitative data was tested. For data with a normal distribution, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed; for non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test was used. The Bonferroni post-hoc test was conducted in both cases. The statistical method used for Tables 2, 3, and S1 obtained through SPSS (V.26.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) analysis are the linear mixed effects model. P<0.05 is defined as statistical difference.
