Factors influencing pediatric COVID-19 vaccination uptake: a quantitative investigation in the Philippines
Authors: Maria Margarita Lota, Paulyn Jean Acacio-Claro, Florian Vogt, Evalyn Roxas, Alvin Abeleda, Soledad Natalia Dalisay, Madilene Landicho, Yoshiki Fujimori, Jan Zarlyn Rosuello, Jessica Kaufman, Margaret Danchin, Vicente Belizario
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCOVID-19 vaccines were recommended for the pediatric population in the Philippines in February 2022, after adults, due to both the direct and indirect effects of COVID-19. Despite ongoing vaccination efforts in the Philippines, pediatric COVID-19 vaccination uptake remains low. As such, this study aimed to determine the enablers and barriers of pediatric COVID-19 vaccination uptake amongst children 5–11 years in the Philippines.MethodsA cross-sectional survey among 546 caregivers of children aged 5–11 years was conducted onsite in three selected regions in the Philippines with low COVID-19 vaccine coverage between July and October 2023. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with pediatric COVID-19 vaccination uptake.ResultsOf the respondents, 143 caregivers had at least one child vaccinated against COVID-19, while 395 had none. The most common reasons for vaccination were protection against COVID-19 (93.7%), influence of government-mandated regulations (48.3%), and recommendation of healthcare workers (19.6%). High vaccine confidence (79%), high perceived vaccine benefit (81.1%), and perceived need for vaccination (78.3%) were enablers of vaccination. Among those who did not vaccinate, the most common reasons were distrust in vaccine safety (58.5%), perception that the child was too young (25.3%), and the child not wanting to get vaccinated (18.2%). Caregivers who decided not to vaccinate also had concerns about side effects (35.7%), low perceived need for COVID-19 vaccination (21.5%), and disagreed at requiring vaccinations for schooling (26.3%). Caregiver age [aOR = 3.00; 95% CI 1.65–5.46 for adults 30–45 years vs. aOR = 2.65; 95% CI 1.17–5.5.99 for adults 46–59 years], and perceived need for vaccination (OR = 2.56; 95% CI 1.31–5.03) were associated with pediatric COVID-19 vaccination uptake in the multivariable analysis. These factors were compared to caregivers aged 18–29 and those who responded ‘neutral’ to the survey, respectively.ConclusionsKey factors associated with COVID-19 pediatric vaccination uptake in the Philippines were perceived protection against COVID-19 illness, government mandated regulations and healthcare worker recommendations, while non-vaccination was associated with safety concerns, and children’s age and preference to not be vaccinated. Understanding the social and behavioral drivers among caregivers may help policy decision-makers and implementers develop targeted interventions to enhance uptake of COVID-19 and routine childhood vaccines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24654-4.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has significantly impacted global health systems since its emergence in 2019. Both adult and pediatric populations have been affected by COVID-19 infection, resulting in over 7 million recorded deaths globally [1]. Aside from the health impacts in children, albeit less severe than adult population groups, the pandemic also affected children’s education, the financial stability of households, and other health-related conditions (e.g., increasing malnutrition, vaccine-preventable diseases due to lower routine vaccine uptake, domestic violence, exploitation, and abuse) [2]. To address the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, COVID-19 vaccines were developed to protect individuals against severe disease leading to hospitalization and death, while also reducing transmission. Vaccines, originally recommended to high-risk groups and healthy adults, were subsequently authorized for children 5–11 years globally in January 2022, while initiatives for pediatric COVID-19 vaccination in the Philippines launched in February of the same year [3]. Although COVID-19 vaccination was not made mandatory throughout the country, different policies were implemented in certain areas to restrict mobility of unvaccinated individuals and encourage vaccination, such as barring unvaccinated individuals from entering certain cities or riding public transport [4]. While the Majority of adults in most countries completed their 2-dose primary COVID-19 vaccine series, the proportion of children < 17 years who completed their primary series remains less than 50% [5]. While we now know that COVID-19 vaccines may not markedly reduce transmission, direct protection of children from severe COVID-19 illness, especially those at higher risk due to certain medical conditions, remains important and the persistent low pediatric coverage raises concerns about future pandemic vaccine programs [6–8]. Thus, understanding and addressing the reasons for low uptake in this population is important.

In the Philippines, the government recommended 2-dose primary vaccination for children aged 5–11 years in February 2022 [9]. Meanwhile, adolescents aged 12–17 years old were recommended to receive COVID-19 vaccinations since October 2021 to aid in the safe reopening of schools [10]. mRNA vaccines, such as those developed by Pfizer and Moderna, were preferred for pediatric and adolescent COVID-19 vaccination and given in lower dosages compared to adults [11]. Various initiatives were employed by the local government units (LGUs), including the creation of child-friendly vaccination sites to create a welcoming atmosphere for children [9]. Partnerships with public and private sectors were also done for the provision of incentives and other logistical support [12, 13]. Despite these efforts, only 38.2% in this age group were vaccinated at the national level as of February 2023 [1]. Region IV-B (MIMAROPA) had the lowest percentage (23.6%) of children who completed the primary series of COVID-19 vaccines, with regions V (Bicol) and VIII (Eastern Visayas) also facing challenges with coverage of 29.6% and 35.1% respectively [14]. Since its launch, no further expansion to children under five years old have been initiated. There was also no local recommendation for booster doses in children aged 5–11, while it was approved for those aged 12-17 [11]. In other countries, several factors have been reported that affect caregivers’ intention to vaccinate, including lack of information and evidence on COVID-19 vaccine safety and effectiveness, low perceived disease risk among children, and misinformation on social media [15]. However, there is limited/no data on the social and behavioral drivers of pediatric COVID-19 vaccination in the Philippines. Understanding the factors affecting parental COVID-19 decision-making and information sources in the Philippines may guide policy decision-makers for the improvement of public health interventions and strategies to enhance uptake of COVID-19, routine, and other vaccines among children in the country. Hence, this study aimed to determine the enablers and barriers of pediatric COVID-19 vaccination uptake in the Philippines.

Methods

Between July and October in 2023, a community-level cross-sectional survey was conducted to determine enablers and barriers of pediatric COVID-19 vaccination uptake as part of a larger, mixed-methods study about COVID-19 vaccine uptake, and routine childhood immunization uptake. Regions IV-B, V, and VIII, were selected as study sites since these regions were considered as priority areas to improve COVID-19 booster vaccine coverage in the adult population. Regions were further stratified into provinces, municipalities, and barangays. In coordination with the regional Department of Health (DOH) offices, two municipalities from each province were selected. Lastly, the Rural Health Units (RHUs) of the municipalities identified two barangays. This resulted in a total of 12 municipalities and 24 barangays included in the survey.

A stratified cluster sampling of primary caregivers aged 18 years and above who cared for at least one child aged 5 to 11 years in each barangay was purposively recruited by barangay health workers based on target client lists. Those aged below 18 years old and did not have children aged 0–11 years were excluded from the overall sampling. In total, 775 respondents were recruited for the overall study. Only 546 were included in the study assessing pediatric COVID-19 vaccine uptake after removing caregivers without children aged 5–11 years old. Caregivers with children below 5 years old were excluded from the study, as COVID-19 vaccination was not recommended for the age group. Similarly, caregivers with children aged 12–17 years old were not included since the overall study also assessed routine childhood immunization. Including respondents with children aged more than 11 years old would present difficulty in recalling information about childhood vaccination. Additionally, the 5–11-year age group was recommended to receive COVID-19 vaccines starting in February 2022, but uptake has remained particularly low since their rollout. Caregivers included grandparents as well as mothers and fathers.

The primary caregivers were then invited to attend an in-person meeting with the researchers to complete the survey face-to-face. Their children were not present during data collection. The survey was mostly self-administered with the researchers guiding the respondents and answering queries when needed. The survey was available in both English and Filipino. In areas where local dialects were spoken, trained data collectors fluent in the language assisted respondents, with interpreters available when needed. For participants with limited literacy, questions were read aloud and clarified as necessary. Validation of immunization records through the children’s vaccination cards issued by the health centers was done where applicable. However, as not all caregivers were able to present their child’s vaccination card, verification of reported vaccination status was limited to those with available documentation.

The paper-based survey questionnaire was developed using the Centers for Disease Control Vaccine (CDC) Confidence Survey Question Bank, World Health Organization Strategic Advisory Group of Experts Vaccine Hesitancy Matrix Questions, and WHO Behavioural and Social Drivers of vaccination (BeSD) survey [16–18]. The questionnaire underwent two rounds of face validity testing. Sociodemographic data was collected and outcome variables assessed including uptake of pediatric COVID-19 vaccines, along with the underlying reasons, perceptions, and information sources driving caregiver vaccination decisions.

Descriptive measures were used to describe the characteristics of caregivers. Binomial logistic regression was conducted to analyze the association between different factors and pediatric COVID-19 vaccination uptake. All analyses were done using STATA/SE version 15.1 [19]. Models for sociodemographic variables and for perceptions on COVID-19 infection and pediatric vaccination were tested. Employing a backward elimination technique, statistically significant variables (p < 0.05) in the unadjusted analyses, i.e., age group and employment status among socio demographic variables and the following variables for perceptions on COVID-19 infection, except ‘Children aged 6 months to less than 5 years old should receive COVID-19 vaccines.’, were retained and combined in a third model for analyses, were retained and combined in a third model for analyses. Variables in the third and final model were either statistically significant (p-value < 0.05) or deemed essential according to likelihood ratio test (LRT) outcomes (p-value < 0.05). This model was adjusted for the effect of municipality (nominal variable was added in the model) to account for the inherent differences in the implementation of vaccination at this level.

Results

The respondents, aged 18 to 73 with a median age of 34 years, predominantly consisted of females (96.9%), were married or in a live-in relationship (90.8%), were Catholic (83.2%), and had elementary or high school education (88.5%). More than half were unemployed (56.8%). Among those who had at least one child vaccinated, Majority were aged 30–45 years old (67.1%), married (61.5%), and had attained high school education (62.9%). Discussion

Achieving high uptake rates among primary school children has been challenging in most countries including the Philippines where only 38.2% of children aged 5–11 years completed the primary vaccine series [5, 14]. In this study, we found that the key social and behavioral drivers of COVID-19 pediatric vaccination were protection against COVID-19 disease, government regulations and healthcare worker recommendations, with non-vaccination driven by vaccine safety and child age and preference to be vaccinated. Regardless of the child’s COVID-19 vaccination status, the DOH remained the leading source of information, with local authorities, health centers, and healthcare workers also important sources of vaccination.

In this study, only a quarter of caregivers (26.2%) had at least one child vaccinated against COVID-19, with protection against infection being a driver of vaccine acceptance. Findings were similar in other countries where it was commonly perceived that COVID-19 vaccines reduce disease severity for children, particularly for those with underlying health conditions [20–25]. Among caregivers who vaccinated their children, most expressed high confidence in COVID-19 vaccines (79%), high perceived benefit of COVID-19 vaccination (81.1%), and high perceived need for their children to receive COVID-19 vaccination (78.3%). Their motivations and perceptions on vaccination may be attributed to the effective information dissemination from the DOH, LGU/health centers and HCWs, and mass media outlets including television, radio, and newspaper. In other countries, health advisories and information shared by government agencies and HCWs are also relied upon as credible sources [20–22, 26, 27]. To comply with restrictions and regulations by the government and institutions, caregivers had their children vaccinated to facilitate their return to school, enable travel, and engage in social activities [20, 25]. HCWs can also influence decision making on vaccination due to their credibility and their ability to foster trusting relationships with caregivers through empathy and understanding [28, 29].

We found that caregivers belonging to the 30–45 year old age group were three times more likely to have a child vaccinated compared to parents 18–29 years. Since children aged 5 to 11 years were eligible for COVID-19 vaccination in the Philippines, it was likely that their caregivers were slightly older and more aware of the benefits of vaccination. Similar to other studies, these age groups of primary caregivers had higher intention to vaccinate their children against COVID-19 compared to young adult caregivers [30–34]. Moreover, younger caregivers were more likely to critically appraise vaccines, carefully considering various factors before making an informed decision [34]. Although careful appraisal of the benefits and risks of vaccines does not inherently lead to vaccine hesitancy, there is a need for further research on how different demographics engage with, interpret, and evaluate vaccination information. In addition, perceived need for vaccines was a factor of vaccination uptake. High perceived need was found to be an enabler in this study, while low perceived need was a barrier, albeit insignificant. Recent research has highlighted the impact of high complacency, defined as the belief that COVID-19 vaccination is unnecessary, as a powerful influence on parental decisions regarding uptake of COVID-19 vaccination for their children [35]. Furthermore, significant associations between complacency and vaccine hesitancy were identified in another study [36].

Vaccine safety and fear of side effects was a major concern amongst parents of unvaccinated children (72.3%). A significant proportion of caregivers expressed low vaccine confidence (35.7%). This distrust May still be related to the Dengvaxia controversy in 2017, which led to public panic, anxiety, and diminished confidence in Dengvaxia and other vaccines, including routine childhood and COVID-19 vaccines [37, 38]. The decline in vaccine uptake May be partly attributed to public distrust stemming from the 2017 Dengvaxia controversy, which was linked to reports of adverse events and led to the suspension of school-based dengue immunization programs [37, 39]. The incident triggered politicized media coverage and widespread misinformation, eroding public confidence not only in Dengvaxia but also in other vaccines, including routine childhood immunizations [37, 38]. including fear of potential side effects such as fever, headache, muscle pain, fatigue, fertility issues, myocarditis, and even death were commonly reported as reasons for parental hesitancy to have their child vaccinated against COVID-19 [15, 25, 40–42].

Additionally, children not wanting to get vaccinated and children being sick were also frequent reasons for non-vaccination in this study. In other countries, respect for child’s autonomy and presence of comorbidities in children were considered in parental decision-making regarding vaccination, which can lead to vaccine hesitancy [22, 25, 43, 44] Further, a notable proportion showed low perceived need, probably attributable to low perceived threat to COVID-19, low perceived risk for children, and non-requirement of vaccination to schools [15, 45–47]. Vaccinated caregivers may also no longer perceive vaccination as a necessity for their children since they themselves have already received the vaccines. These perceptions may also be an impact of mass media outlets, which were among the leading sources of information for these caregivers. Due to the proliferation of misinformation, there is an urgent need to combat fake news and promote accurate and reliable information on vaccines.

The findings of this study have several implications for improving delivery of pediatric COVID-19 vaccines. In particular, health promotion and education materials need to focus on the benefits of COVID-19 vaccines. This can be done by incorporating real-life stories and narratives instead of focusing solely on COVID-19 facts and statistics [48]. Such information may positively influence the perceived need of caregivers to vaccinate their children. To address the distrust in vaccine safety/fear of side effects, enhancing risk communication focusing on the benefits and risks of vaccination through ongoing capacity training of HCWs, peer educators, and community leaders. Furthermore, strategies to improve health service delivery may include flexible opening hours, especially for working parents, and coadministration of pediatric COVID-19 vaccines with other childhood vaccines [32].

Some limitations were identified in this study. Non-selection of all regions in the Philippines due to logistical constraints May have affected the national representativeness of the study. As the Philippines has a decentralized health system, there May be variations in local policies, strategies, vaccine access, and other sociocultural factors that could influence caregiver responses. Additional data on sociodemographic characteristics of caregivers, including the number of children aged 5–11 years in the household, and verified data on the COVID-19 vaccination status of each child in the household would have enriched the study findings. This study also did not consider caregivers’ COVID-19 vaccine status as a predictor of pediatric COVID-19 vaccine uptake, which can be explored in further studies (Appendix C). Social desirability bias may have impacted parental reporting as researchers assisted the respondents to answer the questions face to face.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the factors that influence caregivers’ decisions regarding COVID-19 vaccine uptake for children 5 to 11 years in the Philippines. Key factors associated of COVID-19 pediatric vaccination uptake were perceived protection against disease, government regulations and healthcare worker recommendations, while non-vaccination was associated with safety concerns, and children’s age and preference to not be vaccinated. Understanding the social and behavioral drivers of vaccination can help vaccination program managers and implementers develop targeted interventions to improve uptake. These findings can be used to enhance immunization-related activities of different LGUs to enhance uptake of COVID-19 and routine National Immunization Program vaccines for primary school children in the Philippines.
