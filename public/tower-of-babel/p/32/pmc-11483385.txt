Self-Supplying Photovoltaic–Hygroelectric Coupling System Powering Internet of Things Sensors
Authors: Changbao Xu, Xiang Zhang, Jiahao Fang, Yuming Yao, Yong Zhang, Xulei Lu, Tingting Yang, Mingyong Xin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid advancement in the Internet of Things (IoT) has prompted a proliferation of sensor applications with increasing focus on harnessing environmental energy sources for powering these sensors. However, owing to the variability inherent in climatic and geographic conditions, a singular approach to environmental energy harvesting often fails to deliver sustainable and reliable power. Hygroelectric technology, leveraging the electrical coupling between nanostructured materials and water to convert moisture-derived energy into electricity, complements the advantages of light energy collection technology. In this study, an ion diode moisture-based power generation array was fabricated to yield an open-circuit voltage of 8 V and a short-circuit current of 3 mA under an 86.9% relative humidity (RH). Subsequently, integration of a photovoltaic module with the hygroelectric generator assembly via an energy management circuit augmented the system’s overall power generation, manifesting a remarkable 1150% increase over the original moisture-based power generation configuration. Furthermore, the incorporation of such a hybridized system bolstered the operational efficiency, extending the duration of continuous power supply cycles by up to 78.2% in comparison with the control group with no moisture-derived energy input. This pioneering endeavor unveils a novel light–moisture coupling energy-harvesting paradigm tailored for sustaining uninterrupted power provision in wireless sensor network nodes, and the harvesting of one energy source is not affected by the other energy source at all. With its inherent adaptability, this approach holds promise for deployment in outdoor environments characterized by low illumination levels and high humidity, presenting a versatile solution to address sensor powering challenges.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of the Internet of Things (IoT) industry has significantly increased the deployment of sensors across various applications.1−5 Because of the difficulty in laying transmission lines in environments like bridges, valleys, and tunnels, coupled with inadequate sunlight and wind power for traditional photovoltaic and wind-electric equipment, there is an urgent need to secure stable energy supply for sensors without causing ecological damage.6−8 Technologies based on triboelectric,9−11 piezoelectric,12−14 and thermoelectric15,16 effects are being developed to obtain clean energy directly from the environment.17 Water energy capture, represented by the hydrovoltaic effect18 of carbon nanofunctional materials interacting with raindrops,19,20 liquid water sources,21,22 and ambient moisture,23−25 provides a new direction for solving energy problems. The moisture-based power generation is less affected by weather conditions and light, ensuring continuous power output and enabling versatile applications with extended operational durations.

Despite the continuous operation of moisture-electric generators (MEGs), their output power typically remains at the microwatt (μW) level,26−29 with limited load capacity, thereby constraining their applicability in certain scenarios. In contrast, photovoltaic power generation offers higher power outputs suitable for most small/microsensors,30−33 albeit with limitations such as nighttime inactivity and shortened operational durations. Thus, there is growing interest in leveraging both sunlight and moisture, which are easily accessible in the environment, to design an energy management method that can match the two power generation modes, maximize the use of environmental energy, and improve the efficiency of energy conversion to meet the power supply needs of small/microsensors for extended periods.

To address this problem, Qu’s group at Tsinghua University developed a light-coordinated and water-induced coacting charge-separated MEG, with 2 orders of magnitude improvement over that of most conventional moisture-enabled systems.34 Ren et al., inspired by epiphytes absorbing ambient water and simultaneously capturing sunlight for dynamic photosynthesis, proposed an all-biobased hydrovoltaic–photovoltaic electricity generator (HPEG) scheme, with an output power of up to 1.24 W/m2.35 Hu et al. reported the first hydroelectric–photovoltaic coupling generator utilizing self-assembled sulfur-reducing bacteria-carbon nitride polymer (G.s-CNx) bionanohybrids for simultaneous harvesting of both aqueous and solar energy, which was capable of generating power for 10 days continuously.36 Although the above studies achieved excellent performance, these works stayed at the device level, emphasized the promotion of sunlight on hygroelectric power, and only discussed the effect of two variables of light and humidity on the internal ionization of a single device at the same time, which is beneficial for the integrated fabrication of the device, but if one of the variables is not in the effective operating interval, such as extremely low light or humidity, the overall performance of the device will be limited or even reduced. At the same time, how energy is controlled and stored in actual use has not been adequately considered.

To harvest both sunlight and humidity energy independently, a parallel light–moisture energy management strategy is necessary to design at the system level. However, the power generated by MEGs typically operates at the μW level, characterized by high voltage and microcurrent, while photovoltaic power generation can easily reach milliwatt (mW) levels even with small photovoltaic panels.37 This substantial disparity in energy magnitude presents significant challenges in the integration of the two power generation modes. To date, no technology or device has been identified capable of effectively integrating these two disparate power generation methods.

In this study, high-efficiency MEGs based on an ion diode structure were fabricated. As shown in Figure 1, MEGs were interconnected in series and parallel through a coating process to create arrays capable of delivering power outputs at mW levels. This study pioneered the design of a light–moisture coupling power generation system through a micropower controller and an energy storage capacitor. This system independently harvests environmental energy from both sunlight and humidity. The harvesting of one energy source is entirely independent of the other, allowing for the conversion, storage, and utilization of photovoltaic and humidity energy together. Furthermore, the feasibility of this system for supplying energy to wireless sensor nodes was verified, and its operational performance under the conditions of reduced illumination and high humidity was explored.

Materials and Methods

Material Preparation

To fulfill the function of the MEGs, the primary materials required for fabrication include nano-Al2O3, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), poly(vinylidene fluoride)-hexafluoropropylene (PVDF-HFP), and other necessary components. Among them, nano-Al2O3 with a diameter of 60 nm and tetrahydrofuran (THF, 99%) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. CNTs (10–30 nm) were from Chengdu Organic Chemicals Co., Ltd. PVDF-HFP was provided by Arkema, and anhydrous ethanol was from Chengdu Kelong Chemical Co., Ltd. Prior to the formal fabrication process, the prepreparation of alumina slurry and CNT solution needs to be completed. Alumina slurry serves as the main raw material for the interlayer of MEG. The interlayer requires a porous, water-permeable membrane, allowing water molecules to pass through while isolating the upper and lower electrodes. To achieve this, alumina slurry containing the binder PVDF needs to be prepared.38 The specific fabrication method is as follows: First, 1 g of PVDF-HFP and 3 g of nano-Al2O3 (60 nm) were dissolved in a mixture of solvent and nonsolvent (solvent-to-polymer ratio of 9–12; nonsolvent to polymer ratio of 0.25–0.5). The dissolution process was carried out at 50–60 °C, and the low-speed stirring process continued for approximately 2 h. For the preparation of the CNT solution, 400 mg of CNT powder was initially dissolved in 80 mL of anhydrous ethanol solvent to create a CNT solution with a concentration of 0.5 g/mL. The resulting solution underwent ultrasonication for 2–3 h to ensure the uniformity of the upper electrode material and the complete dissolution of CNT in the solvent.

Fabrication Process of MEGs

The sandwich structure of MEG comprises CNTs in the upper electrode layer, alumina nanoparticles as the interlayer, and liquid metal as the lower electrode layer. The power output of the MEG is derived from the synergistic effect of the ion diode rectification and redox reactions at the electrodes. The constant humidity in the environment ensures a stable supply of water molecules, which are captured by the hygroscopic upper electrode material and undergo a gas–liquid phase transition to release thermal energy. Under the combined effect of thermal energy and the built-in electric field, water is ionized in the nanopore to produce hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions.39 Due to the selective transport of ions in the charged nanopore-limited space, anions move toward the lower electrode, while cations accumulate at the upper electrode. This spatial separation of anions and cations results in a continuous directed ionic flow, which is further converted into an electron flow at the electrode interface due to Coulombic attraction and redox reactions for the external power supply. To achieve a larger power output, the series-parallel integration of multiple MEG units can be achieved simultaneously through a simple arraying process.40 Specifically, highly reliable MEG arrays can be mass-produced using designed masks combined with blade- and spray-coating processes.

Each functional layer material of the array was fabricated layer by layer through different patterned masks, as shown in Figure 2. Initially, the lower electrode material, liquid metal, was coated onto the predesigned PET mask, and the mask was subsequently removed to obtain a lower electrode with smooth edges. Next, the second mask was applied and the alumina slurry was uniformly sprayed onto the mask through a compressed gas cylinder. Repeated drying and spraying ensure the film’s completeness and uniform thickness. During film formation, rapid interdiffusion between the solvent and the nonsolvent occurred, creating a highly porous and permeable structure in the polymer matrix. After complete drying, the CNT solution was sprayed to obtain the upper electrode. The number of series and parallel MEG cells in the array was controlled to design for the desired output voltage and current. The specific performance of the device under different environments will be discussed in Section 3.

Electrical Characteristics of the Power Converter

The disparity in energy output between photovoltaic (PV) panels and MEGs can be as much as 103 times. A single MEG typically produces power in the range of μW with high voltage and microcurrent, while photovoltaic power generation can easily reach mW levels. Challenges arise in selecting power controllers that can effectively manage both power generation modes. Common transformers, for instance, have standby energy consumption that may weaken or completely consume energy from moisture, hindering effective moisture energy utilization. Instead, using micropower controllers matched to the moisture power generation order of magnitude may cause damage from the high currents generated by photovoltaic devices. Furthermore, the MEG array’s output voltage is significantly affected by the load, making stable output difficult to achieve. The requirements for the power converter mainly include:

1)Wide input voltage and power range: capable of accommodating energy inputs from both photovoltaic devices (10 V, mW level) and MEG devices (10 V, μW level) simultaneously.

Wide input voltage and power range: capable of accommodating energy inputs from both photovoltaic devices (10 V, mW level) and MEG devices (10 V, μW level) simultaneously.

2)Low energy loss: ensures that energy consumption is lower than the energy generated by the MEG devices, preventing premature depletion of their output.

Low energy loss: ensures that energy consumption is lower than the energy generated by the MEG devices, preventing premature depletion of their output.

3)Energy storage connectivity: ability to connect to an energy storage module, enhancing system storage capacity under high-light and high-humidity conditions while also stabilizing energy generation in low-light and low-humidity environments.

Energy storage connectivity: ability to connect to an energy storage module, enhancing system storage capacity under high-light and high-humidity conditions while also stabilizing energy generation in low-light and low-humidity environments.

LTC3588, depicted in Figure 3a, is an energy-harvesting and power supply module commonly utilized in ambient energy-to-electricity conversion scenarios such as piezoelectric, photovoltaic, or magnetoelectric fields. LTC3588 integrates a low-loss, full-wave bridge rectifier and a high-efficiency buck converter, allowing charge to accumulate on the input capacitor until the buck converter efficiently transfers a portion of the stored charge to the output.41 With an input protection shunt voltage set to 20 V, it enables greater energy storage with a given input capacitance. Moreover, LTC3588 features two energy inputs, PZ1 and PZ2 (as shown in Figure 3b), serving as a common energy converter for photovoltaic and MEG power sources. It can be connected to external capacitor points and provide various voltage output modes, offering versatility in energy storage for photovoltaic–hygroelectric coupling scenarios. By altering the states of the D0 and D1 pins, different output voltage values (1.8, 2.5, 3.3, and 3.6 V) can be achieved. The photovoltaic input can be connected in parallel with two PV panels, each with specifications of 53 × 30 mm, 5 V, and 30 mA. LTC3588 effectively meets the requirements of hygroelectric and photovoltaic devices, isolates the load on the input side, and facilitates energy storage functionality after the external capacitor is connected.

Results and Discussion

Influence of Relative Humidity on MEG Power Output

As shown in Figure 4b, the CNT porous film as the top electrode utilizes the abundant specific surface area to enhance water adsorption and permeation. Figure 4c illustrates nanoparticles of nano-Al2O3 embedded in PVDF-HFP. A 5 × 20 MEG array was tested under different relative humidity environments to investigate the impact of ambient humidity changes on MEG output performance. With increasing ambient humidity, a significant number of water molecules are captured by the nanopores. As depicted in the schematic diagram in Figure 4a, more H+ cations and OH– anions are ionized by the built-in electric field. H+ cations accumulate toward the CNT layer, while OH– enters the nanochannels of the alumina film. The reverse directional transport of numerous anions and cations in high-humidity environments results in a larger open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current. Figure 4d shows the open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current of a single MEG under different humidity conditions. Figure 4e displays the array voltage curves over time under different humidity conditions. During the testing period of over 3000 s, the MEG was initially exposed to an environment with RH = 55%, where the open-circuit voltage slowly increased from 4.2 V to approximately 4.5 V. Subsequently, the device was placed in an environment with RH = 62.7%, causing the voltage to rapidly rise from 4.5 to 5 V and then gradually increase. Observations reveal a significant acceleration in the increase in open-circuit voltage (VOC) when the device is subjected to higher humidity environments with RH = 78.0 and 86.9% after a 10 min interval, respectively, indicating that higher humidity levels facilitated a higher VOC. This sensitivity to different humidity levels demonstrates that the array’s VOC is notably enhanced in environments with higher humidity levels. Various RH environments were created by sealing vials filled with different saturated salt solutions.

Figure 4f depicts the variation of the short-circuit current of the array device under different humidity environments. At lower relative humidity (RH = 55%), the initial current generated by the arrays is approximately 0.18 mA. As the humidity increases to RH = 62.7%, the short-circuit current significantly rises to a range of 0.6–1 mA. Correspondingly, the short-circuit current is higher in an environment with higher humidity. The observed proportional relationship between the short-circuit current and the humidity change demonstrates that the device exhibits sensitivity to fluctuations in ambient humidity, with higher humidity environments demonstrating enhanced energy conversion. Overall, the devices demonstrate robust power output across the typical atmospheric humidity range (RH = 55–90%), with the MEG array achieving a maximum output of 24 mW when the open-circuit voltage is multiplied by the short-circuit current. When a load is connected, the theoretical maximum power output is approximately 6 mW, which is one-fourth of the product of the open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current. We conducted a power analysis of the output performance of the MEG array by connecting the MEG array in series with a load (variable resistor) and measuring both the series circuit current and voltage across the load. The test was conducted in a high-humidity environment (25 °C, 78% RH), as shown in Figure 4g,h. The output power density of the MEG reaches a maximum value of 1.32 mW when the load is 3 kΩ.

Discussion of Energy Storage Capacitor Discharge Quality

The study of the humidity response of the device in Section 3.1 elucidates the relationship between generation voltage, current, and humidity, addressing how output performance is affected by weather conditions. However, the discharge characteristics of the system, particularly its ability to sustain operation over an extended period, still necessitate discussion. To simulate extreme outdoor conditions, such as darkness, wind, and dryness, we investigated the discharge characteristics of the system’s capacitor after 12 h of inactivity (i.e., without any energy input). As depicted in Figure 4i, experiments were conducted starting from a capacitor voltage below 2.753 V (below the minimum operating requirement of the load at the output) for 14 h, during which the system had no energy input, rendering the moisture-electric components and PV devices inactive. It can be observed from Figure 4i that the voltage of capacitance drops from 2.7 to 1.2 V after 2 h, and the capacitor voltage eventually stabilizes at 1 V after 14 h. This indicates that the capacitor’s stored energy is not completely depleted over time but rather remains stable at 1 V in a low-power state. In the usage scenario designed for the light–moisture coupling system, where natural conditions are entirely devoid of light and humidity, the duration will not exceed 12 h. Hence, the energy storage capacitor of this system is capable of meeting the requirement of prolonged outdoor operation under extreme weather conditions without any energy input.

Design and Structure of the Photovoltaic–Hygroelectric Coupling System

In the circuit design of the light–moisture coupling power generation system (as illustrated in Figure 5a), series-connected photovoltaic panels (PV cell) and MEG arrays (ME cell) are connected to the two inputs of the rectifier bridge of the LTC3588 module with the negative pole grounded. This configuration ensures that if one energy source fails, the other can serve as an alternative. Capacitors C1 and C2 in the circuit are used as input and output capacitors, respectively. The rectified output is stored on capacitor C1 at the VIN pin, serving as an energy reservoir for the buck converter. Capacitor C2 at the VCC pin stores residual electrical energy not consumed by the load in real time. The charging and discharging of C2 occur simultaneously, ensuring uninterrupted power supply to the load while safeguarding the normal operation of the MEGs and PV panels. This “buffer” effect of output capacitor C2 protects the system from damage caused by load voltage fluctuations.

Structure of light–moisture coupling power generation system. (a) Schematic diagram. (b) Layout of the light–moisture coupling power generation system and its stages and components.

Figure 5b elaborates on the connection of components within the light–moisture coupling power generation system and its application schematic for supplying energy to the wireless sensor nodes for environmental detection, corresponding to the physical depiction in Figure 6. The components involved in energy harvesting and signal transmission include the PV cell, MEG cell, input capacitor, LTC3588, and output capacitor. The electrical energy generated by the PV cell and ME cell is routed to the infrared radar proximity sensor for environmental distance sensing after undergoing energy conversion by LTC3588. A single PV panel (53 × 30 mm) can produce a maximum voltage of 5 V and a maximum current of 30 mA, which is considerably less than 20 V when two PV panels are connected in series, aligning with the requirements of LTC3588. The two photovoltaic panels increased the energy input power (open-circuit voltage multiplied by short-circuit current) of the system from 24 to 300 mW, an increase of 1150%. Additionally, a miniature signal transmitter module and a signal receiver are integrated into the system.

Layout of the developed energy-harvesting system prototype to power the IoT sensor.

In the signal reception and presentation section, to mimic real-world usage scenarios where the transmitter and receiver are situated far apart, the signal receiver is powered solely from the 3.3 V-regulated power supply, unlike the transmitter, which draws energy from the photovoltaic–hygroelectric coupling module. The high-level output pin of the proximity sensor directly connects to the VCC of the transmitter module. When the proximity sensor detects an object movement, it outputs a high-level signal, triggering the energization of the signal transmitter. Subsequently, the receiver promptly received the signal. Conversely, when the proximity sensor is in sleep mode, the transmitter remains unpowered and the receiver is in standby mode. Upon an object nearing the antenna, the signal receiver generates a high-level voltage output, as indicated by the LED array illumination in Figure 6, signaling successful reception of the transmitter’s signal.

In Figure 7b, the system is powered by both PV and ME cells supplemented by an artificial light source simulating sunlight irradiation. Performance Analysis of the System

After the basic circuit characteristics of the system were clarified, an experiment was conducted to determine whether the MEG array effectively contributes energy to the system. Figure 8 illustrates the variation of voltage at the input and output terminals of LTC3588 under no-light conditions where RH = 86.9%, with (b–e) and without (e–h) the MEG array supplying energy. In the diagram, the input terminal voltage represents the output voltage of the MEG array, while the output terminal voltage reflects the voltage curve of the output capacitor. The charging and discharging phases of the output capacitor, along with their timing, are visualized in the bottom part of the diagram. During the discharge phase, the voltage drop is attributed to the cumulative work of the proximity sensor and transmitter, which continuously applies excitation. The number of times the proximity sensor operates continuously can be determined by calculating the number of voltage drops. The system’s operating state at different capacitance voltages is distinguished in Figure 8 using three colors: green for capacitance charging, yellow for fully charged capacitance, and brown for capacitance discharging. The difference between the two charging–discharging processes (b–e and e–h) lies in the continuous energy supply from the MEG array during the discharge process in the d–e section compared to no energy input during the g–h section.

Output and input voltage of the system at different states.

The output capacitance used in the experiment is 0.33F, with an initial voltage of 2.5 V. When the MEG array starts charging, the proximity sensor does not work because the minimum input voltage required for its normal operation is 2.86 V (b–e–h line). As the voltage drops below 2.86 V, the sensor does not function properly, reducing the system’s no-load energy dissipation and accelerating capacitor charging. Hence, during a similar charging process, the voltage growth rate of the a–b section is higher than that of the b–c section. Upon reaching 3.28 V (c–d and f–g segments), the output capacitor terminals no longer increase in voltage due to LTC3588’s control of the maximum output voltage. With continuous power supply from the MEG array at the input (d–e), the sensor operates continuously 139 times, lasting for 536 s. In contrast, during no MEG power supply (g–h), the sensor operates continuously 78 times before stopping, lasting for 244 s. It can be calculated that with MEG array energy supplied, the system’s continuous working time increased by 119.6% and the number of working cycles increased by 78.2%. Additionally, from the upper half of Figure 8, it can be observed that the MEG array maintains stable voltage at the output terminal. When no energy provision is needed, its voltage remains around 8 V, close to its no-load voltage. When an energy supply is required, its voltage fluctuates around 4 V.

Assuming that our light–moisture coupling power generation system with a 5 × 20 MEG array is deployed in an environment with RH = 86.9% for a long period of time, under the ideal situation without considering the leakage of capacitors, the proximity sensor operates 139 times from 2.86 V (b) to 3.27 V (c) within 492 s, and the sensor operates for 550 s from 3.27 V (d) to 2.86 V (e). It can be assumed that each 492 s charge enables the proximity sensor to operate 139 times, with each operation consuming 3.54 s of charge, and each operation requires 3.96 s for signal processing. Therefore, every 492 s of charging allows the sensor to operate 139 times, with each operation consuming 3.54 s of charge and 3.96 s for signal processing. In 24 h, the sensor can operate up to 21,818.2 times, and the MEG array’s power generation capacity can support up to 24,406.8 operations per day. If the sensor only needs to operate once every 10 min on average, the daily power generation is sufficient to power the sensor for 339 days. Thus, a 5 × 20 MEG array can simultaneously power 339 such proximity sensors. In many environmental information collection scenarios away from power lines, such as wildlife presence detection, alert water level monitoring, etc., distance sensors do not need to work continuously all of the time. If sunlight is also available, then the system will charge faster, allowing more sensors to be powered. The number of MEGs in the array is constrained by the fabrication process; however, theoretically, the number of devices connected in series and parallel can be increased indefinitely as the printing process improves, and the power generation and load-carrying capacity will also increase. Since the spraying and squeegee coating methods used in this study to fabricate the MEG arrays are suitable for high-volume mass production, the array size can be adjusted according to the system’s power requirements.

In summary, the MEG array can effectively serve as a standalone energy input for powering low-power devices. Moreover, the proximity sensor can operate more times over a longer duration in environments with abundant humidity conditions, ensuring sustained moisture-electric conversion for the array. Supplementary Figure S2 illustrates the input and output terminal voltage changes of LTC3588 when both PV panels and the MEG array power the system simultaneously while also considering the effect of light intensity on the coupling system performance. It provides test results under three different working conditions, validating the system’s performance with a single moisture power supply and the copowering function of photovoltaic–hygroelectric power under varying light conditions. The east coast of China has a high average annual humidity but lower than average annual solar radiation. These areas are experiencing rapid economic development and relatively high electrical loads. Since our device is capable of energy harvesting under low-light and high-humidity conditions, it is well suited as a solution to alleviate the problem of insufficient power supply in these regions and achieve self-sufficiency. Therefore, our research holds great practical significance and has a bright future in helping achieve the goals of energy savings, emission reduction, and carbon neutrality.

Conclusions

In this study, we have developed a novel system that harnesses ambient light and moisture for energy cogeneration, energy storage, and utilization. The power generation module comprises a PV cell with photovoltaic panels and a MEG array, which has multiple MEG units with the ion diode sandwich structure. The LTC3588 energy converter plays a crucial role in efficient energy utilization and storage, given the significant difference between the output powers of the two sources. It ensures stable voltage output with multiple adjustable steps, thereby enhancing the power supply capability of the MEG array to the load. The humidity response characteristics of 5 × 20 series-parallel MEG array were determined through experiments testing the power generation performance of MEGs at various humidity levels. The findings reveal that the array is capable of generating open-circuit voltages of up to 8 V and short-circuit currents of 3 mA under high-humidity conditions.

Furthermore, an application scenario was simulated in which the light–moisture coupling power generation system supplied energy to wireless sensors. The roles and working principles of radar distance sensors, signal transmitters, and receivers in this scenario were analyzed. Experiments were conducted under four different working conditions: no energy input, moisture charging, photovoltaic charging, and signal detection. The results confirm that the light–moisture coupling power generation system can effectively meet the practical needs of small and medium power nodes in wireless sensors. Additionally, the power supply capability of the moisture array was tested when connected to the load. It was observed that, in a capacitive power cycle with the same starting and termination voltages, the experimental group with the MEG array supplying energy showed a 119.6% increase in the continuous working time and a 78.2% increase in the number of working cycles compared to the control group with no energy input capacitance. In summary, the photovoltaic–hygroelectric coupling power generation system enhances the full-time power supply capability compared to a single MEG or PV device, expands its application scope, and provides a new way for the development of green energy technology.
