Transparent chest radiograph foundation model enables explainable human disease profiling
Authors: Chin Lin, Kai-Chieh Chen, Jun-Wei Huang, Wen-Hui Fang, Wei-Chou Chang, Kai-Hsiung Ko, Yi-Chih Hsu, Chin-Sheng Lin, Shih-Hua Lin, Dung-Jang Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chest radiography (CXR) is widely accessible, and its ability to capture subtle manifestations of systemic disease remains underexplored. We developed a contrastively pretrained multimodal CXR foundation model using large-scale image–report pairs and evaluated its capacity to predict diverse human diseases. Using 1074 phecodes derived from electronic health records, we trained linear probes on frozen image embeddings and validated performance across three independent cohorts (n = 90,911; n = 79,786; n = 60,282). The model significantly predicted 554 prevalent and 457 incident phenotypes, with 60 prevalent and 42 incident phenotypes demonstrating consistently high discrimination across all datasets. To enhance interpretability, both diseases and 57 radiologist-curated CXR features were embedded into a shared representation space. This co-embedding analysis identified 28 phenotype clusters driven by recognizable imaging patterns, including cardiomegaly, atherosclerosis, and ground-glass opacities, and enabled reconstruction of most predictions with strong explanatory performance (R² ≥ 0.85). Importantly, the embeddings captured imaging signatures associated with near-term cardiovascular events and critical illness. These findings demonstrate that CXR foundation model embeddings encode rich, clinically relevant information beyond conventional interpretation, providing a scalable and interpretable framework for comprehensive disease profiling and a foundation for future prospective validation.

Introduction

Chest X-ray (CXR) is the world’s most common imaging examination1. It reveals a wide range of thoracic pathologies, making it indispensable for routine diagnosis2. Recent advances in deep learning have enabled artificial intelligence (AI) to interpret CXRs with radiologist-level accuracy for many detection tasks3. However, these models depend on large, manually labeled datasets4, which are time-consuming and expensive to create5, and they can only recognize conditions explicitly annotated during training6,7. As a result, conventional AI struggles to capture subtle clues to rare or future diseases beyond its predefined labels, limiting the scope of CXR analysis.

Emerging foundation models in medical imaging address these limitations by using self-supervised learning on massive CXR–report pairs to learn general-purpose image representations8–10. Once pretrained on millions of studies, they can perform zero-shot predictions: ingesting new natural-language labels and predicting the corresponding findings without additional annotations8–10. Yet their zero-shot capability is confined to features mentioned in the reports, leaving emerging applications, such as predicting mortality11,12, physiologic age13,14, and future major adverse cardiovascular events15, out of reach. Fortunately, subsequent work has shown that foundation-model embeddings, combined with simple linear probes and minimal labeled data, can accurately predict diseases not specified in the pre-trained dataset16. To date, however, no study has systematically explored AI-CXR associations across a broad phenotype spectrum, a gap that our work aims to fill.

Despite notable gains in disease detection17,18, AI-CXR’s clinical adoption is hindered by poor interpretability—a characteristic “black-box” trait of modern AI19—that makes large-scale implementation challenging20,21. This creates a curious paradox: although radiologists assisted by AI-CXR achieve higher diagnostic accuracy than those without AI-CXR support, their performance still falls significantly short of the AI-CXR system alone22—a pattern that has been observed across other medical applications as well23. Existing explainability methods, like saliency maps, localize attention but not the underlying features24,25, and fail to capture fine geometric details. Moreover, AI often latches onto confounding “shortcuts”—inferring age, sex, or device presence—and uses these spurious cues for prediction, compromising generalizability26. A truly interpretable AI should identify which classic radiological signs drive its predictions, localize and quantify lesions, and then map those quantified features to multiple diseases.

Phenotype prediction performance

A total of 1074 phecodes met our inclusion criteria and were analyzed for both prevalent and incident outcomes. Supplementary Data 1 and 2 report, for each phecode, the case counts (prevalent) or event counts (incident) by dataset. Supplementary Data 3 and 4 summarize, for all 1074 prevalent and 1,074 incident phecodes, respectively, the discrimination achieved by (a) CXR embeddings alone, (b) demographic information, (c) CXR embeddings plus imaging view, acquisition position, patient race/ethnicity, gender, and age, and (d) the p-values testing the independent predictive value of the CXR embedding in multivariable models. After adjustment, 554 prevalent and 457 incident phecodes were significantly predicted in all three validation sets, attesting to the broad disease signal in CXR embeddings. Figure 2a shows the AUC distribution for 1074 prevalent classifiers; 60 achieved AUC ≥ 0.85 in hold-out/external and ≥0.80 in MIMIC significantly after Bonferroni-correction, led by circulatory (n = 21), respiratory (n = 12), and neoplasm (n = 7) phenotypes. Figure 2b plots C-index for 1074 incident predictors; 42 reached C-index ≥ 0.80 in hold-out/external and ≥0.75 in MIMIC, again dominated by circulatory (n = 21) and respiratory (n = 7) diseases. To further characterize model performance beyond discrimination metrics, we evaluated calibration statistics and predictive values for representative phenotypes. Calibration performance was summarized using calibration intercept, calibration slope, and Brier score (Supplementary Data 5). As expected for low-prevalence phenotypes, calibration statistics varied across datasets, reflecting differences in case-mix and disease prevalence. In sensitivity analyses accounting for potential confounding from device-related features, model performance remained largely unchanged. Across datasets, discrimination metrics were highly consistent with the primary analysis, with only minimal variations observed in AUC and related measures (Supplementary Data 6 and 7). Supplementary Fig. 1 reveals clear disparities: using anterior-poster (AP) vs. PA views caused moderate (0.05 < Δ ≤ 0.15) or large (Δ > 0.15) drops in 60% and 15% of tasks, respectively, with only 2%/1% showing improvements. Comparing White vs. Asian patients, 47%/5% of tasks showed moderate/large declines, while 5%/1% improved. Older age groups suffered more drops (35% moderate and 6% large for <50 vs. 50–64; 30% and 1% for 50–64 vs. ≥ 65), with few gains. Patients with fewer disease histories and comorbidities saw better performance than those with more. Acquisition position and gender had minimal impact, and Black/Other races performed comparably to Asian. To provide practical guidance for AP-dominant settings, we performed an AP-specific reliability analysis using the same predefined performance thresholds within AP-view subgroups. In total, 12 prevalent and 17 incident phecodes retained robust AP-view performance across validation cohorts (Supplementary Data 8), including cardiovascular or device/procedure-associated phenotypes such as cardiac pacemaker in situ, heart valve replacement, rheumatic heart valve disease, and tricuspid valve disease. These findings suggest that AP-view degradation is phenotype-specific and that AP-dominant deployment should be restricted to AP-validated phecodes.

Predictable phenotype grouping

Supplementary Fig. 2 presents the cosine similarity matrix of 512-dimensional regression embeddings for 102 high-accuracy phenotypes (60 prevalent, 42 incident), and hierarchical clustering identified 28 distinct groups. To assess whether these clusters could be explained by real-world disease co-occurrence, we further evaluated pairwise phecode correlations across the EHR using the phi coefficient (Supplementary Fig. 3). Correlations were generally weak and lacked a clear modular structure. This contrasts with the well-defined clusters observed in the embedding space, suggesting that the clustering is not primarily driven by epidemiological co-occurrence, but instead reflects shared radiographic representations captured by the model. Cross-disease transfer within clusters incurs virtually no loss in AUC or C-index (≤0.05 drop; Supplementary Fig. 4), indicating shared CXR signatures. Temporal stability of incident phenotype predictions

Supplementary Fig. 5 demonstrates a clear temporal decline in predictive performance across all 42 incident phecode models. In the 30–90 days window, most models show a positive ΔC-index, indicating better discrimination than their overall C-index; accuracy then falls progressively in the 90–365, 365–1095, and >1095 days windows. Despite this trend, most models remain within ±0.10 of their overall C-index at all follow-up intervals. Only a handful of phecodes exhibit declines exceeding 0.10 in ΔC-index at the longest time horizon, indicating that CXR-AI captures imaging signatures associated with near- to intermediate-term clinical manifestation in most diseases.

Discussion

In this work, we applied a contrastively pretrained, multimodal foundation model for CXR and demonstrated structured associations between CXR embeddings and a broad array of clinical phenotypes directly from CXR embeddings. Screening 1074 phecodes, the AI-CXR model significantly predicted 554 prevalent and 457 incident conditions, of which 60 prevalent and 42 incident phenotypes consistently achieved high performance. Hierarchical clustering of the resulting regression-coefficient embeddings revealed 28 coherent phenotype groups, within which cross-phenotype transfer incurs virtually no loss of accuracy. Explainable analyses—leveraging 57 radiologist-curated CXR features—reconstructed most cluster-level AI predictions with high discrimination and pinpointed a small set of signature imaging findings driving each cluster. Together, these results establish a scalable, transferable, and interpretable framework for comprehensive disease profiling from routine chest radiographs.

To predict cardiovascular events from a single CXR may at first seem surprising, but numerous AI-CXR studies have already demonstrated the ability of CXR to forecast left ventricular structural abnormalities27, congestive heart failure28, prevalent/incident low ejection fraction29, and future major adverse cardiovascular events15. Although many of these investigations focus on cardiovascular outcomes, they often lack interpretability30. Our explainable-AI analysis revealed that the model depends most heavily on markers of cardiomegaly, atherosclerosis, and ground glass opacity, which is highly consistent with previous radiologist observations31. These features share a key characteristic: when their classic radiographic patterns are present, they are strongly associated with cardiovascular disease, yet radiologists struggle to perceive their most subtle manifestations32,33. Another advantage of AI lies in quantification—previous work has shown that measuring the extent of aortic arch calcification improves the prediction of new cardiovascular events34. In addition, we observed that although many cardiovascular conditions are predictable from CXR, the underlying models nearly always leverage the same radiological features. In the present study, each phecode was modeled independently using separate linear probes. Future AI-CXR research may explore unified multi-task architectures that explicitly share parameters across related phenotypes rather than training independent disease-specific models. Just as kidney diseases can be predicted by the same set of radiological features. Indeed, prior studies have used CXR to predict future chronic kidney disease and found atherosclerosis to be the most relevant predictor35. Coronary artery diseases and chronic heart failure with volume overload create a pro-arrhythmogenic, fibrotic milieu that underlies atrial fibrillation and atrioventricular block36. Beyond the heart, cardio-renal mechanisms are activated. Congestive heart failure with fluid overload leads to reduced renal perfusion and raised venous pressures, which together precipitate glomerular hypofiltration and renal congestion37. Similarly, rheumatic disease and cardiovascular disease also share common underpinnings involving inflammation38. Most importantly, the explainable AI models achieve high R2 when reconstructing the original CXR-AI predictions, indicating that in the future, radiologists—once aided by AI to detect those subtle radiological features—could plausibly and accurately explain why these patients carry a high risk of cardiovascular-related diseases. Regression coefficient embeddings reflect clinically calibrated associations derived from real-world labeled data, whereas zero-shot encoding of phecode descriptions would primarily capture linguistic semantic similarity. Direct comparison between these two representational strategies represents an important future direction.

The use of CXR to elucidate the underlying etiologies of acute diseases and symptoms is a time-honored clinical practice, and we found that AI-CXR can accurately predict critical illness events, septic shock, and infarction. Indeed, there have also been studies applying AI-CXR to predict short-term mortality39 and acute respiratory distress syndrome40. By applying our explainable analytic approach, we identified that atherosclerosis, ground-glass opacity, emphysematous change, and atelectasis can contribute to future critical illness events. Mechanistically, ground-glass opacities reflect diffuse alveolar injury—whether infectious or edematous—and their severity correlates with progression to respiratory failure41. Atelectasis, though often subtle, compromises gas exchange and can precipitate pneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome in vulnerable hosts42. Aortic arch calcifications on CXR signal systemic atherosclerotic burden and reduced physiological reserve, predisposing patients to future events such as infarction or shock34. In the later fibroproliferative phase of acute respiratory distress syndrome, thoracic computed tomographic scanning often demonstrates features of emphysematous destruction43. Collectively, these findings underscore the dual role of CXR as both a window into chronic comorbidity and a real-time gauge of acute pathology, and they demonstrate that explainable, feature-driven clustering can stratify patients by risk and reveal structured radiological-feature alignment that supports biological plausibility but does not establish mechanistic proof.

CXR is a primary cancer screening tool for bronchial and laryngeal carcinoma, primary respiratory malignancies, secondary bone metastases, and even end-stage cachexia. Our analysis shows that its predictive power stems largely from radiographic signs of atelectasis, nodules, and ground-glass opacities. Pulmonary nodules and ground-glass opacities on chest imaging are classic radiologic indicators of neoplastic processes—often reflecting malignant lesions—and therefore warrant prompt evaluation for advanced malignancy and associated cachexia44,45. Although atelectasis itself is not a cancer-specific finding, in advanced malignancy it often reflects central bronchial obstruction by a tumor—classically producing the “Golden S sign” on chest radiographs—and thus serves as a radiologic marker of highly aggressive disease46. Fibrosing alveolitis is another hallmark CXR finding, but radiologists’ accuracy is poor47. Likewise, AI can boost pneumothorax detection3. These expected CXR-detectable conditions align with our findings; although AI may enhance diagnostic precision, final model development should be based on confirmatory diagnosis rather than solely on ICD codes.

Osteoporosis is a well-recognized CXR finding, but its detection accuracy without AI support remains relatively low48. In our analysis, AI-CXR also leverages associations with age-related conditions—such as atherosclerosis and cardiomegaly49—to boost its performance. Indeed, prior saliency-map analyses have shown that AI-CXR models attend to the cardiac region17, suggesting they may exploit cues outside the ribs to improve accuracy. However, osteoporosis is notoriously underdiagnosed, and a previous randomized controlled trial demonstrated that AI-CXR can meaningfully enhance detection rates17; therefore, this particular association may still hold valuable clinical utility. In contrast, the link between prostate disorders and CXR is somewhat unexpected: radiologists know that prostate cancer often causes bone metastases, manifesting as osteoblastic changes on CXR50, and we observed a weak univariate association in our explainability analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6). However, multivariate analysis revealed that AI-CXR primarily predicts prostate disorders via osteophyte formation and aneurysmal dilatation. Osteophytes typify degenerative joint disease, and aneurysms arise from advanced vascular degeneration. Prostate enlargement often coexists with metabolic and vascular risk factors, further linking these findings51. This parallels the osteoporosis example: while AI-CXR’s assessment of osteoporosis has been validated in randomized controlled trials, prostate disorders will likely require further study.

In addition to find many diseases can be predicted by CXRs, the key innovation in our study is a transparent, interpretable AI framework built atop the foundation model. Not only does it help radiologists interpret CXRs, but it also exposes spurious disease associations. By using the text encoder’s zero-shot capability, we cataloged an expandable set of common radiological features. In our explainable-AI analysis, these radiological features reconstruct most AI-CXR scores with very high R2, indicating radiology has already exhaustively mapped CXR signatures. Future work should map additional diseases to these classic features. In practice, AI can help radiologists locate and quantify these classic features, after which clinicians can integrate them into more accurate, multi-disease predictions. This approach may build trust and resolve the paradox of reduced accuracy in human–AI collaboration22,23.

Our study has several important limitations. First, our interpretability framework is representation-level and descriptive rather than causal. It characterizes structured associations within the embedding space and evaluates alignment with recognizable radiological features, but does not establish mechanistic causality. ICD-based phecodes reflect documentation timing rather than true biological onset, introducing potential temporal misalignment. Because ICD codes reflect documentation timing rather than true biological onset, it is not possible to fully distinguish subclinical disease present at baseline from true de novo disease development using retrospective EHR-linked data. Although temporal separation and exclusion of prior diagnoses were applied, short-term incident predictions may partially reflect subclinical disease present at baseline imaging. In addition, pixel-level interpretability methods (e.g., Grad-CAM) were not evaluated in this study. While our approach focuses on representation-level alignment between radiological features and disease embeddings, spatial attribution methods may provide complementary insights by localizing image regions contributing to predictions. Future work incorporating such approaches may further enhance clinical interpretability. Second, our explainable AI framework was built on 57 radiologist-curated features. Although these cover many classic CXR findings, they may omit novel imaging biomarkers not typically recorded in conventional radiology reports. Third, multivariable regression analyses may be affected by collinearity among radiological features within the same group, which could lead the model to select only one representative feature and potentially identify statistically driven associations lacking causal relevance. In addition, age is a strong determinant of cardiovascular risk and may contribute to part of the predictive signal captured by CXR embeddings. Furthermore, the stability of feature coefficients across datasets was not formally evaluated and warrants further investigation in future studies. In addition, attribution stability under probe retraining was not formally assessed. Fourth, although we analyzed 1074 phecodes, rare conditions with few cases may yield unstable estimates and wider confidence intervals, potentially overlooking low-prevalence outcomes. Fifth, variability in acquisition protocols and patient populations across institutions may influence feature-based reconstruction stability and overall model performance. In particular, we observed reduced discrimination in AP-view images, which are more prevalent in ICU settings and often reflect greater illness severity. Models deployed in AP-dominant environments may therefore require view-specific recalibration or retraining. Performance derived from predominantly PA-view cohorts should not be directly extrapolated to ICU-heavy or inpatient settings without additional validation. Sixth, demographic composition and potential bias. The internal cohorts were predominantly Asian, whereas the MIMIC cohort included greater racial heterogeneity and consisted entirely of ICU-based imaging. Observed subgroup performance differences may reflect case-mix composition, illness severity, and imaging view distribution (e.g., higher AP prevalence in ICU settings) rather than intrinsic biological variation. These findings should therefore be interpreted as domain shift effects rather than race-specific biological signals. Prospective multi-ethnic validation is required prior to clinical deployment. Finally, while the foundation model demonstrated robust discrimination across 60 prevalent and 42 incident phecodes, its real-world clinical utility remains untested.

In conclusion, we demonstrate that a contrastively pretrained, multimodal foundation model applied to routine chest radiographs can systematically profile a remarkably broad spectrum of human diseases in a scalable, transferable, and interpretable manner. We found that, beyond the traditional uses of CXR, AI-CXR has the potential to predict more than 500 phenotypes, with our most striking discovery being the ability to stratify risk for a broad range of cardiovascular-related diagnoses. Despite profiling over 1000 phenotypes, many conditions converge onto a limited set of classical radiographic signatures within a shared embedding space. This convergence suggests that CXR-derived representations capture fundamental pathophysiologic patterns rather than isolated disease-specific artifacts. Our analytical framework improves representation-level interpretability of AI-CXR by linking phenotype predictions to recognizable radiological feature patterns. However, the present retrospective study does not establish validated patient-level explanations or real-world clinical utility. Future prospective studies should evaluate whether patient-level explanations improve clinician interpretation, workflow integration, and clinical outcomes. Consequently, we anticipate that future workflows will employ AI to detect and quantify classical CXR findings, allowing radiologists—after review—to interpret a comprehensive disease spectrum through explainable AI. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate its clinical impact.

Methods

Study subjects

The institutional review boards of Tri-Service General Hospital approved this study protocol with a waiver of informed consent (C202305019). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We performed a retrospective, multi-cohort analysis of de-identified chest radiographs (both posterior–anterior [PA] and anterior–posterior [AP] views) linked to structured clinical data. To avoid patient-level bias, only one radiograph per patient was included—if multiple CXRs were available, a single image was chosen at random.

As shown in Fig. 1a, Tri-Service General Hospital’s Neihu General Hospital site (an academic medical center) provided 303,629 patients with at least one CXR during January 2011 to February 2022, which were randomly divided into a development dataset (n = 212,718) and a hold-out dataset (n = 90,911). To assess generalizability, 79,786 patients from January 2011 to February 2022 were obtained from a geographically distinct community hospital (Tingzhou Branch Hospital) as an external dataset. For further external benchmarking, we used 60,282 CXRs from the MIMIC-CXR database (version 2.0.0) as the MIMIC dataset. For each eligible patient, only one CXR was randomly selected before downstream dataset construction and model training. Thus, each patient contributed a single image to only one cohort, and no images from the same patient could appear across different datasets or cross-validation folds. Patients aged less than 18 years old or without records of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) were excluded from this study.

Phenotype definitions

ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes in each database were mapped to standardized phecodes using the publicly available Phecode library (https://phewascatalog.org/Phecodes_icd10cm)52. The timestamp of the CXR examination was used as the index date for merging imaging and clinical records. In the MIMIC-CXR cohort, diagnoses were obtained from the MIMIC-IV (version 2.2) “hosp/diagnoses_icd.csv” and MIMIC-IV-ED (version 2.2) “ed/diagnosis.csv” tables.

In both the hospital EHR and MIMIC systems, ICD codes are recorded at the time a note is completed—either in the outpatient, emergency, or inpatient setting—and therefore may not reflect the exact moment of disease onset. To account for this, we defined: (1) prevalent phecodes as those with a first occurrence within ±30 days of the index CXR date53. Any patient with a record of the same phecode more than 30 days prior to the index date was excluded from the prevalent disease analysis; (2) Incident phenotypes were defined as the first occurrence of a phecode recorded at least 30 days after the index CXR, thereby excluding diagnoses documented on the same day as imaging. (to avoid overlap with prevalent definitions). Follow-up continued from day 31 post-imaging until the end of available data. Patients with any record of the phecode before the start of incident follow-up were excluded from the incident disease analysis. To ensure robust statistical estimation, we further restricted our analysis to phecodes that had at least five cases (for prevalent phecodes) or five incident events (for incident phecodes) in each of the four datasets (development, hold-out, external, and MIMIC).

Foundation model training and chest radiograph preprocessing

As shown in Fig. 1b, the foundation model was pretrained using a contrastive, multimodal objective on a corpus of 1,004,314 CXR–report pairs54. CXRs—comprising both PA and AP views—were resized to 224 × 224 pixels and encoded by a ViT-B/32 image transformer, while free-text radiology reports were tokenized and encoded by a 12-layer transformer text encoder. Both encoders projected their respective outputs into a shared 512-dimensional embedding space, where positive image–text pairs were pulled together, and mismatched pairs pushed apart. A softmax-normalized contrastive loss with temperature scaling was used to align radiograph and report embeddings, encouraging semantically meaningful co-embeddings across modalities. At the conclusion of pretraining, the image and text encoders define a transferable foundation model: downstream linear probes (elastic-net regressions) are trained on frozen embeddings, enabling small-sample fine-tuning with as few as 128 positive and 384 negative examples to achieve near–full-dataset performance. Therefore, we used this foundation model to explore extensive phenotype prediction by CXR. Pretraining was conducted for 50 epochs using a fixed training schedule, with validation loss evaluated at the end of each epoch on a held-out 10% subset. The model with the lowest validation loss was selected as the final pretrained model. No dynamic early stopping was applied, as convergence behavior was stable and no evidence of overfitting was observed across training runs. This protocol follows the training procedure described in the original study54.

Fine-tuning method on phenotype prediction

We trained a separate linear probe for each phecode using embeddings extracted from the frozen foundation model on the development cohort. Specifically, for each phenotype, we fit an elastic-net-regularized regression on the 512-dimensional image embeddings, tuning the mixing parameter (α) and regularization strength (λ) by five-fold cross-validation to maximize the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC). Training was performed once per phecode, yielding a vector of regression coefficients that capture the relationship between the foundation embeddings and the clinical label. These learned coefficients were then applied without modification to the hold-out, external, and MIMIC datasets for out-of-sample evaluation. No additional fine-tuning or recalibration was performed on any validation dataset, ensuring an unbiased assessment of the foundation model’s transferability. Although development dataset images had been included in the pretraining corpus, all samples in the hold-out, external, and MIMIC cohorts were strictly unseen during both pretraining and fine-tuning. The foundation model weights remained frozen during downstream phenotype probe training, minimizing the risk of representation leakage from label supervision.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables describing patient characteristics in each dataset were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as count (percentage). Model discrimination for prevalent phecodes was quantified by the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC)55,56. For incident phecodes, we computed the concordance index (C-index)57. To evaluate whether CXR–derived predictions were independently significant, we fitted for each phecode using multivariable logistic regression (prevalent phecodes) or multivariable Cox proportional hazards model (incident phecodes) adjusted by imaging view (PA vs. AP), acquisition position, patient race, gender, and age. Statistical significance was determined using a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of α = 0.05/1074 (the total number of tested phecodes).

As illustrated in Fig. 1c, we then identified “high-accuracy” phenotypes meeting all of the following in each of the three validation cohorts (hold-out, external, and MIMIC): (1) prevalent phecodes: AUC ≥ 0.85 (hold-out and external) and ≥0.80 (MIMIC); (2) incident phecodes: C-index ≥ 0.80 (hold-out and external) and ≥0.75 (MIMIC); and (3) significance at the Bonferroni-corrected level in all three datasets. Discrimination thresholds were selected based on conventional definitions of strong predictive performance in clinical modeling literature and were required to be met consistently across independent validation cohorts with Bonferroni-corrected significance to ensure robustness. Phenotypes satisfying these criteria were carried forward to further analyses.

For these selected phenotypes, we performed stratified assessments across 12 predefined covariates—imaging view, acquisition position, care setting, race/ethnicity, sex, age group, and comorbidity burden. Within each stratum, we compared AUC or C-index between reference and comparison groups, computed the absolute difference (Δ), and reported the proportion of phenotypes with Δ within ±0.05 (“acceptable”) or exceeding ±0.05/0.15 (“moderate”/“large”) bounds.

Furthermore, for incident phecodes, we partitioned follow-up into four windows (30–90, 90–365, 365–1095, and >1095 days) to track CXR-AI C-index over time. In each interval, we excluded patients with total follow-up shorter than the window’s lower bound, treated events occurring after the upper bound as censored (non-events), and refit Cox models to derive the interval-specific C-index. For selected representative phenotypes, model calibration was assessed using calibration intercept, calibration slope, and Brier score. Calibration intercept and slope were estimated by regressing observed outcomes on predicted probabilities. The Brier score was used as an overall measure of prediction error. To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses. To mitigate potential bias arising from device- or procedure-related signals (e.g., implants, surgical hardware, or indwelling lines), we repeated all primary analyses after excluding individuals with implanted devices or prior surgical interventions.

Explainable analysis

To explore the embedding-level relationships among selected phenotypes and common radiological findings, we first treated each phenotype’s vector of elastic-net regression coefficients (512-dimensional) as its representation in the foundation embedding space. Pairwise cosine similarities were computed between all selected phenotype coefficient vectors, and hierarchical clustering was applied to reorganize phenotypes into data-driven groups based on shared imaging predictors. To validate the robustness of each cluster, we applied the model trained on one disease within a given cluster to predict the outcomes of all other diseases in that same cluster, then examined the corresponding changes in AUC or C-index to quantify any performance shifts.

Next, a panel of board-certified radiologists (Wei-Chou Chang, Kai-Hsiung Ko, and Yi-Chih Hsu) curated a list of 57 frequently reported chest radiological features (34 pathological changes or anatomical/morphological signs; 23 implants/devices or surgical scars/traces) using standardized clinical reporting terminology. The 57 radiological features were curated by board-certified radiologists using our institutional chest radiograph reporting terminology, with reference to standard medical and radiology texts, including the Oxford Concise Medical Dictionary and Essentials of Radiology. Synonymous or closely overlapping descriptors were consolidated before feature embedding to ensure consistent representation of radiographic signs. Synonymous descriptors were consolidated into unified feature labels prior to embedding extraction. To derive embeddings for each feature, we fed the foundation model’s text encoder two inputs—“<radiological feature>” and “No <radiological feature>”—following the same zero-shot prediction strategy used in prior work8. This yields two text embeddings, and we take their difference (“<radiological feature>” minus “No <radiological feature>”) as the feature’s embedding. Mathematically, zero-shot prediction compares the dot products of an image embedding with each of these two text embeddings and applies a softmax, which is equivalent to subtracting the text embeddings first and then applying a sigmoid. That sigmoid of the dot product exactly matches the output of a linear probe (logistic regression) using the difference vector as a coefficient, so our computed feature embeddings are rigorously identical to the regression weights one would obtain by linear probing—and can thus be analyzed in the same embedding space.

For joint visualization, we concatenated the phenotype coefficient embeddings and radiological feature embeddings and applied t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) to project them into two dimensions. Radiological feature embeddings were derived from the pretrained text encoder using standardized terminology and were independent of site-specific manual annotation. We also calculated cosine similarities between every radiological feature and every selected phenotype embedding to quantify their pairwise associations. Phenotype clustering was performed using cosine similarity of regression coefficient embeddings and was not derived from EHR co-occurrence frequencies. By mapping phenotypes and radiological findings in the same low-dimensional space and examining their similarity scores, we gain interpretable insights into which specific image features the model leverages when predicting each disease.

We also construct an “explainable AI mode” by combining radiological feature embeddings. Specifically, we first take the 512-dimensional image embeddings from the development dataset and compute their dot product with each radiological feature embedding (which, as noted earlier, are mathematically equivalent to the linear-probe regression coefficients), thereby producing a “radiological feature score” for each CXR. We then use these feature scores to predict the CXR-AI output for each phecode, fitting the model with non-negative least squares (NNLS) to avoid negative coefficients (which are difficult to interpret). After fitting, we extract the regression coefficient for each radiological feature on each phecode and treat the standardized coefficients as measures of independent association. Finally, we validate the explanatory power (R2) of these models and quantify any changes in AUC or C-index caused by using the “explainable AI model” on the hold-out, external, and MIMIC datasets. In the primary analysis, we used only 34 radiological features representing pathological changes or anatomical/morphological signs, but we also report results from an expanded set that includes an additional 23 features for implants/devices and surgical scars/traces. We also present results obtained using ordinary least squares (OLS) fitting for comparison.
