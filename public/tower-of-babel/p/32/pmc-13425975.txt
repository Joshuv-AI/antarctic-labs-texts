Identification of the AkLOX gene family in Amorphophallus konjac and analysis of its expression patterns under different treatments
Authors: Dandan Gao, Haoliang Shi, Ying Qi, Lifang Li, Min Yang, Feiyan Huang, Jianrong Zhao, Zhipeng Liu, Penghua Gao, Lei Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Amorphophallus konjac is an important specialty cash crop in China and is rich in konjac glucomannan (KGM); however, its long-term exposure to abiotic and biotic stresses has hindered the development of the industry. Lipoxygenase (LOX) is a key enzyme in plant fatty acid metabolism and stress response, playing a vital role in plant growth and development, regulation of secondary metabolism, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. To date, systematic studies on the LOX gene family in A. konjac remain lacking. This study aims to identify the A. konjac LOX gene family at the whole-genome level, analyze its sequence characteristics, evolutionary relationships, and expression patterns under various stresses, and provide candidate genes for stress-tolerant molecular breeding.ResultsBased on the LOX conserved domain, an HMM model was constructed, and 11 AkLOX family members were identified from the A. konjac genome. According to catalytic sites, the gene family was classified into three subfamilies: 9-LOX, 13-LOX Type I, and 13-LOX Type II, which were unevenly distributed on 4 chromosomes. The encoded proteins contained 848–945 amino acids with molecular weights ranging from 97,110.54 Da to 102,962.26 Da, all of which were unstable hydrophilic proteins. Phylogenetic and collinearity analyses revealed 6 homologous gene pairs between A. konjac and Amorphophallus albus, one pair between A. konjac and Cucumis sativus/Arabidopsis thaliana, and two pairs between A. konjac and Oryza sativa/Solanum tuberosum, indicating the phylogenetic conservation of the LOX family. Promoter cis-element analysis identified 23 types of cis-regulatory elements, most of which were related to plant growth and development, hormone responses, as well as biotic and abiotic stress responses. qRT-PCR results showed that the expression levels of AkLOX1/4/8 were significantly up-regulated under low temperature, drought, and MeJA treatments, supporting the reliability of the cis-element analysis. Under Pectobacterium carotovorum subsp. carotovorum (Pcc) stress, the expression levels of AkLOX2/3/11 were significantly up-regulated, showing an obvious stress-specific response pattern.ConclusionsThis study performed genome-wide identification and expression analysis of the AkLOX gene family in A. konjac, and clarified their sequence characteristics, evolutionary relationships, and stress response patterns. It was confirmed that members of this gene family are widely involved in biotic and abiotic stress responses in A. konjac, providing candidate genes for subsequent research on A. konjac resistance to Pcc, low temperature, drought and other stresses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12942-1.Keyword: Amorphophallus konjac, LOX gene family, Abiotic stress, Pcc stress

Introduction

Amorphophallus konjac is one of the major cultivated varieties in China due to its high glucomannan content and significant economic value [1]. In recent years, the continuous expansion of its planting area, coupled with global climate change, has led to various biotic and abiotic stresses during the growth and development of A. konjac. These stresses have become increasingly prominent, posing a serious threat to the sustainable development of the konjac industry [2].

Lipoxygenase (LOX) is a key enzyme involved in fatty acid oxidation within the α-linolenic acid metabolic pathway in plants. It catalyzes the oxygenation of polyunsaturated fatty acids to produce oxylipins [3]. and plays a role in plant growth, development, and responses to various biotic and abiotic stresses [4]. For instance, the Arabidopsis thaliana AtLOX1 and AtLOX5 promote lateral root primordium development and lateral root formation, while positively regulating resistance to Pseudomonas sp. [5]. The AtLOX2 gene enhances the α-linolenic acid hydrogenperoxide reaction by regulating chloroplast singlet oxygen bursts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby activating the jasmonic acid (JA) biosynthesis pathway and improving plant resistance to pests and pathogens [6]. In Zea mays, the expression levels of 13 ZmLOX genes significantly increase under stresses from Fusarium graminearum and methyl jasmonate (MeJA) [7]. The PtLOX gene in Populus plays a positive role in leaf wound healing and defense against pathogen invasion from the soil [8]. In Capsicum annuum, the CaLOX1 gene exerts a positive regulatory role under high-salinity and drought stresses [6], while in Panax ginseng, PgLOX3 is induced to be up-regulated under drought stress [9]. In Oryza sativa, the OsLOX10 gene positively regulates the salt-alkali stress tolerance of rice seedlings by catalyzing linoleic acid degradation, and negatively regulates the storage life of rice seeds [10]; the OsLOX3 gene positively regulates rice resistance to Pyricularia oryzae and drought, and negatively regulate seed quality [11]. In Ipomoea batatas, the IbLOX and IbAOS genes can be activated by the transcription factor IbNAC087 to promote jasmonic acid (JA) accumulation, and enhancing the salt and drought tolerance of Ipomoea batatas [12]. In Malus domestica, the MdLOX2.1 gene can be directly phosphorylated by MdCERK1 at Ser326 and Thr327 sites, which inhibits JA synthesis and reduces resistance to Fusarium solani [13]. In Medicago truncatula, the MtLOX24 gene positively regulates resistance to alfalfa to Spodoptera litura, Pseudomonas syringae pathovar tomato DC3000 and Fusarium chlamydosporum [14]. Given the essential roles of LOX genes in plant stress response, the members of the LOX gene family in A. konjac remain unidentified and poorly understood. Therefore, based on the published whole-genome data of A. konjac, this study identified the members of the AkLOX gene family and analyzed their physicochemical properties. By combining published transcriptome data with real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) technology, the expression patterns of AkLOX genes were characterized under Pcc, mannitol treatment, low temperature and methyl jasmonate (MeJA) stresses. The results establish a foundation basis for further elucidating the functions and stress-response mechanisms of the AkLOX gene family in A. konjac.

Materials and methods

Identification, physicochemical characterization, and chromosomal localization of AkLOX gene family members in A. konjac

The protein sequences of Arabidopsis LOX family members were downloaded from The Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR) database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) as seed sequences. TBtools software was used to performed BLAST searches against the whole-genome data of A. konjac (10.6084/m9.figshare.15169578) to screen for candidate AkLOX genes. The conserved domain (PF00305) of the LOX gene family was subsequently downloaded from the Pfam database, and a Hidden Markov Model was constructed using HMM search. Homologous sequences of AkLOX gene family members in A. konjac were retrieved using TBtools. The intersection of BLAST search results and HMM search results was taken to initially obtain 11 AkLOX gene family members, which were submitted to the InterProScan website for conserved domain validation, resulting in the identification of 11 AkLOX gene family members.

The molecular weight, isoelectric point, instability index, aliphatic index, and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) of AkLOX proteins were analyzed using TBtools software. The subcellular localization prediction of AkLOX proteins was performed via WoLF PSORT software. Transmembrane domains of AkLOX proteins were analyzed with TMHMM 2. 0. software. The secondary structure composition of AkLOX proteins were assessed using SOPMA software. The tertiary structure of the AkLOX protein family was conducted using SWISS-MODEL (http://swissmodel.expasy.org/).

Construction and analysis of phylogenetic tree of AkLOX genes in A. konjac

Multiple sequence alignment of LOX protein sequences were conducted for the monocots A. konjac, O. sativa, and the dicotyledons A. thaliana and S. tuberosum, using MAFFT (v 7.427, auto mode) [15]. Following the alignment, a phylogenetic analysis was conducted on the original dataset utilizing IQ-TREE (v1.6.10) [16]. The optimal substitution model was identified through ModelFinder [17], which determined LG + I + G4 as the most suitable model. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was generated with the Bootstrap value set to 1,000, while all other parameters were maintained at their default values. The resulting phylogenetic tree were then visualized and refined using the online tool iTOL (version 6; https://itol.embl.de/) [18].

Multiple sequence alignment analysis of AkLOX gene family members in A. konjac

Multiple sequence alignment of AkLOX protein sequences was performed using the MAFFT GUI Wrapper plugin integrated into TBtools-Ⅱ. The parameters wereconfigured as follows: Strategy: –auto, Maxiterate: 1000, Retree: 2, and Threads: 2. The resulting alignment was subsequently visualized using the Simple MSA Viewer plugin, with the configuration of X Relative Pos: 0.45, Characters Per Line: 180, and X Intervl Ratio: 0.85.

Gene structure analysis of AkLOX gene family members in A. konjac

The chromosomal location information of AkLOX gene family members was extracted from the GFF files of A. konjac genome annotation data, and visualized using TBtools software. The conserved motifs of AkLOX proteins were analyzed using the online tool MEME (https://meme-suite.org/meme/) with the maximum motif number set to 20 and other parameters maintained at default settings. The conserved domains of AkLOX gene family members were identified using NCBI-CDD. The profiles of protein domains, conserved motifs and gene structures were integrated and visualized using TBtools software.

Promoter cis-element analysis of AkLOX gene family members in A. konjac

The sequences of the core promoter regions (2000 bp upstream of the start codon) of the AkLOX gene family members in A. konjac were extracted using TBtools software. The cis-acting elements in the promoter regions were analyzed using PlantCARE software, and the results of the analysis were visualized with TBtools software.

Colinearity analysis of AkLOX gene family members in A. konjac

The genome files and annotation files for A. konjac, A. albus, Cucumis sativus, A. thaliana, O. sativa and S. tuberosum were obtained from the Ensembl Plants database (https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html). Collinearity relationships of LOX proteins among A. konjac and the other six species was examined using the One Step MCScanX-SuperFast program. The results were illustrated through the Dual Systeny Plot for MCScanX and the Advanced Circos programs. To estimate divergence times, aligned sequences and rooted topologies were transformed into MCMCtree-compatible input files, with calibration constraints applied to the rooted tree. A two-step MCMCtree analysis was conducted, starting with `usedata = 3, followed by an adjustment to usedata = 2. The parameters for posterior dating were configured as clock = 3, model = 0, burnin = 2000, sampfreq = 10, and nsample = 20,000. The resulting posterior chronological timescale was ultimately displayed as a circular time-calibrated phylogenetic tree using the R packages ape, ggplot2, and ggtree.

Different stress treatments and sample collection for AkLOX genes in A. konjac

In this study, the expression profiles of AkLOX gene family members in different tissues were analyzed using the transcriptome data publicly available in the NCBI database, followed by visualization of the analysis results.

Konjac plant materials were supplied by the Yunnan Urban Agricultural Engineering Technology Research Center and the Yunnan Key Laboratory of Konjac Biology. The plants were cultivated in plastic pots (upper diameter 20 cm, lower diameter 12 cm, height 14 cm) filled with sterile soil, and grown in a greenhouse at 27 ± 2 °C with a relative humidity of 80%, a photoperiod of 16 h light/8 h dark, and a light intensity of 2000 Lx. After reaching the three-leaf stage (3 months old), the seedlings were transferred to an illumination incubator (28 ± 2 °C) for subsequent experimental analyses. Each treatment included three biological replicates, with three individual plants per replicate.

Pcc treatment: A total of 100 μL of Pectobacterium carotovorum subsp. carotovorum (Pcc) suspension (1 × 108 CFU/mL) was artificially inoculated into the petioles of healthy A. konjac plants using capillary tubes (0.9 mm × 11 cm). Leaf samples were collected 24 h and 48 h post-inoculation, while sterile water was administered to the control group. Low-temperature treatment: Well-grown plants were incubated in an artificial climate chamber at 4 °C for 24 h, after which the leaves were harvested. Drought treatment: Plant roots were irrigated with 50 mL of 100 mM mannitol solution to simulate drought stress, and leaf samples were collected 24 h following treatment. MeJA treatment: The abaxial surfaces of the leaves were sprayed with a 100 mM methyl jasmonate (MeJA) solution, and leaves were sampled after 24 h. Three biological replicates were established for each treatment, with six plants pooled per replicate. All harvested samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analyses.

Expression profiling of the AkLOX gene family under different treatments in A. konjac

In this study, published transcriptome data from the NCBI database (Table S1) [19] were utilized. Total RNA was extracted from A. konjac plants, and cDNA libraries were constructed for paired-end sequencing using the NovaSeq X Plus high-throughput sequencing platform. Raw reads were quality-controlled and filtered to obtain clean data, which were then mapped to the A. konjac reference genome using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were calculated with RSEM based on genome annotation information, and read counts were normalized to FPKM values. Subsequently, the expression profiles of AkLOX family members in different tissues were analyzed.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis of AkLOX genes in A. konjac.

Specific primers were designed for each gene (Table S2), and the expression patterns of AkLOX family members under low temperature, MeJA, and mannitol treatments were analyzed by qRT-PCR. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the Takara SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Kit. Each 20 μL reaction followed this thermal profile: an initial step of 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The Actin gene served as the internal reference [20], and three technical replicates were performed for each sample. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method [21]. One-way nested analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare target gene expression among samples, followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test with IBM SPSS statistical software [22]. Histograms were created using OriginPro 2021 [23].

Results and analysis

Genome-wide identification and chromosomal localization analysis of AkLOX gene family members in A. konjac

A total of 11 AkLOX genes were identified, and named AkLOX1–AkLOX11 based on their chromosomal locations. The chromosomal mapping profile of AkLOX genes was derived from the genome annotation file of A. konjac. The results showed that four AkLOX genes (AkLOX3/4/5/6) were located on chromosome HIC_ASM_4; two genes (AkLOX1/2) were on chromosome HIC_ASM_3; two genes (AkLOX7/8) were found on chromosome HIC_ASM_5; and one gene (AkLOX9) was on chromosome HIC_ASM_11. Additionally, AkLOX10 and AkLOX11 were located on the scaffold fragments of CTG_17367 and CTG_2646, respectively (Fig. 1). These results indicate the uneven distribution of AkLOX genes across the genome of A. konjac.

Chromosomal localization of members of the AkLOX gene family in A. konjac

Physicochemical property predictions for the AkLOX gene family in A. konjac

Analysis of the physicochemical properties of AkLOX proteins reveals that they encode amino acids ranging from 848 (AkLOX9) to 945 (AkLOX2), with molecular weights between 97,110.54 Da and 102,962.26 Da. Their isoelectric points fall between 5.15 and 8.24, and the aliphatic amino acid index ranges from 81.37 (AkLOX9) to 88.96 (AkLOX8). All AkLOXs proteins are hydrophilic, with AkLOX7/8/9 being stable proteins while the others are unstable. Regarding subcellular localization, 6 AkLOX proteins (AkLOX1/2/4/5/6/10) are likely localized to chloroplasts, 2 AkLOX proteins (AkLOX3 and AkLOX11) are likely localized to chloroplasts and chloroplast-mitochondria, and 3 AkLOX proteins (AkLOX7/8/9) are likely localized to the cytoplasm (Table 1).

Protein structure prediction and gene localization

Secondary and tertiary structure prediction of the AkLOX gene family proteins from A. konjac

Predictions regarding the secondary and tertiary structures of AkLOX proteins from A. konjac indicate the presence of three distinct structural types. These proteins are characterized primarily by α-helices (34.39%−37.53%) and random coils (50.71%−54.31%), with a smaller fraction of extended chains (9.52%−11.91%). Detailed structures are presented in Supplementary Table 3 (Table S3).

The results from the tertiary structure predictions suggest that the AkLOX gene family demonstrates structural evolutionary conservation, which underpins its lipoxygenase function. However, there is notable spatial conformation variability among different members. Specifically, AkLOX2 and AkLOX5 exhibit more tightly packed helical domains, whereas AkLOX6 and AkLOX11 are characterized by a greater presence of disordered regions (Fig. 2).

Phylogenetic tree construction and analysis of the AkLOX gene family in A. konjac

To clarify the evolutionary relationships among AkLOX members, a phylogenetic tree was constructed for LOX genes from various plants, including A. thaliana, O. sativa, Z. mays, and A. konjac (Fig. 3). The results suggest that, according to their catalytic sites, these enzymes can be categorized into three distinct subfamilies: 9-LOX, 13-LOX Type I, and 13-LOX Type II. Among these, the 13-LOX Type I subfamily includes AkLOX7/8/9, while the 13-LOX Type II subfamily comprises AkLOX1/2/3/4/5/6/10/11.

Multiple sequence alignment analysis of the AkLOX gene family proteins in A. konjac

To further investigate sequence conservation differences within the AkLOX gene family, a multiple sequence alignment analysis was conducted. The results revealed a total of 10 conserved motifs identified across the 11 AkLOX genes. Among these motifs, Motif1, a 38-residue motif, comprises a highly conserved sequence specific to LOX genes, consisting of 38 amino acids (Table S4). Further investigation revealed 11 conserved amino acid sites within Motif1: including histidine at positions 1, 6, 11, 29, and 38; leucine at positions 3, 8, 25, and 34; proline at positions 16, 30, and 37; and glutamine, tryptophan, glutamic acid, and serine at positions 2, 7, 15, and 26, respectively (Fig. 4).

Analysis of conserved domains, gene structure, and protein conserved Motifs in the AkLOX gene family of A. konjac

To explore the similarities and differences amon AkLOX proteins, MEME software was employed to predict their conserved motifs [24]. The results revealed that ten conserved motifs were identified within the AkLOX family. All members contained a homologous domain comprising the combination of Motif 4/10/5/7/1/2/3/6, confirming the reliability of the predictions. Notably, AkLOX3 lacked Motif 8, while AkLOX7/8/9 did not contain Motif 9. suggesting that the absence of specific conserved motifs is closely related to the evolutionary divergence of the AkLOX family. The presence of highly conserved motifs will facilitate future functional characterization of LOX proteins in A. konjac. Furthermore, clustering analysis revealed that AkLOX proteins sharing the same branch exhibit similar conserved domains and gene structures, collectively classified into five categories. Regarding exon number, genes AkLOX4/5/6/7/8/9/11 all contain nine exons, while the remaining AkLOX proteins contain fewer exons (7–8), indicating functional conservation among AkLOX proteins (Fig. 5).

Analysis of promoter cis-elements in the AkLOX gene family of A. konjac

To understand the functional roles of promoter sequences in the AkLOX gene family, predictive analysis was conducted on the 2000 bp upstream sequences of the promoters (Fig. 6). The results revealed 23 distinct types of cis-regulatory elements within the AkLOX promoter regions, primarily associated with plant growth and development, hormone responses, and biotic and abiotic stress responses. The cis-elements involved in hormone responses included those that regulate abscisic acid, auxin, gibberellin, salicylic acid, and methyl jasmonate pathways. Stress-response cis-elements encompassed anaerobic induction, drought induction, cold responsiveness, and defense/stress responsiveness. These findings suggest that the LOX gene family in A. konjac may play significant roles in growth and development, light-induced responses, stress resistance, and hormone responses.

Synteny analysis of AkLOX gene family members in A. konjac

Tandem duplication and segmental duplications are primary drivers of plant gene family expansion. Intraspecific synteny analysis of the 11 identified genes revealed no syntenic gene pairs (Fig. 7). To further elucidate the evolutionary mechanisms of AkLOX genes in A. konjac, an interspecific collinearity analysis was performed on the 11 identified AkLOX genes (Fig. 8). Six homologous gene pairs were discovered between A. konjac and A. albus, indicating stronger collinearity within the same species. One homologous gene pair was found between A. konjac and each of C. sativus and A. thaliana; two homologous gene pairs were identified between A. konjac and each of O. sativa and S. tuberosum. These homologous genes likely originated from divergences within a common ancestral lineage (Table S5). Divergence time analysis among the selected species indicated that species differentiation initially occurred approximately 258.5 million years ago (Fig. 9). A. konjac and A. albus, sharing the most recent common ancestor, had the largest number of homologous LOX gene pairs. In contrast, distantly related species, including cucumber, Arabidopsis, rice, and potato, exhibited fewer homologous gene pairs, reflecting their early divergence from the Amorphophallus lineage.

Analysis of expression patterns of AkLOX genes in different tissues

In this study, we analyzed the expression profiles of the AkLOX gene family across various tissues using publicly available transcriptome data from the NCBI database (Fig. 10). The results showed that four genes (AkLOX3/6/9/11) exhibited relatively high expression levels in petioles, while three genes (AkLOX4/5/8) showed relatively high expression in leaves. These findings indicate that the expression of different AkLOX genes is tissue-specific.

Analysis of the expression pattern of the AkLOX gene in different tissues of A. konjac

Analysis of expression patterns of AkLOX genes in A. konjac in response to low temperature, hormones, and mannitol treatment

To further elucidate the expression patterns of AkLOX genes, we randomly assessed the relative expression levels of seven AkLOX genes under MeJA, low temperature, mannitol, and Pcc stress conditions. The results (Fig. 11) revealed that while AkLOX7 exhibited significant downregulation 24 h after MeJA application, the other six genes demonstrated marked upregulation. Specifically, AkLOX1/4/8 showed significant upregulation 24 h after low-temperature treatment. In contrast, AkLOX3/7/11 displayed significant downregulation 24 h after mannitol application, while the remaining four genes exhibited significant upregulation. Under Pcc stress, the expression levels of AkLOX2/3 significantly increased after 24 and 48 h, with AkLOX11 reaching its peak expression 24 h post-treatment. These findings suggest that AkLOX genes may play a role in plant responses to these stresses.

Discussion

The LOX gene family plays a significant role in plant growth, development, and the response to various abiotic and biotic etresses. This study conducted a comprehensive genome-wide identification of the LOX gene family in A. konjac. A total of 11 members of the AkLOX family were identified, displaying an uneven distribution across four chromosomes. Bae et al. classified the LOX gene family into three subfamilies: 9-LOX, 13-LOX Type I, and 13-LOX Type II [8]. In this study, AkLOX genes were also divided into three subfamilies, which was consistent with previous studies. The physicochemical properties and evolutionary relationships of the proteins encoded by these 11 AkLOX genes were characterized. Their expression profiles were systematically analyzed under conditions such as Pcc infection, mannitol-induced drought, MeJA application, and low temperatures treatments.

Conserved functional motifs provide the molecular foundation for lipoxygenase activity within the LOX family and serve as critical markers for identifying gene families [25, 26]. All AkLOX proteins in A.konjac contain highly representative conserved motifs, including a 38-residue motif (Motif 1), a substrate-binding motif (Motif 3), and an oxygen-binding motif (Motif 2) [27]. Conserved motifs with similar numbers of functionally conserved motifs have also been identified in the LOX families of peanut, pearl millet, and yellowhorn. Among these, Motif 1 follows the sequence His-(X)₄-His-(X)₄-His-(X)₁₇-His-(X)₈-His and possesses consecutive conserved histidine residues, which are verified as the core site for maintaining the iron-binding capacity and catalytic activity of LOX proteins. These residues directly influence substrate recognition and catalytic efficiency towards unsaturated fatty acids. Meanwhile, Motif 2 and Motif 3 are responsible for substrate binding and oxygen molecule recognition, which collectively define the catalytic subtype specificity of 9-LOX and 13-LOX members [28–30]. Motif 4 and Motif 10, part of the PLAT/LH2 domain, are primarily located at the N-terminus of proteins and play roles in membrane targeting, lipid interaction, and signal transduction. They serve as essential structural foundations for jasmonic acid biosynthesis and stress response regulation [31]. In LOX analyses of alfalfa and other legumes, Motif 8 and Motif 9 have been recognized as subfamily-specific conserved motifs; their absence signifies an important molecular signature of gene differentiation and functional specialization, closely associated with tissue-specific expression and differential responses to abiotic stresses [32]. In the present study, AkLOX3 lacked Motif 8, while AkLOX7/8/9 did not contain Motif 9. These findings indicate that, while retaining the core conserved architecture formed by Motif 1/2/3/4/10, the A. konjac LOX family has achieved functional divergence among its members through differential motif loss. This reflects the evolutionary and structural conservation of plant LOX genes, providing a solid basis and evolutionary evidence for their involvement in growth, development, and diverse stress responses in A. konjac.

Cis-acting regulatory elements are important molecular switches involved for transcriptionin the dynamic gene regulatory network of transcriptional control [33]. The LOX genes play essential roles in plant growth, development, and responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses [34, 35]. Studies in Arabidopsis have shown that LOX genes participate in multiple physiological processes, including flowering, development, and stress-induced plant senescence [36, 37]. Promoter cis-element analysis in Solanum lycopersicum L. revealed abundant elements related to regulating LOX gene expression during plant development and response to MeJA biosynthesis [38]. In Taxus chinensis, TcLOX1 is significantly upregulated under JA and abscisic acid (ABA) treatments, while TcLOX2 exhibits induced expression exclusively in response to ABA stress [39]. In the present study, abundant cis-acting regulatory elements related to hormone and stress responses were identified in the promoters of A. konjac LOX genes. Meanwhile, qRT-PCR analysis showed that AkLOX genes exhibited varying degrees of induced expression under mannitol, MeJA and drought stresses, which partially supported the reliability of the cis-acting element prediction in AkLOX promoters. The phylogenetic tree indicate that A. konjac AkLOX2/10 were clustered with O. sativa OsLOX5 in the same clade of 13-LOX Type II. Previous studies have demonstrated that OsLOX5 plays a crucial role in the JA biosynthesis pathway and enhances the disease resistance of rice by producing defense-related compounds [40, 41]. Moreover, A. thaliana AtLOX6, which is part of the 13-LOX Type II subfamily, showed a close phylogenetic relationship with A. konjac AkLOX6/11. It has been reported that AtLOX6 is essential for wound-induced JA synthesis [42], Based on qRT-PCR results,we infer that AkLOX2/6/10/11 may be involved in the JA signaling pathway. In addition, AkLOX genes were found to contain various cis-acting elements related to growth, development and photosynthesis, suggesting that AkLOX genes may be involved in the growth and development processes of A. konjac.

During plant evolution, genome expansion is primarily facilitated by whole-genome duplication (WGD), tandem duplication (TD), segmental duplication (SD), and retrotransposon-mediated duplication (TRD) [43]. Among these, TD and SD are recognized as the primary forces behind gene family expansion and play critical roles in genome rearrangement and expansion [44]. Phylogenetic divergence time analysis revealed that the LOX gene family has undergone a complete evolutionary trajectory from ancient divergence to recent expansion. The earliest divergence event separated LOX genes into two major subfamilies: Type I and Type II. Subsequent speciation of monocots (O. sativa) and dicots (A. thaliana and S. tuberosum) resulted in clear lineage separation of orthologous LOX genes between the two groups. Analysis of closely clustered paralogs within multiple species indicated that the LOX family has experienced substantial expansion and functional differentiation through recent gene duplication events. Intraspecific collinearity analysis of the A. konjac LOX family detected no collinear gene pairs, which may be attributed to the low copy number of AkLOX genes or their higher loss rate during evolution. Interspecific collinearity revealed conserved LOX homologs between A. konjac and the five compared species, indicating the high evolutionary conservation of the LOX family. These findings indicate that LOX genes are involved in core physiological processes in plants, and collinear relationships support their derivation from a common ancestor. Time-calibrated phylogenetic trees were constructed with a root age of 258.5 million years based on divergence time estimation. The estimated divergence times were consistent with the observed patterns of homologous gene pairs, further confirming the evolutionary conservation of LOX genes across plant lineages.

Tissue-specific expression patterns of particular genes can further aid in understanding their functions [45, 46]. Previous studies indicate that some LOX genes exhibit unique expression patterns, providing a theoretical foundation for investigating their mechanisms and physiological functions. For example, populus PtLOX7, PtLOX9, and PtLOX10 show high expression levels in roots, while PtLOX1 and PtLOX14 are primarily expressed in nodes [8]. In Cucumis melo L., CsLOX08 and CsLOX16 show distinct expression signals in roots, while CsLOX09 exhibits prominent expression in leaves [47]. In A. konjac, AkLOX10 exhibits high expression levels in the roots of the flowered devil's tongue, while AkLOX6 shows elevated expression in the corms. AkLOX4/5/8 display significant expression in leaves of the flowered devil's tongue. In Z. mays, ZmLOX10/11 rapidly exert positive regulatory effects on circadian rhythm, wounding, pathogen infection and hormone treatment under cold stress [48]. In A. thaliana AtLOX1 is induced by methyl jasmonate (MeJA), abscisic acid (ABA) and Pseudomonas syringae, and positively participates in hormonal signaling and pathogen defense responses [49]. Four StLOX genes in S. tuberosum (Soltu.DM.08G005480, Soltu.DM.01G002140, Soltu.DM.03G037120 and Soltu.DM.01G038840) are significantly upregulated under NaCl treatment, among them, Soltu. DM. 03G037120 shows the most prominent up-regulation with the expression level being 2.67 times higher than that of the control group [50]. In Brassica napus L., BnaLOX3/4 are early-responsive genes to MeJA and salicylic acid (SA), and are strongly induced upon Sclerotinia sclerotiorum infection [51]. In Solanum lycopersicum, SlLOX genes actively respond to Cladosporium fulvum and participate in jasmonic acid (JA) synthesis and resistance regulation [52]. In A. konjac, AkLOX1/4/8 are were significantly induced by low-temperature treatment and were significantly upregulated at 24 h post-treatment. After 24 h of MeJA treatment, the expression levels of AkLOX1/3/4/6/8/11 are all significantly up-regulated; while the expression levels of AkLOX3/7/11 are significantly down-regulated 24 h after mannitol treatment. These results indicate that the LOX gene family responds to hormones and abiotic stresses, and has potential regulatory value in the growth, development and stress adaptation of A. konjac. This study identified the AkLOX gene family and analyzed its expression patterns under biotic and abiotic stresses via qRT-PCR, but did not perform gene function verification; thus, the specific roles of AkLOX genes in growth, development and stress responses remain unclear. Therefore, this study provides candidate genes and a preliminary foundation for molecular research on stress resistance in A. konjac, and further gene function studies will be carried out based on the present qRT-PCR results.

Conclusions

This study identified 11 AkLOX genes in A. konjac, which were unevenly distributed across four chromosomes. Members of the A. konjac LOX family were classified into three subfamilies: 9-LOX, 13-LOX Type I, and 13-LOX Type II. Promoter cis-element analysis identified 23 types of regulatory elements in AkLOX genes, most of which were related to light response, hormone regulation, and stress responses in plants. Tissue-specificity analysis revealed that AkLOX gene family members exhibit tissue-specific expression patterns. qRT-PCR results demonstrated that the tested AkLOX genes subjected to stress were induced to varying degrees, indicating their involvement in stress response processes, For instance, the expression levels of AkLOX1/4/8 were significantly upregulated under low temperature, drought, and MeJA treatments, while AkLOX2/3/11 were significantly upregulated under Pcc stress. These results lay a foundation for future functional studies of AkLOX genes in A. konjac.
