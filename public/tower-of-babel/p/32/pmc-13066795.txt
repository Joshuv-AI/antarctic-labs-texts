Biosynthetic reuterin improved the intestinal health in pigs
Authors: Yangyang Wei, Yajun Wei, Chunyan Liu, Yiyi He, Shihui Ruan, Yanna Huang, Li Wang, Xi Yang, Hongbo Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis experiment aimed to study the effects of dietary supplementation with biosynthetic reuterin (RT) from Escherichia coli cells on the growth performance and intestinal health of pigs. A total of 72 pigs (Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire, 21 d old, 5.7 ± 0.3 kg weight) were randomly divided into basal diet group (CON), basal diet supplemented with 5 × 1010 colony-forming unit (CFU)/kg Lactobacillus reuteri group (LR), and basal diet supplemented with 50 mg/kg reuterin group (RT) with 6 pens (4 pigs per pen) per group for a 14-d period. One piglet was randomly selected from each pen on the 15th d for sampling. The results showed that the addition of RT to the diet significantly improved the growth performance of piglets, specifically increasing average daily gain (ADG; P = 0.004), and reduced diarrhea rate (P = 0.012), improved the intestinal morphology by significantly increasing villus height and the villus height to crypt depth ratio (P < 0.05), and enhanced intestinal barrier and immune functions by upregulating the expression of related genes (ZO1, MUC1, pBD2, and PR39; P < 0.05). Simultaneously, RT upregulated TLR gene expression and activated the MAPK signaling pathway (P < 0.05). Combined analysis of microbiome and non-targeted metabolomics showed that RT improved metabolism by affecting the relative abundance of Phascolarctobacterium succinatutens YIT12067 (known for succinate production and impacting energy metabolism) and Holdemanella (implicated in carbohydrate metabolism and immune modulation) in pigs (P < 0.05). In addition, RT significantly reduced the deposition of intestinal collagen (P < 0.05). In conclusion, this study demonstrated that biosynthetic RT effectively improved the growth and intestinal health of pigs, which may provide some theoretical basis for the RT production as a feed additive.Keywords: Reuterin, Weaned pig, Growth performance, Gut microbiota, Intestinal barrier function, Extracellular matrix

Introduction

Early weaning is a common practice in modern swine production to improve sow productivity, but it often precipitates a cascade of stress in piglets, manifesting as diarrhea, intestinal damage, inflammation, and even mortality. These negative effects are particularly pronounced in the immediate post-weaning period, exacting a substantial economic toll on the swine industry (Cao et al., 2018; He et al., 2022). As the global livestock sector continues to expand, the imperative to develop effective strategies for mitigating weaning stress has become increasingly urgent. In recent years, the ban on antibiotic use in animal feed has spurred the search for alternative strategies to promote animal health and performance. Probiotics have emerged as a promising solution to improve piglet production performance, with Lactobacillus reuteri being one of the most extensively studied probiotics. L. reuteri as a feed additive, has been shown to confer a wide range of beneficial effects, including the prevention of piglet diarrhea (Dell'Anno et al., 2021) and the promotion of intestinal development in piglets (Yang et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2018a, Yi et al., 2018b). It achieves these effects by improving the intestinal barrier function (Gao et al., 2022), regulating intestinal mucosal immunity (Wang et al., 2020; Yi et al., 2018a, Yi et al., 2018b), and attenuating intestinal inflammation (Xie et al., 2021), thereby fortifying the intestine against pathogen invasion (Yang et al., 2015). In addition, L. reuteri exerts positive effects on the intestinal extracellular matrix (ECM) through multiple mechanisms, primarily manifested as enhancing ECM structural stability, regulating ECM metabolic homeostasis, and promoting injury repair (Pang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2020). The ECM serves as a critical structural foundation for tissue architecture and is integral to physiological processes such as cell adhesion, chemotaxis, tissue repair, and maintenance of systemic homeostasis. As a vital component of the intestinal tract, the ECM primarily comprises collagen, fibronectin, tenascin, and other macromolecules. Notably, ECM remodeling has been identified as a hallmark pathological feature of inflammatory bowel disease (Porras et al., 2022). L. reuteri has played a significant role in improving intestinal health. However, the precise mechanisms underlying these effects, particularly those mediated by L. reuteri's metabolites, remain incompletely understood.

L. reuteri is known to produce various metabolites in organisms, among which reuterin (RT) is of particular interest (Lima et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024). The RT is a broad-spectrum, non-protein antibacterial substance with the potential to combat a wide range of pathogens (Yang et al., 2024). The antibacterial activity of RT is not affected by proteases and remains stable under various storage conditions, making it suitable for development as a feed additive (Bennett et al., 2021). Studies have shown that supplementing RT in drinking water improves the growth performance of broilers by increasing average daily gain (ADG) and feed to gain ratio (F:G), enhancing ileal villus structure, and elevating cecal short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) concentrations, thereby modulating their overall metabolism (Zhang et al., 2022). A study by Greppi et al. (2020) isolated a high RT-producing Limosilactobacillus reuteri strain from the poultry gastrointestinal tract and, through genomic analysis, demonstrated the stability of its metabolic pathways, providing a theoretical foundation for engineering microbial strains ADDIN. However, RT has been predominantly explored for applications in food preservation and fermentation processes. Its potential as a feed additive for improving animal health has not been fully exploited.

The present study aimed to bridge this knowledge gap by investigating the effects of RT supplementation in the diet of pigs. By leveraging a biosynthesis approach, Escherichia coli was engineered to produce RT, thereby providing a novel and sustainable source of this bioactive compound. It was hypothesized that dietary RT produced by the engineered E. coli strain could confer beneficial effects on the growth performance, intestinal health, microbiota metabolism, and ECM of pigs. Through a comprehensive series of in vivo experiments, this study aimed to unravel the mechanisms underlying these effects and to evaluate the potential of RT as a novel feed additive for the swine industry.

Materials and methods

Construction of RT genetically engineered strains and investigation of their biosynthetic functions

Genome analysis

Genomic data from 116 L. reuteri isolates, sourced from diverse hosts and geographical locations, were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) genome database. Gene function annotation was performed using PROKKA (v1.14.6) (Seemann, 2014), providing a detailed gene inventory for each isolate. Comparative genomic analysis was conducted with ROARY (v3.11.2) (Page et al., 2015) at a 95% sequence identity threshold to identify orthologous genes and delineate the pan-genome and core-genome. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using MAFFT for core gene alignment (Katoh and Standley, 2013) and FastTree (v2.1.10) for tree construction (Price et al., 2010). The presence of the RT synthesis-related gene cluster (pdu-cbi-cob-hem, encoding enzymes for propanediol utilization and cobalamin biosynthesis) was confirmed using PhyloSuite (v1.2.2). The full names of the abbreviations of all genes and proteins can be found in Table S1. Genomic data and phylogenetic relationships were visualized using ggplot2 in R and ITOL2.

Reuterin-producing plasmid construction

Precise gene design and sequence verification were conducted using SnapGene (v5.0.5). Primers for genetic modifications were designed (Table 1). E. coli BL21(DE3) was used as the host strain, cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium with 100 μg/mL ampicillin. Two pduCDE inserts (native and codon-optimized) were prepared and assembled into the pET32a(+) vector via Gibson assembly to construct plasmids pRP0020 and pRP0030. The plasmids were introduced into BL21(DE3) electrocompetent cells. Transformants were screened on ampicillin-containing LB agar plates, and positive colonies were identified by PCR. For PCR amplification, each 25 μL reaction mixture contained 12.5 μL of 2 × Taq Master Mix (P111, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), 1 μL of each primer (10 μmol/L), 1 μL of template DNA, and 9.5 μL of nuclease-free water. The PCR program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR was performed using a Mastercycler nexus gradient (Model No. 6381, Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany). Colony PCR, restriction digestion, and sequencing were used to validate plasmid integrity (Fig. S1).

Production verification

Engineered strains were cultured in LB medium with ampicillin and isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Cells were harvested at mid-log phase, (optical density at 600 nm [OD600] of approximately 0.6), lysed, and centrifuged to separate protein fractions. Protein expression levels and solubility were analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and stained with Coomassie brilliant blue. Antimicrobial activity was assessed by determining the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) of RT against E. coli BW25113 and Salmonella typhimurium LT2 using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices LLC, San Jose, CA, USA). The RT concentration was measured by colorimetric analysis using acrolein calibration curves, with results expressed in μmol/mL, using the SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader.

Reuterin product purification

Reuterin was produced according to the method previously study (Talarico et al., 1988). Specifically, 500 μL of an L. reuteri ATCC 55730 preculture was inoculated into 50 mL of de Man-Rogosa-Sharpe (MRS) medium and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 3 h. The 50 mL bacterial culture was subsequently transferred to 1 L of MRS broth supplemented with 20 mmol/L glycerol and cultured overnight at 37 °C. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 9300 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, washed twice with 100 mmol/L potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), and resuspended in 300 mL of a 200 mmol/L glycerol solution. This suspension was then incubated at 37 °C for an additional 3 h to induce RT biosynthesis. Following the incubation period, the cultures were centrifuged again at 9300 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were filtered through a 0.22-μm filter (Axygen SFPN-022, Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA) to remove any remaining bacterial cells. The resulting crude RT extract was stored at −80 °C until further analysis. The RT concentration was quantified by colorimetric analysis using acrolein calibration curves at 560 nm, using the SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader, and results were expressed as μmol/L.

Animals and experimental design

Animal ethics statement

The experiment was carried out in accordance with the Chinese guidelines for animal welfare and experimental protocol, and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences (GDIAS20240115).

Experimental design and diet chemical analyses

A total of 72 pigs (Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire, 21 d old, 5.7 ± 0.3 kg weight, 50% male and 50% female) were randomly assigned to 3 treatment groups with 6 replicates per treatment group (n = 6) and 4 pigs per replicate. The control group (CON) was fed a basal diet and formulated into a powdered formula feed according to the nutritional needs following (NRC, 2012). The LR and the RT groups were supplemented with 5 × 1010 colony-forming units (CFU)/kg L. reuteri and 50 mg/kg RT referring to Yang et al. (2014) and Bell et al. (2022), respectively.

The net energy (NE) of feed was calculated according to the NE of each raw material in the raw material composition table (NRC, 2012). The dry matter (DM), ether extract (EE), ash, Ca, and P were determined according to GB/T 6435-2014, GB/T 6433-2025, GB/T 6438-2007, GB/T 6436-2018, and GB/T 6437-2018 (China National Standard, 2014, 2025, 2007; China National Standard, 2018a, China National Standard, 2018b) using a fully automatic fat analyzer (ANKOM XT15i, ANKOM Technology Corp., Macedon, NY, USA) and a muffle furnace (FO611C, Yamato Scientific Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), respectively. The organic matter (OM) was calculated as DM minus ash. The total nitrogen content was analyzed by KJELTEC8400 analyzer (FOSS A/S, Hillerød, Denmark) and was multiplied by the coefficient of 6.25 to estimate the crude protein (CP) content, according to the method of GB/T 6432-2018 (China National Standard, 2018c). The contents of acid detergent fiber (ADF) and neutral detergent fiber (NDF) were determined using Ankom200i fiber analysis system (ANKOM Technology Corp., Macedon, NY, USA) according to the method of NY/T 1459-2022 (Ministry of Agriculture of the People's Republic of China, 2022) and GB/T 20806-2006 (China National Standard, 2006). The composition and nutritional levels of the basic diet were shown in Table 2. During the 14 d trial, these piglets were placed in an environmentally controlled enclosure and fed four times a day at 08:00, 12:00, 16:00, and 20:00. During the experiment, pigs were provided ad libitum feed and drinking water.

Growth performance and diarrhea rate

Piglets were weighed on d 1 and 15 to calculate ADG and the feed consumption in each column was recorded to calculate the average daily feed intake (ADFI). In order to determine the incidence and severity of diarrhea, the feces of pigs were observed twice a day throughout the study. The score was: 0, normal; 1, soft stool; 2, mild diarrhea; 3, severe diarrhea; and scores ≥ 2 points were considered as diarrhea (Tang et al., 2021).

Sample collection

The experiment lasted for 14 d. On the 15th d, 6 piglets (3 barrows and 3 sows) were randomly selected from each group for slaughter and sampling according to the previous method (Feng et al., 2021). Blood samples were collected from the anterior vena cava using sterile syringes and transferred into heparinized tubes. The blood samples were immediately centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to separate plasma and serum. The plasma and serum samples were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Intestinal samples were collected from the middle jejunum and distal ileum. The intestinal segments were carefully dissected, flushed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove digesta, and then divided into two portions. One portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis of intestinal morphology, and the other was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for gene expression analysis. Ileal contents were collected in 5-mL cryovials, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for microbiological analysis.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of inflammatory cytokines IL-1 β (ML025973), IL-6 (ML025981), and TGF-β (ML302363) in serum were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (Shanghai Meilian Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, standard wells, sample wells, and blank wells were set up. A volume of 50 μL of standard samples of different concentrations were added to each standard well (no serum or ELISA reagents were added to the blank well, the rest of the operation was the same). A volume of 40 μL diluent and 10 μL serum to be tested were added to the sample well, mixed, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and washed and dried 3 to 5 times. A volume 50 μL of each of the two developing agents was added to each well, mixed, and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 15 min. Finally, 50 μL termination solution was added to each well to stop the reaction. After observing the color change, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a multifunctional ELISA reader (Bio-Tek Instruments Inc., Winooski, VT, USA) to calculate the concentrations of serum cytokines.

The intestinal morphology analyses

Referring to the previous method, fixed jejunal and ileal samples were embedded in paraffin, and transversely cut into 4-μm sections for staining using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The sections were examined under an optical microscope (Nikon Eclipse E100, Nikon Corp., Tokyo, Japan). Villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured using Image-Pro software (V11, Media Cybernetics, Rockville, ML, USA). At least 5 intact villi per sample were measured, and the average values were calculated. The villus height to crypt depth ratio (VH/CD) was also determined as an indicator of intestinal mucosal integrity and absorptive function.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the jejunum and ileum using Trizol reagent (15596018CN, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. The cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript II First Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (Takara Bio Inc., Tokyo, Japan), with a reverse transcription system as shown in Table S2. Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using CFX96 fluorescence quantitative analyzer (Bio Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). The thermal cycling reaction conditions referred to the previous method (Yi et al., 2018a, Yi et al., 2018b). Briefly: 10 min at 95 °C, followed by 39 cycles of 30 s at 95 °C, 30 s at 60 °C, and 20 s at 72 °C. Each sample was analyzed in duplicate wells, and the average Ct value was used for subsequent data analysis. The primer sequences and accession numbers are shown in Table S3. Beta-actin was used as the internal reference gene, and the relative expression level of the target gene was calculated using the 2-△△Ct method.

Western blot

Western blot was performed according to the method of Miao (Miao et al., 2024). Approximately 0.1 g of jejunum and ileum samples were homogenized inradioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime Biotechnology Inc., Shanghai, China). The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein detection kit (Beyotime Biotech Inc., Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies. Antibody information is shown in Table S4. After washing with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with a secondary antibody (1:5000, Proteintech Group Inc., Wuhan, Hubei, China) at 25 °C for 1 h. Protein bands were detected using NcmECL SuperUltra (New Cell & Molecular Biotech Co., Ltd., Suzhou, Jiangsu, China) and visualized with a multifunctional imaging system (Amersham Imager 600, GE Healthcare Technologies Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (v1.54f, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, ML, USA), and the relative expression levels of the target proteins were calculated by normalizing to the β-actin band intensity.

Microbial composition analysis

Total DNA was extracted from ileal content samples using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. The purity and concentration of DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (V-750, JAS Co., Tokyo, Japan) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The conserved regions of rDNA were amplified with barcoded specific primers (341F: 5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′, 806R: 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTATCTAAT-3′). The PCR amplicons were gel-purified and quantified using a QuantiFluor fluorometer (Promega Corp., Madison, WI, USA) with the QuantiFluor dsDNA System. Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts, ligated with sequencing adapters, and used to construct sequencing libraries. High-throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina PE250 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). After obtaining raw reads from sequencing, low-quality reads were filtered out, followed by assembly to merge paired-end reads into tags. These tags were further filtered to generate clean tags. Clean tags were then clustered, and chimeric tags detected during the clustering and alignment process were removed, resulting in effective tags. Upon obtaining operational taxonomic unit (OTU), the abundance was calculated based on effective tags. Taxonomic assignment of the OTU was performed against the SILVA database (release 138.1). Alpha-diversity indexes (Chao 1 and Shannon) and β-diversity (principal component analysis [PCA]) were calculated to evaluate the richness and diversity of the gut microbiota and the differences between the treatment groups. Biomarker species with significant differences between groups were identified using the linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) method (Miao et al., 2024).

Determination of short chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in ileal contents

For the determination of SCFAs in ileal contents, the method of Yi et al. (2016) was referred. In brief, 1.5 g of the sample was mixed with 1.5 mL of ultrapure water, vortexed, and centrifuged at 4 °C for 15 min at 12,000 × g. The supernatant was mixed with 10% metaphosphoric acid solution (0.1 g/mL), left in a 4 °C refrigerator overnight, and extracted with ether the next day. The obtained supernatant was transferred to a gas chromatography (GC) detection vial to detect the concentration of SCFAs in the sample (GC-2010 Plus, Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan).

Non-targeted metabolomics

An appropriate amount of ileal contents was weighed and mixed with pre-cooled methanol (A456-4, Fisher Scientific Co., LLC, Waltham, MA, USA), acetonitrile (Cat. No. 34967, Sigma–Aldrich LLC, Saint Louis, MI, USA), and water solution (2:2:1, v/v). The mixture was subjected to low-temperature ultrasonication and centrifugation, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was vacuum-dried, and re-dissolved in 100 μL of acetonitrile-water solution (1:1, v/v). After vortex and centrifugation, the supernatant was collected again for quantitative analysis. Metabolite analysis was performed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) coupled to mass spectrometry (MS) on a SCIEX TripleTOF 6600 (AB Sciex LLC, Framingham, MA, USA) and an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). The chromatographic column model was carried out on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide 1.7 μm column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA). The MC data were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes. The raw data were preprocessed using the Progenesis QI software v2.4 (Nonlinear Dynamics, Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA) for peak detection, alignment, and normalization. Multivariate statistical analysis, including PCA and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), was performed to identify differential metabolites between the treatment groups. The differential metabolites were further annotated and classified using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) databases to explore the metabolic pathways involved.

Masson staining

After the jejunum and ileum samples were sectioned, they were stained with Masson staining kit (G1006, Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China), then dehydrated, sealed into pieces with neutral glue, scanned with 3DHISTECH (Pannoramic MIDI II, 3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary), then photographed at 10 and 40 times with Slide Viewer software, and finally the collagen area was calculated with ImageJ (v1.54f).

Calculations and statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0. Normality and homogeneity of variance of the data were tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene's test, respectively. For normally distributed data with equal variances, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the differences between the treatment groups, followed by Duncan's multiple range test for post-hoc comparisons. For data that did not meet the assumptions of parametric tests, non-parametric test, the Kruskal–Wallis test, was used instead. Values are expressed as mean and standard error of the mean (SEM). Results

Comparative genomic analysis

In a collection of 116 L. reuteri strains, 46 produced RT (Fig. 1A). A pdu gene cluster was detected in RT-producing strains and was not detected in three non-RT-producing strains analyzed. Functional annotation within the cluster identified pduCDE and associated genes. Comparative genomic analysis showed that L. reuteri ATCC 55730 harbors a complete RT-associated cluster, whereas E. coli BL21(DE3) lacks several components (Fig. 1B and Table S5). Expression of Pdu proteins and function

Analysis of protein expression in engineered E. coli strains revealed distinct patterns (Fig. 1C). Expression of PduC in RPS0010 and RPS0030 was slightly higher (P = 0.120) than in RPS0020. Expression of PduD and PduE in RPS0020 was significantly higher (PduD, P < 0.01; PduE, P < 0.05). Introduction of PduCDE at different temperatures showed that RPS0020 exhibited higher soluble expression across all tested temperatures compared with RPS0010 and RPS0030. At 37 °C, only RPS0020 synthesized detectable RT. At 25 °C, all 3 strains produced RT. Results indicated 25 °C induction increased RT production, with RPS0020 having the highest yield (Fig. S1 and Table 1, P < 0.05). Findings are consistent with SDS-PAGE analysis, which showed stronger RT bands in RPS0020 at 25 °C than in RPS0010 and RPS0030. The RT yield from RPS0020 at 25 °C exceeded that of L. reuteri at 37 °C (Fig. 1D, P < 0.05). The engineered strain produced RT with antibacterial efficacy comparable to that of the native L. reuteri strain, while achieving an approximately 26.3% higher production rate (Fig. 1D and E).

Reuterin improves growth performance and intestinal morphology of pigs

The dietary supplementation of pigs with either L. reuteri or RT had a significant impact on their growth performance and intestinal morphology (Table 3 and Fig. 2A). Compared to the CON group, piglets in the LR and RT groups exhibited a remarkable increased in final body weight, ADG, and ADFI (P < 0.05), accompanied by a concomitant reduction in the diarrhea rate (P = 0.012). By observing the H&E-stained intestinal sections, it was found that the intestinal villi in the jejunum of the CON group appeared to be ruptured, whereas the intestinal villi in the rest of the sections were structurally intact (Fig. 2A). The statistics of intestinal VH, CD, and VH/CD (Table 4) showed that LR and RT significantly increased the VH/CD in the jejunum (P = 0.016) and VH in the ileum (P = 0.009). Additionally, compared with the CON group, LR significantly improved the VH/CD in the ileum (P = 0.034).

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis of genes related to the physical and chemical barrier in the jejunum and ileum (Fig. 2B and C) further elucidated the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed effects. In the RT group, the expression of MUC1, a key component of the intestinal mucus layer, was significantly elevated, while in the ileum of both the LR and RT groups, the expression of ZO1, a tight junction protein, was significantly increased (P < 0.05).

Reuterin enhances immune function in pigs

The impact of RT on the intestinal immune function of pigs was investigated through a comprehensive analysis of immune-related genes and proteins (Fig. 3A–F). The mRNA expression levels of antimicrobial peptides, such as pBD2, PG1-5, and PR39 in the jejunum, and pBD2 in the ileum, were significantly higher in the RT group compared to the LR group (P < 0.05). Additionally, RT treatment led to a significant increase in the mRNA expression of PR39 in the jejunum compared with the LR group (P < 0.05). In the ileum, the expression of PR39 was significantly reduced in both the LR and RT groups (P = 0.020). These differential expression patterns suggest that RT modulates the expression of antimicrobial peptides in a tissue specific manner, potentially enhancing the local immune defense mechanisms in the intestine. Serum levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine TGF-β were significantly increased in the RT group (Fig. 3C, P = 0.003), indicating that RT may play a role in regulating the inflammatory response in the gut.

The expression of Toll-like receptors (TLR2 and TLR4), which are key sensors of pathogen associated molecular patterns, was affected by supplementation. The expression of TLR2 was significantly elevated in both the jejunum and ileum, and the expression of TLR4 gene was significantly increased in the jejunum (P < 0.05). Moreover, the phosphorylation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) protein, an important signaling molecule in the immune response pathway, was significantly enhanced in the ileum of the RT group compared with the control group (P = 0.026). Moreover, compared with the control group, RT supplementation was associated with higher TLR2 mRNA levels in both the jejunum and ileum, higher TLR4 mRNA in the jejunum, and increased MAPK phosphorylation in the ileum (P < 0.05).

Reuterin affects gut microbiota composition in pigs

The impact of RT on the gut microbiota composition of pigs was assessed using 16S rRNA sequencing of ileal contents. The analysis of OTUs revealed a complex microbial community structure (Fig. 4A). There were 560 shared OTUs among the three treatment groups, with 119, 149, and 103 unique OTUs in the CON, LR, and RT groups, respectively. Interestingly, although there were no significant differences in the β- (Fig. 4B) or α-diversity (Fig. 4C) of the microbial composition, the relative abundances of certain microbial taxa were differentially affected by RT supplementation. The top 10 species composition at the phylum level and genus level is shown in Fig. 4D and F. At the genus level, the relative abundance of Blautia was significantly higher in RT than in LR (Fig. 4F, P = 0.020), whereas no significant difference was observed between RT and CON.

Reuterin alters microbiota metabolism in pigs

Figure 5 shows the results of metabolomic analysis of ileal contents. In the RT vs. CON and LR vs. RT group comparisons, 957 and 549 metabolites were detected to be altered, respectively (Table S6). These metabolites were classified and preliminarily screened based on the criteria (fold change [FC] < 0.67 or FC > 1.5, variable importance in projection [VIP] > 1, P < 0.05). In the RT vs. CON group, 60 metabolites were significantly upregulated and 94 metabolites were significantly downregulated (Table S6 and Fig. 5A). In the LR vs. RT group, 41 metabolites showed significant upregulation, while 104 metabolites exhibited significant downregulation (Table S6 and Fig. 5C). The names of these metabolites are provided in Tables S7 and S8. Subsequently, the preliminarily screened metabolites were further filtered with stricter criteria (FC < 0.5 or FC > 2, VIP >1, P < 0.05). Further screening yielded 33 representative metabolites belonging to 7 categories, namely benzenoids, lipids and lipid-like molecules, nucleosides, nucleotides and analogues, organic acids and derivatives, organic nitrogen compounds, organoheterocyclic compounds and phenylpropanoids and polyketides. It is worth noting that the metabolites belonging to organic acids and derivatives were significantly down regulated in the RT group, except Lys–Gly (Fig. 5B). Moreover, the expression levels of lipid and lipid-like molecules were significantly decreased in the LR group (Fig. 5D).

Reuterin affects ECMand its regulatory factors in pigs

The impact of RT on the ECM and its regulatory factors in the intestine of pigs was investigated using a combination of histological and molecular techniques (Fig. 6). Masson staining of jejunal and ileal sections revealed that weaning led to an increase in collagen content in the intestinal tissue (Fig. 6A), which is indicative of tissue remodeling. The RT supplementation significantly reduced collagen content in both the jejunum and ileum compared with the control group (P < 0.05), whereas LR reduced collagen content only in the ileum (P = 0.001).

Analysis of qRT-PCR in ECM related genes showed that, in the jejunum, RT treatment significantly increased the mRNA expression of COL3A1, ITGα1, and ITGβ2, while decreasing the expression of COL5A1 compared with the control group (Fig. 6B, P < 0.05). In the ileum, RT upregulated the mRNA levels of COL1A2, COL3A1, COL4A2, COL6A1, FN1, ITGα1, and TNC compared with the control group (Fig. 6C, P < 0.05). These findings suggest that RT differentially regulates the expression of ECM genes in the jejunum and ileum, potentially affecting the composition and function of the ECM.

Protein analysis of the ileum ECM further supported these findings. The RT had no significant effect on the expression of COL1A2, COL6, and FN1 proteins in the ileum, and did not significantly alter the protein level of COL4A2 (Fig. 6D, P > 0.05). Additionally, RT modulated the expression of intracellular and extracellular regulatory factors in the ileal ECM (Fig. 6E and F). Compared with the control group, the expression levels of the intracellular regulatory factor SEC6A1 were significantly upregulated, PDE4D were significantly downregulated, and the expression levels of the extracellular regulatory factors MMP2 and PAs were not significantly changed. The results suggest that RT affects the ECM through the regulation of multiple genes and proteins involved in ECM synthesis, degradation, and remodeling, which may contribute to the maintenance of intestinal integrity and function.

Discussion

The successful engineering of E. coli to synthesize RT represents a significant achievement in biosynthesis. By deciphering the genomic determinants of RT production in L. reuteri and transferring the relevant genes to E. coli, a novel and efficient method for biosynthesis of this valuable metabolite was developed (Jayaraman and Nyachoti, 2017; Tang et al., 2022). In the colorimetric assay, the reuterin produced by the engineered E. coli strain showed no difference compared to reuterin produced by L. reuteri, neither in structure nor in bacterial inhibition capability. Similar to reuterin produced by L. reuteri, this natural product is a dynamic multi-component equilibrium consisting of 3-hydroxypropionaldehyde (3-HPA), its hydrate, and the dimer. It is important to note that this composition differs from commercially available RT (CAS No. 118606-97-6). In the trial, the non-optimized codon led to a higher RT producing ratio, and lowering the culture temperature to 25 °C improved RT synthesis. This is because the optimized pduCDE gene codon led to inclusion bodies generation, and then damaged the enzyme activity. This strategy not only overcomes the limitations of traditional production methods but also offers the potential for large-scale and cost-effective production, which is crucial for the practical application of RT in various fields, especially in animal nutrition (Cao et al., 2022; Dell'Anno et al., 2021).

Weaning is a critical period in piglet development, during which the gut undergoes significant morphological and functional changes. The disruption of the gut microbiota and the associated increase in oxidative stress can lead to a cascade of negative effects, including diarrhea, inflammation, and reduced nutrient absorption (Peng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2021; Yi et al., 2018a, Yi et al., 2018b; Yu et al., 2023). The improvement of the intestinal tract by probiotics and some plant extracts is positively correlated with the expression of tight junction protein and mucin related genes (Hudcovic et al., 2012; Xia et al., 2024). The results demonstrate that RT can effectively mitigate these adverse effects, likely through its multifaceted actions on the gut microbiota, intestinal barrier function, and immune response.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis in the jejunum and ileum showed increased expression of MUC1 and ZO1 in RT- and/or LR-treated piglets (Fig. 2B and C), which aligns with a model whereby RT supplementation enhances intestinal barrier function by promoting genes involved in mucus secretion and tight junction assembly. MUC1 is a membrane-tethered mucin that contributes to the physicochemical mucus barrier and limits microbial adhesion at the epithelial surface, thereby reducing pathogen access to the epithelium. In parallel, ZO1 is a scaffolding protein essential for tight junction organization and paracellular sealing; higher ZO1 expression is generally associated with improved epithelial integrity and reduced permeability. Consistent with these roles, the observed upregulation of MUC1 and ZO1 provides a plausible molecular basis for the barrier-protective phenotype under RT supplementation, potentially contributing to resilience against pathogen invasion and the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis. Not withstanding, these data are at the transcriptional level and do not by themselves establish functional enhancement or protein abundance/localization. Future work should quantify epithelial permeability (e.g., fluorescein isothiocyanate [FITC]–dextran flux or ex vivo trans-epithelial electrical resistance [TEER]), assess mucus layer thickness and goblet cell metrics (e.g., Alcian blue/periodic acid-Schiff [PAS] staining), and determine MUC1/ZO1 protein levels and junctional localization (Western blot and immunofluorescence) to verify that RT-induced transcriptional changes translate into measurable barrier improvements.

Metabolites of microbial community act as signal molecules and substrates for metabolic reactions within the host. Targeting metabolites to uncover issues related to host health has been a research hotspot in recent years. The modulation of the gut microbiota by RT is a complex and dynamic process. The changes in microbial composition and metabolite profiles suggest that RT acts as a selective pressure, favoring the growth of beneficial bacteria while suppressing the growth of potential pathogens. The increase in the relative abundance of certain taxa, such as Phascolarctobacterium_succinatutens_YIT_12067 and Blautia in the RT group, may contribute to the production of SCFAs and other lipid metabolites (Holmberg et al., 2024; Shigeno et al., 2019). Lipid metabolites, especially SCFAs, are important fuels for intestinal epithelial cells (IEC). They regulate IEC function through different mechanisms, affect the proliferation and differentiation of IEC and the function of intestinal endocrine cells and other subsets, thereby enhancing intestinal barrier function and host metabolism (Martin-Gallausiaux et al., 2021). The observed alterations in microbial metabolites further support the notion that RT influences gut microbiota metabolism, potentially through the regulation of key enzymatic activities and metabolic pathways, as also reported in recent studies, such as the research on glycerol-derived RT, which shows its impact on human intestinal microbiota and metabolites (Yang et al., 2024).

Recent studies have shown that the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in the development and function of the host immune system. The interaction between the gut microbiota and the host immune response is a bidirectional process, with the microbiota shaping the immune system and the immune system, in turn, regulating the composition and function of the microbiota (Wang et al., 2018; Wang and Ji, 2019; Xia et al., 2022). The addition of probiotic preparations can enhance the intestinal epithelial barrier and improve intestinal immune function in piglets (Xie et al., 2021). In vivo and in vitro studies have shown that RT can inhibit the development of colon cancer in humans and mice, and has cytotoxic and growth inhibitory effects on colon cancer cells (Bell et al., 2022). Research has shown that RT can attenuate the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines induced by lipopolysaccharides and exert anti-inflammatory effects by regulating TLR dependent pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK (Xu et al., 2022). Succinate produced by Phascolarctobacterium_succinatutens_YIT_12067 activates the TLR4-dependent MAPK pathway via MyD88, where MyD88 is an adaptor protein that transduces signals from TLRs to downstream kinases (IRAKs), leading to MAPK and NF-κB activation, thereby suppressing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α and IL-6) and maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis (Watanabe et al., 2012). Studies have shown that Bacillus paralicheniformis reduces cell apoptosis and excessive inflammation by increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Blautia, inhibiting MAPK signaling transduction, and driving macrophage polarization from the M0 to M2 phenotype, where M0 refers to unpolarized/resting macrophages that have not yet been activated toward classically (M1) or alternatively (M2) polarized states (Yang et al., 2025). AMPs can also indirectly regulate the abundance of gut microbiota through the MAPK signaling pathway. For example, PMAPs upregulate the expression of host defense peptides (such as β-defensins) by activating the TLR2/MAPK signaling pathway, thereby indirectly inhibiting the excessive proliferation of Holdemanella and Blautia (Shi et al., 2021). The findings of enhanced intestinal immune function in piglets treated with RT are consistent with this paradigm. The upregulation of antimicrobial peptides and the activation of TLR signaling pathways suggest that RT promotes a more robust innate immune response, which may help to prevent pathogen colonization and invasion. Additionally, the increase in the anti-inflammatory cytokine TGF-β indicates that RT may also play a role in modulating the inflammatory response, thereby maintaining gut homeostasis. Thus, the modulatory mechanisms of RT on the immune function of piglets are complex and diverse.

The effects of RT on the ECM and its regulatory factors are an interesting and novel aspect of this study. The ECM provides structural support to the intestinal epithelium and is involved in cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation. Alterations in the ECM composition and organization have been implicated in various intestinal diseases (Wang and Ji, 2019; Yi et al., 2023). Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is usually accompanied by intestinal fibrosis, characterized by excessive deposition of ECM components caused by ECM remodeling (Biel et al., 2024; Petrey and de la Motte, 2017). The results suggest that RT can modulate the expression of ECM genes and proteins, potentially through the regulation of intracellular and extracellular signaling pathways. This finding opens new avenues for understanding the role of the ECM in gut health and disease and may have implications for the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

In comparison to previous studies on probiotics and their metabolites, this research stands out in several ways. Firstly, the use of synthetic biology to engineer E. coli for RT production provides a more controllable and scalable source of this metabolite compared to relying solely on natural producers. Secondly, the effects of RT on multiple aspects of gut health, including microbiota composition, metabolite profiling, immune function, and ECM, were comprehensively analyzed, leading to a more complete understanding of the mode of action of RT. Finally, the demonstration of the beneficial effects of RT in pigs underlines its potential as a practical and effective feed additive for the swine industry.

Looking ahead, future studies could focus on several areas. Firstly, further investigations are needed to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of RT on the gut microbiota and host physiology. This could involve the use of advanced omics technologies, such as metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, and proteomics, to gain a more detailed understanding of the interactions between RT, the gut microbiota, and the host. Secondly, the long-term effects of RT supplementation on the growth and health of animals need to be evaluated. This will help to determine the optimal dosage and duration of treatment and to assess the potential for any adverse effects over time. Thirdly, the potential application of RT in other animal species and in human health should be explored. Given its broad-spectrum antibacterial and immunomodulatory properties, RT may have implications for the prevention and treatment of various infectious and inflammatory diseases.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this study demonstrate that RT produced via synthetic biology reshaped the gut microbiota structure, promoted the growth of beneficial bacteria, and regulated microbial metabolism. It enhanced the integrity of the intestinal barrier by upregulating the expression of related genes and proteins, activated immune signaling pathways such as the TLR-MAPK pathway, and strengthened the intestinal immune defense. Additionally, RT effectively regulated the expression of its associated genes and proteins at the ECM level, maintaining the homeostasis of intestinal tissue. These combined effects collectively contributed to the healthy growth of piglets.
