Olfactory impairment associated with cognitive decline: findings from cognitive domain assessments among older adults in West Jakarta, Indonesia
Authors: Yvonne Suzy Handajani, Yuda Turana, Antoninus Hengky, Kevin Kristian, Ji-Sook Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlfactory dysfunction has emerged as a potential early marker of neurodegenerative diseases. Despite growing interest, population-based evidence on its association with cognitive domains remains limited, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. This study examined the association between olfactory impairment and multidomain cognitive performance in older Indonesian adults.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study among 205 community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 60 years in West Jakarta. The cognitive assessments included the Indonesian version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA-Ina), verbal fluency, a modified Boston Naming Test (BNT), constructional praxis, word list recall, delayed memory, and word list recognition. Background

Olfactory function, an essential sensory ability, plays a critical role in quality of life and is increasingly recognized as a potential early marker of neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease [1, 2]. Accumulating evidence from epidemiologic studies has shown that olfactory impairment (OI) is associated with an elevated risk of cognitive decline and dementia in older adults [3, 4]. Olfactory testing is non-invasive, cost-effective, and relatively simple to administer, making it a promising and feasible screening tool, particularly in community-based and resource–limited settings [5].

Several population-based cohort studies have demonstrated that OI is associated with an increased risk of cognitive decline, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and dementia, as well as with structural changes in brain regions involved in memory and executive function [6–9]. Notably, a large-scale prospective study in the United States, the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging, reported that reduced olfactory identification ability predicted the onset of amnestic MCI and subsequent progression to Alzheimer’s disease dementia over a mean follow-up of 3.5 years [10].

While this body of evidence is growing, most studies examining the association between OI and cognitive decline have been conducted in high-income countries, and relatively few have examined whether these associations are generalizable across diverse sociocultural and demographic settings, including low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [11, 12]. Available evidence suggests that OI is associated with an increased risk of incident MCI and progression to dementia, with rates and trajectories influenced by demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors [10, 13]. However, population-based longitudinal studies examining cognitive decline in LMICs remain scarce, with a notable absence of evidence from low-income regions and from countries outside China [13]. A recent systematic review highlighted the limited availability of robust longitudinal research examining olfactory dysfunction as a predictor of cognitive decline and dementia [4, 9]. Given that nearly two-thirds of individuals with dementia reside in LMICs, strengthening epidemiological evidence on OI as an early marker of cognitive decline in these settings is essential for informing targeted risk-reduction and early detection strategies [12].

In particular, evidence on OI and cognitive decline remains limited in Indonesia, where rapid population aging has not yet been accompanied by widespread implementation of early detection strategies within community health systems [14]. Moreover, domain-specific associations between OI and distinct cognitive functions, such as memory, executive function, and language, have been insufficiently investigated, particularly in LMIC contexts, despite their potential relevance for early identification and targeted screening. Identifying individuals with OI who are at elevated risk of cognitive decline is therefore essential for developing context-appropriate screening strategies.

This study aimed to investigate the associations between OI and cognitive performance across specific domains—namely, memory, language, and visuospatial function—among community-dwelling older adults living in West Jakarta, Indonesia.

Methods

Population and study design

The database used in this study originated from a pre-existing longitudinal research registry established by the Atma Jaya Ageing Research Centre as part of an elderly quality-of-life study conducted in West Jakarta. This cross-sectional study was conducted among community-dwelling older adults in West Jakarta using baseline data from this quality-of-life study. From a database listing 1,200 older adults in the study area, 300 individuals (25%) were randomly selected for screening, and data were collected through structured interviews conducted by trained physicians. Individuals aged 60 years or older who were able to read and write were eligible for inclusion. Participants were excluded if they had hearing loss, communication difficulties, acute conditions affecting the sense of smell (e.g., coughs, colds, or other respiratory disorders), or a history of conditions that could influence cognitive or olfactory function, such as psychological disorders, depression, cerebrovascular disease, brain tumors, hydrocephalus, epilepsy, alcoholism, severe head trauma, or allergic rhinitis.

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power software version 3.1.9.7 (Franz Faul, Universitat Kiel, Germany), which is based on an effect size of 0.25, a two-tailed test, an alpha level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 95% [15]. When the t-test family was used for ANOVA, the minimum required sample size was 197. To account for an anticipated 10% nonresponse rate, a total of 217 older adults were targeted for recruitment. Among them, 205 participants were included in the final analysis, while 12 were excluded due to missing data. Participant characteristics

This study collected data on participants’ demographic characteristics, including age, sex, marital status, and education level. Education was categorized into a binary variable due to the small number of participants in the lower education categories as low (those who did not complete primary school, completed primary school, junior high school, and senior high school) or high (bachelor’s degree or above). Medical history was obtained by asking participants whether they had been diagnosed with chronic illnesses, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, stroke, or cardiovascular conditions, by a medical professional.

Olfactory function

Olfactory function was evaluated using an 8-item odor identification test adapted from a previously validated Indonesian odor identification tool [16], which was originally developed as a screening instrument for older adults [17]. The original version included nine odor items, but the lime item was excluded in the present study because the research team judged that the lime and lemon scents provided by the third-party supplier were nearly indistinguishable. The final test consisted of eight common odors (coffee, eucalyptus, jasmine, orange, menthol, lemon, lemongrass, and aromatic ginger (galanga)), which are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Each odor was presented in a sealed, labelled bottle containing the liquid scent, and participants were asked to freely identify the odor without response options. If the participant was unable to identify the odor, a multiple-choice question was provided. One point was awarded for each correct identification, regardless of the response format, yielding a total possible score of 8. Olfactory impairment was defined as a score of less than 5, corresponding to the 25th percentile of the score distribution of scores in our sample.

Cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed using the Indonesian version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA-InA), with a score of 26–30 indicating normal cognition, 18–25 indicating MCI, and a score below 18 indicating probable dementia [18]. Regarding education adjustment, in accordance with the MoCA-Ina administration guideline, one additional point was added for participants with ≤ 12 years of formal education. In addition, domain-specific cognitive functions were evaluated using tests adapted from the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease (CERAD) neuropsychological battery [19], covering the language domain (verbal fluency, the Boston Naming Test (BNT)), visuoconstruction (constructional praxis), and memory domain (word list memory, delayed memory, recognition memory). Verbal fluency was tested by asking participants to name as many animals as possible in 60 s; scores below 16 indicated impairment. The modified BNT consists of naming 18 items, with a maximum score of 18 and scores below 14 suggesting impairments. For the constructional praxis task, participants copied 4 geometric shapes (circle, diamond, rectangle, and cube), and performance was scored on the basis of similarity to the reference image. The scoring range is zero to 11, and scores below 11 indicate impairment. The word list memory task involved presenting a list of 10 common nouns shown at 3-second intervals. The list was presented in the same random order in three separate trials, and the participants were asked to recall as many words as possible after each trial. The total score across the three trials ranged from 0 to 30, with a cutoff of 19 indicating possible impairment. Finally, the recall and recognition of the 10 words were tested, with cut-offs of 7 and 10, respectively.

Results

The population characteristics are presented in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2. Among all participants, 27.8% were identified as having olfactory impairment using a culturally adapted odor identification test. More than half of the participants were aged 70 years or older (58.4%), and the majority were female (71.2%). Most participants had a high level of education (75.1%), and 45.9% were currently married. Approximately two-thirds of the population (63.9%) had one or more chronic diseases. On the basis of the cognitive assessment, 15.6% of the participants were classified as having probable dementia, and 57.1% were classified as having probable MCI. The prevalence of impairment in specific cognitive domains was 44.9% for verbal fluency, 54.1% for BNT, 95.1% for visuoconstruction, 64.9% for word list memory, 73.2% for delayed memory, and 35.1% for recognition memory (Table 1; Supplementary Table 3).

As shown in Tables 2 and 3, olfactory test scores differed significantly across cognition-defined groups. Participants were categorized as normal cognition, probable MCI, or probable dementia based on MoCA-Ina, and as cognitively normal or cognitively impaired for domain-specific tests. Compared with those with normal cognition (5.57 ± 1.62, p = 0.003) and probable MCI (5.69 ± 1.69, p < 0.001), participants with probable dementia had lower olfactory scores (mean ± SD: 4.03 ± 2.39). Furthermore, domain-specific cognitive tests, including verbal fluency and BNT, revealed significantly poorer performance in the olfactory-impaired group (p = 0.008 and p = 0.002, respectively). In contrast, no significant associations were found for visuoconstruction, word list memory, delayed memory, or recognition memory (p > 0.05).

Table 4 presents the results of multivariable regression analyses investigating the association between olfactory function and cognitive test performance. In these models, participants with normal cognition and probable MCI were combined into a single non-demented group for comparison with probable dementia. After adjusting for demographic factors and chronic disease status (Model 3), probable dementia according to the MoCA-Ina was significantly associated with increased odds of olfactory impairment (OR: 4.29, 95% CI: 1.87–9.85), as were verbal fluency impairment (OR: 2.25, 95% CI: 1.15–4.39) and BNT impairment (OR: 2.21, 95% CI: 1.12–4.38). In linear regression models, cognitive impairment as assessed by the MoCA-Ina was associated with lower olfactory scores, indicating poorer olfactory function (β= -1.51, p < 0.001), as were verbal fluency impairment (β= -0.87, p = 0.001) and BNT impairment (β= -1.00, p < 0.001). No significant associations were detected between olfactory function and visuoconstruction, word list memory, delayed memory, or recognition memory across any of the models (p > 0.05).

Discussion

In this study of older adults in West Jakarta, we found that olfactory impairment was significantly associated with both global and specific cognitive deficits. The participants with OI had more than fourfold higher odds of probable dementia, as measured by the MoCA-InA. In addition, significant associations were observed between OI and poor performance in verbal fluency and BNT, suggesting an association between olfactory dysfunction and language-related cognitive domains. Interestingly, these associations were not observed in memory-related domains such as word list recall or recognition or in visuoconstruction.

In our study, the prevalence of probable dementia among adults aged ≥ 60 years, as assessed by the MoCA, was 15.6%. This estimate is higher than the 5–10% range reported in previous population-based studies conducted in East Asian countries such as Japan and China but lower than the 22–54% reported in clinic-based samples [21]. These differences in dementia prevalence across studies may partially reflect variations in study settings, population characteristics, health service access, or cultural factors across regions [22–25]. The prevalence of dementia varies widely by ethnicity, age, sex, and education [22], and health service access affects diagnosis and reported prevalence [23, 24]. Moreover, the prevalence may also vary depending on urban/rural status and across different countries [25].

In our study, approximately one-quarter of older adults showed olfactory impairment, as assessed by a culturally adapted odor identification test. Although direct comparisons with other studies should be made cautiously because of methodological and cultural differences, this prevalence is comparable to the pooled estimate of 25.1% (95% CI: 20.7–29.7%) reported in a recent meta-analysis involving over 10,000 participants aged > 55 years across ten countries [26]. These findings suggest a substantial burden of olfactory dysfunction among Indonesian older adults. Given the limited clinical awareness and absence of routine screening for olfactory impairment in this setting, our results underscore the potential value of incorporating olfactory testing into community-based cognitive assessments to facilitate early detection and prevention strategies.

These findings reinforce the observed association between olfactory impairment and global cognitive function as defined by the MoCA-Ina. This finding aligns with a growing body of evidence suggesting that olfactory dysfunction may serve as an early marker of cognitive decline, particularly in relation to MCI and Alzheimer’s disease [27, 28]. While domain-specific cognitive impairments have been investigated in previous studies, our findings were only partially consistent with the literature. We observed significant associations between olfactory impairment and tasks primarily involving language-related cognitive domains, such as verbal fluency and naming, which may also engage executive processes [29, 30]. Although many studies have reported more robust and consistent associations between olfactory dysfunction and memory domains [7, 8], some have also identified associations with language domains [31–33].

These inconsistencies may be attributed to methodological heterogeneity, including the types of cognitive and olfactory tests used, differences in population characteristics (e.g., age, sex, and education), and variations in the stage or type of neurodegenerative disease examined [6, 34]. In addition to methodological factors, several cognitive and neurological perspectives may explain why the relationship between olfactory impairment and memory and language varies across studies. The interplay among memory, language, and odor identification involves multiple processes related to encoding, retrieval, and the integration of perceptual and semantic information.

Dual Coding Theory suggests that odors are better remembered when perceptual-olfactory and verbal codes are both available; evidence shows that naming or labeling odors enhances recognition memory [35]. Similarly, theories of semantic encoding emphasize that difficulty in naming odors-often described as the “tip-of-the-nose” phenomenon-can lead to poorer memory performance because of weaker odor-semantics associations [36]. Associative memory models further point out that olfaction has a unique neural pathway to limbic structures like the amygdala and hippocampus, supporting rapid and emotionally rich memory formation. The Misfit Theory of Spontaneous Conscious Odor Perception (MITSCOP) explores in more detail how this specific neurobiological pathway shapes conscious odor perception in daily life [36]. Moreover, linguistic relativity studies demonstrate how cultural and linguistic environments shape one’s ability to verbally categorize odors-for example, the Jahai can name odors just as easily as colors, indicating that naming ability is shaped by experience rather than fixed [36].

Taken together, these theoretical perspectives suggest that odor identification relies not only on memory functions but also on semantic processing, verbal labeling ability, and cultural-linguistic f First, as the study was conducted in a single urban area (West Jakarta) using a pre-familiarity with odor descriptors. These frameworks may help explain why, in our study, olfactory impairment was more strongly associated with language-related tasks than with memory tasks.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, as the study was conducted in a single urban area (West Jakarta) using a pre-existing longitudinal research registry, the generalizability of the findings to other populations may be limited. Consequently, the study population might overrepresent older adults with higher health service utilization, comorbidities, and educational attainment. In addition, the proportion of participants classified as cognitively impaired in this study was higher than that reported in population-based studies, which may partly reflect the MoCA-Ina as a screening instrument rather than a diagnostic tool in a non–population-based sample. Second, the relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle associations. Nevertheless, the consistent trends observed across multiple cognitive domains suggest that the associations found are unlikely to be due to chance alone. At the same time, because multiple cognitive domains were examined, the risk of inflated type I error should be acknowledged, and the p-values should be interpreted with caution. Third, owing to the cross-sectional design, causal relationships between OI and cognitive decline cannot be established. However, the observed associations provide a foundation for future longitudinal studies to determine whether olfactory dysfunction serves as an early marker of cognitive impairment. Fourth, both the MoCA-Ina and domain-specific cognitive tests are screening rather than diagnostic tools; thus, classifications of probable MCI, probable dementia, and domain-specific impairment may not correspond to clinical diagnoses. Nevertheless, the use of such measures is appropriate in large-scale epidemiological studies for practical and cost-effective assessment. Finally, the high prevalence of impairment observed across multiple cognitive domains, including but not limited to visuoconstruction, may have reduced the ability to detect between-group differences. This pattern may reflect limited score variability and potential misclassification related to the selected cut-off values and score distributions in this population. As such, caution is warranted when interpreting domain-specific findings, and further research is needed to validate impairment thresholds across diverse populations. Some findings may also reflect the measurement properties of the cognitive tests and the classification decisions applied in this study.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence of a robust association between olfactory impairment and global cognitive function, as well as specific deficits in language-related tasks, such as verbal fluency and naming, which may also involve executive control. Given that olfactory assessment is non-invasive, low-cost, and easy to implement, it may serve as a valuable tool in community-based cognitive screening, especially in resource-limited settings. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to establish causality, elucidate underlying neural mechanisms, and assess the utility of olfactory testing in the early detection and management of dementia.
