Serum/Faecal S100A12, CRP and lactoferrin can be used to distinguish ınfectious and non‐ınfectious canine diarrhoea
Authors: Mehmet Maden, Erdem Gülersoy
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic efficacies of selected inflammatory and intestinal biomarkers in cases of infectious and non‐infectious diarrhoea in dogs.MethodsA total of 60 dogs, 12 healthy (Control Group) and 48 with diarrhoea were used. Viral, Bacterial, Parasitic (infectious) and Nutritional diarrhoea (non‐infectious) subgroups (n: 12) were formed according to the aetiology, on the basis of clinical and laboratory examinations. Selected inflammatory and intestinal biomarkers (Calgranulin, S100A12; Lactoferrin, LCTF; C‐reactive protein, CRP) were measured both in serum and faecal samples.ResultsCompared to the Control and Nutritional Diarrhoea groups, the infectious diarrhoea groups had higher serum S100A12, LCTF, CRP, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine (CR), alanine transaminase and alkaline phosphatase, and lower glucose (GLU), sodium (Na) and potassium (K) concentrations (p < 0.05); Viral and Parasitic Diarrhoea groups had lower serum albumin (ALB) and total protein (TP) concentrations (p < 0.05). Faecal S100A12, LCTF and CRP concentrations were higher in infectious diarrhoea groups compared to the Control and Nutritional Diarrhoea groups (p < 0.05). Faecal LCTF and CRP concentrations were higher in the Bacterial Diarrhoea group than in the Viral and Parasitic Diarrhoea groups (p < 0.05).Clinical significanceIt was determined that serum (area under curve, AUC: 0.842 and 0.956) and faecal (AUC: 0.975 and 0.786) S100A12 and CRP concentrations in viral diarrhoea; serum (AUC: 0.956) and faecal (AUC: 0.992) LCTF concentrations in bacterial diarrhoea have diagnostic values in the diagnosis of the presence of intestinal inflammation and damage and can be used in the differential diagnosis of infectious and non‐infectious diarrhoea.Keywords: biomarker, CIBDAI, diarrhoea, dog

INTRODUCTION

Diarrhoea is a primary symptom of intestinal disorders and characterized by alterations in faecal fluidity, volume and frequency of defecation (Nixon et al., 2019). The major pathological conditions affecting the intestines are those that cause structural mucosal damage and result in decreased mucosal surface area (Allenspach & Gaschen, 2008; Allenspach et al., 2007), which are caused by infectious (viruses, bacteria and parasites) and non‐infectious (sudden diet change, food intolerance, irritable bowel syndrome and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), such as ulcerative colitis and hyperthyroidism) factors (Candellone et al., 2020; Neumann et al., 2022).

Viral diarrhoea is often caused by canine Rotavirus, Coronavirus, Herpesvirus, Parvovirus (CPV) and Distemper Virus (CDV) (Caddy, 2018). Bacterial diarrhoea occurs due to the invasion of enteropathogenic bacteria into the intestinal epithelium, resulting in damage and release of enterotoxins. Bacterial diarrhoea in dogs is often caused by Campylobacter spp., Clostridium spp., Enterobacter, Salmonella spp. and pathogenic Escherichia coli (Neumann et al., 2022). Intestinal parasites invade the intestinal wall attach to the intestinal epithelium and cause various gastrointestinal symptoms including diarrhoea. Parasitic diarrhoea in dogs is often caused by Ancylostoma caninum, Strongyloides spp., Toxocara canis, Toxocara mystax, Toxascaris leonina and Trichuris vulpis (Allenspach & Gaschen, 2008). Moreover, Giardia intestinalis, Isospora canis and Cryptosporidium spp. can be listed as common infectious causes (Gülersoy & Ekici, 2022). Contrary to diarrhoea due to infectious aetiology, nutritional diarrhoea can occur due to dietary indiscretion and poor overall ingredient quality (Allenspach et al., 2007; Candellone et al., 2020). In the assessment of gastrointestinal disorders, it is important to exclude conditions secondary to hepatic, pancreatic, renal, adrenal and thyroid diseases. This assessment is best performed with a complete blood count (CBC) and serum biochemistry profiling (Berghoff & Steiner, 2011). In addition, clinical scores can be used to evaluate the clinical manifestation of diarrhoea. Thus, the Canine Inflammatory Bowel Disease Activity Index (CIBDAI) was developed to evaluate the clinical disease activity (Jergens et al., 2003).

It is a well‐known fact that gastrointestinal functionality affects the overall health of animals. Considering the complex interactions of the gastrointestinal system, the use of potential biomarkers to monitor gastrointestinal system health gains importance (Ziese & Suchodolski, 2021). Most of these potential biomarkers can be evaluated both in serum and faeces. Recently, the use of faecal biomarkers in the evaluation of intestinal disease presence, activity and severity of damage has gained importance due to the non‐invasive nature of this method (Félix et al., 2022; Maden, 2015;). S100A12 (Calgranulin) belongs to S100/calgranulin‐protein family which is a molecule related to a damage‐associated molecular pattern (Foell et al., 2007). It is a sensitive and specific biomarker of localized inflammatory conditions including gastrointestinal inflammation and it was reported that its concentration can increase in serum due to various inflammatory disorders, such as protein‐losing enteropathy, IBD and chronic inflammatory enteropathy (Heilmann & Steiner, 2018). It was reported to be a useful biomarker in the diagnosis of systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, respiratory tract diseases and IBD in human medicine (Foell, 2003a; Dabritz et al., 2013). The concentrations of S100A12 were investigated in the diagnosis and prognosis of enteropathy in dogs (Heilmann et al., 2011; Heilmann et al., 2014; Heilmann et al., 2018, 2019). Lactoferrin (LCTF) is found in the oral cavity and intestinal tract, which are in direct contact with pathogens such as viruses and bacteria. LCTF has a bacteriostatic effect by inhibiting the growth of various gram‐positive and negative bacteria due to its iron‐binding ability (Hellweg et al., 2008). Moreover, it is the main component of the secondary granules of neutrophils and has pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory characteristics. Moreover, it was stated that the faecal LCTF concentration increases in case of intestinal inflammation as a consequence of migrating inflammatory cells (polymorphonuclear leukocytes such as monocytes and neutrophils) (Mosli et al., 2015). Increased faecal LCTF concentration was reported to be associated with severe Clostridium difficile infection with high leukocyte (WBC) and low serum albumin (ALB) (Boone et al., 2013). C‐reactive protein (CRP) is a positive acute phase protein and its serum concentration is a non‐specific marker of inflammation (Heilmann & Steiner, 2018). Although not specific to the intestinal tract, serum CRP concentration was determined to be significantly higher in dogs with IBD compared to healthy dogs (Jergens et al., 2003; Berghoff & Steiner, 2011; Lee et al., 2021).

Diagnostic biomarkers are critical in determining whether a patient has any disease condition for which treatment may be indicated, or whether an organism should be included in a clinical study examining a particular disease (Califf, 2018). Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate clinical and laboratory variables along with selected inflammatory and intestinal biomarkers, including serum and faecal S100A12, LCTF and CRP, and investigating their diagnostic efficacies in dogs with diarrhoea classified as infectious and non‐infectious on the basis of clinical and laboratory examinations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was approved by Selcuk University Veterinary Faculty Local Ethics Committee (decision date 20.10.2015 and number 2015/89). Also, the informed owner's consent was obtained.

Animal material

The animal material of this study consisted of 60 dogs, all were admitted to Selcuk University, Animal Hospital for diagnosis/treatment and/or vaccination/routine check‐up purposes. All dogs were client owned, mostly mixed breed (23 females and 37 males) with a median age of 5.5 (3.5–7.5) months. Anamnestic data revealed that most of the dogs were unvaccinated, and some dogs were given an antiparasitic drug once (13 out of 60 dogs).

Clinical examinations and CIBDAI scoring

After anamnestic data evaluation, physical examinations, including body temperature, heart and respiratory rates, colour of mucous membranes, peripheral pulse quality, palpable lymph node, hydration and mental status evaluations, were performed. CIBDAI scoring was performed separately for each of the dogs with diarrhoea. In this context, the dogs’ behaviour/activity, appetite, vomiting, faecal structure and frequency and the presence of weight loss were evaluated. Scoring was evaluated as 0 = normal, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe and total CIBDAI scores of 0–3 were considered insignificant, 4–5 were mild, 6–8 moderate and >9 severe (Jergens et al., 2003).

Blood and faecal sampling

Venous blood samples were obtained via vena cephalica venipuncture (3–5 mL) from all the dogs with minimal patient stress. Blood gas analysis (0.5–1 mL blood sample, using injectors with heparin), CBC (0.5–1 mL blood sample, using tubes coated with K3EDTA) serum biochemistry and biomarker analyses (3–4 mL blood sample, using serum tubes without anticoagulant) were performed. For biomarker measurements, blood samples were centrifuged at 5000 g for 10 min, their serum were extracted and transferred to eppendorf tubes and stored at −20°C until analysis. Faecal samples were collected during defecation; the samples were divided in two: 5 g of fresh faeces was stored at +4°C for faecal microscopic analysis, and the rest was placed in sterile faecal collection containers for faecal biomarker analysis and stored at −20°C. Faecal microscopic examination was performed using the zinc sulphate centrifugal flotation technique. Parasite eggs and oocysts were identified by light microscopy (magnification from ×40 to ×100) according to their morphological characteristics. For pathogen analysis, rectal faecal samples were taken with sterile swab and sent to the bacteriology laboratory (Heilmann et al., 2018).

Laboratory analysis

Within the scope of CBC, total leukocyte (WBC), haemoglobin (Hb), haematocrit (Ht), erythrocyte (RBC), platelet, mean corpuscular volume, mean corpuscular haemoglobin, mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration, lymphocyte, monocyte and granulocyte levels were evaluated using an autoanalyser (MS4S Vet). Within the scope of blood gas and electrolyte analysis, pH, bicarbonate (HCO3), base deficit (BE), partial carbon dioxide pressure (PCO2), partial oxygen pressure (PO2), oxygen saturation (satO2) and lactate concentrations were evaluated using an autoanalyser (GEM Premier Plus). Within the scope of serum biochemistry profiling, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CR), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), glucose (GLU), calcium, cholesterol (CHOL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total bilirubin, sodium (Na), potassium (K), vitamin B12 and folic acid concentrations were evaluated using a biochemical autoanalyser (BT 3000, Italy).

Forming subgroups and inclusion/exclusion criteria

As a result of clinical and laboratory examinations along with rapid diagnostic test kit (IDEXX Veterinary Diagnostics faecal SNAP CPV antigen test; sensitivity 100% and specificity 94%–100% and ASAN Pharm Easy Test CDV antigen test; relative sensitivity: 97.96%, relative specificity: 97.50%) results, the dogs, which were determined to be healthy with a CIBDAI score of 0–3 (insignificant), were included in the Control Group (n: 12). Rapid diagnostic test kit (IDEXX, faecal SNAP CPV and ASAN Pharm Easy Test CDV antigen) results, faecal microscopic zinc sulphate centrifugal flotation examinations along with clinical and laboratory examinations were performed to subdivide the dogs with diarrhoea according to the aetiology of diarrhoea. The dogs with no parasites or eggs in the faecal microscopic examination, no leukocytosis in the CBC and negative rapid diagnostic test kit results were included in the Nutritional Diarrhoea Group (n: 12). The dogs with nematode eggs (T. canis) or protozoa (G. intestinalis) along with negative rapid diagnostic test kit results were included in the Parasitic Diarrhoea (n: 12) group. The dogs with negative rapid diagnostic test kit results and microscopic faecal examination for parasites or eggs, but with significant leukocytosis in the CBC, were included in the Bacterial Diarrhoea (n: 12) Group. The dogs with positive rapid diagnostic test kit results and negative microscopic faecal examination for parasites or eggs were included in the Viral Diarrhoea (n: 12) Group. Consequently, it was ensured that all the dogs with diarrhoea were monoinfected with a single pathogen. Dogs with coinfected diarrhoea were not included in the study. For bacterial diarrhoea, leukocyte count >18,000 cells/mm3 was accepted. The presence of IBD was excluded due to the fact that the gastrointestinal findings lasted less than 3 weeks and the enteropathogens causing the clinical findings had already been identified. The presence of diarrhoea without any sign and symptom such loss of appetite due to sudden diet change, which was learned from the anamnestic data, was accepted as the cause of nutritional diarrhoea (Isidori et al., 2022).

Biomarker measurements

S100A12, LCTF and CRP concentrations were measured using canine specific ELISA kits (BT Lab Bioassay Technologies) according to the manufacturer's instructions from serum samples. Before biomarker measurement of faecal samples, 1 g of faeces from each sample was mixed and dissolved using 2 mL of 0.9% NaCl solution, and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged again at 5000 rpm for 45 min. S100A12, LCTF and CRP concentrations were measured from the remaining supernatant using dog‐specific ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.00 (SPSS for Windows) statistical software. One‐sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to determine whether all the data were parametric or non‐parametric. Parametric data were presented as median (min–max) using Student's t test, and One‐way ANOVA for analysis of variance, Tukey and Mann Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to determine diagnostic cut‐off values to evaluate the presence of intestinal injury and/or the magnitude of the injury between the dogs with diarrhoea due to different aetiologies. Within the scope of the ROC analysis, the area under the curve (AUC), standard error (std. error), cut‐off, sensitivity and specificity were considered. AUC value of 0.5 suggests no discrimination (such as ability to diagnose patients with and without the disease or condition based on the test), 0.7–0.8 is considered acceptable, 0.8–0.9 is considered excellent and more than 0.9 is considered outstanding (Yang & Berdine, 2017) in order to indicate the presence of intestinal damage and/or the severity of the damage. Statistical significance was accepted as p < 0.05 for all the data.

RESULTS

The total CIBDAI score was higher in the dogs with infectious diarrhoea (bacterial, viral and parasitic) than the healthy dogs (p < 0.05). In comparison between the healthy dogs and the dogs with diarrhoea, the CIBDAI score of the Viral Diarrhoea Group was higher than that of the non‐infectious Nutritional Diarrhoea Group (p < 0.05). Receiver operating characteristic analysis results

The serum S100A12 concentration had excellent (AUC: 0.842), and the serum CRP concentration had outstanding (AUC: 0.956) diagnostic value in demonstrating the presence of intestinal inflammation and damage in cases of viral diarrhoea. The serum LCTF concentration had an outstanding (AUC: 0.956) diagnostic value in demonstrating the presence of intestinal inflammation and damage in cases of bacterial diarrhoea. In cases of parasitic and nutritional diarrhoea, it was observed that the selected serum biomarkers had no diagnostic value (AUC < 0.700). The faecal S100A12 concentration had outstanding (AUC: 0.975), and CRP concentration had acceptable (AUC: 0.786) diagnostic value in demonstrating the presence of intestinal inflammation and damage in cases of viral diarrhoea. In cases of bacterial diarrhoea, the faecal LCTF concentration was determined to have an outstanding (AUC: 0.992) diagnostic value. In cases of parasitic and nutritional diarrhoea, it was observed that the selected faecal biomarkers had no diagnostic value (AUC < 0.700). DISCUSSION

Since diarrhoea cases have similar clinical manifestations, determining its aetiology and the presence and/or severity of intestinal inflammation helps both to form a differential diagnosis list and to initiate appropriate treatment protocols. Therefore, in the present study, biochemical parameters along with selected biomarkers in serum and faecal samples in dogs with diarrhoea both of infectious (viral, bacterial and parasitic) and non‐infectious (nutritional) aetiology were evaluated comparatively. Significant differences were observed as a result of serum biochemistry profiling and analysis of serum/faecal biomarkers in dogs with infectious diarrhoea compared to the healthy dogs and the dogs with non‐infectious nutritional diarrhoea. The most prominent differences were detected in the comparative ROC‐based diagnostic analyses; it was observed that serum and faecal S100A12 and CRP in cases of viral diarrhoea, and serum and faecal LCTF in cases of bacterial diarrhoea have excellent diagnostic performances in demonstrating the presence and severity of intestinal damage.

The CIBDAI scoring system is utilized to evaluate the severity of the disease and the response to treatment in IBDs. In a study of 58 dogs with IBD, increased serum CRP concentration was observed in the dogs with a minimum total CIBDAI score of 5 compared to the healthy dogs. In addition, after a medical intervention, a decrease both in serum CRP concentration and in the total CIBDAI score were reported (Jergens et al., 2003). Similarly, in a study conducted in 56 dogs with IBD, intestinal wall thickness (duodenum >5 mm in 6 dogs, jejunum >4 mm in 13 dogs and colon >2 mm in 5 dogs), which was detected by ultrasonographic examination, was observed to be correlated with a higher CIBDAI score (Gaschen et al., 2008). Therefore, CIBDAI score evaluation was reported to be useful for disease prognosis (Jergens, 2004). In the present study, the total CIBDAI scores of all the dogs with infectious diarrhoea were higher than that of the Control Group. The numerically highest CIBDAI score was detected in the Viral Diarrhoea Group (p < 0.05) (Table 1). Higher CIBDAI scores in the dogs with infectious diarrhoea were associated with the severity of diarrhoea‐related clinical findings (Jergens et al., 2003; Jergens, 2004; Gaschen et al., 2008), indicating that the CIBDAI score system is useful in assessing the severity of the disease.

Diarrhoea can cause electrolyte imbalances and dehydration. In addition, due to dehydration, intravascular volume reduction can cause hemodynamic instability, decreased tissue perfusion, cellular hypoxia, hypotension, organ damage and even death. Therefore, evaluation of some clinical, haematological and serum biochemistry analytes helps to determine the presence of damage and/or the severity of damage in target organs (Gülersoy & Ekici, 2022). It was reported that Na and K concentrations decrease in dogs with diarrhoea due to vomiting, diarrhoea and inflammatory changes in the gastric and intestinal mucosa (Kumar & Kumar, 2017). Moreover, it was reported that malnutrition, septicemia and stress‐induced activation of catecholamines cause electrolyte imbalances such as hypokalemia, hyponatremia and hypochloremia in dogs with CPV (Kalli et al., 2010). In the present study, the serum Na and K concentrations of the dogs with infectious diarrhoea were lower than that of the Control Group (p < 0.05) (Table 2). This finding is related to vomiting and diarrhoea caused by gastroenteritis (Kumar & Kumar, 2017). Increased BUN and CR concentrations were reported as a result of dehydration due to vomiting and diarrhoea associated with gastroenteritis, and they were reported to be useful predictors of dehydration (Hoxha et al., 2014). In the present study, BUN and CR concentrations of the dogs with infectious diarrhoea were higher than those of the Control and Nutritional Diarrhoea groups (p < 0.05) (Table 2). This finding was interpreted as a result of dehydration due to fluid loss caused by vomiting and diarrhoea (Salem et al., 2018). ALP and ALT concentrations can be used as useful biomarkers of impaired cellular integrity, cellular toxicity and may signal liver damage or alteration in bile flow (Giannini et al., 2005). In addition, AST, ALT and ALP are hepatic enzymes that increase in hepatocyte damage and inflammatory disorders of the liver and help in the diagnosis and assessing the success of treatment protocols of liver disorders (Latiwesh et al., 2018). Moreover, it was reported that serum ALP enzyme activities are higher in dogs with diarrhoea than in healthy dogs (Arora et al., 2018). It was also reported that serum ALP, ALT and AST enzyme activities are increased in dogs with CPV (Shah et al., 2013). In the present study, ALT concentrations differed among the groups, and the dogs with infectious diarrhoea had higher serum ALP concentrations than the healthy dogs and the dogs with nutritional diarrhoea (p < 0.05) (Table 2). This finding may be related to the presence of hepatic hypoxia due to severe hypovolemia, hepatic damage (Schoeman et al., 2013), disruption of the intestinal barrier and absorption of toxic components as a result of intestinal epithelial damage (Shah et al., 2013). However, the high enzyme concentrations of the present study were not at the levels which were reported for acute or chronic hepatic injury previously (Arora et al., 2018). GLU is the fundamental metabolic substrate that tissues need for energy production (Güemes et al., 2016). In the presence of infection, hypoglycaemia develops as a result of insufficient food intake, depletion of glycogen stores, disruption of gluconeogenesis and increased peripheral GLU demand (Gulersoy et al., 2019). In the present study, the dogs with infectious diarrhoea had lower serum GLU concentration than that of the healthy dogs and the dogs with nutritional diarrhoea (p < 0.05) (Table 2). This finding may be related to the presence of anorexia, malabsorption and increased GLU demand (Kumar & Kumar, 2017). ALB is a plasma protein that contributes to the formation/sustainability of oncotic pressure, and decreased serum ALB and TP concentrations were reported in dogs with chronic inflammatory enteropathy and protein‐losing enteropathy (Allenspach et al., 2007; Heilmann et al., 2014). This decrease was stated to be associated with mucosal damage that causes protein loss from the intestines (Boone et al., 2013). In the present study, the serum ALB and TP concentrations of the Viral and Parasitic Diarrhoea groups were lower than that of the Control Group (p < 0.05) (Table 2). This finding may be associated with the intestinal mucosal/submucosal defect, intestinal haemorrhage and gastrointestinal protein loss due to impaired vascular permeability (Craven & Washabau, 2019). It was reported that serum ALB concentration decreases due to gastrointestinal protein loss in dogs with chronic inflammatory enteropathy, and the presence of hypoalbuminemia can be used as a prognostic factor (Allenspach et al., 2007; Wennogle et al., 2017). Within this scope, serum ALB and TP concentrations can be used to assess the presence and severity of intestinal damage in cases of infectious diarrhoea.

Calgranulin or S100A12 is a calcium‐binding protein belonging to the S100 family, expressed from neutrophils and monocytes. It was discussed that the interaction of S100A12 with a receptor for advanced glycation endproducts (RAGE) has a major role in inflammatory processes (Heilmann et al., 2010). It was stated that serum and faecal S100A12 concentration could be an important clinical biomarker that may allow the investigation of new therapeutic approaches through the RAGE‐S100A12 axis (Heilmann et al., 2014, 2016). In humans, increased serum and plasma S100A12 concentrations were reported in inflammatory disorders, including cystic fibrosis, rheumatoid arthritis (Foell, 2003a) and chronic IBD (Foell, 2003b). Moreover, it was reported that higher serum S100A12 concentration is associated with neutrophil infiltration in individuals with IBD (Collins et al., 2013). The sensitivity (82.4%) and specificity (68.4%) of S100A12 in dogs with IBD were similar to those in children with IBD (Leach et al., 2007). It was stated that mucosal S100A12 mRNA expression is increased in dogs with IBD, and this increase is caused by increased activated macrophage and mucosal S100A12 mRNA. This finding indicates that myelomonocytic and epithelial cells in the tissue increase S100A12 expression depending on the disease activity across a range of inflammatory states (Luckschander et al., 2010; Heilmann et al., 2012; Meijer et al., 2012). It was reported that increased S100A12 concentration in patients with Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis is due to neutrophils infiltrating the inflamed intestinal region (Foell, 2003a). Thus, faecal S100A12 concentration was reported to be a useful diagnostic and prognostic biomarker (Heilmann et al., 2014; Heilmann et al., 2016). In the present study, it was determined that the serum and faecal S100A12 concentrations of the Viral, Bacterial and Parasitic Diarrhoea groups were higher than those of the Control and Nutritional Diarrhoea groups (p < 0.05). Increased serum S100A12 concentrations of the dogs with infectious diarrhoea in the present study could be associated with intestinal damage and increased expression of S100A12 from polymorphic nuclear cells, primarily by neutrophils, infiltrating the inflammatory intestinal region (Foell, 2003a; Luckschander et al., 2010; Meijer et al., 2012). Also, increased faecal S100A12 concentrations of the present study could be related to an intestinal inflammatory response, polymorph nuclear cell infiltration and increased mucosal S100A12 mRNA expression (Heilmann et al., 2012; Collins et al., 2013). Moreover, as a result of the ROC‐based diagnostic performance analysis, it was determined that serum and faecal S100A12 concentrations also differ according to the severity of intestinal‐mucosal barrier damage depending on the aetiology of diarrhoea. According to these findings, it was determined that serum and faecal S100A12 concentrations are useful in evaluating the presence and/or severity of intestinal damage, especially in cases of viral diarrhoea.

Lactoferrin is an iron‐binding, neutrophil‐derived protein secreted from mucosal surfaces, and is abundantly found in the oral cavity and intestinal tract, which are in direct contact with pathogens such as viruses and bacteria. It acts as a bacteriostatic agent by inhibiting the growth of most gram positive/negative bacteria with its iron‐binding ability (Hellweg et al., 2008). It was reported that faecal LCTF concentration correlates positively with the degree of the mucosal inflammation and increases in case of intestinal inflammation (Mosli et al., 2015), and diarrhoea due to Salmonella spp. and C. difficile species (Langhorst & Boone, 2012), but decreases in viral gastroenteritis due to Rotavirus and Norovirus (Chen et al., 2011). In the present study, the serum and faecal LCTF concentrations of all the dogs with infectious diarrhoea were higher than those of the healthy dogs and the dogs with nutritional diarrhoea (p < 0.05). Also, as a result of ROC‐based diagnostic performance analysis, the serum and faecal LCTF concentrations were determined to have outstanding performance in the diagnosis of bacterial diarrhoea. These findings may be related to intestinal migration of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (Mosli et al., 2015). The elevated serum LCTF concentration was associated with an increase in local (intestinal) LCTF secretion (Chen et al., 2011; Langhorst & Boone, 2012). Thus, in line with the ROC analysis results, it was evaluated that the measurement of serum and faecal LCTF concentrations may help the diagnosis of bacterial diarrhoea.

CRP is a positive acute phase protein which is synthesized from the liver in cases of infection, inflammation and/or cancer. It was reported that serum CRP concentrations increase in pathogen‐related intestinal‐mucosal inflammation (Chen et al., 2011). Also, in a study conducted with 40 children with diarrhoea, it was determined that the serum CRP concentration was higher in children with bacterial diarrhoea compared to children with non‐bacterial diarrhoea, and it was reported that evaluation of the serum CRP concentration was a useful biomarker in the diagnosis of bacterial diarrhoea (Al‐Asy et al., 2017). Moreover, it was determined that serum CRP concentrations were higher in dogs with viral and bacterial diarrhoea than that of the healthy ones (Otoni et al., 2018). In the present study, serum CRP concentrations of the Viral, Bacterial and Parasitic Diarrhoea groups were higher than those of the Control and Nutritional Diarrhoea groups (p < 0.05). When evaluated in combination with the faecal LCTF concentrations, this finding was associated with a positive acute phase response (Chen et al., 2011). Similarly, the faecal CRP concentrations were higher in the Viral, Bacterial and Parasitic Diarrhoea groups compared to the Control and Nutritional Diarrhoea groups (p < 0.05). In addition, the faecal CRP concentration of the Bacterial Diarrhoea Group was higher than those of the Viral and Parasitic Diarrhoea groups in the comparison between the dogs with infectious diarrhoea (p < 0.05). The increased faecal CRP concentrations were associated with the development of intestinal‐mucosal damage (Chen et al., 2011). In addition, in the ROC‐based diagnostic performance analysis, the faecal CRP concentration was determined to have an acceptable diagnostic performance in the diagnosis of bacterial diarrhoea. Considering the lack of a study evaluating faecal CRP concentration in the dogs with diarrhoea, evaluation of faecal CRP concentration in combination with the biomarkers such as LCTF and S100A12 may provide diagnostic information in determining the presence/severity of intestinal damage, and in the diagnosis of bacterial diarrhoea.

Despite the fact that small sample sizes are frequently encountered in medical testing, there has not been much research on how to generate confidence/credible intervals for the AUC using small sample sizes for different methodologies. It has been noted that there is a greater disparity between the outcomes of various approaches the larger the genuine AUC value and the smaller the sample size (Feng et al., 2017). Consequently, the main limitation of this study is the small number of animals that may have an impact on the outcomes of the ROC‐based diagnostic performance analyses.

As a result, it was determined that the total CIBDAI score, serum/faecal S100A12, LCTF and CRP concentrations were associated with the presence of intestinal inflammation and damage in dogs with diarrhoea. It was determined that serum and faecal S100A12 (AUC: 0.842 and 0.956, respectively) and CRP (AUC: 0.975 and 0.786, respectively) concentrations in cases of viral diarrhoea; serum and faecal LCTF (AUC: 0.956 and 0.992, respectively) in cases of bacterial diarrhoea have diagnostic importance in the diagnosis of intestinal inflammation and damage. In addition, it was concluded that the aforementioned biomarkers can be used in the differential diagnosis of infectious and non‐infectious diarrhoea. Although these results are promising, clinical and prognostic uses of these parameters warrant further research.
