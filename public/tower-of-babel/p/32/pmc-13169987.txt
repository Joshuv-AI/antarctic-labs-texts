Sudden monocular vision decrease caused by isolated choroidal tuberculoma during anti-tuberculosis treatment: a case report
Authors: Cheng Zuo, Ya-Li Yang, Jun-Feng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMycobacterium tuberculosis primarily causes pulmonary diseases; however, it can also affect extrapulmonary organs, including the eyes. The choroid, as the intraocular tissue with the richest blood supply, is one of the most susceptible structures to intraocular tuberculosis. Abnormal ocular symptoms that occur during systemic anti-tuberculosis treatment (ATT) can easily be misdiagnosed as drug side effects or treatment failure. Abruptly discontinuing a specific medication could lead to the recurrence of extraocular tuberculosis and irreversible vision loss. This case study focuses on a young female who exhibited an abnormal ocular reaction during systemic ATT. Owing to an incorrect assessment in the initial stages, timely and appropriate treatment could not be initiated. Isolated abnormal ocular reactions during ATT are relatively rare. To help doctors promptly and accurately assess abnormal eye conditions, we suggest that patients who are diagnosed with tuberculosis in any organ visit an ophthalmologist immediately for a comprehensive examination to establish baseline data. This approach aims to minimize the risk of adverse deterioration during treatment as much as possible.Keywords: Choroidal tuberculoma, Extraocular tuberculosis, Anti-tuberculosis treatment, Paradoxical response

Case presentation

A 21-year-old female with a two-week history of decreased visual acuity and metamorphopsia in her left eye visited the ophthalmology clinic.

Four months prior, the patient developed a cough and low-grade fever. She was initially diagnosed with pneumonia and treated with antibiotics in the internal medicine department, but her symptoms persisted. Two months before this ophthalmology visit, the patient was transferred to an infectious disease hospital. The interferon gamma release assay (IGRA) test returned positive, and chest CT revealed findings consistent with active pulmonary tuberculosis. The patient was eventually diagnosed with pulmonary tuberculosis. She subsequently started a quadruple antituberculosis therapy (ATT) regimen consisting of oral isoniazid, ethambutol, rifapentine, and pyrazinamide. Her systemic symptoms improved after ATT. There was no evidence of a history of hypertension or diabetes.

Approximately one month after starting ATT, the patient suddenly experienced decreased vision and metamorphopsia in her left eye. At that time, the infectious disease specialist suspected ethambutol toxicity, and the drug was discontinued. However, her ocular symptoms did not improve, which prompted a referral to the ophthalmology department. The patient reported no associated ocular redness or pain.

Initial ophthalmic examination revealed that the best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 1.0 in the right eye and 0.4 in the left eye. The intraocular pressure (IOP) was 20 mmHg in the right eye and 19.7 mmHg in the left eye. Slit-lamp examination of the anterior segment revealed unremarkable findings in both eyes. A dilated fundus examination of the posterior segment revealed that the right eye was normal, but the left eye presented with a raised, round, subretinal yellow-white lesion located superotemporally to the optic disc. This lesion was accompanied by surrounding patchy hemorrhages and retinal neurosensory detachment in the macular area. The remainder of the retina appeared flat (Fig. 1a).

To further evaluate the diagnosis, several imaging investigations were performed. Ocular B-ultrasound revealed localized elevation at the posterior pole of the left eye (Fig. 1b). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) revealed separation between the neurosensory retinal layer and the retinal pigment epithelium layer in the macular region of the left eye, a dome-shaped, raised subretinal lesion located superior to the optic disc, and a substantial hyperreflective lesion beneath the neurosensory retinal layer (Fig. 1c and d). Fluorescein fundus angiography (FFA) revealed a nodular lesion located superotemporally to the optic disc of the left eye, which exhibited slightly increased fluorescence in the early phase. Surrounding patchy hemorrhages showed blocked fluorescence. As the angiography progressed, the fluorescence of the lesion intensified, and fluorescein leakage was observed in the late phase, along with fluorescein pooling in the macular area (Fig. 1e, f, g and h). Visual field testing and visual evoked potential (VEP) measurements were normal.

On the basis of the clinical presentation and imaging findings, the initial diagnoses were choroidal tuberculoma in the left eye, exudative retinal detachment in the left eye, pulmonary tuberculosis, and bilateral refractive errors. After consultation with the infectious disease physician, it was determined that there was no clear correlation between ocular symptoms and ethambutol toxicity. Therefore, the full ATT regimen (including ethambutol) was resumed. Given the need to control intraocular inflammation, prednisone tablets were added to the regimen at an initial dose of 30 mg, which was administered orally once daily.

At the two-week follow-up, the patient’s BCVA of the left eye improved to 0.6. Fundus examination revealed that the superotemporal lesion had diminished, the surrounding hemorrhage had been absorbed, and the macular neurosensory detachment had improved (Fig. 2a and b). The prednisone dosage was subsequently tapered by 5 mg per week.

One-month follow-up revealed that the BCVA of the left eye had fully recovered to 1.0. OCT confirmed the complete resolution of the macular neurosensory detachment and the absorption of the subretinal lesion. The subneurosensory lesion had shrunk and scarred (Fig. 2c and d). Prednisone was gradually discontinued, while ATT was maintained. No further changes in visual acuity or fundus appearance were observed during subsequent follow-up visits (Fig. 2e).

Discussion

This case report describes a young patient with pulmonary tuberculosis who experienced a decrease in visual acuity one month after starting ATT. While the referring physician initially suspected that the symptoms were due to ethambutol toxicity, a comprehensive ophthalmologic evaluation revealed a choroidal tuberculoma with associated exudative retinal detachment. The excellent response to corticosteroid therapy, despite the continuation of ethambutol, further supported an inflammatory or infectious etiology rather than a toxic etiology.

This patient’s diagnosis of choroidal tuberculoma was established on the basis of a combination of clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings, as well as the therapeutic response. Although histopathologic or microbiologic confirmation from ocular tissue was not possible because of the posterior pole location of the lesion and the risks of biopsy, a documented history of active pulmonary tuberculosis, a positive IGRA test, characteristic multimodal imaging features, and rapid clinical and anatomical improvement after ATT and corticosteroid therapy all provided strong supporting evidence. We acknowledge the lack of tissue confirmation as a limitation, which is common in ocular TB case reports.

The initial misattribution of vision loss to drug toxicity in this case highlights a significant diagnostic challenge for non-ophthalmologists. Studies have shown that the incidence of ethambutol-induced optic neuropathy (EON) is 1–5% [1]. It manifests mainly as bilateral, progressive, painless optic neuritis, accompanied by visual field defects and early color vision abnormalities. It usually occurs 4–12 months after treatment initiation [2]. In contrast, our patient presented with unilateral symptoms after only one month. The funduscopic and OCT findings revealed a distinct structural lesion that was not characteristic of EON. The lack of visual recovery after ethambutol withdrawal, followed by rapid improvement with corticosteroid therapy, confirms that the primary pathology was an inflammatory choroidal disease, not optic nerve toxicity. While formal color vision testing (Ishihara/Farnsworth) was not performed at presentation-a limitation we acknowledge-the normal VEP and visual fields provide objective evidence that the optic nerve pathway was not primarily involved. This case underscores that while drug toxicity is a common concern, it should not be the default diagnosis without first ruling out infectious or inflammatory causes, especially in the presence of focal fundus lesions.

A notable limitation of this report is the lack of a baseline ophthalmic examination before the initiation of ATT. Thus, it is impossible to determine whether choroidal tuberculoma represents a preexisting silent lesion that becomes symptomatic due to the host’s evolving immune response, or whether it strictly adheres to the formal definition of a “paradoxical reaction” (Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction), which is defined as the exacerbation of a known lesion or the appearance of a new lesion following initial clinical improvement [3, 4]. Given this diagnostic ambiguity, we emphasize that the clinically relevant message is not the precise pathophysiological mechanism but rather the need to recognize that sight-threatening choroidal inflammation can manifest during ATT and must be differentiated from drug toxicity.

The management of choroidal tuberculoma involves a delicate balance between eradicating the infection and controlling inflammation. While ATT is the cornerstone of treatment [5], the role of adjunctive corticosteroids remains a subject of debate. In our patient, systemic TB symptoms were controlled; however, the presence of extensive exudative retinal detachment involving the macula posed an immediate threat to vision, warranting aggressive anti-inflammatory intervention. Following consultation with infectious disease specialists, the benefits of controlling intraocular inflammation outweigh the risks. Therefore, systemic corticosteroids are initiated, leading to rapid anatomical and functional recovery. An initial dose of 30 mg oral prednisone daily (approximately 0.5 mg/kg/day) was chosen, which is consistent with standard regimens for noninfectious posterior uveitis [6]. Systemic therapy was preferred over local corticosteroids because the pathology involved the choroid and extensive retinal detachment, necessitating therapeutic drug levels throughout the posterior segment. Oral dosing enables controlled titration under systemic monitoring. The subsequent tapering was guided by clinical and OCT improvements, with the aim of preventing inflammatory rebound. This finding aligns with the recommendations of the Collaborative Ocular TB Study (COTS), which strongly support the use of oral corticosteroids as adjunctive therapy for choroidal tuberculomas that are not associated with systemic tuberculosis [7].

The unique feature of this case is that the patient developed isolated ocular lesions during ATT, without any accompanying exacerbation of systemic reactions. Although there have been similar reports in the past, such occurrences are relatively rare [8, 9]. The mechanism underlying the occurrence of this solitary eye sign remains incompletely understood. The key point from this case is the urgent need for heightened awareness among physicians managing TB patients. Vision-related complaints should trigger a referral to an ophthalmologist, and a baseline ophthalmologic evaluation should be strongly considered for all patients commencing ATT. This evaluation should include visual acuity, color vision, visual field, fundus examination, and measurement of the thickness of the retinal nerve fiber layer. This practice would not only aid in detecting silent lesions but also establish a crucial baseline for differentiating between disease progression, paradoxical reactions, and drug toxicity in the future. A multidisciplinary approach involving pulmonologists, infectious disease specialists, and ophthalmologists is indispensable for optimizing visual outcomes in TB patients.

While this case does not describe a novel pathophysiological entity, it contributes to the literature by highlighting several distinctive features. The patient developed significant ocular pathology without any systemic paradoxical worsening. Symptoms emerged only one month after the initiation of ATT, which is earlier than typical drug toxicity and is worth noting. Multimodal imaging provides a detailed visual record of the evolution and resolution of a choroidal tuberculoma, serving as a valuable teaching resource.

Conclusion

This case reinforces a crucial lesson: misdiagnosing drug toxicity can delay appropriate treatment for inflammatory choroidal disease. The primary significance of this case lies in highlighting the practical clinical lesson that a comprehensive ophthalmic evaluation should be included in the management of tuberculosis to prevent misdiagnosis and preserve vision. We aim to promote the establishment of a multidisciplinary platform for tuberculosis prevention and strengthen collaboration among different specialties to optimize patient management.
