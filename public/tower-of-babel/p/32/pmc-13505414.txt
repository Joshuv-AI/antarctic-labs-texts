Sustained nitric oxide-releasing aliphatic lubricants: Infusion in polymers for enhanced slippery and antibacterial efficacy
Authors: Grace H. Nguyen, Mark Garren, Vicente D. Pinon, McKenna Clary, Hitesh Handa, Elizabeth J. Brisbois
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Implanted medical devices frequently trigger infection, thrombosis, and surface fouling, creating a critical need for new surface-based strategies that reduce biological complications without systemic drugs. Typically, patients receive treatment for these complications, with the more invasive treatments inherently carrying a greater risk for infection, thrombosis, and adverse events, especially with blood-contacting medical devices. With higher risks and incidence rates of infections and blood clots, patients experience longer hospitalizations that would otherwise not occur. Therefore, the need for more effective preventive and treatment strategies is increasingly evident in the research literature. Anti-fouling surface coatings, particularly those that prevent biomolecule accumulation and bacterial attachment, have emerged as a promising alternative to systemic antimicrobial and anticoagulant therapies. In this work, the aliphatic, thiolated lubricant tert-dodecylmercaptan was nitrosated (SNTDM), diluted with n-hexadecane (C16), and incorporated into crosslinked polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) in a facile impregnation method to render the substrate nitric oxide (NO)-releasing and slippery. The n-hexadecane/SNTDM-swelled samples exhibit slippery behavior with sliding angles <20◦ while releasing physiological levels of NO for up to 14 d. The samples with n-hexadecane did not elicit cytotoxic responses from human BJ fibroblasts while still significantly reducing the viable number of Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus by 1.97 and 1.70 log, respectively. The 16% SNTDM-C16 sample also demonstrated effective reduction of adsorbed fibrinogen and adhered platelets by ~39.17% and 67.5%, respectively. Together, these results demonstrate that SNTDM/n-hexadecane-infused PDMS provides a simple but effective strategy for fabricating NO-releasing slippery surfaces that significantly reduce bacterial viability, fouling, and thrombogenic responses.

Introduction

Hospital medical treatments, including drug therapy and implanted devices, carry inherent risks, particularly for vascular access devices, which are among the most frequently used implants and are highly susceptible to infection, thrombosis, and fouling due to continuous blood contact. In the United States, over 2 million hospital-acquired infections (HAI) occur annually, with an estimated 50–70% associated with implanted medical devices [1], and treatment is typically limited to systemic antibiotics or device removal and replacement. While antibiotics can suppress bacterial growth, they may promote drug resistance, and surgical replacement can increase tissue damage, inflammation, and hospitalization time. Similarly, medical device-related thrombosis is a critical challenge that can lead to device failure and complications like ischemic stroke and venous thromboembolism [2,3]. The current standard of care for treating thrombosis is systemic anticoagulation with agents such as heparin, warfarin, and argatroban [4,5]. Like treatments for infection, systemic administration of anticoagulants is effective but carries increased risks, such as excessive bleeding [6]. Although these treatments carry inherent risks, healthcare professionals continue to use them as the standard of care.

Over the last decade, research has focused on developing techniques and applications to eliminate the need for and reliance on systemic administration antibiotics and anticoagulants, and rather localize this to the medical device interfaces. One such development is the incorporation of nitric oxide (NO) into medically relevant biomaterials. NO is a small endogenous gasotransmitter radical produced in the endothelium that is inherently antibacterial and antiplatelet, making it a strong agent for improving the biotolerability of medical devices [7–10]. As a small gaseous molecule, NO has a short half-life, making it difficult for bacteria to develop resistance. However, because of the short half-life of NO, endogenous and synthetic NO donors have been developed to be incorporated into polymers to extend the duration of NO release [11–16].

A category of NO donors that has been widely researched and is commonly used in medical-grade polymers is S-nitrosothiols (RSNOs) due to their facile synthesis and incorporation into polymers [9,10,17–21]. Traditional small molecule RSNOs like S-nitroso-N-acetylpenicillamine (SNAP) and S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO) take on solid and/or powder forms. More recently, an alternative NO donor has been developed from the isomeric aliphatic thiolated oil, tert-dodecylmercaptan (TDM), to form S-nitroso-tert-dodecylmercaptan (SNTDM) [22–24]. This donor can be easily incorporated into silicone rubber with a solvent-free impregnation technique, is especially stable due to its tertiary structure, and is a highly effective antibacterial agent due to its heightened NO release longevity [24]. This facile incorporation method is advantageous as it enables impregnation into commercially available silicone-based polymer devices (e.g., polydimethylsiloxane and related elastomers) that have been manufactured or extruded [24].

While all these NO-releasing materials have exceptional antibacterial and antiplatelet properties, NO alone cannot prevent biofouling due to cellular debris from the surface. In response to this challenge, NO-releasing materials have been combined with anti-fouling strategies, such as liquid-infused (LI) slippery surfaces, to address the clinical problem [18,25–27]. Inspired by the Nepenthes pitcher plant, LI slippery surfaces employ a lubricant oil infused into a polymer matrix to act as a finite lubricant reservoir and physical barrier that prevents fouling agents from adhering [28,29]. When depleted, the surface lubricant is replenished by diffusion from the lubricant reservoir. Typically, LI slippery surfaces are inert and possess only passive anti-fouling capabilities without a bioactive component, like NO or other bioactive therapeutics [30], to chemically modulate, cleave, or disrupt surrounding fouling agents [31]. However, when combined with a bioactive molecule such as NO, LI slippery surfaces can be transformed into a bactericidal and antithrombotic material that prevents biological debris from fouling the surface [18]. Mechanistically, the infused liquid layer minimizes initial protein and cell deposition by reducing solid-surface interactions, while NO actively exerts bactericidal effects and inhibits platelet activation. Together, these complementary passive and active mechanisms suppress biofouling and maintain surface functionality.

In this study, the antibacterial, antithrombotic, and anti-fouling performance of silicone rubber incorporating a NO-releasing oil and a slippery lubricant, n-hexadecane (C16) was evaluated (Fig. 1). S-nitrosothiol-modified TDM (SNTDM) was synthesized by acidified nitrite nitrosation of TDM and confirmed by FTIR spectroscopy. Surface wettability of oil-swelled samples was assessed over 14 days through static water contact angle and sliding angle measurements. NO release was quantified over the same period, followed by biological assessments including biocompatibility with human fibroblasts, antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, and hemocompatibility via platelet adhesion, hemolysis, and fibrinogen adsorption assays. Together, these studies establish a comprehensive framework for assessing the multifunctional performance of the platform for cardiovascular biomaterial applications. This approach builds on recent advances in lubricative anti-adhesion biomaterials by incorporating an active, therapeutic gas rather than relying solely on passive fouling resistance [32,33]. The primary advantage of this work lies in the integration of a liquid NO donor with a lubricant-infused slippery system using a solvent-free impregnation approach. Unlike prior NO-releasing slippery surfaces that rely on solid or encapsulated donors, this system enables simultaneous control of NO flux, lubricant stability, and anti-fouling behavior through simple compositional tuning of an aliphatic oil.

Materials & methods

Materials

Tert-dodecylmercaptan (mix of isomers, 98.5%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%), magnesium sulfate (MgSO4, 99.5%), and Luria-Bertani (LB) broth were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO USA). Crystal Violet (CV) stain, sodium nitrite (≥ 97%), potassium bromide (KBr, ≥ 99%), n-hexadecane (99%), tetrahydrofuran (THF), penicillin- streptomycin (P/S), and acetic acid were obtained from Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA USA). DOW Sylgard 184 was purchased from Ellsworth Adhesives (Germantown, WI USA). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 10 mM) containing sodium chloride (139 mM), potassium chloride (2.68 mM), sodium phosphate monobasic (1.8 mM), sodium phosphate dibasic (8.2 mM), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, 100 μM) was adjusted to a pH of 7.4 via pH meter and used for all material characterizations and nitric oxide analyzer (NOA) measurements. Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), calcium- and magnesium-free phosphate-buffered saline (CMF-PBS, 1×) were obtained from Corning Incorporated (Corning, NY USA). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was purchased from VWR (Radnor, PA USA). The drip-flow bioreactor apparatus was obtained from Biosurface Technologies Corporation (Bozeman, MT USA). Human Fibrinogen Purified, FITC Labeled (>95%) was purchased from Innovative Research (Novi, MI USA) and used in the protein adsorption assay. Bacterial strains Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus, ATCC 6538) and Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC 25922) for the bacterial adhesion assay and human BJ fibroblast cells (ATCC CRL-2522) for cell compatibility were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA USA). Fresh porcine whole blood was obtained from the University of Georgia Swine Unit (Athens, GA USA) and used for the platelet adhesion and hemolysis assays.

Preparation of sample materials

Fabrication of Sylgard 184 PDMS

Sylgard 184 PDMS, herein referred to as PDMS, was fabricated as the substrate for the nitrosated tert-dodecylmercaptan (SNTDM) via solvent-free impregnation. Briefly, the Sylgard Elastomer base was thoroughly mixed with the curing agent in a 10:1 ratio. After combining the reagents, the mixture was degassed for 1 h to remove any bubbles. The PDMS was then cured at 100 ◦C for 1 h in a glass petri dish (100 mm diameter). The thickness of the PDMS films was measured with a micrometer (Table S2) and were stored at room temperature until further impregnation with the oils.

Tert-dodecylmercaptan (TDM) nitrosation and dilution with slippery lubricant

As a thiolated oil, TDM's single thiol group can be S-nitrosated to synthesize a stable NO-releasing oil. Using a modified protocol [24], TDM (30 mL) was combined with 1 M HCl (15 mL) with continuous stirring. Sodium nitrite (10.5 g) was dissolved in DI water (40 mL), then slowly added to the acidified TDM solution. The reagents were stirred vigorously while protected from light. After reaction, the nitrosated TDM molecule, herein referred to as SNTDM, was washed twice with DI water to remove excess HCl and NaNO2. The final product was dried over MgSO4 and stored at − 80 ◦C.

It is hypothesized that the branched structure of the TDM would hinder its diffusion from the bulk of the PDMS to the surface due to steric hindrance, thereby improving lubricant retention and RSNO stability and providing some anti-fouling behavior. Therefore, the SNTDM was diluted with n-hexadecane (referred to as C16) in the following concentrations to compensate for the lack of slippery ability: 1 part SNTDM to 5 parts n-hexadecane (16% SNTDM-C16, where 16% v/v of the swelling lubricant is SNTDM and is balanced with 84% v/v n-hexadecane), 0.5 parts SNTDM to 5.5 parts n-hexadecane (8% SNTDM-C16, where 8% v/v of the swelling lubricant is SNTDM and is balanced with 92% v/v n-hexadecane), and 0.25 parts SNTDM to 5.75 parts n-hexadecane (4% SNTDM-C16, where 4% v/v of the swelling lubricant is SNTDM and is balanced with 96% v/v n-hexadecane) (see Table S1 for full descriptions).

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was conducted using a Spectrum 3 spectrometer (Perkin-Elmer, Waltham, MA). A potassium bromide (KBr) loading method was used to confirm the presence of NO on the SNTDM. A spectral range of 4000 to 650 cm− 1, with a resolution of 4 cm− 1, was used to study the samples, averaged over 64 scans each. The Perkin-Elmer Spectrum 10 software was used to process the spectra.

PDMS-oil impregnation

To determine the time required to fully and sufficiently impregnate the SNTDM, its dilutions, and its controls into PDMS, swelling ratios and rates were calculated. Briefly, the initial weights of each sample were recorded. The samples were immersed in their respective oils, and the weight of each sample was recorded at 2-h intervals over 24 h. The swelling ratios and rates were then calculated according to Eqs. 1 and 2, respectively, where mx is the mass of the sample at a certain time point, minitial is the mass of the sample before immersion in the oils, ΔSR is the change in swelling ratio, and Δt is the change in time.

The swelling ratios and rates reached equilibrium after 4 h; therefore, the PDMS samples were immersed in their respective oils for 4 h. The resulting NO-releasing films, referred to as SNTDM, 16% SNTDM-C16, 8% SNTDM-C16, and 4% SNTDM-C16, as well as their controls, were used in subsequent studies. The final weights for all the sample types were recorded (Table S2).

Static water contact angle

To evaluate sample wettability, the static water contact angle (WCA) was measured using a Contact Angle Goniometer (Ossila Ltd., Sheffield, England). All test groups (PDMS, TDM, 16% TDM-C16, 8% TDM-C16, 4% TDM-C16, SNTDM, 16% SNTDM-C16, 8% SNTDM-C16, and 4% SNTDM-C16; n ≥ 6) were set on the goniometer stage. The camera was focused while a 5 μL droplet of DI water was placed on the sample surface and allowed to stabilize. For each sample, static contact angle measurements were obtained using multiple independent droplets to ensure reproducibility. Subsequently, the contact angle was measured and analyzed using Ossila Contact Angle software.

Sliding angle

Initial sliding angle

The samples' slippery behavior was also characterized to further study surface lubricity. All test groups (PDMS, n-hexadecane, TDM, 16% TDM-C16, 8% TDM-C16, 4% TDM-C16, SNTDM, 16% SNTDM-C16, 8% SNTDM-C16, and 4% SNTDM-C16; n ≥ 6) were measured via digital protractor. Briefly, the sample was placed on a digital protractor, and a 10 μL droplet of methylene blue-dyed DI water was placed on the sample. The tilting stage was adjusted in 1◦ increments until the water droplet began to slide. The angle at which the droplet began sliding was recorded as the sliding angle. If the water droplet was pinned to the surface, the sliding angle was reported as 90◦. For the oil-swelled samples (TDM, 16% TDM-C16, 8% TDM-C16, 4% TDM-C16, SNTDM, 16% SNTDM-C16, 8% SNTDM-C16, and 4% SNTDM-C16), the films were removed from the oil, and the excess was removed with a cellulose wipe. The samples were left to equilibrate for 1 min to allow oil to replenish on the surface, and the sliding angle was then measured as described.

Sliding angle stability

The sliding angle of all oil-swelled samples (TDM, 16% TDM-C16, 8% TDM-C16, 4% TDM-C16, SNTDM, 16% SNTDM-C16, 8% SNTDM-C16, and 4% SNTDM-C16) was assessed over 7 d to determine the longevity of the slippery functionality. The sliding angle was measured as previously described [27]. All samples were incubated in dynamic physiological conditions (10 mM PBS w/ 100 μM EDTA, 37 ◦C, 150 rpm) between time points. After incubation, the samples were removed from the PBS, and the excess PBS was removed with a cellulose wipe. The samples were allowed to replenish the surface oil for 1 min before the sliding angle was again measured.

TDM loading quantification

After lubricant impregnation, oil loading in silicone rubber was evaluated by solvent extraction. Briefly, the oil-swollen samples (SNTDM, 16% SNTDM-C16, 8% SNTDM-C16, and 4% SNTDM-C16, n = 4) were incubated in tetrahydrofuran (THF, 2 mL) for 2 h at room temperature, protected from light. After incubation, the absorbance of the THF extraction medium was measured between 300 and 650 nm using an Agilent Cary 60 UV–vis spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), with the maximum absorbance at 340 nm for SNTDM (molar absorptivity = 52.28 M− 1 cm− 1). The amount of SNTDM loaded into the PDMS samples was then calculated.

TDM leaching

As the oil in the swelled samples is only present in the silicone rubber matrix physically and is not covalently bound to the polymer, the leaching of the oil was tested over 5 d. Oil-swelled samples (TDM, 16% TDM-C16, 8% TDM-C16, 4% TDM-C16, SNTDM, 16% SNTDM-C16, 8% SNTDM-C16, and 4% SNTDM-C16, n = 4) were incubated in physiological conditions (2 mL of 10 mM PBS w/ 100 μM EDTA, 37 ◦C) to prepare the leachates. At the predetermined time points, the absorbance of the leachates was read between 300 and 650 nm using an Agilent Cary 60 UV–vis spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). The incubation PBS was replaced with fresh buffer to prevent saturation between time points.

Nitric oxide (NO) release

To evaluate the NO release profile of the SNTDM samples and their diluted samples, NO release was measured via chemiluminescence detection using the gold-standard Zysense Nitric Oxide Analyzer (NOA) 280i (Frederick, CO). Briefly, the NO-releasing samples were submerged in 10 mM PBS w/ 100 μM EDTA (3 mL) at 37 ◦C in an amber sample chamber to mimic physiological conditions. A nitrogen bubbler and sweep gas were placed in the amber sample chamber solution at a rate of 200 mL min− 1 to facilitate the collection of NO in the liquid phase. The cell pressure of the NOA is kept constant around 9 Torr to maintain the high sensitivity of the instrument. The samples were measured until the release equilibration was complete. The samples were measured for release for 14 d or until the donor supply was exhausted. Each sample was incubated in PBS w/ EDTA at 37 ◦C and protected from light between time points.

In vitro cytotoxicity evaluation

The general biocompatibility of SNTDM slippery materials was first evaluated using human BJ fibroblasts (ATCC CRL-2522) in accordance with ISO 10993–5, with slight modifications [34]. Human fibroblasts were cultured in a T75 (75 cm2) flask in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin- streptomycin at 37 ◦C and 5% CO2 until they reached ~80% confluency. The cells were then seeded into a sterile 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 live cells per well to ensure full coverage. To prepare the leachates, all control and test groups (n = 4) were UV-sterilized for 30 min (15 min on each side of the sample). After sterilization, the samples were incubated in complete DMEM at 37 ◦C and 5% CO2 for 24 h with an extraction ratio of 3 cm2 mL− 1 according to ISO 10993–12 [35]. After 24 h, the samples were removed, and the leachates were exposed to cells for 24 h.

After the cells were incubated with the sample leachates, the leachates were removed and replaced with a diluted thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT) solution (0.5 mg mL− 1). The cells were incubated at 37 ◦C and 5% CO2 for 2.5 h with the MTT solution to allow formazan precipitation. Wells were aspirated, and formazan crystals were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Absorbance was measured at 570 nm and 690 nm with a BioTek Cytation 5 Imaging Reader (Winooski, VT). The relative cell viability was then calculated according to Eq. 3, where ABSsample is the absorbance of cells exposed to sample leachates (i.e. PDMS, TDM, 16% TDM-C16, 8% TDM-C16, SNTDM, 16% SNTDM-C16, 8% SNTDM-C16), ABScontrol is the absor-bance of the untreated cells, and ABSblank is the absorbance of the plate.

In vitro bacterial adhesion assessment

24 h adhesion

To assess the antibacterial properties of the proposed materials, a 24-h adhesion study was conducted. Bacterial cultures of E. coli (ATCC 25922) and S. aureus (ATCC 6538) were inoculated from stocks in LB broth media and grown at 37 ◦C with agitation at 150 rpm until the log phase of growth. After growth, cultures were diluted to 0.1 OD600 (107 CFU mL− 1) in 10 mM PBS for exposure to samples. Before exposure, circular coupons of each substrate modification were irradiated with ultraviolet (UV) light for 15 min on both sides for sterilization. Following UV sterilization, samples were exposed to the respective bacterial cultures and incubated at 37 ◦C with agitation of 150 rpm to mimic a dynamic physiological environment.

After the 24 h exposure, samples were removed from the bacterial solution, gently rinsed with sterile PBS to remove non-adhered bacteria, and homogenized at 25,000 rpm in 1 mL of sterile PBS to remove adhered bacteria. Homogenate solutions were diluted and plated on LB agar plates for CFU quantification. CFU formation was quantified using an EddyJet SphereFlash colony counter (IUL Instruments; Farmingdale, NY). Final results are normalized to the sample surface area (CFU cm− 2) using log-reduction quantification and Eq. 4.

3 d Drip-flow bioreactor model

To further assess the bacteriostatic and antibiofilm properties of the proposed material under dynamic physiological conditions, a 72-h drip-flow bioreactor study was conducted. For this study, a Biosurface Technologies DFR-110–4 drip-flow reactor was used for biofilm formation and for media supplementation of the tested samples. Before exposure, overnight cultures of E. coli (ATCC 25922) and S. aureus (ATCC 6538) were inoculated into LB media and diluted to an OD600 of 0.1 (107 CFU mL− 1). Tested samples (PDMS, 16% TDM-C16, 16% SNTDM-C16) were attached to glass slides for insertion into reactor channels and irradiated with UV light for 15 min on each side to sterilize them before bacterial exposure. The samples were inserted into their respective channels and exposed to a 2 g L− 1 LB solution containing the diluted bacterial culture for 6 h to form a batch-phase biofilm. After completion of the batch phase, the continuous phase of the experiment was conducted for 72 h at 37 ◦C, with LB media supplementation, at a flow rate of 0.8 ± 0.03 mL min− 1.

After completion of the drip-flow bioreactor study, the tested samples were removed from their respective reactor channels and gently rinsed with sterile PBS to remove unadhered bacterial cells. For quantification of biofilm formation, a crystal violet (CV) staining procedure was conducted. Samples were exposed to 0.4% CV for 15 min at room temperature. After exposure, samples were gently washed with sterile 1× PBS to remove excess stain, then placed in 30% acetic acid for 1 h to extract bound stain. After extraction, the absorbance of these solutions was read at 560 nm on a BioTek Cytation 5 plate reader (Winooski, VT).

In vitro hemocompatibility assessment

Platelet adhesion

Porcine whole blood was collected into sodium citrate at a 9:1 volumetric ratio to prevent coagulation. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and platelet-poor plasma (PPP) were then isolated by centrifugation, separating them from the red blood cells and buffy coat.

Platelet adhesion to the SNTDM samples was evaluated using a cellular viability assay (lactate dehydrogenase, LDH). The PRP was diluted with PPP and restituted with calcium chloride (final concentration of 2.5 mM) to achieve a working count of 2 × 108 platelets mL− 1. All control and test groups (n = 6) were incubated in the PRP working solution for 90 min at 37 ◦C with gentle rocking. After incubation, the samples were carefully removed from the PRP, without disturbing the adhered platelets, and gently washed with calcium- and magnesium-free PBS (CMF-PBS) to remove loosely adhered platelets. After rinsing, the viable adherent platelets were lysed in 2% Triton X-100 for 30 min. The lysing solution was plated and combined with the Roche solution and incubated for 30 min at room temperature, protected from light. The absorbance of the plate was read on a BioTek Cytation 5 Imaging Reader (Winooski, VT) at 492 nm and 620 nm. The absorbance was then converted to a platelet concentration normalized to the sample surface area. Final results are reported as a mean ± standard deviation (n = 8, p < 0.05).

Hemolytic activity

The hemolytic activity of the oil-swollen samples was determined using a hemolysis assay according to the direct contact method of ISO 10993–4 [36]. Briefly, porcine whole blood (WB) was collected and citrated to prevent premature coagulation. The hemoglobin (Hgb) concentration was determined, and the whole blood was diluted with CMF- PBS to a final Hgb working concentration of 10 mg mL− 1. All control and test groups (n = 6) were incubated in diluted whole blood (DWB) at an extraction ratio of 3 cm2 mL− 1, according to ISO 10993–12 [35] at 37 ◦C for 3 h, with manual inversions every 30 min. A positive control (1 mL DWB in 7 mL DI water), a negative control (high density polyethylene (HDPE) in DWB), and a blank (1 mL DWB in 7 mL CMF-PBS) were also prepared and incubated for the same duration as the samples.

After incubation, the samples were removed from the DWB, and the exposed DWB was centrifuged to separate free Hgb from lysed red blood cells (RBC) and other whole blood components. The supernatant from the centrifuged samples was plated in triplicate and reacted with Drabkin's reagent for 15 min at room temperature. At the same time, a standard curve was prepared through a 1:1 serial dilution of a porcine hemoglobin solution (2 mg mL− 1). The standard curve solutions were also plated in triplicate and reacted with Drabkin's reagent. The absorbances of the plate were read with a Biotek Plate Reader (Winooski, VT) at 540 nm. The hemolytic percentage was calculated using Eq. 5, where [Hgb]sample is the Hgb concentration in the supernatant of DWB exposed to samples, [Hgb]total is the Hgb concentration in the supernatant exposed to lysing agents (i.e., positive control), and [Hgb]blank is the Hgb concentration in the supernatant without lysing agents (i.e., blank). The hemolytic index was then determined according to Eq. 6. Final results are reported as a mean ± standard deviation (n = 6, p < 0.05).

Protein adsorption

Assessment of protein adsorption was conducted through sample exposure to FITC-labeled fibrinogen (FITC-Fg). Briefly, all control and test groups (n = 8) were placed in a black polypropylene 96-well plate and incubated in CMF-PBS at 37 ◦C for 1 h to achieve surface saturation. A background scan was collected of the samples on a Biotek Plate Reader (Winooski, VT) with excitation at 490/20 and emission at 525/20. Concurrently, a working concentration (4 mg mL− 1) of Fg was prepared by diluting 10% FITC-Fg into unlabeled Fg.

A standard curve was prepared by serially diluting (1:1) the working solution of Fg and plated in triplicate. Subsequently, the working solution of Fg (100 μL) was added to each sample well without removing the CMF-PBS, and the mixture was incubated at 37 ◦C for 90 min. After incubation, each well was washed with CMF-PBS several times (8–18 times) to remove non-adsorbed protein. The fluorescence intensity was then measured on the plate reader (Ex: 490/20, Em: 525/20). The amount of Fg adsorbed on the surface was then calculated, and the final results are reported as a mean ± standard deviation (n = 8, p < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

All experiments performed in this study are presented in n ≥ 3, and all statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) to determine statistical significance using a one-way ANOVA test with Tukey's test for comparison between sample groups. Outliers were determined using ROUT method with a Q = 10%. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Results & discussions

TDM Nitrosation and characterization

Tert-dodecylmercaptan (TDM) is a branched tertiary alkyl thiol that has shown strong potential as an alkyl RSNO following nitrosation. Previous studies reported nitrosating TDM with tert-butyl nitrite (TBN), followed by condensation to remove excess TBN and tert-butanol byproduct [24]. However, the high lipophilicity of TBN, TDM, and t-butanol causes them to partition together, making efficient separation and purification difficult. In this work, TDM was instead nitrosated using acidified nitrite, eliminating the need for rotory evaporation and preventing residual TBN from affecting NO release behavior (i.e., residual TBN acts as a direct NO donor) and contributing to cellular toxicity.

Through this method, the SNTDM yield was characterized by Fourier Transform Infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). Using a KBr loading method, characteristic peaks of C–H stretching (2960–2874 cm− 1) were found in both the TDM and SNTDM samples, indicative of the branched hydrocarbon nature of the alkyl lubricant (Fig. 2). Similarly, the characteristic peaks around 1465 cm− 1 and 1380 cm− 1 also indicate the presence of C–H bond bending. However, after nitrosation, a characteristic peak at 1500 cm− 1 (N–O stretching) emerges in the SNTDM product, indicating successful nitrosation (Fig. S1). With this confirmation, these lubricants were then swelled into PDMS.

TDM/SNTDM dilution and impregnation

Previous literature has studied neat SNTDM impregnated into silicone rubber for its antibacterial efficacy [24]; however, no research has been conducted to assess the anti-fouling behavior of the lubricant. While observing the full capacity of the neat oil was necessary, diluting the oil and studying the antibacterial, antithrombotic, and anti-fouling effects is beneficial to know the effective concentrations. Thus, the TDM and SNTDM oils were diluted with n-hexadecane (referred to as C16) by different volumetric proportions: 1 part TDM or SNTDM to 5 parts n-hexadecane, 0.5 parts TDM or SNTDM to 5.5 parts n-hexadecane, and 0.25 parts TDM or SNTDM to 5.75 parts n-hexadecane (Table S1).

To incorporate TDM, SNTDM, and their dilutions into silicone rubber, a facile oil-swelling technique was developed by incubating the PDMS samples in lubricant for a specified period. After measuring mass change over 24 h, the swelling ratios and rates were calculated. It was determined that 4 h was sufficient to swell the PDMS samples with each oil combination, yielding swelling ratios of about 1.8 and 1.4 for TDM/SNTDM and n-hexadecane/TDM or n-hexadecane/SNTDM, respectively (Fig. 3A-B). Although the neat TDM and SNTDM samples possessed higher swelling ratios, this result is expected for the n-hexadecane/oil samples, as previous literature has reported similar values [26,27,37]. It is hypothesized that the n-hexadecane does not have as much affinity for the PDMS due to the different chemical structure being a linear aliphatic chain. The branched TDM and SNTDM structures, on the other hand, are more similar in nature to PDMS, allowing for more interaction and more swelling. After swelling the samples, the neat SNTDM had about 4.65 mg cm− 2 of SNTDM loaded (~42.70 w/w%), while the n-hexadecane/SNTDM samples all had significantly lower amounts of SNTDM loaded (0.68 w/w% to 5 w/w%), between 0.22 and 0.61 mg cm− 2 (Fig. S3A-C, Table S3).

While the impregnation strategy is demonstrated here using PDMS, which readily swells in hydrophobic mediums due to its higher free volume, translation to other clinically relevant polymers (e.g., polyurethane, polyvinyl chloride, polyethylene) is not guaranteed. These materials exhibit lower swelling ratios and different solubility parameters, which may limit lubricant uptake and retention. Future investigation would be necessary to extend the applicability beyond silicone-based substrates.

Surface wettability

Initial sliding angle

Implanted or indwelling medical devices frequently face complications from bacterial colonization or blood component deposition, leading to impaired performance and eventual device failure [38,39]. Therefore, anti-fouling techniques like slippery surfaces are necessary. One key characteristic of a slippery surface is its ability to allow foulants to slide off. Although static water contact angle (WCA) measurements provide some insight into a surface's wettability, they do not capture its slippery behavior dynamically. In contrast to the static measurement of WCA, the sliding angle, which is the angle at which a droplet of liquid can slide off a surface, is better able to characterize the surface's anti-fouling capabilities. Slippery surfaces are typically characterized by a sliding angle of less than or equal to 20◦ [18], indicating low surface interactions [18].

In the present work, all samples exhibited hydrophobic tendencies with WCA > 90◦ (Fig. S2). When measuring the sliding angle of the samples, it was observed that water droplets would be pinned to the PDMS surface, as indicated by a sliding angle of 90◦ (Fig. 4A). In contrast, all TDM- and SNTDM-swelled surfaces demonstrated a significant reduction in sliding angles compared to control PDMS, with initial sliding angles below 20◦ (Fig. 4A). These results confirmed the successful fabrication of a slippery surface and indicated the anti-fouling properties of the oil-swollen samples. The sliding behavior is most likely due to the relatively low surface tension of the TDM (45.61 mN m− 1) and n-hexadecane (27.38 mN m− 1) [40,41]. With low surface tension, interactions between the oils and other phases with higher surface tension are less favorable. In this case, the high interfacial tension between the oil and water phases supports the overall slippery behavior as the oil and water are not miscible, and the water can slide off [31].

Sliding angle stability

Because many clinically relevant blood-contacting devices, such as vascular access devices and extracorporeal circuits, are used for relatively short periods, maintaining anti-fouling performance over the first several days is particularly critical. Therefore, recent developments in slippery surfaces have demonstrated retention of slippery behavior while maintaining the ability to prevent bacterial viability and blood component adhesion [42].

While the initial sliding angle of a surface is a short-term indicator of the material's slippery behavior, it does not fully characterize the longevity and retention of the slippery surface's ability. Therefore, an extended sliding angle study was conducted to assess slippery behavior over 14 d under dynamic physiological conditions (150 rpm, 37 ◦C, incubated in PBS w/ EDTA). Initially, all oil-swelled samples exhibited sliding angles <20◦; however, after incubation under dynamic physiological conditions, the sliding angles of the neat TDM and SNTDM samples increased. By day 5, the average sliding angles of the neat TDM and SNTDM were > 20◦, indicating the loss of slippery functionality (Fig. 4B). On the contrary, all samples that were swelled with an n- hexadecane/TDM or n-hexadecane/SNTDM dilution maintained a sliding angle below 20◦ for the duration of the study (Fig. 4B). It was hypothesized that the branched structure of the TDM prevented the oil from self-replenishing the surface of the substrate, as it could not easily diffuse out of the crosslinked PDMS, thereby decreasing its slippery capability. The thiol groups present in the TDM and after the NO group was cleaved from the SNTDM were also hypothesized to dimerize over time and form disulfide bonds that would increase the bulkiness of the molecule structure and further prevent lubricant diffusion. N-hexadecane, a linear aliphatic oil, has been shown to be a slippery lubricant in previous work [26,43] and was therefore used to dilute the neat TDM and SNTDM. The linearity of the n-hexadecane structure would enable lubricant diffusion from the polymer bulk phase back to the surface, while allowing NO release from the nitrosated TDM. Despite the different amounts of n-hexadecane incorporated into the samples, there was no significant difference between the sliding angles of the n-hexadecane/TDM-swelled or n-hexadecane/SNTDM-swelled samples.

Nitric oxide release profile

In the endothelium, nominal levels of NO are estimated at 0.5–4 × 10− 10 mol min− 1 cm− 2 [44], which is sufficient to keep infection at bay and platelets in the resting state. Endogenous and synthetically produced RSNOs can be catalyzed to cleave the S–N bond and release NO by thermal, photo-, or metal catalysis [8,45,46]. As a small, free radical gaseous molecule, NO can diffuse through various polymer matrices, which is advantageous for incorporation into medical devices [11,27,47–50].

The NO release from SNTDM and all n-hexadecane/SNTDM samples were measured for 14 d or until exhaustion. In the context of this study, sustained performance refers to continuous NO release and slippery behavior over a two-week period under simulated physiological conditions. While SNTDM exhibited a burst release of 41.77 ± 6.98 × 10− 10 mol min− 1 cm− 2 (units herein referred to as flux) at d0 (Fig. 5A), 16% SNTDM-C16, 8% SNTDM-C16, and 4% SNTDM-C16 released NO in either near physiological or within physiological range (6.76 ± 2.85 flux, 3.57 ± 2.15 flux, 3.27 ± 1.11 flux, respectively, Fig. 5B). The amount of SNTDM loaded into each sample (Fig. S3C) is attributed to the flux, as neat SNTDM had the most SNTDM loaded, whereas 4% SNTDM-C16 had the least. By day 5, while SNTDM, 16% SNTDM-C16, and 8% SNTDM-C16 were still releasing NO above or within physiological levels, the NO release from 4% SNTDM-C16 fell below 0.5 flux due to the lower concentration of SNTDM (Fig. 5B). After day 7, the NO donor in 4% SNTDM-C16 was exhausted; however, SNTDM and 16% SNTDM-C16 maintained physiological levels of NO until at least day 14 (Fig. 5B). As 4% SNTDM-C16 exhausted its NO loaded capacity quickly, its use and control (4% TDM-C16) were discontinued in subsequent studies.

Although the NO release from the n-hexadecane/SNTDM samples was significantly lower than that of SNTDM, these samples lacked the burst release at d0 that could potentially cause cytotoxic effects in mammalian cells. Not only that, the diluted samples maintain physiological levels of NO release for up to 14 d, similar to other RSNO-impregnated substrates [7,25,51]. As the NO flux observed in the diluted SNTDM samples is within the physiological endothelial range and aligns with values previously reported to reduce platelet and bacterial adhesion on blood-contacting devices such as catheters and extracorporeal circuits [17,47], it would be sufficient for these applications. Although SNTDM is not a crystalline NO donor like SNAP, its branched nature and tertiary structure lends itself to stable and consistent release of NO within the physiologically relevant range, making it suitable for longer-term applications.

In vitro cell cytocompatibility evaluation

As medical devices are implanted in patients, the device material must not elicit adverse effects, such as tissue cell death. It is especially important to assess mammalian cell cytocompatibility when new components, like bioactive molecules and lubricants, are introduced into a device's polymer matrix. Therefore, sample leachates were exposed to human BJ fibroblasts, and the cells' metabolic activity after exposure was measured using an MTT assay.

After leachate exposure to the BJ fibroblasts, a significant reduction in relative cell viability was demonstrated by cells exposed to TDM and SNTDM compared to control PDMS and all other oil-swelled samples. The cells exposed to these samples exhibited 42.69 ± 13.64% and 47.39 ± 17.61% viability, respectively, while all other cells exposed to the control PDMS, 16% TDM-C16, 8% TDM-C16, 16% SNTDM-C16, and 8% SNTDM-C16 leachates showed >95% cell viability (Fig. 6). Biocompatibility of medical device materials is defined as >70% cell viability according to ISO 10993–4 [36] making the neat TDM and SNTDM samples unsuitable for biomedical applications.

These results highlight a critical trade-off between donor concentration, NO release kinetics, and free thiol content, emphasizing the need for controlled dilution to balance antimicrobial efficacy with mammalian cell compatibility. Since there were negligible amounts of oil leaching from the samples (Fig. S3D), the cytotoxicity of these samples stems from the high concentration of free thiols present, in the case of TDM, or high release of NO within the first 24 h, in the case of SNTDM. While the free thiol content of TDM make it desirable for reactive modifications such as nitrosation seen in this work, it is hypothesized that the free thiols in TDM reacted with complete media components (e.g., disulfide-containing amino acids) to render the media unsuitable for cell growth and proliferation [52]. On the other hand, the high burst release of NO around 42 flux from the nitrosated counterpart SNTDM contributes to the cell death. Low levels of NO release have been shown in previous reports to promote fibroblast migration and cell proliferation [53]; however, these proliferative responses to NO are cell-dependent and not universally observed across all mammalian cell lines. In contrast, although high levels of NO well beyond the physiological range could prevent excessive bacterial adhesion and infection, they have been consistently reported to be especially cytotoxic and cause oxidative and nitrosative stress [54–56]. Dilution of SNTDM with n-hexadecane reduced the initial NO burst release while improving lubricant mobility and surface replenishment. This dual effect mitigated mammalian cell toxicity while preserving antibacterial and antithrombotic efficacy with sustained NO release well within endothelial ranges. Due to these results, control groups TDM-only and SNTDM-only (SNTDM) were discontinued for further biological studies.

It is important to note that these cytotoxicity assessments were performed using open in vitro cell culture conditions, in which gaseous NO can diffuse away from the cell interface more readily than in confined in vivo microenvironments associated with implanted medical devices. As such, these assays may underestimate peak local NO exposure relative to certain implantation scenarios. However, the NO flux observed by the 16% SNTDM-C16 remains within or near the physiological range reported for healthy endothelium and well below levels commonly associated with mammalian cell toxicity. Due to its short biological half-life, NO does not accumulate indefinitely even in diffusion-restricted tissues; rather, a steady-state concentration is established that is primarily dictated by release rate. Therefore, these results do not claim to establish definitive in vivo safety, but rather suggest a low likelihood of acute local cytotoxicity at the tested NO release levels.

In vitro antibacterial efficacy

24 h adhesion

Upon removal from a sterile environment, any medical device is susceptible to contamination, which can lead to device-associated infections. Whether bacteria are introduced through non-sterile techniques during implantation or through contamination of the bloodstream, the devices can become compromised by early infection and, in some cases, biofilm formation. Once infection or biofilm formation takes root, it becomes increasingly difficult to eradicate, relying mainly on bacterial susceptibility to antibiotics and other antimicrobial treatments [57]. Hence, it is important to implement other antibacterial strategies that do not rely on antibiotic administration to prevent the onset of infection.

The NO-releasing samples and their controls were challenged with the gram-positive S. aureus and the gram-negative E. coli, and the samples were exposed to the respective bacterial solutions for 24 h. Nonadherent bacteria were rinsed off, and the adherent bacteria were mechanically detached from the surfaces, counted, and normalized to surface area. 16% SNTDM-C16 and 8% SNTDM-C16 exhibited a 1.70 log and 1.52 log reduction of viable adherent S. aureus, respectively, compared to control PDMS (Fig. 7A), with 16% SNTDM-C16 showing significance in reduction compared to its non-NO-releasing counterpart (16% TDM-C16). Similarly, 16% SNTDM-C16 and 8% SNTDM-C16 reduced viable E. coli adhesion by 1.97 log and 1.51 log, respectively, compared to control PDMS (Fig. 7B).

While a 1.5–2 log reduction does not represent all clinical applications like sterilization, this level of reduction is clinically relevant for delaying early colonization and preventing biofilm establishment during the critical initial implantation period. The combination of NO release from SNTDM with the slippery behavior contributed by n-hexadecane showed comparable antibacterial efficacy to previous NO-releasing and liquid-infused substrates [18,25] with a reduction of viable bacteria by 1 log. This study suggests that SNTDM diluted in n-hexadecane provides a significant and relevant level of antibacterial activity for medical-grade plastics.

72 h drip flow bioreactor model

Indwelling medical devices will experience environments and conditions that are more diverse and dynamic than what a standard in vitro adhesion assay can mimic. Therefore, more rigorous models, such as drip-flow bioreactor models, are employed to mimic the physiological environment better. While the drip-flow bioreactor represents an accelerated fouling model rather than direct replication of any single clinical device, this model can be used to understand dynamic conditions for devices like short-term intravascular catheters or extracorporeal circuits, thus providing a conservative assessment of antibiofilm potential under continuous nutrient flow. In this study, a 72 h drip-flow bioreactor model was used to challenge the NO-releasing, slippery 16% SNTDM-C16 constructs against gram-negative E. coli and gram-positive S. aureus. These bacterial strains were chosen for their clinical relevance and prevalence in healthcare infections, and for their ability to survive in dynamic environments [58,59]. In particular, E. coli poses a greater challenge because of the additional protection provided by the peptidoglycan cell wall and the lipopolysaccharide outer membrane [60]. Gram-negative bacteria are generally less susceptible to the bactericidal effects of NO [61,62]; therefore, it is hypothesized that the combination of NO release and slippery behavior will be more effective against gram-negative biofilm formation.

After the initial 6-h batch phase, during which the substrates sit in a bacterial solution under static conditions, the samples are then exposed to a continuous flow of bacterial solution for the following 72 h. After the 72-h exposure, there was a clear and significant reduction of 78.05% in the amount of E. coli biofilm accumulated on 16% SNTDM-C16 compared to the control PDMS and 16% TDM-C16 (Fig. 7C). While the 16% TDM-C16 also reduced E. coli biofilm production compared with PDMS, the combination of SNTDM and n-hexadecane's slippery behavior is more effective than either strategy alone. Against S. aureus, both 16% TDM-C16 and 16% SNTDM-C16 performed comparably with a significant reduction of 76.42% of biofilm accumulated against the control PDMS (Fig. 7D). Therefore, this promising result demonstrates 16% SNTDM-C16's ability to prevent bacterial infection and proliferation in medically relevant environments.

In vitro hemocompatibility assessment

Protein adsorption

Blood-contacting medical devices, such as vascular grafts and catheters, interact with various blood components, including red blood cells, platelets, and plasma proteins. For example, fibrinogen (Fg) is a key plasma protein in the common pathway of the coagulation cascade [63]. When activated, Fg is converted to fibrin and increases platelet and red blood cell aggregation, which aids in thrombus formation [63]. It has previously been reported that increased fibrinogen adsorption also increases bacterial adhesion and overall infection [64]. While NO has excellent antibacterial properties, the presence of the gaseous molecule tends to increase fibrinogen adsorption [65]. The mechanism for this increase is under-investigated. Despite this, combinations of NO-release and anti-fouling strategies have been shown to decrease Fg adsorption compared to control materials [18,25,26], making these techniques promising.

The control PDMS had 7.48 ± 2.99 μg cm− 2 Fg adsorbed onto the surface (Fig. 8A). In contrast, all oil-swelled samples showed a reduction in Fg adsorbed, with up to a 39.97% decrease compared to the control (Table S3). There was no significant difference between the control 16% TDM-C16 and 8% TDM-C16 and their NO-releasing counterparts, indicating that the presence of NO did not significantly increase the amount of Fg adsorbed onto the surface. The slippery anti-fouling properties of the oil itself counteracted the tendency of the NO-release to promote Fg adsorption, as reported in the literature [18,25–27].

Platelet adhesion and hemolytic activity

Blood-contacting devices face the complexity of blood-material interactions and the challenges posed. Given the risk of platelet activation and aggregation, as well as negative interactions with red blood cells (RBCs), these devices must be hemocompatible and reduce thrombogenic surface interactions. Surface exposure to whole blood presents a more complex system to address, which was achieved in this study through a hemolysis assay. In essence, the samples were exposed to dilute whole blood to observe red blood cell (RBC) lysis. According to ISO standards, a hemolytic index of less than or equal to 2% is considered non-hemolytic. All control samples and NO-releasing samples exhibited hemolytic indices less than 2% (Table 1), indicating that the samples would not interrupt normal RBC function and induce cell lysis. As the oil-swelled and NO-releasing samples did not elicit a cytotoxic response from human BJ fibroblasts, it was expected that the materials would also not lyse RBCs. In the absence of a high burst release of NO, RBCs exposed to 16% SNTDM-C16 and 8% SNTDM-C16 maintained homeostasis, and the materials remained suitable for blood-contacting applications.

As the hemolysis assay mainly assessed the blood toxicity of the samples, a more rigorous assay to evaluate hemocompatibility, such as a platelet adhesion assay, was conducted. Nitric oxide inhibits platelet activation by modulating cGMP levels, which is an essential mechanism for reducing thrombotic events on medical devices; prior NO-releasing materials have demonstrated sufficient NO release to reduce platelet activation and thereby decrease platelet adhesion and aggregation [8,18,66]. The SNTDM materials and their controls were exposed to platelet-rich plasma (PRP) to assess efficacy to prevent platelet adhesion. Control PDMS had around 1.74 × 106 platelets cm− 2 adherent platelets, but almost all other samples exhibited a significant reduction in platelet adhesion, with 16% SNTDM-C16 and 8% SNTDM-C16 showing the lowest numbers of adherent platelets, 5.65 × 105 and 3.66 × 105 platelets cm− 2, respectively (Fig. 8B). Similar to other LI slippery surfaces, the 16% TDM-C16 and 8% TDM-C16 prevented some platelet adhesion compared to the control PDMS, due to the low surface tension of the combination oils [40,41]. The slippery surfaces' ability to allow fouling agents to slide off the surface, in combination with NO's ability to maintain platelet inactivation, reduced the number of platelets adhered compared to slippery surfaces alone. While reduced platelet adhesion and fibrinogen adsorption are indicative of improved hemocompatibility, in vivo thrombosis involves additional systemic factors that were not tested in the present assays and must be further investigated. Nevertheless, the degree of reduction observed in this study also aligns with that reported for other NO-releasing slippery surfaces [18,67,68].

Conclusions

Clinical complications, such as indwelling device-induced infection, thrombosis, and fouling, have continued to be threatening challenges in healthcare settings. These complications have contributed to extended hospital stays, increased risk of device failure, and higher treatment costs, with nothing to offset the burden. However, with the emergence of new strategies and technologies to combat these challenges, patient outcomes are improved with more effective solutions.

In this work, the tertiary thiol tert-dodecylmercaptan (TDM) was nitrosated, combined with n-hexadecane to provide further anti-fouling properties, swelled into silicone rubber, and assessed for its antibacterial, antithrombotic, and anti-fouling capabilities. These multifunctional biomaterial systems aim to integrate surface lubrication with bioactive or therapeutic functions to overall enhance the medical applications. While the SNTDM lost its slippery nature after 3 d, the n-hexadecane/ SNTDM-swelled samples exhibited lubricity for up to 14 d and released NO within physiological ranges for up to 14 d. In addition to losing its slippery nature, SNTDM elicited cytotoxic effects in human BJ fibroblast cells, whereas 16% SNTDM-C16 and 8% SNTDM-C16 had relative cell viabilities >70%, making them suitable for biomedical applications. Against E. coli and S. aureus, 16% SNTDM-C16 reduced viable bacterial adhesion by 1.97 and 1.70 log, respectively, showing that the combination of NO release and slippery behavior was more effective. The 16% SNTDM-C16 sample exhibited a reduction of Fg adsorption and platelet adhesion against control PDMS, while not being hemolytic. One of the main advantages of the diluted SNTDM-C16 samples lies in the enhanced lubricity and tunable NO release. By diluting the SNTDM with n-hexadecane, the burst release of NO observed on day 0 is mitigated and falls within physiological ranges of the endothelium for 14 d while maintaining slippery behavior. The NO release of the diluted samples promotes cell cytocompatibility while preserving antibacterial and antithrombotic levels of NO. The enhanced lubricity allows for better surface lubricant replenishment which prevents the biological fouling agents like bacteria and platelets from adhering. The combined effects of surface slippery behavior and controlled NO release produced a significantly more effective antibacterial, antiplatelet, and anti-fouling interface than untreated PDMS, highlighting its potential to improve the performance and safety of implantable medical devices.
