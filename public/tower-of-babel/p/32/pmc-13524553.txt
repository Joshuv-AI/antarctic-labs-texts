Dynamic Medication Challenges in Hemodialysis: Rethinking Medication Therapy Management Strategies Through Longitudinal Trends
Authors: Shan Lii Ching, Ernieda Hatah, Farida Islahudin, Nurul Ain Mohd Tahir, Hui Yin Tay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveA pharmacist-led Hemodialysis-Medication Therapy Adherence Clinic mitigates drug-related problems (DRPs), yet its poorly understood trajectory keeps strategies reactive, limiting timely interventions. Longitudinal trends of DRP prevalence, characteristics, and associated factors of patients attending a Hemodialysis-Medication Therapy Adherence Clinic were explored.Study DesignRetrospective longitudinal cohort study.Setting & ParticipantsComplete records of adult patients undergoing maintenance HD who attended the Hemodialysis-Medication Therapy Adherence Clinic between January 2021 and August 2023 at a tertiary public hospital in Malaysia were included and examined.ExposureHemodialysis-Medication Therapy Adherence Clinic consisting of medication reconciliation, review and record, medication-related action plan, intervention, documentation, and follow-up.OutcomesLongitudinal trends, characteristics, and predictive factors of DRPs.Analytical ApproachPrevalence was descriptively analyzed. Characteristics were classified using the Pharmaceutical Care Network Europe Association DRP Classification (v9.0), and predictive factors were determined using generalized linear mixed models.ResultsA total of 76 patients’ records involving 569 visits across 11 time points were included, with 546 DRPs identified. Longitudinal analysis showed high prevalence with periodic fluctuations, reaching the highest at visit 4 (n = 74, 13.6%) and lowest at visit 11 (n = 20, 3.7%). The DRP of suboptimal drug-treatment (P1.2) was the highest, followed by unnecessary drug-treatment (P3.2) in all visits, except in visit 7, where both categories were equally divided, whereas P3.2 predominated followed by P1.2 at visit 9. Patient-related factors, particularly nonadherence, were the primary causes across all visits except visit 9, where dose selection predominated. Multivariable analysis reported visit phase and anemia treatment had higher likelihood of clinically driven DRP, whereas longer hemodialysis duration and monitoring and administration-related issues reduced this likelihood.LimitationsThis single-center retrospective study was limited by reduced generalizability and attrition over time.ConclusionsIdentifying temporal patterns and predictors of DRPs can optimize Hemodialysis-Medication Therapy Adherence Clinic delivery. Future prospective multicentered research is warranted to increase generalizability and scalability.Index Words: Drug-related problems (DRPs), hemodialysis (HD), Hemodialysis-Medication Therapy Adherence Clinic, longitudinal trend, medication reconciliation, medication therapy management (MTM)

Patients undergoing hemodialysis (HD) are among the most medically complex and vulnerable populations, facing a consistently high risk of drug-related problems (DRPs) due to complex therapeutic regimens. They experience high pill burden of 5-14 medications daily due to multiple comorbid conditions, frequent care transitions,1 and altered disease pharmacokinetics, which necessitate medication adjustments between dialysis and nondialysis days to balance optimal therapeutic effect versus risk of drug accumulation and toxicity.2,3 Together, these factors make medication management extremely challenging. DRP prevalence as high as 98% has been reported, with nonadherence, dosing issues, adverse events, and suboptimal treatment identified as common DRPs.4,5 DRPs are extremely costly as they not only hinder treatment effectiveness, but compromise patient safety through hospitalization, prolonged hospital stays, and mortality. As almost 59% of DRPs are preventable,4 implementing preventive measures is crucial to ensure safe and effective treatment.

The medication journey of a patient receiving HD is highly dynamic, shaped by disease progression, frequent treatment adjustments, and evolving social circumstances that challenge care continuity. The intensive HD schedule, combined with complex medication regimens and strict dietary and fluid restrictions, imposes significant physical and psychological burden on patients.6 Consequently, >50% of patients are nonadherent,7 with less than half continuing their prescribed regimen a year after initiation. Although fatigue, financial strain, and diminished perceived benefit undermine adherence, disruptive life events such as acute stress or medical emergencies pose the greatest risks, particularly in previously adherent patients with fewer baseline barriers.8 Effective self-care management involving medication adherence, symptom monitoring, and emotional coping is critical for delaying disease progression and achieving therapeutic goals. As patients undergoin HD are vulnerable to disruptive life events, effective self-care management driven by health literacy, self-efficacy, and social support is essential to enhance quality of life and clinical outcomes.6

Pharmacist-led Medication Therapy Management (MTM) incorporates self-care management-supporting elements and has emerged as an effective strategy in enhancing therapeutic outcomes and improving medication safety and adherence in chronic disease management.9, 10, 11 Although structure and terminology vary across countries, the core goal of optimizing therapeutic outcomes through systematic medication management that ensures treatment appropriateness, effectiveness, safety, and improved medication adherence12 remains consistent. In the United States, MTM was consensually defined as “a distinct service or group of services that optimize therapeutic outcomes for individual patients” and incorporates 5 core elements, namely medication therapy review, personal medication record, medication-related action plan, intervention and/or referral, and documentation and follow-up.13 In Malaysia, MTM, or nationally known as Medication Therapy Adherence Clinic (MTAC), shares similar core elements, with the service being solely pharmacist-led.14

Despite the increasing implementation of MTM services, existing research primarily reports cross-sectional DRP burden at single time points. This static approach fails to capture the evolving nature of medication-related issues as patient conditions, treatment goals, and complex regimens shift over time. DRP trajectories, particularly how they appear, resolve, or recur over time, remain poorly understood. Without this insight, opportunities for early intervention may be missed, with MTM strategies remaining reactive rather than proactive. To our knowledge, few studies have examined the longitudinal pattern of DRP prevalence and characteristics in this population. This study therefore aimed to identify the longitudinal trend of DRP prevalence and characteristics among patients receiving HD who were enrolled in the pharmacist-led HDMTAC, in addition to identifying factors that predict DRP characteristics.

Methods

Study Design and Population

The study population were patients enrolled in HDMTAC, the standard of care at a Malaysian tertiary public hospital. During each HDMTAC visit (hereafter; visit, V), pharmacists performed standard activities, such as adherence assessment, medication reconciliation, review, and counselling. When pharmaceutical issues arise, immediate interventions are recommended to physicians or patients, with all processes and outcomes, including acceptance or rejection of recommendations, documented in patients’ individual HDMTAC records. This study utilized a retrospective review of patients’ longitudinal HDMTAC records. Patients with kidney failure receiving chronic HD, aged ≥18 years, who attended HDMTAC from January 2021 to August 2023 were screened for inclusion in the study sample. Records that were ≥20% incomplete were excluded.

Data Collection

Data such as patients’ sociodemographic information, DRP prevalence, and characteristics were extracted and collected from HDMTAC entries for each patient’s visit. C.S.L., a practicing renal pharmacist with 18 years of experience, conducted classification of the DRP characteristics using the Pharmaceutical Care Network Europe Association (PCNE) classification tool (version 9.0). DRP classification was verified by a 3-member pharmacist panel (E.H., F.I., and N.A.T.), each with a minimum of 15 years of experience and expertise in DRPs and medication review services. Each identified DRP was categorized into 1 of the 4 problem categories defined by PCNE; P1: Treatment Effectiveness, P2: Treatment Safety, P3: Other, and Unspecified Problem category (hereafter; ‘Unspecified’). In this study, DRPs not belonging to categories P1, P2, or P3 were categorized as ‘Unspecified.’ This included DRPs from incorrect medication administration from which subsequent monitoring or laboratory results remained within normal limits. Each identified DRP may have either ‘no cause’ or one or multiple causes simultaneously, as defined by PCNE: C1: Drug Selection, C2: Drug Form, C3: Dose Selection, C4: Treatment Duration, C5: Dispensing, C6: Drug use process, C7: Patient-related, C8: Patient Transfer-Related, or C9: Other. DRPs due to intolerance or adverse reaction occurrence preventing continuation of prescribed medication administration are categorized as ‘No Cause.’

DRP causes were further stratified into clinical (C1-C4) and nonclinical categories (C5-C9, ‘No cause’) to distinguish between provider-led decisions impacting therapeutic appropriateness and implementation factors such as logistics or patient behavior. This binary classification is supported by Hatah et al,15 who identified that 18.1% of DRPs in adherence-focused reviews were clinical in nature, necessitating a clear distinction between therapeutic optimization and regimen execution.

Data Analysis

Data was descriptively analyzed and reported as counts (proportions) or median (interquartile range, range) as appropriate. A generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with logit link function was employed to analyze the longitudinal binomial outcome of DRP causes (clinical vs nonclinical) over 11 visits (32 months), with DRP cause utilized as the primary unit of analysis. This addressed the hierarchical nesting of DRP cases (level 1) within visit phase (level 2) and patients (level 3), utilizing a random intercept for Patient ID to account for intra- and inter-subject correlations. The GLMM approach assumes data are missing at random, thereby minimizing attrition bias by including all available data points regardless of visit completion. Fixed effects included demographic factors (age, sex, race) and clinical factors (total medications, visit, DRP-associated medication class, HD duration, number of diseases and regimen change). Visit number serves as primary temporal predictor, representing cumulative exposure to pharmacist interventions rather than chronological time, to account for irregular consultation intervals. This ensures data points aligned with clinical encounters at which standardized medication reconciliation and review facilitated DRP identification and intervention. Variables were retained if they demonstrated univariate significance (P < 0.25) or functioned as confounders (coefficient shift >10%).16 Age, sex, and total medications were included based on common practice in health research. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor and tolerance values, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 for the final model. To ensure statistical power and model stability, the 11 visits were consolidated into 3 distinct phases, namely initial (V1-V4), intermediate (V5-V8), and maintenance (V9-V11). Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion while assumptions were verified through residual analysis. Sensitivity analyses compared full (all), completer (completed 11 visits) and noncompleter (completed <11 visits) cohorts to determine if predictors exerted disproportionate influence on the primary endpoint. Baseline normality was obtained and attrition bias was evaluated using χ2 and Mann–Whitney U tests. Analyses were conducted using Statistical Package for Social Sciences, SPSS (version 29) and Microsoft Excel.

Ethical and Classification Tool Approvals

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for the human study was granted by the Medical Research Ethics Committee under the Ministry of Health Malaysia, reference number NMRR-ID-23-02733-6SH (IIR). Permission to utilize the PCNE (version 9.0) classification tool was obtained from the PCNE DRP Group.

Patient and Public Engagement Summary

This retrospective study involved data collected from a secondary source (HDMTAC patient files), and exemption from informed consent exemption was obtained from the ethics board prior to study commencement, as the study did not involve active recruitment of patients. Dissemination of findings will occur through presentations at scientific meetings and integration into quality improvement activities within the current program, with the aim of enhancing service delivery, improving therapeutic outcomes, and ensuring results are meaningful for both patients and providers.

Results

Patient Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

This study included the records of 76 patients, involving a total of 569 visit records that spanned across 11 HDMTAC visits. Male (n = 50, 65.8%) and Malay patients (n = 38, 50.0%) were the most prevalent groups. The median age was 54.5 years (interquartile range, 32.3; range, 18-80) with the majority of the patients already undergoing HD treatment for at least 2 years. Patients’ characteristics across all visits are presented in Table 1. Patients had a median of 8 comorbid conditions (range, 7-9), with the majority prescribed with minimum of 5 medications daily or a median pill burden of 16 (range, 11-25). More than half received phosphate binders (n = 553, 97.2%), vitamin D receptor agonists (n = 466, 81.9%), erythropoietin-stimulating agents (n = 395, 69.4%), antihypertensives (n = 389, 68.4%), and oral anemia preparations (n = 298, 52.4%) across all visits. Regimen changes occurred in 288 (50.3%) visits, with the highest recorded at V2 (n = 42, 63.6%), followed by V3 (n = 40, 61.6%), and the lowest in V8 (n = 23, 51.1%).

DRP Prevalence, Characteristics, and HDMTAC Intervention Acceptance

A total of 546 DRPs were identified and addressed in 314 (57.5%) visits, with 73 (96.1%) patients experiencing at least 1 DRP (Table 2). The majority of DRPs were associated with treatment for chronic kidney disease–mineral and bone disorder (n = 232, 42.5%), followed by cardiovascular (n = 112, 20.5%) and anemia-related indications (n = 103, 18.9%). Longitudinal analysis showed persistently high DRP prevalence with periodic fluctuation, reaching the highest at V4 (n = 74, 13.6%) and lowest at V11 (n = 20, 3.7%). Most DRPs were categorized under P1: Treatment Effectiveness (n = 340, 62.3%) and P3: Others (n=153, 28.0%), primarily driven by subdomains P1.2: suboptimal drug-treatment (n = 268, 49.2%) and P3.2: unnecessary drug-treatment (n = 136, 25.0%) (Table S1). Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3 present the longitudinal trends in the average distribution of identified DRPs, categorized by both the domain and subdomain of problem and cause categories across visits. Apart from V7 and V9, P1.2 DRPs consistently predominated, followed by P3.2. This pattern shifted to both categories being equally divided in V7, and then inverted in V9 as P3.2 surpassed P1.2. Patient-related factors (C7), particularly C7.1: Nonadherence, was the primary cause across visits except in V9, where it shifted to C3: Dose Selection. Of 717 interventions made, 652 (90.8%) recommendations were accepted, resulting in 424 (72.7%) identified DRPs successfully addressed (O1.1). A total of 187 (26.0%) interventions resulted in regimen changes (Table S2).

GLMM Analysis

Of the 76 patients enrolled, 69 patients with a total of 587 DRP cases were included in the GLMM analysis. Based on the binomial outcome of DRP cause, each DRP case represents 1 DRP cause.

Seven patients were excluded as they did not experience any DRPs during study duration. The final model adjusted for visit phase, sex, age, HD duration, DRP-associated medication class, race, and number of medications, of which HD duration, visit phase, and DRP-associated medication class emerged as significant independent predictors. The multivariable model showed maintenance phase (odds ratio [OR], 2.268; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.194-4.308) and anemia treatment (OR, 3.360; 95% CI, 1.730-6.528) to be more than twice and thrice likely, respectively, to be clinically driven. Conversely, HD duration (OR, 0.956; 95% CI, 0.916-0.998) and monitoring and administration-related issues (OR, 0.095; 95% CI, 0.016-0.562) were protective against the likelihood of DRPs caused by clinical factors. A significant random-intercept variance of 0.294 (P = 0.03) and intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.067 confirmed that 6.7% of the total variance was attributable to patient-level clustering, justifying the multilevel approach. The final model achieved Akaike information criterion 2581.6226, Bayesian information criterion 2585.980, and classification accuracy 69.0%. Pearson and deviance residual plots revealed no systematic patterns, confirming the appropriateness of the logit link function and absence of influential outliers. No collinearity was detected among predictors. Tables 3 and 4 summarize the univariate and multivariable analyses of predictors associated with DRP for all 3 cohorts.

Sensitivity (Subgroup) Analysis

Subgroup analyses were performed to assess overall model robustness by stratifying participants into completer (n = 17) and noncompleter (n = 52) cohorts. In the completer cohort, several predictors that were statistically significant in the overall model were attenuated and no longer significant. In contrast, the noncompleter cohort demonstrated associations consistent with the overall model, which were visit phase, HD duration, and DRP-associated medication categories. The direction of effects remained largely comparable across models, although effect estimates in the completer subgroup were accompanied by wider CIs. The distribution of DRPs across repeated visits among completers showed variability without a clear increasing trend, whereas noncompleters exhibited predictor-dependent patterns. Overall, these findings suggest that the primary model estimates were generally stable, with attenuation observed predominantly in the smaller completer subgroup. Discussion

The prevalence and clinical profile of our study population are consistent with global data.1,17 The cohort exhibited a substantial comorbidity burden, with nearly all patients prescribed at least 5 medications, and a median daily pill burden exceeding 15 doses. Multimorbidity drives therapeutic complexity, which in turn amplifies DRP risk and underpins the elevated prevalence of DRPs observed in this population.18 In terms of characteristics, the majority of identified DRPs were associated with chronic kidney disease–mineral and bone disorder treatment, followed by anemia and cardiovascular disease, and this pattern was similar to that of a systematic review involving US-based studies that examined the impact of clinical pharmacy services in chronic kidney disease and HD populations.2 Our findings reinforced the broader literature by highlighting the persistently high DRP prevalence in the HD population, a well-documented challenge that remains a priority for clinical intervention.

A nonlinear longitudinal trend characterized by notable variability in DRP prevalence and characteristics was observed over time. The majority of DRPs were consistently attributed to drug selection (C1), dose selection (C3), and patient-related factors (C7), with medication nonadherence (C7.1) predominating within the C7 category in our study. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting similar causal patterns in HD populations.17,19 Although C7.1 remained the most frequent cause, the relative dominance of causal categories shifted dynamically across visits, particularly at V4, V7, and V9, suggesting temporal fluctuations in DRP drivers. A slight increase in DRP prevalence at V4 followed periods of medication regimen changes observed at V2 and V3, indicating a potential association between therapy modification and DRP occurrence. This observation aligns with qualitative evidence from an Australian HD center, where adherence was strongly linked to the familiarity of a stable medication-taking routine. Patients experiencing recent regimen changes were more vulnerable to nonadherence, frequently citing forgetfulness as a contributing factor.7 Additional determinants identified included medication knowledge, beliefs, awareness, attitudes, self-management capacity particularly in differing situations, family support, and self-control.7,11 The multifactorial nature of nonadherence underscores the need for strategies grounded in a comprehensive understanding of patients’ perspectives that are shaped by past experiences with illness, personal beliefs, emotions, and concerns. Accordingly, individualized interventions are essential to address patient-specific barriers and support sustained medication adherence.

The distribution of DRP categories demonstrated clear temporal variation across follow-up phases. Nonclinical DRPs predominated the early phase (V1-V4), maintaining higher counts than clinical DRPs. During the intermediate phase (V5-V8), nonclinical DRPs declined while clinical counts fluctuated, including a transient increase at V9. By V11, both categories reached their lowest recorded counts. This phase-specific pattern indicates that nonclinical DRPs were more prominent during earlier visits, whereas clinical DRPs became relatively more noticeable at selected later time points. Although causal interpretations cannot be established from this descriptive analysis, the higher burden of nonclinical DRPs in the early phase may reflect initial identification of adherence, administrative, or utilization-related issues. The subsequent reduction across later phases may correspond with ongoing clinical review, therapy optimization, and stabilization of treatment regimens. Repeated pharmacist-led medication reviews and cumulative effects of structured education, patient empowerment, and medication counselling, which enhanced adherence, may also have contributed to the prevention or early resolution of potential DRPs, thereby reducing their observed frequency over time.10 Studies by Pai et al20 and Manley et al21 support the longitudinal impact of pharmacist-led reviews. Pai et al20 demonstrated that 8-weekly pharmacist visits over a 2-year period achieved a decrease in medicine use (14%) as well as frequency and duration of all-cause hospitalizations. Manley et al21 further detailed DRP prevalence over 10 months, yielding 2 important insights. DRPs in the HD population are not a ‘one-time fix’ but persist due to medical instability and frequent regimen changes, whereas consistent pharmacist interventions led to steady decline in DRP frequency over time. Collectively, these findings underscore the dynamic nature of DRPs in the HD population and highlight the importance of sustained monitoring and adaptive pharmaceutical care throughout longitudinal follow-up.

The multivariable model identified maintenance phase, HD duration, and medication class as key predictors of clinical DRPs. Although maintenance phase and anemia therapy were positively associated with these DRP subtypes, HD duration and monitoring and administration-related issues demonstrated protective effects. The higher risk of clinically driven DRPs in anemia therapy stems from synergy of disease progression and treatment-related challenges, characterized by progressive loss of erythropoietin production as kidney function diminishes, and further exacerbated by iron deficiency, inflammation, occult bleeding, and dialysis-related blood loss. The narrower therapeutic window risks more immediate cardiovascular complications, unlike chronic kidney disease–mineral and bone disorder, which progresses slower with less overt short-term impact.22,23 In contrast, the protective effects of monitoring and administration-related DRPs and HD duration indicated that non–drug-related issues carried significantly lower odds of reaching clinical-grade severity, whereas the loss of residual renal function as HD progresses stabilizes pharmacokinetic fluctuations, resulting in subsequent need for frequent clinical intervention.24 In the completer cohort, the attenuation of associations may reflect reduced variability in both clinical and nonclinical DRPs. Recurrent pharmacist encounters may have facilitated early detection and resolution of medication-related issues, potentially diminishing the influence of predictors over time. Additionally, the smaller sample size of this subgroup may have limited statistical power. In contrast, associations persisted in the noncompleter cohort, possibly reflecting earlier stages of service exposure, at which DRP occurrence remained more strongly influenced by baseline clinical and medication-related factors, including HD duration and DRP-associated medication categories.

The nonlinear, evolving trajectory observed underscores the need for adaptive, data-driven MTM models that shift from the static ‘one-size-fits-all’ approaches to dynamic, patient-specific strategies that respond to clinical changes over time. Our findings provide an added layer of insight to enhance MTM implementation, supporting the transition to interventions that respond to clinical changes over time. This shift is crucial in complex chronic populations like patients undergoing HD, for whom medication reconciliation and review must be strategically timed and tailored to each phase of care and needs. Medication reconciliation is especially critical at MTM initiation or during care transitions, when discrepancies and omission are most likely to occur, whereas medication review should remain ongoing, with clinical necessities dictating comprehensiveness. Integrating different review intensities is key to this efficiency. Ferries et al25 demonstrated that targeted medication reviews, which focus on specific preidentified issues, effectively improved adherence and reduced health care utilization more than the broader, time-intensive comprehensive medication reviews. Although comprehensive medication reviews offer essential in-depth medication assessment and patient education, targeted medication reviews provide a faster, problem-focused approach for ongoing monitoring and intervention. Both strategies have complementary values and are most effective when applied based on patient needs and clinical priorities.25 Despite persistent resource constraints,10, 11, 12 leveraging anemia volatility as a predictive trigger allows pharmacists to shift from comprehensive medication reviews to high-yielding targeted medication reviews during the maintenance phase, optimizing both clinical impact and workforce efficiency. By understanding these trends, medication management becomes more targeted and efficient, aligning intervention intensity with patients’ evolving needs.

The study’s limitations primarily include its single-center setting and predominantly Malay and Chinese cohort, which may limit transferability to populations with different genetic predisposition or traditional beliefs that influence DRP causes, such as adherence. Although the retrospective design precluded socioeconomic and health literacy variables, utilizing a GLMM under missing at random assumptions effectively mitigates attrition bias and hierarchical nesting by maximizing all available data points. Additionally, as Malaysia’s first and largest HDMTAC center, it provides unique longitudinal perspectives over 32 months. Robustness was further ensured through sensitivity analyses, revealing shifting predictors from high-sensitivity medication categories to regimen complexity as patient engagement evolved. Despite these constraints, the 32-month longitudinal design provides a robust internal baseline that cross-sectional studies lack, establishing a critical foundation for future research. Multicenter studies incorporating broader geographic regions and diverse ethnic backgrounds are warranted to further validate these DRP trajectories across diverse clinical settings.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the dynamic nature of DRPs in the HD population, highlighting how identifying temporal patterns and predictive factors across different subtypes enhances clinical practice. Transitioning to a data-informed, effective, and phase-specific MTM model allows pharmacists to provide adaptive, patient-centered interventions rather than static care. Our findings establish a longitudinal baseline, revealing how DRP characteristics shift as patient engagement evolves. Future prospective, multicentered study designs are warranted to validate these DRP trajectories across broader ethnic populations and diverse health care systems. Incorporating potentially influential patient-level factors such as health literacy and socioeconomic status will further clarify DRP patterns and enhance scalability and applicability of these findings across diverse clinical settings.
