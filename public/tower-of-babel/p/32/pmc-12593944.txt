Anticholinergic and sedative medications use among community dwelling older adults: risk for polypharmacy and poor physical function
Authors: Aysu Selcuk, Volkan Atmis, Seher Sen, Emine Gemci, Busra Yurumez, Ahmet Yalcin, Asuman Bozkir, Sevgi Aras, Murat Varli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnticholinergic and/or sedative mediations are one of the most commonly prescribed medication groups in older adults. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of anticholinergic and/or sedative mediations use in community dwelling older adults as well as potentially associated factors with their use.MethodsA cross sectional study was conducted among community dwelling older adults who accepted invitation for geriatric screening in the Public Education Centres (PEC) in Türkiye. The subgroup study population was selected among those who used at least one medication. The prevalence of anticholinergic and/or sedative mediations use, comprehensive geriatric tests, drug–drug interactions and polypharmacy were assessed. Statistical analysis was performed to identify associated factors with anticholinergic and/or sedative mediations use.ResultsA total of 608 older adults voluntarily participated in the study from the 16 PEC. Among these 372 were eligible (61%). The prevalence of anticholinergic and/or sedative medications use was 36% among the community dwelling older adults. Polypharmacy was present in 32% of the them. Drug–drug interactions involved anticholinergic and/or sedative medications was common (42%). Polypharmacy (p < 0.001) and poor physical function (frailty, p = 0.026; sarcopenia, p = 0.003; instrumental activities of daily living, p = 0.001; and activities of daily living, p = 0.045) were found to be associated with the use of anticholinergic and/or sedative medications in this study.ConclusionsWith common anticholinergic and/or sedative mediations use and its associated risk for polypharmacy, poor physical function and drug-drug interactions, their use must be balanced with their potential risks and benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06504-5.Keywords: Community dwelling, Older adults, Hypnotics and sedatives, Cholinergic antagonists

Introduction

Older adults are often prescribed medications with systemic anticholinergic effects, which are associated with a higher risk of adverse health outcomes [1]. They are the largest consumers of medications [2, 3]. As a result, polypharmacy, which is defined as the use of five or more medications and highly associated with adverse drug events and inappropriate medication use in older adults, has become an issue in these population. Due to the many medications and comorbidities involved, appropriate medication selection for older adults is a difficult task for the prescribers [3]. The major challenge is balancing the risks of polypharmacy with the potential benefits of medications in older adults [4].

One of the most commonly prescribed medication groups in older adults are anticholinergic (antimuscarinic) and sedative medications [2]. The prevalence was estimated between 37% and 63% according to systematic review of 46 studies and studies conducted in UK and Ireland [5–8]. These drugs are prescribed for the treatment of various medical conditions, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, dementia, mental illness, nausea, urinary incontinence, pain disorders, and sleep disturbances, which commonly occur later in life [2, 9]. On the other hand, these medications have sedative effects through the central nervous system and associated with the conditions such as impaired muscle strength, depressive symptoms, frailty, decline in cognitive function and respiratory depression, particularly in older adults with comorbidities [2, 10]. Due to these conditions, older adults are at risk for falls and bone fractures, hospitalization and readmission to hospitals [11].

To measure the severity of exposure to anticholinergic and sedative medications, there were scales that have been developed and validated including anticholinergic risk scale, anticholinergic drug scale, anticholinergic cognitive burden scale, chew scale, and drug burden index (DBI) [12]. Among these, the DBI is a unique and validated tool that measures the cumulative burden of the doses of a patient’s anticholinergic and sedative medications and also predict outcomes such as falls, functional impairment and mortality in older adults [4, 9]. It can also help prioritise older adults for medication review and guide the medication review process among them [13].

Although there is a well-established scales for the exposure of anticholinergic and sedative medications, little is known about potential factors that might be associated with anticholinergic and sedative medications for our older adults. There was a research gap about anticholinergic and sedative medications use in community dwelling older adults, especially those who handles medications at homes in Türkiye. These scales are likely to be an applicable tool for older adults residing in long-term care facilities to measure the appropriateness of medication use. To determine the burden of anticholinergic and sedative medications among community dwelling older adults is particularly meaningful since they manage their medications and medical conditions by themselves without professional health care support at home. The objective of this study was to assess the prevalence of anticholinergic and sedative medications used by community dwelling older adults. Secondarily, we aimed to assess factors associated with anticholinergic and sedative medications among community dwelling older adults such as polypharmacy, geriatric assessment scores, a history of falls, hospital admissions and drug‒drug interactions.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross sectional study was conducted at the sixteen Public Education Centres (PEC) in various regions in Ankara province in Türkiye. These centres are non-formal education institutions that provide free courses in vocational, social, economic, sportive and cultural fields for citizens of all ages regardless of their education, income, cultural levels and social status in the country. Citizens can register for the courses and become the member of the centre. In this study, adults, who were 60 years or older, were invited for the community screening project based on their records of PEC. There were 27 PEC in Ankara province with 102,366 registrants and 60% of the PEC were included in this study [14]. The sample size was 368 participants with 95% confidence interval (CI), 5% margin of error and 60% response rate in known population.

The Ethics Committee for Human Research of Ankara University Faculty of Medicine approved the study (Date: 25.06.2018, Decision No: 11–747-18).

Eligibility of the study participants

Community dwelling older adults who were able to visit the centres and keen for medical screening during the study period were included for the screening. They were eligible for screening if they aged 50 years or older, able to visit PEC independently with or without care giver, and able to communicate verbally between 1 January 2019 and 30 April 2019. All the subjects gave informed consent and participant anonymity were preserved during the study by not collecting any personally identifiable information in the data. Subgroup data were extracted from the master data set for those who used at least one medication for this study. The exclusion criteria included the following if the older adults were younger than 50 years, did not use any medication, and did not want to participate in the study.

Data collection

The data were collected during consultation sessions between physicians and older adults. Participant characteristics, including gender, age, marital status, education level, disease and medication history, weight, height, body mass index (BMI), use of dietary and herbal products, smoking status, history of hospital admission, and geriatric syndromes, including urinary incontinence, dry mouth and falls were collected. Participants were asked to bring their medications and medical documents during the screening and/or printed lists of medications. All medications including medication with or without prescription and herbal supplements were recorded. The participants’ BMI was classified as obese when it is 30 kg/m2 or more.

The prevalence of anticholinergic and/or sedative medications use was determined from the number of community dwelling older adults who were taking anticholinergic and/or sedative medications versus the number of eligible older adults who used at least one medication on the day of geriatric screening.

Participants’ geriatric screening

The geriatric screening was done by the physicians investigators who had 2 year geriatrics speciality after 4 year internal medicine specialty. For the comprehensive geriatric assessment, the following tests were used. Katz Index for independence in activities of daily living (Katz ADL) includes 6 questions and higher scores indicates full independence for daily living activities [15]. Lawton Scale for instrumental activities of daily living scale (IADL) includes 8 questions and higher scores means full independence for activities in daily living [16]. FRAIL Scale for the assessment of frailty is a 5-item scale and the higher scores indicates low health status associated with frailty [17]. Sarcopenia Screening Test (SARC-F) for the prediction of sarcopenia is a 5-item scale including the components of strength, assistance with walking, rise from a chair, climb stairs and falls [18]. The higher scores are associated with the sarcopenia and poor outcomes [18]. The Hendrich II Fall Risk Model for the fall risk (Hendrich II) includes 11 questions and 5 or higher scores indicates great risk for fall [19]. The short form of Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA-SF) for nutritional status includes 6 questions to identify malnutrition [20]. The scores lower than 12 and 8 are associated with risk for malnutrition and presence of malnutrition, respectively [20]. Eating Assessment Tool (EAT-10) for self-perceived oropharyngeal dysphagia includes 10 items [21]. If the EAT-10 score is 3 or higher, it demonstrates the presence of dysphagia [21]. Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) for prediction of major depressive disorders is 15 item scale and a score of 5 or more suggests the depression [22]. Pittsburgh Agitation Scale (PAS) for the severity of agitation associated with dementia in older adults includes 4 items and higher scores are associated with the agitation [23]. Standardised Mini Mental Screening Test (Standardised MMSE) for cognitive impairment is a set of 11 questions [24]. The scores lower than 24 are considered to be abnormal and indicates possible cognitive impairment [24]. The Turkish validated versions of these scales were used when applicable.

Medication assessment

Drug–drug interactions (DDIs) were assessed by using the Medscape Drug Interaction Checker® database and categorized accordingly [25]. Categories of “Serious-Use Alternative” and “Monitor Closely” were assumed to be potential clinically severe DDIs [25]. Because these severe DDIs requires interventions such as changing therapy while non-severe interventions requires follow up.

The authors developed a master list of medications that had potential anticholinergic and/or sedative effects by reviewing literature. The authors made a consensus among the lists developed by O’Connell et al. and Duran et al. to determine the final list for anticholinergic and/or sedative medications [26, 27]. All medications were categorized based on the World Health Organization Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system [28].

Polypharmacy was defined as the use of five or more medications in this study [12]. All medications were assessed for prevalence, polypharmacy and DDIs. The comparisons were made among the participants who had at least one anticholinergic and/or sedative medications and those who had no anticholinergic and/or sedative medications in their prescriptions to determine possible associations with their use.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to demonstrate study population characteristics. Continuous variables are reported as the mean (standard deviation (SD)) or median (interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. The normality was checked among the continuous variables. The chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used when appropriate. The logistic regression analysis was conducted when p is < 0.05 from the univariate analysis. All factors with a p < 0.05 were included in the multivariate analysis. Those with a p ≥ 0.05 were subsequently removed one at a time until only statistically significant factors remained. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 21.0. A statistically significant level was determined as p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence of anticholinergic and/or sedative medications use among community dwelling older adults and possible associations related to use of these medications. According to the results of this study, more than one in third of the study population used at least one anticholinergic and/or sedative medications. Similarly, a study conducted among community dwelling ≥ 75-year-old adults in Finland found that 38% of the adults prescribed with anticholinergic and/or sedative medications [29]. It was 27.7–31.3% among community dwelling older men (≥ 70 years) in Australia [30]. In the Netherlands, 32% of the Dutch older adults using at least one medication were dispensed at least one anticholinergic and/or sedative medications [31]. It was 33% among community-dwelling patients aged ≥ 65 years in France [32]. Our study’s findings highlight a consistent global trend and support the international evidence showing that a substantial proportion of older adults are exposed to anticholinergic and/or sedative medications. Although the percentages are quite similar between the studies, list of medications involved for anticholinergic and/or sedative effects were slightly different among the studies.

Nearly half of the community dwelling older adults experienced DDI involving anticholinergic or sedative medications (42%). Along with the frequent DDI incidences, there is room for pharmacist-physician collaborative interventions for deprescribing, which is “the process of withdrawal of an inappropriate medication, supervised by a health care professional with the goal of managing polypharmacy and improving outcomes” [33], in community dwelling older adults. For example, multidisciplinary medication review was the most common collaboration between them. It is conducted by regular meetings and a patient follow-ups to reduce adverse effects [34]. Collaboration in deprescribing interventions on the specific population such as older adults in nursing homes could be more beneficial.

The deprescribing interventions was also in line with the study findings of deprescribing anticholinergic and sedative medications [35]. Among 46 older adults who used at least one anticholinergic or sedative medications in residential aged care setting, 72% of deprescribing recommendations suggested by the pharmacist were implemented by general partitioners with the outcome of improved mood, frailty, reduced falls and adverse effects [35].

Polypharmacy were found to be associated with use of anticholinergic or sedative medications in this study. This was similar to the findings of a previous study conducted in Türkiye. Among the 420 older adults, polypharmacy was found to be associated with the anticholinergic and sedative medications burden as well as other anticholinergic medication use scales, such as the anticholinergic activity scale, anticholinergic burden classification, anticholinergic cognitive burden scale, anticholinergic drug scale, anticholinergic load scale, anticholinergic risk scale and clinician-rated anticholinergic scale [10]. Similarly, another local study including 423 patients found that the anticholinergic and sedative medications burden is associated with both polypharmacy and geriatric syndromes such as falls [36]. Both polypharmacy and the anticholinergic and sedative medications burden are found to be associated with adverse geriatric outcomes, including impaired physical and cognitive function in another study [37]. These factors such as falls, frailty, impaired cognition, increased hospital admissions, and adverse drug reactions are independently associated with poor clinical outcomes in older adults [38, 39]. In this study, there were older adults who did not know their medications (22%). This can highlight the issue of health literacy among older adults. It can include knowing the indication, doses or frequency of medications that patients take. Both physical impairment such as hearing and vision loss and cognitive impairment can affect health literacy in older adults [40]. Moreover, poor health literacy causes poor health [40]. The incidence of not knowing which medication the older adults took can be associated with poor health literacy in this study population. There is a need for further studies to measure health literacy among older adults, its impact on their health as well as the medications especially the anticholinergic and sedative medications on health literacy. As health literacy is independently associated with understanding medication regimen [41], this can be one of the areas that health care professionals must screen for older adults.

The results of the study showed that use of anticholinergic and/or sedative medications could affect the physical/muscle function of the older adults. It also demonstrated that there was a possibility of negatively affecting older adults’ activities since the anticholinergic and/or sedative medications users were more likely to be dependent in their activities of daily living.

Since the population of the study was community dwelling older adults and the study required them to voluntarily visit PEC, the good cognitive and physical function as well as low use of anticholinergic and/or sedative medications was expected. Older adults who were dependent and immobile, were not able to visit the centres and thus participate in the study. This was also in line with the geriatric assessment results which showed good cognitive function (median of Standardised MMSE: 26, PAS: 23 and GDS: 4) and low dependency (median of IADL: 8, Katz ADL: 6, FRAIL: 1 and Hendrich II: 1) among the study. In Türkiye, a study which selected community dwelling older adults from the geriatrics outpatient clinic, demonstrated that receiving at least 1 drug with either possible or definite anticholinergic properties was independently associated to falls [42]. Moreover, high anticholinergic load was found to be negatively associated with psychological status similar to our findings [43].

Another limitation was doses of the medications were not recorded. Due to retrospective study design and lack of medication doses, DBI was not calculated. However, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study reported prevalence of anticholinergic and/or sedative medications use among community dwelling older adults in Türkiye. The large sample size from multicentre settings was the strength of the study. Another notable strength of the study is its use of real-world data, which reflects actual clinical practices and patient experiences outside the controlled environment such as hospital settings. This enhances the external validity of the findings and provides valuable insights into medication use and prescribing patterns in routine healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This study highlighted that a anticholinergic and/or sedative medications use was prevalent among the study population, underscoring a critical area of concern in geriatric pharmacotherapy. These medications are not only associated with adverse effects such as cognitive decline, falls, and functional impairment, but may also contribute to clinically significant drug–drug interactions. Therefore, their use must be balanced with their potential risks and benefits. The findings emphasize the importance of routine medication reviews by healthcare providers, with a strong consideration for deprescribing when appropriate and exploring non-pharmacological or safer pharmacological alternatives to optimize patient safety and health outcomes in older adults.
