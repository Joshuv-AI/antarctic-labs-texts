Viral coexistence and insertional mutations in the ORF8 region of SARS-CoV-2: A possible mechanism of nucleotide insertion
Authors: Miuko Kurose, Akima Yamamoto, Abeer Mohamed Abdelfattah Elsayed, Basirat Mojisola Lawal-Ayinde, Toshihito Nomura, Akifumi Higashiura, Takashi Irie, Masaya Fukushi, Miyuki Kanda, Hidetoshi Tahara, Daichi Morita, Teruo Kuroda, Ko Ko, Kazuaki Takahashi, Junko Tanaka, Takemasa Sakaguchi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Virus from swab sample S66 in hiroshima had T-base insertions in the ORF8 region.•Cloning, and TIDE analysis confirmed mixed viruses with different T-base insertions.•Virus with one t (T1+) was 70–75 %; others included T0, T2+, T3+, T4+, and T5+.•C27911T point mutation led to a sequence of seven or more consecutive t bases.•Study highlights a potential mechanism for insertion mutations in SARS-CoV-2.

Introduction

The severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), initially isolated in Wuhan, China in 2019, is the etiological agent responsible for the infectious disease COVID-19, which is characterized by acute respiratory symptoms (Goldstein et al., 2023). Up to July 2022, approximately 768 million individuals had been infected with SARS-CoV-2 and over 6.9 million fatalities had been reported (WHO, 2023), and the pandemic exerted substantial health, economic, and sociopolitical burdens globally.

One of the factors contributing to the worldwide dissemination of SARS-CoV-2 is its proclivity for mutation, which leads to changes in viral characteristics. SARS-CoV-2 possesses a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA genome that comprises roughly 30,000 nucleotides (Goldstein et al., 2023). Mutations accrue through replication, with an estimated mutation rate of approximately 1.1 × 10^−3 substitutions per site per year, as reported by Duchene et al. (2020). Occasionally, significantly mutated variants emerge. The World Health Organization has classified certain strains—exhibiting attributes such as increased transmissibility, heightened risk of severe disease, and reduced susceptibility to vaccines and therapeutic interventions—as variants of concern (VOC), which include the alpha, beta, gamma, epsilon, delta, and omicron variants. These variants, upon emergence, often superseded the circulating strains in specific regions or globally, thereby instigating waves of epidemics (Carabelli et al., 2023; Goldstein et al., 2023).

In Hiroshima, Japan, there were eight epidemic waves from the inception of the outbreak in 2020 to May 2023 (Prefecture, 2023). A peculiar virus harboring a frameshift mutation in the ORF8 protein gene was discovered in a PCR-positive sample (Sample ID: S66) from the second wave occurring in July–August 2020 (Ko et al., 2021). The frameshift mutation is characterized by the addition of a thymine nucleotide after position 27,906 in the ORF8 coding region, leading to an early stop codon and consequently truncating the ORF8 protein from 121 to 20 amino acids.

On the other hand, we successfully obtained and cultured the virus by inoculating the same S66 sample into cultured cells. RNA was extracted from this virus, and genomic nucleotide sequences were analyzed using Nanopore sequencing. The reported single T insertion was not observed. Analysis of the contig sequences from Nanopore sequencing revealed that the site contained numerous DNA fragments with varying numbers of T insertions, not just a single T insertion (Fig. S1).

In this study, we performed RNA extraction and RT-PCR cloning of DNA fragments from the S66 sample to analyze this phenomenon further. Additionally, we cloned the viruses in the S66 sample by limiting dilution, evaluated the occurrence rates of viruses with different numbers of T insertions, and analyzed the characteristics of viruses with different T insertion counts.

Materials and methods

Cells

VeroE6/TMPRSS2 cells (African green monkey kidney-derived cells expressing human TMPRSS2) (Matsuyama et al., 2020) were provided by the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB) Cell Bank (JCRB1819). The cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; FujiFilm-Wako Pure Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan) containing 5 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biosera, Kansas City, MO, USA), 1 mg/mL G418 (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), penicillin G and streptomycin (Meiji Seika Farma, Tokyo, Japan) at 37 °C in 5 % CO2.

Viruses

Sample S66 is an oropharyngeal swab sample from a 60-year-old male taxi driver in Kumano Town, Hiroshima Prefecture. The patient developed symptoms on July 26, 2020, presented with moderate respiratory symptoms, and was hospitalized. He recovered and was discharged 13 days later. No viral transmission to other individuals from this patient was observed. The nucleotide sequences of the virus derived from sample S66 have been reported to include two types differing in the number of T insertions, as mentioned previously. These details, including their database registration numbers, are summarized in Table S1.

The other viruses used in this study were S11: SARS-CoV-2/JP/Hiroshima-46059T/2020, EPI_ISL_6,289,932, MZ853926; S48: SARS-CoV-2/JP/Hiro94253/2020, EPI_ISL_6,603,670, OL588232, S61: SARS-CoV-2/JP/Hiro96977/2020, EPI_ISL_6,603,673, OL588228; S65: SARS-CoV-2/JP/Hiro97166/2020, EPI_ISL_6,595,984, OL589596; S68: SARS-CoV-2/JP/Hiro97618, EPI_ISL_6,595,987, OL589599; and S89: SARS-CoV-2/JP/Hiro 81,924/2020, EPI_ISL_6,595,995,OL589607. The S11 virus belongs to clade GR and Pango lineage B.1.1. Other viruses belong to clade GR, and Pango lineage B.1.1.284. Additionally, for comparative analysis, the sequence of Wuhan-Hu-1 (GenBank: MN908947) was used.

Virus stocks were prepared by centrifuging the culture supernatant of infected cells at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and the clarified supernatant was filtered through a 0.45-µm filter, divided into small aliquots, and stored at −80 °C until use. The virus titer was determined by the standard 50 % tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) method and expressed as TCID50/ml as described previously (Nazmul et al., 2023). All experiments with infectious SARS-CoV-2 were performed at the biosafety level 3 (BSL3) facility at Hiroshima University.

Cloning of PCR fragments

The PCR-amplified fragment was subcloned in the pMD20-T vector by using the Mighty TA-cloning Reagent Set for PrimeSTAR (TAKARA BIO). Each colony of transformed Escherichia coli was picked up, amplified, and processed for plasmid DNA purification. The nucleotide sequence was analyzed by the Sanger method with the fwd2 or fwd3 primer (Table S2).

Virus cloning

Viruses were cloned using the limiting dilution method. Briefly, the virus was 10-fold serially diluted with DMEM and inoculated into VeroE6/TMPRSS2 cells in a 96-well plate. The cells were incubated for 3–5 days until cytopathic effects (CPE) developed, and the culture medium from the well of maximum dilution out of which CPE appeared was collected and designated as a cloned virus.

Viral RNA sequencing

For viral genome analysis, viral RNA was purified from swab samples or viruses amplified in VeroE6/TMPRSS2 cells by using Maxwell RSC Instrument (Promega, Milwaukee, WI, USA). cDNA preparation and amplification were performed following protocols published by the ARTIC network (https://artic.network/ncov-2019) using the V4 version of the ARTIC primer set from Integrated DNA Technologies to create tiled amplicons across the virus genome. Nucleotide sequence analysis was performed by using MinION Mk1C (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK). Consensus sequences were obtained according to the ARTIC-nCoV-bioinformatics-SOP v1.1.0. Variant calling was performed using the Nextclade website (https://clades.nextstrain.org) (Aksamentov et al., 2021). The nanopore reads were generated and converted to a sorted, indexed BAM file using SAMtools (v1.5) (Danecek et al., 2021). Alignments to ORF8 were made using the bam2aln module in breseq (v0.38.1) (Deatherage and Barrick, 2014) .

For analysis of the ORF8 gene region, DNA fragments were amplified from the genomic RNA by RT-PCR by using PrimeScript One Step RT-PCR Kit Ver.2 (TAKARA BIO, Otsu, Japan) with the forward1 (fwd1) and reverse (rev) primers (Table S2).

The amplified 547-base fragment was processed for Sanger sequencing (Sanger et al., 1977) with the fwd2 or fwd3 primer (Table S2) and the BigDye Terminator v3.1 kit (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The nucleotide sequence was analyzed by ABI PRISM 3130xl Genetic Analyzer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Investigation of sequence mixing using TIDE analysis

The web-based tool TIDE (Tracking of Indels by DEcomposition; https://tide.nki.nl) was used to analyze base insertions or deletions using Sanger method waveform data. A virus clone (No. 5), documented in Supplementary Table S1, served as control waveform data for comparison. This clone was considered to be free of viruses composed of mixed sequences. For the guide sequence—which dictates the locus of analysis—the sequence of the S66 virus was used. Waveform data of the S48, S61, S65, and S68 viruses were analyzed by using a distinct sequence as a guide sequence. These guide sequences, along with the control waveform data, are summarized in Table S3. Default parameters were employed for the analysis, including an alignment window of 100–156, a decomposition window of 181–614, an indel size of 10, and a P-value threshold of 0.001.

Searching for viruses with T insertion sequences similar to those in S66

NCBI BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) was utilized to identify viruses with the same mutation in ORF8 as that of the S66 virus. The database specifications are as follows: Title: Betacoronavirus; Description: Betacoronavirus nucleotide sequence data; Molecule Type: mixed DNA; Update date: 2023–07–02; Number of sequences: 7694,686.

Default presets were used for the analysis: word size of 7; match/mismatch settings of −1/3; and gap costs for existence/extension set at 5/2. Only sequences that exhibited a 100 % match with the search sequence were counted as hits. Additional information, such as the sequence submission date and country of registration for T1+ hits, was extracted from GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/). Sequences with T1+ hits were further analyzed using Nextclade to map their phylogenetic relationships.

Western blotting

Infected cells (ca. 1.0 × 10^6 cells) in a 6-well plate were lysed in 150 µl of 1x SDS sample buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8, 2 % sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 10 % glycerol, 840 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, bromophenol blue]. After heat denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, 10 µl of the sample was fractionated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes and subjected to immunoblotting using Supersignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The antibodies used were SARS-CoV/SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) nucleocapsid antibody (GTX632269) and SARS-CoV/SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) ORF8 antibody (GTX135591) from GeneTex (Irvine, CA, USA) and anti-actin monoclonal antibody MAB1501 (Chemicon, Temecula, CA, USA). Chemiluminescence was detected using the LumiCube equipment (LIPONICS, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantification RT-PCR and growth curves

After washing VeroE6/TMPRSS2 cells with DMEM, the cells were inoculated with a virus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 15 or 0.01. After adsorption at 37 °C for 1 h, the inoculum was aspirated, fresh DMEM was added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator. The culture supernatant was collected at a defined time and stored at −80 °C.

SARS-CoV-2 Direct Detection RT-qPCR kit (TAKARA BIO) and LightCycler480 System II (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) were used for viral genomic RNA quantitative RT-PCR. The copy number of viral RNA was determined using a standard curve based on New Coronavirus Positive Control RNA (Nippon Gene Research Institute, Sendai, Japan).

Results

Cloning of viruses contained in the S66 sample

Two approaches were used to investigate the virus species contained in the S66 swab sample: PCR fragment cloning and virus cloning. First, RNA was extracted from the S66 sample, and the ORF8 coding region was amplified by RT-PCR. The resulting DNA fragments were then ligated into plasmids and introduced into E. coli. The E. coli colonies were cloned and cultured, followed by plasmid DNA extraction. Finally, the sequences were analyzed using the Sanger method.

Sequence analysis of 30 clones revealed several clones with different insertion patterns in the ORF8 gene region. Specifically, clones without base insertions ("T0"), clones with a single T base insertion ("T1+"), clones with two consecutive T base insertions ("T2+"), clones with three consecutive T base insertions ("T3+"), clones with four consecutive T base insertions ("T4+"), and clones with five consecutive T base insertions ("T5+") were identified. Additionally, 63 virus clones were obtained from the S66 sample using the limiting dilution method in VeroE6/TMPRSS2 cells. Sequence analysis of the ORF8 region in each clone revealed four different clone types: T1+, T2+, T3+, and T4+ (analysis data for each clone are listed in Table S4). Despite obtaining many clones, no clones without T insertions (T0) were found. This approach also revealed that the T1+ clone was the most common, accounting for approximately 70 % of all clones (Fig. 1B).

Sequence analysis of the T base insertion sites in the entire S66 sample

Total RNA was extracted from the S66 swab sample, the ORF8 coding region was amplified by reverse transcription (RT)-PCR, and the sequences were determined (Fig. 1C). No cloning was performed in this analysis. Sanger sequencing from the 5′ end revealed a significant mixture of waves at the position corresponding to 27,914 in the wild type (T0) (Fig. 1C). This observation aligns with the hypothesis of a single nucleotide thymidine (T) insertion after position 27,906 in the ORF8 coding region, occurring with approximately 70 % probability.

Furthermore, examination of the sequencing waveform revealed disturbances after the single nucleotide insertion site (Fig. 1C). These disturbed waveforms suggested that the S66 swab sample might be a mixture of viruses with different sequences. Individual sequences of viruses with different numbers of T nucleotide insertions are shown in the figure, assuming a mixture of viruses with varying T nucleotide insertions (Fig. 1C).

In this study, we attempted to estimate the mixing ratio of clones with different numbers of T insertions from the Sanger sequencing waveform data. We used the web tool TIDE (Tracking of Indels by Decomposition). Since we could not obtain a T0 virus as a control for the Sanger sequencing waveform data, we used the T1+ virus clone (No. 5) as a substitute. We then analyzed the sequencing waveform of the S66 swab sample compared to the waveform of virus clone No. 5 (Fig. 1D). Compared to virus clone No. 5, the proportion indicating no deletions or insertions ("0″, i.e., T1+) was 75 %. Other proportions were T0 at 3.2 %, T2+ at 15.2 %, T3+ at 3.5 %, and T4+ at 1.5 %. These results are consistent with the previously mentioned cloning results, revealing that the S66 sample contains approximately 70 % to 75 % T1+ virus clones and a mixture of other virus clones with different T insertions.

Furthermore, we searched for ORF8 subgenomes with mutations in the NGS data but could not detect any. Possible reasons for this include conducting NGS analysis on virus particles rather than infected cells, or the short read lengths of the Nanopore data used in the study.

Analysis of T insertion mutations in other viruses

To determine whether the phenomena observed in S66 were present in other strains, we conducted similar analyses on swab samples S61, S65, and S68 collected during the same period (second wave) as S66. Analysis of the Nanopore raw reads from next generation sequencing (NGS) revealed no T insertions after nucleotide 27,906 in any of the three strains (Fig. S1). RNA was extracted from each swab sample, and after RT-PCR, the nucleotide sequence of the ORF8 coding region was determined using the Sanger method. Almost none of these viruses had any mixed base observed at position 27,914, which showed mixed bases in the S66 swab sample (Fig. 2A). Using the S11 virus, obtained during the first wave in Hiroshima Prefecture and passaged multiple times in VeroE6/TMPRSS2 cells, as a comparison, TIDE analysis of the S61 sequence waveform data showed that 95 % of the virus was T0 (Fig. 2B). For S65 and S68, T0 was calculated to be 93.7 % and 89.9 %, respectively (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the ORF8 coding region of the S61 sample was amplified by RT-PCR, and the resulting DNA fragments were cloned in E. coli. Search for viruses with T insertion mutations in GenBank

Aligning nucleotide sequences around the T insertion site near the start codon of ORF8 is shown for multiple genome sequences, including the viruses analyzed so far (Fig. 3A). In the consensus sequence of S66, nucleotide position 27,911 is T, resulting in a stretch of seven consecutive Ts, including the inserted base, making eight consecutive Ts. In contrast, in Wuhan-Hu-1, S61, S65, and S68 swab samples, in which there are no T insertion mutations, position 27,911 is C, dividing the consecutive Ts into two segments of four and two. Furthermore, in the sequences of four viruses with T insertions obtained from GISAID, position 27,911 is also T, as in the case of S66 (Fig. 3A). We mapped 134 viruses with the T1+ consensus sequence of the S66 virus onto a phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3B). T1+ viruses were found predominantly in the B.1.1 lineage close to the S66 virus, but they were also present in other lineages and distributed throughout the phylogenetic tree. The latest sample collection date was July 2023, indicating the virus's continued emergence until this period. Regarding the countries of sequence registration, the majority were from the UK and the USA, but this mutation was also reported in other countries.

Changes in T base insertion virus clones (T1+) during passage in cultured cells

Next, we investigated the possibility that the stretch of T bases might cause T insertions during virus proliferation. The T1+ virus clone (No. 5) was passaged through VeroE6/TMPRSS2 cells for ten generations. The method of passage was as follows: The virus was inoculated into cells, and upon observing cytopathic effects (CPE), a portion of the virus present in the culture supernatant (approximately 5 × 10^4 TCID50) was inoculated into the next set of cells (approximately 1.1 × 10^6 cells).

RNA was extracted from the viruses of the 1st, third, and 10th generations, and after RT-PCR, the nucleotide sequences were analyzed using the Sanger method. Analysis of the waveform data showed that, as generations progressed, mixed bases were observed at the position corresponding to 27,914 in the wild type (T0) (Fig. 4A). Additionally, using TIDE analysis to evaluate the composition of T-inserted viruses, we found that compared to the 0th generation (S66 virus clone No. 5), a single nucleotide insertion occurred at a rate of 3.5 % in the 3rd generation virus (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, in the 10th generation virus, single nucleotide insertion increased to 11.4 %, and a 2-base insertion appeared at a rate of 2.8 % compared to the 0th generation (Fig. 4C). These observations suggest that the proportion of viruses with more base insertions increases with the number of passages. Expression of ORF8 protein and proliferation in cultured cells in T base insertion virus clones

The ORF8 protein consists of 121 amino acids. In the T1+ virus, a frameshift is expected to result in a deletion mutation of 20 amino acids following the first six amino acids of the wild type. Similarly, in T2+ and T4+ viruses, the first six amino acids are the same as those in the wild type, producing deletion mutants shortened by 7 and 21 amino acids, respectively. We evaluated the expression of ORF8 using T1+ (No.5), T2+ (No.25), T3+ (No.45), and T4+ (No.64) virus clones obtained from the S66 sample. Since we could not obtain virus clones (T0) without T base insertions after nucleotide 27,906 in the ORF8 coding region, we used the S11 virus as a reference for T0. After inoculating each virus into VeroE6/TMPRSS2 cells and culturing, full-length ORF8 bands were observed by Western blot analysis of cell lysates in T0 and T3+ viruses but not in T1+, T2+, and T4+ viruses (Fig. 4B). These observations suggest that ORF8 is expressed as expected in cells infected with each clone.

We also compared the proliferation of virus clones in VeroE6/TMPRSS2 cells using the virus as mentioned for earlier clones. Using the S11 virus as a reference for T0, each virus was inoculated into cells at an MOI of 10. Culture supernatants were collected at specified times, and the virus quantity in the supernatants was quantified using RT-qPCR. In this single-cycle growth assay, viruses with T insertions tended to show a lower rate of proliferation at 12 h post-infection; the differences diminished over time (Fig. 5C).

Next, we conducted a multi-step growth experiment by inoculating each virus into VeroE6/TMPRSS2 cultured cells at an MOI of 0.01. Culture supernatants were collected at 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-infection, and the virus quantity in the supernatants was quantified using RT-qPCR. In this test, we used the S89 virus, closely related to the S66 virus, as the control T0 virus. The results showed no significant differences in the growth rates of T0 to T4+ virus clones (Fig. 5D). These results suggest that T insertion mutations do not significantly affect the proliferation of viruses in cultured cells.

Discussion

An oropharyngeal swab sample obtained from patient S66 in Hiroshima Prefecture was investigated in this study. We found that it was not a single virus but a mixture of viruses with different numbers of T insertions. Viruses with one T base insertion (T1+) accounted for 70–75 % of the viruses, and T0, T2+, T3+, T4+ and T5+ were also confirmed to be present.

The study of the S66 sample was prompted by the fact that two genome sequences obtained from S66 differed: one was T1+ and the other was T0. The results indicated that the sequence from RNA extracted from the S66 sample and analyzed by MiSeq (No. 1 in Table S1) was correct. In contrast, the consensus sequence from RNA extracted from the virus after a single passage in VeroE6/TMPRSS2 cells and analyzed by Nanopore (No. 2 in Table S1) must be corrected. Many of the Nanopore raw reads contained T insertions, but the insertions were likely overlooked when assembling the reads to create the consensus sequence.

This study utilized TIDE analysis, a web tool that calculates the proportion of insertion and deletion mutations from Sanger sequencing waveform data. TIDE analysis is typically used to evaluate the efficiency of genome editing tools such as CRISPR-Cas9 (Brinkman et al., 2014) and it is applied to mixed pathogen infections (Dettwiler et al., 2022). In the S66 sample, there was no significant difference between the cloning results for the viruses included in the sample and the TIDE analysis results of the Sanger sequencing waveform, confirming the utility of Sanger sequencing and TIDE analysis for suspected viral gene populations.

Other samples collected simultaneously in Hiroshima Prefecture (S48, S61, S65) did not contain these viruses, suggesting a specific mechanism in the S66 variant. Alignment of nucleotide sequences around the T insertion site suggested that the C27911T mutation in S66 might be involved in T insertion. In the S66 consensus sequence (T1+), nucleotide position 27,911 is T, resulting in a stretch of eight consecutive Ts, including the inserted base. The number of base insertions gradually increased when the S66-derived virus clone No. 5 (T1+) with this eight-T sequence was repeatedly passaged in cultured cells. It is believed that the consecutive Ts promote further T insertions.

The T insertions might occur due to polymerase stuttering at consecutive T sites. Polymerase stuttering occurs during RNA replication when the polymerase replicates the same base multiple times at T or U sites in the template. This is related to mRNA polyadenylation or mRNA capping (Zheng et al., 1999). Garushyants et al. (Garushyants et al., 2021) investigated insertion mutations in SARS-CoV-2 using the GISAID database and found that 45 % of short insertions (<9 nucleotides) had a specific nucleotide composition of uracil. Of the 230 short insertions they identified, 43 occurred in consecutive U or A stretches. This suggests that short insertions in SARS-CoV-2 may occur due to polymerase stuttering.

The mechanism behind the high frequency (70–75 %) of viruses with a single T base insertion (T1+) is unclear. However, research on paramyxoviruses has shown that base insertions occur during transcription of the P gene due to polymerase stuttering, and the distribution and frequency of these insertions depend on the template sequence and upstream cis-acting sequences (Hausmann et al., 1999). A similar mechanism may be involved in the SARS-CoV-2 ORF8 region.

ORF8 is an accessory protein of SARS-CoV-2 (Goldstein et al., 2023). Experiments with ORF8 knockout viruses showed that infection in mice increased the expression of host immune-related factors early in infection, inhibited viral replication, and reduced lung pathogenicity (Bello-Perez et al., 2023). This suggests that ORF8 enhances pathogenicity and induces an immune response early in infection. It has also been suggested that ORF8 might bind to chromatin and inhibit the regulation of important histone post-translational modifications, disrupting the epigenetic control of host cells (Kee et al., 2022). Additionally, ORF8 might mimic interleukin-17 (Lin et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2022) or cause MHC-I downregulation (Zhang et al., 2021). ORF8 has also been reported to be involved in IFN-beta synthesis inhibition due to increased endoplasmic reticulum stress (Rashid et al., 2021). Thus, ORF8 is thought to affect the immune system against SARS-CoV-2. In 2020, a variant with partial loss of ORF7b and significant loss of ORF8 caused a localized outbreak in Singapore (Young et al., 2020). This variant was reported to be highly infectious but with a lower risk of severe disease. Therefore, ORF8 may act to increase pathogenicity in vivo.

Previous reports have shown that viruses lacking the ORF8 protein have reduced replication in cultured cells (Kee et al., 2022). However, in this study, there was no significant difference in the proliferation of ORF8 deletion mutant viruses with T insertions compared to wild-type viruses in VeroE6/TMPRSS2 cells. This might be because ORF8 is not completely absent in T insertion viruses or might be due to differences in the cell type used.

When the S66 virus was found in Hiroshima, strict measures against COVID-19 such as isolating infected individuals and conducting active epidemiological surveys had been implemented. The S66 virus did not spread beyond the patient. Given that viruses with the T1+ sequence of S66 found in databases were spread throughout the viral phylogenetic tree, the C27911T mutation might have occurred accidentally and repeatedly. However, because its transmission ability is not high, the descendants of viruses disappear without causing widespread infection.

This case demonstrates one mechanism by which SARS-CoV-2 acquires insertion mutations. Viruses with frameshift mutations like the S66 strain, resulting in protein deletions, or viruses with base insertions in other parts of the genome may occasionally arise regardless of the region or strain. While viruses with lethal mutations cannot survive, these viruses may have higher transmissibility or pathogenicity than that of previous strains. Therefore, it is essential to remain vigilant about the potential impact of such viruses on the spread and severity of infections.
