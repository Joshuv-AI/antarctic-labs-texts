Fabrication of chitosan-based nanocomposites reinforced with zeolite and gold nanoparticles for enhanced bactericidal efficiency
Authors: Hossein Mohammadi, Sepideh Etemadifar, Mohammad Salmani Mobarakeh, Salmia Beddu, Mona Ebadi, Muhammad Rahimi Yusop, Mohsen Safaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of novel antibacterial biomaterials is urgently needed to address infection, which is a key global issue that threatens human health. For this purpose, polymer-based nanocomposites augmented with nanoparticles have drawn attention as effective materials for antibacterial goals, offering bactericidal effectiveness and durability. This study presents a novel chitosan-based polymer nanocomposite augmented with zeolite and gold (Au) nanoparticles. The nanocomposite was prepared via a direct synthesis method. A thorough structural and morphological characterization was conducted on the nanocomposite. The findings demonstrated the interactions between the components and, in turn, the successful preparation of the nanocomposite. The bactericidal efficiency of the synthesized samples was assessed through the number of colony-forming units per millimetre (CFU/ml). The results confirmed the bactericidal activity of the synthesized samples against S. mutans. The samples synthesized in experiment 7 (chitosan 6 mg/ml, zeolite 3 mg/ml and Au 4.5 mg/ml) presented the lowest survival rate of S. mutans bacteria (0.24 log10 CFU/ml). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed a contribution percentage of 51.28% for Au nanoparticles, which shows that Au nanoparticles contributed the most to the antibacterial performance of the nanocomposite. This was followed by 24.32% and 16.84% for chitosan and zeolite, respectively. The interaction effects further verified that the combination of third-level Au nanoparticles and chitosan had the most synergistic effects, which led to a 23.29% reduction in the survival rate of S.mutans. Overall, this nanocomposite holds significant potential in dental applications as an antibacterial agent. Nonetheless, toxicology assessments in vitro and in vivo are warranted, ensuring their biocompatibility before their clinical application.Keywords: Chitosan, Antibacterial, Nanocomposite, Streptococcus mutans

Introduction

The increase in antibiotic-resistant bacteria is a worldwide crisis that has negatively impacted treatment results and increased the morbidity of infected people [1, 2]. The formation of biofilms is the fundamental cause of antibiotic resistance, through which bacteria might learn to resist the effects of antibiotic therapy [3]. The main modulator of dental caries formation is S. mutans [4]. This is because dietary sucrose is metabolized into insoluble exopolysaccharides, which increase bacterial accumulation on the tooth surface and encourage the development of acidogenic biofilms [5]. Biofilm infections are highly challenging due to their intrinsic resistance to antimicrobial agents [6]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to expand beyond conventional antibiotic strategies and develop novel antibacterial dental biomaterials to address biofilm infections. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are naturally occurring protein molecules with antimicrobial activity. They are produced as a host defence mechanism against infection, emerged as an alternative promising approach to antibiotics, and to combat multidrug resistance [7]. This is due to their potency and different modes of action leading to a broad-spectrum activity against different microorganisms [8]. The readers are encouraged to consult the review paper published elsewhere on the application of AMPs in the treatment and prevention of dental caries [9]. The other sophisticated strategy to address dental biofilms is the use of nanocomposites, in which two or three different materials are combined to produce a new material [10]. To achieve this goal, a mixture of organic polymer networks with inorganic constituents is of considerable value.

Chitosan is an organic material that has been used in dentistry because of its biocompatibility and antibacterial properties [11, 12]. However, like many polysaccharides, it has inherent disadvantages, such as poor thermal and mechanical stability, hindering its broader biomedical application [13, 14]. In addition, the antibacterial properties of the nanocomponent chitosan are far from optimal [15]. Therefore, its properties still need to be improved. One way to overcome these issues is to combine it with other materials with desired characteristics and develop hybrid composite materials [16].

Zeolite is an inorganic crystalline material in which hydrated sodium aluminosilicate in which oxygen ions are linked together with MO4 tetrahedra (M = Si or Al) [17]. Its capacity to take up and release ions, excellent biocompatibility and long-lasting effects inside the mouth have attracted the attention of researchers for dental applications [18–20]. Additionally, they can be utilized as an antibacterial agent. For instance, calcium-silver zeolite was developed in a previous study and showed a strong inhibitory effect against common cariogenic microorganisms [21]. Also, the incorporation of silver-embedded zeolite into chemically acrylic resin can effectively reduce the adhesion of S.mutans to polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), which in turn prevents the propagation of bacteria for up to 45–60 days [22]. Moreover, the incorporation of zeolite-loaded chlorhexidine (CHX) significantly enhanced the antibacterial performance of commercial glass ionomer cement (GIC) towards S.mutans [23]. To this end, the zeolite-A/chitosan hybrid nanocomposite showed superior antibacterial activity against E. coli and S.mutans compared to pure chitosan, respectively [24, 25].

Gold (Au) nanoparticles stand out among nanomaterials due to their distinctive properties, such as a higher surface area and the ability to adopt different morphologies. They have found applications in dentistry [26, 27]. For instance, their high X-ray adsorption coefficient enhances the imaging of incipient occlusal caries [28]. In addition, Au nanoparticles have the potential to be used in the management of dental diseases [29]. For example, in a study, the antibacterial activity of Au nanoparticles with photodynamic therapy (PDT) was evaluated for potential dental application. It was found that increasing concentrations of Au nanoparticles combined with PDT effectively inhibited the production of S. mutans dental biofilms [30]. Also, they have a high affinity to functional groups such as amino (-NH2), which can enhance the stability of chitosan [31].

The Taguchi method is a well-established approach to experimental design and was named after the Japanese statistician Genichi Taguchi. This method is utilized to minimize performance variability by identifying and optimizing design parameters that impact the final product’s quality [32]. Furthermore, it allows simultaneous analysis of multiple factors with minimal numbers of trials, revealing the contributions of individual factors and predicting the optimum conditions [33]. As the concentrations of the components in the composite are the controlling process parameters for antibacterial activity, the Taguchi method (L9) was employed by manipulating the concentrations of three factors: chitosan, Au, and zeolite. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the antibacterial properties of hybrid chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposites have not been reported in the literature. Thus, the significance of the present study lies in two aspects: (I) synthesis of chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite via direct synthesis method to enhance the stability and antibacterial properties of chitosan and (II) optimisation of synthesis condition by Taguchi design to achieve the highest antibacterial effects on S. mutans.

Materials and methods

Materials

Chloroauric acid (HAuCl4, 99%), sodium citrate (Na3C6H5O7, 99%), chitosan, and zeolite clay are the chemicals utilized in the present study. They were obtained from Merck, and no purification was performed on any of them.

Synthesis of Au nanoparticles

The gold salt (HAuCl4) solution was heated until it reached the boiling point. After that, stirring was started, and drops of trisodium citrate (Na3C6H5O7) were added to the solution. After a short time, a significant change in the colour of the solution occurred, changing from dark purple to bright red. After centrifuging the sample at 12,000 rpm for 20 min and subsequently removing any residual moisture in the oven, various amounts of Au nanoparticles are synthesized. With this particular approach, the dimensions of the resulting particles range from approximately 15 to 30 nm. Notably, in this method, citrate ions play a dual role and simultaneously act as stabilizers and reducers [34, 35].

Synthesis of chitosan-based nanocomposites

Qulitek-4 software and the Taguchi method were utilized to design nine experiments for the synthesis of chitosan-based nanocomposites to identify the optimal conditions for achieving the highest antibacterial activity of these nanocomposites (Table 1). These nine experiments were conducted under Taguchi L9 orthogonal array using different concentrations of chitosan biopolymer solution (2, 4, and 6 mg/ml), Au solution (1.5, 3, and 4.5 mg/ml), as well as zeolite clay (3, 5 and 7 mg/ml). For the synthesis of the nanocomposites, each component solution was stirred via a magnetic stirrer for 1 h. After that, an ultrasonic homogenizer was used to disperse the three solutions for 15 min at room temperature. The drop method was used to simultaneously add a solution of Au and zeolite clay to the chitosan polymer solution. The resulting solution was stirred for one hour and then dispersed for 15 min. Finally, the prepared solution was placed in an oven at 60 °C for 24 h to form deposits of the nanocomposite [36].

Characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Bruker D8, England) was used for phase analysis of the nanocomposite. Diffraction patterns were obtained via an X-ray diffractometer equipped with a CuKα radiation source with a wavelength of λ = 1/542 Å in the angle range of 2θ = 20–80°. The functional groups of the chitosan-based nanocomposites were evaluated via FTIR (PerkinElmer One, USA). The spectrum was obtained at 400–4000 cm–1 under transmittance mode. The optical characteristics of the nanocomposite were investigated via UV‒visible (UV‒vis, Shimadzu) spectroscopy at wavelengths ranging from 200 to 700 nm. The morphology and microstructure were analysed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Philips, Germany) and field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Zeiss, Germany). Moreover, EDX/mapping analysis was carried out to determine the distribution of elements in the nanocomposite. Finally, the thermal behaviour of the chitosan and chitosan-based nanocomposites was studied via simultaneous thermal analysis (Netzsch STA, Germany) covering the thermal range between ambient temperature and 1000 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C/min. To investigate the antibacterial activity of the chitosan-based nanocomposite on S. mutans biofilms, the bacterial strain (11083 IBRC-M) was prepared from the National Centre for Genetic and Biological Resources of Iran. Then, an S. mutans bacterial suspension was made by culturing the bacteria for 24 h in agar medium. The bacterial suspension with a turbidity of 0.5 McFarland was subsequently prepared via careful dilution with distilled water. After being transferred to 96-well plates, the mixture was incubated for 72 h at 37 °C. Following the formation of the biofilm, it was washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove planktonic S. mutans from the biofilm. The biofilms were then treated with chitosan-based nanocomposites at different concentrations for 24 h. The number of living cells in the biofilm was determined by collecting cells from the well wall during 24 h of incubation at 37 °C. After three cycles, those cells that still adhered to the well were suspended in 1 mL of PBS solution. The resulting suspension was subsequently homogenised via a vortex machine. Finally, the colony-forming units (CFUs) were counted via serial dilutions of the bacterial suspensions (10 times). Every experiment was carried out three times [36].

Results and discussion

XRD

XRD analysis was carried out to determine the crystallinity and phase composition of the synthesized chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite [37]. The XRD patterns of the synthesized nanocomposites, as well as those of each component alone, are comparatively presented in Fig. 1. As shown in Fig. 1, pure chitosan exhibited two diffraction peaks at 10.4° and 19.92° corresponding to (020) and (110) Miller indices. This is the characteristic of chitosan with a semi-crystalline nature (hydrated crystal and partial amorphous structure, as corroborated by previous studies [38, 39]. Zeolite (LTA-type) displayed peaks at 21.46°, 22.46°, 26.86°, 27.81°, 29.81°, and 34.86°, indexed to (600), (622) (642), (644), (660), and (840) Miller indices based on the JCPDS card no.39–222. These well-defined peaks attest to the high crystallinity and phase purity of the zeolite structure, in line with prior findings [40]. The diffraction peaks of Au nanoparticles at 38°, 44°, 65°, and 78° align with the (111), (200), (220), and (311) Miller indices of the face-centred cubic (FCC) structure of gold, confirming the successful formation of crystalline Au nanoparticles based on the JCPDS card no. JCPDS 65-2870 [41].

XRD patterns of chitosan, zeolite, Au, and chitosan-based nanocomposites

In the nanocomposite, several alterations were observed. Firstly, the peak intensity of chitosan was reduced, indicating a decrease in the crystallinity of chitosan. This is caused by the interactions between chitosan and the incorporated phases. The peaks at 26.41° and 28.12° in chitosan disappeared. Such subtle alternations could be due to the interaction of chitosan with Au nanoparticles and zeolite, which increased the amorphous phase of the nanocomposite [42, 43]. Secondly, a slight shift in the peaks was detected, which may stem from the highly disordered intercalation between the chitosan and zeolite and the incorporation of Au nanoparticles [42, 44]. Thirdly, the characteristic peaks of zeolite and Au nanoparticles were attenuated and disappeared due to their embedment into the chitosan polymeric matrix. Our observations are consistent with recent literature. For instance, Mostafa et al.. reported peak attenuation and shift upon the incorporation of zeolite into the chitosan matrix [45]. Overall, the XRD result confirms the successful integration of chitosan with zeolite and Au nanoparticles.

FTIR

FTIR analysis was carried out to confirm the presence of functional groups within the nanocomposite structure [37]. Figure 2 presents the FTIR spectra of the individual constituents and the resulting nanocomposite in the range of 500–3500 cm–1. The spectrum of pure chitosan displayed adsorption peaks at 3421 cm–1 and 1592 cm–1 corresponding to O–H stretching and N–H bending vibrations, respectively. This indicates the association of a hydrogen bond between them [46]. The peaks at 1651 cm–1, 1592 cm–1, and 1381 cm–1 are attributable to the peak of stretching and bending vibration of the amide I group (C = O), amide II (N–H), and the stretching vibration of the amide III (C–N). The obtained results were supported by a previous report in the literature [46].

FT-IR spectra of chitosan, zeolite, Au nanoparticles, and chitosan-based nanocomposites

Zeolite unveiled prominent bands at 555 cm⁻¹ and 794 cm⁻¹, corresponding to internal vibrations of zeolite in the tetrahedral framework or symmetrical vibration of the Si–O–Al and Si–O–Si linkages, respectively. The findings are in line with previous reports in the literature [47]. The broad adsorption peaks between 3200 cm1 and 3700 cm–1 are assigned to adsorbed water molecules, which are consistent with the hydrophilic nature of zeolite [48]. Previous reports in the literature confirmed the findings of this study [45].

The citrate-capped Au nanoparticles displayed adsorption bands at 3410 cm–1, 1586 cm–1, 1780 cm–1, 1380 cm–1, revealing the presence of O–H, symmetric and asymmetric C = O, and C–O on the surface of citrate-capped Au nanoparticles, and are consistent with the previous report [49, 50]. The adsorption peak at 607 cm–1 is attributed to the metal-oxygen stretching vibration, indicating the capping of Au nanoparticles by citrate ions via RCOO–→Au coordination [51]. The presence of citrate molecules on the surface of Au nanoparticles has two key functionalities. Firstly, it lowers the surface energy of nanoparticles and stabilises them, preventing their agglomeration. Secondly, it maintains nanoparticles in a well-dispersed state, which is crucial for their interactions with other molecules.

The distinctive peaks from each component were retained in the chitosan/zeolite Au nanocomposite spectrum. However, chitosan’s peak intensity of amide I (C = O) and amide II (N–H) were weakened. This may be caused by the hydrogen-bonding interactions between the hydroxyl and amino groups in chitosan with surface hydroxyl groups in zeolite [52]. Moreover, the peak intensity related to C–N was decreased compared to chitosan due to the electrostatic interaction between the metallic nanoparticles and polymer through the sharing of lone pairs of electrons between nitrogen and the metal cation [42]. These spectral changes suggest the intramolecular interactions, illustrating the successful formation of chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite.

UV Visible

UV‒visible spectroscopy was carried out to assess the optical properties of the chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite and its successful formation. Figure 3 displays the UV–vis spectra of the nanocomposite and its components in the range of 200–700 nm. The chitosan exhibited a distinct adsorption band at 207 nm, which is linked with the n-σ* transition of amino and OH groups, which is consistent with previous findings [53, 54]. The zeolite showed an adsorption band centred at 264 nm due to the π(t1)→e charge transition in the tetrahedral group TO4, where T = Si/Al [55]. The citrate-capped Au nanoparticles displayed an absorption band around 542 nm. According to the literature, the presence of various metallic nanoparticles is depicted by the UV spectra wavelength in the range of 300–800 nm. Generally, Au nanoparticles are detected by the absorption peaks ranging between 500 and 580 nm. This arises from the collective oscillation of electrons in the conduction band in resonance with the specific wavelength [56]. Moreover, the presence of this peak within the spectrum of chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite is considered evidence for the inclusion of Au nanoparticles.

UV‒Visible spectra of a chitosan, zeolite, Au, and chitosan-based nanocomposites and magnified peak of adsorption b gold, c zeolite, and d chitosan, respectively

The spectrum of chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite showed a combination of characteristic peaks of individual components. The nanocomposite displayed an adsorption band at 207 nm, which is similitude to the adsorption band observed in the chitosan. Nonetheless, the nanocomposite gave an additional absorption band at the wavelength in the range of 500–600 nm, which is indicative of Au nanoparticles in the chitosan polymeric matrix. Moreover, the intensity of the adsorption peak for zeolite was slightly reduced. These alterations in the adsorption spectra of the nanocomposite suggest the successful integration of individual components, which are in line with XRD and FTIR analyses.

FESEM

FESEM is a commonly used method for characterizing nanostructures and nanomaterials [37]. In the present study, the FESEM was employed to investigate the surface morphological features of the nanocomposite. Figure 4a and b shows the SEM images of the synthesised chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite, and the corresponding particle size distribution histogram, respectively. As can be seen, the nanocomposite exhibited a particle size variation in the range of 50–90 nm with an average diameter size of 68.40 nm. This demonstrates the nanoscale structure of the chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite. In addition, as can be seen, the micrograph resembles a rough and irregular surface. Such irregularity might be due to the integration of zeolite and Au nanoparticles inside the chitosan polymeric matrix.

Morphological analysis of the chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite a SEM image and b particle size distribution histogram

The EDX spectroscopy was carried out to assess the elemental composition of the synthesized nanocomposite. As shown in Fig. 5a, distinct peaks corresponding to C, Si, Al, Ca, Na, O, and Au were detected. The presence of C, O, and Ca confirmed the chitosan matrix [43, 57], while the Au peaks validated the successful inclusion of Au nanoparticles within chitosan. The presence of Al, Si, and Na provides evidence for the incorporation of zeolite (LTA-type). This finding was corroborated by a previously published study [58]. The integration of these components, as summarized in Table 2, verifies the successful formation of chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite.

TEM

The TEM technique is used to investigate the morphological characteristics of nanomaterials, including particle shape, size and dispersion [37]. A TEM micrograph of the chitosan-based nanocomposite is displayed in Fig. 5b. As can be observed, the TEM micrograph reveals that the Au nanoparticles were well-dispersed within the chitosan matrix. The Au nanoparticles exhibit mostly spherical morphology, which is consistent with a previous report on citrate-capped Au nanoparticles [49]. Their dispersion indicates that the presence of citrate prevented their aggregation. In addition, EDX analysis confirmed the presence of Au nanoparticles, which displayed distinct peaks corresponding to Au nanoparticles. This proves that the spherical morphology seen in TEM images is attributable to Au nanoparticles. The zeolite is characterised by irregular morphology observed in the chitosan polymeric matrix. This micrograph provides evidence on the successful integration and distribution of zeolite and Au nanoparticles within the chitosan matrix.

TG/DTA

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) is a thermal analysis that determines weight loss as a function of temperature. This approach is useful for examining the material’s thermal stability [37]. Differential thermal analysis (DTA) is a calorimetric method that records the temperature and heat flow associated with a material’s thermal transition. This technique allows the determination of weight loss in response to temperature variations [37]. Figure 6 shows the thermogravimetric curves of pure chitosan and the chitosan-based nanocomposites. The first thermal step for chitosan refers to the release of water molecules, which are mostly restricted to the amino and hydroxyl groups of chitosan. The release starts at 25 °C, reaches 162 °C, and shows a 13% weight loss. The second thermal stage is related to the thermal degradation of chitosan. This stage starts at 162 °C and ends at 500 °C, which corresponds to a weight loss of 87.82%. For the chitosan-based nanocomposite, three main regions where weight loss was observed were observed. The initial weight loss (4%) was observed in the range of 40–150 °C, which was related to the evaporation release of adsorbed water. This result was further confirmed by previous literature reports [43]. The second weight loss occurred in the temperature range of 162–500 °C, which corresponds to the dissociation of the chitosan structure [59].

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of pure chitosan and the chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite

The ability of a polymer to resist heat and maintain its properties at a given temperature is called thermal stability [60]. As shown in Fig. 6, the chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite resulted in a slower rate of weight loss than pure chitosan. In addition, a comparison of the weight loss of pure chitosan (87.82%) and that of the chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite (57.79%) revealed that the thermal stability of the nanocomposite improved. This increase in thermal stability could be attributed to the interaction between the components in the nanocomposite. Yu et al.. have shown that the zeolite/chitosan interaction enhances the thermal stability of chitosan [24]. According to Ren et al.., the thermal stability of graphene oxide/chitosan nanocomposites is enhanced by the inclusion of Au nanoparticles [61].

Antibacterial activity

Bacterial infection is a crucial challenge in the context of dental biomaterials because it negatively affects the healing process and compromises clinical outcomes. Therefore, the development of dental biomaterials with antibacterial activity is of paramount importance [62]. Figure 7 illustrates the antibacterial activity of chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite against S.mutans. Among the trials, the nanocomposite synthesised in experiment 7, containing chitosan (6 mg/ml), zeolite (3 mg/ml), and Au nanoparticles (Au 4.5 mg/ml), unveiled the most pronounced bactericidal activity. In contrast, experiment 1 exhibited the least antibacterial effects, with a survival rate of 2.73 log10 CFU/ml. The synergistic interactions between the three constituents are likely the reason for the enhanced antibacterial effects observed in experiment 7. Zeolite exerts antibacterial activity via the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and ion exchange. Through ionic exchange, zeolite can exchange its structural cations with antibacterial metals such as Au, silver (Ag), and copper (Cu), which are subsequently released into the surrounding environment. Furthermore, zeolite can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) through catalytic reactions, which can harm bacterial cells, contributing to improved bactericidal effect [63]. The antibacterial mechanism of zeolite is schematically shown in Fig. 8. Au nanoparticles exhibit antibacterial activity through distinct mechanisms. In the first mechanism, it interferes with t-RNA binding to the ribosome, impairing the biological process in bacterial cells. In the second mechanism, Au nanoparticles alter the bacterial membrane potential and prevent the ATP synthase activity, leading to a reduction in ATP levels. This disruption in metabolism weakens the bacteria cells and eventually contributes to cell death. These mechanisms are independent of ROS and suggest that Au nanoparticles can exert antibacterial effects without inducing oxidative stress [64] (see Fig. 8).

Schematic representation of the antibacterial mechanism of action for zeolite

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to quantify the percentage contribution of each three factors [33], enabling us to evaluate the effect of each component (chitosan, zeolite, and Au nanoparticles) on the survival rate of S.mutans. The ANOVA results revealed that the Au nanoparticles possessed the most substantial impact on reducing bacterial cell survival, which accounts for 51.28% of the total variation. Chitosan and zeolite contributed 24.32% and 16.84%, respectively (Table 3). These findings show that Au nanoparticles play the dominant role in the antibacterial efficiency of the nanocomposite, likely through interference with bacterial metabolism. While zeolite showed the least antibacterial contribution, it likely plays a role in enhancing the stability of the nanocomposite. Chitosan exhibited a moderate antibacterial effect, which is attributed to its cationic nature, facilitating the electrostatic interactions with negatively charged bacterial cell membranes. This interaction compromises the integrity of the bacterial cell membrane, resulting in increased permeability and leakage from the cell, which ultimately causes cell death [65]. It is worth mentioning that these reported percentages represent the percentage contribution and importance of each component to the observed antibacterial effect within the experimental design and are not directly translated into a percentage decrease in bacterial cell viability. Overall, the ANOVA results underscore the importance of optimising the Au nanoparticles to reach the highest antibacterial effect while showing the positive complementary role of chitosan and zeolite in the nanocomposite.

The interaction effects of the three components (chitosan, zeolite, Au nanoparticles) on the survival rate of S. mutans are presented in Fig. 9a. The results demonstrate that increasing the concentrations of the components to their third level led to a decline in the survival rate of S.mutans. This reveals the role of component concentration in improving antibacterial performance. As depicted in Fig. 9b, the strongest interaction effect was observed between the chitosan and Au nanoparticles at their corresponding third levels, which led to a 23.29% reduction in the survival rate of S.mutans. This can be attributed to the combined antibacterial mechanisms of both components. Another distinguished interaction was observed between zeolite at the first level and Au nanoparticles at the third level, which led to a 20.68% reduction in bacterial cell viability. Conversely, the lowest interaction effect was observed between zeolite at the first level and chitosan at the third level, resulting in a 13.25% decrease in bacterial cell viability. This finding may suggest that the antibacterial effect of chitosan alone may be insufficient without the complementary role of zeolite and Au nanoparticles. The interaction effects highlight the importance of combining factor levels to obtain the effective antibacterial effect. The results further underpin the key role of Au nanoparticles in the nanocomposite when combined with the chitosan polymeric matrix.

Overall, the optimal synthesis condition for the chitosan/zeolite/nanocomposite was determined to be at the third-level concentrations of chitosan and Au nanoparticles as the most dominant factors contributing to antibacterial activity, with contribution percentages of 51.28% and 24.32% based on ANOVA analysis. The interaction studies in Fig. 9a and b further confirm this finding, where the combination of the third levels of chitosan and Au nanoparticles led to the greatest reduction in bacterial cell survival. This correlation between the statistical analysis and experimental outcomes confirms the synergistic effect of the components in the nanocomposite on the antibacterial activity and validates the Taguchi optimisation design in the present study. The optimal synthesis condition for the chitosan/zeolite/Au nanoparticles, as shown in Table 4, led to the highest antibacterial effect. Under this synthesis condition, the nanocomposite reduced the bacterial cell viability to 0.33 log₁₀ CFU/ml, indicating a significant antibacterial effect.

Conclusions

In the present study, a chitosan/zeolite/Au nanocomposite was synthesised using a direct approach, and the synthesis conditions were optimised via the Taguchi experimental design. Structural analysis, including X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), confirmed the successful integration of the three components. The thermal analysis revealed improved thermal stability of the nanocomposite compared with pure chitosan, which is attributable to the incorporation of zeolite and Au nanoparticles. Moreover, the nanocomposite exhibited significant antibacterial activity against S.mutans, with the least bacterial survival rate of 0.24 (log10 CFU/ml) under the optimal conditions (experiment 7). These findings indicate that the nanocomposite can be a potential dental biomaterial. Nonetheless, further in vitro and in vivo biocompatibility studies are warranted to assess their performance for oral and clinical dental applications.
