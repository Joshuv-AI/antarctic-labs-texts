Effect of inappropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy on time to hospital discharge in patients with community-acquired acute pyelonephritis: a propensity score-weighted retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sebastián Salinas-Mendoza, Álvaro Javier Burgos Cárdenas, Estivalis Geraldine Acosta Gutierrez, Sergio Velasco Malagon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute pyelonephritis is a common reason for emergency department admission. The increasing prevalence of resistant pathogens has raised concerns about the effectiveness of empirical therapy and has led to increased use of broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy. The primary objective was to compare time to hospital discharge between patients treated with appropriate antimicrobial therapy and those who received inappropriate therapy. A balanced pseudopopulation was created using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), and a Cox proportional hazards regression model was fitted to evaluate time to hospital discharge between groups. Background

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the leading reasons for emergency department visits and admissions [1–4]. Their incidence has been estimated at 0.5–0.7 episodes per person-year, with higher rates among sexually active women [5]. The most frequently isolated pathogens are Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae, both members of the order Enterobacterales [6, 7]. In recent years, increasing antimicrobial resistance among Enterobacterales has placed patients with UTIs at higher risk of complications and mortality due to ineffective empirical therapy [8].

Most UTIs are treated empirically, based on local epidemiology and patient-level risk factors. Urine culture and antimicrobial susceptibility results are usually available within 48–72 h. In up to 40% of cases, the isolated pathogen has a resistance pattern against the selected antibiotic regimen, a situation commonly referred to as inappropriate antimicrobial therapy [9]. This increasingly frequent condition has been evaluated in different clinical settings, with conflicting findings regarding clinical outcomes [10–13].

The rising incidence of resistant microorganisms causing UTIs has led to greater use of broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy, contributing to increased bacterial selection pressure and higher health care costs [7, 14].

Current evidence regarding the impact of inappropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy remains inconsistent [15, 16]. Although a recent Colombian study evaluated inadequate empirical cefazolin therapy in patients with community-acquired acute pyelonephritis, evidence remains limited regarding the overall effect of inappropriate empirical therapy across different treatment regimens [11]. Furthermore, previous studies relied primarily on conventional regression approaches [17].

Therefore, this study aimed to determine whether patients with community-acquired acute pyelonephritis who received inappropriate antimicrobial therapy had different clinical outcomes compared with those who received appropriate therapy.

Materials and methods

Design

We conducted a retrospective analytical cohort study of adults admitted with community-acquired acute pyelonephritis through the emergency department of Hospital Serena del Mar, Cartagena, Colombia, between January 2021 and August 2025. Potentially eligible patients were identified from all positive urine cultures processed during the study period.

The diagnosis of acute pyelonephritis was established by the treating physician based on compatible urinary symptoms, signs of systemic infection, and laboratory findings. During chart review, investigators confirmed the diagnosis of acute pyelonephritis using predefined clinical criteria based on the documented clinical presentation, and patients whose findings were compatible only with lower urinary tract infection were excluded. We also excluded patients with septic shock or hemodynamic instability at presentation, indwelling urinary catheters or nephrostomy tubes, recent urinary tract surgery, concomitant infections requiring antimicrobial therapy, negative or polymicrobial urine cultures, referral to another institution, voluntary discharge, or hospitalization prolonged for reasons unrelated to acute pyelonephritis.

Information source and collection

Positive urine cultures were identified from the microbiology laboratory database using WHONET software [18]. Clinical information was obtained by reviewing electronic medical records (HIS.ISIS system). Data were collected and managed using the REDCap platform [19].

Exposure

The exposure of interest was inappropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy, defined as administration of empirical antibiotics for at least 48 h when the isolated microorganism demonstrated resistant or intermediate in vitro susceptibility to the initial antimicrobial regimen according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) criteria [20].

Covariates

We included sociodemographic characteristics, such as age and type of health insurance; comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, cancer, ischemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, and benign prostatic hyperplasia; history of urinary tract infection; and previous antibiotic exposure. We also considered the presence of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), defined as temperature > 38 °C or < 36 °C, heart rate > 90 beats per minute, respiratory rate > 20 breaths per minute, and leukocyte count > 12,000 cells/mm³ or < 4,000 cells/mm³; presence of bacteremia, defined as a positive blood culture during the same hospital admission; antibiotic change before or after the culture report; type of treatment at discharge; and rehospitalization due to urinary tract infection within 30 days after discharge.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated for the primary outcome of time to hospital discharge using the Schoenfeld method for Cox proportional hazards models [21]. Assuming a hazard ratio of 1.50, a two-sided α of 0.05, 80% statistical power, and an exposed-to-unexposed ratio of 1:4. Because acute pyelonephritis is a low-mortality condition, and after excluding patients who were referred to other institutions or discharged voluntarily, we anticipated no losses to follow-up or competing events.

Therefore, we considered the number of events equivalent to the total sample size. Under these assumptions, we estimated a sample size of 251 patients.

Outcomes

We defined the primary outcome as time to hospital discharge, calculated as the interval from emergency department admission to medical discharge. As a secondary outcome, we assessed rehospitalization due to urinary tract infection (UTI) within 30 days after discharge. We defined rehospitalization as readmission to the emergency department of Hospital Serena del Mar with signs and symptoms compatible with UTI and a final clinical diagnosis of urinary tract infection within 30 days after discharge.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics were described without formal hypothesis testing, and covariate balance between treatment groups was assessed using absolute standardized mean differences (SMDs). Comparisons of clinical outcomes were performed using the independent-samples t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

To control for measured confounding, propensity scores were estimated using multivariable logistic regression including variables selected a priori through a directed acyclic graph (DAG) (Fig. S1): initial empirical antibiotic, age, comorbidities, functional dependence, complicated urinary tract infection, and inflammatory response. Age was modeled using restricted cubic splines. The average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) was estimated using stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences, considering values < 0.10 indicative of adequate balance [22, 23]. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations, resulting in complete datasets for all variables included in the analyses (Figs. S2–S3). After IPTW, all baseline covariates achieved adequate balance, with absolute standardized mean differences < 0.10 (Figs. S4–S5).

The association between inappropriate empirical therapy and time to hospital discharge was evaluated using weighted Cox proportional hazards regression with robust variance estimation in the pseudopopulation created using IPTW. We evaluated the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals.

Thirty-day readmission was analyzed using weighted logistic regression. Sensitivity analyses included complete-case analyses and doubly robust models. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05 [24].

Additional details regarding multiple imputation, propensity score estimation, inverse probability of treatment weighting, covariate balance assessment, and sensitivity analyses are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Results

Study population

Between January 2021 and August 2025, 1,515 patients with positive urine cultures were identified. After applying the predefined exclusion criteria, 321 patients were included in the final analysis of whom 261 (81.3%) received appropriate empirical therapy and 60 (18.7%) inappropriate therapy (Fig. 1).

Microbiological isolates

The most frequently isolated pathogens were Escherichia coli (n = 272; 84.7%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (n = 25; 7.8%), Proteus mirabilis (n = 6; 1.9%). The proportion of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) producing microorganisms was 14.1%.

The observed resistance rates were 58% for ampicillin, 31% for ampicillin–sulbactam/amoxicillin–clavulanate, 43% for trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and 18.70% for cefazolin (Table S1).

Among Enterobacterales isolates with complete susceptibility data, 55.1% remained susceptible to amoxicillin-clavulanate (AAC), cefuroxime, and ciprofloxacin. Combined resistance to all three oral agents was uncommon overall (3.0%) but was substantially more frequent among patients who received inappropriate empirical therapy than among those who received appropriate therapy (35.7% vs. 0.5%) (Table S2).

Antibiotic treatment

Median antibiotic treatment duration was 8 days (IQR 7–11). Cefuroxime was the most frequently prescribed empirical antibiotic (n = 231; 72%), followed by ceftriaxone (n = 23; 7.2%) and ertapenem (n = 21; 6.5%) (Fig. S6). At hospital discharge, the most commonly prescribed oral antibiotics were cefuroxime, cephalexin, and ciprofloxacin, with cefuroxime accounting for 75% of oral prescriptions. Among patients discharged through the home-hospitalization program, the most commonly used antibiotics were ertapenem, cefepime, and ceftriaxone.

At discharge, 87.5% were discharged to complete oral antimicrobial therapy at home, 2.8% completed treatment during hospitalization, and 9.7% completed treatment through a home-hospitalization program. In the appropriate therapy group, 93.1% of patients were discharged with oral therapy at home, completed treatment during hospitalization 1.9%, and 5% continued treatment through home hospitalization. In comparison, these proportions in the inappropriate therapy group were 63.3%, 6.7% and 30%, respectively (p = < 0.001).

Antibiotic therapy was changed before culture results were reported in 1.70% of patients in the inappropriate therapy group and 2.40% of those in the appropriate therapy group. This difference was not statistically significant (p = 1.000). After culture results were reported, antibiotic therapy was changed in 16.1% of patients in the appropriate therapy group compared with 40% of those in the inappropriate therapy group (p = < 0.001).

Crude clinical outcomes are summarized in Table 2. Patients receiving inappropriate empirical therapy more frequently required home parenteral antimicrobial therapy, whereas no significant differences were observed in hospital length of stay or 30-day rehospitalization. No in-hospital deaths were observed among the 321 included patients during the study period.

Adjusted analysis

After inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to estimate the average treatment effect on the treated. The robust Cox model showed that inappropriate therapy was not significantly associated with time to hospital discharge (HR = 0.97; 95% CI 0.71–1.33; P = 0.87). (Fig. 2) Assessment of the proportional hazards assumption using the Schoenfeld test showed no evidence of violation of the proportional hazards assumption (p = 0.068). (Fig. S7)

Cox proportional hazards survival curves for length of hospital stay according to appropriateness of empirical antimicrobial therapy

As a secondary outcome, we evaluated rehospitalization for urinary tract infection within 30 days after hospital discharge. After IPTW adjustment, inappropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy was not significantly associated with the risk of 30-day rehospitalization for urinary tract infection. (OR = 1.42; 95% CI 0.65–3.06; P = 0.37).

Patients receiving inappropriate empirical therapy were significantly more likely to require continuation of intravenous antimicrobial treatment through a home parenteral antimicrobial therapy (HPAT) program than those receiving appropriate therapy (30.0% vs. 5.0%). This association remained significant after adjustment for prespecified confounders (aOR 10.79, 95% CI 4.46–26.06; P < 0.001). However, when healthcare dependence was defined as the time from hospital admission to completion of antimicrobial treatment, including both inpatient care and home parenteral therapy, inappropriate empirical therapy was not associated with a longer time to the end of healthcare dependence in the IPTW analysis (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.59–1.14; P = 0.241) (Table S3).

Sensitivity analyses

Results were consistent across all prespecified sensitivity analyses, with no statistically significant association between inappropriate therapy and time to hospital discharge (Table S4). Inappropriate empirical therapy was not associated with 30-day rehospitalization in either the crude (OR = 1.79; 95% CI 0.83–3.65) or adjusted logistic regression models (aOR = 1.46; 95% CI 0.63–3.12) (Table S5).

Model assumptions were met for the primary weighted Cox model (Fig. S7); diagnostic plots for the remaining models are provided in Supplementary Figs. S8–S10.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of adults hospitalized with community-acquired acute pyelonephritis, inappropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy was not associated with prolonged hospital stay or an increased risk of 30-day rehospitalization for urinary tract infection. These findings remained consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses.

As an exploratory secondary outcome, patients who received inappropriate empirical therapy were significantly more likely to require completion of treatment through a home parenteral antimicrobial therapy (HPAT) program. After adjustment for prespecified confounders, inappropriate empirical therapy remained strongly associated with HPAT (adjusted OR 10.79, 95% CI 4.46–26.06; P < 0.001).

In our cohort, the proportion of inappropriate therapy was 23.00%, which is lower than the 40.00% reported in previous international studies [9]. This difference may be explained by the fact that our cohort included only patients with acute pyelonephritis. In addition, compared with the study by Onyango et al., fluoroquinolone use remained low in our cohort. This finding may also reflect local resistance patterns, adherence to institutional treatment guidelines, and the increasing global prevalence of resistant Enterobacterales in the outpatient setting [8].

The low prevalence of bacteremia in our cohort suggests that the included patients had less severe infections. In other series, bacteremia associated with inappropriate therapy has been linked to a higher risk of mortality [25, 26], underscoring the importance of considering the clinical spectrum when interpreting outcomes.

The median hospital length of stay in our cohort was shorter than that reported in other published series, and the prevalence of bacteremia was also low, suggesting that our population included patients with less severe infections than those described in previous studies [10, 11, 17, 27]. This finding likely reflects both local admission practices and the characteristics of our study population. At our institution, patients with community-acquired acute pyelonephritis who present with systemic inflammatory response are routinely admitted for 24–48 h of inpatient observation and intravenous antimicrobial therapy, even in the absence of septic shock or other markers of severe disease. Furthermore, hospitalization in Colombia is frequently driven by the need for initial intravenous antimicrobial therapy, pain control, vomiting, or limited access to reliable outpatient follow-up. These factors may explain the relatively young age, low comorbidity burden, and short hospital stay observed in our cohort despite hospital admission.

The median treatment duration of 8 days (IQR 7–11) observed in our cohort reflects routine clinical practice at our institution during the study period. According to our institutional protocol, treatment duration was individualized based on the patient’s clinical response and the treating physician’s judgment, in accordance with local clinical practice guidelines [28]. Moreover, contemporary international guidelines available during the study period generally recommended treatment durations of 7–14 days for acute pyelonephritis, depending on the antimicrobial agent used and the patient’s clinical response [29]. Therefore, the treatment durations observed in our cohort are consistent with both local practice and international recommendations in place during the study period.

In addition, only rehospitalizations related to urinary tract infection were systematically collected. Information on all-cause readmissions was not available, precluding assessment of the overall impact of inappropriate empirical therapy on subsequent healthcare utilization.

Our findings complement those of both Nocua-Báez et al. and Cortés et al., two recent Colombian studies evaluating empirical antimicrobial therapy in patients with acute pyelonephritis [11, 17]. Similar to these studies, we found no association between inappropriate empirical therapy and hospital length of stay. However, our study extends the available evidence by evaluating inappropriate empirical therapy irrespective of the initial antimicrobial regimen and by applying a causal inference framework based on DAG-guided confounder selection and propensity score weighting to improve control of measured confounding. In addition, we analyzed time to hospital discharge using survival analysis rather than treating hospital length of stay as a conventional continuous outcome, allowing a more appropriate assessment of recovery over time. Consistent with our findings, Chang et al. also reported no differences in hospital length of stay or microbiological cure among women with acute pyelonephritis caused by cefuroxime-resistant Escherichia coli treated with cefuroxime [27].

Likewise, Lee et al. reported that ceftriaxone use in cases of acute pyelonephritis caused by resistant pathogens was associated with longer time to fever resolution, lower microbiological cure rates, and longer hospital stays [30]. Zhu et al. reported that inappropriate fluoroquinolone-based therapy was associated with longer hospitalization [31]. Differential fluoroquinolone use across cohorts may partly explain the observed discrepancies, because resistance to this antimicrobial class has been consistently associated with worse clinical outcomes and a higher risk of treatment failure [15, 16]. These discrepancies suggest that the clinical impact of inappropriate therapy may be mediated by the characteristics of the specific antimicrobial treatment used.

Patients receiving inappropriate empirical therapy were substantially more likely to require completion of treatment through a home-hospitalization program. Although outpatient intravenous therapy was not included in the primary outcome, this finding suggests that inappropriate therapy may increase healthcare resource utilization even in the absence of longer inpatient stays. Future studies should evaluate total healthcare dependence rather than hospital stay alone.

From a pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic perspective, our findings may be explained by the ability of several antimicrobial agents to achieve urinary concentrations that substantially exceed the susceptibility breakpoints determined in vitro [32, 33]. This phenomenon may allow clinical bacterial eradication even when microbiological profiles are categorized as resistant, especially when the infection remains confined to the urinary tract. In addition, urinary factors such as pH may further influence antimicrobial activity and bacterial eradication [34]. Therefore, microbiological resistance does not necessarily translate into clinical failure, particularly when the infection remains confined to the urinary tract.

The main strength of this study is the use of a causal inference framework based on propensity score weighting and prespecified covariate selection using a directed acyclic graph (DAG). This approach improved covariate balance, reduced measured confounding, and strengthened the internal validity of the estimated treatment effect by minimizing overadjustment and avoiding inappropriate adjustment for mediators and colliders [22, 35].

Several limitations should be considered. First, the retrospective design carries an inherent risk of information bias and residual confounding. Although we documented adequate covariate balance using absolute standardized differences and assessed common support of the propensity score, the assumption of no unmeasured confounding cannot be empirically verified. Propensity score–based methods do not correct errors introduced during study design or variable measurement [23, 36]. Second, after weighting, the effective sample size (190 nonexposed and 60 exposed patients) provided 80% power to detect hazard ratios ≥ 1.52 or ≤ 0.66 for the primary outcome of time to hospital discharge; therefore, smaller clinically relevant effects cannot be excluded. This issue should be considered when interpreting the absence of an observed association. Alternative methods, such as overlap weighting or entropy balancing, may provide more stable estimates in small samples and should be explored in future research [37, 38]. Third, because patients with septic shock or hemodynamic instability were excluded, our findings should not be extrapolated to patients with severe sepsis or bloodstream infection.

Conclusion

In this cohort of patients with community-acquired acute pyelonephritis, inappropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy was not associated with prolonged hospital stay or an increased risk of 30-day rehospitalization. These findings support individualized empirical treatment based on local epidemiology and antimicrobial stewardship principles, although prospective studies are required before these results can be generalized to patients with more severe infection.
