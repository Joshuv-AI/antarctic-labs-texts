Consensus recommendations in the management of jaw (gnathic) osteosarcoma

ABSTRACT
AbstractGnathic osteosarcoma (OS), which includes mandibular and maxillary jaw OSs, account for 6%–9% of OS. Single‐institutional and multi‐institutional retrospective studies, as well as population‐level databases, suggest that clinical differences exist among gnathic OS and OS of other sides, including other craniofacial OS. To date, no specific prospective studies of gnathic OS have been reported, and aspects of clinical management are controversial. Some elements of care are aligned with extragnathic OS, e.g., margin negative (R0) surgery, whereas others, such as chemotherapy and radiation, are not clearly defined. The authors reviewed the available literature for the diagnosis, treatment, and supportive/survivorship care patients with gnathic OS and offer consensus statements for the comprehensive management of this rare disease.

INTRODUCTION

Osteosarcoma (OS) is the most common primary malignant bone tumor in adolescents and young adults. Gnathic OS, which includes mandibular and maxillary jaw OS, account for 6%–9% of OS cases. 1 , 2 , 3 To date, no specific prospective studies of gnathic OS have been reported; and, when included in prospective studies, there have been an insufficient number of patients to allow for subgroup analysis. Single‐institutional and multi‐institutional retrospective studies, as well as population‐level databases, suggest that clinical differences exist among gnathic OS and OS of other sites, including other craniofacial OS.

Because of the rarity and differences in the clinical and epidemiologic features of gnathic OS, the management of OS in the gnathic location is controversial. 4 , 5 To build consensus recommendations for the management of gnathic OS, we assembled a multidisciplinary, international working group with expertise in bone sarcoma, head and neck surgical oncology, medical oncology, pediatric oncology, radiation oncology, pathology, survivorship, and supportive care. Subgroups were formed for each section topic (work‐up, surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, survivorship), and these groups reviewed the available literature, discussed relevant established clinical practices, and generated the initial section consensus management statement. The entire working group then went through an iterative review/feedback/revise process for each section’s recommendation statement until a full consensus was achieved. Because the published literature is predominantly comprised of retrospective studies, both single‐institution and multi‐institutional, in our critical review, we prioritized identifying imbalances in major prognostic factors, e.g., margins status and tumor grade, that may have introduced bias in the outcomes reported. Herein, we report our review and offer consensus recommendations for the comprehensive management of gnathic OS, taking care to identify when management differs from that for OS of other locations.

PRESENTATION AND WORK‐UP

Gnathic OS is seen in older patients; only approximately 2% of OS diagnosed in patients aged 10–24 years arises in the mandible, face, or skull, compared with nearly 10% of OS diagnosed in those older than 60 years. 6 The National Cancer Institute's Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database analysis from 1973 to 2011 identified 541 patients and indicated that the mean age at diagnosis was 41.3 years, 74.9% of patients were White, 16.8% were African American, and 8.2% were of other races. 7 Clinical presentations of gnathic OS vary but commonly involve pain, swelling, paresthesia, and/or ulceration. 8 Mandibular OS often occurs in the horizontal ramus, and maxillary OS most frequently occurs in the palate and alveolar ridge. 9 All histologic subgroups of OS are represented in the gnathic location. 7

The incidence of metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis may be lower in gnathic OS compared with extragnathic OS, although data are incomplete and often conflicting. Studies report that the incidence of pulmonary metastatic disease at diagnosis is approximately 2% in gnathic OS, which is significantly less than the 10%15% seen in extragnathic OS, although approximately 14%–18% of patients with gnathic OS will later develop metastatic disease. 1 , 2 , 10 , 11 Another Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results analysis reported that, among patients older than 60 years, the 5‐year survival rate from mandible/face/skull OS was better than the rate for axial or pelvic primary tumors but worse than the rate for extremity tumors (34%–48% vs. 10%–18% vs. 50%, respectively). 6 Among patients younger than 60 years, mandible/face/skull and extremity OS outcomes were similar, and slightly better outcomes for mandible/face/skull versus extremity OS were reported among patients younger than 25 years. 6 A retrospective analysis focusing specifically on gnathic OS reported disease‐specific survival rates of 66.8% at 5 years and 59.2% at 10 years. 1

Clinical presentations of gnathic OS vary, and diagnosis can be challenging. Initial imaging evaluation for a suspected gnathic OS is largely aligned with that for OS of any site, despite lower incidence of metastatic disease. Briefly, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the head and neck are performed to fully characterize the primary tumor; high‐resolution chest CT is used to evaluate for pulmonary metastases 11 , 12 ; whole‐body imaging (e.g., positron emission tomography/CT) is used to evaluate for distant extrapulmonary metastasis 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ; and a biopsy, either core‐needle or incisional biopsy, should be performed at a high‐volume sarcoma center where possible. 18 , 19 A lower risk of local relapse is associated with biopsies performed in an expert center, which is specifically relevant in patients who have gnathic OS because resection with clear surgical margins (R0 resection) is the cornerstone of treatment and can be anatomically challenging. 18 , 19 , 20 As with any OS diagnosis, evaluating for cancer predisposition syndromes as well as offering germline testing is recommended; some reports note that gnathic OS is more frequent in patients who have Li–Fraumeni syndrome. 21

Prebiopsy coordination with pathology is essential to ensure sufficient tissue is obtained and appropriately processed for all diagnostic, extended workup, and research needs, including biorepository opportunities. Specifically, the need for possible molecular characterizations (e.g., next‐generation sequencing panels, fluorescence in situ hybridization probes, or reverse transcriptase‐polymerase chain reaction analysis) necessitates avoiding harsh acid decalcification for at least part of the biopsy tissue. When decalcification is needed, we recommend ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid–based decalcification to best preserve nucleic acid integrity for molecular evaluation. 22 , 23

Historically, gnathic OS has been regarded as a specific subtype of OS; however, the fifth edition of the World Health Organization classification of soft tissue and bone tumors does not regard gnathic OS as a stand‐alone entity. 9 In general, gnathic OS are histologically similar to their extragnathic counterparts in that they are generally categorized as either low‐grade or high‐grade, 1 and most high‐grade gnathic OS are regarded as conventional OS in the current World Health Organization histologic classification. Although chondroblastic OS is classically regarded as the most common histologic subtype of gnathic OS (Figure 1), 24 the osteoblastic subtype predominates in several studies. 1 , 25 , 26 Low‐grade central/well differentiated OS is more common than parosteal OS. 27

The differential diagnosis for gnathic OS includes a wide range of tumors. Mesenchymal chondrosarcoma has a predilection for the jaw and histologically is a biphasic tumor containing cellular cartilage nodules and an undifferentiated spindled or round cell component with a prominent vascular proliferation. 9 Osteoid production is also sometimes seen in mesenchymal chondrosarcomas, causing confusion with OS. Mesenchymal chondrosarcomas are characterized genetically by an HEY1::NCOA2 fusion, and the detection of this fusion is useful in excluding a diagnosis of OS. 28 Some gnathic OS are very low‐grade, with radiographic and microscopic features that mimic a benign fibro‐osseous lesion. Genetically, most low‐grade central and parosteal OS exhibit supernumerary ring and giant rod marker chromosomes with amplification of 12q13‐15, a feature typically not found in benign fibro‐osseous lesions. 29 The cell cycle–related genes MDM2 and CDK4 are located on this segment of chromosome 12, and amplification of these genes and/or overexpression of their protein products can be demonstrated with fluorescence in situ hybridization probes, immunohistochemistry, or ancillary diagnostic tools (next‐generation sequencing panels), which are very useful in separating low‐grade OS from benign fibro‐osseous lesions. Osteoblastic OS needs to be distinguished from unusual variants of osteoblastoma, including epithelioid osteoblastoma and epithelioid multinodular osteoblastoma, both of which occur in the jaw and are associated with FOS and FOSB rearrangements. 30 Collectively, these tumors are sharply circumscribed and lack the true cytologic atypia and mitotic activity of osteoblastic OS. 31 Given the wide range of tumors in the differential diagnosis of gnathic OS, establishing an accurate diagnosis can be difficult with limited or poorly preserved biopsy material.

Consensus statement

Diagnostic procedures and work‐up of gnathic OS should occur at centers with multidisciplinary sarcoma experts in head and neck surgical oncology, radiation oncology, medical/pediatric oncology, pathology, reconstructive surgery, maxillofacial prosthodontics, as well as ancillary support teams, such as speech language pathology. Staging and pathology recommendations are aligned with those for OS of any site.

SURGICAL MANAGEMENT

Radical surgical resection is the only intervention consistently agreed upon for the management of gnathic OS. Consequently, the primary surgical aim is to perform an en bloc R0 resection with negative margins in the first operative attempt. Whereas resection guidelines for long bone OS recommend margins of 1–2 cm, 32 applying these standards to gnathic OS is challenging and often impossible because of adjacent critical anatomic structures. Intraoperatively, margins are estimated based on gross inspection and preoperative imaging. Frozen‐section analysis is limited by the osteoid matrix; therefore, margin evaluation by proxy—using periosteal, medullary, or neural tissue—may be attempted. Margin status, however, can only be definitively determined by permanent histopathology. Current clinical practice guidelines do not provide a precise definition of negative margins, and scenarios of close margins are frequent. Because of higher local recurrence rates in head and neck OS, margins of at least 3 mm on permanent histology have been proposed. 32 , 33 When clear margins are not obtained within the first surgery, the feasibility of a secondary surgery to obtain free margins needs to be considered because the main treatment remains radical surgical resection. However, surgical reconstruction is typically performed at the time of resection, making revision of margins based on permanent pathologic analysis generally infeasible once reconstruction is completed. If a positive margin is subsequently identified, reoperation may be considered on a case‐by‐case basis.

Outside of standard recommendations for the surgical management of OS, augmented/virtual reality has been increasingly applied in complex maxillofacial reconstruction. 34 For example, three‐dimensional preoperative planning and composite cutting guides can facilitate free fibula reconstructive surgery to limit ischemic time and total operative time and increase accuracy, especially in larger reconstructions. 35 , 36 , 37 Reconstructive surgery should be performed by a dedicated plastic/head and neck surgical team that has experience with complex reconstructions in the head and neck area to reduce the risk of complications, such as fistulas, salivary leaks, or loss of the flap, that would not only compromise the reconstruction but can also risk delaying adjuvant treatments (e.g., chemotherapy or radiotherapy). Reconstruction should address facial aesthetics as well as functional aspects, such as swallowing, mastication, speech, and occlusion. Limited posterior maxillectomy defects or marginal mandibulectomy defects can be reconstructed with soft tissue–only reconstruction, such as locoregional flaps or fasciocutaneous free flaps. However, large composite resections including segmental mandibulectomy or anterior maxilla will require osteocutaneous free flaps to provide both bony and soft tissue components. Furthermore, maxillofacial prostheses may also aid in reconstruction. Importantly, the removal of teeth is often required, and reconstruction may include dental implants. This dental rehabilitation is paramount after jaw reconstruction to restore occlusal function, and this can be performed either immediately (e.g., jaw‐in‐a‐day) or in a delayed fashion. Adult cohort studies and a systematic review of limited cohorts report that immediate (direct) dental implant placement performed during ablative surgery or shortly before radiotherapy did not delay the initiation of radiotherapy in those series. 32 , 33 , 38 Importantly, the need for such reconstruction, similar to the provision of prosthetics for limb amputation, should be considered part of standard medical management of patients with gnathic OS, and costs of these procedures should be covered by medical insurance.

Postoperative care is best provided by an inpatient team with specialized nursing care and ancillary team members, including speech and language pathology, nutrition, psychosocial workers, and physical and occupational therapy. Many patients require a temporary tracheostomy and/or nasogastric feeding tube; early rehabilitation of speech and swallowing function is essential to enable timely decannulation of the tracheostomy tube and removal of the nasogastric tube. Resumption of cancer‐directed therapy, as discussed below (see Radiation Management and Chemotherapy Management), can typically occur within 6 weeks, once the surgical team deems that adequate healing has occurred.

Consensus statement

Negative margin R0 resection is the cornerstone of surgical treatment for gnathic OS, which is aligned with surgical treatment for OS of any site. Resection and reconstruction should be performed by a specialized head and neck surgical team. Dental reconstruction and rehabilitation are essential parts of the management of patients with gnathic OS.

RADIATION MANAGEMENT

Although OS is considered a radioresistant disease, radiation therapy is a valuable tool in the clinical management of gnathic OS. Whereas prospective data evaluating the effect of radiotherapy on reducing local recurrence in OS are missing, radiation therapy should be considered in patient at high risk of local failure (e.g., <3 mm margin on permanent section) after surgical resection and for definitive local therapy in inoperable patients.

Indeed, consensus guidelines for the management of OS published by the Children’s Oncology Group and the International Society of Pediatric Oncology recommend the use of postoperative radiotherapy for patients with positive or unclear margins. 39 In a large, single‐institution series evaluating the effect of radiotherapy on resectable OS of the head and neck, radiation therapy demonstrated a benefit in overall survival (80% vs. 31%; p < .02), disease‐specific survival (80% vs. 35%; p < .02), and local control (75% vs. 24%; p < .006) for patients with uncertain or positive margin status. 40 Thus, for such patients with gnathic OS, the use of postoperative radiotherapy of 60–66 grays (Gy) in 30–33 fractions is recommended, with doses up to 70 Gy for macroscopic disease. 39 Postoperative radiation is strongly preferred over preoperative radiation to permit accurate pathologic margin status determination and to reduce the risk of postoperative complications (Figure 2). There are no definitive data on the benefit of preoperative radiotherapy versus postoperative radiotherapy; and, because these tumors are largely radiation‐resistant, delaying surgery is not advised.

Surgical clearance for adequate healing from jaw reconstruction is needed before initiating radiation and/or chemotherapy. Postoperative radiotherapy is common clinical practice after complex reconstruction of head and neck carcinomas and is typically initiated by 6 weeks postoperatively, after adequate reconstruction healing. The feasibility of concurrent chemotherapy with radiotherapy depends on the regimen used, e.g., methotrexate/doxorubicin/cisplatin (MAP) or ifosfamide/etoposide, and the involved radiation field. General recommendations include the avoidance of concurrent high‐dose methotrexate and radiation because of the sensitive anatomy (brain parenchyma) associated with specific gnathic OS locations as well as the possibility of overlapping toxicity (mucositis). In addition, concurrent doxorubicin and radiation may lead to increased skin toxicity and radiation recall (although the administration of doxorubicin on days 1 and 2 of chemotherapy is an established, safe clinical practice). Ifosfamide and etoposide are routinely given with concurrent radiation in Ewing sarcoma treatment protocols, although they can be associated with overlapping toxicity (mucositis). 41 Past Children's Oncology Group studies and the current AOST2032 study (MAP with or without cabozantinib; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05691478) recommend sequencing adjuvant radiation after the completion of systemic chemotherapy.

Advanced radiotherapy delivery with intensity‐modulated radiation therapy and image guidance is recommended. The clinical target volume should encompass the entire operative bed and reconstruction plus a margin of 1–2 cm but can be limited to a smaller margin where natural barriers to spread exist, e.g., intracranial structures, skin, and salivary glands. Given the very low risk of lymph node OS metastasis, elective regional lymph node irradiation is not recommended.

Photon therapy remains the most widely available treatment, but the steep dose gradient of proton therapy offers a lower dose to surrounding normal tissues. Therefore, proton therapy has the potential to reduce toxicity in tissues like the oral tongue, nontarget mandible, and contralateral salivary glands. In addition, the higher biologic effectiveness of heavy‐ion therapy (e.g., proton and carbon) may help overcome the radioresistant properties of OS, and its use in unresectable OS is an area of active research. For example, in a series of 51 patients with head and neck OS, patients who received combined ion therapy of 54 Gy (relative biologic effectiveness) proton therapy with an 18‐Gy (relative biologic effectiveness) carbon‐ion boost demonstrated a survival benefit compared with those who received other combinations of proton and heavy‐ion treatments. 42

Consensus statement

External‐beam radiotherapy should be recommended to all patients with gnathic OS who have positive surgical margins and should be strongly considered for those who have close/uncertain margins. When available, proton radiotherapy offers a lower integral dose to surrounding normal tissue, which can be advantageous, especially in younger patients.

CHEMOTHERAPY MANAGEMENT

The generally accepted standard of care for localized, high‐grade OS is neoadjuvant chemotherapy, resection, and adjuvant chemotherapy. 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 However, as outlined below, there is much confounding information regarding the role of chemotherapy in the management of patients with gnathic OS. Because of the rarity of gnathic OS, the evidence with which to address the value of chemotherapy is limited.

Patients with gnathic OS were not included in recent large international trials, including AOST033 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT00134030)/EURAMOS1 (International Standard Randomized Controlled Trial Number ISRCTN 67613327), ISG/OS2 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04383288), and GEIS‐33 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01459484). 47 , 48 , 49 No randomized controlled trials have reported on the use of chemotherapy for this population, and the literature is limited to retrospective case series and meta‐analyses. The reports available often have considerable variation in treatment and key disease characteristics, such as tumor grade, or lack sufficient detail to adequately evaluate the reported outcomes, 1 , 40 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 thus caution is necessary when interpreting or generalizing the results. Kuo and Kent provide a detailed literature review summarizing results and recommendations for the management of mandibular OS from 27 relevant articles published between 1967 and 2017 and highlight the disparate results and conclusions from these studies. 58 Table 1 summarizes the literature reviewed and referenced in the current article and calls out gnathic specific data where available.

Now nearly 30 years old, the systematic review by Smeele et al. of the effect of chemotherapy on 201 patients with craniofacial OS demonstrated improved event‐free and overall survival in those who received chemotherapy, including statistically significant improvements in event‐free and overall survival within both the completely resected and incompletely resected subgroups. However, 30% of patients included in that analysis had cranial nonmaxillary, nonmandibular OS, which may have a higher rate of metastases and theoretically may benefit more from chemotherapy. 57

An analysis of 214 patients with gnathic OS in the German–Austrian–Swiss DOESAK registry (1964–2009) included variables like treatment era, resection status, size, location, chemotherapy use, radiation use, recurrence rates, and survival rates. The majority of those patients underwent surgery only (n = 127). Only 24% (n = 51) received (neo)adjuvant chemotherapy with or without radiation in addition to surgery, and it is unclear which variables contributed to the decision to treat with chemotherapy. Notably, 26 of 214 patients (12%) never achieved an R0 resection. No benefit from chemotherapy was identified. 1

More recently, Boon et al. reported on 77 Dutch patients with head and neck OS, of whom 50 had high‐grade or intermediate‐grade OS, and compared outcomes for 29 patients (19 gnathic) who received chemotherapy versus 21 (16 gnathic) who did not. Multivariable analysis indicated that those who did not receive neoadjuvant chemotherapy had a significantly higher risk of local recurrence (hazard ratio, 3.66; 95% confidence interval, 1.21–11.08; p = .022), but there was no significant effect on disease‐free or overall survival. 2 Chen et al. evaluated a cohort of 157 patients with head and neck OS, of whom 38 received chemotherapy and 119 did not; on multivariate analysis, chemotherapy (p = .025), tumor site (p = .034), margin status (p = .006), and histologic subtype (p = .012) were significant factors for overall survival. 59 The survival benefit of chemotherapy lost significance after the 2‐year mark, which is similar to an National Cancer Database study demonstrating that chemotherapy plus surgery improved 18‐month survival compared with surgery alone (95.8% vs. 78.5%; p = .031), but no difference was maintained in 5‐year overall survival analysis. 65 Although not definitive, collectively, these studies suggest that chemotherapy has a role in the management of high‐grade gnathic OS, which is also supported by a recent opinion statement on the role of chemotherapy in osteogenic sarcoma of the head and neck by Bialick et al. 67

Two randomized controlled trials will provide insight into the role of chemotherapy for gnathic OS. The currently enrolling Children’s Oncology Group study AOST2032 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05691478) is a randomized controlled clinical trial evaluating the addition of cabozantinib to standard chemotherapy for patients with newly diagnosed OS, including gnathic OS. The Fight Osteosarcoma Through European Research (FOSTER) Consortium study (FOSTER‐CabOs) will evaluate the addition of cabozantinib maintenance treatment in patients of all ages with newly diagnosed OS, including craniofacial OS, who have completed standard OS treatment. These trials may provide benchmark outcomes for the treatment of gnathic OS and inform future studies, although the numbers will still likely be small.

In the absence of prospective investigation of chemotherapy in gnathic OS, our consensus is to recommend chemotherapy in high‐grade gnathic OS according to institutional/national standard OS chemotherapy protocols in a shared decision‐making process with patients and their multidisciplinary team. 43 , 68 MAP chemotherapy is the most frequently used regimen for newly diagnosed osteosarcoma. Ifosfamide plus etoposide regimens are known to be active in OS, and there may be reasons to consider tailoring regimens based on patients’ local control needs (e.g., concurrent chemotherapy and radiation). 69 , 70 There are no data available to support the administration of an abbreviated chemotherapy regimen for osteosarcoma (e.g., two or four cycles of MAP), thus we recommend completion of the entire chemotherapy regimen (e.g., six cycles of MAP). We emphasize that these are general recommendations and that each individual patient will be best served by treatment recommendations from a comprehensive and multidisciplinary tumor board evaluation.

The timing of chemotherapy is an additional practical consideration in the management of gnathic OS. There may be reasons to specifically consider neoadjuvant chemotherapy in addition to adjuvant therapy for gnathic OS (Figure 2) because the complex surgical resection/reconstruction necessary often requires a 6‐week postoperative recovery period before adjuvant chemotherapy can be delivered. In patients who have extremity OS, there is a survival benefit to re‐starting adjuvant chemotherapy within 21 days of local control surgery. 71 Therefore, consideration may be given to completing more than two preoperative cycles of chemotherapy, thus limiting the effect of treatment delays postoperatively. Indeed, a retrospective study of 111 patients with mandibular OS indicated that 63 patients (67%) had clear (>2 cm) margins after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, whereas five (40%) had clear (>2 cm) margins in the group of patients who underwent upfront surgery. 51 Importantly, five patients with unresectable disease had progression on chemotherapy. Although those patients had unresectable disease, the findings highlight the risk for disease progression on chemotherapy and the critical importance of radiographic assessments and clinical evaluation by treating surgeons during neoadjuvant treatment to detect early nonresponding patients.

The significance of the histologic response to systemic chemotherapy in gnathic OS is uncertain; the association of treatment effect and clinical outcome is informed by clinical trials that excluded patients with gnathic OS. Furthermore, only anecdotal evidence suggests that gnathic OS resection specimens can be mapped in a fashion similar to that used for extremity specimens and can be evaluated for therapy response by using the accepted threshold of ≥90% necrosis for a favorable histologic response. Therefore, caution should be used when interpreting the histologic response to chemotherapy in patients who have gnathic OS.

Consensus statement

In the absence of prospective investigation of chemotherapy in patients with gnathic OS, our consensus is to recommend chemotherapy for the treatment of high‐grade gnathic OS according to institutional/national standard OS chemotherapy regimens, in shared decision‐making process with patients and their multidisciplinary team. Chemotherapy may be omitted in patients who have low‐grade gnathic OS.

SURVIVORSHIP AND SUBSEQUENT MANAGEMENT

Survivors of gnathic OS benefit from a coordinated survivorship program that has experience in patients who undergo complex head and neck surgical reconstructions. Extensive supportive services must continue to be involved, including head and neck surgery, oral‐maxillofacial surgery, otolaryngology, oncology, dentistry (including dental reconstruction), physiotherapy, occupational therapy, lymphedema therapy, psychosocial workers, speech and language pathology, and dieticians. 72 Attention to gnathic OS–specific, long‐term needs include evaluation and treatment for malocclusion; temporomandibular joint dysfunction; soft tissue retractions; lymphedema; difficulties in mastication, deglutination, and phonation; as well as cosmetic concerns, which may also lead to psychosocial distress, including depression, anxiety, and body image disorder. After chemotherapy exposure, the same surveillance should occur for patients who have gnathic OS as that used for those who have OS of any other site. 62 When radiation is given, surveillance for skin malignancies is necessary and depends on the radiation field; additional late effects can be observed, including endocrinopathies, xerostomia, poor dentition/dental health, dysphagia, and trismus.

A wide range in the recurrence rate of gnathic OS is reported, spanning from 8% to 64%; the majority of relapses occur locally (range, 17%–70%), and the development of distant metastases is less frequent (range, 7%–20%). 2 , 51 , 59 , 73 Most local recurrences occur within 5 years of surgery, and the median time to the development of a local recurrence is usually shorter than that for metastatic disease (7.9 vs. 23 months). 40 , 50 , 60 Positive margins, high‐grade tumors, and a history of prior irradiation in the head and neck region are associated with an increased risk for recurrence. 50 , 60 , 63 , 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 Data on large cohorts of patients with relapsed gnathic OS are rare, and few reports include clinical and therapeutic details for this subgroup of patients. The clinical management of recurrent gnathic OS is individualized; and, when available, participation in clinic trials is encouraged.

Consensus statement

A multidisciplinary approach to survivorship care coordinated by a team that has experience with head and neck reconstructive surgeries is standard for patients with gnathic OS. The management of recurrent gnathic OS is individualized; and, when available, participation in clinic trials is encouraged.

Conclusions

Gnathic OS includes mandibular and maxillary OS and accounts for 6%–9% of OS, or approximately 50–100 cases annually in the United States. Although most guidelines do not differentiate gnathic OS from extragnathic disease, controversy and practice variation exist in the clinical management of gnathic OS. Like other rare entities in oncology, the limited incidence of gnathic OS makes appropriately powered, prospective studies challenging. We have synthesized the available literature and constructed consensus expert management recommendations for each major component of clinical care. Continued coordinated efforts among international institutions and organizations are needed to drive progress in the clinical management of gnathic OS.
