A Case of Unilateral Adrenal Tuberculosis in an Ethiopian Patient Presenting as a Tumor: A Diagnostic Challenge in a Resource‐Limited Setup
Authors: Kidan Fssaha Tsehaye, Dirar Medhanie Gebremedhin, Birhanu Kassie Reta, Bisrat Tesfay Abera, Tsega Hagos Mehari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTuberculosis remains a major public health burden in developing countries, with adrenal involvement being rare and typically bilateral. Isolated adrenal tuberculosis accounts for 1%–2% of adrenal masses and often presents with nonspecific symptoms. Its imaging findings can mimic primary adrenal tumors. Hereby we report a 66‐year‐old male from the Tigray region of Ethiopia presented with a one‐year history of right flank pain and weight loss. A computed tomography scan revealed a 5 cm right adrenal mass with an impression of a primary adrenal tumor. His routine laboratory test results were within normal range except for the elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate. Histopathological examination following open adrenalectomy revealed caseating granulomatous inflammation suggesting adrenal tuberculosis. A subsequent acid‐fast bacilli stain was done from the tissue block, and it was positive. Adrenal function tests and hormonal levels were planned, but the tests were not available at our center. The patient was started on anti‐tuberculous therapy, and he has recovered upon completion of the regimen. In a suspected case of adrenal tuberculosis presenting with a mass lesion, the patient should be referred to better setups for minimally invasive tests like ultrasound‐guided fine needle aspiration cytology and assessment of adrenal function.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant health concern in developing countries. Adrenal gland involvement is rare and may occur in isolation or alongside pulmonary tuberculosis [1]. Approximately 25% of patients with adrenal TB have no active extra‐adrenal involvement. Isolated adrenal TB accounts for 1%–2% of adrenal masses, and adrenal involvement has been identified in 6% of autopsy cases of patients with active tuberculosis [2]. Notably, 75%–100% of adrenal TB cases involve bilateral adrenal glands, making unilateral adrenal TB an uncommon clinical entity [3].

In its early stages, adrenal tuberculosis typically presents with no clear symptoms and is often detected incidentally during routine physical examinations or the diagnosis of other conditions, leading to frequent clinical misdiagnosis. Symptoms of adrenal insufficiency typically appear late, only after at least 90% of the adrenal cortex has been destroyed [4, 5].

Imaging findings often lack specificity; ultrasound may show unilateral or bilateral adrenal masses, while computed tomography (CT) imaging features of adrenal TB can resemble those of primary tumors. TB lesions typically exhibit central hypodensity with peripheral rim enhancement [6]. Adrenal tuberculosis had the appearance of an adrenal tumor on CT scan in about 75% of patients during the first year after primary adrenal deficiency diagnosis that resulted from tuberculosis [7] Histopathological examination remains the gold standard for diagnosis.

Antituberculous (anti‐TB) therapy is the cornerstone of treatment, and in cases of adrenal insufficiency, hormone replacement is required. Hereby, we report a case of a unilateral adrenal mass initially considered to be a primary adrenal tumor on imaging, which was ultimately diagnosed as adrenal tuberculosis, and completely responded to antituberculous therapy.

History and Examination

The patient is a 66‐year‐old male from rural areas of Tigray, Ethiopia, who presented with a one‐year history of right flank pain. He has also associated unintentional weight loss, loss of appetite, low‐grade fever, and irritability. He denied having a cough, frequent urination, dysuria, or hematuria. No pertinent findings on examination of other systems. No history of contact with a known tuberculosis patient. No significant past medical or surgical history. On physical examination, his vital signs were within the normal range. He had mild costovertebral angle tenderness.

Methods (Investigation and Treatment)

Laboratory investigations were all within the normal range except for elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate, which was 97 mm/h. (Table 1) He was also screened for retroviral infection status and was found to be non‐reactive. An abdominopelvic ultrasound examination revealed a 5 cm × 2.5 cm hypoechoic lesion in the right suprarenal region with a lobulated surface, suggestive of an adenoma. A contrast‐enhanced CT scan of the abdomen was performed for further characterization, and the findings were a well‐defined hypodense right adrenal lesion (mean attenuation: 38 Hounsfield Units (HU)) with thick peripheral enhancement. Otherwise, there was no evidence of calcification, macroscopic fat, or extension into adjacent structures (Figure 1). A chest X‐ray was also done, and it was non‐revealing. Given these findings, the primary differentials included after the imaging findings a lipid‐poor adenoma and adrenocortical carcinoma. With the diagnosis of a right adrenal mass secondary to adrenocortical adenoma, open surgery with a right adrenalectomy was performed, and the specimen was sent for biopsy evaluation. The patient was then discharged on the 10th postoperative day without any complications.

The histopathologic examination revealed a 5 cm mass with irregular areas of whitish friable component. Multiple sections representing different areas of the mass revealed numerous well‐formed granulomas, forming a mass with large areas of typical caseous necrosis rimmed by epithelioid histiocytes and multinucleated Langhan's type giant cells, lymphocytes, and plasma cells. Adjacent areas of the normal adrenal cortex were also present (Figures 2 and 3). Acid‐fast bacilli (AFB) stain was done from the tissue block, and it was positive. Based on these histopathological findings and the AFB stain, adrenal tuberculosis was the final working diagnosis, and the patient was referred to the infectious disease clinic for treatment. He is treated with anti‐TB for 6 months, with strict follow‐up with abdominal ultrasound and organ function tests.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient was treated with anti‐TB medication for six months and is currently being followed in the outpatient clinic. His abdominal pain, fever, and loss of appetite have improved, and he has gained weight.

Discussion

Extra‐pulmonary tuberculosis accounts for approximately 15%–20% of all TB cases, commonly affecting the lymph nodes, pleura, gastrointestinal tract, bones, central nervous system, genitourinary system, and endocrine system [8]. Among the endocrine organs, the adrenal gland is the most frequently involved site; however, its involvement remains rare compared to other extra‐pulmonary sites. Adrenal TB typically presents bilaterally due to hematogenous and lymphatic dissemination from a primary site of infection. Unilateral cases, such as in our patient, are particularly uncommon [9].

Distinguishing adrenal TB from other adrenal masses is crucial. The differential diagnosis includes adrenocortical carcinoma, adrenal adenoma, adrenal incidentaloma, metastases, myelolipoma, and pheochromocytoma. In the early stages of TB infection, contrast‐enhanced CT imaging typically reveals enlarged adrenal glands with central necrotic hypodense areas and peripheral enhancement. In later stages, adrenal atrophy with irregular margins and calcification is common [10]. Our patient's CT scan showed a centrally hypodense lesion with peripheral thick enhancement and no calcification, which is consistent with the early stage of adrenal TB.

Lesion size is another important factor in differentiating adrenal TB from malignancies. Tumors larger than 6 cm are more likely to be malignant, with adrenal carcinomas and metastases being the most frequent [11]. In contrast, smaller tumors (< 4 cm) are more often benign, with adenomas being the predominant type [12]. Untreated TB lesions are generally smaller than primary adrenal tumors (2.8 ± 1.3 cm vs. 3.5 ± 2.4 cm) [6], while pheochromocytomas and adrenocortical carcinomas tend to be larger, with mean diameters of 5.7 ± 2.3 cm and 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, respectively [6]. However, lesion size alone is not a definitive diagnostic criterion, as our patient's lesion measured 5.0 × 5.5 cm.

CT attenuation values also provide valuable diagnostic clues. In adrenal TB, unenhanced and contrast‐enhanced scans reveal reduced central attenuation (7 ± 4 HU) compared to the peripheral region (32 ± 14 HU) [13]. In contrast, pheochromocytomas typically demonstrate high enhancement (> 110 HU), while adrenocortical carcinomas exhibit variable enhancement. In our case, the mean attenuation was 38 HU, which did not definitively exclude malignancy, emphasizing the necessity of histological confirmation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is now considered the imaging modality of choice for further characterization of indeterminate adrenal lesions [14]. It offers superior soft‐tissue contrast and functional sequences such as chemical shift imaging, which can aid in distinguishing adenomas from malignant or infectious lesions [14]. However, MRI was not performed due to limited local availability at the time of evaluation.

Adrenal TB may occur without active extra‐adrenal disease in approximately 12% of cases, as seen in our patient, making biopsy confirmation essential [15]. Fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) may be considered an initial diagnostic modality for a wide range of adrenal masses, particularly when infectious or metastatic disease is a differential diagnosis and when imaging features are inconclusive. In our case, the lesion's imaging characteristics and size initially raised concern for adrenocortical carcinoma. In such cases, biopsy is generally avoided due to the risk of tumor seeding and limited sensitivity in distinguishing carcinoma from other pathologies [16, 17, 18].

The histopathological hallmark of TB is granulomatous inflammation with caseating necrosis, often accompanied by Langhans‐type giant cells and epithelioid macrophages. Over time, fibrosis and calcification may also develop. Imaging alone is often insufficient to differentiate adrenal TB from malignancies and benign tumors due to overlapping features [19]. Histological examination confirms the diagnosis by identifying tuberculous granulomas and acid‐fast bacilli, thereby distinguishing TB from conditions such as adrenocortical carcinoma and pheochromocytoma [8]. Accurate diagnosis is crucial for appropriate management, as adrenal TB requires targeted anti‐TB therapy, whereas other adrenal masses may necessitate surgical or alternative interventions [20]. In our particular case, the histopathologic examination revealed caseating granulomatous inflammation, with subsequent AFB stain identifying the bacilli.

The treatment of adrenal TB follows the standard anti‐TB regimen. Patients with adrenal insufficiency require hormonal replacement therapy. The standard treatment regimen includes isoniazid (5 mg/kg/day), rifampicin (10 mg/kg/day), pyrazinamide (30 mg/kg/day), and ethambutol (20 mg/kg/day) for 2 months, followed by isoniazid and rifampicin for 4 months. Since up to 70% of patients with active TB may experience subclinical adrenal insufficiency, careful monitoring is essential to prevent adrenal crisis [21]. Our patient took antituberculosis therapy for six months; he is on follow‐up and has recovered from his symptoms. This case of unilateral adrenal tuberculosis highlights its rarity and diagnostic challenges. It underscores the importance of considering adrenal TB in the differential diagnosis of adrenal masses. Minimally invasive diagnostic techniques, such as image‐guided FNAC, should be considered to facilitate diagnosis and reduce unnecessary surgeries. Early histopathological confirmation is crucial for guiding appropriate treatment, which typically consists of anti‐tuberculous therapy.

Limitation of the Case Report

Adrenal function tests, hormonal status, culture and sensitivity, and preoperative fine needle aspiration cytology from the adrenal mass were not done due to the availability of the tests as well as a lack of experienced physicians in our setup. As much as possible, patients should be referred to better setups for appropriate workup.
