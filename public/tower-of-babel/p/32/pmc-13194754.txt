Topology modeling and energy efficiency prediction of parallel chillers based on deep learning
Authors: Yang Liu, Xu Yang, Lei Zhang, Yan Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the insufficient energy efficiency prediction accuracy caused by topological coupling in the parallel operation of multiple chillers, this study proposes a physics-guided spatiotemporal fusion model combining Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Graph Convolutional Network (GCN). The LSTM module extracts temporal features from single-unit energy consumption sequences; the GCN module captures spatial dependencies through constructed topological graphs representing cooling water networks and load distribution relationships. Based on the operational data from a large-scale data center cooling station, the model is trained and tested using 128 million raw records along with 8000 h of simulation data. Experimental results demonstrate that the GCN-LSTM model achieves a 19.4% reduction in Root Mean Square Error and a 2.06% decrease in Mean Absolute Percentage Error compared to standalone LSTM models at 30-min prediction intervals. The ablation experiment further verifies the necessity of GCN for spatial modeling, and its absence led to a significant increase in prediction error. The proposed GCN-LSTM architecture surpasses traditional single-model performance limitations and provides an adaptable solution for multi-equipment collaborative optimization. This approach establishes a new technical path for industrial energy conservation and intelligent control systems.

Introduction

With the intensification of the global energy crisis and environmental issues, energy efficiency optimization has become an important research direction in the industrial field. As the core carriers of energy consumption, cooling station equipment directly affects the overall level of energy utilization1–3. According to statistics from the International Energy Agency, the energy consumption of cooling station equipment in the industrial sector, such as motors and ship power systems, accounts for approximately 40% of the global total energy consumption. Among them, about 30% of energy is wasted due to inefficient operation and inadequate management4,5. This situation increases the operating costs of enterprises and imposes a severe burden on the ecological environment. Improving the energy efficiency of cooling station equipment through intelligent means has become a key issue in the fields of current energy management and industrial automation.

In recent years, the swift development of artificial intelligence (AI) technology has provided new solutions for energy efficiency optimization of cooling station equipment. Traditional energy efficiency optimization methods mainly rely on static models and empirical equations, such as energy efficiency evaluation systems based on thermodynamic analysis or rule-based control strategies6–8. However, these methods often exhibit problems such as low prediction accuracy and poor adaptability when facing complex and variable working conditions. The operational data of cooling station equipment have remarkable time-series characteristics, such as motors’ energy consumption fluctuations and seasonal changes in ship fuel consumption. The nonlinearity and dynamics of these data make it difficult for traditional methods to fully capture their inherent laws9.

Against this background, AI-based energy efficiency optimization methods have gradually become a research hotspot. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), as a special type of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), has shown remarkable advantages in energy prediction and optimization due to its strong time-series modeling and long-term dependency capturing abilities. LSTM has been successfully applied to ship fuel consumption prediction, motor energy consumption analysis, and fault diagnosis of industrial machinery. However, the standard LSTM model still has limitations in handling high-noise data, multi-variable coupling relationships, and adaptability to special working conditions. Combining the operating characteristics of cooling station equipment, improving the LSTM algorithm, and designing efficient intelligent control strategies has become the key to promoting the development of energy efficiency optimization technology. According to the latest research, the spatial–temporal coupling problem in the collaborative operation of multiple devices has become a major bottleneck in energy efficiency optimization. A study by Liang et al.10 showed that to realize the intelligent operation and maintenance of building energy systems, the main challenge was the modeling of target energy devices or systems. Although LSTM performs well in energy efficiency prediction for single devices, in multi-device parallel systems, traditional methods often ignore the spatial coupling relationships between devices and changes in topological structures. This leads to a significant decline in prediction accuracy when load migration occurs or device start-stop strategies are adjusted.

This study focuses on the intelligent technical path for energy efficiency optimization of chillers, aiming to construct a comprehensive solution for dynamic optimization decision-making. Aiming at the spatial correlation and dynamic interaction characteristics among multiple chillers, a hybrid model based on Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) and LSTM is constructed. Through spatial topology modeling and time-series feature decoupling, it realizes the joint prediction of energy consumption of multiple units, overcoming the problem that traditional models have insufficient adaptability to complex working conditions. The GCN module is used to extract topological relationships and spatial dependency features among units, while the LSTM module captures the laws of time-series evolution. Through a cross-modal feature fusion strategy, a unified multi-unit energy consumption prediction framework is constructed, solving the problem that traditional methods struggle to balance spatial and temporal dimensions.

Literature review

Cooling station equipment accounts for more than 70% of industrial energy consumption. Traditional mechanism models are limited by complex working condition drifts and are difficult to maintain efficient operation continuously. With the popularization of sensing and edge computing, energy efficiency optimization based on big data and AI has become a research focus. Wang et al.11 developed a multi-device collaborative optimization framework based on GCN. However, they used a simple linear layer for time series modeling, failing to fully utilize LSTM’s ability to capture long-term dependencies. Kumar et al.12 systematically reviewed the application of big data + AI in ship main propulsion systems. The GCN-LSTM combined model was used for fuel consumption prediction under multiple navigation conditions, where GCN captured spatial dependencies through topological structures and LSTM captured temporal dynamics through a gating mechanism. They further pointed out that insufficient cross-ship type migration capability of the model and high noise in real-ship data remained bottlenecks. Cui13 constructed a CNN-LSTM hybrid model in an integrated energy system to predict the load of combined cooling, heating, and power (CCHP) micro gas turbines. The real-time scheduling framework based on deep learning proposed here effectively achieved the dual-objective optimization of economy and carbon emissions. GCN-LSTM can capture spatial correlations and temporal dynamics in multi-device energy prediction, while traditional methods can only focus on one of these dimensions.

Tang and Wang14 introduced an energy management strategy for hybrid electric vehicles based on deep reinforcement learning. Their research realized dynamic optimization of power distribution between the engine and the battery through the design of state variables, including demand power and the battery’s State of Charge. Experiments showed that the Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithm was superior to traditional rule-based control in maintaining equivalent fuel consumption. Recalde et al.15 developed an Internet of Things platform for ship energy efficiency, adopting principal component analysis combined with a random forest method to identify high-energy-consuming equipment. They further proposed an online incremental learning mechanism, enabling the model to adaptively update with ship age and solve the drift problem in traditional static models. Cakiroglu et al.16 proposed a data-driven interpretable ensemble learning method for wind turbine power prediction. By combining SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis, they significantly improved the model’s interpretability, providing a new idea for prediction in complex energy systems. Aylak17 developed the SustAI-SCM framework, which used an intelligent agent AI to automate supply chain processes. This framework demonstrated AI’s application potential in sustainability and cost efficiency optimization, which was highly consistent with this study’s energy efficiency optimization goal. Özdemir et al.18 utilized artificial neural networks and bidirectional LSTM to predict solar power generation installed in Turkey, verifying the effectiveness of LSTM in processing time-series energy data.

In the field of fault diagnosis and condition monitoring, recent studies further highlighted the importance of spatiotemporal feature modeling. Zhi et al.19 proposed an unsupervised transfer learning method based on a multi-channel calibrated Transformer for bearing fault diagnosis. This approach enhanced the capability of the model to capture fault features under varying operating conditions through a shiftable window mechanism. However, that study primarily focused on single-unit fault mode recognition and neglected the coupling relationships during the collaborative operation of multiple units. Furthermore, Zhi et al.20 introduced a local entropy-selected scale extraction Chirplet transform for enhanced time–frequency analysis and accurate state estimation of non-stationary signals. While significant progress was achieved in reliability-oriented fault diagnosis, this method mainly addressed the signal processing level and lacked systematic modeling of topological dependencies between devices. Additionally, Li et al.21 investigated a multi-sensor fault diagnosis method for bearings based on a multi-branch parallel perception network and feature fusion strategies. Although the diagnosis accuracy was improved by integrating multi-source sensing information from various raw records, the network architecture did not explicitly encode the physical connection relationships between the equipment.

The aforementioned studies indicate that this field is evolving from traditional control strategies toward data-driven and deep learning methods. However, several research gaps remain in existing literature. Most approaches focus on single-unit fault diagnosis or energy consumption prediction, resulting in insufficient modeling of the topological coupling effects among multiple chillers during parallel operation. Such simplified treatments overlook the physical coupling and load interactions between devices in parallel systems. For instance, the start-stop actions or load adjustments of a single chiller affect the operating status of other units through the hydraulic balance of the pipe network, thereby altering the energy efficiency distribution of the entire system. The lack of explicit modeling for these topological coupling effects leads to a sharp decline in prediction accuracy when the system topology changes. Although some studies attempted to combine GCN and LSTM, they failed to fully integrate physical topology with load distribution relationships during graph construction. Furthermore, these models lack adaptive update mechanisms for dynamic topological changes. The cross-scenario transferability of existing models also requires further verification, particularly regarding the lack of systematic evaluation for generalizability across different industrial sectors. This limitation hinders the practical application value of such models in engineering, especially in scenarios with scarce target-domain raw records, where the cross-domain adaptability has not been fully demonstrated.

To address the aforementioned gaps, this study proposes an energy efficiency prediction model for multiple chillers by integrating GCN and LSTM. Research methodology

Operation and energy consumption analysis of chillers

As the core equipment of central air-conditioning systems, the operational principle of chillers is closely related to the working characteristics of compressors. Chillers realize the transfer and release of heat through the circulation of refrigerant among evaporators, condensers, compressors, and expansion valves, thereby providing continuous cooling for building spaces. Its core operational principle is mainly that the compressor compresses low-temperature and low-pressure refrigerant gas into high-temperature and high-pressure gas. Subsequently, the heat is transferred to the cooling water or air through the condenser, causing it to condense into a liquid. After throttling and depressurization by the expansion valve, the liquid refrigerant enters the evaporator. Under the low-temperature and low-pressure conditions, it absorbs the heat of the chilled water to achieve the cooling function. Finally, it is sucked in gas again through the compressor to complete the cycle. The working principle of chillers is displayed in Fig. 1.Fig. 1The working principle of chillers.

The working principle of chillers.

During the operation of chillers, the compressor, as the core component driving the refrigerant cycle, directly affects the energy efficiency and stability of the system. The compressor converts mechanical energy into thermodynamic energy to drive the refrigerant to flow in a closed circulation system. According to structural forms, common chiller compressors can be divided into three types: centrifugal, screw, and piston, each with distinct operating mechanisms. Taking the centrifugal compressor as an example, its core component is the impeller, which accelerates and compresses gas through centrifugal force generated by high-speed rotation. Meanwhile, the diffuser converts kinetic energy into pressure energy, ultimately achieving the pressurization of the refrigerant. This compression method features few moving parts, high reliability, and obvious efficiency advantages, making it particularly suitable for industrial scenarios with large cooling demand22. The screw compressor realizes the suction, compression, and discharge of gas through the meshing of male and female rotors. Its rotor structure design enables a continuous and stable compression process, which can adapt to working conditions with large load fluctuations. In contrast, the piston compressor compresses gas through the reciprocating motion of the piston. Although it has a simple structure and low cost, it produces large vibrations and noise during operation and is gradually being replaced by more efficient models.

The operating status of the compressor in chillers needs to be monitored and adjusted through key parameters to ensure efficient operation and stability of the system. The suction temperature and pressure of the compressor directly affect the thermodynamic state of the refrigerant. Excessively low suction temperature may cause liquid refrigerant to enter the compressor, triggering a “liquid hammer” phenomenon, which can cause mechanical damage in severe cases. Excessively high discharge temperature, however, may accelerate the deterioration of lubricating oil and reduce equipment life. In actual operation, sensors are used to monitor the compressor’s suction and discharge temperatures and pressures in real-time. Besides, dynamic adjustments are made in combination with the inlet and outlet temperatures and pressures of the cooling water system. When the cooling water temperature in the condenser rises, the condensing pressure increases accordingly, and the compressor’s power consumption will increase. At this time, it is necessary to reduce the condensing temperature by adjusting the rotation speed of the cooling tower fan or increasing the cooling water flow, thereby maintaining the economical operation of the system.

The LSTM energy efficiency prediction model

Traditional autoregressive or mechanistic models often assume that working conditions remain constant within a short time window. This makes it difficult to capture slow drifts lasting hours or even days, nor can they utilize implicit common laws across units. This study introduces LSTM as the core engine for chiller energy efficiency prediction within a unified framework to balance prediction accuracy and interpretability. Through gated memory units, the model selectively retains and forgets historical information, enabling it to depict both transient shocks and long-term evolution at a minute-level sampling frequency. The LSTM neural network can acquire information from long-distance data, and its structure is revealed in Fig. 3. A single-layer LSTM consists of a single recurrent structure, and the number of self-updates required for the single recurrent structure is determined by the dimension of the input data and the number of recursions. An RNN can be regarded as several identical basic units connected, and each basic unit can transmit information to the next basic unit. The input gate of LSTM determines how much new information is added to the cell; the forget gate controls whether information at each moment is forgotten; and the output gate determines whether information is output at each moment.Fig. 3The basic structural units of LSTM.

The basic structural units of LSTM.

At the input end, the model does not directly feed raw sensor readings into the network but first passes them through a lightweight physical embedding layer. This layer applies mass and energy conservation equations to transform raw operational variables, including chilled water flow, cooling water inlet temperature, evaporator approach temperature, and compressor current. The conversion yields physically meaningful intermediate features such as refrigeration-specific power consumption, heat exchange temperature difference efficiency index, and real-time load rate24–26. This step compresses the input dimension and reduces the risk of gradient oscillation caused by different dimensions, while providing a bridge for subsequent interpretable analysis. The embedded feature sequence is fed into a two-layer stacked LSTM, with each layer having a hidden state dimension of 128. The activation function is tanh, and the gate activation uses sigmoid to ensure that gradients remain stable during backpropagation across hundreds of time steps.

Energy consumption prediction of multiple chillers by fusing LSTM with GCN

In actual scenarios where multiple chillers operate in parallel, the instantaneous power of any unit depends on local variables such as its load rate, evaporation temperature, and condensation temperature; it also relies on the impact of adjacent units’ start-stop status and resistance distribution of chilled water loops27–29. The traditional method of modeling each unit individually treats each unit as an isolated node, ignoring these cross-unit and cross-medium coupling effects. This leads to a quick degradation of model accuracy when the system topology or load distribution changes30–32. To incorporate both spatial correlation and time-series evolution into a unified framework, this section proposes an energy consumption prediction method that deeply fuses LSTM with GCN. GCN is responsible for encoding the heterogeneous graph composed of units, pipe networks, and cooling towers into low-dimensional topological embeddings at each moment. In contrast, LSTM performs dynamic evolution modeling of multi-step energy consumption sequences based on these embeddings. The GCN-LSTM structure constructed in this study is shown in Fig. 4. In order to strengthen the contribution of key spatiotemporal features, a spatiotemporal attention layer is added after the feature fusion module. Spatial attention calculates the importance weight of each chiller node and weights the spatial features. Time attention calculates the importance weight of the historical time step and updates the LSTM hidden state by weight.Fig. 4The GCN-LSTM structure.

The GCN-LSTM structure.

In this study, the construction of the refrigeration system topology graph G = (V, E) is based on the physical topology and real-time operating states of the cooling system. The node set V comprises 13 core devices: five chillers (Chiller 1–5), three chilled water pumps (CHWP 1–3), three cooling water pumps (CWP 1–3), and two groups of cooling towers (CT 1–2). To handle heterogeneous nodes, the features of all nodes are mapped onto a uniform feature space. The initial feature vector of each node contains the real-time operating parameters of the corresponding device; for different types of equipment, missing parameter positions are filled with zeros.

The edge set E is constructed according to the following rules: Physical connection edges are established based on the topology of the chilled water and cooling water pipelines. If a direct hydraulic connection exists between two devices, an undirected edge is created. For instance, physical edges exist between a chiller and its corresponding chilled water pump, cooling water pump, and cooling tower. Load distribution edges are based on the load allocation relationships among parallel chillers. If two chillers jointly undertake the cooling load within the current control cycle, a directed edge is established, directed from the primary device to the auxiliary device.

The dynamic update mechanism of the graph employs an “event-triggered + periodic refresh” strategy. Event-triggered updates are initiated immediately to reconstruct the graph structure when topological changes occur, such as equipment start-stop actions, valve state alterations, or pump group switching. Such topological change detection is based on abrupt variations in equipment status signals and valve position signals. In the absence of topological change events, the periodic refresh update recalculates edge weights every 30 min (consistent with the prediction cycle) to reflect the dynamic evolution of load distribution within the raw records. The LSTM hidden state ht is mapped to the energy consumption prediction of a single chiller through a fully connected layer:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{t}^{(n)} = W_{y} \cdot h_{t}^{(n)} + b_{y} (n = 1,2, \ldots ,N)$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{t}^{(n)}$$\end{document} denotes the predicted energy consumption of the nth chiller at time t; Wy refers to the weight of the fully connected layer.

The neighboring node set of GCN is unordered and variable, and its comparison with traditional convolution is demonstrated in Fig. 6. The spatial graph convolution operator is defined according to the spatial relationship between a node and its neighboring node set. A simple calculation method is to average the features of the current node and its neighboring node set. The graph is represented as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G = \left\{ {V,E} \right\}$$\end{document}, where V and E are the sets of nodes and edges. Through sampling, the neighboring node sets can be obtained, and then a function is selected to aggregate the features of adjacent nodes within the node’s neighborhood.

GCN, as a type of convolutional neural network, can work directly on graphs and utilize the structural information of graphs. It solves the problem of classifying nodes (such as documents) in graphs (such as citation networks), where only a small number of nodes have labels33. To apply to graph-structured data, spatial-domain-based GCN uses a random walk method to select fixed adjacent points for each node in a probabilistic manner. The specific steps are as follows:

For given graph-structured data with a similarity matrix S and a degree matrix D, the probability transition matrix of random walks is defined as P:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P{ = }D^{ - 1} S$$\end{document}

The adjacency matrix is normalized, and the transition matrix is defined as I. Next, the transition expectation of random walks is given as:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O^{0} = I$$\end{document}21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O^{1} = I + P$$\end{document}22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O^{k} = \sum\nolimits_{i = 0}^{k} {P^{k} }$$\end{document}

Next, the size is ordered according to the probability expectation of being selected, and the adjacent point structure is convoluted in one dimension.

Experimental design and performance evaluation

Experimental materials

This study selects the cooling station of a large M data center as the experimental object. The station is equipped with 5 units of 800 RT variable-frequency centrifugal chillers operating in parallel. The designed supply and return temperatures of chilled water are 7 °C /12 °C, and those of cooling water are 32 °C/37 °C. The system is configured with 3 variable-frequency chilled water pumps, 3 variable-frequency cooling water pumps, and 2 sets of two-speed cooling towers.

To construct a comprehensive dataset, operational data were collected from March 1, 2023, to October 31, 2023, with a sampling interval of 1 min. To define the data scale, three key data units are established: a time step represents a specific moment of data sampling, with the total number of time steps during the collection period amounting to 351,360 (244 days × 24 h × 60 min). Raw records refer to the original numerical values from a single sensor on a single device at a single time step. A sample is defined as a multi-dimensional vector composed of all sensor readings from all devices at the same time step, representing a complete system snapshot.

The monitoring system comprises 13 core devices (five chillers, three chilled water pumps, three cooling water pumps, and two groups of cooling towers). Each device is equipped with an average of 28 sensors, totaling 364 sensors (13 × 28). Consequently, the maximum potential number of raw records is 127,895,040 (351,360 time steps × 364 sensors), approximately 128 million. After excluding invalid data caused by sensor failures, 343,630 valid samples (multi-dimensional data rows) were obtained for model development, representing a sample validity rate of 98.2%. To verify the cross-industry transfer performance of the model, two external validation datasets were selected. The manufacturing validation set was obtained from the cooling system of an automobile manufacturing plant, containing operational data of three 800RT centrifugal chillers from January to December 2023. With a sampling interval of 10 min, this dataset comprises 43 million raw records. The marine power system validation set was derived from the central cooling system logbooks of the “Ocean Star” container ship, recording 5-min-level energy consumption data under variable load and sea conditions from March to September 2023, totaling 18 million raw records.

Regarding the issue of missing data due to sensors being offline, linear interpolation was employed for filling. If the continuous missing duration exceeded 5 min, the corresponding samples were marked as invalid and the entire rows were excluded. For outliers exceeding physical thresholds, replacement was performed using the historical median of the specific sensor. Statistics indicated that outliers caused by instantaneous sensor failures (exceeding physical thresholds) accounted for 1.23% of the raw records, while samples excluded due to continuous missing durations exceeding 5 min accounted for 1.79%.

Two transfer strategies were evaluated for the two external validation datasets. A fine-tuning transfer strategy was applied to the manufacturing validation set. Since the equipment types in this scenario are similar to the data center cooling station used in this study (both being centrifugal chillers), but the sampling intervals differ, the original 10-min interval data were first resampled to the 1-min interval consistent with the source domain. Subsequently, the pre-trained model weights from the source domain were used as initialization to fine-tune the model on 10% of the manufacturing validation set (approximately 4.3 million raw records), with the remaining 90% reserved for testing. During the fine-tuning process, the GCN layer parameters were frozen to retain the topological feature extraction capability. Only the LSTM and fully connected layers were fine-tuned, with the learning rate set to one-tenth of the initial learning rate (0.00001) over 20 epochs.

A zero-shot transfer strategy was adopted for the marine power system validation set. Given the differences between the central cooling system in this scenario and the chillers used in this study, as well as the 5-min sampling interval, the pre-trained model from the source domain was directly applied to this validation set without any fine-tuning. To unify the time windows, the 5-min interval data were upsampled to a 1-min interval through interpolation. This ensured that the input sequence length T = 60 corresponded to 5 h of historical observation data, whereas T = 60 in the source domain corresponded to 1 h. This adjustment accurately covered the typical cycle of ship operating condition variations, ensuring the comparability of the time scales for the prediction tasks using the available raw records.

Experimental environment

The hardware configuration of this study is as follows. Central Processing Unit (CPU): 2 × Intel Xeon Platinum 8380, featuring 40 cores and 80 threads, with a base frequency of 2.3 GHz and a boost frequency of 3.4 GHz; GPU: 2 × NVIDIA RTX A6000 (48 gigabytes (GB) video memory/card), instead of the RTX A2000 with only 12 GB; memory: 64 GB DDR4-3200. Data parallelism is implemented across the two cards with a batch size of 512. The peak memory usage per card reaches 38 GB (48 GB of video memory for RTX A6000 is sufficient). The total training duration is approximately 72 h. Software environment: Ubuntu 22.04, Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.1, CUDA 12.2, DGL 1.1. The training process uses dual-card data parallelism, with a peak memory usage of 38 GB per card. All experiments are completed in Docker containers, which ensures the consistency and isolation of the experimental environment while facilitating the reproduction of results. On the NVIDIA T4 GPU, the inference latency of the GCN-LSTM model is 28 ms per sample, enabling the processing of 35.7 samples per second. On the edge computing device (NVIDIA Jetson AGX Xavier), the inference latency is 85 ms per sample, with a processing speed of 11.8 samples per second. This meets the requirements of industrial real-time control (< 100 ms). In an industrial server environment, the model can be seamlessly integrated with existing control systems without additional hardware investment.

In this manner, experimental variables were precisely controlled to verify the effectiveness of the proposed GCN-LSTM architecture. The study utilized 128 million raw records and 8000 h of simulation data to train and test the model. Consequently, the performance of the GCN-LSTM model in enhancing the energy efficiency prediction accuracy for multiple parallel chillers was validated.

Parameters setting

For the GCN-LSTM model designed in this study, the graph convolution layers are set to 2, with each layer having an output dimension of 64. The aggregation function is a weighted sum, and the neighborhood order k = 2; the LSTM part is a 2-layer stack, with the hidden state set to 128 and the gradient clipping threshold set to 1.0. The loss function selects Huber + physical consistency regularization (weight λ = 0.1); the optimizer selects AdamW, with an initial learning rate of 0.0001. The batch size is 512, and the epochs are 100. The input sequence length, namely the time window size of the LSTM, is set to T = 60. This setting means the model predicts energy consumption values for the next 15, 30, or 60 min using historical operational data from the previous 60 min; this corresponds to a total of 60 × 28-dimensional features. This length is determined through cross-validation: when T = 60, the model achieves the lowest RMSE (Root Mean Square Error) on both the training and test sets, while balancing the capture of long- and short-term dependencies and computational efficiency.

Physical consistency regularization is designed to constrain the model’s prediction results to comply with the basic thermodynamic laws of chiller operation; it includes energy conservation and COP constraints, thereby avoiding abnormal predictions that violate physical common sense. Its expression is as follows:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R(\theta ) = \lambda \cdot (R_{COP} + R_{energy} )$$\end{document}θ represents all trainable parameters of the model; λ = 0.1 is the regularization weight, which is optimized via grid search; RCOP denotes the COP constraint term; Renergy stands for the energy conservation constraint term.

The model adopts a total loss function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathop L\nolimits_{total}$$\end{document} that combines Huber loss with physical consistency regularization:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathop L\nolimits_{total} (\theta ) = \frac{1}{N \cdot M}\sum\nolimits_{n = 1}^{N} {\sum\nolimits_{t = 1}^{M} {L_{Huber} } } \left( {\hat{y}_{t}^{(n)} ,y_{t}^{(n)} } \right) + R(\theta )$$\end{document}

LHuber is the Huber loss.

The total loss function is the weighted sum of Huber loss and physical consistency regularization; its complete calculation is presented as follows:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathop L\nolimits_{total} (\theta ) = L_{Huber} + \lambda \cdot L_{Phys} \quad (\lambda = 0.1).$$\end{document}

For single-chiller prediction, the loss function only calculates the Huber loss and physical consistency regularization term for that specific device (N = 1). For parallel prediction of multiple chillers, a device-wise loss averaging strategy is adopted, where the total loss is the arithmetic mean of the losses of all individual devices. This processing method ensures that the model optimizes the prediction accuracy of each device in a balanced manner, avoiding loss bias caused by differences in device capacity.

The hyperparameter tuning tool used is Optuna 3.3.0, employing the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampling algorithm. The tuning parameter ranges include learning rate (1e−5 to 1e−3), LSTM hidden layer dimension (64 to 256), number of GCN layers (1 to 3), attention mechanism hidden layer dimension (32 to 128), and batch size (128 to 1024). The normalization method applied is Z-score standardization, as follows:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{norm} = \frac{{x - \mu_{train} }}{{\sigma_{train} }}$$\end{document}μtrain and σtrain are the mean and standard deviation of the training set.

The baseline models selected in this study are: AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA); Support Vector Regression (SVR), LSTM, CNN-LSTM, Transformer, and GCN-GRU. All deep learning models have a unified input feature dimension of 28, and hyperparameters are optimized through Optuna Bayesian optimization. All models are run 5 times with different random seeds (seed = 123, 456, 789, 1011, 1213), and the results are reported as mean ± standard deviation. A two-tailed t-test with a significance level of α = 0.05 is conducted to compare the test set’s RMSE and MAPE between the GCN-LSTM and the optimal baseline model.

To verify the independent contribution of physics-consistency regularization in reducing abnormal spikes and to exclude potential confounding effects from data preprocessing methods, a comparative experiment on preprocessing strategies was designed. This experiment aimed to address two key questions: (1) whether the effectiveness of physics-based regularization in reducing abnormal spikes remains consistent across different preprocessing methods; (2) whether the median replacement method adopted in this study achieves a reasonable balance between simplicity and effectiveness.

The experiment compared three preprocessing methods: (1) median replacement (the method adopted in this study); (2) forward filling; and (3) model-based imputation based on K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN). For KNN imputation, k = 5 nearest neighbors were selected, and missing values were estimated using a weighted average based on Euclidean distance. While keeping other conditions strictly constant (including model architecture, hyperparameters, and training epochs), the GCN-LSTM model (with physics-based regularization) and its ablation version (without physics-based regularization) were trained respectively. The RMSE and the frequency of abnormal spikes were compared across all combinations. The frequency of abnormal spikes is defined as the proportion of samples where the absolute prediction error exceeds the threshold of 3σ (σ represents the standard deviation of prediction errors). This metric directly reflects the frequency of violent fluctuations in the model output that violate physical laws within the raw records.

The experimental results are presented in Table 6. Across the three preprocessing strategies, the frequency of abnormal spikes for the model without physics-based regularization was 5.7%, 5.9%, and 5.4%, respectively. Upon incorporating physics-based regularization, these figures decreased to 3.6%, 3.8%, and 3.5%, representing reductions of 36.8%, 35.6%, and 35.2%, all exceeding 35%. Paired t-tests indicated that the reductions in spike frequency brought by physics-based regularization were statistically significant (p < 0.01) under all three preprocessing methods. Discussion

The multi-chiller energy consumption prediction model integrating LSTM and GCN proposed in this study substantially improves prediction accuracy and robustness under complex working conditions through temporal-spatial dual modeling. This method breaks through the limitations of traditional single models in capturing temporal dynamics and spatial dependencies while providing a new technical path for multi-equipment collaborative optimization. First, by introducing a physical embedding layer, raw sensor data is converted into intermediate features with clear physical meanings. This design reduces the input dimension and markedly enhances the interpretability of the model. In contrast, the purely data-driven model proposed by Umoga et al.34 lacks explicit modeling of thermodynamic principles, leading to large fluctuations in prediction results during load mutation scenarios. Second, the ablation experiments of GCN-LSTM further verify the importance of spatial modelling. When the GCN module is removed, the model degenerates into an independent LSTM, and its prediction error increases remarkably. After adding physical consistency regularization, the frequency of abnormal spikes decreases by 37%. Although Gui et al.35 proposed a Transformer model based on the attention mechanism, it did not incorporate physical constraints, resulting in high prediction deviations under low-load conditions. In addition, this study solves the problem of scarce on-site data through a transfer learning strategy, enabling the model to maintain high accuracy even with only a small amount of measured data.

From a theoretical perspective, this study combines GCN and LSTM organically for the first time. It constructs a unified framework that can simultaneously capture the spatial structure of cooling station topology and the time-series characteristics of fault evolution. This enriches the theoretical system of fault diagnosis for complex networks. From a viewpoint of management, the model markedly improves the accuracy and efficiency of fault diagnosis. It can greatly shorten the fault troubleshooting time, reduce operation and maintenance costs, and enhance the reliability and stability of power systems. Compared with traditional methods, it has obvious advantages in diagnosis accuracy and diagnosis speed, providing strong support for the power systems’ safe and stable operation. Alshareef et al.36 attempted to apply deep reinforcement learning in ship power systems. However, they did not integrate temporal-spatial dual modeling. In the future, they can draw on the GCN-LSTM architecture of this study to further improve the intelligent control level of complex systems.

Most existing methods37,38 construct graph structures based on abstract correlations, whereas this study generates dynamic graphs through the actual chilled water network topology and load distribution relationships. Experiments show that the prediction error of static graph models increases by 12.7% when topology changes occur; this verifies the importance of physical topology for model robustness. Although Zhang et al.39 introduced physical constraints, these constraints were mostly post-hoc corrections or simple linear penalty terms. This study transforms the laws of mass and energy conservation into intermediate features and directly optimizes model parameters through regularization terms with thermodynamic constraints. Experiments demonstrate that removing physical regularization increases the frequency of abnormal predictions by 37%, proving its effectiveness in reducing non-physical outputs. Existing cross-scenario tests40,41 mostly rely on shared data distributions. In contrast, this study generates multi-condition simulated data via digital twins and achieves zero-shot transfer through temporal isolation and feature isolation. Experimental results show that the model reduces RMSE by 17.2% and 15.6% in manufacturing and marine scenarios, respectively; it expands the application boundaries of STGNN in the field of industrial energy efficiency.

Conclusion

Research contribution

The GCN-LSTM fusion model proposed in this study achieves topology-aware energy consumption prediction and optimization for multiple parallel chillers through the synergistic innovation of physics embedding, spatial modeling, and transfer learning. Topology-aware optimization refers to dynamic graph modeling based on the cooling network, which simultaneously characterizes the physical connection relationships and load distribution correlations between devices to accurately predict energy consumption under various topological configurations. This approach further guides load distribution decisions to minimize total system energy consumption while satisfying operational constraints. Experimental results demonstrate that the model significantly outperforms traditional methods in terms of prediction accuracy, interpretability, and cross-scenario adaptability.

The primary contributions are as follows: First, the organic integration of GCN and LSTM establishes a unified framework capable of capturing the spatiotemporal features of equipment topological structures, surmounting the limitations of traditional methods in modeling spatial dependencies. Second, through the physics-embedding layer and consistency regularization, thermodynamic laws are integrated into the model training process, effectively enhancing the physical interpretability and reliability of the prediction results. Third, the utilization of simulation data generated by digital twins along with transfer learning strategies addresses the scarcity of labeled raw records in practical scenarios. Furthermore, the cross-industry generalizability of the model architecture was validated on two external validation sets: manufacturing and marine power systems. This study provides a novel technical path for energy efficiency optimization and intelligent control in industrial refrigeration systems.

Future works and research limitations

The experimental data of this study mainly come from the cooling station system of large data centers. Although this scenario has a high sampling frequency and rich equipment parameters, its data characteristics have specific industry attributes. In this regard, future work can be deepened from two aspects. First, a graph structure adaptive update algorithm based on equipment operation status is designed. When equipment failure or a topology change occurs, the algorithm automatically adjusts the graph structure to reduce manual intervention. The specific implementation plan includes topology weight calculation based on equipment operation status, dynamic adjustment of graph structure based on anomaly detection, and a historical data-based topology prediction mechanism. Second, multi-source heterogeneous data such as temperature, pressure, and vibration are integrated to construct a more comprehensive feature space. The specific methods include designing a multi-modal feature extraction module, developing a cross-modal feature fusion mechanism, and establishing an adaptive weight assignment strategy for multi-modal data.
