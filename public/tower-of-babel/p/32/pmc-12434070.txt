Challenges in the Treatment of Hepatorenal Syndrome–Acute Kidney Injury: A US Chart Review of Treatment Patterns and Survival Outcomes
Authors: Arun J Sanyal, K Rajender Reddy, Kimberly A Brown, Charles S Landis, Giuseppe Cullaro, Xingyue Huang, Sneha S Kelkar, Rutika Raina, Shelby Corman, Nehemiah Kebede, Patrick Edmundson, Khurram Jamil, Andrew S Allegretti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTreatments for hepatorenal syndrome with acute kidney injury (HRS‐AKI) that are not FDA‐approved have been widely used in the United States (US) with variable outcomes. This study describes the practice patterns, outcomes, and healthcare utilization around vasopressor use before terlipressin approval in 2022.MethodsA retrospective chart review study was conducted at 10 US medical centers, assessing adult patients diagnosed with HRS‐AKI between 2016 and 2019. The primary outcome was treatment response (change in serum creatinine [SCr] from the day of vasopressor treatment initiation to Day 14/vasopressor discontinuation). Secondary outcomes included overall and transplant‐free survival, treatment patterns, and healthcare resource use.ResultsOf the 198 eligible patients, 129 and 69 had mild/moderate (SCr < 5 mg/dL, acute‐on‐chronic liver failure [ACLF] ≤ 2) and severe disease (SCr ≥ 5 mg/dL, ACLF > 2), respectively. The mean age was 57 years; 52.5% were males, and 71.2% were White. Alcohol‐associated cirrhosis (53.5%) was the most common cause of cirrhosis. All 198 patients had a physician‐diagnosed HRS‐AKI, and only 30.3% met all International Club of Ascites (ICA)‐HRS criteria. Most patients (85.4%) initiated treatment with midodrine and octreotide for a median of 7 days. The overall response rate (n = 157) was 20.3%. Median (95% CI) overall and transplant‐free survival from vasopressor initiation was 48 (32–81) and 28 (19–36) days. Notably, 33.8% of patients died during hospitalization, and 31.3% required renal replacement therapy.ConclusionBefore 2022, hospitalized HRS‐AKI patients experienced suboptimal treatment response with off‐label treatments and poor survival. There remains an unmet need for safe and effective non‐transplant treatments for hospitalized HRS‐AKI patients in the United States.Keywords: acute kidney injury, chart review, cirrhosis, retrospective, vasopressors

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) affects up to 50% of patients hospitalized with cirrhosis [1]. Hepatorenal syndrome with AKI (HRS‐AKI), previously referred to as HRS type 1 (HRS‐1) [2], is a unique subset of AKI representing about 11%–20% of AKI episodes in patients with cirrhosis [3]. HRS‐AKI is characterized by a severe and sudden decline in renal function in patients with advanced cirrhosis [4, 5]. In such patients, the estimated incidence of HRS‐AKI is 18% by 1 year and 39% after 5 years [6].

Several published studies using US hospital administrative databases have highlighted the substantial clinical and economic burden associated with HRS. Over a 10‐year period, the incidence of HRS hospitalizations increased from 22 864 in 2008 to 42 985 in 2018 [7]. HRS patients have high in‐hospital mortality rates (26%–37%) with only 50% surviving to 1 month [7, 8]. A retrospective cohort study published in 2023, utilizing data from 11 US hospital networks, revealed comparable 90‐day mortality rates for patients hospitalized with HRS (49.0%) or acute tubular necrosis (ATN) (52.7%) [9]. Moreover, approximately one‐third of patients with HRS are readmitted within 30 days of discharge [8]. Mean 90‐day post‐hospitalization costs for HRS patients are high ($157 665 for commercially insured and $48 322 for Medicare) [10]. These database analyses are limited by a lack of clinical and treatment details.

Before the availability of terlipressin in the United States, HRS‐AKI therapeutic options had been limited to off‐label use of midodrine with octreotide, norepinephrine, and vasopressin, in combination with albumin [4]. Practice patterns can vary widely according to local standards of care, and the effectiveness of these agents is poorly understood in real‐world settings. There is a lack of real‐world studies assessing current treatment patterns and their impact on HRS‐related outcomes. To establish a baseline against which future studies of terlipressin can be evaluated, we conducted a multicenter retrospective medical chart review of HRS‐AKI patient characteristics, treatment patterns, clinical outcomes, and healthcare resource use during a 4‐year period before terlipressin's availability in the United States.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective medical chart review study was conducted among adult patients hospitalized for HRS‐AKI from January 1, 2016 to June 30, 2019, at 10 US medical centers. Participating physicians (or their designated staff) reviewed the medical charts of patients hospitalized with a clinical diagnosis of HRS‐AKI, from hospital admission (otherwise referred to as index hospitalization) to 90 days post‐discharge or until death, and extracted data from these charts using electronic case report forms. The case report form was hosted on a secure website. The New England Institutional Review Board approved the study and granted a waiver of informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with legal and regulatory requirements and followed accepted research practices, as outlined in the guidelines issued by the International Society for Pharmacoepidemiology, the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research, and the Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America.

Selection Criteria

Physician Selection

One physician investigator was recruited from each of the 10 large teaching hospitals and/or transplant centers. Each investigator or a supervised designee (e.g., nurse, other physician) collected data on up to 25 patients with HRS‐AKI. The study sponsor, consultants, and study team personnel not responsible for study site recruitment and data collection were blinded to the participating physicians. Physician investigators agreed to all study rules and procedures (e.g., resolution of data queries) and received an honorarium for their time and effort.

Patient Selection

Charts were selected from electronic medical records by site personnel and determined eligible for inclusion if patients were at least 18 years of age and had a physician‐diagnosed episode of hepatorenal syndrome–acute kidney injury (HRS‐AKI) during hospitalization between January 1, 2016, and June 30, 2019. To obtain a representative sample of patients, physicians were prompted to select a patient based on a randomly generated letter as the first letter of their first name. Although specific criteria for HRS diagnosis were not required, we later captured data on the use of International Classification of Disease (ICD) 9th revision (ICD‐9) or 10th revision code (ICD‐10) of HRS (572.4 and/or K76.7), or acute renal failure, unspecified (N17.9) with other and unspecified cirrhosis of the liver (K74.6) or ICA‐HRS. Other inclusion criteria were patients who were enrolled in any clinical trial during hospitalization or had a hospital stay of 2 days or shorter (as an albumin challenge of 48 h was required before diagnosing HRS‐AKI) were excluded from the study. Patients with incomplete lab data for assessment of treatment response (defined as a SCr on the day of vasopressor initiation and discontinuation) were also excluded.

Study Variables

Data were collected from index hospitalization to 90 days post‐discharge and included demographics, baseline clinical characteristics, treatment history, interventions received, and healthcare resource use pre‐ and post‐index hospitalization. Initial vasopressor therapy was identified as the earliest vasopressor treatment administered during the index hospitalization, and combination vasopressor therapy was defined as the receipt of ≥ 2 vasopressors with an overlap in treatment duration.

HRS‐AKI treatment response was based on change in serum creatinine (SCr) concentration from baseline (defined as SCr on Day 1 of vasopressor treatment) to Day 14 or vasopressor discontinuation, whichever came first. Response was evaluated from the start of vasopressor treatment among a subset of patients (the response cohort) who were required to have a baseline SCr > 1.5 mg/dL and a SCr value available at vasopressor discontinuation or within the 2 days before vasopressor discontinuation. For patients who underwent renal replacement therapy (RRT), liver transplantation, or transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt placement (TIPS) during index hospitalization, the SCr value obtained on the day before the intervention was used to assess response. If the intervention occurred within 2 days of treatment initiation, the patient was excluded from the response analysis because the response could not be sufficiently attributed to vasopressor treatment without the confounding effects of the intervention. Response criteria were defined based on ICA‐HRS criteria [11] with input from key opinion leaders. Treatment response was defined as complete response if SCr decreased from Day 1 of vasopressor treatment to a final (Day 14 or vasopressor discontinuation, whichever came first) level of ≤ 1.5 mg/dL, as partial response if there was a ≥ 30% decrease in SCr from Day 1 of vasopressor treatment to a final (Day 14 or vasopressor discontinuation, whichever came first) level of > 1.5 mg/dL, and as no response if there was < 30% SCr decrease from Day 1 of vasopressor treatment. Overall response was defined as either a complete or a partial response.

Other study outcomes included overall and transplant‐free survival, HRS‐AKI treatment patterns including vasopressor and albumin use during the index hospitalization, and healthcare resource use including length of index hospital stay (i.e., overall and intensive care unit), procedures performed during the index hospitalization, and healthcare resources used following discharge from the index hospitalization (i.e., subsequent hospitalizations, emergency department visits, and procedures).

Patients were categorized by underlying liver disease severity. The mild to moderate disease cohort included patients with baseline SCr < 5 mg/dL and acute‐on‐chronic liver failure (ACLF) grade ≤ 2; the severe disease cohort included patients with a baseline SCr of ≥ 5 mg/dL or an ACLF grade 3.

Statistical Analyses

Analysis of patient demographics, clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, clinical outcomes, and healthcare resource utilization was descriptive in nature and conducted for the overall population and by disease severity. Categorical variables were summarized using counts and percentages; continuous variables were summarized using mean, standard deviation (SD), median, range, and interquartile range (IQR). Group differences were tested using Pearson's chi‐square test or Fisher's test for categorical variables and Student's t‐test or equivalent nonparametric tests for continuous variables. Time to response, overall survival, and transplant‐free survival were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methods. All inferences were made assuming a two‐sided test with an alpha of 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Physician investigators collected data for 200 patients. One patient did not have treatment or outcomes data, and one patient did not have a final SCr value; both were excluded from the final analysis (Figure 1). The diagnosis of HRS‐AKI was based on physicians' confirmation in all patients, while an ICD code for HRS‐AKI was reported in only 36.4% of these patients. All 6 criteria for ICA‐HRS [11] were met by 30.3% of patients, while 49.5% met at least 5 criteria, and 73.7% met at least 4 criteria. The most commonly met criteria were the presence of cirrhosis and ascites (94.9%) and SCr > 1.5 mg/dL (86.4%). Among all patients, the mean age at index hospitalization was 57.0 years (SD: 11.7), 52.5% were men, and 71.2% were White (Table 1). For most patients (85.9%), this was their first known HRS‐AKI diagnosis. Alcohol‐associated cirrhosis (53.5%) and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis or nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (20.7%) were the most common causes of cirrhosis. Diuretic therapy (46.0%) and large‐volume paracentesis (40.4%) were the most common precipitating events for HRS‐AKI. Ascites at index hospitalization were present in 90.9% of patients.

Almost two‐thirds of patients (65.1%, n = 129) had mild to moderate disease at baseline; the remaining had severe disease (34.8%, n = 69). Patients with mild to moderate disease were older (mean age: 58.1 vs. 54.9 years), a greater proportion had Medicare (46.5% vs. 21.7%), and a smaller proportion had commercial insurance (19.4% vs. 36.3%) or Medicaid (14.0% vs. 26.1%). The underlying cause of cirrhosis was metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis or metabolic dysfunction‐associated fatty liver disease in a greater proportion of patients with mild to moderate than severe disease (25.6% vs. 11.5%).

A total of 169 (85.4%) patients were initially treated with midodrine and octreotide, 4.0% (n = 8) with norepinephrine, and 9.6% (n = 21) with a different vasopressor regimen (Table S1). The median duration of initial vasopressor therapy among patients treated with midodrine and octreotide was 7 days (IQR: 3–13), while those treated with norepinephrine were 4 days (IQR: 3–9). Nearly all (98.5%) patients received albumin (median total dose: 50.0 g; IQR: 33.2–66.7). Most patients (64.1%) received combination midodrine and octreotide therapy, while 34.8% received midodrine and octreotide in combination with other vasopressor therapies anytime during hospitalization. Due to the descriptive nature of this study, no formal comparisons were made between study variables. However, no numerical differences were also seen in initial or subsequent vasopressor therapy, duration of vasopressor therapy, or albumin administration and dose among patients with mild to moderate disease compared with those with severe disease (Table S1).

Out of 198 patients, 157 patients were included in the response cohort. The overall response rate was 20.3%, with 14.6% of patients experiencing a complete response and 5.7% a partial response (Figure 2). Median (IQR) SCr was 2.7 (2.1–3.6) mg/dL at baseline and 2.8 (1.9–4.2) mg/dL at end‐of‐treatment/Day 14. The median (IQR) time from initiation of vasopressor treatment to response was 5 (3–10) days, with the median time for complete responders being longer than partial responders (CR: 8.0 [6.0–14.0] days vs. PR: 6.0 [3.0–14.0] days). The overall response rate among patients with mild to moderate disease at baseline (n = 104) was 16.3% (CR: 12.5%, PR: 3.8%) versus 28.3% (CR: 18.9%, PR: 9.4%) among patients with severe disease (n = 53).

During the index hospitalization, 33.8% of all patients died, with a significantly smaller proportion of patients with mild to moderate disease than severe disease at baseline dying (24.8% vs. 50.7%; p < 0.001). For all patients, median (95% CI) overall survival from initiation of vasopressor treatment was 48 days (32–81); median overall survival was significantly longer in patients with mild to moderate disease at baseline than in those with severe disease at baseline (64 [47–214] vs. 19 [15–48] days; HR: 0.67 [0.47–0.97]; Figure 3A). The probability (95% CI) of survival for all patients was 70% (64%–77%) at Day 15, 57% (50%–64%) at Day 30, and 45% (38%–53%) at Day 60. From baseline through study end, the probability of survival was higher for patients with mild to moderate disease than for those with severe disease at baseline (Figure 3A).

Twenty‐eight patients (14.1%) received a transplant (liver transplant, n = 26; kidney transplant, n = 1; liver and kidney transplant, n = 1) during the index hospitalization. Median (95% CI) transplant‐free survival from initiation of vasopressor treatment was 28 (19–36) days for all patients; transplant‐free survival was significantly longer for patients with mild to moderate disease compared with severe disease at baseline (38 [28–50] vs. 15 [10–23] days; HR: 0.67 [0.48–0.93]; Figure 3B).

Patients with a length of stay > 300 days or missing data (n = 2) were considered to have a reporting error and were excluded from the initial hospitalization length of stay analysis. Among patients with valid length of stay data (n = 196), median (IQR) length of stay for the index hospitalization was 14 (8–27) days, 15 (9–27) days for those with mild to moderate disease, and 12 (8–23) days for those with severe disease at baseline (Table 2). Out of 198 patients, 22 patients (11.3%) were admitted to the intensive care unit during their initial hospital stay; the median (IQR) duration of these intensive care unit stays was 6 (2–10) days. Sixty‐two patients (31.3%) received RRT, and five patients (2.5%) underwent TIPS placement during the index hospitalization.

Discussion

This retrospective multicenter real‐world study indicates that most patients hospitalized for HRS‐AKI before the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of terlipressin (2016–2019) were initially treated with midodrine and octreotide vasopressor therapy, and that most patients received albumin. However, vasopressor response rates were low (14.6% complete response/HRS reversal), and median overall survival was < 2 months regardless of disease severity. Patients with severe disease at baseline had a higher overall response rate (complete response, 28.3%) than those with mild to moderate disease (16.3%), with a median survival of only 19 days. During the index hospitalization, healthcare resource use was substantial, irrespective of disease severity. An exploratory analysis found that SCr ≥ 5 and hepatic encephalopathy at baseline were associated with lower odds of response (although SCr was not statistically significant, likely due to small sample size), which is consistent with the literature [2, 13].

Diagnosing HRS can be challenging, as there is no specific confirmatory biomarker or direct testing method that can be used. The 198 patients included in this analysis all had physician‐diagnosed HRS‐AKI. However, only 36.4% of patients also had ICD‐9/ICD‐10 codes reported in their charts, and only 30.3% of patients met all six ICA‐HRS criteria. Thus, most patients with HRS‐AKI were identified only using clinical judgment [11]. In real‐world settings, physicians were not consistently applying the ICA‐HRS criteria when treating patients with HRS, and approximately 70% of patients included in this analysis did not meet all six ICA‐HRS criteria [11]. Therefore, it was important to include both categories of patients, as continued utilization of vasoconstrictors like terlipressin is likely in patients who do not always fulfill all HRS diagnostic criteria.

Results from real‐world studies provide additional insights into the treatment of HRS‐AKI preceding terlipressin's approval [9, 14, 15]. Among 249 patients with HRS hospitalized for AKI (identified using ICD‐10 codes for cirrhosis and physician adjudication for HRS‐AKI criteria) in 2019 at 11 different hospital network systems in the United States, 77.1% received vasoconstrictor therapy and 94.8% received albumin during the admission [9]. Many patients (53.2%) required an intensive care admission, 27.2% required mechanical ventilation, and 26.1% required RRT. In‐hospital mortality was 24.5%; 30‐, 60‐, and 90‐day mortality were 36.1%, 45.4%, and 49.0%, respectively. Despite differences in study design and disease state definitions across these studies, all reported heterogeneity in administered vasopressors [15], low treatment complete response rates (15%–41.8%) [9, 14, 15], high healthcare resource utilization (~14 days median length of hospital stay; 53.2% required an intensive care admission, 27.2% required mechanical ventilation, and 26.1% required RRT), [9, 14] and high in‐hospital mortality at 30 (~36%–41%) and 90 days (49%) [9, 14, 15]. Studies evaluating HRS patient outcomes are challenging to interpret due to heterogeneity in study methods and treatment response definitions. These variations make it difficult to compare outcomes across studies and to find consensus when making informed decisions on treatment courses.

Our study serves as “real‐world” historical cohort reference for comparing HRS patient outcomes with more recent data or published cohorts treated after the approval of terlipressin in the United States. While the literature is sparse for evaluating HRS treatment response rates in real‐world clinical settings, the 20% overall response rate reported in our study was similar to that reported in an Italian clinical trial comparing midodrine and octreotide with terlipressin for the treatment of HRS [13]. The response rate, defined as either a complete response (decrease in SCr to ≤ 1.5 mg/dL) or partial response (≥ 50% decrease in baseline SCr to a final value > 1.5 mg/dL), was 29% for the midodrine and octreotide arm versus 70% for terlipressin. A recent meta‐analysis reported HRS reversal rates (defined as a decrease in SCr to 1.5 mg/dL or lower during treatment) of 47.9% for terlipressin‐treated patients and 39.9% for those treated with norepinephrine [14]. Our study could not robustly evaluate the response rate for norepinephrine because it was used in only < 5% of patients. In a recently published real‐world study of terlipressin use among 206 US patients with alcohol‐associated disease and HRS‐AKI, 46.2% experienced HRS reversal after terlipressin, and in‐hospital mortality was 32.5% [15]. These studies demonstrate a substantial difference in the recovery of renal function between midodrine/octreotide or norepinephrine and terlipressin.

Terlipressin, approved in the EU for over 20 years, is currently recommended as the first treatment option by the European Association for the Study of the Liver [4, 16]; however, terlipressin was approved by the US FDA in 2022 based on results from the CONFIRM study showing significantly higher HRS reversal rates in patients receiving terlipressin compared to placebo [17]. An indirect treatment comparison study using real‐world HRS patient data from this US study and UK HRS cohort found that relative to the current US study cohort [9], UK patients treated primarily with terlipressin had higher reversal rates (54.2% vs. 23.6%; defined as achieving SCr ≤ 1.5 mg/dL at any time during the treatment window) and complete response (53.2% vs. 16.9%) [16]. These results corroborate the FDA label recommendation that terlipressin should be used in HRS patients with SCr < 5 mg/dL, ACLF < 3, and without hypoxia, worsening respiratory symptoms, ongoing coronary, peripheral, or mesenteric ischemia.

Most patients in our study, conducted before terlipressin approval, were treated predominantly with midodrine/octreotide as their initial vasopressor (85.4%); the overall complete response rate was only 14.6%. A recent hospital database (Premier) analysis of early terlipressin treatment adoption among 125 HRS‐AKI patients in the United States from 2022 to 2024 found that the majority of patients were still receiving midodrine and octreotide (71.2%) as first line, while only 20.8% received terlipressin first line [17]. Despite later lines of terlipressin, 47.6% of patients with SCr data achieved HRS reversal [18]. This finding reiterates the effectiveness of terlipressin, the need for increased awareness about its efficacy relative to other available HRS treatments, and makes a strong case for incorporating terlipressin into clinical treatment recommendations by US hospital systems.

This real‐world study builds on findings from previous studies [9, 19, 20] and offers valuable insights into the treatment of HRS‐AKI in real‐world clinical practice in the United States. This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective collection of data from patients' medical charts may contain transcription errors and may not fully capture post‐discharge resource use. Second, the captured treatment patterns and clinical characteristics may represent the practices of physicians from large academic hospitals associated with a transplant center, where more severe patients are likely admitted. The potential for selection bias also extends to records being more readily available for more recently diagnosed or surviving patients. Third, most HRS‐AKI patients were identified by clinical judgment rather than by ICD codes, which could indicate high underreporting in studies based solely on the use of ICD codes. Further studies are needed to better understand the challenges in using the ICA‐HRS criteria [11] to substantiate HRS diagnosis in a real‐world clinical setting. Finally, as with any retrospective study involving descriptive analyses, these results should be interpreted as associations rather than as causal relationships.

Conclusions

Our real‐world chart review study revealed that before 2022, hospitalized HRS‐AKI patients experienced suboptimal treatment response with off‐label treatments, primarily midodrine and octreotide, and median survival was poor. In the absence of transplant as an option for many patients, there remains an unmet need for safe and effective FDA‐approved treatments for patients with HRS‐AKI admitted to the US hospital system. Future studies should evaluate real‐world clinical responses and outcomes of terlipressin, the only FDA‐approved pharmacological treatment for adults with HRS‐AKI.
