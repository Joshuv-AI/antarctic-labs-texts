Letter to the Editor: ‘Life expectancy of UK physicians in the early 21st century: An analysis of 1,000 fellows from the Royal College of Physicians’ Munk’s Roll’
Authors: Lucy Williams
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I read the article ‘Life expectancy of UK physicians in the early 21st century: An analysis of 1,000 fellows from the Royal College of Physicians’ Munk’s Roll’1 with interest. I wish to highlight some areas of further discussion around the strength of their suggestions of differing life expectancies.

Firstly, the authors start their possible reasons of these discrepancies by stating that female physicians in the mid-20th century faced limited access to medical education, fewer career development opportunities and workplace discrimination. However, they have not provided an evidence base for this. Therefore, this statement may only be considered as an assumption rather than empirical observation that conclusions can be based upon, therefore weakening their overall argument. The authors have then inferred that chronic stress in female physicians, partly as a result of workplace-based discrimination and domestic responsibilities, may be a reason for their lower life expectancy. Some of the research used as the basis of this argument2, 3 was carried out in the 21st century. Applying these research findings retrospectively should be done with caution as they may not be reflective of mid-20th century female physician populations.

Another area to highlight is regarding the life expectancy differences between physicians with differing countries of primary medical qualification. The authors have described the healthy migrant effect as a possible reason for why physicians from Australasia and North America had longer lifespans than their counterparts. Their suggested possible reasons for physicians from the Indian subcontinent having lower life expectancies include intense service provision and lack of training development. However, the authors have again not used any evidence base to back up their suggestions. It might have been useful to explore the differences of the migrant effect within different populations. There is literature suggesting that immigrants from the Indian subcontinent have a lower mortality than the native UK population, even adjusting for lifestyle factors.4 Although not specific for physicians, this could suggest an avenue of further research as to why Munk’s Roll data do not correspond with these findings.
