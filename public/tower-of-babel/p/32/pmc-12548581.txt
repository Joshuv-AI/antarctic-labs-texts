Prevalence, incidence and risk factors of chronic kidney disease in people with diabetes and hypertension, and the prognosis and kidney function decline in Indonesia: a multicentre cross-sectional study in primary care centres
Authors: Ni Made Hustrini, Endang Susalit, Kuntjoro Harimurti, Ira Susanti Haryoso, Adriana Eunike Legrans, Kirana Damarjati, Sitri Minangsih, Lida Nurhisan, Ma’mun Zaini, Sendy Oktavianti, Sujie Pratiwi, Muhammad Galih Pradesa, Geby Anthony, Dwi WIgati Ratna Sari, Rosa Chandra Purnama, Woro Rengganis Maesya Puspitasari, Monica Juwita Rajagukguk, Merel van Diepen, Joris Rotmans
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo examine chronic kidney disease (CKD) prevalence, incidence, prognosis, kidney function decline and associated risk factors among people with diabetes and/or hypertension.DesignCross-sectional multicentre study.Setting14 primary care centres across Jakarta.ParticipantsAdults (≥18 years) with diabetes and/or hypertension were included. Exclusion criteria were receiving kidney replacement therapy, language barrier, cognitive impairments, refusal to consent and pregnancy. Participants were grouped into three categories: hypertension only, diabetes only and both.InterventionsNone.Primary and secondary outcomesPrimary outcomes included CKD prevalence, incidence, number-needed-to-screen, KDIGO-based prognosis and annual kidney function decline. Secondary outcomes were risk factors for CKD, uncontrolled blood glucose, blood pressure and albuminuria.ResultsA total of 1263 participants were enrolled: 51% had hypertension, 17.6% diabetes and 31.4% both. Mean age: 57.1±10.2 years, 72.2% female and 76% obese. Renin angiotensin aldosterone system inhibitors were prescribed in 32.3%, and only 1.2% used insulin despite a median glycated haemoglobin of 7.5% (IQR: 6.5–9.1). CKD prevalence was 14.8%, with an incidence rate of 9.1 per 100 person-years; number-needed-to-screen was 7. Based on KDIGO criteria, 48.9% were at moderate-to-very high risk of adverse outcomes. Baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate was 80.9 (SE=10.1), declining by 4.7 (SE=9.9) mL/min/1.73 m2 annually. CKD incidence was higher with albuminuria (OR 3.6, p=0.007) in the combined group; older age (OR 4.5, p<0.001), male (OR 2.3, p=0.026) and haematuria (OR 2.5, p=0.034) in the hypertension group and cardiovascular disease (OR 14.9, p=0.004) in the diabetes group.ConclusionsCKD burden is high among people with diabetes and hypertension. Nearly half were at elevated risk despite preserved kidney function, highlighting the need for targeted early screening.

Background

The prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) in Indonesia is increasing, although the exact number of cases remains unknown. The 2023 Indonesian Health Survey (Survei Kesehatan Indonesia, SKI) reports a low CKD prevalence of 0.18%, which is significantly lower than in other countries.1 However, data from the 2020 Indonesian Renal Registry show a rise in the number of haemodialysis patients since the implementation of Universal Health Care in 2014.2 This discrepancy may suggest an underestimation of CKD cases in Indonesia, with many individuals remaining undiagnosed until they develop advanced kidney dysfunction requiring kidney replacement therapy (KRT).

Our recent investigation revealed that diabetic kidney disease is the leading cause of kidney failure in Indonesia, accounting for 27.2% of cases, followed by glomerulonephritis (13%) and hypertension (11.5%).3 The increasing prevalence of diabetes mellitus and hypertension—both major contributors to kidney impairment—has been observed across the Southeast Asian region, including Indonesia.4

Globally, around 537 million people are currently living with diabetes, and this number is projected to rise to 700 million by 2045.5 In Indonesia, the prevalence of diabetes has increased significantly from 5.6 million adults in 2000 to 19.5 million in 2021 and is expected to reach 28.6 million by 2045.5 Indonesia now ranks among the top five countries for diabetes prevalence, second only to China in the Western Pacific region. The age-adjusted prevalence in 2021 was 10.6%, indicating that roughly one in nine adults is affected, with nearly 75% of cases remaining undiagnosed. Diabetes-related mortality in Indonesia is estimated at around 237 000 deaths per year, with an average annual treatment cost of approximately US$323.8 per diagnosed individual.5 The 2023 SKI reported a diabetes prevalence of 11.7%, with an additional 32% of the population in a pre-diabetic state.1

The prevalence of hypertension in Indonesia is notably high. According to the 2023 SKI, 29% of adults are affected.1 A pooled analysis of over 1200 studies reported an even higher overall prevalence of over 35%, with rates of 36% in males and 45% in females.6 7 These figures are higher than those in most Southeast Asian countries, with the exception of Malaysia and Brunei. The high burden of hypertension is a significant concern, as it is a major risk factor for the development of CKD. Moreover, the age-standardised mortality rates for CKD attributed to hypertension in Southeast Asia are substantially higher than the global average–11.1 vs 5.9 deaths per 100 000 individuals.6

As a result, targeting high-risk subgroups—such as people with diabetes or hypertension—for screening may be more cost-effective than screening the general population. CKD is a major risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, making early detection and prevention essential to slowing disease progression, managing comorbidities and enabling timely dialysis initiation.

This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence and incidence of CKD among people with diabetes and/or hypertension, assess CKD prognosis and the decline in kidney function and examine the risk factors associated with CKD development, as well as those related to uncontrolled blood pressure (BP), blood glucose and albuminuria.

This multicentre, cross-sectional study focused on a high-risk CKD population and was conducted from 1 March 2022 to 31 August 2023, across 14 primary care centres in Jakarta. The participating centres included:

Participants

We included participants aged 18 years or older with a documented history of diabetes and/or hypertension, along with complete medical records. Exclusion criteria were those receiving KRT, pregnant women, unable to communicate in Bahasa Indonesia, with cognitive impairments, and those who declined informed consent. Participants were classified into three groups: hypertension only, diabetes only and both hypertension and diabetes.

Coordination was established with the 14 participating centres for participants’ recruitment. Most of the participants were enrolled from the PROLANIS programme (a national chronic disease management programme), which focuses on the management of individuals with hypertension and diabetes. A consecutive sampling technique was used for participant enrolment.

Patient and public involvement

Participants and the public were not involved in the development of this research.

Variables and definitions

A structured interview was performed to gather information on medical history. Diabetes was defined as a prior diagnosis or the use of antidiabetic medications. Hypertension was defined as a history of high BP or current use of antihypertensive medications. CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/minute/1.73 m2, calculated using the CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) 2021 creatinine-based formula.8 Obese was defined as body mass index (BMI) >25 kg/m2 according to BMI Classification for Asia-Pacific.

Uncontrolled blood glucose was defined as glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) >7%, and uncontrolled BP was defined as a systolic BP >140 mm Hg and/or diastolic BP >90 mm Hg. Albuminuria was determined based on the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR) examination, categorised based on the KDIGO 2012 and 2024 classification9 10: normal to mildly increased (uACR <30 mg/g), moderately increased (30–300 mg/g) or severely increased (>300 mg/g). Haematuria was diagnosed when five or more red blood cells per high power field were observed in urinalysis.

Serum creatinine (sCr) values used to calculate eGFR were obtained from collected samples and analysed using a standard enzymatic method on an automated analyser.

Risk factors

Potential risk factors were recorded, including demographic and socioeconomic characteristics such as age (in years), gender (male vs female), level of education, income, current medications, medication adherence, smoking habits, family history of hypertension, diabetes or kidney disease, presence of comorbidities, knowledge of hypertension or diabetes, and duration of hypertension or diabetes.

Clinical data collected for all participants included BP (mm Hg), body weight (kg), body height (cm), (sCr, in mg/dL) and urinalysis. For participants with diabetes, additional assessments included uACR (in mg/g), random blood glucose (in mg/dL) and HbA1c (in %).

Data sources/measurements

Demographic data were collected from medical records. Participants were requested to complete a structured questionnaire to gather information on socioeconomic variables and to assess their knowledge of hypertension and/or diabetes.

Laboratory parameters from the year prior to study visit—including sCr, eGFR and random blood glucose—were also retrieved from medical records.

During the study visit, blood and urine samples were collected. sCr and urinalysis were performed for all participants, while uACR and HbA1c were measured only for participants with diabetes.

Bias

To minimise bias in eGFR estimation, all sCr samples were analysed in a single laboratory using a standardised enzymatic method on an automated analyser.

Study size

The required sample size was calculated based on the reported prevalence rate of diabetes1 and hypertension,7 resulting in an estimated minimum of 350 participants. Details are provided in online supplemental materials.

Quantitative variables

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise all variables. Continuous variables were presented as mean±SD if normally distributed, or median with IQR if not. Categorical variables were expressed as proportions.

Statistical methods

Participants’ characteristics were analysed using univariate analysis.

CKD prevalence was calculated as the proportion of individuals with eGFR <60 mL/minute/1.73 m2 and expressed as percentage. Participants who met the CKD criteria for the first time between 2022 and 2023 were considered incident cases, and the corresponding incidence rate was calculated per 100 patient-year.

The NNS to identify one case of CKD was calculated as:

NNS=1/(CKD prevalence/100).

CKD prognosis was classified according to the KDIGO 2012 and 2024 guidelines,9 10 stratified by eGFR and albuminuria categories. This analysis included only participants with complete data for both eGFR and uACR data.

A linear mixed effects model was used to assess the association between disease subgroup and kidney function decline over time, adjusting for age and gender.

We also investigated the relationship between clinical risk factors, including age, gender, education level, income level, current medications, smoking status, disease subgroup, BMI category, mean arterial pressure, HbA1C, uACR and haematuria, and the following outcomes:

CKD incidence.

CKD incidence.

BP control.

BP control.

Blood glucose control.

Blood glucose control.

Albuminuria.

Albuminuria.

Initial analyses were conducted using univariate logistic regression. Variables with a significant level of p<0.05 were included in a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent associations.

Missing data

We performed a complete case analysis to deal with the missing data by simply omitting those cases with the missing data and subsequently analyse the remaining data. During the analysis of eGFR to estimate the prevalence and incidence of CKD, missing eGFR values were handled using multiple imputation via the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method. A total of 713 eGFR values were missing at baseline, and 6 were missing at the study visit.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS V.29. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology cohort reporting guidelines (see online supplemental materials).

Results

Our study included 1263 participants (see figure 1), with 51% diagnosed with hypertension, 17.6% with diabetes and 31.4% with both conditions. The mean age was 57.1±10.2 years, and 72.2% were female. Most participants (59.8%) had a middle level of education, and 53.6% were classified as low income. Despite more than half of participants having hypertension, only 32.3% were prescribed renin angiotensin aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors, with the lowest rate (26.4%) observed in the diabetes group. Metformin, alone or in combination with sulfonylurea, was the most commonly used antidiabetic medication, while insulin use was minimal (1%). Medication adherence was high across all participants. However, only 20% of those with hypertension reported monitoring their BP at home. Most participants demonstrated a good understanding of their condition(s), and over half had received their diagnosis within the past 5 years. A significant proportion of the cohort was obese (76%). Over half of the participants achieved adequate BP control (BP <140/90 mm Hg in 84.1%). In contrast, only 36.8% of participants achieved HbA1c below 7%. The hypertension group showed the lowest kidney function, with a median eGFR of 88 (IQR 72–100) mL/min/1.73 m², and the highest rate of haematuria (8.4%) (see tables1 2).

Multivariate analysis showed that the risk of developing CKD was significantly higher among participants with positive albuminuria in the hypertension and diabetes group (OR 3.6, 95%CI 1.421 to 9.098, p=0.007). In the hypertension group, older age (OR 4.5, 95% CI 2.146 to 9.637, p<0.001), male gender (OR 2.3, 95% CI 1.101 to 4.608, p=0.026) and presence of haematuria (OR 2.5, 95% CI 1.073 to 5.757, p=0.034) were all associated with a higher likelihood of CKD. Meanwhile, in the diabetes group, participants with cardiovascular disease had a markedly increased risk of CKD (OR 14.9, 95% CI 2.333 to 94.547, p=0.004) (see table 5). Among males and participants using RAAS inhibitors in the hypertension and diabetes group, there was a higher likelihood of uncontrolled BP (OR 2.6, 95% CI 1.164 to 5.964, p=0.02 and OR 2.3, 95% CI 1.044 to 5.158, p=0.039; respectively) (see online supplemental table S1). In the diabetes groups, the risk of uncontrolled blood glucose was found to be higher in participants with a low level of education (OR 2.5, 95% CI 1.088 to 5.721, p=0.031) (see online supplemental table S2).

Discussion

Our study found that hypertension was the most common condition among participants, with a large proportion also classified as obese. Although half of the population had hypertension, only one-third were using RAAS inhibitors, with the lowest rate observed in the diabetes group. Most participants achieved good BP control. However, despite inadequate blood glucose control, insulin use remained very low (1%). The prevalence of CKD was 14.8%, with an incidence rate of 9.1 per 100 person-year. The overall NNS was 7. Nearly half of the cohort was classified as being at moderate to very high risk for adverse renal and cardiovascular outcomes. A higher kidney function over time was observed in the younger age group and in females. The likelihood of developing CKD was higher in participants with positive albuminuria in the hypertension and diabetes group; in older, male participants and those with haematuria within the hypertension group; and in participants with cardiovascular disease in the diabetes group.

The overall prevalence of CKD in this high-risk population is consistent with previous reports, where global CKD prevalence ranges from 8% to 16%.1114 Countries such as Indonesia, Bangladesh and others have reported over 10 million CKD cases each.13 However, the prevalence was higher in the diabetes (14.4%) and hypertension and diabetes (17.4%) groups, which aligns with findings from other low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).15 The age-standardised incidence rate (ASIR) of CKD varies widely across regions. In Asia, the overall ASIR was 206 per 100 000 in 2019, with Saudi Arabia reaching as high as 496 per 100 000.16 In Southeast Asia, the ASIR was estimated at 231 per 100 000 in 2021, showing an upward trend of 48% between 1990 and 2021.17 In comparison, our study exhibited a much higher incidence rate of 9.1 per 100 person-years, equivalent to 9105 per 100 000 person-years. This finding is consistent with a study from the Netherlands, which reported a CKD incidence of 1213 per 100 000 person-years, and up to 25 000 per 100 000 among people with diabetes.18 It is important to note that our study population consisted exclusively of individuals with diabetes and/or hypertension—both well-established risk factors for CKD. As such, this population represents a high-risk group, and the observed incidence rate is expectedly higher than that of the general population. Furthermore, the relatively small and closed nature of the study sample (n=1263) may exaggerate the incidence estimate when expressed per 100 000 person-years. Although the calculated rate remains methodologically valid, it should be interpreted with caution, as it does not reflect a general population-based rate. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of CKD aetiology in LMICs should be acknowledged, including non-communicable diseases, infection, environmental and occupational exposures.15 Our cohort included individuals with other risk factors such as older age, obesity, low education levels and low income. Of note, a high rate of haematuria in the hypertension group suggests that causes beyond hypertensive nephrosclerosis may be involved and warrant further investigation.

Despite poor control of BP and glucose levels, the use of RAAS inhibitors and insulin was notably low. The effectiveness of RAAS inhibitors in CKD has been validated by multiple randomised controlled trials, demonstrating benefits in managing hypertension and proteinuria.1922 However, calcium-channel blockers–particularly amlodipine–were more commonly prescribed, potentially explaining the underuse of RAAS inhibitors. Likewise, it is well-established that timely initiation of insulin therapy can delay the onset of diabetes-related complications,23 24 yet nearly half of our cohort had been diagnosed with diabetes for over 5 years, a duration linked to a higher risk of CKD. Thus, optimising oral antidiabetic medications and integrating insulin therapy into primary care is critical for better glycaemic control. The low utilisation of insulin in primary care is influenced by multiple factors. Under the national health insurance scheme, only specific types of insulin—namely basal human insulin and basal insulin analogs—are authorised for initiation and prescription at the primary care level.25 Other types of insulin require initial prescription and dose stabilisation at an advanced healthcare facility, after which they may be continued in primary care through a referral-back mechanism.25 Additionally, the limited number of healthcare providers trained to initiate and manage insulin therapy, along with a shortage of diabetes educators, pharmacists and adequate monitoring infrastructure, further contributes to the underuse of insulin in this setting.

Early-stage CKD (stages 1–3) is often asymptomatic, reinforcing the importance of targeted screening to identify cases before disease progression.14 Our study supports the principle of early detection among high-risk populations, demonstrating that targeted case finding is more effective than broad population-based screening.142628 This approach successfully identified a majority of individuals with CKD who could benefit from timely therapeutic interventions. Specifically, our findings indicate that to identify one person with an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m², it is necessary to screen six individuals with both hypertension and diabetes, eight individuals from the hypertension group and seven individuals from the diabetes group. However, this benefit may not be generalisable to populations with different risk profiles.

The primary goal of early CKD detection is to slow disease progression and prevent the need for dialysis or kidney transplantation. This study revealed that 49% of participants were at moderate to very high risk for CKD progression, emphasising the urgent need for proactive screening and optimal management of hypertension and diabetes. We recommend prioritising the use of RAAS inhibitors to control BP and prevent renal and cardiovascular complications, and to lower the threshold for initiating insulin therapy to improve glycaemic control. Additionally, enhancing educational programs focused on weight reduction is crucial for effective CKD prevention strategies.

As expected, the diabetes group had higher eGFR values than the hypertension group. Glomerular hyperfiltration is a hallmark of early stages of diabetic kidney disease and may contribute to CKD progression,29 making these findings clinically significant. Furthermore, over half of the participants (53.5%) presented with albuminuria, highlighting the importance of the KDIGO guideline that recommends comprehensive CKD risk stratification using both eGFR and albuminuria (uACR) assessments.30

This study has several limitations, including a short follow-up period and challenges with collecting retrospective data across multiple sites. As with all cross-sectional studies, there is a possibility of unmeasured confounders that may influence the results. Additionally, the study primarily reflects the urban population, limiting its broader applicability. The predominance of female and obese participants in our study may limit the generalisability of the findings to the broader population. We were unable to fully meet the CKD diagnostic criteria outlined by the KDIGO guidelines due to incomplete uACR data for all participants. The uACR measurements were conducted only within the diabetic subgroup because of budget constraints. Consequently, the calculation of CKD prevalence and incidence was based solely on eGFR. Excluding participants without uACR data may result in an overestimation of albuminuria prevalence.

In conclusion, the prevalence and incidence of CKD were high in this high-risk Indonesian population. An NNS of 7 supports that screening individuals with diabetes and hypertension for CKD is efficient. Nearly half of our cohort was in a moderate-risk to very high-risk group for adverse renal and cardiovascular outcomes. Improving glycaemic control, possibly through intensive insulin therapy, encouraging the use of reno-protective medications such as RAAS inhibitors and promoting weight loss strategies are all vital measures for slowing CKD progression in Indonesia.
