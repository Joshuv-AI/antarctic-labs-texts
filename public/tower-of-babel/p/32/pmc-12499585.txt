Acquired Chiari malformation type I after atlanto-occipital dislocation: illustrative case

Chiari malformation type I (CM-I) is characterized by herniation of the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum. It is reported that this herniation is associated with a decrease in the posterior fossa volume and variable skull base dysplasia that may lead to significant compression of the upper cervical spinal cordand brainstem.1 The descent of the tonsils leads to crowding of the neural structures in the foramen magnum region, compromising the normal flow and dynamics of the CSF. A wide variety of clinical presentations have been described in these cases, ranging from asymptomatic patients to cases with severe neurological symptoms. There is a group of patients who develop acquired CM-I that is associated with a condition that triggers the herniation of cerebellar tonsils. Acquired CM-I is thought to result from pathology causing high pressure above the foramen magnum or low pressure below the cerebellum, displacing the tonsils downward.2 Herein we report the case of a patient who initially experienced atlanto-occipital dislocation (AOD) associated with craniocervical hemorrhage and subsequently developed CM-I, not previously reported in the literature.

Illustrative Case

This patient is a 31-year-old male. Three years earlier, he was struck by a car while riding his motorcycle. He had been wearing a helmet, which was found at a distance from him. On admission, he was alert and oriented, reporting neck pain. Initial examination revealed multiple facial lacerations. Neurologically, his Glasgow Coma Scale score was 15, but tetraparesis (3/5) was noted. CT imaging of the brain demonstrated a circumferential intradural hematoma at the craniocervical junction, corroborated by MRI (Fig. 1). The supratentorial region appeared normal. Cervical spine CT imaging showed both right and left atlanto-occipital intervals to be increased (right: 7 mm, left: 6 mm), indicating AOD (Fig. 2A–C). Additionally, some degree of displacement was observed in the right atlantoaxial joint. The patient also sustained a hemomediastinum and a retroperitoneal hematoma. The patient underwent surgical stabilization of the occipital–C2 region (Fig. 2D). During the procedure, no epidural hematoma was found. The patient made an uneventful recovery after surgery and showed progressive improvement in motor function in both the upper and lower extremities. He was discharged from the hospital on postoperative day 15, showing a normal examination of all four limbs.

The patient resumed normal activities in the following months. However, 1 year later, he developed new onset of recurrent headaches. Despite symptomatic treatment by his primary care team, the headaches progressively worsened, becoming more difficult to alleviate with medications. As the headache intensified, it became localized to the occipital region, worsened with coughing, sneezing, and bending forward. Two months later, he noticed decreasing dexterity in his hands and difficulty walking due to imbalance. He was referred back to our clinic where examination revealed normal strength in both upper and lower extremities, generalized hyperreflexia, impaired tandem gait, and bilateral Hoffmann’s reflex.

A new cervical spine MRI study showed a descent of the cerebellar tonsils by 15 mm and significant signal changes in the cervical spinal cord, indicating CM-I with a presyrinx condition (Fig. 3). Given the clinical and radiological findings, the patient underwent surgery to remove previous hardware and decompress the craniovertebral junction. A suboccipital craniectomy, C1 laminectomy, partial laminectomy of C2, and duraplasty were performed. Then, the instrumentation was replaced. The patient was discharged from the hospital to continue rehabilitation as an outpatient. One year later, the patient reported a complete recovery of neurological symptoms, referring only to sporadic headaches of no significance to him. Postoperative MRI revealed adequate decompression of the neural structures and reversal of the signal changes in the spinal cord (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Observations

While a congenitally small posterior fossa is the most common cause of cerebellar tonsillar herniation, acquired cerebellar descent can result from conditions that create pressure gradients in the craniovertebral area. Acquired CM-I may occur through two distinct pathological mechanisms that lead to the downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils: 1) low CSF pressure beneath the craniovertebral junction, which creates a pulling effect from below; and 2) increased downward pressure from above the foramen magnum. The former is usually due to conditions such as spontaneous CSF leakage, CSF-venous fistula, tethered cord, lumboperitoneal shunt insertion, or repetitive traumatic lumbar spinal taps, while the latter occurs from hydrocephalus, subdural collections, pseudotumor cerebri, or brain masses.2–7

In cases of acquired CM-I, addressing the underlying lesion or condition typically improves both CM-I and syringomyelia. A few patients with acquired CM-I following spinal cord injury have been documented in the literature. McKean et al. described 4 young patients who developed CM-I after spinal cord injuries, one of whom exhibited AOD.4 However, the description of the imaging findings and pathophysiology is rather superficial and inconclusive. The authors suggested that dural tethering may have occurred posttrauma, resulting in dynamic changes in CSF circulation that led to the downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils.4

Regardless of pathophysiological mechanism of acquired CM-I, the end result is descent of the cerebellar tonsils into the foramen magnum. Low-lying cerebellar tonsils cause obstruction of normal CSF flow in the fourth ventricle and the surrounding subarachnoid space, thereby hindering CSF circulation in both directions. During systole, the displaced cerebellar tonsils and medulla generate pressure waves that propel CSF into the spinal canal, while also impeding its return to the cranial compartment, resulting in an increase in pressure within the spinal subarachnoid space. This rise in intraspinal CSF pressure can be transmitted to the spinal cord, displacing CSF into the spinal cord substance, which leads to cord edema and the subsequent formation of syringomyelia.8

In the current case, the patient initially presented with traumatic AOD associated with a subdural or subarachnoid hematoma at the craniocervical junction. Most likely, the local injury and the presence of bleeding led to reactive arachnoiditis and the formation of scarring in the area, thus compromising and obstructing the normal CSF flow at the foramen magnum. We hypothesize that a CSF pressure gradient was created above (intracranial) and below (intraspinal) the obstruction, resulting in an increase in CSF pressure above the foramen magnum. Over time, there was a progressive descent of the cerebellar tonsils, leading to CM-I as the final event. The initial MRI, when the AOD occurred, does not show CM-I, which indicates that CM-I occurred de novo some years after the traumatic event. The significant clinical and radiographic improvement observed in this patient after suboccipital decompression supports the conclusion that CM-I was the origin of the clinical picture in this case. Before the suboccipital decompression, MRI showed a 15-mm descent of cerebellar tonsils, causing a very crowded space and cervicomedullary compression in the foramen magnum area. Additionally, an extensive change in the signal was observed in the cervical spinal cord. The signal changes can be explained by a presyrinx state in the spinal cord. A presyrinx state is a condition observed in CM-I cases, manifesting as increased signal and cord enlargement, which precedes syrinx formation. This presyrinx state is a reversible anomaly of spinal cord edema caused by alterations in CSF flow dynamics, and it has been shown to reverse after suboccipital decompression.9

Lessons

In summary, this paper presents a rare case of a patient who initially experienced an AOD and intradural hematoma in the craniovertebral junction and subsequently developed CM-I. Although this scenario has not been reported previously in the literature, the most reasonable explanation of this clinical presentation is that the local trauma and the intradural hematoma led to scarring and arachnoiditis in the area, compromising the normal bidirectional circulation of the CSF, causing a pressure gradient above and below the obstruction, leading to cerebellar descent as the final event. Clinicians should be aware of this uncommon scenario when treating patients with injury of the craniovertebral junction who subsequently develop neurological symptoms in a delayed fashion.
