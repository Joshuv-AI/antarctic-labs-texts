M1 muscarinic receptor modulation drives sex-specific alterations of Alzheimer’s pathophysiology in APPswe/PSEN1ΔE9 mice
Authors: Wesam Bassiouni, Emily Ye, Tash-Lynn L. Colson, Stephen S.G. Ferguson, Khaled S. Abd-Elrahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder marked by cognitive decline, where females account for 60% of diagnosed cases. AD is featured by β-amyloid deposition, which influences the activity of M1 muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (M1 mAChRs) that are essential for memory and learning. We previously demonstrated that VU0486846, a M1 mAChR positive allosteric modulator, improves cognitive function in AD mice by ameliorating β-amyloid pathology. However, it remains unclear how changes in M1 mAChR signaling and subcellular localization in AD brain influences its mechanism and how this differs between sexes. Nine-month-old male and female APPswe/PSEN1ΔE9 (APP/PS1) and wild-type mice were treated with VU0486846 (10 mg/kg/day) or vehicle via drinking water for 8 weeks. Elevated extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 signaling, a plausible driver for β-amyloid pathology, was observed in female but not male APP/PS1 mice cortices, whereas VU0486846 mitigated this change. Additionally, VU0486846 enhanced the phosphorylation of the neuronal transcription factor cAMP response element-binding protein and increased the level of c-Fos, which regulate neuronal survival, in female wild-type and APP/PS1 mice only. In contrast, only male APP/PS1 mice exhibited increased levels of p62/SQSTM1 and decreased glycogen synthase kinase-3β activity, indicative of impaired autophagic flux, a change that was ameliorated by VU0486846 treatment. In subcellular fractions obtained from mice cortices, VU0486846 only increased cytosolic M1 mAChR level in female wild-type and APP/PS1 mice but not in males. Our findings indicate that M1 mAChR signaling is disrupted in APP/PS1 mice in a sex-dependent manner. VU0486846 enhances prosurvival signaling in females, potentially by increasing the cytosolic pool of M1 mAChRs, while modulating autophagy pathways in males, highlighting a sex-specific mechanism of action and underscoring its therapeutic potential.Significance StatementThe study highlights the sex-specific role of M1 muscarinic acetylcholine receptor (M1 mAChR) in Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathophysiology, providing evidence that the ability of M1 mAChR positive allosteric modulation to alleviate β-amyloid pathology occurs through distinct mechanisms. It rescues neuronal activity in females, which could be linked to enhanced cytosolic receptor pooling, while restoring autophagic flux in males. The study also reinforces the therapeutic potential of M1 mAChR positive allosteric modulators and supports the development of sex-tailored interventions for AD.

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a decline in the cognitive function of the brain, as well as neuronal loss and synaptic dysfunction.1 AD is characterized by the accumulation of β-amyloid (Aβ) aggregates and hyperphosphorylation of the microtubule-associated protein, tau, which play a significant role in the neurotoxic insults observed in the AD brain.2 AD disproportionately affects females, accounting for almost 60% of diagnosed cases. Females also exhibit higher risk and pathological burden compared to males.3,4 The precise molecular mechanisms underlying AD and this sex disparity are not well understood, which limits the development of therapeutic options that can slow down the disease progression in the most vulnerable population.5,6

The cholinergic nervous system is considered among the neurotransmitter systems that are markedly affected by AD pathological changes in the early stages of the disease, as cholinergic neurons of the basal forebrain have been shown to be sensitive to the neurodegenerative sequelae occurring during disease progression.7,8 The M1 muscarinic acetylcholine receptor (M1 mAChR) is a subtype of muscarinic receptors and one of the most abundant G protein-coupled cholinergic receptor in the hippocampus and cortex. It plays a critical role in regulating several key brain functions, including learning and memory, neuronal survival, and synaptic homeostasis.9, 10, 11 M1 mAChR is Gq/11-coupled receptor, and upon stimulation it activates phospholipase C, which in turn stimulates a downstream signaling cascade including extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)/mitogen-activated protein kinase, protein kinase C (PKC), and cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB)-dependent gene transcription, which are involved in regulation of cell survival pathways, long-term potentiation (LTP) and neuronal excitability.12,13 Therefore, it is conceivable that disruption of M1 mAChR signaling by Aβ accumulation may predispose the brain to synaptic dysfunction and cognitive decline. Additionally, M1 mAChR knockout in AD mice increased amyloidogenic amyloid precursor protein (APP) processing, which enhanced toxic Aβ pathology.14

Given the central role of M1 mAChR in AD pathophysiology, the use of M1 mAChR agonists has emerged as a potential therapeutic option to improve cognitive function during AD. Indeed, pharmacological activation of M1 mAChRs in a mouse prion disease model of neurodegeneration improved cognitive function and alleviated memory deficits.15 However, the use of orthosteric agonists has been limited by their adverse off-target cholinergic effects including seizures, sweating, salivation, and gastrointestinal disturbance.16 Meanwhile, M1 mAChR positive allosteric modulators emerged as another option to stimulate M1 mAChR activity without inducing these off-target cholinergic effects and showed promising results as a procognitive therapy as we previously demonstrated.17 Our group demonstrated that VU0486846, a novel positive allosteric modulator of M1 mAChR, improves cognitive function in female APPswe/PSEN1ΔE9 (APP/PS1) mice, a model of AD pathology. Treatment also reduced Aβ pathology, astrogliosis, and microgliosis, prevented neuronal loss, and promoted the nonamyloidogenic processing of APP.17,18 Similar results were observed in male APP/PS1 mice; however, no significant differences in Aβ deposition was detected when the same treatment paradigm was followed.19 Additionally, a recent study also reported sex-specific differences in cholinergic signaling during AD, where cholinergic tone was found to regulate Aβ pathology only in male and ovariectomized female mice.20 However, the mechanisms underlying sex-dependent differences in M1 mAChR signaling in AD remain unclear, particularly how Aβ disrupts M1 mAChR signaling dynamics in the AD brain of both sexes.

A critical aspect in understanding the role of M1 mAChR modulation in improving AD pathology is its subcellular localization. M1 mAChR, such as other G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), dynamically translocate between the plasma membrane and intracellular compartments, and its location within the cell can dictate its downstream signaling.21,22 Notably, in models of AD, alteration in GPCR trafficking and internalization in response to Aβ accumulation has been reported.23 Here, we aimed to shed light on the mechanistic differences in M1 mAChR signaling underlying neuronal resilience between male and female APP/PS1 mice and to determine whether these differences arise from changes in receptor trafficking between cellular comportments, particularly in response to Aβ accumulation and allosteric modulation.

We report that VU0486846 reverses alterations in ERK1/2 phosphorylation and enhances CREB phosphorylation in female APP/PS1 mice, whereas it selectively rescues glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK3β)-mediated autophagy in male APP/PS1 mice. Interestingly, VU0486846 increased the cytosolic M1 mAChR pool in female, but not male, mice, suggesting that sex-specific differences in receptor dynamics and subcellular localization may contribute to the divergent M1 mAChR signaling responses in AD mice between sexes.

Materials and methods

Reagents

VU0486846 ((R)-4-(4-(1H-Pyrazol-1-yl)benzyl)-N-((1S,2S)-2-hydroxycyclohexyl)-3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]oxazine-2-carboxamide) (Cat# 3271) was obtained from Axon Medchem. Mouse anti-GAPDH (Cat# AM4300), anti-Na+/K+-ATPase (Cat# MA3-915), and HRP-linked goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Cat# 31460) and anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Cat# 31430) secondary antibodies were purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific. Rabbit anti-vinculin (Cat# ab129002), anti-phospho-mTOR-pS2448 (Cat# ab109268), anti-c-Fos (Cat# ab190289), and anti-p62/SQSTM1 (Cat# ab109012) antibodies were purchased from Abcam. Rabbit anti-phospho-GSK3β-pS9 (Cat# 9323), anti-ERK1/2 (Cat# 9102), anti-phospho-ERK1/2-pT202/Y204 (Cat# 9101), anti-PKCα (Cat# 2056), anti-phospho-PKC-pT638/641 (Cat# 9375), anti-mTOR (Cat# 2983), anti-COX IV (Cat# 4850), and anti-CREB (Cat# 9197) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. Rabbit anti-M1 mAChR (Cat# M9808) and anti-phospho-CREB-pS133 (Cat# 06-519) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Other biochemical reagents were purchased from BioShop Canada and Bio-Rad Laboratories.

Animals

All animal experimental protocols were approved by the University of Ottawa Institutional Animal Care Committee and in accordance with the Canadian Council of Animal Care guidelines. Mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratories and bred to establish littermate-controlled male and female wild-type (B6C3F1/J, stock# 100010, RRID: IMSR_JAX:100010) and APPswe/PSEN1ΔE9 (APP/PS1) (B6C3-Tg (APP/PSEN1ΔE9)85Dbo/J, stock# 34829). Mice were group-housed in cages of 2 or more animals, received food and water ad libitum and maintained on a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle at 24 °C. Wild-type and APP/PS1 mice were aged to 9 months, randomized and blindly treated for 8 weeks with VU0486846 (10 mg/kg/day dissolved in 10% v/v DMSO) or vehicle in drinking water containing 1% w/v sucrose and 0.5% v/v tween. Drug concentration was calculated based on water intake on an average water consumption of 4 mL/day for a 25 g mouse.24 Mice were weighed once weekly, and water consumption was assessed on a regular basis. In our previous studies, no significant differences in body weight were observed between groups at the start or end of the study, and body weight remained stable throughout the treatment period with no signs of dehydration, suggesting that the observed effects are unlikely to be secondary to systemic metabolic alterations.17,19 At the end of the 8-week treatment duration, mice were sacrificed by exsanguination, and brains were collected for biochemical analysis. The age of 9 months was chosen to conduct our experiments as this age represents a stage where APP/PS1 mice exhibit established Aβ pathology, synaptic dysfunction and cognitive impairment,25 which allows us to evaluate the effect of M1 mAChR positive allosteric modulation as a potential therapeutic option in conditions that resemble the symptomatic disease.

Sample preparation and subcellular fractionation

Cortices were divided into small portions. One portion was used for subcellular fractionation to obtain cytosolic, organelle membrane and plasma membrane cellular fractions, and another portion was homogenized to obtain a whole tissue homogenate. For whole tissue homogenization, 200 μL lysis buffer (Cat# FNN0021, Invitrogen) (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4; 250 mM NaCl; 5 mM EDTA; 50 mM NaF; 1 mM Na3VO4; 1% NP40; 0.02% NaN3) containing 2:1000 v/v protease inhibitor (Cat# 539134, Calbiochem) was added to 20 mg tissue and kept on ice for 5 minutes. The tissue was then homogenized using a stainless-steel tissue homogenizer for 1 minute (in cycles of 15 seconds on, 15 seconds off). The homogenate was rotated for 1 hour at 4 °C followed by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. The debris was discarded, and the supernatant was transferred to another tube and centrifuged again at 20,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant, designated as the whole tissue homogenate was collected and stored at −80 °C. After that, the total protein concentration in each sample was measured by Bradford assay using bovine serum albumin as a standard.

A subcellular fractionation kit (Cat#SM-005, Invent Biotechnologies Inc) was used to isolate the plasma membrane and organelle membrane fractions from the cytosol as follows; a 20–30 mg tissue was placed in a filter cartridge, and 500 μL buffer A containing 2:1000 v/v protease inhibitor was added, and the tissue was grinded and homogenized using a plastic rod. Then the homogenized tissue was centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 30 seconds, the filter cartridge was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended by vigorously vortexing for 10 seconds. The resulting tissue homogenate was then centrifuged at 700 × g for 1 minutes and the supernatant was transferred to another tube and centrifuged at 4 °C for 30 minutes at 16,000 × g. The resulting supernatant was kept as the cytosolic fraction, whereas the pellet (containing the total membrane protein fraction including organelles and plasma membranes) was washed 2 times with 0.3 M sodium chloride in PBS, pH 9.5. The pellet was then resuspended in 200 μL buffer B containing 2:1000 v/v protease inhibitor and centrifuged at 7800 × g for 20 minutes at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was washed twice, resuspended in lysis buffer (Cat# FNN0021, Invitrogen) containing 2:1000 v/v protease inhibitor and designated as the organelle membrane fraction, whereas the supernatant was transferred to another tube, mixed with 1 mL PBS and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 30 minutes at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer containing 2:1000 v/v protease inhibitor and designated as the plasma membrane fraction. The collected samples were stored at −80 °C, and the total protein concentration in each sample was measured by Bradford assay. The purity of the different fractions was assessed by immunoblotting using 3 housekeeping proteins: Na+/K+-ATPase (plasma membrane marker), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; cytosolic marker), and cytochrome C oxidase subunit IV (COX IV; organelle membrane marker). Fraction purity was confirmed by the minimal or absent detection of marker proteins specific to the other fractions.

Immunoblotting

To measure the level of a protein of interest in whole tissue homogenate or subcellular fraction, homogenates were diluted in lysis buffer and 3× loading buffer (187.5 mM Tris-HCl, 30% v/v glycerol, 6% w/v SDS, 0.006% w/v bromophenol blue) containing 15% v/v β-mercaptoethanol, and boiled at 95 °C for 10 minutes. Notably, 20–30 μg total protein was then separated on 7.5, 10 or 4–15% SDS-polyacrylamide gels by electrophoresis. Then proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Membranes were blocked for 1 hour at room temperature in Tris-buffered saline (TBST with 0.05% v/v Tween 20, pH 7.6) containing 5% nonfat dry milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (1:1000 in TBST with 1% milk or bovine serum albumin). Vinculin, GAPDH, Na+/K+-ATPase and COX IV were used as loading controls for the whole tissue homogenate, cytosolic, plasma membrane, and organelle membrane fractions, respectively. After washing in TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000 in TBST with 1% milk) for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized with Pierce ECL chemiluminescent detection reagent (Cat# 32209, ThermoFisher Scientific). Band intensities were quantified using ImageLab software (Biorad), normalized to loading controls or the corresponding total protein, and expressed as fold of the control band intensity.

Data analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± SEM of n independent experiments (where n represents an individual mouse brain cortex sample). For multiple comparisons, three-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used (GraphPad Prism version 10). Statistical significance was considered at P < .05.

Results

VU0486846 reverses the changes in ERK1/2 activity and increases PKCα level in female but not male APP/PS1 mice

We previously demonstrated that VU0486846 markedly improves cognitive function and reverses memory deficits in both female and male APP/PS1 mice, as evidenced by enhanced performance in the novel object recognition and Morris water maze tasks. In parallel, VU0486846 differentially modulates neuronal survival markers in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice, warranting further investigation into the underlying mechanisms driving these effects.17,19 Thus, we investigated changes in the levels and activities of key neuronal survival regulators, including ERK1/2 and PKC, in cortical homogenates from APP/PS1 mice after 8-week treatment with VU0486846 (10 mg/kg).

It is worth noting that physiological ERK1/2 activation is involved in regulating neuronal survival by promoting key neuronal functions such as synaptic plasticity, memory formation and LTP induction under basal conditions.26 However, under stress conditions such as in AD, ERK1/2 activation can act as a pathological driver, promoting Aβ deposition by promoting the amyloidogenic APP processing.27,28 Here we found that, in females but not males, APP/PS1 mice cortices exhibited higher ERK1/2 phosphorylation indicating its increased activity compared to wild-type (Fig. 1A). Treatment with VU0486846 attenuated this increase in female APP/PS1 cortices compared to vehicle-treated ones. We also investigated the changes in PKC, another neuronal survival regulator whose activation is important in maintaining calcium homeostasis and mitochondrial integrity as well as protecting against oxidative stress-induced apoptosis.29 PKC is also downstream of M1 mAChR signaling and activation of the PKCα isoform is reported to promote the nonamyloidogenic processing of APP.30,31 Upon comparing the changes in PKC levels and phosphorylation, no significant difference in PKC level was observed between wild-type and APP/PS1 mice cortices regardless of sex. However, VU0486846 treatment resulted in a significant increase in PKC expression in both wild-type and APP/PS1 female mice cortices but not in male mice, whereas both male wild-type and APP/PS1 mice cortices had higher PKC levels compared to females, indicating that the VU0486846-mediated increase in PKC level is sex-specific (Fig. 1B). On the other hand, no significant differences in PKC phosphorylation were observed among the different groups when comparing phospho-PKC to total PKC levels (Fig. 1B).

VU0486846 increases the activity of the neuronal transcription factor, CREB, and c-Fos level in female but not male wild-type and APP/PS1 mice

To confirm the sex-specific role of M1 mAChR in neuronal survival in APP/PS1 mice, changes in the levels and activities of different neuronal transcription factors were measured in cortical homogenates using immunoblotting. Among these key transcriptional factors that regulate neuronal activity and survival is CREB, which plays a critical role in learning and memory consolidation and regulating LTP signaling.32 It also modulates the expression of c-Fos, another critical regulator of neuronal survival that controls the transcription of genes involved in DNA repair and neuronal growth.33,34 No significant differences in c-Fos level or CREB phosphorylation were observed between wild-type and APP/PS1 mice cortices regardless of sex (Fig. 2, A and B). However, VU0486846 treatment significantly increased c-Fos level in wild-type and CREB phosphorylation, indicating higher activity, in both wild-type and APP/PS1 female mice cortices but not in male mice. VU0486846 activates autophagic flux in male but not female APP/PS1 mice

We then examined how VU0486846 treatment affects Aβ-mediated changes in autophagic signaling in APP/PS1 mice cortices by measuring the changes in the levels and activities of different autophagic markers, including GSK3β, which is known to have a dual role in autophagy regulation depending on the cellular condition, where under stress it can promote autophagy by inhibiting mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) activity.35 Our laboratory previously reported that male APP/PS1 mice exhibit impaired autophagy through GSK3β-dependent mechanisms, which is associated with an increase in the key autophagy marker p62/SQSTM1, a protein responsible for delivering ubiquitinated substrates for proteasomal degradation.36,37 We found that male, but not female, APP/PS1 mouse cortices exhibited elevated levels of p62/SQSTM1 along with increased GSK3β phosphorylation, indicative of reduced GSK3β activity, suggesting impaired autophagic flux in males but not females (Fig. 3, A and B). Treatment with VU0486846 attenuated these increases in male APP/PS1 mice cortices compared to vehicle-treated ones, meaning that it improved autophagic flux in males. No significant difference was observed in female cortices among the different groups, suggesting that these changes are sex-specific (Fig. 3, A and B). Cytosolic M1 mAChR expression is upregulated by VU0486846 in female but not male wild-type and APP/PS1 mice

We then attempted to understand the mechanism behind these observed sex differences by assessing the differences in receptor dynamic and how it is affected by receptor modulation, considering that receptor location within the cell can alter its downstream signaling.21,22 Our previous studies showed that VU0486846 increases the expression of M1 mAChR in the hippocampus of female but not male APP/PS1 mice.17,19 However, how it is changing between the different subcellular fractions within the cell remained unclear. In this regard, we measured the protein level of M1 mAChR in different subcellular fractions obtained from male and female wild-type or APP/PS1 mouse cortices to assess the changes in M1 mAChR subcellular localization in response to VU0486846 treatment. Purity of the fractions was confirmed by immunoblotting each fraction against the 3 housekeeping proteins: Na+/K+-ATPase (plasma membrane marker), GAPDH (cytosol marker), and COX IV (organelle membrane marker), where each fraction showed abundant level of its specific marker with minimal or no level of the markers specific for other fractions, confirming their purity and the reliability of the fractionation protocol (Fig. 4A). There were no significant differences in subcellular M1 mAChR protein expression between vehicle-treated wild-type and APP/PS1 mice in the different fractions regardless of sex (Fig. 4, B–D). However, in cortices obtained from female mice, VU0486846 significantly increased M1 mAChR expression in the cytosolic fractions from both wild-type and APP/PS1 female mice cortices compared to vehicle-treated ones, whereas no significant difference was observed in males (Fig. 4B), indicating that VU0486846-induced increase in M1 mAChR expression is sex-dependent. On the other hand, VU0486846 did not significantly affect M1 mAChR expression in organelle membrane or plasma membrane fractions obtained from cortices of wild-type or APP/PS1 mice compared to vehicle controls regardless of sex (Fig. 4, C and D). Discussion

Developing effective therapies that slow or prevent the progression of AD remains a major challenge, largely because of the complexity of its underlying pathophysiology and the limited translational success of many promising preclinical approaches. This challenge is further compounded by the underrepresentation of sex as a biological variable in AD research, despite clear differences in disease prevalence, progression, and therapeutic response between males and females. Building on our previous work demonstrating that positive allosteric modulation of M1 mAChR with VU0486846 improves cognitive performance in APP/PS1 mice,17,19 the present study sought to further define some of the cellular mechanisms underlying these beneficial effects and to determine whether they differ between sexes. Here, we show that M1 mAChR signaling is disrupted by Aβ accumulation in a sex-dependent manner and that pharmacological activation of M1 mAChR signaling using the positive allosteric modulator, VU0486846, alters these pathological changes through distinct mechanisms in males and females. Specifically, M1 mAChR modulation preferentially restored baseline neuronal function and increased neuronal transcriptional activity in female APP/PS1 mice, while enhancing autophagic flux in males. In addition, we demonstrate that VU0486846 dynamically alters M1 mAChR subcellular localization in a sex-specific manner, increasing cytosolic receptor localization in females but not males in both wild-type and APP/PS1 cortices. Together, these findings extend our earlier behavioral and pathological observations by providing mechanistic insight into sex-specific vulnerabilities in cholinergic signaling during AD, reinforcing the therapeutic potential of M1 mAChR positive allosteric modulators, and highlighting the importance of incorporating sex-specific biology into the development of targeted strategies aimed at modifying disease progression.

To better understand how M1 mAChR positive allosteric modulation improves Aβ pathology in APP/PS1 mice and how this differs between sexes, we investigated the changes in different downstream pathways involved in regulation of neuronal survival signaling and autophagy in cortices obtained from male and female wild-type and APP/PS1 mice treated with VU0486846 or vehicle. Our data shows that ERK1/2 phosphorylation is enhanced in female, but not male, APP/PS1 mouse cortices, suggesting an increase in its activity. This marker is mainly involved in promoting synaptic plasticity and neuronal survival.26 However, under stress conditions, ERK1/2 may act differently, with reports suggesting that ERK1/2 activation during AD can also act as a pathological driver that triggers neurodegeneration via inducing neuroinflammation and promoting tau phosphorylation and Aβ deposition.27 Inhibition of ERK1/2 signaling was shown to reduce β-secretase activity, which in turn lowers the amyloidogenic processing of APP, supporting the role of ERK1/2 in mediating Aβ pathology.28 Another study reported that ERK1/2 activation can suppress the expression of the Aβ degrading enzyme, neprilysin, hindering the clearance of Aβ.38 This explains the Aβ-triggered increase in ERK1/2 activity we observed in female APP/PS1 mice cortices. We also show that this increase was attenuated by VU0486846 treatment, suggesting that M1 mAChR positive allosteric modulation stabilizes synaptic signaling and reduces its pathological overactivation. This pathological change was not observed in male APP/PS1 cortices, suggesting that male neurons may experience less neuronal or synaptic stress compared to females in response to Aβ at the same age and that dysregulation of synaptic plasticity in females is more responsive to cholinergic modulation than in males. Considering that ERK1/2 pathological activation can promote Aβ deposition, the increase we observe in ERK1/2 activity in female APP/PS1 mice cortices and the ability of VU0486846 to attenuate it is also consistent with the ability of VU0486846 to reduce the levels of Aβ burden in female but not male APP/PS1 mice hippocampus as we reported in our previous studies, indicating that the changes in ERK1/2 activity can be correlated with changes in Aβ levels.17,19

We also investigated the changes in PKC, specifically the PKCα isoform, downstream of M1 mAChR activation that is known to inhibit amyloidogenic processing of APP by promoting the nonamyloidogenic α-secretase pathway.30,31 We found that VU0486846 elevated PKCα levels in cortices samples from female, but not male, wild-type and APP/PS1 mice. This in turn can result in enhanced α-secretase activity and reduction in Aβ accumulation in female cortices, which would improve synaptic function and neuronal survival in a sex-specific way. This is in line with our previous findings that VU0486846 increases α-secretase level in female, but not male, APP/PS1 mice, which could be linked to the increase in PKC level or activity.17,19 Interestingly, PKC is known to act as an upstream activator of key neuronal transcriptional factors essential for neuronal survival such as CREB and c-Fos, promoting their signaling cascade in response to neuronal stimuli.39, 40, 41 Therefore, we investigated how the activity and/or expression of these 2 transcriptional factors change with VU0486846 treatment. We found that VU0486846 selectively increased CREB phosphorylation as well as c-Fos expression in samples from female, but not male, wild-type and APP/PS1 mice, suggesting that VU0486846 induces protective neuronal transcription, in a similar pattern to the changes we observed in PKCα. Considering that CREB is critical for memory function and LTP, a process that is linked to M1 mAChR activation as previously reported,32,42 this suggests that females are more responsive to enhanced M1 mAChR activity and its associated neuronal transcriptional activation that could be also linked to estrogen-mediated CREB activation.43 On the other hand, male APP/PS1 cortices samples exhibited higher CREB phosphorylation levels compared to females regardless of VU0486846 treatment. Prior studies also suggest potential sex differences in hippocampal CREB responses, where male rodents exhibited higher level of phosphorylated CREB compared to females under basal conditions.44 However, the mechanism underlying this pattern observed in cortices remain unclear and need further investigation. It is important to acknowledge that our female APP/PS1 mice were approaching reproductive senescence, a stage associated with profound hormonal changes and fluctuating estradiol levels that typically begin around 10–12 months of age.45 These hormonal changes may influence neuroinflammatory, synaptic, and autophagy-related signaling pathways and could therefore contribute to some of the observed sex-dependent differences. Future studies incorporating direct hormone measurements and monitoring will be important to better distinguish hormonal effects from intrinsic sex-specific mechanisms.

Another critical signaling pathway we investigated is the changes in autophagic regulation, which is essential for the clearance of misfolded proteins and plays a major role in Aβ and tau pathology.46 Our data shows that male APP/PS1 mice cortices exhibited higher p62/SQSTM1 levels and increased inhibitory phosphorylation of GSK3β, suggesting an impaired autophagic flux by Aβ accumulation in male brains, an effect that was not observed in female mice. These deficits in autophagic flux would result in rapid accumulation of damaged proteins and unbalanced proteostasis.47 As such change was exclusive to male mice, females seem to have higher proteostasis capacity, which is suggested by previous reports.48 Notably, estrogen is reported to enhance autophagy as well as mitochondrial quality control, which serves as another mechanism of clearing misfolded and damaged proteins,49,50 protecting females from any disruption in autophagic clearance. We also found that VU0486846 was able to attenuate these observed changes in males, suggesting that M1 mAChR positive allosteric modulation can rescue defective autophagy in response to Aβ accumulation. This is consistent with the changes we observed in hippocampal samples obtained from the same mice showing impaired autophagic flux in male APP/PS1 hippocampus that was rescued by VU0486846,19 indicating that the observed alterations in cortical autophagy are consistent with those reported in the hippocampus.

The subcellular localization of GPCRs is critical in controlling receptor availability and their downstream signaling.23 A previous study reported that prolonged in vitro treatment with Aβ results in uncoupling of M1 mAChR from its associated G protein within the plasma membrane.51 Additionally, there is also evidence that M1 mAChR is not only localized to the plasma membrane, but is also highly expressed in the intracellular compartments in hippocampus and cerebral cortex from rodents and human.21 Our data revealed that VU0486846 significantly increased cytosolic M1 mAChR levels in female, but not male, wild-type and APP/PS1 mice, whereas no significant differences were found when comparing its levels in the plasma membrane or the organelle membrane fractions. This increase in cytosolic M1 mAChR level may reflect an enhanced receptor synthesis, reduced degradation or increased cytosolic receptor pooling. We previously showed that VU0486846 increases the expression of M1 mAChR in the hippocampus of female APP/PS1 mice, with no differences in males,17,19 with the current findings suggesting that these increases could be exclusive to the cytosol-localized receptors. This cytosolic receptor pooling in females may prime cortical neurons in a sex-dependent manner to respond more robustly to acetylcholine-mediated signaling, which in turn enhances its downstream pathways. This sex difference could be attributed to the ability of estrogen to modulate mAChR expression and intracellular signaling.52 It is also known that estrogen plays a crucial role in regulating cholinergic signaling, particularly in the basal forebrain cholinergic neurons.53 Such sex-dependent regulation of M1 mAChR can provide a mechanistic framework for the divergent signaling response we observed when looking into how Aβ affects its downstream pathways.

Some limitations of the current study include targeting specific signaling pathways known to be downstream of M1 mAChR activation, whereas a more comprehensive and broader approach may reveal additional signaling mechanisms. We also did not include experiments illustrating the effect of sex hormone ablation. This will be investigated in future studies by subjecting the mice to surgical or pharmacological silencing of sex hormones to understand how they specifically interact with M1 mAChR downstream pathways. Incorporation of presymptomatic intervention at earlier stages of the disease is necessary to test the ability of VU0486846 to delay or prevent the pathology. Although monitoring of water intake did not reveal major differences between groups, direct pharmacokinetic assessment of VU0486846 concentration in the brain was not performed, which makes it difficult to confirm uniform brain exposure. Future studies including plasma and brain pharmacokinetic analyses would further strengthen the interpretation of the behavioral and biochemical outcomes. Nonetheless, the significant improvement in cognitive function that we reported in our earlier studies in both sexes provides confidence that the mice achieved adequate exposure to the drug.17,19 Finally, although we previously investigated alterations in autophagic flux in the hippocampus of these mice, changes in neuronal survival–related signaling markers, such as ERK1/2, were assessed only in the cortex, where signaling may exhibit region-specific differences. Therefore, future studies directly examining hippocampal ERK1/2 signaling are warranted.

In conclusion, our study provides novel insight into the sex-specific role of M1 mAChR in the pathophysiology of AD. We are showing evidence of sex-specific mechanistic differences in M1 mAChR-mediated alleviation of Aβ pathology, where in females, it is mediated via rescuing synaptic function and neuronal activity, whereas in males, it involves restoring autophagic flux, demonstrating distinctive mechanisms of how M1 mAChR positive allosteric modulation benefits each sex. We are also showing that M1 mAChR positive allosteric modulation enhances the cytosolic pooling of the receptor only in female mice cortices. These findings fill in a critical gap in literature, where sex-specific differences in M1 mAChR signaling and its directed therapies have been largely overlooked, underscoring the importance of sex as a biological variable in cholinergic pathways and how they contribute to AD pathology. They also provide more evidence of the therapeutic potential of M1 mAChR positive allosteric modulators in management of AD and how they could benefit in designing sex-tailored therapy.
