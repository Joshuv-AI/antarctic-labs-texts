Research in context

Evidence before this study

Short tandem repeat (STR) expansions are increasingly recognised as important genetic contributors to neurodegenerative diseases. Previous STR analyses in whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data using ExpansionHunter (EH) primarily focused on amyotrophic lateral sclerosis/frontotemporal dementia (ALS/FTD), ataxia, or movement disorder cohorts. Although these studies demonstrated high concordance between EH and PCR, they tended to underestimate repeat counts when the repeat length exceeded the read length. To date, no systematic studies have examined STR variations across major neurodegenerative dementias (NDDs), including Alzheimer's disease (AD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), and progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP). The contribution of STR expansions to the unexplained heritability and phenotypic heterogeneity of these dementias remains largely uncharacterised, particularly in Asian populations.

Added value of this study

Using integrated EH and EHDn analyses of WGS data from 1568 patients with NDD and 1522 cognitively normal controls, this study systematically profiled 22 neurological disease–associated STR loci. The combined approach markedly improved detection sensitivity for C9orf72 repeat expansions and uncovered additional pathogenic C9orf72 cases that EH alone failed to identify. Across all loci, pathogenic STR expansions were identified in 2.12% of patients with NDD compared to 0.47% in controls. While this frequency difference showed a trend toward enrichment (OR = 2.46, p = 0.09), it did not reach statistical significance. However, burden-based logistic regression analyses demonstrated that the presence of pathogenic STR expansions was significantly associated with NDD risk (OR = 3.57, p = 4.7 × 10−2). At the locus level, C9orf72 expansions remained significantly enriched in FTD after correction for multiple testing (p = 2.00 × 10−3). In addition, intermediate-length TBP alleles showed a nominal enrichment in PSP compared with controls (p = 2.00 × 10−2); however, this association did not withstand correction for multiple testing. Together, these results delineate the most comprehensive spectrum of known pathogenic STR mutations reported to date in patients with NDD.

Implications of all the available evidence

The integration of EH and EHDn enhances the clinical sensitivity for detecting C9orf72 repeat expansions in WGS data and facilitates the identification of missed pathogenic expansions. The relatively high prevalence and phenotypic diversity of STR expansions across dementia subtypes highlight the potential value of incorporating STR screening into the genetic evaluation of patients with early-onset, atypical, or familial dementia. Furthermore, the observed nominal enrichment of intermediate-length TBP alleles in PSP suggests a possible contribution of repeat-length variation to disease susceptibility, warranting further investigation in larger and independent cohorts.

Introduction

Neurodegenerative Dementia (NDD), including Alzheimer's disease (AD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), and progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), is a group of disorders with overlapping symptoms such as cognitive impairment, neuropsychiatric features, and movement disorders.1 Their heterogeneous and coexisting pathologies make accurate diagnosis difficult,2, 3, 4, 5 with early misdiagnosis rates up to 40%.6 With the emergence of disease-modifying, pathology-targeted therapies across NDD,7 accurate etiological diagnosis has become increasingly critical, as treatment strategies are expected to depend on the underlying molecular pathology rather than clinical presentation alone.

Short tandem repeats (STRs)—1-6 base pairs of tandem repeats accounting for ∼5% of the human genome—are a key yet underexplored source of pathogenic variations in NDD.8,9 Pathogenic STR expansions cause several neurodegenerative diseases, such as C9orf72 in FTD/amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), HTT in Huntington's disease (HD), FMR1 in fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome (FXTAS), and Notch2NLC in neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease (NIID).10, 11, 12, 13 Polyglutamine disorders, including SCA17, DRPLA, and SCA2, often manifest cognitive symptoms.14

Notably, expansions at the same STR locus can exhibit multiple clinical phenotypes. C9orf72 expansions, while typical of FTD/ALS, are also found in AD and PSP-like cases,15 and NOTCH2NLC expansions occur in AD/FTD-like phenotypes.16 Pathogenic ATXN2 expansions have even been identified in an autopsy-confirmed PSP case.17 Repeat length strongly correlates with disease penetrance and phenotype, as illustrated by intermediate HTT or ATXN2 alleles increasing risk for ALS/FTD.18, 19, 20 Even CAG repeat lengths in ATXN1 have been linked to AD cognitive performance.21

Systematically characterising disease-associated STR distributions in NDD is crucial for improving diagnostic precision and understanding dementia risk. Advances in whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and bioinformatics now enable large-scale screening of pathogenic STR.22 Yet, most studies have focused on ALS/FTD or other neurological disease cohorts,20,23 leaving comprehensive analyses in neurodegenerative dementia cohorts lacking.

ExpansionHunter (EH) is the primary tool for STR detection.24 EH uses flanking sequences to anchor genomic positions but is limited by read length, leading to insufficient anchored reads, misalignment, and false negatives for long expansions.25 ExpansionHunter Denovo (EHDn), by contrast, performs without predefined loci and is not constrained by read length. EHDn employs a sliding-window approach across the whole genome to directly identify genomic regions with abnormal enrichment of repeat motifs.26 Another method, STRling, addresses the challenge of misaligned reads by efficiently counting informative k-mers to detect expansions at both known and novel STR loci, offering high sensitivity and positional accuracy.27 Given the pivotal role of C9orf72 repeat expansions in dementias, particularly in FTD, and the unique core repeat motif (GGGGCC) only associated with C9orf72, we hypothesise that integrating complementary approaches, including EHDn motif analysis, STRling, and EH, could improve sensitivity to identify C9orf72 and other STR expansions.

To address this gap, we systematically screened 22 neurological disorder–associated STR loci using WGS data from 1568 Chinese patients with dementia and 1522 cognitively normal controls (NC). By integrating EH, EHDn, and STRling, we improved detection sensitivity for C9orf72 expansions. Our results revealed a high prevalence of STR expansions across NDDs and identified a suggestively significant link between intermediate TBP alleles and PSP. These findings provide insights into the genetic architecture of NDDs and support the use of STR profiling in early risk assessment and precision diagnostics.

Methods

Participants

A total of 1568 patients with NDDs, including 950 with AD, 222 with FTD, 165 with DLB, and 231 with PSP, were recruited from Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Changsha, China). Diagnosis was established independently by two neurologists according to international consensus criteria.28, 29, 30, 31, 32 Specifically, first, a neurologist established a preliminary clinical diagnosis for each patient. Subsequently, a second neurologist, blinded to the first's diagnosis, performed an independent review of all available medical records, laboratory results, and neuroimaging to determine if the case definitively met the established diagnostic criteria. Only cases meeting diagnostic criteria after both reviews were included.

Additionally, 1522 normal controls were recruited from the communities in Changsha, southern China. They underwent comprehensive clinical and neuropsychological assessments, and individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or dementia were excluded. To ensure genetic independence, we explicitly confirmed during recruitment that controls were not known first- or second-degree relatives of any participants. All participants or their legal guardians provided written informed consent and completed the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE).

STR loci associated with neurodegenerative diseases were analysed by ExpansionHunter (EH) and ExpansionHunter Denovo (EHDn), with PCR validation of potential expansions. Single-nucleotide variant (SNV) screening for dementia-associated genes was performed in parallel. The study design is summarised in Fig. 1.

Short tandem repeats detection using ExpansionHunter

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes using a QIAGEN kit. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was conducted on the BGI-seq 500 platform (Wuhan Huada Medical Laboratory) with 150 bp paired-end reads. The mean sequencing depth was 39.01 × for each sample. Quality control was performed on original sequencing FASTQ data using FastQC (v0.11.9; Babraham Bioinformatics). Then, filtered reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17).33 Final cram files were sorted and indexed with SAMtools (v1.8).34 Mean read length and insert size (fragment length) were extracted for each sample from alignment statistics generated using samtools stats (v1.8). Mean genome-wide sequencing depth was estimated as the total number of mapped bases (CIGAR-adjusted) divided by the effective hg38 genome size (3.09 Gb). Using PLINK 1.9, individuals were excluded if they had conflicting sex assessments between recorded sex and inferred sex, cryptic relatedness (identity by descent >0.15), or deviating heterozygosity (±3 standard deviations). Principal component analysis (PCA) was then conducted on the remaining participants to remove the outliers.

We selected STR loci implicated in conditions such as spinocerebellar ataxias with cognitive or psychiatric involvement, Huntington disease, fragile X–associated disorders, NIID, FTD/ALS, and other related repeat expansion disorders with cognitive impairment. 22 STR-associated neurodegenerative disease genes (Supplementary Table S1) were genotyped using ExpansionHunter (v4.0.1) according to the recommended process,22 producing variant call format (VCF) files containing repeat length estimates and confidence intervals. To improve repeat size estimation for loci known to harbour large expansions and/or alternative genomic sites containing long stretches of similar repeat motifs, we enabled the off-target mapping mode. Off-target regions were systematically defined using the ExpansionHunter Catalogue Creator workflow (https://stripy.org/expansionhunter-catalogue-creator). Additional off-target regions were included for 20 STR loci (except THAP11 and ZFHX3).

With respect to locus-level quality control, no additional manual filtering was applied beyond the internal filters implemented by EH. EH flags STR loci as LowDepth when either (a) the average coverage across the repeat region is below 10 × , or (b) fewer than five reads cross the boundary between the repeat and either flanking region, based on both spanning and flanking reads. This filtering criterion is applied uniformly to both homozygous and heterozygous STR genotypes, as documented in the EH software. Loci flagged as LowDepth were excluded from downstream analyses. Sample-level sequencing depth and fragment length were retained as covariates in all association analyses to account for potential batch effects related to library preparation and sequencing.

Detection of G4C2 repeat in C9orf72 using ExpansionHunter Denovo

Anchored in-repeat reads (IRRs), which span both the flanking sequence and the repeat region, are primarily used by EH to estimate repeat sizes. Repeats exceeding the sequencing fragment length can generate paired IRRs that may be misaligned by EH.25 To identify potential false negatives caused by short-read limitations in EH, we applied ExpansionHunter Denovo (EHDn) to the same WGS dataset.

EHDn was used in two complementary analyses: (1) motif-based analysis comparing normalised paired IRR counts of specific motifs between dementia and control samples, and (2) locus-based analysis summarising per-locus anchored IRR counts. No IRRs are expected for repeats shorter than 150 bp.26 Because the G4C2 motif uniquely defines pathogenic C9orf72 expansions, we focused on motif- and locus-based comparisons for GGGGCC and C9orf72 signals in subsequent analyses.

STRling analysis

To complement EH and EHdn, we additionally analysed the cohort using STRling, following the recommended workflow for joint calling, genotype calling, and outlier detection. STRling was run in joint-calling mode across all samples. Outlier loci were ranked using the allele2_est repeat size estimate and the corresponding p_adjust statistic. The resulting STRs.tsv output was queried for STR variants located within ±200 bp of the 22 disease-associated STR loci included in the EH analysis. Candidate expansions identified by STRling were compared with EH, EHdn, and PCR validation results to assess concordance and specificity.

PCR validation of repeat expansions

Repeat-primed PCR (RP-PCR) was performed to validate EH-predicted repeat that exceeded the intermediate threshold (PCR primers and conditions are available upon request). For C9orf72, pathogenic expansions were defined as alleles with >45 repeats. This threshold was selected because alleles in the 30–45 range have been reported in healthy individuals without evidence of somatic mosaicism or neurodegenerative phenotypes.35 For EHDn findings, RP-PCR targeting C9orf72 was applied to samples showing a positive G4C2 motif comparison count. PCR products were analysed by capillary electrophoresis on an ABI 3500 Genetic Analyser (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and repeat sizes were determined using internal size standards.

Variant calling

Variant calling and annotation were performed using GATK36 and ANNOVAR,37 respectively. Identified variants were queried against public databases (gnomAD, ExAC, ChinaMAP) to determine their population frequencies.38 For variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) below 0.01, pathogenicity was assessed using twenty-four in silico prediction tools (e.g., SIFT, PolyPhen2, LRT, MutationTaster, ReVe).39 56 dementia-related genes (Supplementary Text) were screened for pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines.40,41

Statistics

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test, while categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. The linear relationship between PCR-measured and EH-estimated repeat lengths was assessed using the Pearson correlation coefficient.

To assess associations between pathogenic expansions or intermediate alleles and disease status, we employed Firth's penalised likelihood logistic regression, which is robust to rare events and separation. All regression models were adjusted for potential confounders, including sex, age, mean sequencing depth, mean insert size, and the first three principal components (PC1–PC3). Multiple testing correction was performed using a Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of p < 0.05/22, corresponding to the total number of STR loci analysed. Statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.2), and data visualisation was performed using GraphPad Prism (v8).

In addition to locus-specific analyses, we performed burden-based analyses to evaluate the cumulative effect of pathogenic expansions or intermediate alleles on disease status. Associations between STR burden and disease status were assessed using Firth's penalised likelihood logistic regression, adjusted for sex, age, mean sequencing depth, mean insert size, and the PC1–PC3. To address potential heterogeneity in age at onset related to STR length, we further examined the relationship between STR repeat size and age at symptom onset among NDD cases. For each STR locus, repeat lengths estimated by EH were treated as continuous variables. Linear regression models were fitted with age at onset as the dependent variable and EH-estimated repeat length as the independent variable, adjusting for sex, mean sequencing depth, mean insert size, and PC1–PC3.

Statistical power analyses were performed using GPower (v3.1.9.7) to evaluate the sensitivity of the cohort sizes. To account for the multiple hypotheses tested in this study (22 genes across 4 disease phenotypes), we applied a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of α = 5.7 × 10−4 (0.05/88 tests). At this rigorous threshold, the overall NDD cohort (n = 1568) compared with cognitively normal controls (n = 1522) was sufficiently powered to detect moderate-to-large effect sizes, whereas smaller effect sizes would be difficult to detect. In contrast, for subgroup analyses with smaller sample sizes, such as PSP (n = 231) vs controls, statistical power was limited, particularly for detecting small-to-moderate effects. Accordingly, subgroup results were interpreted cautiously.

Results

Participant characteristics

Patients with NDD showed distinct clinical characteristics across subtypes. WGS-based STR analyses were performed for 950 patients with AD, 222 with FTD, 165 with DLB, and 231 with PSP. As summarised in Table 1, patients with FTD had the earliest mean age at onset (59.65 ± 10.30 years; Kruskal–Wallis test, H = 130.4, p < 0.0001). A positive family history was more common in AD and FTD (∼30%) than in DLB or PSP (χ2 = 56.2, p < 0.0001). The APOE ε4 allele frequency differed significantly among groups (χ2 = 296.7, p < 0.0001), being higher in AD (49.37%), DLB (38.18%), and FTD (30.18%) than in controls (20.04%), but lower in PSP (15.15%). The PSP cohort exhibited higher MMSE scores than other NDD groups (Kruskal–Wallis test, H = 1457, p < 0.0001), while education years did not differ significantly among NDD subtypes (Kruskal–Wallis test, p > 0.05). Across all samples, sequencing was performed using paired-end reads. The mean fragment length was 334.3 bp. Mean genome-wide sequencing depth was 38.6 × in all samples. In our cohort, we identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in PSEN1 (n = 11), APP (n = 3), PSEN2 (n = 3), MAPT (n = 4), FUS (n = 4), ERBB4 (n = 4), DCTN1 (n = 3), VCP (n = 2), CCNF (n = 2), and PRKAR1B (n = 1).

Identification and validation of STR variants

After sample quality control, 1559 patients with NDD (945 with AD, 221 with FTD, 230 with PSP, and 163 with DLB) and 1495 controls remained for analysis. EH identified 18 candidate expansions exceeding the lower threshold of the pathogenic range across nine STR-associated genes, all of which were subjected to confirmatory RP-PCR (Fig. 2A). The nine genes included HTT (HD), FMR1 (FXTA), Notch2NLC (NIID), DMPK (DM1), TBP (SCA17), and PPP2R2B (SCA12), which are known to be associated with cognitive impairment, as well as AR (SBMA), ATXN8OS (SCA8), and CACNA1A (SCA6). Among the 18 EH-predicted expansions, 14 (77.8%) were confirmed by RP-PCR to be within the pathogenic range, whereas 3 (16.7%) were within the normal range [Notch2NLC (n = 1), TBP (n = 1), and AR (n = 1)], and 1 (5.5%) was reclassified as an intermediate allele [FMR1 (n = 1)] (Fig. 2A).

In addition, EH identified 164 candidate intermediate alleles across 15 STR-related genes. Following RP-PCR validation, 135 (82.3%) were confirmed to fall within the intermediate-length range. Notably, 23 (14.0%) candidates were confirmed as pathogenic expansions [C9orf72 (n = 8), ATXN8OS (n = 8), Notch2NLC (n = 2), FMR1 (n = 2), AR (n = 2), and DMPK (n = 1)], while 6 (3.7%) cases were reclassified as normal [Notch2NLC (n = 3), FMR1 (n = 1), and HTT (n = 1)] (Fig. 2A).

Complementary analysis using EHDn identified four additional C9orf72 pathogenic expansions missed by the initial EH screen, three of which were confirmed as pathogenic by RP-PCR. In contrast, STRling did not identify any additional pathogenic expansions or intermediate alleles beyond those detected by EH and EHDn.

Neurodegenerative disease pathogenic STR expansions identified in 2.12% of patients with NDD

In our cohort, 33 patients harboured PCR-validated pathogenic STR expansions, representing 2.12% (33/1559) of the NDD cohort. The FTD cohort had the highest proportion of pathogenic expansions (5.88%, 13/221), with the C9orf72 gene being the most common affected (8/221, 3.62%), followed by Notch2NLC (2/221, 0.91%), ATXN8OS (2/221, 0.91%), and HTT (1/221, 0.45%). The AD cohort showed the second-highest prevalence (15/945, 1.60%), with pathogenic repeats identified in eight genes: C9orf72 (0.32%), ATXN8OS (0.32%), FMR1 (0.21%), Notch2NLC (0.21%), DMPK (0.21%), AR (0.11%), CACNA1A (0.11%), and HTT (0.11%). Among patients with PSP (n = 230), three carried pathogenic ATXN8OS expansions (1.30%). In DLB, two pathogenic expansions were observed in AR (0.61%) and PPP2R2B (0.61%). Overall, C9orf72 accounted for the largest number of pathogenic expansions (11/1559; 0.71%), followed by ATXN8OS (8/1559; 0.51%) and Notch2NLC (4/1559; 0.26%). In controls, pathogenic expansions were also observed in ATXN8OS (0.33%), HTT (0.07%), and FMR1 (0.07%) (Table 2, Fig. 3).

Across all loci, pathogenic STR expansions were more frequent in patients with NDD than in controls (2.12% vs 0.47%). After covariate adjustment, this difference showed a trend toward enrichment (OR = 2.46, p = 0.09), but did not reach statistical significance. However, burden-based logistic regression analyses demonstrated that the presence of pathogenic STR expansions was significantly associated with NDD status (OR = 3.57, 95% CI = 1.01–9.57, p = 4.70 × 10−2). Comparisons between the four subtypes and controls showed higher frequencies of pathogenic expansions in FTD (5.88% vs 0.47%; OR = 8.18, p = 1.63 × 10−4). Pathogenic C9orf72 expansions were significantly enriched in FTD relative to controls (p = 2.00 × 10−3) (Table 2).

Neurodegenerative disease intermediate STR alleles identified in 5.26% of patients with NDD

Intermediate-length STR alleles were identified in 82 patients with NDD, accounting for 5.26% of the cohort. Intermediate alleles were more frequent in patients with NDD than in controls overall (5.26% vs 3.61%; OR = 1.31), although this difference did not reach statistical significance after covariate-adjusted logistic regression (p = 0.23). Among NDD subtypes, the PSP group showed the highest proportion of intermediate alleles (6.09%, 14/230), followed by FTD (5.43%, 12/221), AD (5.08%, 48/945), and DLB (4.91%, 8/163). Within the PSP cohort, intermediate TBP alleles were the most frequent (2.61%, 6/230), followed by intermediate HTT alleles (1.30%, 3/230). After logistic regression, intermediate TBP alleles showed a nominal enrichment in PSP compared with controls (2.61% vs 0.54%; OR = 6.25, p = 0.02; Table 2); however, this association did not remain statistically significant after Bonferroni correction. Across the entire NDD cohort, intermediate alleles were most commonly observed in HTT (1.54%, 24/1559), followed by TBP (0.96%) and ATXN2 (0.51%). HTT intermediate alleles were distributed across AD (1.69%), FTD (0.91%), DLB (1.84%), and controls (1.27%). Furthermore, intermediate-length STR alleles showed no significant association with NDD status (OR = 1.28, 95% CI = 0.86–1.91, p = 0.23).

Clinical characteristics of pathogenic STR expansion carriers

Using EH-estimated repeat lengths as continuous variables, we fitted covariate-adjusted linear regression models within patients with NDD to assess associations between repeat length and age at onset. No significant associations were observed after multiple corrections.

Despite the lack of linear correlation with repeat length, early onset was a prominent feature in this cohort. Among the patients carrying pathogenic expansions, 72.73% (24/33) had an age at onset younger than 65 years old, and 60.61% (20/33) were APOE ε4-negative. Further analysis among patients with NDD revealed no difference in APOE ε4 frequency between carriers and non-carriers of pathogenic expansions or intermediate alleles (39.5% vs 40.5%; χ2 = 0.05, p = 0.80). However, compared with controls, patients carrying STR expansions still showed a significantly higher APOE ε4 frequency (39.5% vs 20.0%; χ2 = 24.8, p < 0.0001).

Of the 11 patients with pathogenic C9orf72 expansions, three presented with isolated memory loss without language or neuropsychiatric symptoms. All three had disease onset before age 60 (youngest 44 years) and a positive family history. During follow-up, one of these patients developed right upper limb bradykinesia with rigidity (Parkinsonian syndrome) one year later; initial brain MRI showed generalised atrophy without prominent frontal or temporal involvement. Another patient experienced recurrent absence seizures at onset, with MRI revealing mild temporal horn asymmetry (right larger than left). The third developed hoarding behaviour and anxiety approximately one year after initial symptoms. The remaining eight C9orf72 expansion carriers all exhibited language impairment or prominent psychiatric and behavioural symptoms (Table 3).

Performance of the STR screening pipeline

Overall concordance between EH estimates and RP-PCR

The concordance between EH–estimated and PCR-measured repeat lengths is summarised in Fig. 2B and C and Table 4. Overall, 74.7% of estimates were within ±2 repeats of the PCR size. Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated a strong linear relationship between EH estimates and PCR lengths for trinucleotide repeats (Pearson's r = 0.84, p < 2.2 × 10−16); in contrast, the GGGGCC repeat (C9orf72) showed no significant linear correlation (Pearson's r = 0.13, p = 0.67). This discrepancy is consistent with our concordance analysis results, where gene-specific concordance exceeded 85% for ATXN2, ATXN3, CACNA1A, HTT, PPP2R2B, AR, TBP, ATN1, and ATXN1, but was markedly lower for DMPK, FMR1, ATXN8OS, C9orf72, and Notch2NLC.

STRling results and validation

STRling analysis showed high concordance with EH for known disease-associated expansions. Based on allele2_est and p_adjust ranking, STRling identified some EH-detected and PCR-validated expansions, including 19 HTT expansions, 14 ATXN8OS expansions, seven ATXN2 expansions, six ATXN1 expansions, six DMPK expansions, three PPP2R2B expansions, two TBP expansions, one ATXN3 expansion, and 10 intermediate/pathogenic C9orf72 alleles, as well as one additional C9orf72 expansion detected by EHdn motif-based analysis. In addition, STRling identified four C9orf72 repeat outlier signals that were not detected by either EH or EHdn. PCR validation of these four samples did not confirm repeat expansions, suggesting that these STRling-only outliers represent false-positive signals rather than true pathogenic expansions.

Additional C9orf72 pathogenic expansions detected by EHDn and RP-PCR

Locus-based EHdn analysis detected anchored IRR counts >0 in eight samples at the C9orf72 G4C2 locus, all of which had been previously identified as pathogenic by EH and confirmed by RP-PCR (Fig. 4A). Motif-based EHdn analysis identified four additional samples (M1–M4; Fig. 4B) with paired IRR counts >0. RP-PCR validation confirmed three of these as pathogenic expansions and one as a normal repeat. For the three pathogenic cases, EH had estimated repeat lengths below 20 (within the normal range), whereas PCR sizing revealed pathogenic expansions, confirming these as EH false negatives (Supplementary Table S2). Read alignments from EH displayed no apparent mismatches or quality degradation, suggesting that visual inspection alone could not correct these EH false negative predictions (Fig. 4B).

In samples M2–M4, paired IRRs were detected solely by motif-based analysis, with anchored IRR counts at the locus being zero. This pattern suggests that EH misaligned these paired IRRs, leading to a failure in both detection and size estimation of the expansion. In contrast, sample M1 exhibited only a low paired IRR count in motif analysis and was PCR-confirmed as normal. Therefore, a high enrichment of paired IRRs in motif-based analysis appears to be a specific indicator of likely pathogenic C9orf72 expansions.

Assessment of false-negative rate for the EH/EHdn screening

A recognised limitation in the short-read sequencing-based screening of repeat expansions is the lack of assessment of false-negative rates, as studies have primarily focused on validating positive results. To directly address this gap and evaluate the sensitivity of our pipeline, we performed validation on a randomly selected subset of initially screening-negative cases. Specifically, we selected 24 patients with FTD who screened negative for pathogenic or intermediate alleles using both EH and EHdn. These individuals then underwent confirmatory testing via RP-PCR across the 22 STR loci included in our study. In this group, only one patient was found to carry an intermediate allele (41 CAG repeats) in the TBP gene that the initial screening had not detected, indicating a low false-negative rate for our approach in this cohort.

Discussion

This study provides an integrated analysis of WGS data using EH and EHDn to investigate known pathogenic STRs in 1568 individuals with NDDs and 1522 cognitively normal controls. Integration of EHDn with EH markedly improved the detection of C9orf72 expansions from WGS data. Across all loci, pathogenic STR expansions were identified in 2.12% of patients with NDD compared to 0.47% in controls. While this frequency difference showed a trend toward enrichment (OR = 2.46, p = 0.09), burden-based logistic regression analyses demonstrated that the presence of pathogenic STR expansions was significantly associated with NDD status (OR = 3.57, p = 4.7 × 10−2). Separately, intermediate-length STR alleles were identified in 5.26% of the NDD cohort (vs 3.61% in controls; OR = 1.31), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.23).

Given that heritability estimates for AD, FTD, DLB, and PSP range from 40% to 80%, but common single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) explain only a fraction of this risk,42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48 our findings emphasise the contribution of structural variants such as STRs to the “missing heritability” of these disorders. Burden-based logistic regression analyses further demonstrated that the presence of pathogenic STR expansions was significantly associated with NDD status (OR = 3.57, p = 4.7 × 10−2). At the locus level, C9orf72 expansions remained significantly enriched in FTD after correction for multiple testing (p = 2.00 × 10−3). In contrast, intermediate TBP repeat showed a nominal association with PSP (p = 0.02), which did not remain statistically significant after Bonferroni correction.

Systematic characterisation of STR expansions revealed extensive clinical pleiotropy and phenotypic heterogeneity among carriers. In the AD cohort, multiple expansions were detected in C9orf72, FMR1, and NOTCH2NLC, often presenting with atypical features. We acknowledge the possibility of coincidental overlap, as both AD and certain expansions (like FMR1 expansions) are not rare in the population. Some C9orf72 carriers exhibited isolated memory impairment or Parkinsonian syndrome rather than classic FTD symptoms, confirming its previously reported broad phenotypes (including AD-like manifestations).49,50 Female FMR1 premutation carriers displayed AD clinical presentations, including amyloid-positive PET scans. However, determining whether this represents a genuine phenotypic manifestation or a coincidental association requires further analysis in larger, dedicated cohorts. Notch2NLC cases lacked classic DWI corticomedullary junction high signal, but both showed T2-FLAIR white matter hyperintensities, supporting its potential role as a neuroimaging biomarker for NIID, as we reported before.16 DMPK carriers showed relatively low repeat counts, presenting with prominent cognitive decline and mild myopathy, consistent with the reported mild form of myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) in the Chinese Han population.51 All ATXN8OS carriers presented isolated memory impairment without cerebellar atrophy or ataxia, emphasising the phenotypic variability of spinocerebellar ataxia type 8 (SCA8).52, 53, 54, 55, 56 HTT expansion carriers developed typical motor symptoms within two years after disease onset, initially manifesting as memory decline, anxiety, and irritability. Neither CACNA1A nor AR carriers showed STR-related motor syndromes. These findings suggest that cases with C9orf72, Notch2NLC, and HTT expansions may require reclassification as FTD, NIID, or HD, respectively.

Within the FTD cohort, pathogenic expansions were most common in C9orf72 (n = 11), followed by Notch2NLC and ATXN8OS. Most C9orf72 carriers had a positive family history (60%) and early onset age (<60 years; 12/15), highlighting the diagnostic value of STR analysis in suspected familial or early-onset FTD. The observed frequency of pathogenic C9orf72 expansions in our cohort exceeds previously reported rates in Asian FTD populations.57,58 This discrepancy may be attributed to the higher proportion of positive family history and younger onset age in our cohort. Intermediate alleles in HTT, ATXN2, and Notch2NLC showed no significant association, consistent with prior findings that ATXN2 intermediate repeats are unlikely to be a primary risk factor for FTD, but may function as phenotypic modifiers.59,60

Among patients with PSP, TBP intermediate alleles (CAG repeats 41–46) were identified in six individuals and were significantly more frequent than in controls (2.61% vs 0.54%; OR = 6.25, p = 0.02). All carriers exhibited the classic Richardson syndrome phenotype without ataxia or cerebellar atrophy. TBP expansions cause elongated TATA-box binding protein, leading to neuronal dysfunction and SCA17.61 Our identification of the TBP intermediate alleles as a potential risk factor for PSP is consistent with a prior report of PSP-spectrum disorders in carriers. Notably, a TBP intermediate repeat (CAG repeats 45) was previously reported in a Chinese patient presenting with progressive ataxia, autonomic dysfunction, Parkinsonism, supranuclear palsy, and cognitive impairment.62 Although synergistic pathogenicity between intermediate TBP alleles and STUB1 variants has been proposed,63 no rare STUB1 variants were detected in this cohort. Compared to recent reports associating TBP repeats >38 with an increased risk of multiple system atrophy (OR = 1.64, P = 0.03),64 the risk association observed here in PSP was stronger (OR = 5.04). Prior genetic studies of PSP have established MAPT as the most robust risk locus, with additional contributions from LRRK2, LRP10, DCTN1, and GRN.65 Importantly, none of our TBP expansion carriers harboured these known risk variants,66 suggesting that TBP CAG repeats may represent an independent risk factor for PSP. We also acknowledge the potential impact of false-negative screening results inherent to short-read sequencing-based detection of repeat expansions. Any misclassification of true TBP intermediate allele carriers as non-carriers would be expected to occur in a non-differential manner with respect to case–control status and would therefore bias effect estimates toward the null. Consequently, the significant association observed between TBP intermediate alleles and PSP should be interpreted as a conservative estimate of the true effect size, supporting the robustness of the main finding despite the possibility of undetected carriers. Nonetheless, validation in larger, multi-center cohorts will be essential to refine risk estimates in the future.

Previous studies have used EH to detect pathogenic STRs followed by PCR validation.20,23 However, most focused on ALS/FTD or movement-related disorders. In contrast, our work systematically analysed STRs across major NDD subtypes using matched controls, confirming high overall concordance between EH predictions and PCR validation.20 As reported for DMPK and FMR1,23 EH tended to underestimate expansion size when repeats exceeded read length. Our data indicate that C9orf72 is particularly prone to such underestimation, while Notch2NLC shows poor concordance regardless of length, likely due to GC-rich sequence coverage bias.67 Nevertheless, EH achieved strong overall accuracy: 95% of 182 predicted expansions or intermediate alleles were PCR-confirmed, consistent with validation rates reported in ALS/FTD cohorts (≈93.6%). EHDn further addressed limitations inherent to short-read alignment. By applying both locus- and motif-based analyses, we identified three additional C9orf72 expansions missed by EH, demonstrating that EHDn effectively detects ultra-long repeats independent of predefined catalogues.26 Samples with elevated normalised paired IRR counts for the G4C2 motif were PCR-confirmed as pathogenic, while those with locus-based IRR counts >0 were all correctly classified by EH. Eight samples showed concordant positive results in both analyses, indicating that a combined EH + EHDn workflow increases detection sensitivity for C9orf72 expansions. Although EHDn has previously been used for RFC1 repeat identification,68 our analysis highlights the feasibility of applying motif-based detection to NDD-related STRs, expanding its screening feasibility. Additionally, the low false-negative rate observed for the STR screening pipeline supports the potential of WGS coupled with EH/EHdn as an efficient initial step to prioritise samples for confirmatory PCR testing.

A comprehensive mutation spectrum was delineated across known neurological disease-associated STRs in patients with AD, FTD, PSP, and DLB, providing the most extensive report of STR variations in Chinese patients with NDD to date. Integration of EH and EHDn improved the sensitivity of C9orf72 expansion detection and demonstrated the utility of combining complementary bioinformatics tools for reliable STR identification from WGS data. Our findings revealed that pathogenic STR expansions (C9orf72, HTT, and Notch2NLC) are not uncommon in clinically diagnosed patients with AD and FTD, highlighting the clinical and molecular heterogeneity of dementia. Systematic STR screening should therefore be considered in patients with atypical presentations, early onset, or strong family history. Early identification of STR expansions may assist in accurate diagnosis and patient counselling. Furthermore, the identified association between intermediate TBP alleles and PSP provides mechanistic insight into PSP pathophysiology and suggests a potential genetic biomarker for risk stratification.

This study has several limitations. First, although our patient cohort captures geographic diversity by including individuals from multiple provinces across Southern and Central China (e.g., Hunan, Jiangxi, Guizhou, Guangdong), recruitment was centred at a single hospital. We acknowledge that this design may not fully represent the broader Chinese population. Future multi-center studies across China are needed to establish the population prevalence and spectrum of STR expansions. Second, the analysis was restricted to 22 previously reported pathogenic STR loci. Reanalysis of sequencing data for new STR loci could identify additional expansions contributing to NDD risk. Third, clinical overlap among dementia subtypes may lead to diagnostic uncertainty, as current clinical criteria alone cannot always resolve atypical or mixed presentations. Therefore, larger, ethnically diverse, and phenotypically well-characterised cohorts are required to further evaluate the genetic burden and phenotypic specificity of STR expansions in NDDs.

In conclusion, our integrated genomic approach reveals that STR pathogenic expansions and intermediate alleles are significantly more prevalent in patients with NDD than in controls and identifies TBP intermediate alleles as a potential independent risk factor for PSP. These results underscore the contribution of repeat expansions to dementia risk and implicate these expansions in the pathogenesis of NDD.
