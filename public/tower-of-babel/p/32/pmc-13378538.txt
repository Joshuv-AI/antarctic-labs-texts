Optical-based stretchable multimodal sensors for detecting strain and light with periodic buckling structures
Authors: Tomohiro Anda, Yusuke Ebihara, Mari Koizumi, Kento Yamagishi, Tomoyuki Yokota, Takao Someya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stretchable optoelectronic sensors are essential for next-generation wearable health monitoring; however, a critical limitation remains unresolved. When a stretchable photodetector deforms, the effective light-detection location shifts across the skin surface, complicating the interpretation of spatially localized physiological signals. Herein, we report a periodic-buckling stretchable organic photodetector (OPD) that enables dual optical and strain sensing within a single device, allowing real-time estimation of changes in the effective detection position. The open-circuit voltage (Voc) depends solely on light intensity, whereas the short-circuit current (Isc) reflects both light intensity and variations in the active area, enabling geometric localization of the photodetection site. The OPD exhibits stable performance under applied strains of up to 60%, with variations in Voc below 1.1% and a strain gauge factor of 0.44 derived from Isc. This approach addresses a key limitation of stretchable photodetectors and provides a pathway toward high-resolution, position-aware wearable optical biosensing.

INTRODUCTION

Organic photodetectors (OPDs) are emerging as a versatile photodetection platform for next-generation flexible, stretchable and wearable optoelectronics, including biomedical sensors (1–4) and large-area imagers (5). Their mechanical compliance, inherent conformability, and compatibility with solution-based fabrication enable intimate integration with curved, soft, and dynamically deforming surfaces. In addition, their spectral tunability and high responsivity support a broad range of optical sensing applications (6).

Stretchable OPDs have attracted increasing attention because mechanically deformable photodetectors are essential for devices that must operate reliably under bending, twisting, and tensile strain. Among the various strategies to impart stretchability, a widely adopted approach involves laminating a flexible device onto a prestretched elastomer substrate to induce a buckling architecture (7–15). Prior demonstrations of stretchable OPDs based on this strategy have shown stable photodetection under repeated mechanical deformation. Nevertheless, further improvements in mechanical robustness and strain tolerance are necessary to ensure reliable operation under practical, real-world conditions.

To achieve high mechanical stability in stretchable devices with buckling architectures, precise control over buckling periodicity and orientation is critical. In the absence of such control, buckling becomes disordered, leading to localized strain concentration and, consequently, accelerated mechanical degradation. To mitigate this issue, several studies have reported the periodic control of buckling structures in organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) and polymer solar cells (PSCs), enabling more uniform strain distribution across the device (16, 17). These systems exhibited high stretchability along with excellent mechanical durability. In this study, structures with uniform wavelength and amplitude are defined as periodic buckling, whereas disordered structures are defined as random buckling.

Periodic buckling structures have been reported to exhibit improved repeatability compared with random buckling structures. The random buckling profile can change after cyclic stretching because bonding regions between the ultrathin device and the elastomeric substrate vary spatially. In contrast, periodically controlled buckling exhibits minimal structural fluctuation, resulting in high repeatability.

Beyond mechanical considerations, another critical challenge remains unresolved: Upon elongation of a stretchable OPD, the effective spatial location of light detection can shift relative to the target surface. Such positional drift severely compromises sensing accuracy in applications that rely on spatially localized optical sampling, including wearable biomedical monitoring, deformable imaging arrays, and soft machine vision systems. In stretchable imagers, OPD arrays deform nonuniformly, resulting in image distortion and inaccurate imaging. Therefore, accurate determination of the OPD detection position during deformation, inferred from strain information, is essential.

Strain during mechanical deformation can be estimated by integrating additional strain sensors. For example, stretchable display systems have used external strain sensors to calibrate pixel displacement and suppress image distortion (18–20). However, such calibration is often limited by spatial misalignment between the strain-sensing elements and the OPD or OLED pixels. Moreover, incorporating separate strain sensors increases circuit complexity and fabrication overhead. Consequently, a strategy that enables simultaneous measurement of optical signals and mechanical strain within a single device is highly desirable yet has not been previously reported.

In this study, we demonstrate multimodal detection of light and strain by measuring the open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current of a single stretchable OPD. The open-circuit voltage is known to depend solely on light intensity, whereas the short-circuit current is influenced by both light intensity and the effective illuminated area, which varies with applied strain (8). By concurrently monitoring these two electrical parameters, the relative shift in the optical detection position can be estimated without the need for separate sensing elements.

To realize strain detection, we integrated a flexible OPD with a stretchable substrate to achieve geometric stretchability through a buckling architecture. In intrinsically stretchable OPDs, elongation of the active materials inevitably alters device characteristics (21–23), and such performance variations are often unstable, limiting their applicability for reliable strain detection. In contrast, in geometrically stretchable OPDs, the semiconductor layers themselves are not directly stretched. Thus, device performance remains stable during deformation, and strain can be inferred from changes in the effective illuminated area.

Furthermore, we introduced a periodic buckling structure into the flexible OPD to enhance mechanical stability. The device performance was systematically evaluated in terms of strain sensitivity, multipoint detection capability, and real-time sensing behavior. This multimodal device architecture enables position-aware optical sensing and improved imaging accuracy in OPD arrays.

Device structure and control of the buckling

Here, we report multimodal detection of light and strain using a stretchable OPD integrated onto a stretchable substrate. Figure 1A presents the fabricated device, which is capable of stable operation under dynamic mechanical deformation.

The fabrication process involved separate preparation of the flexible OPD and the stretchable substrate, followed by their integration. The flexible OPD was fabricated on a supporting glass substrate; its detailed structure is shown in fig. S1. The flexible substrate comprised parylene and SU-8 layers with thicknesses of 1.5 and 7 μm, respectively. The OPD architecture consisted of indium tin oxide (ITO) as the cathode, zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) as the electron transport layer (ETL), poly[[2,5-bis(2-hexyldecyl)-2,3,5,6-tetrahydro-3,6-dioxopyrrolo[3,4-c]pyrrole-1,4-diyl]-alt-[3′,3″-dimethyl-2,2′:5′,2″-terthiophene]-5,5″-diyl] (PMDPP3T):[6,6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) as the active layer, molybdenum oxide (MoOx) as the hole transport layer (HTL), and silver as the anode. A 1.5-μm-thick parylene layer was subsequently deposited to serve as a passivation layer.

The stretchable substrate was fabricated using two silicone elastomers, Ecoflex 00-10 and Dragon Skin 30, as shown in fig. S2. The use of two elastomers served distinct purposes. The region onto which the flexible OPD was attached was composed of the softer Ecoflex 00-10, facilitating conformal adhesion and efficient strain transfer. This region incorporated a bellows structure designed to induce periodic buckling in the thin-film OPD. The bellows pattern consisted of a series of rectangular features with a height of 150 μm and a grating period of 800 μm, as determined by finite-element simulations described in the Materials and Methods section. In contrast, the main body of the substrate was fabricated from the stiffer Dragon Skin 30, providing higher resilience and minimizing performance fluctuations of the OPD under repeated stretching and compression.

Last, the flexible OPD was transferred onto the stretchable substrate (fig. S3). In the initial state, the OPD exhibited a buckled configuration. Upon uniaxial stretching of the substrate, the buckled structure gradually flattened, whereas releasing the applied force restored the buckled morphology. This design allowed the flexible OPD to undergo repeated stretching and compression without direct material elongation. Figure 1B shows a cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of the integrated device, whereas Fig. 1 (C and D) presents a cross-sectional schematic and an optical image, respectively. These results confirm successful device fabrication with well-controlled, periodic buckling. For comparison, a stretchable OPD with randomly distributed buckling was also fabricated as shown in fig. S4. The details of the fabrication process are described in the Materials and Methods section.

The periodic buckling structure improved mechanical stability compared with random buckling structures, as summarized in table S1. Humans and robots commonly experience small deformations more than 100,000 times; therefore, sensors must operate consistently and precisely over extended periods (25). Whereas many previously reported sensors endure only hundreds to thousands of deformation cycles, the present stretchable OPD demonstrates remarkable mechanical stability through controlled buckling architecture.

To further evaluate the impact of periodic buckling on mechanical robustness, an additional experiment was conducted using ITO, which is known to be one of the most mechanically fragile components in OPDs (26). ITO films with random and periodic buckling were fabricated, and their electrical resistance was measured during repeated release-stretch cycling. An image of the test structure is shown in fig. S6. The applied strain ranged from 0 to 50%, with a cycling speed of 30 cycles/min. The relationships between normalized resistance and the number of deformation cycles are presented in fig. S7. For ITO with random buckling, the normalized resistance increased steadily, reaching values of 4.2 and 32 after 100 and 1000 cycles, respectively, indicating crack formation and progressive mechanical degradation. In contrast, the ITO film with periodic buckling exhibited minimal resistance change, with normalized resistance values of 1.4 and 2.5 after 100,000 and 300,000 cycles, respectively. Between 300,000 and 400,000 cycles, failure occurred in the stretchable substrate rather than in the flexible ITO film, suggesting that the intrinsic mechanical stability of periodically buckled ITO is even higher than indicated by these measurements. These results confirm that periodic buckling is an effective strategy for enhancing the mechanical robustness of stretchable devices, even for mechanically fragile components such as ITO.

Multimodal detection of light and strain

Two fundamental figures of merit for evaluating OPD performance are the short-circuit current (Isc) and the open-circuit voltage (Voc), as illustrated in Fig. 3 (A and B). It is well established that Isc is a function of both the incident light intensity and the device active area. This dual dependence arises because Isc is defined as the product of the short-circuit current density (Jsc) and the active area, where Jsc originates from the photogeneration of electron-hole pairs per incident photon (27). The behavior of Jsc is equivalent to that of the photocurrent density (Jph) observed in the previous experiments. In addition, the active (illuminated) area changes proportionally with applied strain.

In summary, changes in the illuminated area cause variation in the short-circuit current (Isc) but do not influence the open-circuit voltage (Voc) or the short-circuit current density (Jsc), whereas changes in light intensity affect all three parameters. Similarly, when the area of a solar cell increases, Voc remains constant whereas only Isc increases (29). By exploiting these distinct dependencies, multimodal detection of light and strain can be achieved through simultaneous measurement of Voc and Isc. Variations in light intensity can be directly extracted from changes in Voc, whereas strain-induced modulation of the effective active area can be quantified by isolating the light-intensity contribution from the total measured change in Isc.

The measured current-voltage (I-V) characteristics of the stretchable OPD with periodic buckling under varying light intensity and applied strain are shown in Fig. 3 (C and D), respectively, and are consistent with the expected trends. As the light intensity increased from 0.22 to 47 W/m2, Isc increased from 0.17 to 34 μA, and Voc increased from 0.37 to 0.53 V. In contrast, as the applied strain increased from 0 to 60%, Isc increased from 27 to 34 μA, whereas Voc remained nearly constant at ~0.53 V. The dependences of Isc on light intensity and applied strain are summarized in Fig. 3 (E and F), respectively.

To evaluate strain-sensing performance, the gauge factor (GF) is commonly used in the literature. Various types of strain sensors, including resistive strain sensors (30–32) and capacitive strain sensors (33–36), use this metric. Resistive strain sensors detect deformation through changes in electrical resistance, whereas capacitive strain sensors rely on variations in capacitance. The GF is defined as the ratio of the relative change in resistance (ΔR/R0) or capacitance (ΔC/C0) to the applied strain (ε), expressed asGF=∆R/R0ε,∆C/C0ε(3)

Our stretchable OPD detects the applied strain by measuring Isc; thus, in our work, the GF is defined as the ratio of the relative variation of Isc to strain, which can be expressed as followsGF=∆Isc/Isc0ε(4)

The GF of this OPD was 0.44. The nearly identical GF values obtained under different illumination conditions demonstrate that the strain-sensing response is largely independent of light intensity, which is a desirable characteristic for reliable operation. These GFs were further compared with values reported in previous studies. Resistive strain sensors typically exhibit GFs ranging from 10−1 to 106, whereas capacitive strain sensors generally show GFs in the range of 0.7 to 1 (37–39). The GF of the stretchable OPD presented here falls within the same order of magnitude as capacitive strain sensors and a subset of resistive strain sensors.

High sensitivity, corresponding to a GF of ~10 or higher, is required for detecting subtle biological signals such as respiration, pulse, and muscle activity. In contrast, a GF below 1 is sufficient for monitoring macroscopic skin and joint deformations, including movements of fingers and knees. Because the present stretchable OPD is designed to detect dynamic skin motion, the achieved GF is considered appropriate for the targeted application. Beyond sensitivity, stretchability, hysteresis, and linearity are also important performance metrics for strain sensors. The maximum achievable strain of 60% satisfies the requirements for detecting skin deformation, as discussed earlier. Minimal hysteresis was observed in the stretchable OPD, as shown in fig. S8. Furthermore, the relationship between Isc and applied strain was well described by a simple linear model, with coefficients of determination exceeding R2 > 0.96.

To clarify the limitations of strain sensing, we additionally measured the relationship between Isc and strain when the applied strain was changed by 2% increments. As shown in fig. S9, it was confirmed that the Isc increased almost linearly. On the basis of these results, we can conclude that our OPD can detect strain with a resolution of at least 2%. According to a previous report, whereas this resolution can detect dynamic movements of hands, arms, and knees, it is insufficient for detecting subtle muscle movements like the face and neck (37).

The photoresponsivity of the stretchable OPD was calculated from Isc to be 0.24 A/W at 0% strain and 0.20 A/W at 60% strain. These values are comparable to the photoresponsivity measured at −1 V of bias voltage (0.24 A/W), as mentioned earlier. The slightly higher responsivity at a lower strain is attributed to enhanced light absorption caused by reflection and scattering at the buckled surface of the stretchable OPD. Similar behavior has been reported for stretchable photodetectors and organic photovoltaic (OPV) devices, which absorb more photons in the compressed state owing to the same effect (11, 15, 40). A consistent trend was also observed in the EQE spectra of the present OPD, as shown in fig. S10. Moreover, the relationship between Isc and light intensity exhibited excellent linearity (R2 = 1.000) across all applied strain levels.

The behavior of Voc is shown in Fig. 3 (F and G). Theoretically, Voc is independent of applied strain. Experimentally, only a slight variation of up to 0.006 V was observed at a light intensity of 0.47 W/m2, corresponding to ~1.7%, which is negligible. In contrast, and in agreement with theoretical predictions, Voc exhibited a clear logarithmic dependence on light intensity under all applied strain conditions, as evidenced by the linear relationship on a logarithmic scale (R2 = 1.000).

These characteristics were compared with previously reported sensors in table S2 (7, 8, 21, 22, 37, 38, 41–43). To the best of our knowledge, multimodal sensors capable of simultaneous light and strain detection have not been reported; therefore, this study represents dual sensing of light and strain within a single device. Accordingly, comparisons were made with individual OPDs and conventional strain sensors. The OPD exhibited sensitivity comparable to conventional light sensors and showed good linear fitting behavior in the logarithmic plot. When evaluated as a strain sensor, it also demonstrated excellent performance, including high linearity and negligible hysteresis. Although the GF is lower than that of conventional strain sensors, the sensitivity is sufficient for detecting dynamic deformation.

Four stretchable OPDs with periodic buckling were fabricated on a single stretchable substrate, and a polyimide mask was applied, as shown in Fig. 4A. When the OPDs were covered with the mask, only two devices were directly illuminated, whereas the remaining two were exposed to attenuated light. The I-V characteristics of these OPDs under applied strains ranging from 0 to 60% are shown in Fig. 4B, revealing the same trends in Voc and Isc as discussed in the previous section. From the measured Voc values, it was possible to distinguish between directly illuminated OPDs and those covered by the mask. The masked OPDs exhibited Voc values of ~0.48 to 0.49 V, whereas the unmasked OPDs showed higher Voc values of ~0.52 to 0.53 V. Demonstrations using multimodal sensors

Real-time detection of light intensity and strain was further demonstrated using a single stretchable OPD. The open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current were measured while independently varying the light intensity and applied strain. Because the open-circuit and short-circuit states cannot be realized simultaneously, the two states were alternated every 100 ms. In this approach, the OPD response time must be much shorter than the switching duration. Therefore, the transient responses of Voc and Isc were first measured. The results are shown in fig. S11 and table S3, indicating that the maximum response time was 900 μs, sufficiently shorter than the switching time. The corresponding values of light intensity and strain were then estimated from the measured Voc and Isc, using calibration relationships established in the previous section, as described in the Materials and Methods. The real-time detection was conducted under two conditions where light intensity was changed and where strain was changed.

First, the dynamic response of Voc and Isc to changes in light intensity is shown in Fig. 5A. When the light intensity was decreased in three steps (46, 19, and 4.6 W/m2), the measured values of Voc were 0.53, 0.50, and 0.46 V, whereas the corresponding Isc values were 23, 9.9, and 2.5 μA, respectively. Using the estimation function described in the Materials and Methods section, the corresponding light intensities were calculated to be 45, 20, and 4.6 W/m2 (Fig. 5B and Table 1), indicating that the maximum variation was 1 W/m2 (5.2% as the ratio).

Next, the response to applied strain is shown in Fig. 5C and movie S2. When the strain was increased in three steps (30, 40, and 50%), the measured Voc remained constant at ~0.53 V, whereas Isc increased gradually from 23.1 to 24.0 and 24.8 μA. These results demonstrate that real-time sensing of light intensity and mechanical strain can be achieved with high accuracy by monitoring Voc and Isc in a single stretchable OPD. To evaluate potential wearable applications, the OPD was attached to a subject’s finger and repeatedly stretched and released through finger-joint bending, confirming that the stretchable OPD can conform to flexible human motions. The resulting variations in Voc and Isc during these motions are shown in fig. S12. When a stretched finger was bent, the Isc increased approximately from 3.9 to 5.1 μA, and the Voc increased approximately from 0.39 to 0.42 V. The increase in the Isc was attributed to the increased strain in the stretchable OPD induced by finger bending. On the other hand, as shown in Fig. 3, only the Isc is expected to change when strain is varied. Nevertheless, the observed change in the Voc can be attributed to variations in light intensity caused by changes in the angle of light incidence. The effect of the incident angle will be discussed in detail later. In this experiment, the OPD was not integrated with a light source, and measurements were conducted under ambient light; therefore, changes in the incident angle upon bending became notable.

As mentioned in the previous experiment, the I-V characteristics of the OPD are influenced by the angle of light incidence. Therefore, we evaluated the incident-angle dependence of the Isc and Voc. The measurement setup is shown in fig. S13 (A and B), where the incident angle varied from 0° to 60°. The Isc and Voc of the stretchable OPD and the rigid OPD are presented in fig. S13 (C and D). When the incident angle was increased from 0° to 60°, the Isc decreased by ~50 to 60%, whereas the Voc decreased by about 0.02 to 0.03 V. This is because, as the incident angle increases, the amount of light per unit active area decreases (it is reduced to half at an incident angle of 60°). As described above, the Isc varies linearly with light intensity, whereas the Voc exhibits a logarithmic dependence; therefore, these results are consistent with such relationships.

In addition to incident light angle and bending, operating temperature also influences Voc and Isc in OPDs. Ideally, sensing should be performed under constant incident angle, bending state, and temperature; however, temperature dependence can be compensated using the dark current density. The temperature dependence appears in the slope of the linear region, expressed as e/nkBT. The result shown in fig. S14 follows this theoretical trend. Therefore, temperature can be estimated from the slope of the J-V curve, allowing correction of variations in Voc and Isc caused by temperature changes.

DISCUSSION

We developed a fabrication process for a stretchable OPD incorporating a periodic buckling structure and successfully demonstrated its performance. Precise control of the buckling geometry enabled stretchability of up to 60%, which is sufficient to achieve conformal contact with human skin, including over joint regions. Moreover, under such highly dynamic mechanical deformation, the device exhibited stable J-V characteristics, indicating excellent mechanical robustness and reliable photodetection. In particular, the OPD showed no observable degradation even after 100,000 release-stretch cycles, highlighting the effectiveness of periodic buckling control for durable wearable applications.

In addition, multimodal detection of light and strain was achieved using a single stretchable OPD by measuring Voc and Isc. The light and strain sensitivities derived from Isc 0.24 A/W and a GF of 0.44, respectively. The light sensitivity was comparable to that obtained under reverse-bias operation, and although the GF was relatively low, it was sufficient to detect dynamic skin deformation. Both light-dependent and strain-dependent responses exhibited good linearity and minimal hysteresis. Meanwhile, Voc increased logarithmically with light intensity and remained essentially independent of applied strain. Leveraging these complementary characteristics, we demonstrated multipoint detection and real-time sensing, enabling estimation of the effective light-detection position during mechanical deformation.

To further enhance mechanical robustness and sensing performance, two future improvements are envisioned. First, operation in the near-infrared spectral region, where tissue transparency and light absorption characteristics are favorable, may improve performance for wearable health monitoring. Second, further reduction of the flexible OPD thickness is expected to enhance mechanical stability as flexural rigidity scales with the cube of the thickness (44). Together, these approaches will facilitate the practical application of the proposed OPD architecture in next-generation wearable sensing systems.

Fabrication of the flexible OPD

First, a flexible substrate (2.4 cm by 2.4 cm) was fabricated. A glass substrate was coated with a fluoropolymer release layer by spin-coating a solution of Novec 1700 in Novec 7100 (1:6, v/v) at 2000 rpm for 30 s. The coated substrate was annealed at 180°C for 30 min under a nitrogen atmosphere to facilitate subsequent film release. A 1.5-μm-thick parylene layer, serving as the primary structural layer, was then deposited by chemical vapor deposition (CVD). To planarize the parylene surface, a 7-μm-thick SU-8 3005 layer was spin coated at 500 rpm for 5 s followed by 4000 rpm for 30 s. The SU-8 layer was soft baked at 95°C for 3 min, exposed to ultraviolet light for 5 min, and then postbaked at 65°C for 1 min and 95°C for 3 min in ambient air. Last, the substrate was hard baked at 180°C for 30 min under nitrogen to stabilize the film structure.

In the second step, the OPD was fabricated on the flexible substrate. ITO was deposited by dc sputtering and patterned by photolithography followed by wet etching. The ITO surface was activated by oxygen plasma treatment at 50 W for 10 s. Subsequently, the device layers were deposited sequentially. A ZnO NP solution (10 wt % in isopropanol) was spin coated at 5000 rpm for 30 s and annealed in ambient air. The photoactive layer solution was prepared by dissolving PMDPP3T (6 mg) and PCBM (18 mg) in a mixed solvent of 1,2-dichlorobenzene (0.2 ml) and chloroform (0.8 ml), followed by overnight stirring at 60°C and 300 rpm under nitrogen. After being dispensed onto the substrate for 3 min, the active layer was formed by spin-coating at 4000 rpm for 1 s. A 7.5-nm-thick layer of MoOx and a 100-nm-thick silver electrode were then deposited by thermal evaporation. Last, the device was encapsulated with an additional 1.5-μm-thick parylene layer deposited by CVD. The active area of the OPD was 0.04 cm2.

Fabrication of the stretchable substrate and attaching the flexible OPD

A stretchable substrate designed to control the buckling structure of the flexible OPD was fabricated following the fabrication process shown in fig. S2. First, Ecoflex 00-10 was cast onto a bellows-patterned metal mold. The mold, which defined the buckling geometry, consisted of a linear array of rectangular features with a height of 150 μm and a grating period of 800 μm, as determined by finite-element simulations described in the following section. The Ecoflex 00-10 layer was thermally cured to a thickness of ~0.7 mm, after which the nonpatterned excess regions were removed. Subsequently, Dragon Skin 30 was cast onto the cured Ecoflex 00-10 layer and thermally cured, resulting in a composite stretchable substrate with a total thickness of ~1 mm.

The flexible OPD was then integrated onto the stretchable substrate, as shown in fig. S3. The stretchable substrate was first prestretched to 80% strain, whereas an adhesive layer was prepared on the flexible OPD. The adhesive was formulated by mixing Super XG and toluene at a weight ratio of 3:2 and spin coated onto the flexible OPD at 1800 rpm for 30 s. The adhesive-coated flexible OPD was subsequently laminated onto the prestretched substrate, and a compressive force of 10 N was applied for 4 hours to ensure uniform bonding. After the adhesive had fully cured, the supporting glass substrate was carefully peeled off. Upon release of the prestretch, the stretchable substrate recovered to its original length, inducing a periodic buckling structure in the flexible OPD that conformed to the underlying bellows pattern.

The stretchable OPD with random buckling was fabricated on the same stretchable substrate without geometric patterning.

Simulation of the buckling property

When the prestretch applied to a stretchable substrate is released, a flexible device bonded to its surface undergoes compressive strain, resulting in the formation of a periodic, wavy buckling structure. It is well established that the wavelength and amplitude of the buckling depend on the prestretch ratio and the thickness of the flexible device (45). The wavelength (λ) and amplitude (A) are expressed asλ=λ0(1+εpre)(1+ξ)1/3,A=A01+εpre(1+ξ)1/3λ0=2πt(Ef3Es)1/3,A0=tεpreεc−1εc=0.25(3EsEf)2/3,A0=tεpreεc−1(5)

Here, t and εpre denote the thickness of the flexible device and the prestretch ratio, respectively. Ef and Es denote the plane-strain moduli of the film and the substrate. According to Eq. 5, when the prestretch ratio is fixed (80% in this study), both the buckling wavelength and amplitude are proportional to the thickness of the flexible device. The theoretical wavelength and amplitude were simulated using Eq. 5 for a flexible OPD with a nominal thickness of 10 μm. The Young’s moduli of parylene, SU-8, Ecoflex 00-10, and Dragon Skin 30 were taken as 4 GPa, 2 GPa, 55 kPa, and 600 kPa, respectively. The Poisson’s ratios of the flexible OPD and the stretchable substrate were set to 0.50 and 0.48, respectively. This simulation predicted a wavelength of 699 μm and an amplitude of 148 μm, as shown in fig. S15. On the basis of these results, a patterned mold with a wavelength of 800 μm and an amplitude of 150 μm was designed and used for fabrication.

The resulting stretchable OPD, shown in the cross section in fig. S15, exhibited a well-defined periodic buckling structure. However, the experimentally measured wavelength and amplitude were ~928 and 320 μm, respectively, which are larger than the designed values. This discrepancy arises from the presence of the adhesive layer used to bond the OPD to the stretchable substrate. The initial simulation considered only the 10-μm-thick OPD stack, whereas the adhesive layer contributes additional thickness to the buckling system. This increased effective thickness leads to a longer buckling wavelength and larger amplitude. Furthermore, the adhesive has its own mechanical modulus; the inclusion of a relatively soft adhesive layer modifies the effective stiffness of the composite film and the modulus mismatch with the substrate, thereby altering the final buckling geometry.

Stretching experiments

Cyclic deformation tests were performed using a sheet tensioning device (DMLHP-FSN, YUASA SYSTEM Co.).

Measurement of device characteristics

The J-V characteristics and EQE measurements were conducted using a spectrophotometer (SM-250 Hyper Monolight System, Bunko Keiki Co.) and a source meter (Keithley 2400). For real-time measurements, the circuit was alternated between open-circuit and short-circuit states every 100 ms using a switching matrix (Keithley 707A), whereas cyclic strain was applied using a motorized linear stage (LTS150/M, Thorlabs Inc.). Measurements were performed at an incident angle of 0° without bending.
