Undulanoids A−D: Unexpected sesterterpenoids as potent S100A8/A9 complex inhibitors for psoriasis treatment
Authors: Yuyi Zheng, Xiaotian Zhang, Xiaoxia Gu, Yongqi Li, Qin Li, Yingli Zhu, Bingbing Dai, Yu Liang, Ruping Fan, Chunmei Chen, Weiguang Sun, Yonghui Zhang, Hucheng Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsoriasis is a common immune-mediated skin disorder manifesting in abnormal skin plaques, and remains a challenge in its management. Blocking the release or inflammatory effects of two proinflammatory molecules of the S100-alarmin family, S100A8 and S100A9, in keratinocytes is a promising strategy for future therapeutic approaches. Undulanoids A−D (1−4), four novel sesterterpenoids possessing a highly congested pentacyclic 6/5/5/6/5 ring system with eight stereogenic centers, including three all-carbon quaternary centers, two quaternary carbon centers at the bridgehead, and a 1,4,11-trimethyltricyclo[5.3.1.04,11]undecane fragment, were isolated from Aspergillus undulatus. Their structures were elucidated by spectroscopic data and single-crystal X-ray diffraction. Strikingly, undulanoid B (2), the most promising lead compound, inhibits the expression of genes related to tumor necrosis factor and interleukin-17 signaling pathways. Furthermore, reverse target prediction, cellular thermal shift assay, and dynamic simulation indicated that compound 2 could target with the expression of S100A9 and keratinocyte proliferation. As the pioneering S100A8/A9 complex and inhibit its secretion. Moreover, compound 2 showed a potent therapeutic effect on the psoriasiform skin lesions induced by imiquimod in mice by inhibiting the expression of S100A9 and keratinocyte proliferation. As the pioneering examples of natural products demonstrate inhibitory action against S100A8/A9 complex, this discovery provides a series of compelling lead compounds with novel molecular scaffold for treating psoriasis.Key words: Undulanoids, Psoriasis, S100A8/A9 heterocomplex, Aspergillus undulatus, Sesterterpenoids, Immunosuppressant, Natural products, Structure elucidation

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease induced by multiple environmental factors. It is characterized by erythematous plaques covered with silver white scales and affects about 2%−3% of the global population1, 2, 3. The typical histological features of skin plaques include over-proliferative keratinocytes in the epidermal layer and infiltration of dendritic cells, T lymphocytes, neutrophils, and macrophages in the dermal layer4. The efficacy of conventional topical treatments for psoriasis, such as corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, vitamin D analogs, anthralin, and coal tar, is often limited by their side effects and tolerability5. Thus, new types of anti-psoriatic treatments are urgently needed.

The pathogenesis of psoriasis is complicated and not yet fully understood. Current research suggests that the most likely pathogenic mechanism is the interaction of immune cells and keratinocytes via cytokines6. During the development of psoriasis, abnormally activated dendritic cells secrete cytokines such as interleukin-23 (IL-23) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)7. Then, Th17 is initiated by the action of IL-23 to form IL-23/IL-17 axis. Th17 plays a pivotal role in this process, and the IL-23/IL-17 axis has been recognized as the primary immune pathway associated with the development of psoriasis8.

Two proinflammatory molecules of the S100 family, S100A8 (myeloid-related protein 8, calgranulin A) and S100A9 (myeloid-related protein 14, calgranulin B), are highly upregulated in keratinocytes and leukocytes of psoriatic skin but are undetectable in the skin of healthy controls5,9. Traditional wisdom holds that the heterocomplex of S100A8/A9 (calprotectin) is released during cellular stress or tissue damage and acts as a damage-associated molecular pattern molecule or alarmin10,11. Recent research has demonstrated numerous biological functions of the S100A8/A9 complex, including calcium homeostasis, cytoskeletal dynamics, inflammatory response regulation, energy metabolism, and antimicrobial properties12, 13, 14. S100A8/A9 complex is involved in the development of autoimmune-associated diseases such as psoriasis15, 16, 17, rheumatoid arthritis18, and systemic lupus erythematosus19,20. Inhibition of S100A8/A9 complex production or release has been shown to produce notable therapeutic benefits, highlighting the potential of S100A8/A9 complex-targeting therapies.

Sesterterpenoids constitute a rare but highly significant class of terpenoid compounds. Current research reveals that merely 1600 sesterterpenoids have been isolated and structurally characterized, representing a remarkably low 2.0% of the total terpenoid family21,22. Many sesterterpenoids have been isolated from Aspergillus, exhibiting a wide array of biological properties23, 24, 25, 26, such as immunosuppression and antitumor activity27, 28, 29, 30, 31. Studies of the structural and biological diversity of Aspergillus metabolites and the ongoing chemical investigations of Aspergillus undulatus32, 33, 34, 35, have resulted in the isolation and identification of four novel sesterterpenoids with a highly congested pentacyclic 6/5/5/6/5 ring system, named as undulanoids A−D (1−4, Fig. 1A). Remarkably, undulanoid B (2) significantly inhibited TNF and IL-17 signaling pathways in TNF-α induced HaCaT cells, and ameliorated psoriasis-like dermatitis in a murine model of imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasis. Mechanistic studies show that compound 2 can bind to the hydrophobic pockets of calprotectin to induce dissociation. To our knowledge, this is the first example of natural products that can inhibit calprotectin expression and exhibit potent anti-psoriasis potency. Their isolation, structure elucidation, plausible biogenetic pathway, biological evaluation, and target identification are described herein.

Results and discussion

Structural elucidation of sesterterpenoids with 6/5/5/6/5 ring system

The fungal strain Aspergillus undulatus was fermented on a solid-substrate medium (rice 70 kg) for 40 days at 25 °C. After being extracted with EtOH to obtain a residue, the residue was dispersed in water and successively partitioned with EtOAc. The EtOAc extract (600 g) of the fermentation was fractionated by repeated silica gel chromatography, C-18 column chromatography (CC), Sephadex LH-20 column chromatography, and finally high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to obtain compounds 1−4.

Undulanoid A (1), obtained as a colorless crystal, has a molecular formula of C25H40O2 as deduced by HRESIMS (m/z 395.2925 [M+Na]+, Calcd. for C25H40O2Na+, 395.2926), corresponding to six indices of hydrogen deficiency. The IR absorption peak at 3363 cm−1 suggested the presence of hydroxy group. The 1H NMR data of compound 1 (Supporting Information Table S1) showed six methyls, including three tertiary (δH 0.91, 0.88, and 0.86) and three secondary (δH 0.95, d, J = 6.6 Hz; 0.92, d, J = 5.6 Hz; and 0.81, d, J = 5.6 Hz) ones, and one oxygenated methine (δH 2.91, m). The 13C NMR data of 1 (Table S1) exhibited 25 carbon resonances assignable to six methyls, seven methylenes, six methines (an oxygenated), and six nonprotonated carbons (two olefinic, one oxygenated, and three quaternary). The double bond accounts for one of six indices of hydrogen deficiency, reflecting a pentacyclic system from the remaining five degrees of unsaturation.

The planar structure of 1 was determined mainly by 1H−1H COSY and HMBC spectra (Fig. 1B). The 1H−1H COSY spectrum showed cross-peaks of H2-1/H-2 and H2-13/H-14/H-18/H2-17/H2-16, along with HMBC correlations from H3-23 to C-1, C-14, C-15, and C-16, and from H2-13 to C-2, C-12, and C-15 confirmed the fusion of the five-membered ring E with the cyclohexane ring D. In addition, 1H−1H COSY correlations of H-18/H-19/H3-24 (H3-25) and HMBC interactions from H3-24 and H3-25 to C-18 indicated that an isopropyl group was attached to C-18. The 1H−1H COSY correlations of H2-4/H2-5 and H3-21/H-7/H-8/H2-9, in combination with the HMBC correlations from H3-20 to C-2, C-3, C-4, and C-11, from H3-21 to C-6, C-7, and C-8, from H3-22 to C-3, C-6, C-10, and C-11, from H2-5 to C-3, C-6, C-7, and C-11, from H-7 to C-6 and C-11, and from H2-9 to C-10, C-11, and C-12, constructed an unexpectedly rigid 6/5/5 tricyclic moiety, in which rings A−C fused via C-3/C-11, C-6/C-11, and C-10/C-11 to form a cobridged center at C-11. The complete structure of 1 was established by the fusion of ring C with ring D via C-2 and C-12, as revealed by the HMBC correlations from H2-13 to C-2, C-10, and C-12, and from H-2 to C-3, C-4, C-10, and C-11. Therefore, the planar structure of 1 was deduced to possess an unusual 6/5/5/6/5 pentacyclic carbon scaffold with a cyclopenta[cd]indene fragment.

The relative configuration of 1 was determined by analysis of the NOESY spectrum. The NOESY correlation (Fig. 1C) of H3-23/H-2 revealed their 1,3-diaxial relationship and they were randomly assigned β-orientation. The correlations of H3-23/H-13β and H-13β/H-19 suggested that H-18 should be α-oriented, and interactions of H-1α/H-14 revealed the α-orientation of H-14. Furthermore, the cross-peaks of H-2/H-4β, H-4α/OH-6, and OH-6/H3-22 assigned the α-orientation of H3-20, H3-22, and OH-6 as well as the cis-fusion patterns of the A/B and B/C rings. This unique formation of three tightly coupled rings resembles a nest. The Δ10,12-double bond and conjugation with rings A and B confer a near-coplanar structure to the atoms of ring C. In addition, NOESY interactions of H3-22/H-7 and H-5β/H-8 suggested the α-orientation of H-7 and the β-orientation of H-8. Finally, the structure of compound 1 was confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction using Cu Kα radiation (Fig. 1D), and its absolute configuration was defined as 2S,3R,6R,7S,8R,11R,14S,15R,18R based on the Flack parameter of −0.05(4).

Undulanoids B and C (2 and 3) were obtained as colorless crystals with a molecular formula of C25H40O2 as determined by the HRESIMS ion ([M+Na]+ ion at m/z 395.2913 for 2 and 395.2922 for 3, Calcd. for 395.2926). Their 1H and 13C NMR data (Table S1) closely resembled that of 1, indicating that they shared the same carbon skeleton. The difference between 1 was the migration of the hydroxy group from C-8 to C-16 in 2, as shown by the 1H−1H COSY correlations of H3-21/H-7/H2-8/H2-9 and H2-13/H-14/H-18/H2-17/H-16, together with HMBC correlations from H3-21 to C-6, C-7, and C-8 (δC 32.7), and from H3-23 to C-1, C-14, C-15, and C-16 (δC 79.4) (Fig. 1B). In addition, the hydroxy group located at C-6 in 1 migrated to C-16 in 3, which was verified by 1H–1H COSY correlations of H2-4/H2-5/H-6/H-7 (H3-21)/H-8/H2-9 and H2-13/H-14/H-18/H2-17/H-16, and the HMBC correlations from H3-21 to C-6 (δC 51.8), C-7, and C-8 and from H3-23 to C-1, C-14, C-15, and C-16 (δC 77.1) (Fig. 1B). The α-orientation of OH-16 in 2 and 3 was assigned based on the NOESY interaction of H3-23/H-16 (Fig. 1C). Finally, the absolute configurations of compounds 2 and 3 were determined by single-crystal X-ray diffraction using Cu Kα radiation (Fig. 1D).

Undulanoid D (4) was obtained as a colorless oil. It possesses the same molecular formula as 1−3 deduced from its sodium adduct ion [M+Na]+ at m/z 395.2912 (Calcd. for 395.2926). The primary difference in 4 is the oxidation of H3-21 to hydroxymethyl, verified by the HMBC correlations from H2-21 to C-6, C-7, and C-8 (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, H-7 was determined to be α-oriented based on the NOESY correlation of H3-22/H-7 (Fig. 1C). Based on the similar electronic circular dichroism spectra (Supporting Information Fig. S1) of 4 with 1, the absolute configuration of 4 was established as 2S,3R,6R,7R,11R,14S,15R,18R.

Compounds 1−4 possess an unexpected 6/5/5/6/5 pentacyclic carbon skeleton, for which we propose a possible biosynthetic pathway in which the distinctive terpene synthases and post-modification enzymes are poised to capture the attention of biosynthetic researchers (Fig. 1E). Starting from geranylfarnesyl pyrophosphate, a series of cyclizations and Wagner−Meerwein rearrangements generate the key intermediate v, which possesses a high-tension cyclobutane ring. The intermediate v underwent two consecutive Wagner−Meerwein 1,2-alkyl shift reactions to generate intermediate vii, then underwent dehydrogenation to generate the sesterterpene hydrocarbon viii. Ultimately, multistep oxidations led to compounds 1−4.

Preliminary biological assay of compounds1−4in HaCaT cells

Natural products are an essential source of inflammatory disease therapies36. Given that one of the primary therapeutic strategies for psoriasis is targeting keratinocyte proliferation, we evaluated the anti-hyperproliferation effects of compounds 1−4 in HaCaT cells induced by TNF-α. Indeed, 1−4 exerted strong inhibitory activities with half-maximal inhibitory concentration values of 2.47, 1.25, 6.72, and 8.54 μmol/L (Fig. 2A), respectively, with low cytotoxicity in normal HaCaT cells (Supporting Information Fig. S2). Flow cytometry was used to detect CD4, CD8, and natural killer cell populations in ConA−induced mouse primary spleen cells (Fig. 2B and Supporting Information Fig. S3). ConA triggered a significant increase in the CD4/CD8 ratio (>2.5), a typical feature of autoimmune disorders. In this experiment, compounds 1 and 2 restored the immune cell ratio to normal levels, indicating effective immunoregulatory activity.

Compound2inhibitsTNF andIL-17signaling pathways in HaCaT cells

Preliminary analysis led to the selection of 2 for efficacy and mechanistic studies. The mechanism of 2 was characterized by using transcriptome-based RNA sequencing of HaCaT cells to identify differentially expressed genes after exposure to TNF-α and compound 2. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the RNA-sequencing data demonstrated clear functional clusters (Fig. 3A). Compared to the control, 280 genes were upregulated and 317 were downregulated in the model group. In contrast, the compound 2-treated group showed 1709 upregulated and 2704 downregulated genes. These results were illustrated in volcano plots with upregulated genes in red and downregulated genes in blue (Fig. 3B and C). We then examined the overlapping upregulated and downregulated genes across groups and found that 194 and 241 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were restored 2 to baseline levels (Fig. 3D and E). Immune regulators TNF-α and IL-17, both of which represent key pathogenic pathways in psoriasis37, were enriched in the pathway analysis. The dot plot highlights enriched KEGG pathways based on DEGs, with pathways such as TNF signaling, IL-17 signaling, and NF-κB signaling boxed in red, indicating their involvement in the observed phenotypic changes among the groups (Fig. 3F). Additionally, we identified 29 and 18 DEGs associated with IL-17 signaling, and 40 and 21 DEGs related to TNF signaling, with 5 and 7 overlapping DEGs respectively (Fig. 3G). Finally, a heatmap was generated to illustrate the expression patterns of selected DEGs across control, model, and treatment groups, highlighting genes associated with inflammation (Fig. 3H). Given the observed enrichment for inflammatory pathways, we focused on the expression of proteins in these pathways. We observed a consistent decrease in inflammation-related proteins such as COX-2, NLRP3, p-IκB, and p-NF-κB (Fig. 4A). Nuclear translocation of p65 is a pivotal event in NF-κB signaling38. Indeed, immunofluorescence imaging revealed a compound 2-associated decrease in NF-κB p65 phosphorylation in the cytoplasm and blockade of p65 nuclear translocation (Fig. 4B), and 1 induced a similar decrease (Supporting Information Fig. S4).

S100A8/A9is identified as a direct targetofcompound2

We performed a structure-based virtual screen using Pharm Mapper (http://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/) to identify the putative direct molecular targets of compound 2 (Supporting Information Table S2)39, 40, 41. Pharmmapper is a powerful free tool for identifying molecular targets of small molecules using a pharmacophore mapping approach. The analysis identified S100A8/A9 as the top-ranking target for the inflammatory response. Calprotectin functions as an (S100A8/S100A9)2 heterotetramer in the physiological environment (Fig. 5A), and Ca(II) coordination facilitates the association between two αβ heterodimers and mediates affinity between the protein and transition metals42,43. Molecular docking analysis was performed to validate the interaction of calprotectin with compounds 1−4, using a 1:2 protein: ligand ratio, given the two hydrophobic binding sites for endogenous ligands, and 4GGF as the protein model. Two equivalents of all four compounds were successfully docked into the hydrophobic pocket of each αβ heterodimer (Fig. 5B). Western blot analysis of S100A9 in HaCaT cells showed that compound 2 produced a dose-dependent decrease in expression (Fig. 5C). Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) confirmed the direct binding of 2 with S100A9. As Fig. 5D shows, compound 2 stabilized S100A9 when temperature increasing, and the same situation was observed with compound 1 (Supporting Information Fig. S5).

Compounds1−4bind to two hydrophobic pockets of calprotectin and hinderits interaction with endogenous ligands

The above results prompted the selection of compound 2 for further molecular dynamics simulations in calcium and zinc-bound states under apo, com (protein: ligand = 1:1), and com2 (protein: ligand = 1:2) conditions, respectively. The molecular docking results were used as the original coordinate files for the simulations. During the simulation, the initial location of the first ligand (in pocket 1) in com and com2 was sustained, while the second ligand (in pocket 2) shifted outside the pocket, as shown in the root mean square deviation (RMSD) plot. And the trajectory of the ligand (after protein alignment) and the distance between ligand 1 and Arg85 (B) and that between ligand 2 and Arg85 (D) are shown in Fig. 6A and Supporting Information Fig. S6A. To more precisely locate the ligands, we measured the distance between each ligand and its corresponding pocket from four directions [Ile62 (B), Ala89 (B), Trp88 (D), and His95 (D) of pocket 1 and Ile62 (D), Ala89 (D), Trp88 (B), and His95 (B) of pocket 2]. In all four directions, ligand 1 remained stably positioned, while ligand 2 slowly shifted out of pocket 2 but remained bound at the entrance (Fig. S6B). Ligand 1 formed a stable complex with calprotectin (ΔGbinding = −33.72 ± 0.04 kcal/mol in com) and was unaffected by ligand 2 binding (ΔGbinding = −35.03 ± 0.05 kcal/mol in com2). However, ligand 2 harbored a relatively weak affinity with the protein (ΔGbinding = −19.79 ± 0.07 kcal/mol in com2) (Supporting Information Table S3). During the simulation, the protein reached a steady state after about 2 ns and remained compact in all three groups (Fig. 6B). We calculated the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) of residues from four chains of calprotectin and found that compound 2 mainly interacted with residues from chains B and D, both of which surrounded the two binding sites. The interactions were specific to residues 96−108 of chain B (decreased fluctuation) and residues 51−57 and 96−106 of chain D (increased fluctuation) (Fig. 6C). Residues surrounding the zinc ions also were affected. These included the coordinating residues of Zn_1 (His91, His95, His103, and His105 of chain B) (decreased fluctuation), Zn_3 (His87 and His83 of chain C) (decreased fluctuation), and Zn_4 (His91, His95, His103, and His105 of chain D, and His17 and His27 of chain C) (increased fluctuation), which resulted in correspondingly decreased fluctuation of Zn_1 and Zn_3 and increased fluctuation of Zn_4 (Fig. 6D and Fig. S6C). However, calcium ions bound at the EF-hand were not affected, and their stability was sustained in all three groups (Fig. S6D).

The size of pockets were measured as the distance between Ile62 (B) and Trp88 (D) and between Ala89 (B) and His95 (D) for pocket 1, and between Ile62 (D) and Trp88 (B) and between Ala89 (D) and His95 (B) for pocket 2 during the simulation and in the lowest energy conformation from each group after PCA. The docking conformation was used as a reference, a crystal structure in apo. Both measurements showed that the calprotectin pockets in the apo state were largely consistent with the docking conformation. Following binding of compound 2 (1:1 or 1:2), pocket 1 expanded, but pocket 2 tended to shrink (Fig. 6E and Fig. S6E). The hydrophobic pockets are binding sites for endogenous ligands of calprotectin42. The first molecule of compound 2 already occupied pocket 1, and meanwhile the second one hindered interaction with residual endogenous ligands even though it only bound at the entrance of pocket 2. Therefore, compound 2 could fully prevent endogenous interaction even though in low concentrations (1:1, not fully saturated) since pocket 2 was restrained.

Conformational change analyses showed that compared with the docking conformation, compound 2 in pocket 1 of com and com2 was similarly pulled inside and almost overlapped. In contrast, the ligand in pocket 2 swung around one of its edges and drifted outward (Fig. 6Fi). We identified the key residues that mediated the interaction between calprotectin and compound 2, and their conformational changes induced by 2 (Fig. S6F). In pocket 1, these residues included Ile62 (B), Arg85 (B), and His95 (D) (Fig. S6G). Ile62 (B) formed a hydrophobic interaction with compound 2 benefiting from the pulling-back, which was too far and inaccessible in docking conformation (Fig. 6Fii). Arg85 (B) was similarly situated but shared more hydrophobic interactions between its sidechain and compound 2. It adopted a flipped conformation in the apo state, leading to steric interference with the ligand (Fig. 6Fiii). There was also a flip in His95 (D) but in opposing directions in apo and com/com2. The flip in apo caused steric interference with the ligand but yielded a hydrogen bond with the sidechain of Arg85 in com2 (B), which was inaccessible in com, limited by the distance and the angle (Fig. 6Fiv). Those steric interference was present in apo but missing in com/com2, reflecting the induced accommodation of pocket 1.

The key interacting residues with compound 2 in pocket 2 were His95 (B), Glu96 (B), and Ile62 (D) (Fig. S6G). Induced by the intermolecular interaction, the sidechain of His95 (B) in com/com2 moved slightly toward the ligand in contrast to the apo conformation (Fig. 6Fv). Affected by the torsion of compound 2, Glu96 (B) in com2 was pushed away relative to that in the docking, apo, and com conformations (despite the formation of multiple hydrophobic interactions), and that in the latter three groups caused steric interference with the ligand (Fig. 6Fvi). Some interaction might be lost due to the swing of the ligand, such as the initial hydrophobic interaction between Ile62 (D) and compound 2 (Fig. 6Fvii). The binding of compound 2 also affected the conformation of other subsidiary residues and with them compound 2 also formed stable interactions, including Leu49 (B), Leu82 (B), Leu86 (B), Ala89 (B), Trp88 (D), His91 (D), and Gly97 (D) from pocket 1 (Supporting Information Fig. S7A), and Lys93 (B), Gly97 (B), Val58 (D), and Arg85 (D) from pocket 2 (Fig. S7B).

The affinity inside and between αβ heterodimers were measured to investigate whether the binding of compound 2 affected the association of the (S100A9/S100A8)2 heterotetramer. Compound 2 did not appreciably interfere with the affinity between αβ heterodimers chain A_chain B or chain C_chain D (Supporting Information Table S4) or between chain AB_chain CD (Table S3), and the interface away from its binding site (chain A_chain C). It did, however, reduce the interaction between the chains surrounding the pockets (chain B_chain D) (ΔGbinding = −15.69 ± 0.09 kcal/mol for apo, −14.20 ± 0.10 kcal/mol for com, −10.62 ± 0.07 kcal/mol for com2) (Supporting Information Table S5). The decreased affinity was mediated by residues 57−65 (B), 81−89 (B), 96−99 (B), and 94−100 (D) (Fig. S6H), located at the interface of chains B and D and around the two hydrophobic pockets.

Compounds1−4bind to and destabilizecalprotectin

Surface plasmon resonance binding assays validated the affinity between compounds 1−4 and calprotectin. All four compounds bound calprotectin at the micromolar scale (KD values were 9.59 μmol/L for 1, 1.49 μmol/L for 2, 5.07 μmol/L for 3, and 8.10 μmol/L for 4, respectively) (Fig. 7A, Supporting Information Table S6). Stability studies showed that 2 and, to a lesser extent, 4 reduced the thermal stability of calprotectin (with a lower Tm value) (Fig. 7B, Supporting Information Table S7). Compounds 1 and 3 seemed not affecting the protein stability. Combined with molecular dynamics simulation results, compounds 1−4 might bind to the hydrophobic pockets and trigger dissociation between chains B and D, thus disrupting the stable fold of calprotectin and interfering with the binding of endogenous ligands. Although 1 and 3 did not affect the thermal stability of calprotectin, their occupancy at the ligand binding sites may interfere with calprotectin-mediated regulation of inflammatory processes and immune response.

In addition, the effect of compound 2 on S100A9 (chains B and D of calprotectin) stability was evaluated in vitro. TNF-α induced expression of S100A9 and this effect was reversed by 2, consistent with the positive control tapinarof (Fig. 7C). We concluded that compound 2 inhibits TNF-α-induced inflammation by binding to the hydrophobic pockets of calprotectin, causing dissociation and instability and leading to a decrease in the expression of S100A9 or calprotectin.

Compounds1and2alleviatepsoriasis-likedermatitis in mice induced by IMQ in mice

An animal model for psoriasis was established to evaluate the effects of compounds 1 and 2 in vivo by topical administration of IMQ. After seven consecutive days of IMQ treatment, the symptoms of psoriasiform dermatitis were significant and included skin erythema, thickening, and scaling. Treatment with compounds 1 or 2 alleviated these symptoms in mice (Fig. 8A), as shown by remarkable reductions in the psoriasis area and severity index (PASI) and scaling thickness (Fig. 8B and C). IL-17 can induce S100A9 expression and Ki67 is often used as a marker for cell proliferation44,45. Immunohistochemistry analysis showed that S100A9, IL-17, and Ki67 were upregulated in the IMQ group and significantly reduced after treatment with 2 (Fig. 8D). These results suggest that compound 2 can alleviate psoriasis-like dermatitis in mice by inhibiting S100A9 secretion and keratinocyte proliferation.

Conclusions

This study isolated four novel sesterterpenoids with a highly congested pentacyclic 6/5/5/6/5 ring system from Aspergillus undulatus. They contain eight stereogenic centers, including three all-carbon quaternary centers, two quaternary carbon centers at the bridgehead, and a cyclopenta[cd]indene fragment. Undulanoid B (2) blocked the activation of inflammatory signaling to regulate the immune response. Reverse target prediction, CETSA, and dynamic simulation confirmed that compound 2 directly bound to the S100A8/A9 heterocomplex, which plays an important role in the pathogenesis of psoriasis. Furthermore, compound 2 relieved the symptoms of epidermal hyperplasia associated with psoriasis by inhibiting keratinocyte proliferation and S100A9 expression. The discovery of this novel class of compounds marks the first instance of natural products capable of inhibiting S100A8/A9 complex protein secretion, making it an appealing candidate for antipsoriatic pharmaceutical development. We anticipate that these sesterterpenoids will attract widespread interest from scientists for synthetic and biosynthetic purposes.

Experimental

General experimental procedures

Melting points were obtained using a Micro melting point instrument (SGW X−4B). Optical rotations were measured on PerkinElmer 341 polarimeter (PerkinElmer Inc., Fremont, CA, USA) in MeOH. IR spectra were obtained on a Bruker Vertex 70 FT-IR spectrophotometer (Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany), and UV spectra were measured by Lambda 35 instrument (PerkinElmer Inc.). ECD spectra were acquired using a Jasco 810 instrument (JASCO Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). NMR data were conducted on Bruker AV-400 and Bruker AV-600 NMR instruments with DMSO-d6 (δH 2.50/δC 39.52) and CDCl3 (δH 7.26/δC 77.16) as solvents. High-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HRESIMS) data were collected using SolariX 7.0T Bruker Daltonics. Crystallographic experiments were performed on a XtaLAB PRO MM007HF (Rigaku, Japan) Cu Kα radiation. Column chromatography was performed by silica gel (80−120 mesh and 200−300 mesh, Qingdao Marine Chemical Inc., Qingdao, People's Republic of China), ODS (50 μm, YMC, Japan). Semipreparative HPLC separations was conducted on Agilent 1260 HPLC system with a DAD detector using a reversed-phase (RP) C18 column (5 μm, 10 mm × 250 mm, Welch Ultimate XB-C18). Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) performed with silica gel 60 F254 was detected with 10% H2SO4 in EtOH followed by heating.

Fungal material

The fungal strain Aspergillus undulatus was isolated from the soil collected from Shengnongjia, Hubei Province. The strain used in this study was obtained from the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (No. 3.6295). A voucher specimen has been deposited in the culture collection center of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology.

Fermentation, extraction, and isolation

The strain Aspergillus undulatus was cultivated on potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates at 28 °C for 40 days. Then the agar was cut into pieces (0.3 cm × 0.3 cm × 0.3 cm) and inoculated into 1000 mL Erlenmeyer flasks, 70 kg of rice in total, previously sterilized by autoclaving, each containing 250 g rice, and 250 mL of distilled water. After 40 days of cultivation at 25 °C, the mycelia, and rice medium were extracted with ethanol ten times to obtain a residue, then the residue was dispersed in water and successively partitioned with EtOAc. Ultimately, both EtOAc solutions were concentrated under reduced pressure to give the crude extract (600 g).

The crude extract was subjected to column chromatography (CC) over silica gel and eluted with PE−EA−MeOH (15:1:0−1:1:0−0:0:1, v/v/v) to four seven fractions Fr.1−Fr.6.

Immunofluorescence

HaCaT cells were inoculated into 6-well plates at a cell density of 2.5 × 105 cells per well. Compounds 1 (2.5 and 5.0 μmol/L) or 2 (1.25 and 2.5 μmol/L) groups were co-incubated with 10 ng/mL TNF-α for 24 h, and then cells were fixed with 4% cold paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Following fixation, cells were subjected to three consecutive PBS washes (5 min each) and subsequently blocked with 5% (w/v) BSA for 1 h at room temperature. NF-κB p65 or S100A9 antibodies were added to the cells for overnight at 4 °C, and sequentially incubated with a secondary antibody labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594 for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Subsequent staining with DAPI for 30 min at 37 °C was performed, followed by coverslip washing and sealing. Images were taken with a Nikon (Eclipse Ci-L) microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Thermal shift assay (TSA)

The purified protein was diluted to 0.5 mg/mL with 20 mmol/L Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 2 mmol/L CaCl2, and 2 mmol/L ZnSO4, and incubated with compounds or DMSO for 30 min at room temperature. The reaction was conducted in 384-well plates, with 10 μL per well containing 9 μL mixed solution and 1 μL SYPRO™ Orange Protein Gel Stain (Invitrogen). After centrifugation, the melting curve was monitored in Applied Biosystems® QuantStudioTM6 Pro Real-Time PCR System (Thermos Fisher Scientific) with the first step of incubation at 25 °C for 10 min, and then gradient heating from 25 to 95 °C at a rate of 0.075 °C/s. Tm values were fitted and calculated by the Quantstudio™ Design & Analysis Software v1.5.2. The experiment was performed in triplicate.

Surface plasmon resonance

The affinity of calprotectin and compounds was determined by Biacore 1K (Cytiva). The recombinant protein was immobilized to series S sensor chip CM5. The association or dissociation was performed under a predefined procedure (multi-cycle kinetics/affinity) with 5 gradient-diluted concentrations of compounds in 10 mmol/L HEPES pH 7.4, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 0.005% (v/v) Surfactant P20, 5% (v/v) DMSO. The results were also analyzed with a predefined procedure (multi-cycle kinetics) to calculate the Ka, Kd, and KD values.

The establishment of psoriasis-like dermatitis in mice

BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomly divided into five groups: blank control (Vaseline, n = 5), IMQ groups (n = 5), compound 1 (10 mg/kg, n = 5), compound 2 (10 mg/kg, n = 5), and positive control (tapinarof, n = 5). Except for the blank control, all the groups received a daily dose of IMQ (62.5 mg/piece) on the shaved dorsal skin for 7 consecutive days to induce psoriasis. To evaluate the therapeutic effects of compounds 1 and 2, both agents (10 mg/kg) were administered via intraperitoneal injection (i.p.) daily for 7 consecutive days. Tapinarof was dissolved in a mixture of 60% anhydrous ethanol and 40% glycerol to achieve a 1% (w/v) concentration, then applied to the shaved dorsal area of the mice. On Day 8, after retro-orbital blood sampling, the mice were humanely euthanized. The dorsal skin of mice was taken for experiments. The body weight and changes in the exposed skin condition of the mice were monitored and recorded throughout the experimental period. PASI evaluations were conducted every day based on factors including erythema, scaling, and thickness on a scale of 0−4: 0, none; 1, mild; 2, moderate; 3, severe; and 4, very severe. The animal experiments have been approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (IACUC Number: 3407).

Statistical analysis

Data were visualized and analyzed in Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) or SPSS v. 19.0 (Chicago, IL). Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experimental replicates. Comparisons between the two groups were conducted using an unpaired, two-tailed Student's t-test. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons test. And P < 0.05 was considered statistically meaningful.
