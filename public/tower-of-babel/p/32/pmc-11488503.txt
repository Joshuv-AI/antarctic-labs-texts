Linker and Conjugation Site Synergy in Antibody–Drug Conjugates: Impacts on Biological Activity
Authors: Michihiko Aoyama, Minoru Tada, Hidetomo Yokoo, Takahito Ito, Takashi Misawa, Yosuke Demizu, Akiko Ishii-Watabe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) produced using general conjugation methods yield heterogeneous products containing mixtures of species with different numbers of payloads per antibody (drug–antibody ratios) conjugated at multiple sites. This heterogeneity affects the stability, efficacy, and safety of ADCs. Thus, various site-specific conjugation methods have been developed to achieve homogeneity in ADCs. It was reported that linker structures and conjugation sites generally affected the characteristics of site-specific ADCs such as stability, efficacy, and safety. However, the combined effects of conjugation sites and linker structures on the physicochemical and biological characteristics of site-specific ADCs have remained unclear. In this study, we generated 30 homogeneous site-specific ADCs with a combination of six conjugation sites and five linker structures using THIOMAB technology and evaluated the characteristics of these homogeneous ADCs. We found that both conjugation sites and linker structures affected characteristics unique to ADCs (linker stability as well as target-dependent and target-independent cytotoxicity) in site-specific ADCs. Especially, conjugation to the constant regions of the light chain and the presence of polyethylene glycol structures in the linker are important for those ADC-specific characteristics. Interestingly, we also found that the effects of linker structures on the target-independent cytotoxicity of homogeneous ADCs at certain conjugation sites differed from those seen in conventional heterogeneous ADCs. Our results suggest that optimizing linker structures based on the conjugation site may be necessary for site-specific ADCs.

Introduction

Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), which are monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) conjugated with highly toxic small molecules (payloads) via linkers, are among the fastest-growing categories of next-generation therapeutic mAbs for cancer treatment. ADCs achieve high tumor-killing efficacy by delivering payloads specifically in line with mAbs’ functions while minimizing adverse effects compared to traditional chemotherapy. Recent advances in antibody, payload, and linker technologies, which affect the in vivo pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and safety of ADCs, have hastened the development and commercial application of ADCs.1−3 Of the 13 ADCs that have been approved by the FDA thus far, 9 were authorized after 2019; 85 candidates are currently in the clinical development stage in various countries.4,5

Typical conjugation methods utilized in the commercial production of ADCs involve conjugating to amino groups in lysine residues through N-succinimidyl esters (NHS esters) or via reduced interchain disulfide bonds using a maleimide group. Both of these conjugation methods result in heterogeneous products, comprising a mixture of species with different numbers of payloads per antibody (drug–antibody ratio; DAR) conjugated at multiple sites.2 It was reported that the heterogeneity of ADCs, encompassing DAR and conjugation sites, affected the in vivo pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and safety of ADCs,6−8 the same as other ADC-specific key factors such as the payload, linker structure, and average DAR.5,9,10 Therefore, site-specific conjugation methods aimed at producing homogeneous ADCs with specific numbers of payloads at predetermined sites have been developed. In recent decades, various site-specific conjugation methods have been developed, including antibody engineering to introduce specific conjugation sites (such as cysteine or unnatural amino acids) and enzymatic conjugation to specific positions (using microbial transglutaminase or glycotransferase).11−14 Homogeneous ADCs produced by site-specific conjugation showed a superior therapeutic index and improved in vivo stability compared to heterogeneous ADCs.15 Furthermore, in-depth studies on site-specific ADCs revealed that the conjugation site influences the linker stability, aggregation, and tumor-killing efficacy of homogeneous ADCs.16,17 These results suggested that optimizing the conjugation site could improve the efficacy and safety of ADCs. However, the effects of the conjugation site on the characteristics and biological functions of ADCs remain poorly understood. In most studies, several different specific conjugation sites were selected to generate site-specific ADCs with single or two linker structures, and the effects of the conjugation site on the characteristics of site-specific ADCs were assessed independently with each linker structure. Thus, the impact of the combination of linker structures and conjugation sites remains to be elucidated.

In this study, we produced several homogeneous ADCs conjugated with different noncleavable linker structures, including cis–trans isomers of the cyclohexane linker at specific sites by using THIOMAB technology, involving the introduction of cysteine residues at particular positions through antibody engineering.12 We evaluated various characteristics of homogeneous ADCs, including thermal stability and antigen binding, Fcγ receptor (FcγR) interaction, and the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) binding property, which were characteristics mainly derived from the mAb portion, as well as ADC-specific characteristics (hydrophobicity, linker stability, and target-dependent and target-independent cytotoxicity). Our study revealed that the interplay between conjugation sites and linker structures affected the attributes of homogeneous ADCs, which are pertinent to the stability, pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and safety of these therapeutic agents.

Results

Homogeneous ADC Production

In this study, we evaluated the impact of conjugation sites and linker structures on the characteristics of homogeneous ADCs, employing the anti-HER2 antibody trastuzumab as a model. To generate site-specific ADCs, specific cysteine residues were additionally introduced at four positions in the constant region of the heavy chain (HC-A118C, HC-A140C, HC-E258C, or HC-S400C) or at two positions in the constant region of the light chain (LC-K149C or LC-V205C) through amino acid substitutions (Figure 1a). These six specific conjugation sites were chosen for their exceptional conjugation efficacy and low aggregation based on a previous study that evaluated the aggregation and linker stability of site-specific ADCs.17 To evaluate the impact of linker structures on the characteristics of site-specific ADCs while eliminating the effects of linker cleavage, we employed two varieties of noncleavable linkers, which had been well-used in research, namely, the 4-(N-maleimidomethyl)cyclohexane-1-carboxylate [mcc] linker (L1) and maleimidocaproyl [mc] linker (L2), and two types of noncleavable linkers with polyethylene glycol (PEG) structures for evaluating the PEG influence, namely, the PEG2 linker (L3) and PEG6 linker (L4), to conjugate monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) (Figure 1b). The mcc-MMAE (L1) contained cis–trans isomers, with trans-mcc-MMAE (L1a) and cis-mcc-MMAE (L1b) being isolated through reverse-phase chromatography (L1a and L1b, Scheme S1). To assess the impact of the linker structure on the pharmacological activity of a payload, we evaluated the pharmacological activity of five maleimide noncleavable linker-payloads (LPs) using a tubulin polymerization assay. The maleimide reactive groups of these linkers were blocked by the addition of a 10-fold molar excess of cysteines before use in the assay. In the tubulin polymerization assay, tubulin polymerizes over time at 37 °C, leading to an increase in fluorescence corresponding to the polymerized tubulin. In the presence of tubulin polymerization-inhibiting compounds such as MMAE, polymerization is inhibited, leading to a suppression of the increase in fluorescence intensity. In this assay, the positive control of 2 μM MMAE completely blocked the increase in fluorescence intensity related to tubulin polymerization. As shown in Figure 1c, the four LPs other than L1a demonstrated comparable inhibitory activity in a dose-dependent manner in the tubulin polymerization assay, while 2 μM L1a exhibited a level of inhibition similar to that of 1 μM for the other LP. This result suggested that L1a had about half the inhibitory activity compared to the other LPs, indicating that the L1a linker affected the pharmacological activity of the payload, whereas the other noncleavable linker structures did not affect the pharmacological activity.

By using these cysteine-introduced mAbs and LPs, we generated site-specific ADCs with five different linker structures and six different conjugation sites. Hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC) analysis revealed that these ADCs contained several species with different DAR values (0, 1, and 2), and the average DAR of the ADCs varied across the conjugation sites (avg. DAR = 1.2–1.8). To obtain homogeneous ADCs, we purified ADCs with a DAR of 2 by HIC separation using a HiTrap Phenyl-HP. As shown in Figure 2a, ADCs with a DAR of 2 (fraction 2) and ADCs with DAR of 0 and 1 (fraction 1) were separated by HIC separation using a HiTrap Phenyl-HP. After separation, homogeneous ADCs with an average DAR of 1.8–2.0, featuring diverse linker structures and specific conjugation sites, were obtained (Figure 2b). Thus, these homogeneous ADCs were utilized as model ADCs to assess the effects of conjugation sites and linker structures on the characteristics of site-specific ADCs. In addition, HIC analysis of homogeneous ADCs postseparation suggested that the hydrophobicity of these ADCs was affected by both linker structures and conjugation sites. Specifically, the conjugation sites showed higher impacts on the hydrophobicity of homogeneous ADCs with a DAR of 2 compared to the effects of linker structures used in this study. Nevertheless, in this study, no specific correlation between hydrophobicity and conjugation sites was observed.

Fc Receptor Binding Properties and Thermal Stability of Homogeneous ADCs

It has been reported that the conjugation of linker-payloads may affect the antigen binding, Fc receptor binding, and thermal stability of ADCs.18 First, we evaluated the antigen binding properties of homogeneous ADCs through surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis. In this study, we introduced a cysteine residue and conjugated the LPs to the constant regions of the antibody to prevent negative effects on the antigen binding property. As expected, the homogeneous ADCs showed similar antigen (HER2) binding properties across different linker structures and conjugation sites (Figure 3a). However, it was reported that the Fc receptor binding properties of ADCs are influenced by the linker structure and DAR.18 In addition, some site-specific ADCs abolish FcγR binding properties.19 These findings suggested that chemical conjugation, including site-specific conjugation, could impact the Fc receptor (FcγRs and FcRn) binding properties, which are pertinent to the pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics of ADCs. Thus, we evaluated the FcRn- and FcγRs-binding properties of homogeneous ADCs by SPR analysis. As shown in Figure 3b,e, we found that HC-E258C mAb showed lower FcRn binding properties than other THIOMABs (unconjugated Cys-introduced mAbs) by the introduction of cysteine at HC-Glu258, and conjugation of the non-PEG linker to HC-E258C (HC-E258C-L1a, HC-E258C-L1b, and HC-E258C-L2) showed further lower FcRn binding properties than unconjugated HC-E258C mAb or other homogeneous ADCs. Given that HC-Glu258 is situated close to the FcRn binding sites between CH2 and CH3, we considered that the introduction of cysteine at HC-Glu258 and conjugation of the non-PEG linker to HC-Glu258 affected the structures of FcRn binding sites. Furthermore, we found that certain homogeneous ADCs showed slight changes of FcRn binding properties by LP conjugation such as HC-A118C-L3, HC-A140C-L3, and LC-V205C-L1a, LC-V205C-L3, and LC-V205C-L4. The conjugation sites except for HC-E258C are positioned distantly from the FcRn binding site. Thus, we hypothesized that the slight changes in FcRn binding activity induced by site-specific conjugation may have been influenced by linker structures, although additional research is warranted for confirmation. Regarding FcγRs binding, FcγRIa demonstrated consistent binding characteristics with homogeneous ADCs across various linker structures and conjugation sites (Figure 3c). In contrast, though the KD value could not be calculated due to low binding affinity of FcγRIIIa, HC-E258C-MMAE exhibited reduced FcγRIIIa binding properties compared to other homogeneous ADCs, irrespective of linker structures (Figure 3d,f). Interestingly, HC-S400C mAb, LC-K149C mAb, and LC-V205C mAb showed higher FcγRIIIa binding activity than other THIOMABs and homogeneous ADCs. However, the FcγRIIIa binding affinity of HC-S400C-MMAE, LC-K149C-MMAE, and LC-V205C-MMAE decreased to the level of other homogeneous ADCs after linker conjugation, irrespective of linker structures.

In our previous study, we reported that the FcγRIIIa binding properties of mAbs with different glycan profiles were correlated with thermal stability.20 Thus, we measured temperature-dependent protein unfolding using differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) to compare the thermal stability of THIOMABs (Figure 4a) and homogeneous ADCs (Figure 4b). The mAbs and ADCs have first (Tm1) and second (Tm2) transition temperatures, which are associated with the unfolding of the CH2 domain and the CH3 or Fab domain, respectively. As shown in Figure 4a, HC-E258C mAbs showed a slightly higher Tm1 value compared to trastuzumab and other THIOMABs, whereas trastuzumab and THIOMABs exhibited comparable Tm2 values. Interestingly, HC-E258C-MMAE showed a significant decrease in the Tm1 value across linker structures, while other homogeneous ADCs showed similar Tm1 values in Figure 4b. No significant changes were observed in the Tm2 values of homogeneous ADCs, irrespective of the linker structure and conjugation sites. These results indicated that site-specific conjugation to HC-E258C diminished the FcγRIIIa binding properties resulting from the reduced thermal stability of HC-E258C-MMAE, regardless of amino acid substitutions in HC-E258C. Our findings, along with previous reports, indicate that chemical conjugation to the CH2 domain, located away from the FcγRIIIa binding sites, impacted the thermal stability of the CH2 domain, leading to a decrease in FcγRIIIa binding properties. Conversely, the enhancement of FcγRIIIa binding properties observed in HC-S400C, LC-K149C, and-V205C (as shown in Figure 3d,f) did not stem from an enhancement in the thermal stability of the CH2 domains. A recent study has reported that the LC region of IgG1 and the Fc region, the canonical Fc receptor binding site, both contribute to FcγRIIIa binding.21 Therefore, our results may indicate that the introduction of a cysteine residue at the LC region or the CH3 region, located away from the FcγRIIIa binding sites, could serve as a useful strategy for increasing FcγRIIIa binding affinity if such characteristics are desired. We discovered that the site-specific conjugation of LPs to the CH2 domain might affect the Fc receptor binding properties, including those of FcRn and FcγRIIIa, which have binding sites on the CH2 domains on site-specific ADCs.

Target-Dependent and Target-Independent Cytotoxicity of Homogeneous ADCs

Next, we evaluated the linker stability and target-dependent and target-independent cytotoxic activity of homogeneous ADCs. Linker stability is important for the in vitro and in vivo efficacy and safety of ADCs. It was reported that incubating ADCs in a culture medium and serum at 37 °C may cause the detachment of maleimide linkers through the retro-Michael reaction.22 Thus, we evaluated the linker stability of homogeneous ADCs under cell-based assay conditions by examining the retained DAR of the homogeneous ADCs after 0, 24, 48, and 96 h of incubation in a culture medium (Figure 5a). Based on the results, a small quantity of LP release was detected in the majority of homogeneous ADCs after 4 days of incubation, irrespective of the linker structures and conjugation sites. We found that the linker stability of HC-S400C-MMAE with a non-PEG linker (L3 and L4) was significantly lower than those of other homogeneous ADCs, while the release of LP from HC-S400C-MMAE with the PEG linker was suppressed compared to those with a non-PEG linker. In a previous study, site-specific conjugation to HC-S400C with a cleavable non-PEGylated linker exhibited significantly lower linker stability compared to conjugation to HC-A118C and LC-V205C.16 These results suggested that the conjugation site affects linker stability, consistent with prior studies. Moreover, linker modifications including PEGylation could improve the linker stability of site-specific conjugation.

Next, we evaluated the target-dependent and target-independent cytotoxicity of homogeneous ADCs in the HER2-expressing cell line SK-BR-3 cells and the HER2-negative Jurkat cell line (Figure 5b,c). We found that linker structures significantly influenced both the target-dependent and target-independent cytotoxicity of homogeneous ADCs. In target-dependent cytotoxicity (Figure 5b,d), homogeneous ADCs featuring PEG linkers (L3 and L4) showed higher cytotoxicity than those with non-PEG linkers (L1a, L1b, and L2) in SK-BR-3 cells, irrespective of conjugation sites, mirroring the behavior observed in heterogeneous ADCs, which had an average DAR of about 2 (Figure S1a,b). In addition, the target-dependent cytotoxicity of homogeneous ADCs, especially those with non-PEG linkers such as L1b and L2, was influenced by the specific conjugation sites. ADCs conjugated at the LC portion (LC-K149C-MMAE and LC-V205C-MMAE) exhibited higher cytotoxicity compared to those at the HC portions. These results indicated that the presence of PEG structures and conjugation to the LC portions could improve the target-dependent cytotoxicity of site-specific ADCs. The impacts of site-specific conjugation to LC portions could be found in homogeneous ADCs with the PEG linker (IC50 of homogeneous ADCs with the L3 linker: HC-A118C, 0.09 nM; HC-A140C, 0.12 nM; HC-258C, 0.08 nM; HC-S400C, 0.16 nM; LC-K149C, 0.07 nM; LC-V205C, 0.07 nM as indicated in Figure 5d), albeit to a lesser extent compared to non-PEG linker ADCs. For instance, the IC50 values of homogeneous ADCs with the L2 linker were as follows: HC-A118C, 0.72 nM; HC-A140C, 0.64 nM; HC-E258C, 0.61 nM; HC-S400C, 1.28 nM; LC-K149C, 0.23 nM; LC-V205C, 0.22 nM. Figure 3a reveals that the HER2 binding properties of homogeneous ADCs remained consistent across various conjugation sites and linker structures. Furthermore, all the homogeneous ADCs used in this study exhibited target-dependent cytotoxic activity levels that were at least equivalent to that of trastuzumab. Further research is warranted, as our findings indicate that conjugation to the LC regions and the use of PEG linkers may impact postbinding processes with HER2, including internalization, lysosomal transport, and antibody degradation of site-specific ADCs.

In Jurkat cells (Figure 5c), certain homogeneous ADCs showed target-independent cytotoxicity only at significantly higher concentrations compared to the target-dependent cytotoxicity observed in Figure 5b. HC-S400C-MMAE, conjugated with a non-PEG linker that exhibited lower linker stability (Figure 5a), showed higher cytotoxicity than other homogeneous ADCs. HC-S400C-MMAE with a non-PEG linker showed slightly lower target-dependent cytotoxicity in SK-BR-3 cells compared to other homogeneous ADCs with non-PEG linkers (Figure 5b). Thus, we attributed the higher cytotoxicity of HC-S400C-MMAE with non-PEG linkers in Jurkat cells to the release of payloads though further studies using site-specific homogeneous ADCs with payloads, which could not cross the cell membrane and will be required to confirm the mechanism. Interestingly, the impact of linker structures on target-independent cytotoxicity varied among different conjugation sites for homogeneous ADCs with site-specific conjugation (Figure 5e). We found that the target-independent cytotoxicity of heterogeneous ADCs with similar DAR profiles in Jurkat cells depended on the linker structures, with cytotoxicity ranking as follows: L1b > L1a > L2 ≈ L3 ≈ L4 (Figure S1c). While L1a exhibited approximately a 2-fold decrease in tubulin polymerization inhibitory activity compared to other LPs, heterogeneous ADCs with the L1b linker, having an average DAR of about 2, showed significantly greater cytotoxicity than those with the L1a and L2 linkers in various HER2-negative cell lines, including Jurkat cells. In homogeneous ADCs, on the other hand, HC-E258C-L1a and LC-V205C-L2 showed higher target-independent cytotoxicity compared to those with the L1b linker. In Figure 5a, most of the homogeneous ADCs, such as HC-E258C-MMAE and LC-V205C-MMAE, exhibited comparable linker stability irrespective of the linker structure, whereas HC-S400C-MMAE with non-PEG linkers showed significant LP release. Thus, the increased target-independent cytotoxicity of HC-E258C-L1a and LC-V205C-L2 compared to those with the L1b linker was unrelated to linker stability. These findings indicated that the impact of linker structures on the target-independent cytotoxicity of site-specific ADCs might differ based on the specific conjugation sites. Our results suggested that certain combinations of linker structures and conjugation sites showed target-independent cytotoxicity, which cannot be predicted from an understanding of the connection between linker structures and the target-independent cytotoxicity of conventional homogeneous ADCs. Interestingly, LC-K149C-MMAE showed no target-independent cytotoxicity across the range of concentrations examined in this experiment. However, LC-K149C-MMAE, especially LC-K149C-L3 and LC-K149C-L4, showed strong target-dependent cytotoxicity compared with heterogeneous ADCs with an average DAR of about 2. These results implied that optimizing both conjugation sites and linker structures may improve the efficacy and safety of ADCs.

Discussion

In this study, we generated 30 site-specific homogeneous trastuzumab-based ADCs utilizing a combination of six conjugation sites and five noncleavable linker structures and then evaluated their physicochemical and biological characteristics, including receptor binding properties, linker stability, and both target-dependent and target-independent cytotoxicity. These studies revealed the pivotal roles of both conjugation sites and linker structures in determining the characteristics of site-specific ADCs. Particularly, conjugation sites strongly affected several characteristics, including Fc receptor binding, linker stability, and target-dependent cytotoxicity. In our study, site-specific conjugation of noncleavable LPs to two positions within the constant region of the LC showed superior ADC-specific characteristics compared to conjugation to the constant regions of the HC. Furthermore, linker structures incorporating PEG structures improved the biological characteristics of site-specific ADCs, especially site-specific conjugation to constant regions of heavy chain, including HC-S400C. These site-specific ADCs showed higher target-dependent cytotoxicity and lower target-independent cytotoxicity than heterogeneous ADCs with the same LP, which had an average DAR of about 2. Hence, these results suggested that optimizing the combination of conjugation sites and linker structures could improve the efficacy and safety of ADCs in comparison to heterogeneous ADCs.

In addition, we revealed the importance of PEG structures in noncleavable linkers regarding the characteristics of site-specific ADCs. In general, PEGylation is expected to improve the hydrophilicity of protein drugs, including mAbs and ADCs. It has been reported that the hydrophobicity of ADCs correlates with their plasma clearance, hepatic uptake, and target-independent cytotoxicity.9,23 Previous studies have found that PEG structures incorporated at a branching point within the linker, as opposed to those incorporated into a linear linker, improved the hydrophobicity of maleimide-conjugated ADCs with a DAR of 8. Additionally, these structures improved the pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and safety of ADCs.9,24 In the present study, we found that L3 and L4 linkers, with PEG structures of different lengths incorporated into a linear linker, slightly increased hydrophobicity compared to L2, which had a linear linker without a PEG structure (Figure 2b). Considering these results, it was expected that the target-independent cytotoxicity of ADCs with L3 or L4 would be equal to or slightly higher than those with L2 from the perspective of hydrophobicity. Surprisingly, however, homogeneous ADCs with the L3 linker showed higher target-dependent cytotoxicity and lower target-independent cytotoxicity compared to homogeneous ADCs with the L2 linker, irrespective of the conjugation sites (Figure 5b,c). In addition, we found that the linkers with PEG structures (L3 and L4) improved the linker stability of site-specific ADCs at HC-S400C (Figure 5a). While further studies are needed, this result suggested that PEGylation of linker structures improves the safety and efficacy of site-specific ADCs, with these effects being independent of improvements in hydrophobicity.

While the optimization of ADC conjugation sites is influenced by various factors such as the target protein/tissue, the need for Fc functionality, linker structures, and so on, we proposed a strategy of selecting conjugation to the constant region of the LC to enhance the characteristics of site-specific ADCs using noncleavable linkers. Shen et al. reported the impact of site-specific conjugation of a maleimide LP to three sites with different structural and chemical environments: HC-A114C, Fc-S396C, and LC-V205C.16 They concluded that LC-V205C, situated in partially varied regions, showed greater target-dependent cytotoxicity and in vivo efficacy as a result of increased linker stability resulting from enhanced thio-succinimide hydrolysis in a positively charged environment and lower solvent accessibility compared to HC-A114C and Fc-S396C, located at solvent-accessible sites.16,25,26 We also found that site-specific conjugation to LC-V205C resulted in higher linker stability and target-dependent cytotoxicity compared to HC-A140C and HC-S400C, irrespective of linker structures. On the other hand, our results revealed that site-specific conjugation to LC-K149C and LC-V205C showed superior target-dependent cytotoxicity and lower target-independent cytotoxicity compared to HC-A140C and HC-E258C, which demonstrated robust linker stability. Hence, we posit that the conjugation of the LP to not only LC-V205C but also any constant regions of LC may confer advantages in improving the efficacy of ADCs. Nonetheless, further studies are needed to elucidate the influence of site-specific conjugation to the constant regions of LC.

There are some limitations to this study. Specifically, we evaluated the biological function of ADCs through in vitro assays rather than in vivo assays, such as pharmacokinetics and efficacy profiles in tumor-bearing models. It is unknown whether the impact of conjugation sites and linker structures on target-dependent and target-independent cytotoxicity of site-specific ADCs correlates with their in vivo efficacy, pharmacokinetics, and off-target toxicity, though some correlations have been suggested in previous studies. In addition, we evaluated the impacts of noncleavable linkers in the present study. Ohri et al. reported that high-throughput screening of conjugation sites using site-specific ADCs with MC-vc-PAB-MMAE and PDS-MMAE showed good correlation in terms of conjugation efficacy and aggregation.17 In contrast, it was reported that a critical internalization process in target-dependent internalization of ADCs differed between noncleavable linkers and cleavable linkers27 and that conjugation sites affected the metabolism of LPs.25 These results may suggest that the cleavability of the linker is not critical for some physicochemical characteristics but has a significant impact on biological characteristics, and the optimization of conjugation sites and linker structures for site-specific ADCs differs between cleavable and noncleavable linkers. Although further studies are necessary to elucidate the impacts of conjugation sites and linker structures, including cleavable linkers, on in vivo efficacy and safety, our study using 30 site-specific ADCs with a combination of conjugation sites and linker structures revealed that optimizing these components could enhance the efficacy and safety of ADCs.
