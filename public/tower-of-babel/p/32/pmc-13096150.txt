Selecting AI-enabled music learning technologies in higher education using AHP and TOPSIS
Authors: Min Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Digital and AI-supported music learning tools are increasingly adopted in higher education, yet their selection is often driven by informal preferences rather than transparent, evidence-based procedures. This study implements a multi-criteria decision-making framework to rank AI-enabled music-learning technology options using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) and the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to an Ideal Solution (TOPSIS). Six technology categories were evaluated across 25 sub-criteria grouped into five dimensions, based on judgments from a 20-member expert panel and questionnaire responses from 100 higher-education music learners. The weighting results indicate that pedagogical value and learner experience received the greatest emphasis, followed by technical fit, implementation feasibility, and governance and ethics. The resulting shortlist ranked notation/score-based learning platforms highest overall, followed by LMS-integrated assessment and feedback tools and ear-training/musicianship applications, while the lowest-ranked category consistently underperformed on governance and technical-fit requirements. Sensitivity and scenario analyses indicated that the top tier remained stable, although the top choice can shift under governance-led or resource-constrained priorities. The framework supports defensible procurement and curriculum design by integrating expert and learner evidence, validated weighting, and robust ranking outputs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43769-1.

Introduction

Music programs in higher education increasingly rely on digital and AI-enabled technologies, including notation platforms, digital audio workstations, ear-training applications, AI-accompaniment and practice-support systems, and learning management system integrations1. Recent scholarship indicates that technology use in music education is generally associated with positive learning outcomes. Still, the magnitude of benefit depends on factors such as technology type, learning context, and implementation conditions2–4. At the same time, higher education leaders are under growing pressure to justify technology adoption decisions because tools now affect not only the delivery of instruction but also assessment integrity, data practices, accessibility, and the student experience. This creates a decision environment where informal selection based on familiarity, vendor persuasion, or isolated preferences can lead to misalignment with curriculum goals or institutional responsibilities5–7.

A parallel research direction emphasizes the need for evidence-based practice in music teaching and learning more broadly, suggesting that music education benefits when interventions are evaluated systematically rather than assumed effective8. Extending this logic to educational technology selection implies that institutions should adopt transparent, repeatable decision-making processes that integrate pedagogy, learner experience, technical fit, feasibility, and governance requirements into a single, defensible evaluation model.

The literature provides growing evidence that familiar music-learning technology categories can support measurable outcomes, yet this evidence remains fragmented across tool types and learning contexts. For example, ear-training tools are frequently studied as scalable supports for musicianship development, including (i) reviews of computer-assisted aural skills instruction and software-supported ear training and (ii) empirical studies evaluating ear-training applications in formal education contexts and intervention designs using dedicated software9–11. Similarly, notation platforms are increasingly integrated into learning activities such as sight singing, dictation support, and iterative composition tasks, with quasi-experimental studies reporting improvements in related skills when notation software is integrated into instruction.

Digital audio workstations are widely treated as foundational environments in production-oriented curricula and are increasingly used beyond production programs as creative and instructional tools. Studies on DAW-based learning report benefits for creativity, learning engagement, and skill development in specific instructional settings, while also indicating that infrastructure requirements and training burdens remain major barriers to adoption12. Across these categories, the literature suggests that no single tool is universally optimal, because pedagogical benefit interacts with usability, cohort needs, resource constraints, and local teaching goals. This motivates the development of evaluation models that compare alternatives across multiple criteria rather than relying on a single dominant feature, such as cost or popularity.

Generative AI and AI-assisted tools are now prominent in discussions about music learning due to their potential to support practice, feedback, creativity, and personalized learning pathways. A recent review of generative AI in music education highlights both potential benefits and risks, including questions about appropriate pedagogical framing, the reliability of outputs, and the need for teacher mediation13. Systematic syntheses also emphasize that AI in music education can enable personalization and interactive learning, while simultaneously raising concerns around transparency, bias, and the responsible integration of AI into learning ecosystems.

Acceptance research provides well-established frameworks for understanding why educators and learners adopt technologies. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) emphasizes perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use as central predictors of acceptance, whereas the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) integrate broader determinants, including performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions. Consistent with these frameworks, this study’s learner-facing criteria explicitly capture perceived usefulness (LE5) and ease of use (LE1), while technical fit and implementation feasibility operationalize facilitating conditions at the institutional level (e.g., integration, reliability, training/support burden)14. To ensure the framework reflects AI-specific adoption issues, we also draw on a recent grounded meta-analysis review of educators’ AI adoption, which synthesizes factors beyond classical acceptance models—highlighting themes such as AI literacy/self-efficacy, institutional support, perceived transparency/bias/reliability, and ethical concerns. This supports treating governance and ethics as first-class evaluation dimensions rather than peripheral considerations15,16. These findings are consistent with broader governance guidance that calls for educational institutions to validate generative AI systems for ethical and pedagogical appropriateness, including attention to privacy and safeguards around use in teaching and assessment. Together, these strands of literature justify treating governance and ethics as first-class evaluation dimensions rather than optional checks added after tools are selected. Recent governance-aware research on AI-based decision-support systems further reinforces this choice. Scenario-based evaluation approaches show how regulatory compliance and fairness risks can be assessed jointly, illustrating why governance criteria must be integrated into evaluation frameworks rather than treated as an afterthought17. Likewise, fairness-aware and explainable modeling work in educational prediction highlights the need to evaluate transparency and bias alongside performance, supporting the inclusion of fairness and transparency as explicit criteria in education-facing AI systems18. Finally, recent bibliometric and trend-mapping studies of AI adoption in education underscore the field’s rapid growth and the parallel emphasis on responsible, policy-aware deployment, which aligns with the governance-oriented structure proposed in this study19.

Higher education music programs face distinctive selection challenges because tool needs differ across performance, theory, composition, music education, and music technology pathways, and because teaching environments vary between classrooms, studios, labs, and ensembles. Research on digital teaching in university-level music education indicates that technology use and teaching formats changed substantially around recent periods of expanded digital delivery, and that preferences and practices vary across institutions and instructors20. This variation makes informal selection risky: a tool that works well for one pathway or teaching context may perform poorly elsewhere due to accessibility issues, integration limitations, or support burden.

In addition, AI-enabled tools introduce institutional risks that are not always visible to individual instructors, such as data protection compliance, security posture, and academic integrity controls. UNESCO guidance explicitly emphasizes the need for validation processes and governance measures when adopting generative AI tools in education and research, underscoring that responsibility cannot be delegated to individual users. These challenges point to the need for a structured multi-criteria approach that incorporates multiple stakeholder perspectives and evaluates competing requirements transparently.

Multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) methods are widely used in decision-support settings where choices require transparent trade-offs across multiple, competing objectives. Foundational work in multiple-attribute decision making (MADM) provides the conceptual basis for structuring such problems and for comparing alternatives systematically21. In this study, AHP is used to derive criterion weights through structured pairwise comparisons, with consistency checks, following established AHP formulations for measurement and prioritization22. TOPSIS is then used to rank alternatives based on their relative distance to ideal best and ideal worst solutions, aligning with classic MADM approaches to alternative evaluation. Recent evidence also shows a growing use of MCDM methods in educational research and higher-education decision-making contexts, supporting the relevance of an AHP and the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to an Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) integration for technology selection problems in education23. Accordingly, the proposed AHP–TOPSIS workflow is framed as a decision-support tool that makes weighting assumptions explicit and produces an interpretable ranking that can be adapted to local priorities. Prior work shows that MCDM methods are widely used to support the selection of educational and e-learning technologies, where multiple competing criteria must be balanced transparently. Systematic reviews in education and higher education report frequent use of AHP and hybrid MCDM models (including AHP–TOPSIS) for institutional decision problems, supporting the methodological choice adopted here24.

Within educational research, reviews show increasing use of multi-criteria methods for decision problems such as evaluation, selection, and ranking in higher education contexts25. Recent education-oriented applications also demonstrate the practical feasibility of combining AHP and TOPSIS for structured selection tasks, including platform evaluation scenarios in higher education settings. However, the music education technology domain has a gap: the literature discusses the benefits and risks of tools by category, but institutions often lack an integrated selection process that connects those insights to transparent weighting, ranking, and robustness testing aligned with stakeholder priorities.

The literature indicates three needs that are not fully addressed together. First, evidence suggests that technology can improve music learning outcomes, yet effects vary by tool type and context, making ad hoc selection unreliable. Second, AI-enabled tools create governance and ethics requirements that institutions must manage proactively rather than reactively. Third, multi-criteria decision making provides a mature methodological foundation for transparent selection. Still, music-specific adoption decisions rarely operationalize this rigor in ways that integrate expert judgment, learner evidence, and institutional constraints25. This paper addresses the gap by presenting a structured AHP and TOPSIS framework tailored to higher-education music programs, grounded in literature-derived criteria, refined through expert review, and populated with both expert evaluations and questionnaire data from 100 music learners. The framework produces a ranked shortlist and assesses ranking stability through sensitivity and scenario analyses, enabling defensible decision-making in procurement and curriculum design. The full student questionnaire (including all items and response scales) is provided in Appendix C.

Although AHP–TOPSIS has been used in prior educational technology selection studies, this study contributes a domain-specific decision model for AI-supported music education by (i) defining a specialized criteria set that elevates governance/ethics, pedagogical alignment, and music-specific functionality as first-class dimensions; (ii) integrating expert weighting with student evidence as performance inputs to reduce purely expert-centric evaluation; and (iii) reporting robustness checks that clarify when and why rankings change under plausible variations in priorities and judgment uncertainty.

Methodology design and modeling framework

Research design and decision problem formulation

This research adopts a multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) approach to address the inherently multidimensional nature of selecting AI-enabled music-learning technologies in higher education. Technology choices in music programs rarely depend on a single factor; instead, they require balancing pedagogical benefit, student experience, technical compatibility, implementation constraints, and governance/ethical responsibilities. To make this selection process transparent and defensible, the study integrates Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) and TOPSIS. AHP is used to elicit and validate the relative importance (weights) of evaluation criteria through structured pairwise comparisons and consistency checks, reflecting expert priorities rather than informal preference. TOPSIS is then applied to rank the technology alternatives by measuring each option’s closeness to an “ideal” solution (best performance across all criteria) and distance from a “negative ideal” (worst performance), producing a transparent and interpretable shortlist suitable for procurement and curriculum planning.

Alternatives are modelled as tool archetypes rather than vendor-specific systems because institutional decision-making often begins by selecting a functional category aligned with curriculum needs and governance constraints before shortlisting products. Category-level evaluation supports early-stage strategy, reduces volatility from rapid vendor changes, and enables comparison across capabilities and institutional risk. Vendor-level adoption should be treated as a subsequent step that requires local verification of security, accessibility, compliance, integration feasibility, and total cost of ownership. Zhongyuan Institute of Science and Technology confirmed that all questionnaires and the online protocol were completed and that the obtained data are confidentially retained to preserve respondents’ anonymity.

For TOPSIS computation, each sub-criterion is classified as either a benefit criterion (higher values preferred) or a cost criterion (lower values preferred). Benefit criteria typically include learning effectiveness, quality of feedback, engagement support, accessibility, usability, and interoperability, because improvements in these dimensions enhance educational outcomes or the user experience. Cost-related constructs (e.g., total cost of ownership, staff training effort, and support burden) were scored using a reverse-coded 1–5 rubric, with higher values indicating lower burden / lower cost strain (i.e., better feasibility). Accordingly, these criteria are treated as benefit-oriented inputs in TOPSIS (equivalently, they can be viewed as cost criteria that are reverse-coded prior to computation). This classification is established during the criteria refinement stage with stakeholder agreement to avoid inconsistent interpretation during scoring. The benefit/cost designation enables the TOPSIS ideal solution to be defined correctly, maximizing benefits while minimizing costs, and ensures the resulting ranking reflects both educational ambition and operational realism (Fig. 1).

This study evaluates AI-supported music technologies using a single hierarchical criteria model organized into five dimensions: Pedagogical Value, Learner Experience, Technical Fit, Implementation Feasibility, and Governance & Ethics (Table 2). Section 2.2 provides the full criteria-development procedure and justification for the hierarchy. The governance and ethics dimension aligns with widely cited policy and regulatory guidance on trustworthy AI in education and institutional settings. UNESCO’s guidance on generative AI in education emphasizes privacy, transparency, accountability, and human-centered oversight, while the OECD AI Principles similarly highlight robust, trustworthy, and rights-respecting AI governance. In the European context, the EU AI Act’s risk-based framework further underscores the need to assess compliance, documentation, and institutional risk when adopting AI-supported systems26.

Criteria development and expert elicitation procedure

Section 2.1 provides a high-level overview of the evaluation dimensions; it documents the full hierarchy of criteria, including how it was derived and refined, culminating in the final structure reported in Table 2. The evaluation criteria were developed through a structured synthesis that combined a focused literature survey with practice-based input from music teaching and music technology contexts. A targeted search on Google Scholar was conducted to identify recurring constructs used to evaluate music learning technologies and AI-supported educational tools in higher education. The search covered research on notation platforms, digital audio workstations, ear-training applications, AI accompaniment and practice tools, learning analytics in music learning, technology acceptance in music education, accessibility in educational technology, and privacy and ethical governance of AI in education. Recurrent constructs reported across studies were extracted and organized into an initial criterion pool comprising educational value, student experience, technical suitability, adoption feasibility, and governance responsibilities. This initial pool was then triangulated through discussions with music trainers and audio technology specialists who described authentic classroom and studio workflows, assessment practices, student support needs, and typical adoption barriers. The combined evidence-building process produced a final hierarchical set of criteria grouped into five dimensions. Although the criteria were derived inductively from the literature and refined with expert input, we cross-checked them against established acceptance frameworks to strengthen theoretical grounding and transparency. TAM’s perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use map directly to perceived usefulness (LE5) and ease of use (LE1), and UTAUT’s performance/effort expectancy and facilitating conditions correspond to learner experience constructs (LE1, LE5) and the institutional enablers represented in technical fit and implementation feasibility (e.g., interoperability, reliability, training/support capacity). In addition, a grounded meta-analysis review of educators’ AI adoption synthesizes AI-specific themes—such as transparency, bias, reliability, institutional support, and ethical concerns—that are explicitly represented in this framework’s governance/ethics and technical-fit criteria. This comparison shows that the proposed model is compatible with established adoption theory and extends it to procurement-oriented, governance-aware evaluation in higher education16. The initial scan of candidate tool categories was conducted using Google Scholar as the primary academic search engine, supplemented by targeted checking of (i) major publisher indexes (via citation chasing from key papers) and (ii) institutional procurement/IT guidance pages and vendor documentation identified through expert recommendations. In the second step, we consulted N =20 practitioners/experts (drawn from the expert panel in Table 3) to validate that the shortlisted categories reflect tools commonly encountered in higher-education music programs and to confirm that no major category was missing. Practitioner suggestions were iteratively reviewed, and no new alternative categories emerged after the final consultation round, which we treated as a practical indication of thematic saturation for category identification.

The criterion pool was mapped to recurring themes in prior work: (i) computer-assisted aural skills/ear-training tools and interventions informed musicianship-development and feedback/engagement constructs; (ii) informed perceived usefulness, ease-of-use/usability, and adoption-related learner experience constructs; (iii) accessibility standards informed accessibility criteria; and (iv) education-focused AI governance guidance informed privacy, transparency, integrity, and institutional risk criteria10. Representative anchors include computer-assisted aural skills/ear-training literature, foundations of technology acceptance, digital accessibility guidance (WCAG)27, and UNESCO guidance on generative AI governance in education and research26.

The initial criterion pool was refined to ensure each criterion was distinct, measurable, and consistently interpretable across stakeholder groups. Overlapping items were consolidated, broad constructs were operationalized as observable indicators, and each sub-criterion was defined operationally, grounded in the realities of higher-education music programs. These definitions guided the development of a scoring rubric in which each performance level corresponded to clear descriptors rather than subjective impressions. Refinement was carried out by iteratively balancing literature-grounded constructs with expert feedback, ensuring that the criteria remained academically defensible while also reflecting institutional constraints, such as cohort size, device diversity, and support capacity. During this step, each sub-criterion was designated as either a benefit criterion or a cost criterion so that subsequent ranking computations treat preference directions correctly.

Expert panel selection and recruitment

Expert input was used to validate the criteria structure and to provide judgments for AHP weighting and selected scoring tasks. A purposive sampling strategy was used to recruit panel members who collectively represent the decision roles typically involved in institutional adoption. The panel included music educators spanning performance, musicianship, theory, composition, and music education, as well as audio technology specialists experienced in studio teaching and digital production workflows. It also included expertise in learning technology and integration, such as learning designers or LMS administrators, as well as governance expertise, such as data protection or institutional technology policy roles. Panellists were selected based on direct experience evaluating or deploying music learning technologies, because pairwise comparisons in AHP require informed judgments about relative importance rather than general familiarity. Expert involvement at this stage strengthens the legitimacy of the weighting structure and ensures alignment with both pedagogical goals and operational realities. The expert panel and student respondents were recruited using non-probability sampling (purposive/convenience) from a limited set of institutional contexts. Experts were approached through existing professional and institutional networks and invited based on role relevance and direct experience evaluating or deploying music-learning technologies. Student participants were recruited through course- and program-level outreach (e.g., announcements or messages distributed via departmental channels) within the participating institution(s). Participation was voluntary, and the resulting samples should be interpreted as context-specific rather than representative of all higher-education music programs; consequently, external validity is constrained, and findings are most generalizable to settings with similar program structures, resourcing, and digital infrastructure.

For criteria informed by both expert assessment and learner experience, we combined the two aggregates using an equal-weight average (50% expert, 50% learner). This choice was made to (i) avoid an expert-dominant model that would marginalize the intended end-user perspective, while also (ii) avoiding a learner-only model for constructs where technical feasibility, pedagogical alignment, and institutional constraints require expert interpretation. Equal weighting provides a transparent, assumption-minimizing baseline when no external standard exists for the “correct” stakeholder weighting. To ensure this modeling assumption does not unduly influence conclusions, we assess robustness through a sensitivity analysis using stakeholder-priority scenarios (expert-leaning vs. learner-leaning).

Elicitation rounds and instruments

Data collection was organized into sequential rounds to separate validation, weighting, and scoring while maintaining traceability across steps. In the first round, experts reviewed the criteria, operational definitions, and rubric to verify completeness, clarity, and relevance, and to confirm the criterion-type assignments. Weight derivation (AHP) and ranking (TOPSIS) followed the procedures detailed later in the Methods and fully specified in Appendices A and B. In parallel with expert scoring, the study captured learner perspectives through questionnaires completed by 100 music learners recruited from higher education institutions across various music-related fields, including performance, composition, music education, music technology, audio production, and musicology, as well as by students who take music modules as electives where applicable. These learners were recruited via convenience-based channels available to the research team (program/course outreach in the participating institution(s)), so the learner sample reflects those institutional settings and may not represent broader national or international student populations.

Students completed questionnaires following structured exposure to the shortlisted technologies through guided demonstrations, trial tasks, or short pilot activities, where feasible. All student participants completed ratings for all six shortlisted technology categories (A1–A6) after standardized exposure sessions organized by the research team. Exposure consisted of (i) a short, guided demonstration introducing the core workflow of each category, followed by (ii) a structured hands-on task aligned with typical course use (e.g., notated playback/annotation; basic DAW editing; ear-training drills; accompaniment/practice control; LMS-feedback workflow; and a constrained AI-assisted composition prompt). To ensure comparability, each category was experienced in the same setting (in-class/lab) and using the same rating instrument immediately after the interaction. Exposure time per category was brief but sufficient to complete the standardized task; therefore, ratings reflect short-term, use-based perceptions rather than long-term adoption experience. Where exposure differed by category, differences were limited to task setup complexity (e.g., DAW and LMS workflows) while keeping the task scope equivalent. Alternatives are labeled A1–A6 for readability (Table 1).

AHP pairwise comparison aggregation method

AHP judgments were collected from experts individually and aggregated to create group comparison matrices using the geometric mean, which is appropriate for ratio-scale judgments and preserves reciprocity in pairwise comparisons. The aggregated matrices were used to compute criterion weights using the principal eigenvector approach, and the exact equations used for aggregation, eigenvector weight derivation, and normalization are provided in Appendix A. This aggregation approach reduces the influence of any single expert and yields weights that represent a consolidated institutional viewpoint. To support subsequent robustness testing, the study also prepared role-informed weight profiles by grouping experts into pedagogical, technical, and governance-oriented perspectives, thereby allowing the ranking to be recalculated under alternative priority profiles without altering the underlying performance scores.

Consistency testing was applied to each aggregated AHP matrix to verify that expert judgments were logically coherent. The consistency index and consistency ratio were computed using the measures reported in Appendix A and compared against accepted thresholds. When the consistency ratio exceeded the threshold, a revision protocol focused on the comparisons that most contributed to inconsistency. When CR exceeded the threshold, experts revised only the flagged comparisons using the shared operational definitions (see Sect. 2.2.6).

Sensitivity and scenario analysis design

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the ranking’s robustness to plausible changes in priorities and constraints. Weight sensitivity tests varied key criterion weights within defined ranges while monitoring for rank reversals among the alternatives in Table 1. Scenario analysis recalculated results using stakeholder-oriented weight profiles derived from subgroup AHP weights to represent different institutional perspectives, such as a pedagogically led scenario prioritizing learning outcomes, a learner-centered scenario prioritizing usability and accessibility, a technical stewardship scenario prioritizing integration and reliability, and a governance-oriented scenario prioritizing privacy and integrity. Resource-constrained scenarios were also tested by increasing the relative emphasis on feasibility-related cost criteria such as total cost of ownership, staff training effort, and support burden. These analyses allow the study to report which rankings are stable across scenarios and which options are sensitive to shifts in institutional priorities.

The study applied multiple measures to strengthen reliability, validity, and fairness. Content validity was supported by grounding criteria in a documented literature survey and then refining them through expert review and operational definition development, as reported in Table 2. Construct clarity was reinforced through anchored rubric descriptors, reducing interpretive variability across raters. The student questionnaire administered to 100 learners was piloted for clarity, and internal consistency of multi-item constructs was evaluated using appropriate reliability measures, ensuring that aggregated learner ratings in the TOPSIS matrix are stable indicators of learner experience. Common method bias was reduced by separating the weighting task from the scoring task and by using distinct evidence sources for each criterion, rather than relying on a single respondent group. Expert bias was mitigated through aggregation of judgments, consistency checks, and documented revision steps. Student sampling bias was reduced by recruiting from multiple music-related fields and study routes and by providing standardized exposure to the technologies prior to rating collection. Transparency and replicability were supported by maintaining an audit trail that records the final alternative shortlist in Table 1, the criteria and evidence plan in Table 2, the aggregated questionnaire statistics used for learner criteria, and the AHP and TOPSIS computations reported in Appendices A and B. As shown in Fig. 2, the study moves from identifying literature- and practice-based criteria through expert weighting using AHP and consistency checks, to constructing the TOPSIS decision matrix using combined evidence from expert assessments and 100 student questionnaires, before conducting sensitivity and scenario analyses.

The expert data in Tables 3 and 4, and 5 were used in two ways in the model. First, experts conducted AHP pairwise comparisons, and their judgments were aggregated using the geometric mean to produce group-level comparison matrices, which in turn generated the final criterion weights used subsequently, as described in Appendix (A) Second, experts provided rubric-based performance scores for each alternative, which were averaged across Round 3 respondents to form the expert component of the TOPSIS decision matrix described in Sect. 3.5. Student questionnaire data from 100 higher-education music learners were aggregated separately for learner-focused criteria by averaging item responses to produce criterion-level scores for each alternative. These learner aggregates were then merged with the expert aggregates for criteria that intentionally combine learner and expert evidence, creating the complete decision matrix, which is subsequently normalized and ranked using the TOPSIS procedure in Appendix (B) Procedural details are provided in Methods and Appendices; the Results section reports outputs and highlights only the key interpretations needed to understand the findings.

Overall rank stability is high (Spearman’s ρ = 0.89; Kendall’s τ = 0.73), indicating that student-facing priorities can meaningfully reshape the shortlist ordering without overturning the leading positions.

Table 16 suggests that rank swaps within the top tier reflect priority trade-offs, while the lowest-ranked option remains consistently separated across scenarios. Because some expert subgroups were small (e.g., governance roles n = 3), scenario weights are intended as plausible priority profiles for stress testing rather than precise estimates of stakeholder preferences.

Discussion

Building on the reported weights, decision matrix values, and closeness coefficients, this section interprets the ranking’s implications for institutional selection of AI-supported music technologies. We discuss (i) the practical drivers of the top-ranked alternatives, (ii) how governance/ethics and feasibility trade off against learner-facing benefits, and (iii) how different stakeholder priorities can shift the shortlist under the scenario analyses. The dimension weights in Table 8 show that experts placed the greatest emphasis on pedagogical value and learner experience, with technical fit close behind. This pattern aligns with broader evidence in higher education that technology is most beneficial when it directly supports learning outcomes and engagement rather than being adopted for novelty. A recent meta-analysis of technology use in music education reported an overall positive effect on achievement, with variation by technology type and learning context, supporting the need for structured selection frameworks that account for contextual differences rather than assuming that all tools contribute equally28. At the sub-criterion level, Table 9 indicates that feedback usefulness and alignment with course learning outcomes had the most substantial pedagogical influence. In contrast, ease of use and perceived usefulness were the strongest drivers of learner experience. These priorities are consistent with acceptance research that repeatedly identifies usefulness and effort-related perceptions as key predictors of adoption intentions. In music-specific contexts, recent work on acceptance of generative AI in teaching similarly highlights that adoption is shaped by perceived value and perceived ease, underscoring why learner experience criteria in Tables 2 and 9 materially affect final rankings15.

The baseline TOPSIS ranking in Table 13 places A1 first, A5 second, and A3 third, and the dimension profiles in Table 11 help explain these outcomes. A1 performs strongly across pedagogical value, learner experience, and technical fit, which is essential because Tables 8 and 9 assign substantial combined weight to these dimensions and their highest priority sub-criteria. The result is consistent with the role of notation tools as core infrastructure across performance, theory, and composition teaching, where feedback, iteration, and assessment alignment are central learning needs. A5 ranks highly primarily because the governance, ethics, and institutional integration criteria are strong, as shown in Table 11 and the governance rows in Table 12. This aligns with the growing emphasis in higher education on privacy, security, and integrity controls when integrating advanced digital tools, particularly those that involve data collection or AI-enabled functions. Reviews of AI in music education repeatedly emphasize both the opportunities for motivation and autonomy and the importance of careful integration and oversight, which is consistent with the finding that governance performance can elevate an LMS-integrated option in institution-level decisions29. A3 ranks in the top three because it combines high learner experience and strong feasibility with adequate pedagogical value, as shown in Table 11 and the feasibility scores in Table 12. This result is consistent with recent empirical work reporting the effectiveness and perceived usefulness of ear training software among university learners, supporting the idea that such tools can scale formative practice support with relatively low implementation burden30.

A2 and A4 fall in the middle ranks in Table 13, which reflects trade-offs rather than failure. A2 scores very strongly on technical fit in Table 11, which is expected because digital audio workstation environments are central to production learning and studio-based assessment. Recent studies on DAW-supported learning report positive links to motivation and performance outcomes in specific training contexts, supporting targeted adoption when production outcomes are primary rather than universal adoption across all music programs31. A4 scores strongly on learner experience but is constrained by governance and certain aspects of technical fit in Tables 11 and 12, which help explain its placement in Table 13. This aligns with current literature describing AI-assisted practice tools as potentially valuable for real-time feedback and practice self-regulation, while also raising questions about transparency, data practices, and appropriate pedagogical framing32.

A6 ranks last in Table 13 and exhibits the weakest governance and technical fit profiles in Table 11. This is consistent with a recurring theme in the AI in music education literature: generative and highly automated systems can offer creative and motivational benefits, but they also introduce challenges for integrity, transparency, and accountability that institutions must manage explicitly. Studies examining AI-enabled music teaching note measurable learning benefits in some settings, yet al.so imply that governance requirements and classroom policy design become more important as AI capability increases, which explains why governance criteria in Table 2 can materially change adoption attractiveness33.

The stability patterns in Tables 14 and 15 indicate that the shortlist is robust yet sensitive in meaningful ways to governance emphasis and resource constraints. This is a practical advantage of combining AHP for validated weights with TOPSIS for ranking because institutions can see both a baseline recommendation and how it changes under alternative strategic priorities. The approach is consistent with established multi-criteria decision-making literature, which positions integrated AHP and TOPSIS as suitable when decisions require transparent weighting, mixed qualitative and quantitative evidence, and defensible ranking outputs34. While incorporating multiple stakeholder perspectives improves face validity and practical relevance, the empirical calibration of this study (weights and performance inputs) reflects a single national and institutional context. Therefore, the resulting shortlist should be interpreted as context-dependent and most applicable to institutions with curricula, infrastructure, governance expectations, and resourcing comparable to those of the institutions in question. In addition, learner ratings were collected after standardized but relatively brief exposure (guided demonstrations and short trial tasks), meaning learner criteria capture short-term perceived usability/engagement rather than sustained adoption effects. These constraints should temper claims of transferability; future work should replicate the framework across multiple institutions and include longer pilot deployments (e.g., semester-long use with repeated measures and/or usage logs) to validate whether rankings remain stable under deeper exposure and different governance/procurement regimes.

The AHP-derived weights represent the expert panel’s normative preferences and therefore encode value judgments rather than objective truths. This creates risks of subjectivity, institutional bias, and path dependence (e.g., overweighting governance or cost may systematically disadvantage innovative tools, while overweighting pedagogy may understate compliance and sustainability constraints). Accordingly, the ranking should be interpreted as a transparent decision-support output contingent on stakeholder priorities, and institutions should treat it as a starting point for local deliberation rather than a definitive recommendation.

Limitations and future work

Although the study benefits from combining expert judgments and student perspectives, both the expert panel and the student respondents were recruited using purposive/convenience sampling from limited institutional context(s). As a result, the weighting structure and performance ratings may reflect local program priorities, available infrastructure, and governance practices in the participating setting(s). These sampling constraints limit external validity; replication across multiple institutions, regions, and curriculum models (e.g., conservatoire vs. comprehensive university; differing procurement/governance regimes) is needed before generalizing the ranking patterns more broadly.

First, the alternatives in Table 1 represent categories and representative tools rather than a single fixed vendor configuration, and actual procurement outcomes can vary depending on licensing models, support contracts, and integration options. A practical extension is to apply the same framework to institution-specific vendor finalists whose security, accessibility, and cost documentation have been verified. Second, although 100 student questionnaires strengthen the learner evidence base, learner ratings may still reflect short exposure windows and novelty effects. Future work should include longer pilot periods and repeated measures over a semester to examine how perceived usefulness, engagement, and learning impact evolve with sustained use.

Third, the scoring structure in Table 12 combines expert and student evidence, and some criteria inevitably rely on judgment because objective measurement is not always available. Future studies can strengthen objectivity by incorporating log-based usage data, performance assessment outcomes, and support ticket metrics, and then validating whether these indicators correlate with rubric scores. Fourth, governance and ethics scores depend on the availability and clarity of the documents. Some vendors provide limited transparency regarding AI features and data practices, which can constrain the quality of evaluations. A future improvement is a standardized evidence checklist aligned with institutional policy that includes minimum disclosure requirements for inclusion on the shortlist. Finally, the AHP weights in Tables 8 and 9 reflect the composition of the expert panel in Table 3. While the scenario analysis in Table 15 mitigates the risk of overreliance on a single viewpoint, future work could expand the panel to include multiple institutions and additional stakeholder groups, such as disability support services and library or procurement specialists, to improve external validity and transferability across institutional contexts. In addition, vendor-level selection should not rely solely on the ranking presented here. Any local adoption decision requires context-specific verification of (i) security and data protection requirements, (ii) accessibility compliance and learner support needs, and (iii) total cost of ownership (e.g., licensing, integration, training, and ongoing support).

Learner ratings were collected after standardized demonstrations and short trial tasks designed to ensure all respondents could evaluate each category consistently. However, these exposures do not substitute for longitudinal adoption; therefore, criteria such as engagement/motivation should be interpreted as short-term perceived support during trial use, not evidence of sustained practice outcomes. Future work should validate learner-facing criteria using longer pilot deployments, usage logs, and repeated-measures designs that capture retention and practice behaviors over time.

The 50/50 aggregation of expert and learner inputs for shared criteria is a modeling assumption rather than an empirically identified parameter; different institutions may legitimately prefer different stakeholder weightings. Future work will estimate stakeholder weights through participatory governance processes and further quantify sensitivity by using wider stakeholder-weight ranges and uncertainty-aware simulation. Scenario weights were derived from AHP judgments within expert subgroups. However, some subgroups were small, most notably the governance-roles subgroup (n = 3; Table 2), which increases sensitivity to any single participant’s judgments and may reduce the stability of subgroup-aggregated weights. Although consistency checks were applied to aggregated matrices, small-n subgroup weighting remains more vulnerable to idiosyncratic preferences or minor inconsistencies; therefore, scenario-based results should be interpreted as illustrative stress tests rather than definitive stakeholder weight profiles. Future work will replicate subgroup weighting using larger, more diverse governance panels.

Beyond classical AHP–TOPSIS, recent MCDA research emphasizes improved normalization/comparison procedures, an explicit treatment of judgmental inaccuracy, and approaches to reidentifying weights in complex decisions. For example, advanced normalization and decision-area comparison methods have been proposed to improve robustness under ambiguity35, and methods that account for inaccuracy in expert judgments when identifying criteria relevance directly motivate uncertainty-aware weighting and robustness checks36. Contemporary approaches to re-identifying weights further highlight opportunities to validate or reconstruct model parameters under uncertainty37. In future work, these directions can be incorporated as alternative normalization checks and uncertainty-aware weighting extensions alongside the scenario-based and Monte Carlo robustness analyses reported here.

Conclusions

A transparent and evidence-based framework was established for selecting AI-enabled music-learning technologies in higher education by combining AHP to derive validated criterion weights and TOPSIS to rank alternatives against an ideal solution. The evaluation covered six technology alternatives and 25 sub-criteria, grouped into five dimensions, using judgments from a 20-member expert panel and questionnaire data from 100 higher-education music learners. The weighting results confirmed that learning driven priorities dominated the decision structure, with pedagogical value weighted at 0.30 and learner experience at 0.22, followed by technical fit at 0.20, implementation feasibility at 0.16, and governance and ethics at 0.12. Applying these weights to the aggregated performance matrix produced a context-specific shortlist in which the notation and score learning platform category A1 achieved the highest TOPSIS closeness coefficient at 0.659, followed by the LMS integrated assessment and feedback tool category A5 at 0.575 and the ear training and musicianship application category A3 at 0.538. At the same time, A6 ranked last with 0.189, primarily due to weaker governance and technical fit performance. Robustness testing showed that the recommended shortlist remained stable across stakeholder profile scenarios and weight perturbations, with A1, A5, and A3 consistently ranking among the top three. However, institutional priorities can change, and the question is which option should be adopted first, such as A5 becoming the top choice when governance requirements dominate and A3 becoming the top choice when resource constraints dominate. The resulting outputs may support defensible procurement and curriculum planning in institutions with similar contexts, while vendor-level adoption still requires local verification of security, accessibility, and cost. However, because the model is empirically calibrated within a single institutional/national setting and learner ratings reflect brief exposure, broader transferability should be treated cautiously until multi-institution replications are conducted.
