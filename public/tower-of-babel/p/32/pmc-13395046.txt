The genetic landscape of congenital diarrheas and very early onset inflammatory bowel disease in the Middle East
Authors: Lily Gillette, Muna Al Safar, Ganeshwaran H Mochida, Casey Johnson, Stephen Babcock, Klaus Schmitz‐Abe, Michael Field, Jessica Yang, Richelle Bearup, Ahmed Megahed, Mohammed Ezz El Regal Abbas, Tarek Baraket, Sherine Elziny, Ali Sobh, Gulin Eren, Fawaz AlRefaee, Rana Bitar, Mohamed Miqdady, Pankaj B Agrawal, Scott B Snapper, Jay R Thiagarajah, Jodie Ouahed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesMonogenic causes of congenital diarrheas and enteropathies (CoDE) and very early onset inflammatory bowel disease (VEOIBD) are mostly recessive and therefore more prevalent in populations with increased consanguinity rates. To assess the genetic basis of these disorders in a likely high‐prevalence population, we established a multi‐center cohort of patients across the Middle East.MethodsPatients were enrolled across four centers in the Middle East. Clinical data, including self‐reported consanguinity, were collected. Trio whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed, and genomic data were processed through a standardized variant identification pipeline. Patients who inherited a rare variant consistent with the disease in a gene known to be causative for CoDE or VEOIBD were classified as having a monogenic etiology. Self‐reported consanguinity and homozygosity mapping were analyzed and correlated with monogenic diagnoses.ResultsWES from trios of 27 patients (13 CoDE and 14 VEOIBD) were analyzed. Higher frequency of consanguinity was self‐reported by the CoDE families (11/13, 84.6%) and confirmed by homozygosity mapping than in the VEOIBD group (4/14, 28.6%). A monogenic cause of disease was identified in 10 of 13 CoDE patients (76.9%) in the following genes: EPCAM, SPINT2, DGAT1, MYO5B, SPINT2, and STXBP2. A single VEOIBD patient (7.1%) had an identifiable monogenic cause of disease in SKIV2L.ConclusionsWithin this limited cohort, the diagnostic yield for known genes associated with CoDE was high, consistent with the primarily monogenic etiology and high consanguinity. For VEOIBD, the diagnostic yield was lower and similar to that reported in North America.Keywords: CoDE, consanguineous, monogenic, VEOIBD

INTRODUCTION

Congenital diarrheas and enteropathies (CoDEs) and very early onset inflammatory bowel disease (VEOIBD) are rare gastrointestinal diseases that present with severe diarrhea and other clinical manifestations in infancy or early childhood. For each, a growing number of monogenic causes of disease have been identified: over 70 monogenic causes of VEOIBD, 1 and over 40 for CoDEs. 2

Identifying monogenic causes of diseases facilitates understanding the underlying cellular and genetic pathways involved, enabling personalized treatments and, in some circumstances cures. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 For example, optimization of dietary management and tailored nutritional plans based on genetic diagnosis can aid in the reduction of dependence on parenteral nutrition in patients with CoDE. 6 For VEOIBD, personalized treatments include anti‐interleukin (IL) 1 agents for VEOIBD secondary to mevalonate kinase deficiency, 7 abatacept for liposaccharide‐responsive and beige‐like anchor (LRBA) or cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte‐associated protein 4 (CTLA4) deficiency, 8 and hematopoietic bone marrow transplants for IL10 signaling defects. 9 Furthermore, identifying specific gene variants can provide information on disease severity, clinical prognosis, and can help guide future family planning and resource allocation.

The diagnostic rate for identifying a monogenic etiology among patients with CoDEs in a multi‐center North American cohort was recently reported as approximately 50%. 10 The general diagnostic rate of CoDE in China was found to be 58%–72%, depending on the mode of detection. 11 The diagnostic rate of monogenic VEOIBD has been reported to be ~8% in two single‐center North American studies. 12 , 13 In European cohorts, monogenic VEOIBD diagnostic rates are roughly 32%. 14 Whereas in China, it ranges between 63%–72% among patients tested for monogenic disease. 15 , 16 As with other monogenic disorders, monogenic diagnoses of VEOIBD were most prevalent in infants presenting with symptoms prior to 12 months of age (42.9%). 13 Across the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, disorder‐specific CoDE and VEOIBD have been reported in several single‐center studies. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 However, monogenic CoDEs and VEOIBD have not yet been reported across multiple sites within the MENA region. Other monogenic diseases, including ciliary disorders, hearing loss, and congenital structural anomalies, are over‐represented in Middle Eastern consanguineous populations. 25 The overall increased prevalence of genetic disease in populations with higher rates of consanguinity would suggest an increased probability of finding causative recessive loss‐of‐function variants in children presenting with CoDE and VEOIBD.

To assess this, we prospectively established a multi‐center cohort of patients from four countries in the MENA region, historically characterized with highly consanguineous populations, 18%–65%. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Enrolled patients presented to pediatric gastroenterologists with symptoms associated with CoDE and VEOIBD. We sought to understand the overall genetic architecture of monogenic gastrointestinal disease in this population, including the relationship between self‐reported consanguinity or genetically confirmed homozygosity and diagnostic yield.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Boston Children's Hospital (BCH) (IRB‐P00000529 and IRB‐P00027983). The Ethical Review Committee at Sheikh Khalifa Medical City approved the study (REC‐19.06.2018 [RS‐507]). Izmir Behcet Uz Children's Hospital relied on the BCH protocol via an IRB reliance agreement. At the Mansoura University Hospital in Egypt, this study was approved (R.20.05.851 for recruitment of VEOIBD patients and RP.20.03.56 for the CoDE patients). Addan Hospital and Al‐Amiri Hospital in Kuwait were approved (#7085). Parent/guardian and patient consent/assent were obtained for all participants.

Study design

Our primary aim in establishing a multi‐center cohort was to identify monogenic causes of early‐onset pediatric gastrointestinal diseases, including VEOIBD and CoDEs, in populations historically described as being highly consanguineous. Our cohort includes participants from sites across four Middle Eastern countries: Addan Hospital and Al‐Amiri Hospital in Kuwait, Sheikh Khalifa Medical City (SKMC) in Abu Dhabi, Mansoura University Hospital in Egypt, and Izmir Behcet Uz Children's Hospital in Turkey. Enrollment took place between October 1, 2018, and June 30, 2022.

Inclusion/Exclusion criteria

Enrolled patients with CoDEs comprised deficits in the following categories: epithelial structure, trafficking and polarity, epithelial transport, and epithelial enzymes and metabolism. Patients with VEOIBD diagnosed prior to 6 years of age included ulcerative colitis, Crohn's disease, or inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)‐unclassified. Participants who had undergone a bone marrow transplant were excluded, since this work was designed to target identifiable monogenic causes of disease.

Recruitment

Participant recruitment efforts were facilitated by having a genetic counselor (M.AS.) working in concert with the team at BCH, present at the time of enrollment. Our genetic counselor, in collaboration with local physicians, identified families in which at least one infant or child is eligible using established inclusion criteria and assessed their willingness to participate according to the protocols approved by a local Research Ethics Board. Consent or assent was obtained for each participant in accordance with ethical standards. Consent was provided in Arabic or English language(s) according to the preference of the family. The genetic counselor also facilitated networking opportunities and fostered collaboration across the sites toward improved understanding of the genetics of CoDEs and VEOIBD disorders among local physicians and researchers.

Data collection

Our genetic counselor facilitated the collection of relevant clinical information by administering a clinical requisition form specific to the participant's diagnosis at the time of the clinic visit, to minimize recall bias. The genetic counselor worked with families to map out pedigrees and collect self‐reported consanguinity. Monthly meetings were conducted between the BCH team and our genetic counselor to proactively identify candidate subjects in upcoming clinics in the Middle East.

Sample collection

For each participant, blood was collected locally in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes. When blood could not be obtained, cheek brush or saliva collection kits were used. Biospecimens were sent to BCH.

DNA isolation and whole exome sequencing

Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) was isolated using Qiagen Puregene kits and quantified on a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed on available trios (mother, father, proband) in 10/13 of the CoDE families, duos in 1 of the 13 CoDE families, while clinical sequencing was performed on the remaining two patients. Among the VEOIBD cohort, WES was performed on 13/14 trios, and clinical sequencing was performed on the last proband (Table 1). Clinical sequencing was performed through Centogene. Research‐based sequencing was performed through Regeneron, Novogene, or Macrogen (Table 1).

Analysis of WES data

Raw WES data were returned to BCH Research Genomics via automated upload of FASTQ files. WES datasets were uniformly processed through a standard variant calling pipeline, the GeneDx Discovery Platform, managed by Genuity in a research environment, as previously described. 30 , 31

Evaluation of variants in genes known to be responsible for CoDEs and VEOIBD

Known monogenic causes of disease were defined by genes previously described as monogenic for CoDEs or VEOIBD, as long as they were rare (minor allele frequency < 0.01) and inherited in a fashion consistent with disease development. 1 , 2 Results were discussed among our group, including two gastroenterologists, a genetics researcher with expertise in international projects, and a genetic counselor. Actionable findings were relayed to the referring physician.

Evaluation of levels of homozygosity

The variant explorer pipeline (VExP) was employed to assess the levels of homozygosity among the families with available sequencing data. 32 , 33

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis between groups was performed through prism using one‐way analysis of variance Kruskall–Wallis test with Dunn's multiple comparisons test. The levels of homozygosity of the CoDE patients and VEOIBD patients were also compared to a cohort of patients with rare diseases that are primarily non‐consanguineous. 32

RESULTS

Cohort demographics

The study cohort included a total of 27 patients: 11 from the United Arab Emirates, nine from Kuwait, four from Egypt, and three from Turkey (Table 1). This cohort was comprised of 13 (48.1%) patients with CoDEs and 14 (51.9%) patients with VEOIBD. For CoDEs, the range of symptom onset was from birth to 9 months, and the age of diagnosis ranged from less than 1 month to 36 months old. For VEOIBD, the range of symptom onset was 9–54 months, while the age of diagnosis ranged from 4 to 60 months (Figure 1).

Cohort demographics. Histogram illustrating (A) age of diagnosis and (B) age of symptom onset, of patients with CoDE and VEOIBD. CoDE, congenital diarrheas and enteropathies; VEOIBD, very early onset inflammatory bowel disease.

Clinical features of CoDE and VEOIBD cohort

Among 12 of the 13 CoDE patients with available clinical data, 11/12 (91.7%) required nutritional interventions. Parenteral nutrition was required in 11/12 (91.7%) CoDE patients, and 4/11 (36.4%) required specialized formula (Supporting Information S1: Table 1A). The majority of VEOIBD patients had ulcerative colitis 7/14 (50%), followed by IBD‐unclassified 4/14 (28.6%) and Crohn's disease 3/14 (21.4%). Among the 13 VEOIBD patients with available clinical data 11/13, (84.6%) were exposed to at least two distinct classes of medication to target their IBD. Immunomodulators were used on 8/13 (61.5%) VEOIBD patients, and anti‐tumor necrosis factor (anti‐TNF) agents in 6/13 (46.2%) patients. No other biologics or small molecule inhibitors were employed in this VEOIBD cohort (Supporting Information S1: Table 1B).

Identification of monogenic etiologies of disease

Among the CoDE patients, we identified a monogenic cause of disease in 10 of the 13 patients (76.9%). This included inheritance of variants in a homozygous recessive fashion and compound heterozygous fashion. Specifically, in 6 of 10 patients, established pathogenic variants were inherited in genes known to be responsible for CoDE. In 4 of 10 patients, rare variants of unknown significance (VUS) were inherited in genes known to be responsible for CoDE (Table 2). None of the remaining three patients inherited rare variants in a fashion consistent with disease in any of the known monogenic causes of CoDE.

Among the 14 VEOIBD patients, we identified a rare variant inherited in a heterozygous fashion in SKIV2L (Ski2‐like RNA Helicase) in a single individual (7.1%). While damaging mutations in SKIV2L result in VEOIBD and trichohepatoenteric syndrome, this variant is reported as a VUS by ClinVar and also identified in his healthy mother, weakening its potential contribution to this patient's VEOIBD.

Altogether, we identified nine distinct variants in five genes known to be responsible for the disease in question in 11 patients: EPCAM (epithelial cell adhesion molecule) (five patients), DGAT1 (diacylglycerol O‐acyltransferase 1) (two patients), SPINT2 (serine peptidase inhibitor, Kunitz Type 2) (two patients), SKIV2L (one patient), and MYO5B (Myosin 5B) (one patient). The most commonly identified genetic mutation was in EPCAM, where three distinct variants were identified: p.Gln167fs was inherited in two unrelated patients in a homozygous recessive fashion; p.Gln167fs and c.492‐2A>G were inherited in a compound heterozygous fashion in two siblings; and p.Cys110Gly was inherited in a homozygous recessive fashion in a fifth patient. Additionally, the same variant NM_021102.4:c1A>G (p.Met1?) in SPINT2 was identified in two unrelated patients in a homozygous recessive fashion. Among the remaining four distinct variants, one was inherited in a heterozygous fashion with presumably autosomal dominant inheritance (SKIV2L), and three variants were each inherited in a homozygous recessive fashion. Figure S1 illustrates the predicted protein structural change introduced by VUS found in two well‐described disease genes, MYO5B and SKIV2L. The protein change in MYO5B (p.Asp492Gly) occurs in the motor‐head domain, which is a common region for disease‐causing mutations within this protein. 34

Self‐reported consanguinity and measured levels of homozygosity

In the CoDE cohort, 12 of 13 families (92.3%) self‐identified as being consanguineous, while in the VEOIBD cohort, only 4 of 14 (28.6%) self‐identified as being consanguineous (Table 1). VExP was employed to assess the levels of homozygosity among the families with available sequencing data (23/27 or 85.2%). 32 Levels of homozygosity were significantly higher among the CoDE cohort than the VEOIBD cohort, and these were each found to also be significantly higher than a control cohort of patients with rare diseases described as being relatively non‐consanguineous (Figure 2A). 32 In our cohort, we report high rates of consistency between self‐reported consanguinity and measured homozygosity, with 21 of 22 (95.5%) aligning (Table 1). Of the homozygous variants with available data (n = 6), 100% reside in an area of homozygosity (Table 2), consistent with expected genetic inheritance of rare variants in highly consanguineous populations. DISCUSSION

We investigated identifiable monogenetic etiologies for two groups of gastrointestinal disorders, CoDEs and VEOIBD, presenting in infancy and early childhood, in a prospectively recruited multi‐center cohort from four Middle Eastern countries, historically described as being highly consanguineous. We leveraged this study cohort to provide an initial description of the genetic landscape of monogenic CoDE and VEOIBD in the Middle East, in relation to self‐reported consanguinity and analysis of genetic homozygosity.

In our specific cohort, we provide initial data with regard to the genetic architecture and yield of identifiable monogenic diagnoses of CoDEs in the Middle East. First, a high rate of monogenic etiologies were identified in the CoDE patients of 76.9% (10 of 13), higher than the recently reported rate of ~48% described in a predominantly North American population, 10 but may reflect an overestimate due to selection bias. Identifiable monogenic CoDE was congruent with the very high rate of both self‐reported consanguinity (12 of 13 families, 92.3%) and measured genetic homozygosity (9 of 9 evaluated families, 100%). Most patients were also diagnosed prior to 12 months of age. For CoDEs, our findings support that the degree of consanguinity and an infantile age of onset as central considerations when prioritizing genetic testing in the Middle East. Moreover, universal pre‐ or post‐conception genetic screening for CoDEs can start to be considered for high‐risk populations such as those with a known family history of monogenic CoDE and especially in cases where there is also self‐reported consanguinity. While not standard policy in the Middle East, advanced prenatal genetic testing and genetic counseling are available on a for‐profit basis.

In contrast, the rate of monogenic etiologies identified in our limited VEOIBD cohort of 7.1% (1 of 14) resembles rates reported in two large North American studies, not historically considered to be reflecting a highly consanguineous population. 12 , 13 The VEOIBD cohort consisted of a minority of families self‐reporting consanguinity (4 of 14 families, 28.6%), paralleling measured shared genetic homozygosity (3 of 13 analyzed families, 23%). Patients with VEOIBD exhibited a later age of symptom onset and diagnosis than those with CoDEs, with most manifesting disease after 12 months of age. In North America, the younger the age of VEOIBD diagnosis, the higher the diagnostic yield of monogenic diagnosis with rates of ~40% when diagnosed prior to 12 months old. 13 In comparison, a multi‐center Iranian cohort reported a diagnostic yield of 81% in a VEOIBD cohort that was highly consanguineous (75%) presenting at a younger age (median of 43.5 months). 24 However, given the size of these studies and the potential variance in inclusion criteria in a phenotype‐based cohort studies, it is difficult to ascertain and directly compare diagnostic rates. Much larger population studies are therefore needed to assess if identifiable monogenic etiologies are more readily detected among VEOIBD patients in the Middle East presenting at younger ages and with increased homozygosity. Of note, the single patient with presumed monogenic VEOIBD in our cohort did not come from a consanguineous family and inherited this variant in a heterozygous fashion. No recessive loss of function variants responsible for VEOIBD were identified in this cohort, despite the still relatively high rate of consanguinity.

With regard to the specific monogenic causes of disease identified, the most common CoDE disorder was related to variants in EPCAM, where damaging variants were identified in five of the 13 patients (38.5%). Moreover, a specific mutation, p.Gln167fs, identified in four of the five affected patients in either a homozygous recessive fashion (two patients) or as part of compound heterozygous mutations (two siblings), is reflective of a likely founder effect. This variant has been previously described in the MENA region. 20 , 35 Another variant (p.Cys110Gly) is a novel disease‐causing variant with structural modeling suggesting disruption of a key disulfide bond (Cys110‐Cys116) in the conserved thyroglobulin‐like domain. The next most common monogenic causes were identified inSPINT2, where two patients from different families inherited the same novel variant, and two patients from different families seen at Izmir Behcet Uz Children's Hospital in Turkey inherited distinct variants in DGAT1.

Our study provides useful preliminary insights into the genetic landscape of monogenic CoDE and VEOIBD across the Middle East, including rates of monogenic diagnosis and genetic etiologies. Execution of the study required coordination of prospective enrollment of participants and families and sample collection across multiple countries, facilitated by a dedicated on‐site genetic councilor across the entire project. This allowed integration of clinical and genetic data from multiple centers and enabled detailed assessment of self‐reported and analyzed homozygosity and their relation to monogenic causes of disease. Notable findings include the identification of the same rare variant in EPCAM in four patients from three separate families in Kuwait, as well as an identical rare variant in SPINT2 in two patients from separate families in SKMC in Abu Dhabi. The clustering of these variants suggests the contribution of a founder effect carried through these families and consistent with their high rates of genetic homozygosity. Limitations of this work include one‐time data capture of clinical and demographic information resulting in incomplete clinical metadata for some patients and liable to recall bias of the reporting patient/caregiver as well as the overall cohort size. Monogenic CoDE and VEOIBD disorders remain rare diagnoses even in the populations within our study, making assembling larger cohorts logistically challenging, especially across multiple countries. Future population scale genetic sequencing efforts being organized across several countries in the Middle East should allow further insights in these severe childhood gastrointestinal conditions. This work supports the rationale for conducting larger prospective studies to assess the future prevalence of CoDE and VEOIBD in the Middle East. Our data provide a clearer understanding of the genetic architecture and diagnostic rate of monogenic disease in the Middle East for two groups of genetic gastrointestinal disorders. This information may help establish guidelines for prioritizing patients based on age of disease onset and likelihood of genomic homozygosity for prompt sequencing as well as allocation of resources for research into putative novel causes of CoDE or VEOIBD.

CONCLUSIONS

This work reflects the first multi‐center, multi‐country Middle Eastern cohort describing monogenic causes of CoDE and VEOIBD. In our cohort, we showed a very high diagnostic rate (77%) of identifiable genetic disorders among CoDE patients, consistent with the high degree of self‐reported consanguinity and measured homozygosity among these families. Patients with VEOIBD in this group exhibited a lower monogenic diagnostic rate (7%), consistent with both lower self‐reported consanguinity, and later onset of disease symptoms.
