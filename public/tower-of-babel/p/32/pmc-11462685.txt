Revisiting the association between pretreatment thrombocytosis and cancer survival outcomes: an umbrella review of meta-analyses
Authors: Chi Shu, Xiran Wang, Changtao Li, Jun Huang, Xuan Xie, Hong Li, Jichun Zhao, Ziqiang Wang, Yazhou He, Yanhong Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough associations have been reported linking pretreatment thrombocytosis to cancer survival outcomes, the validity and strength of existing observational evidence have been contested. This study aimed to conduct an umbrella review to comprehensively appraise the strength, validity and credibility of these reported associations.MethodsWe searched Medline, Embase and Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews from inception to 8 April 2023 to retrieve meta-analyses of observational studies. Meta-analyses were re-performed using a random-effect model and the strength of evidence was graded as convincing, highly suggestive, suggestive and weak according to seven pre-defined quantitative criteria reflecting statistical significance, amount of data, heterogeneity, and evidence of bias. The quality of review was appraised using the AMSTAR2 checklist. The umbrella review was reported adhering to the PRISMA guideline and was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42023455391).ResultsA total of 21 unique meta-analyses investigating ten cancer subtypes were included. All meta-analyses reported inferior survival outcome in cancer patients with pretreatment thrombocytosis, and 18 of them (85.7%) yielded statistically significant results (P < 0.05). Consistent effects were observed across meta-analyses that adopted different cut-off values (i.e. platelet count > 300 or 400 × 109 /L) to define thrombocytosis. Although evidence appraisal did not identify convincing evidence (Class I), the associations of thrombocytosis with inferior overall survival of lung, gastric, colorectal cancer and malignant mesothelioma were classified as highly suggestive evidence (Class II). According to AMSTAR2 ratings, no meta-analysis was identified with high or moderate quality.ConclusionsOur findings consolidated the association between pretreatment thrombocytosis and poor survival outcomes in various cancers. Nonetheless, the absence of convincing associations indicates a need for further large-scale, high-quality evidence to confirm whether platelets can serve as a prognostic predictor or a therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13027-6.Keywords: Thrombocytosis, Cancer, Prognosis, Survival outcomes, Umbrella review

Introduction

Cancer is the second leading cause of death worldwide in 2020, with 19.3 million new cases diagnosed and projections estimating an increase to 28.4 million in 2040 [1, 2]. Despite significant advancements in cancer treatment, challenges such as undetectable occult metastases and recurrences persist [3, 4], necessitating the identification of reliable prognostic markers to predict patient outcomes and guide therapeutic decisions.

Recent studies have explored the role of tumor-platelet interaction in tumorigenesis and progression [5, 6], which involves multiple complex processes including inflammation, angiogenesis and vascular integrity maintenance [6, 7]. Platelets can modulate the tumor microenvironment by releasing cytokines such as VEGF, TGF-β and prostaglandin E2, which can not only diminish NK cell activity but also promote tumor cell survival [8–10]. Activated platelets can extravasate into the tumor microenvironment through focal adhesion kinase (FAK) to fuel tumor growth [11]. Platelets can also bind to the surface of circulating cancer cells, shielding them from the shear stress, TNF-α and recognition of immune cells such as NK and T cells, eventually leading to dissemination to distant tissues [5, 12, 13]. Platelet-related biomarkers, featured by platelet counts (PCs), have emerged as potential predictors of cancer prognosis, which can also be readily accessed from routine blood tests at a relatively low cost [14–16].

Thrombocytosis, defined as an abnormally elevated PC level, has been associated with inferior survival outcome across multiple cancer subtypes [5, 12, 17]. However, variability in diagnostic thresholds (varying from 300 to 450 × 109/L) [18–20] and diverse conclusions from studies with observational designs and small sample sizes have led to inconsistencies [21, 22], and a comprehensive evaluation appraising the breadth, validity and credibility on existing evidence is still lacking [19, 23, 24]. Therefore, we performed an umbrella review to evaluate the existing meta-analyses that captured relatively large amount of evidence by grading the credibility of evidence on the prognostic significance of pretreatment thrombocytosis in cancer patients.

Methods

The umbrella review was reported adhering to the guidelines for Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [25], with the study protocol registered on PROSPERO (CRD42023455391).

Search strategy and selection criteria

We performed a systematic search across Pubmed, Embase and Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews to identify meta-analyses from inception to 8 April 2023, using a structured search strategy featuring medical subject headings (MeSH) and key words including (“thrombocytosis” or “platelet”) AND (“cancer”) AND (“systematic review” or “meta-analysis”). The detailed literature search strategy is presented in the Supplementary Table S1. Two independent reviewers (CS and XW) screened the titles, abstracts and reviewed full texts for eligibility. Any discrepancies were resolved by discussion with another senior investigator (YZ).

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined based on framework including Population, Exposure and Outcome (PEO) [26]. Included studies were systematic reviews with meta-analyses of observational studies evaluating the association between pretreatment thrombocytosis and any cancer survival outcomes, including overall survival (OS), progression free survival (PFS), disease free survival (DFS), cancer specific survival (CSS) and recurrence free survival (RFS). Meta-analyses were included if they assessed specific cancer subtypes or combined multiple cancers in a single analysis. Studies were excluded if they did not report sufficient data for replication of the meta-analysis and further assessment. Network meta-analysis, narrative reviews and conference abstracts were also excluded, as were systematic reviews without meta-analysis. Bibliography of reference of the included studies were manually checked to identify additional eligible studies.

Data extraction

Two independent investigators (CS and XW) extracted the following information from each eligible study: first author, publication year, cancer type, PC cut-off threshold, outcomes definition, age, number of included studies, sample size along with number of outcome events, follow-up time, effect estimates including hazard ratio (HR), relative risk ratio (RR) and odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) and between study heterogeneity (I2). If multiple meta-analyses focused on the same outcome and cancer site (overlapping meta-analyses), the latest one with the greatest number of patients included was utilized in subsequent analysis and evaluation.

For study reporting multiple outcomes (OS, RFS, DFS, etc.), we extracted and evaluated each outcome as a separate unique analysis. Also, considering that the cut-off value of PC to diagnose thrombocytosis varied across different studies, we reported meta-analyses separately based on different cut-off standard (e.g. 300 or 400 × 109/L) for thrombocytosis to minimize potential heterogeneity before further evidence triangulation.

Data analysis

We performed DerSimonian-Laird random-effect meta-analysis to re-generate summary estimates with 95% CI and P-values, in consideration of the inherent heterogeneity across different studies [27]. We extracted study metrics of effect estimates adopted by each eligible meta-analysis including HRs, RRs, and ORs. Considering the relatively low prevalence of events, where evidence had demonstrated that these metrics could be acceptably approximated by one another [28, 29], we visualized these effect estimates in forest plots.

A set of quantitative statistics were utilized to evaluate evidence strength including heterogeneity, prediction intervals (PIs), and tests for risk of bias. The between-study heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic, and an I2 > 50% was deemed as large heterogeneity [30]. The 95% PIs were calculated to assess the impact of uncertainty around the summary estimated effect and between-study variance (Tau) [31, 32]. PIs explain the uncertainty caused by heterogeneity when estimating the distribution of true effect sizes with intervals predicting the effect size of a future study [33]. The small study effect was examined using the Egger’s regression asymmetric test, where a p < 0.10 was indicative of potential small study effect [34]. Possible excess significance was investigated using a X2 test that assessed whether the observed number (O) of statistically significant studies (p < 0.05) was greater than expected (E) [35, 36]. A lenient significance threshold (p < 0.10) was also employed for evaluating excess significance given the limited power [36].

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata (Version 16.0; Stata Corp LP, College Station, TX) and R (Version 4.2.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) software.

Critical appraisal and evidence triangulation

For each study outcome, we categorized the evidence strength according to our previously published criteria comprising five grades: convincing (Class I), highly suggestive (Class II), suggestive (Class III), weak (Class IV) and not significant (NS) [37–41]. In order for an association to be classified as convincing, the meta-analysis needed to meet the following criteria: (1) statistically significant finding with a p < 10–6 [42–45]; (2) encompassing more than 1,000 outcome events; (3) the largest primary study included in the meta-analysis presenting a nominal statistically significant result (p < 0.05); (4) yielding a PI excluding the null value (Effect size = 1); (5) exhibiting no large heterogeneity (I2 < 50%); (6) no indication of small-study effect using the Egger regression asymmetric test (p > 0.10) [34]; (7) no excess significance bias (p > 0.10), which means that the observed number of studies with statistical significance was not greater than expected using a X2 test [35, 36]. The same set of metrics were applied to identify the other three tiers of evidence, and the detailed criteria for each tier are presented in Table 1.

We also appraised the quality of review using the AMSTAR2 (A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews 2) checklist, which consists of 16 items and categorizes the quality into four levels: high, moderate, low and critically low [46]. Findings were compared with results of evidence strength evaluation. The details of each item are presented in the Supplementary Table S8.

Results

Characteristics of included meta-analyses

A total of 1,255 publications were retrieved from the systematic search in the three databases after deduplication. After reviewing titles and abstracts, 35 published articles were deemed eligible for the full-text assessment (Supplementary Table S2). Of them, four publications did not report adequate data for synthesis. The remaining 31 articles with 54 meta-analyses were further screened to identify unique associations (Supplementary Table S3). Finally, 21 unique meta-analyses from 15 publications were retained for subsequent analysis [47–61] (Detailed flow diagram presented in Fig. 1). A total of ten cancer subtypes were included. As for outcomes, 15 meta-analyses reported OS, while the other six reported DFS, PFS and CSS. The essential characteristics of the 21 re-performed unique associations are presented in Table 2.

Summary effect size and amount of evidence

All meta-analyses reported effect estimates that were consistent in direction (Effect size > 1, reported estimates ranging from 1.28 to 3.14), and effects with nominal statistical significance (p < 0.05) were observed in 18 (85.7%) meta-analyses. At a more rigid threshold of p < 10–6 used for umbrella review, the number of statistically significant meta-analyses decreased to nine (42.9%), which comprised of lung, gastric, colorectal, renal, ovary, endometrial cancer and malignant mesothelioma. Six meta-analyses reported over 1,000 events. All but one meta-analyses reported the same direction of effect estimates between the pooled and the largest individual study, where in cervix cancer patients with cut-off value of PC at 300 × 109/L, the summary effect was opposite to that of the largest study (1.51 vs. 0.66).

Heterogeneity, small-study effects and excess significance

Large heterogeneity was observed in ten (47.6%) meta-analyses with an I2 > 50%, involving patients diagnosed with lung, gastric, colorectal, pancreatic, renal, cervical cancer and malignant mesothelioma. Consequently, the PI was calculated for all 21 included meta-analyses, six meta-analyses (28.6%) yielded PIs excluding the null. As for small-study effect, only three (14.3%) meta-analyses on lung, colorectal and renal cancer yielded significant P-values (p < 0.10), showing potential small-study effects. The details of I2 estimates and tests for small-study effect are presented in the Supplementary Table S4.

Greater observed number of significant studies than expected was shown in four meta-analyses (19.0%) covering lung, gastric, pancreatic and renal cancer, while excess significance (p < 0.10) was detected in meta-analyses reporting lung and renal cancer. The details of excess significance test of included meta-analyses are presented in the Supplementary Table S5.

Critical appraisal and evidence triangulation

Eighteen (85.7%) meta-analyses reported statistically significant effects (p < 0.05). Of them, none was identified as convincing level evidence (Class I). The associations between thrombocytosis and overall survival of colorectal, lung, gastric cancer, and malignant mesothelioma were identified as highly suggestive (Class II) evidence. There was no consistent reason why these meta-analyses were downgraded from Class I, though the most common reasons were existence of small-study effect or excess significance bias. One association was graded as suggestive (Class III) evidence and the remaining 13 associations presented weak (Class IV) evidence. The summarized details of evidence grading for each meta-analysis with forest plots are presented in Fig. 2.

With the diagnostic threshold of thrombocytosis set at > 400 × 109/L, the associations with inferior OS for gastric cancer (Effect size = 1.61, 95%CI = 1.35–1.86), colorectal cancer (Effect size = 1.47, 95%CI = 1.36–1.59) and malignant mesothelioma (Effect size = 1.58, 95%CI = 1.36–1.59) were classified as highly suggestive (Class II) evidence. While evidence for lung cancer (Effect size = 1.73, 95%CI = 1.35–2.21) was graded as suggestive (Class III). Other eight associations with inferior OS of renal, ovary, cervical and endometrial cancer patients, and inferior DFS of endometrial cancer, PFS of ovary cancer, CSS of colorectal and renal cancer, showed weak (Class IV) evidence. The classification details were presented in Supplementary Table S6.

As for thrombocytosis diagnosed at 300 × 109/L, however, except for the association between pretreatment thrombocytosis and OS among lung cancer patients (Effect size = 1.47, 95%CI = 1.26–1.70) which was graded as highly suggestive evidence, the other six associations were categorized as weak evidence (inferior OS for esophageal, gastric, colorectal, pancreatic and ovary cancer patients) or not significant (inferior OS for cervical cancer). The classification details were presented in Supplementary Table S7.

Quality assessment

According to the AMSTAR2, none of the reviews achieved high or moderate quality. Four of the 21 (19.0%) meta-analyses were assigned as low, while the remaining 17 were found with critically low quality with major flaws such as failing to provide detailed lists for primary studies excluded from the meta-analysis, no protocol registration or missing disclosure on funding sources, with more details presented in the Supplementary Table S8.

Discussion

This umbrella review including 21 unique meta-analyses identified consistent adverse prognostic effects of thrombocytosis across all cancer sites, with highly suggestive evidence for associations with survival outcomes of lung, gastric, colorectal cancer and malignant mesothelioma. However, we did not identify convincing evidence due to an insufficient mount of evidence, multiple sources of bias, flaws in quality, and discrepant diagnostic thresholds.

Although not listed in current clinical guidelines, our findings underpinned the potential of pretreatment thrombocytosis as an adverse prognostic indicator for cancer patients. We identified 21 unique meta-analyses on various cancer types reporting consistent associations between thrombocytosis and inferior cancer survival, which consolidated previous mechanistic evidence revealing the crosstalk between platelet bioactivities and cancer development [5–13]. In combination with our findings at the population level, it could be promising to explore the effect of PC-lowering therapy on clinical outcomes of cancer patients.

Our umbrella review identified a relatively higher level of evidence for the associations with inferior OS of lung, gastric, colorectal cancer and malignant mesothelioma (Class II). Intriguing, p-selectin, a crucial adhesion molecule mainly expressed on the surface of activated platelets, is more prominently expressed in platelets of lung, gastric and colorectal cancer patients in comparison to that of healthy populations [62–64], which suggested an important role of platelets in development and progression of these particular cancers. In addition, the platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRα) is a key downstream mediator of STXBP4, which serves as a key regulator for driving the growth of lung squamous cell carcinoma [65]. Meanwhile, the high expression of PDGFC, D were significantly associated with poor prognosis in gastric cancer patients, where the PDGFRβ was predominantly expressed in diffuse-type gastric cancer stroma [66]. A recent mendelian randomization study reported that elevated PC had potential causal impact on gastric cancer risk [67], which was also consistent with our findings.

It should be noted that previous observational studies mostly performed blood tests after cancer diagnosis. Therefore, our umbrella review on observational studies could not address temporality of the association as with each meta-analysis. Although aforementioned biological evidence indicated that elevated PC could promote cancer cell proliferation, angiogenesis and metastasis [6, 7], it might possibly be a consequence of cancer development, as tumor cells can induce platelet aggregation and release platelet agonists [16, 68, 69]. Previous mendelian randomization studies reporting causal effects of elevated PC on increased risk of lung and gastric cancer [67, 70] could not rule out the presence of bidirectional causality. However, whether the causal relationship exist between thrombocytosis and cancer prognosis remains to be further investigated.

According to the World Health Organization, the diagnostic threshold for thrombocytosis is PC > 450 × 109/L [71]. However, most of the current studies used 300 or 400 × 109/L as the cut-off value, revealing a lack of standard criteria in clinical applications, which might cause potential between study heterogeneity if a meta-analysis includes primary studies with diverse cut-offs. We re-performed the meta-analysis after harmonizing the cut-off thresholds of each primary observational study. However, previous evidence has demonstrated that dichotomizing variables in continuous scale could lead to information loss and subsequently incur biases [72]. Future studies should further investigate this using continuous scale while exploring both the linear and non-linear relationship underlying the observed association.

Apart from PC, other measurement assessing platelet function, such as mean platelet volume, platelet crit and other platelet-related key molecules may also influence cancer clinical outcomes [14, 73–75]. These measures are mainly associated with hypercoagulation, particularly cancer-related coagulation. However, except for PC, systematic review and meta-analyses on other measures were limited in number with inclusive findings [15, 76], which merits future investigations.

Although the grading system in umbrella review has been applied in a wide spectrum of medical research [40, 41, 64, 77, 78], it can not evaluate the design and reporting deficiencies of included meta-analysis, which necessities further validation and comparison with other systems. We therefore adopted the AMSTAR2 checklist to help comprehensively assess these aspects of included reviews. The AMSTAR2 assessment results of reviews identified low or critically low credibility for current evidence, mostly due to absence of study protocols and lists for excluded primary studies, which impaired the reproducibility of included meta-analyses. Given that the grading system did not identify convincing level evidence, and most of the reviews showed low credibility using AMSTAR2, our findings from the umbrella review, especially these highly suggestive associations, should be interpreted with caution in clinical decision-making.

It is worth noting that the association between pretreatment thrombocytosis and cancer survival outcomes might be affected by potential effect modifiers and confounders. For example, the observed association may vary across different cancer stages and tumor grades [19, 49]. However, although some meta-analyses have extracted adjusted effect estimates of primary studies from multivariable models, none of them has reported the details of adjusted covariates, which might differ among the primary studies in each meta-analysis. Moreover, the categories of subgroup analyses varied across meta-analyses on different cancers, and effect modifications were not formally tested. In considering of the amount of work, we did not examine all the primary studies included by each meta-analysis. Age, tumor size, tumor depth, comorbidity and antiplatelet therapy are potential confounders which might affect both the platelet count and cancer survival outcomes, although the effects varied among studies on different cancer sites [18, 20, 79, 80]. Also, systematically reviewing the influence of effect modifiers and confounders reported by primary studies included by eligible meta-analyses is beyond the scope of an umbrella review [38], and could be prohibitive to be incorporated in our analysis. Based on that, more advanced approaches such as mendelian randomization are merited in the future to overcome the potential confounding effects.

The current study is to our best knowledge the first umbrella review which systematically summarized and assessed the strength of evidence regarding prognostic effect of pretreatment thrombocytosis among various cancers. Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. Firstly, given the nature of umbrella review that synthesizes evidence from published systematic review and meta-analysis, the prognostic value of thrombocytosis in certain cancers with insufficient evidence for systematically reviews are not covered by this umbrella review; Secondly, as with other umbrella review on observational evidence, our study could not elucidate whether the observed association is attributed to causality, reverse causation or confounding factors; Thirdly, the scope of an umbrella review makes it unable to evaluate the effects on the observed associations from potential effect modifiers or confounders. Future well-designed studies with subgroup analysis as well as causal inference, e.g. Mendelian randomization, are needed; Fourthly, the grading system used in this umbrella review needs further validation; Lastly, this umbrella review only evaluated evidence collected from meta-analysis of observational studies published in peer-reviewed journals. Grey literature, conference abstracts, letter and comments were therefore excluded.

Conclusion

Although existing meta-analyses reported consistent associations between pretreatment thrombocytosis and inferior cancer survival outcomes, the strength of evidence is mostly limited by the insufficient amount of evidence, multiple sources of biases and methodological flaws. Highly suggestive evidence was found for the associations with inferior overall survival of patients with lung, gastric, colorectal cancer and malignant mesothelioma. However, considering that all included meta-analyses were of low credibility using AMSTAR2, and convincing evidence was not revealed, more high-quality evidence is needed before elevated PC could become a valid prognostic predictor and therapeutic target to improve cancer survival outcomes.
