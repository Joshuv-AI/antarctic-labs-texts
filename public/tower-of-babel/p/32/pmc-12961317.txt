Determinants of small vulnerable newborn births in two rural community-based cohorts in India: a prospective study
Authors: Sreedevi Kotamreddy, Rajan Srinivasan, Neeraj Sharma, Arun Singh Jadaun, Esther Pulimala, Midhun Sasikumar, Shajin Thankaraj, Sunitha Varghese, Ramachandran Thiruvengadam, Prasanna Samuel, Roshine Mary Koshy, Sarmila Mazumder, Jiji Elizabeth Mathews, Shinjini Bhatnagar, Gagandeep Kang, Venkata Raghava Mohan

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe Small Vulnerable Newborn (SVN) Consortium introduced the concept of SVN, combining preterm, low birth weight (LBW), and small for gestational age (SGA) into a single category. Limited data on the actual SVN burden and its determinants formed the basis of this study, where we estimated its incidence and associated factors in two distinct pregnant women cohorts in India.MethodsThe study followed pregnant women cohorts from Hodal, Haryana, and Lowairpoa, Assam, through their antenatal period until 12 months postpartum to identify adverse maternal and infant outcomes. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Preterm birth, small for gestational age (SGA), and low birth weight (LBW) are significant causes of adverse birth outcomes, such as stillbirth, neonatal death, illness, and long-term health problems. These three conditions are usually studied and managed separately, limiting the effectiveness of interventions. To address this gap, the concept of small vulnerable newborns (SVN), which combines these categories to improve identification, prevention, and care continuum, has been proposed. We searched PubMed using keywords such as “small vulnerable newborn,” “India,” and “risk factors” for studies published until February 2025. While prior research has assessed the prevalence of the individual categories of SVN (LBW, SGA, and preterm), data on the full SVN spectrum and associated factors across distinct geographic and socioeconomic contexts within India are limited.

Added value of this study

This study estimates the incidence and determinants of SVN across two distinct rural regions in India: Hodal (Haryana) and Lowairpoa (Assam). It is also a community-based study, as opposed to typical hospital-based studies. We found that nearly half of all newborns fell into the SVN category, with term-SGA being the most prevalent. Furthermore, preterm-SGA infants had the highest stillbirth rates. Risk factors such as short maternal stature, LBW history, nulliparity, antepartum hemorrhage, and exclusive use of biomass cooking fuel were significantly associated with SVN in both regions. However, we also observed site-specific differences in associated factors, highlighting the role of regional and cultural relevance. These findings offer insight into region-specific vulnerabilities that may not be captured in national-level estimates.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings emphasize the need to contextualize interventions to address the burden of SVN in India. Interventions targeting maternal nutrition, reproductive health, and cleaner household energy use could reduce the risk of SVN. The study findings also suggest the need to incorporate SVN classification into routine surveillance to help identify high-risk neonates more effectively and tailor postnatal care.

Introduction

In recent years, India has made significant progress in reducing child mortality rates. Between 2000 and 2020, the under-five mortality rate decreased from 83.1 to 32.0 per 1000 live births, and the neonatal mortality rate reduced from 38.0 to 20.0 per 1000 live births.1,2 Global initiatives, such as the Every Newborn Action Plan, supported by the World Health Organization (WHO) and United Nations Children's Fund, aim to reduce stillbirth and neonatal mortality rates to twelve or fewer per 1000 total births by the year 2030.3 Despite these advancements and targets, a considerable number of newborns in India continue to experience preventable birth complications, including low birth weight (LBW), preterm birth, and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR)/small for gestational age (SGA), which contribute to child mortality.4

The early growth period, from the fetal stage to two years of age, influences development, productivity, and future health. Poor growth during this critical period is associated with morbidity and developmental delays.5 Newborns who are preterm, SGA, or LBW are not only at higher risk of mortality but are also more likely to develop chronic conditions, such as hypertension, asthma, and obesity, over their lifetime.6 A study from Brazil estimated that individual projections for preterm, LBW, and SGA underestimated the overall burden of newborns at mortality risk by 50%.7 To address these issues, the Small Vulnerable Newborn (SVN) Consortium introduced a single term, SVN, that includes those “(i) who are preterm (born before 37 completed weeks of gestation), (ii) SGA at birth (defined as a birth weight below the 10th percentile of the recommended international, sex-specific birth weight for gestational age standard), or (iii) LBW (less than 2500 g).”8 A global study using data from 23 countries highlighted that 50% of neonatal deaths occurred within the SVN group.9 The Lancet series on SVN also highlighted a knowledge gap regarding SVN rates and the associated factors in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).8,9 In South Asia, studies have reported an increased risk of SVN with maternal undernutrition, bad obstetric history, pre-eclampsia, shortened cervical length, and bacterial vaginosis.10,11 However, the impact of these risk factors varies across geographic and socio-demographic contexts, making it essential to consider regional differences when identifying risk factors.

The objective of this analysis was to determine the incidence of SVN births and identify the maternal, sociodemographic, and environmental factors associated with SVN births in two (primarily) rural cohorts in India. This study will strengthen the existing evidence and provide robust information on the key aspects related to SVN.

Methods

This was a prospective cohort study registered on the Surveillance and Pregnancy/Perinatal Research Platform, conducted in two rural settings situated in the North and Northeast regions of India.12 The study aimed to evaluate the risk factors associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. The site in Haryana had a population of 199,000, located in the Hodal block of Palwal district. In Assam, the study area covered a population of 150,000 in the Lowairpoa Block of Sribhumi District. The primary study facilities were the Sub-Divisional Hospital in Hodal and the Makunda Christian Leprosy and General Hospital in Lowairpoa. This study enrolled 17,821 pregnant women between July 2021 and December 2024. All participants were followed up from the antenatal period through 12 months postpartum, with home and facility visits. The exclusion criteria included participants aged <18 years of age, those unwilling to provide consent, women with chronic hypertension, unconfirmed pregnancies, families not planning to remain in the study area, incomplete forms without participant ID, and other situations such as miscarriage, tuberculosis, cancer, heart disease, or inability to travel.

Enrolled pregnant women were scheduled for four antenatal follow-up visits at enrollment, 20, 28, and 36 (±1) weeks of gestation. Gestational age (GA) was determined using ultrasound scans before 20 weeks of pregnancy, when unavailable, last menstrual period (LMP) dates were used. All pregnancy outcomes, including delivery details and intrapartum events, were collected from either recall or from available medical records with participants. Live-born infants and mothers were assessed at five postpartum visits at 7 days, 28 days, 42 days, 6 months, and 12 months. The study teams conducted active follow-ups at either study facilities or participants’ homes. Outcome data for those who were unavailable for visits were obtained through telephonic interviews.

For this analysis, we included all enrolled women, except those who experienced pregnancy loss (abortion or medical termination), were lost to follow-up, or did not have the necessary data for outcome assessment. The primary outcome for this analysis was SVN, defined as a binary outcome (yes/no), which includes neonates born preterm or SGA, or with LBW. Neonates fulfilling at least one of these criteria were classified as SVN. SVN can be broadly categorized into three main groups, preterm newborns who are small for gestational age (preterm–SGA), preterm newborns who are not small for gestational age (preterm–non-SGA), and term newborns who are small for gestational age (term–SGA).8 Preterm birth was defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation, and SGA was defined as birthweight for GA and sex below the 10th percentile, as per INTERGROWTH-21st standards.13 Low birth weight was defined as birth weight <2500 g.

Participant characteristics included maternal age at enrollment (<20 years, 20–35 years, and >35 years), maternal education (years of schooling), and maternal occupation (gainfully employed or not gainfully employed). Housing conditions were categorised based on the materials used for the walls, roof, and floor. Clean cooking fuel was defined as electricity, solar energy, gasoline, liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), or ethanol, while other fuels included kerosene, coal, charcoal, wood, grass, crop residues, dung, and garbage. Drinking water sources and sanitation facilities were categorised as improved or unimproved using the WHO indicator definitions. Antenatal assessments included anthropometric measurements (weight and height), physiological parameters (Haemoglobin (Hb) and blood pressure), and medical conditions (e.g., infections) during pregnancy were recorded. Parity was defined based on the self-reported number of previous deliveries. Previous obstetric history (e.g., previous abortions, preterm birth, stillbirth, and low birth weight) was recorded as a self-reported binary indicator (yes/no) for each pregnancy reported. Pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH) was defined as the presence of elevated blood pressure, including systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg post 20 weeks of pregnancy, or a clinical diagnosis of gestational hypertension, and women with chronic hypertension were excluded. Moderate to severe anaemia during pregnancy was defined as a Hb levels <10 g/dL, while mild anaemia was defined as 10.0–10.9 g/dL, using values from the earliest recorded antenatal visit. Infections during pregnancy were recorded as a binary variable (yes/no) based on laboratory-confirmed results or clinical diagnosis documented in the medical records during antenatal care. Antepartum hemorrhage (APH) was also recorded as bleeding from the genital tract during pregnancy before delivery. Birth outcomes, including birth weight, sex, delivery location, and any congenital malformation of the baby, were also documented. Potential exposure factors were selected based on previous literature regarding adverse birth outcomes. The study was coordinated by CMC Vellore, and data was collected using the Tangerine application, which was hosted on a central cloud-based server at CMC. For more information about the cohort design and data collection methods, please refer to the publication on the cohort study design.12

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using STATA (version 16, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA), and visualizations were created using ggplot2 package in R (version 4.3.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Separate analyses were conducted for each site and combined data, as shown in the tables. Kappa statistics were used to assess the agreement between ultrasound and LMP data for GA estimation in SVN incidence among women with both data. As the agreement was >80%, women with only LMP-based GA were also included in the analysis (Supplementary Table S1). Continuous variables are described using the mean with standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. We estimated the incidence of SVN and its categories, expressing them as percentages with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Modified Poisson regression was performed for singleton deliveries, and models were created for both sites to explore the association between risk factors and the outcome. Factors with a p-value of 0.2 or less from the bivariate analyses were included in the models, and the significance level was set at p < 0.05. Additionally, models including site as a covariate were performed, with modified Poisson regression for SVN, and multinomial regression for SVN categories. Body mass index (BMI) was excluded from the multivariable analysis as first-trimester BMI data were unavailable for many pregnant women. Additionally, we estimated the proportions of stillbirths among SVN and its categories. SVN incidence was estimated in the overall cohort including multiple gestations (n = 15,616), while SVN subcategories were estimated among those with complete gestational age and birthweight data (n = 14,295), and regression analysis was conducted for singleton births (n = 15,345), with 14,047 newborns included for SVN subcategory analyses (Fig. 1).

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committees of all collaborating institutions, including the Society of Applied Studies (SAS/ERC/ARC Study/2020), the Makunda Christian Leprosy and General Hospital (232/2), and Christian Medical College Vellore (IRB NO: 12375(OBSERVE) dated 20.11.2019). After reading and understanding the participant information sheet, all eligible participants provided their written informed consent. For participants who could not read and write, the study procedures were explained in the presence of a literate witness, and consent was recorded using a thumb impression, with the witness providing a signature.

Role of the funding source

The funders had no involvement in the study design, data collection, analysis and interpretation, report writing, and publication decision.

Results

Between July 2021 and December 2024, 19,929 women were screened, of which 17,821 (89.4%) were enrolled in the study. A total of 15,487 women who had delivered (Hodal: 10,772; Lowairpoa: 4715) and their 15,616 newborns were included in the current analysis based on data availability; exclusions were due to abortions, women who were yet to deliver at the end of follow-up, and missing birth outcome details (including birth weight or gestational age) (Fig. 1). Details of enrollment and delivery outcomes by site, including multiple and singleton births, are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

The mean maternal age for the cohort was 24.7 years (SD 4.4), and the mean GA at enrollment was 19 weeks and 2 days (SD 9 weeks and 5 days). Environmental conditions, such as access to improved drinking water and sanitation facilities and housing conditions, were lower in Lowairpoa and exposure to second-hand smoking was more prevalent in Hodal (Table 1). Term-SGA was the most common SVN category (32.2%, 4602/14,295), while preterm-SGA was the least common (2.9%, 417/14,295). SVN incidence varied between singleton and multiple gestations. Within singleton births, term-SGA was more common and preterm non-SGA was the most common in multiple gestations (Supplementary Table S3a and b).

The multivariable analysis, based on site-specific modified Poisson regression models, showed that factors associated with a higher risk of SVN that were common in both sites included maternal height (<145 cm), LBW history, nulliparity, APH, and exclusive use of biomass cooking fuel (Fig. 3). In Hodal, APH increased the risk of SVN by 29% (adjusted relative risk [aRR] = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.15–1.44), while in Lowairpoa, it was associated with an 88% increase in risk (aRR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.58–2.24). In Hodal, a maternal height of less than 145 cm increased the risk by 30% (aRR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.21–1.39), and in Lowairpoa, the risk increased by 24% (aRR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.12–1.37). Mothers with a history of LBW had an increased risk of SVN of 33% in Hodal (aRR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.25–1.42) and 47% in Lowairpoa (aRR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.23–1.76). Nulliparity was associated with a 31% increased risk of SVN in Hodal (aRR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.23–1.38) and a 39% increased risk in Lowairpoa (aRR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.24–1.55). Exclusive use of biomass fuel increased the risk of SVN by 13% in Hodal (aRR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.06–1.20) and 26% in Lowairpoa (aRR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.09–1.46). In Hodal, additional statistically significant risk factors for SVN included maternal age of 18–19 years, low maternal education (0–5 years), household size greater than six members, fewer than four antenatal care visits, a history of previous preterm birth, and stillbirth, congenital malformations, and deliveries occurring at home, in transit, or at other healthcare facilities, while grand multiparity (≥5) was found to be a protective factor. In Lowairpoa, PIH was associated with an increased risk of SVN (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table S6). The site-adjusted combined analysis and multinomial regression results for SVN categories are presented in Supplementary Tables S7 and S8a and b.

Discussion

In this study, we estimated the incidence of SVN and identified the associated factors in two geographically distinct locations: Hodal, Haryana and Lowairpoa, Assam. Nearly half of the newborns in our study cohorts were classified as SVN, with a significantly higher incidence among multiple gestations compared to singletons. Among the SVN categories, term-SGA was the most prevalent, accounting for 32.2% of vulnerable newborns. This proportion was similar to an earlier Indian study (35.1%) but higher than estimates from neighboring LMICs (27.5%).10,11 We also observed a higher proportion (9.8%) of stillbirths among preterm-SGA infants compared to other SVN subgroups. This rate was higher than the one reported in an Indian study but lower than global SVN estimates of 7% and 16%, respectively.9,11 Our results show that short maternal stature, LBW history, nulliparity, APH, and exclusive use of biomass cooking fuel were significantly associated with SVN at both locations. While some risk factors were consistent across both sites, others were specific to each region. This highlights the influence of socioeconomic and geographical disparities on pregnancy outcomes. While literature specifically focused on SVN is limited,10,11 most of our study's findings were comparable to findings of studies on risk factors for individual adverse neonatal outcomes.

Several maternal factors consistently correlated with an increased risk of SVN across both study sites. Short maternal stature, indicative of chronic malnutrition, was associated with increased risk of SVN. This association may be attributed to limited uterine space, smaller pelvic size, nutritional deficiencies, and obstetric complications, as suggested in published literature.14,15 Similarly, a history of adverse obstetric outcomes, such as LBW and stillbirths, was associated with poor birth outcomes, aligning with the findings of our study.16,17 Nulliparity emerged as an important risk factor, as evidenced by prior studies on SVN conducted in LMICs. This highlights the need for targeted antenatal care for primiparous mothers to prevent SVN.10,11 Among obstetric complications, APH exhibited comparatively strong association with SVN at both sites. Previous studies have linked APH with placental abruption or previa,18 conditions that may contribute to fetal distress and maternal infection and often require emergency cesarean sections.19 Early diagnosis and management of these conditions are essential for improving outcomes. While anemia and infections were significant factors in previous studies,11,20 they did not show statistical significance for SVN analysis, but moderate-severe anemia (Hb < 10 g/dl) was associated with a higher risk for preterm SGA. Exclusive use of biomass fuel was found to be associated with higher SVN risk, consistent with previous studies suggesting that biomass exposure contributes to fetal hypoxia through oxidative stress and inflammation, resulting in LBW, preterm birth, and IUGR.21,22

In the Hodal cohort, several site-specific factors were associated with an increased risk of SVN births. These included maternal age of 18–19 years, low maternal education, and less than four ANC visits, which is consistent with previous studies linking adolescent pregnancy, poor education, and inadequate ANC to adverse birth outcomes.23, 24, 25 Other factors in the Hodal cohort included congenital malformations, likely due to the related increased risk of preterm births,26 and a larger household size (more than six members), which may indicate overcrowding, stress, and resource constraints that can impact pregnancy outcomes.27 Deliveries outside the study facility, especially in other hospitals, showed a higher risk. This may be attributed to the study-initiated referral system services provided at the study facility, ensuring that high risk pregnancies were referred to higher facilities. Interestingly, grand multiparity (≥5) emerged as a protective factor in the Hodal cohort, however, this finding requires further exploration into cultural factors. At Lowairpoa, PIH was independently associated with a statistically significant higher risk of SVN. This finding is consistent with previous research, which has demonstrated an association between PIH and adverse outcomes,28 highlighting the importance of timely detection and management of hypertensive disorders during pregnancy.

The primary strength of this study resides in its larger cohort sizes, which address the heterogeneity between the study cohorts and enhance the generalizability of our findings among similar settings. However, given India's substantial regional diversity in cultural practices, socioeconomic conditions, and health system capacity, the findings are reflective of study settings rather than the whole country. Furthermore, site-specific comparisons facilitate an understanding of how variations in socioeconomic and environmental factors influence outcomes, thereby paving the way for targeted site-specific interventions. However, one of the limitations of the study is the reliance on LMP dates for calculating GA, which could have a bearing on the incidence of SVN. This issue arises from the lack of ultrasound scans for mothers before 20 weeks of gestation and missing data on gestational age and birth weights, which could introduce bias in estimating the outcome. Sources such as the National Family Health survey29 still rely on maternal recall or LMP; it is challenging to categorize preterm and SGA babies, both of which are components of SVNs, without accurate estimates of GA. This situation highlights how the Lancet series9 has also advocated improved health records to maintain accurate gestational age and birth weight data for all infants, including both deceased and live births. Other limitations include the potential for recall bias among mothers when reporting their history, and missing data due to incomplete recordings during study visits. In addition, many of the excluded participants were those who delivered at home, which may have led to selection bias. Another key limitation in our analyses is the exclusion of diabetic status, mainly due to the lack of uniform laboratory testing methods and a significant proportion of missing data. Our findings highlight the role of maternal height as a risk factor for SVN births. Evidence from other studies has suggested that incorporating maternal height into growth standards may improve the accuracy of SGA and LGA classification, as shorter mothers are more likely to have LBW or SGA infants.30 This study provides valuable insights into the factors associated with SVN within the Indian context, adding important region-specific evidence. Geographical differences in risk factors suggest that tailored interventions are necessary to address risk factors by location. Although the use of LPG has substantially increased in the country, sustained efforts are required to raise awareness among pregnant women regarding the health risks associated with exposure to biomass-based fuels. These findings emphasize the importance of the early identification of women with high-risk factors and facilitation of targeted interventions, including early first-trimester ultrasound dating during antenatal care, routine maternal health screening and monitoring (e.g., for hypertension, undernutrition, and infections), clean fuel access to reduce indoor air pollution, improved fetal growth surveillance, and health education on these risk factors to help reduce the burden of SVN. Among the SVN categories, term-SGA is the most common, highlighting the need for long-term monitoring to understand its impact and improve health outcomes. Furthermore, efforts should focus on addressing environmental and socioeconomic disparities among similar settings. Future research should focus on linking SVN status with long-term outcomes, such as neurodevelopmental trajectories, non-communicable disease risks, and economic productivity in adulthood. In addition, studies are needed to develop and validate birthweight charts customized for maternal height. Establishing such life-course evidence is essential to fully understand the implications of being born as an SVN. Efforts are needed to align Civil Registration and Vital Statistics systems and hospital electronic registries with the SVN taxonomy, enabling accurate, real-time surveillance and informing responsive public health strategies.
