Ex‐SPECTing success: Predictors of successful SPECT radiotracer injection during presurgical video‐EEG admissions
Authors: Carmela Cosentino, Said Al Maawali, Thanomporn Wittayacharoenpong, Tracie Tan, Hue Mun Au Yong, Lubna Shakhatreh, Zhibin Chen, Paul Beech, Emma Foster, Terence J. O'Brien, Patrick Kwan, Andrew Neal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo determine predictors of successful ictal single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) injections during Epilepsy Monitoring Unit (EMU) admissions for patients undergoing presurgical evaluation for drug‐resistant focal epilepsy.MethodsIn this retrospective study, consecutive EMU admissions were analyzed at a single center between 2019 and 2021. All seizures that occurred during the admission were reviewed. “Injectable seizures” occurred during hours when the radiotracer was available. EMU‐level data were analyzed to identify factors predictive of an EMU admission with a successful SPECT injection (successful admission). Seizure‐level data were analyzed to identify factors predictive of an injectable seizure receiving a SPECT injection during the ictal phase (successful injection). A multivariate generalized linear model was used to identify predictive variables.Results125 EMU admissions involving 103 patients (median 37 years, IQR 27.0–45.5) were analyzed. 38.8% of seizures that were eligible for SPECT (n = 134) were successfully injected; this represented 17.4% of all seizures (n = 298) that occurred during admission. Unsuccessful admissions were most commonly due to a lack of seizures during EMU‐SPECT (19.3%) or no injectable seizures (62.3%). Successful EMU‐SPECT was associated with baseline seizure frequency >1 per week (95% CI 2.1–3.0, P < 0.001) and focal PET hypometabolism (95% CI 2.0–3.7, P < 0.001). On multivariate analysis, the only factor associated with successful injection was patients being able to indicate they were having a seizure to staff (95% CI 1.0–4.4, P = 0.038).SignificanceCompleting a successful ictal SPECT study remains challenging. A baseline seizure frequency of >1 per week, a PET hypometabolic focus, and a patient's ability to indicate seizure onset were identified as predictors of success. These findings may assist EMUs in optimizing their SPECT protocols, patient selection, and resource allocation.

INTRODUCTION

Approximately, one‐third of people living with epilepsy develop drug‐resistant seizures, which continue despite adequate trials of two or more antiseizure medications (ASMs). 1 , 2 For a subgroup of people with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy, surgical resection of the epileptogenic zone (EZ) offers the highest probability of achieving seizure freedom. 3 Patients who are potential surgical candidates undergo presurgical evaluation with the aim of identifying the EZ. 4 Failure to correctly identify the EZ contributes to the underutilization of epilepsy surgery and unsuccessful outcomes following surgical resection. 5

Multimodality concordance of noninvasive data is an important predictor of surgical success. 6 Single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) is a well‐established imaging modality commonly used to localize regional changes in cerebral blood flow that helps to localize the EZ. Regional cerebral hyperperfusion can be detected by ictal SPECT and used as a surrogate marker for increased neuronal activity in the epileptic network involved at the time the radiotracer reaches the brain. 7 The success of SPECT relies on the injection of a radiotracer immediately after the onset of a seizure to localize the region of transient hyperperfusion. As seizure propagation to other brain regions can occur rapidly, 8 injection latency diminishes the diagnostic yield of regional changes in cerebral blood flow. 9 , 10 , 11 Ictal SPECT has been shown to correctly localize the EZ in up to 71.0% of patients with mesial temporal epilepsy and 83.0% of patients with neocortical epilepsy and is predictive of outcome following epilepsy surgery. 10 , 12 Ictal SPECT is particularly useful in patients with negative or discordant magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings and can aid in the planning of intracranial electrode placement. 13 , 14 , 15 The accuracy of SPECT can be enhanced by utilizing subtraction Ictal SPECT co‐registered to MRI (SISCOM), which involves subtraction of interictal SPECT images from ictal SPECT to improve anatomical localization 6 , 12 , 16 , 17 and is one of the best tools for localizing the EZ in patients with no visible MRI lesion. 18

Despite its established utility in presurgical assessment, ictal SPECT has important practical limitations. Performing an ictal SPECT study requires prolonged video‐EEG monitoring in a dedicated Epilepsy Monitoring Unit (EMU), and constant and vigilant presence of nursing staff, neuroscientists and/or medical personnel to observe the patient and review the EEG in real time to detect the onset of the seizure and to timely administer the radiotracer during hours with tracer availability. 12 In many centers, the radiotracer is only available for certain hours and days of an EMU admission. Additionally, there is a paucity of information available regarding patient or seizure characteristics which may help predict the completion of a successful SPECT study.

Given that ictal SPECT is a resource‐intensive procedure, this study aims to improve the understanding of positive predictors of successful SPECT studies based on seizure, patient, and operational factors.

METHODS

We performed a retrospective study of consecutive patients with focal drug‐resistant epilepsy admitted to the Alfred Hospital (Melbourne, Australia) EMU between 15 October 2018 and 12 July 2021 with the intention of performing an ictal SPECT study as part of a presurgical evaluation (EMU‐SPECT). Patients were excluded from our study if they were diagnosed with functional seizures or generalized epilepsy, or if the data available were incomplete.

Study setting

The Alfred provides a national EMU service, offering approximately 200 admissions each year. Patients are generally referred in by Neurologists, having already undergone thorough outpatient review, and EMU is requested for diagnostic or surgical workup purposes. Patients are admitted to the EMU from Monday to Friday. A team of epilepsy‐trained neurologists, neurophysiology scientists, clinical neuropsychologists, neuropsychiatrists, neurosurgeons, and neuroradiologists, led by a board‐certified Neurologist with specialist Epilepsy training, review all available clinical data at the conclusion of the admission, and provide a consensus management plan at time of discharge. For the majority of patients admitted for presurgical assessment, an ictal SPECT study is planned as part of the admission.

Radiotracer on the ward

The radiotracer, technetium Tc‐99 m Bicisate dihydrochloride (Neurolite®) for ictal SPECT injection is available from Tuesday to Thursday from 9:00 am to 4:00 pm. Neurolite® (and hence SPECT injections) are not available outside these hours. The trolley is prepared 30 minutes prior to Neurolite's expected arrival. Tracer is placed within the Neurolite lead‐lined box and on the Neurolite trolley's shelf next to the patient's bed. There is a 1:2 nurse‐to‐patient ratio, and Neurolite® is readily accessible at the patient bedside to allow for rapid injection into a pre‐sited peripheral intravenous catheter. Within the EMU, a neurophysiology scientist is typically also present during hours with SPECT availability to monitor the patient and EEG for electroclinical seizures. Neurolite® is manually injected by the bedside nurse as soon as possible after the onset of the seizure. After the Neurolite® injection, a saline flush is immediately administered.

Subsequent ictal perfusion imaging is performed within 4 hours of the SPECT injection to identify ictal regional cerebral perfusion. A SPECT injection can be attempted multiple times throughout a patient's admission, provided there are at least 24 hours between injections, until a satisfactory SPECT study is obtained. Where possible, patients are admitted during periods of seizure clusters. Several individualized strategies are implemented to help increase the likelihood of daytime seizures, including temporary down‐titration or cessation of regular ASMs, sleep deprivation, and exercise conducted on a stationary exercise bike. For patients with predominantly nocturnal seizures, sleep reversal is implemented so seizures are most likely to occur when Neurolite® is available for injection.

Clinicodemographic factors

For this study, baseline (pre‐EMU admission) clinicodemographic variables were extracted from electronic medical records. Key features included age, sex, current and past ASMs, International League Against Epilepsy seizure type(s) and frequency, 19 and epilepsy type and location, and presence of MRI epileptogenic lesions and/or focal hypometabolism on fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)‐positron emission tomography (PET), defined as sublobar or lobar hypometabolism based on qualitative review by a neuroradiologist (PB) as part of the EMU multidisciplinary meeting.

EMU factors

Epilepsy Monitoring Unit‐specific provocative measures examined as variables in this study included in‐hospital changes to ASMs (ASMs were not routinely altered prior to admission), sleep deprivation, and reversal of the sleep–wake cycle.

Seizure‐level analysis

All seizures captured during the EMU admission are peer‐reviewed by the Epilepsy Unit's standing team of 11 Epileptologists, with input also provided by approximately 6 Epilepsy Fellows (the number fluctuates due to clinical rotations to external sites). Each individual seizure was reviewed by a single Epilepsy Fellow or Epileptologist. If there was uncertainty, a senior Epileptologist acted as an adjudicating evaluator. Fellows are within 1 year of fulfilling their training requirements for recognition as Epileptologists. For this study, these peer‐reviewed seizures were carefully re‐reviewed by investigators and the timing of seizure onset was documented. Injectable seizures were defined as seizures occurring during hours when Neurolite® was available in the EMU. Seizures falling outside these times were also noted.

For each injectable seizure, additional data that were collected included seizure duration, ILAE seizure type, presence of hyperkinetic semiology (as defined by Fisher et al. 20 ) presence of an unequivocal ictal EEG rhythm during a seizure (defined as a sustained rhythmic discharge, that represents a change from the background rhythm, and evolves over time and space), presence of a bedside witness (health professional vs. family), and whether the patient indicated the onset of the seizure to staff via calling out, gesturing, or pushing a bedside push button device. The push button device offers an opportunity for the patient to indicate the onset of an event in a rapid and nonverbal manner, prior to seizure progression to impaired awareness, or development of paucity of speech or movement. A push button device therefore maximizes the chance for a patient to indicate the presence of a seizure in real time to staff and gives time for injection for the SPECT scan. SPECT injection data that were collected included time from electrographic/clinical onset to injection (whichever occurred first), and clinical phase at injection (aware, impaired awareness or tonic–clonic semiology). Additionally, for each seizure, the optimal electroclinical method for earliest unequivocal seizure detection was retrospectively determined (EEG, clinical, or combined EEG and clinical).

Statistical analysis

The two primary outcomes were (a) successful SPECT injection and (b) successful EMU‐SPECT admission. A successful SPECT injection was defined as the injection of Neurolite® during the ictal phase of a clinical seizure. The ictal phase was defined electroclinically as the period during which there was an electrographic and/or clinical seizure. Once a patient had a successful injection all subsequent seizures were no longer defined as injectable seizures. A successful EMU‐SPECT was defined as an admission with a successful SPECT injection. The reasons for an unsuccessful SPECT injection and EMU‐SPECT were collected.

Data were analyzed to identify patient and admission factors predictive of a successful EMU‐SPECT admission. Seizure‐level data were analyzed to identify factors predictive of a successful SPECT injection. All continuous variables were assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test to determine the normality of variables. If the distribution was normal, the descriptive variables were presented as mean and standard deviation, otherwise, median and interquartile range (IQR) were used. The categorical variables were presented as numbers (n) and percentages.

Binary univariate logistic regression was performed initially. Explanatory factors with a P value of <0.200 were identified. A logistic mixed effects model was then conducted with patient data as a random intercept to create the predictive model for a successful SPECT outcome. The factors selected from univariate analyses (e.g., P < 0.2 were sequentially added to the model, and the level of significance was set at P < 0.05 to find the best‐fitted model for the outcome. SPSS (version 26) (IBM Corp. Released 2019. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0: IBM Corp) was used to conduct the statistical analysis.

RESULTS

Baseline information

Between 15 October 2018 and 12 July 2021, a total of 317 EMU admissions were performed. Of these, 125 of these admissions (involving 105 unique patients) were performed with the intention to complete an ictal SPECT study, all of which were included in our study. The remaining admissions were for diagnostic purposes, for example, distinguishing epileptic from nonepileptic events, and “counting” events in people with known epilepsy. 11 EMU‐SPECT admissions were excluded due to functional seizures, generalized epilepsy, or incomplete data. Of the analyzed patients, 18 (17.5%) were admitted to the EMU twice, and three (2.9%) underwent three admissions. On admission, the mean patient age was 37 years [IQR 27.0–45.5] and 55.3% (N = 58) were male. Their average duration of epilepsy was 17 years [IQR 7.0–29.5], and baseline seizure frequency prior to EMU admission was four per week [IQR 2.0–16.0].

EMU‐SPECT level analysis

A total of 51/125 (40.8%) EMU‐SPECT admissions resulted in a successful SPECT injection (Table 1). When considering all seizures that occurred during admission, 17.4% had successful SPECT injections. On univariate analysis, more than one seizure per week pre‐EMU admission, increased number and proportion of seizures occurring during hours with SPECT availability, and focal FDG‐PET hypometabolism, were associated with successful SPECT admissions. 25.5% of patients with no focal FDG‐PET hypometabolism experienced a successful SPECT. Additionally, out of the 25 patients who experienced less than two seizures per week prior to their EMU‐SPECT admission, six of these patients experienced a successful SPECT (24.0%).

On multivariate analysis, the only seizure‐level predictor of a successful ictal SPECT injection was the patient being able to indicate that a seizure was occurring (95% CI 1.0–4.4, odds ratio = 2.132, P value = 0.038). Out of the 134 injectable seizures, 77 seizures occurred where the patient did not indicate the seizure onset to staff and 24 of these seizures were injected successfully (31.2%).

Eighty‐two injectable seizures (61.1%) were unsuccessfully injected for reasons including seizures unrecognized by staff (51/82, 62.3%), the decision not to inject (16/82, 19.5%), and technical difficulties with the injection process (17/82, 20.7%) (Figure 1B). EMU staff may decide not to inject an otherwise injectable seizure for several reasons, with the most common being that the seizure was nonhabitual, or the patient was in a tonic–clonic phase that was judged to be unsafe for injecting.

DISCUSSION

Ictal SPECT is a valuable tool in the presurgical evaluation of patients with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy. 21 There is a paucity of studies exploring predictors of a successful ictal SPECT study. Previous studies have identified factors that can hinder the completion of a successful SPECT study, including the lack of availability of trained personnel, seizure timing, absence of seizures, and lack of seizure identification. 22 , 23 This study aimed to examine predictive factors associated with a successful ictal SPECT admission. The major findings are that successful EMU‐SPECT admissions are associated with higher baseline seizure frequency and focal FDG‐PET hypometabolism, and successful ictal SPECT injection is associated with the patient's ability to alert witnesses to seizure onset.

In our study, the median time to injection of Neurolite® was 33.5 seconds from seizure onset. This is consistent with reported injection times in the literature, supporting the generalisability of our results. Previous studies have demonstrated that delay in injection timing is one of the primary reasons for the failure of SPECT to localize the EZ. 9 , 11 , 24 , 25 Previous studies have demonstrated that injection times of less than 60 seconds from seizure onset are sufficient for ictal hyperperfusion to be captured in the EZ, 9 , 11 whereas other studies suggest EZ localization is improved when injection occurs within 30 seconds of the seizure onset. 11 , 12 Of note 76.9% (N = 103) and 59.7% (N = 80) of injectable seizures in our cohort were injected within 60 and 30 seconds, respectively.

Admission‐level predictors of successful EMU‐SPECT

Our study demonstrated that patients having more than one seizure per week prior to admission were more likely to complete a successful SPECT admission. Previous studies in the pediatric population have supported this finding, suggesting that there is limited utility for admitting patients to the EMU unit who have seizures less frequently than once a week, even in the context of identifiable provocative factors. 26 Additionally, a recent systematic review found baseline seizure frequency to be associated with the overall success of the EMU admission. 27 However, in our cohort, this relationship was not observed for overall seizure frequency as a continuous variable. This finding may be explained by the notion that patients experiencing high seizure frequency may be diagnosed with specific types of epilepsy that are difficult to inject, that is, sleep‐related hyperkinetic epilepsy, or multiple brief focal aware seizures.

Interestingly, focal FDG‐PET hypometabolism was significantly associated with a successful SPECT admission. Interictal FDG‐PET is also an important functional imaging modality used in the presurgical evaluation of patients with epilepsy. 28 FDG‐PET measures cerebral glucose uptake and is a marker of cerebral metabolism. It provides a dynamic biomarker of dysfunctional brain tissue. Interictal FDG‐PET can identify focal hypometabolism in approximately 60.0% of people with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy and may be concordant with the ictal EEG in approximately 70.0% of people with temporal lobe epilepsy and 50.0% of people with extratemporal lobe epilepsy. 29

There is a well‐established relationship between FDG‐PET hypometabolism and epileptogenicity; the EZ consistently overlaps with hypometabolic tissue. However, FDG‐PET hypometabolism typically extends beyond the EZ, with a gradient being observed to the propagation zone and noninvolved cortex. 30 , 31 In keeping with these findings, electroclinical patterns also show concordance with FDG‐PET, with more focal semiology being associated with more restricted hypometabolism. 30 In our study, we hypothesize that the association, between PET hypometabolism and successful EMU‐SPECT, could be explained by this group of patients having a higher frequency of prolonged seizures involving restricted epileptic networks. These epileptic networks could generate both restricted recurrent seizures focally impairing metabolism and produce semiology conducive to SPECT injection. This hypothesis is further supported by our finding that epileptogenic MRI lesions are not associated with a successful SPECT admission, as MRIs are a static imaging modality that does not reflect the epileptogenic network dysfunction, even if there is an overlap with the EZ. However, this finding needs further validation.

Seizure‐level predictors of successful SPECT injection

Successful injection of Neurolite® was also significantly correlated with a patient indicating the seizure onset to staff. Interestingly, successful EMU‐SPECT was not associated with patients who were documented as experiencing an aura as part of a current or historical seizure type. However, this medical documentation of aura may not necessarily indicate a current seizure type, highlighting the importance of an up‐to‐date semiological history. 32 Additionally, the successful injection was not associated with the bedside presence of a friend or relative during the seizure, reinforcing the difficulty in identifying focal seizures with subtle semiology.

There is limited literature investigating which type of seizures or types of epilepsy are more likely to receive a successful SPECT injection. Our study did not demonstrate any significant association between the type of seizure or epilepsy (lobar localization) and a successful injection. However, nonsignificant trends were observed whereby only 25.0%–30.0% of patients with either exclusively nocturnal seizures, frontal lobe seizures, and/or hyperkinetic seizures achieved successful EMU‐SPECT or injection. A larger study would need to be conducted to determine whether these variables are significantly associated with successful SPECT injections.

Unsuccessful injections and SPECT admissions

More than half of all admissions were considered unsuccessful, with 30.0% of these admissions attributed to seizures occurring during hours when the Neurolite® injection was not available. Given this significant proportion of seizures, extending the hours of radiotracer availability may help increase the likelihood of completing a successful SPECT study, however, this would need to be balanced with both the availability and cost of staff and resources. Additionally, in this single‐center cohort, we have shown that the highest hourly rates of seizures occur during our 9 am–4 pm “SPECT hours,” which questions the yield of extending Neurolite® availability beyond this time.

Performing a SPECT study relies on a cohesive, interdisciplinary team effort, with experienced staff in proximity to a patient's room to identify seizure onset, and personnel familiar with handling and administrating the radioactive materials. 30 , 33 , 34 Therefore, a major limitation of performing ictal SPECT studies centers on the practical ability to inject the radiotracer in a timely fashion. This limitation was observed in our study, where of the seizures that were considered injectable, 37.0% were unrecognized by staff and 7.0% were not injected due to technical reasons including failure of an intravenous cannula or difficulty connecting the syringe to the cannula. In addition, 12.0% of admissions were unsuccessful due to postictal injection of Neurolite®. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated that seizures unnoticed by medical personnel account for a significant proportion of seizures not injected. 33 Although the consistency of trained staff during the COVID‐19 pandemic posed a significant challenge, educating staff on general and patient‐specific signs to identify seizure onset may improve the number of unrecognized seizures. Five percent of injectable seizures were also not injected due to hyperkinetic activity which precluded the ability of staff to efficiently inject Neurolite®. The limitation of manual error and difficulties associated with injection of hyperkinetic seizures may be overcome using an automated injection system, which has demonstrated promising results in recent studies. 35

Limitations

Our study is limited by the modest sample size derived from a single center with specific operational factors and SPECT protocol. However, we do note that many features noted in our study are similar to previous reports, providing some reassurance as to the possible generalizability of our findings. As our study included hospital admissions both prior to and during the SARS‐CoV‐2 (COVID‐19) pandemic, potential factors such as ongoing geographical change in the location of the EMU within the hospital, to accommodate the surging need for COVID‐19 beds, and a reduction in availability of experienced staff on the ward, may have resulted in increased number of unrecognized seizures or failed injections of the radiotracer. During the COVID‐19 pandemic, the presence of bedside family or friends was also limited due to visitation restrictions, reducing the number of personnel available to assist in identifying and alerting staff of seizure onset. A wristband alert button or using automated seizure detection software or self‐injection devices may have been a better standardized way for patients to alert their seizure onset earlier and faster injection. 36 , 37 Additionally, our paper assessed the relationship between a successful ictal SPECT and the effect of either partial or complete withdrawal of ASMs but did not assess ASM load reduction; the significance of the latter variable may be examined in future studies. 38 Furthermore, our study considered all seizures injected during their ictal phase as a successful SPECT study, which may overestimate the number of clinically meaningful SPECT studies which localize the EZ given the known rapid propagation of seizure activity. 11 As such, future studies assessing predictors of a successful SPECT study which provides EZ localizing value and contribution to presurgical evaluation decision‐making, would offer further, valuable clinical information. Moreover, further analysis assessing predictors of injection delay and correlation with an ictal SPECT study that localizes the EZ would also be clinically useful.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, our findings may provide clinical guidance regarding the optimal selection of patients for admission to EMU. This is particularly important in settings with a high demand for specialized epilepsy services. Our study has demonstrated that patients with infrequent seizures, normal FDG‐PET, and inability to indicate the onset of a seizure to witnesses have a lower chance of achieving a successful EMU‐SPECT. Variables including nocturnal seizures, hyperkinetic semiology, a lack of clear ictal rhythm, short seizure duration, and extratemporal lobe epilepsy did not confer a statistically negative likelihood of achieving an ictal SPECT injection and should not deter ictal SPECT attempts. If patients already have concordant multimodality information and have multiple negative predictors of SPECT success, it may be reasonable to forgo additional attempts at ictal SPECT. However, it is well established in patients, where there is no clear EZ hypothesis, and/or a lack of multimodality concordance, that ictal SPECT offers nonredundant information that aids in the localization of the EZ and surgical success.

Performing a SPECT study is costly and labor intensive, requiring multi‐day hospital admissions to an EMU, with high rates of unsuccessful admissions in many centers. Therefore, the selection of patients with preadmission characteristics which may influence the likelihood of a successful SPECT study is important both clinically and from a health‐economic perspective. Further studies with larger sample sizes, across multiple centers will be important to confirm the reproducibility of our results and identify additional predictors of a successful SPECT study. This ultimately may assist in reduced re‐admission rates and facilitate more streamlined presurgical evaluation.
