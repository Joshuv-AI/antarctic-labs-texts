Introduction

Medical image segmentation is a pivotal component of modern clinical workflows, aiming to accurately delineate regions of interest (ROI), such as organs, tumors, or blood vessels, from complex anatomical structures [1, 2]. This technology plays an irreplaceable role in disease diagnosis, surgical planning, radiotherapy localization, and treatment efficacy assessment. In recent years, with the rapid advancement of deep learning techniques, represented by Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), fully supervised segmentation methods have achieved remarkable performance [3]. However, their success largely depends on large-scale, high-quality voxel-level annotated data. First, the annotation process for 3D volumetric data requires professional radiologists to delineate structures slice-by-slice, which is extremely time-consuming and expensive. Second, due to complex lesion morphology and variations in imaging quality, annotations often suffer from subjective inter-observer discrepancies [4]. This “annotation bottleneck” severely restricts the widespread deployment and application of deep learning models in clinical scenarios [5, 6].

To alleviate the heavy reliance on extensive annotated data, Semi-Supervised Learning (SSL) has emerged as an efficient alternative. SSL aims to leverage a small amount of labeled data while mining inherent feature distribution information from abundant unlabeled data [7, 8]. In current SSL research, methods based on Consistency Learning dominate the field [9, 10]. These methods are founded on the “smoothness assumption,” positing that the model’s output predictions for unlabeled data should remain invariant under slight perturbations applied to the input data or model parameters.

Under this paradigm, the Mean Teacher (MT) model [9] has become a cornerstone. It constructs a teacher model via the Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of student model parameters to generate stable pseudo-labels. Subsequent studies have further refined this approach; for instance, UA-MT [11] introduced uncertainty estimation to filter out unreliable pseudo-labels, while SASSNet [12] utilized shape-aware constraints to enhance the geometric rationality of predictions. Despite these successes, existing teacher-student architectures still face two core limitations when processing complex medical images: the problem of Overfitting to Spatial Context and the issue of Confirmation Bias induced by noisy pseudo-labels.

First, regarding the issue of overfitting to spatial context. Existing consistency methods typically employ perturbations such as rotation, scaling, or noise injection, which largely preserve the original spatial structure of the image. As pointed out by TraCoCo [13], anatomical structures in medical images (e.g., CT or MRI scans) possess relatively fixed topological relationships (e.g., the pancreas is always located anterior to the spine). Models trained on such data tend to “memorize” background positional patterns rather than learning the intrinsic features of the foreground anatomy. This leads to poor generalization capabilities when the background layout changes, a limitation further highlighted by recent research like LESS-Net [14]. Therefore, designing a strategy to achieve “spatial decoupling” and force the model to focus on the foreground is an urgent problem to be solved.

Second, regarding the issue of confirmation bias. In the early stages of semi-supervised training, the pseudo-labels generated by the model often contain significant noise. In standard MT architectures, the strong parameter coupling between the teacher and student models promotes their convergence to similar local minima, hindering the ability to correct errors. To address this, the Co-training framework [15] was introduced, which trains two distinctly initialized networks for mutual supervision. Recent works like CC-Net [16] and CPS [17] have demonstrated the potential of co-training using complementary auxiliary models. However, most existing methods directly employ Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss or standard Cross-Entropy loss for supervision, treating all pixels equally. This fails to distinguish high-confidence predictions from ambiguous ones, potentially causing the model to learn from erroneous pseudo-labels and fall into a vicious cycle of “confirmation bias” [18]. Thus, introducing a reliable mutual learning mechanism to effectively filter noise is critical.

To comprehensively address these challenges, we propose a novel framework termed Spatially Decoupled Reliable Mutual Learning (SDRML), as illustrated in Fig. 1. This framework innovatively integrates translation consistency with a co-training architecture to simultaneously resolve the problems of context overfitting and confirmation bias. Specifically, our method comprises three core components: 1) Spatial Decoupling Strategy: Inspired by TraCoCo [13], we utilize translation consistency to break the dependency on background context. By cropping the input volume into two views with different centers but overlapping regions, we force the model to yield consistent predictions in the overlap area. This compels the model to ignore view-dependent background information and focus on translation-invariant foreground features. 2) Tri-Model Co-training with Input Perturbation: We design a collaborative architecture consisting of one main model and two auxiliary models. The auxiliary models receive inputs with added random noise. This strong input perturbation prevents mode collapse and ensures that the main model receives diverse and robust complementary supervision signals [15, 17]. 3) Reliable Mutual Learning Mechanism: To fundamentally resolve confirmation bias, we replace the traditional MSE loss with a Confident Regional Cross-entropy (CRC) loss. By introducing a dynamic confidence threshold, the CRC loss acts as a “noise filter,” utilizing only high-confidence regions for gradient back-propagation. This ensures high-quality knowledge transfer between models and prevents the propagation of errors.

We conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed SDRML framework on three widely used benchmark datasets: the ACDC cardiac segmentation dataset [19], the Left Atrium (LA) MRI dataset [20], and the Pancreas-CT dataset [21]. Experimental results demonstrate that SDRML significantly outperforms state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods, exhibiting superior robustness particularly in low-data regimes (e.g., utilizing only 10% of labeled data).

Related work

Semi-supervised learning (SSL) has witnessed substantial progress in the field of medical image segmentation. Based on the source of supervision signals and training strategies, existing semi-supervised methods can be broadly categorized into three main streams: adversarial learning-based methods, pseudo-labeling and entropy minimization-based methods, and consistency regularization-based methods.

Overview of semi-supervised medical image segmentation

Early semi-supervised segmentation methods were largely inspired by Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). In these approaches, a discriminator network is trained to distinguish whether the input segmentation map originates from the Ground Truth or the segmentation network’s prediction. This adversarial training compels the segmentation network to generate more realistic and anatomically plausible segmentation maps, thereby achieving regularization on unlabeled data [22]. However, the training process of GANs is notoriously unstable and often struggles to reach a Nash equilibrium. Subsequently, methods based on Entropy Minimization and Pseudo-labeling gradually gained prominence. Entropy minimization posits that the decision boundary for unlabeled data should lie in low-density regions of the data distribution, thus encouraging the model to make confident predictions by penalizing high entropy values in the predicted probability distribution [23]. Pseudo-labeling methods utilize a model pre-trained on labeled data to generate hard or soft labels for unlabeled data, which are then used as supervision signals for retraining.

Although these early methods laid the foundation for semi-supervised learning, they often neglect the potential geometric transformation invariance inherent in unlabeled data. Furthermore, pseudo-labels are highly susceptible to being misled by errors in the initial model predictions. In contrast, the proposed SDRML framework not only leverages unlabeled data for consistency constraints but also introduces a reliable mutual learning mechanism. By filtering out unreliable pseudo-labels via a dynamic threshold, SDRML effectively mitigates noise interference during the early stages of training.

Consistency regularization and its evolution

Currently, Consistency Regularization represents the most mainstream and effective paradigm in semi-supervised medical image segmentation. Its core premise is the “smoothness assumption,” which implies that the model’s output predictions should remain invariant when slight perturbations are applied to the input data or model parameters.

The Rise of Mean Teacher Architecture: The Mean Teacher (MT) model proposed by Tarvainen et al. [9] stands as a milestone in this field. The MT framework consists of a student model and a teacher model, where the teacher’s parameters are an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the student’s parameters. Recent studies have further refined this architecture; for instance, the SSMCE framework [24] utilizes multi-model ensembling to enhance the robustness of the teacher.

Uncertainty-based Improvements: To address the issue that pseudo-labels generated by MT may contain noise, Yu et al. [11] proposed UA-MT (Uncertainty-aware Mean Teacher). This method employs Monte Carlo Dropout to perform multiple forward passes for estimating the model’s uncertainty map and filters out high-uncertainty regions. The recent URCA algorithm [3] adopts a similar rationale, utilizing uncertainty-based region clipping to prevent the model from learning erroneous boundary information.

Task-aware Improvements: Beyond uncertainty, researchers have introduced auxiliary tasks to enhance feature representation. For example, SASSNet [12] leverages Signed Distance Maps (SDM) to constrain the geometric shape of segmentation results; DTC [25] proposes dual-task consistency, utilizing geometric information by simultaneously predicting pixel classification maps and level-set regression maps. Recent work such as SemiSAM+ [26] explores new avenues for feature enhancement by integrating Foundation Models.

Despite the numerous improvements in feature representation made by these MT-based methods, most have not addressed the spatial dependency inherent in the input data itself. Moreover, the parameter coupling (EMA) between the teacher and student models in the MT architecture can lead to both converging to the same erroneous local minimum. The proposed SDRML abandons the parameter-coupled EMA mechanism in favor of an independently initialized multi-model co-training architecture, incorporating a confidence-based CRC loss.

Spatial context and decoupling strategies

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) possess powerful context extraction capabilities, which is an advantage in fully supervised learning but can become a double-edged sword in semi-supervised learning. Research by Lai et al. [14] in LESS-Net points out that under limited annotation scenarios, models tend to utilize background context rather than foreground object features for prediction.

To address this issue, data augmentation is widely used to alter input appearance. However, Liu et al. [13] in TraCoCo noted that traditional consistency learning (e.g., rotation, noise addition) largely preserves the original spatial structure and contextual information of the image. To compel the model to focus on the foreground, TraCoCo introduced translation consistency as a spatial decoupling means. By randomly cropping sub-views with different backgrounds but overlapping foregrounds, TraCoCo forces the model to maintain consistency in the overlapping regions, thereby decoupling the dependency between foreground and background. The SDRML framework adopts this core idea of translation consistency but extends it significantly: we integrate spatial decoupling into a multi-model mutual learning architecture.

Co-training and mutual learning mechanisms

Co-training [15] aims to overcome the model coupling issue in MT architectures by training two networks initialized differently to generate pseudo-labels for mutual supervision.

Complementary Consistency and Cross-Supervision: Recently, methods like CC-Net [16] and CPS [17] have emphasized the importance of model structural perturbations, constructing complementary views by introducing different Dropout rates or skip connection configurations. This complementarity allows one model to correct the other’s errors in ambiguous regions. The latest work by Huang et al. [27] further validates the effectiveness of uncertainty awareness in Cross-training.

Confirmation Bias and Reliability: Although co-training introduces view diversity, existing methods still fall short in handling pseudo-label noise. Most methods directly use Cross-Entropy loss or MSE loss for mutual supervision, which easily leads to “Confirmation Bias,” where incorrect predictions are continuously reinforced [18].

Addressing the confirmation bias prevalent in existing co-training methods, the proposed SDRML introduces a reliable mutual learning mechanism, replacing the traditional MSE loss with Confident Regional Cross-entropy (CRC) loss. The CRC loss acts as a strict “gatekeeper,” ensuring that only high-confidence pseudo-labels are used to guide other models, thereby achieving performance significantly superior to existing co-training methods on low-labeled data.

Methodology

In this section, we provide a comprehensive elaboration of the proposed Spatially Decoupled Reliable Mutual Learning (SDRML) framework. This framework is specifically designed to address the pervasive issues of spatial context overfitting and pseudo-label confirmation bias in semi-supervised medical image segmentation. The organization of this section is as follows: Sect. 3.1 provides the problem definition and mathematical notations for semi-supervised segmentation; Sect. 3.2 outlines the overall network architecture of SDRML; Sect. 3.3 details the spatial decoupling strategy based on translation consistency; Sect. 3.4 elucidates the reliable mutual learning mechanism driven by Confident Regional Cross-entropy; finally, Sect. 3.5 presents the overall optimization objectives and loss functions of the model.

Spatial decoupling strategy

Existing medical image segmentation networks are highly prone to overfitting to fixed anatomical positional relationships (e.g., “the left atrium is always located in the center of the image”). To break this spatial dependency, inspired by TraCoCo [13], we design a spatial decoupling strategy based on translation consistency. The core idea is that regardless of how the input view is translated, the model’s prediction for the overlapping regions of anatomical structures should remain consistent.

Significance of decoupling

The key to this strategy lies in addressing the absolute positional bias. Although the intrinsic topological relationships (e.g., the liver is adjacent to the spleen) remain unchanged within the crop, the absolute spatial coordinates of these structures shift significantly between views. By enforcing consistency on the overlapping region where the same anatomical structure appears at different local coordinates (e.g., top-left in v1 vs. bottom-right in v2), SDRML compels the network to ignore view-dependent background cues and absolute positional priors. This fosters the learning of translation-invariant features (texture and shape) rather than memorizing global background layouts, thereby achieving effective “spatial decoupling”.

Reliable mutual learning mechanism

Having decoupled the spatial context, the second core issue we address is “Confirmation Bias” in semi-supervised learning. In standard co-training, if one model outputs an incorrect pseudo-label and uses it to supervise another model, the error gets amplified. To this end, we propose the Reliable Mutual Learning (RML) mechanism, which comprises input noise injection and the Confident Regional Cross-entropy (CRC) loss.

Experiments

To comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed SDRML framework, we conducted extensive experiments on two widely used 3D medical image segmentation benchmark datasets and one 2D cardiac segmentation dataset. This section first introduces the datasets and experimental settings, then presents quantitative comparisons with state-of-the-art (SOTA) semi-supervised methods. Subsequently, we verify the contribution of each core component in SDRML through ablation studies, and finally, we provide a qualitative analysis of the experimental results.

Datasets

We validated our method on the following three public datasets, covering both MRI and CT modalities, as well as different anatomical structures (heart, pancreas):

Left Atrium (LA) Dataset [20]: Sourced from the 2018 Atrial Segmentation Challenge, this dataset contains 100 3D MRI scans with an isotropic resolution of mm.

Pancreas-CT Dataset [29]: Comprising 82 contrast-enhanced 3D CT scans provided by the NIH Clinical Center. This dataset exhibits high anisotropy with an in-plane resolution of and a slice thickness of mm. Following the settings in [11, 25], we clipped voxel values to HU and resampled the data to mm. The dataset was split into 62 samples for training and 20 for testing.

ACDC Dataset (Automated Cardiac Diagnosis Challenge) [19]: To verify the generalizability of our method, we extended our experiments to a 2D segmentation task. This dataset contains 100 cardiac MRI scans involving the segmentation of the left ventricle (LV), right ventricle (RV), and myocardium (Myo).

Implementation details

Network Architecture: For the LA and Pancreas-CT datasets, we employed V-Net [28] as the backbone network; for the 2D ACDC dataset, we used 2D U-Net [30]. All models were trained from scratch without loading pre-trained weights.

Training Settings: The SDRML framework was implemented using PyTorch. Training was conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU (24GB). We used an SGD optimizer with momentum set to 0.9 and weight decay to 0.0005. The initial learning rate was set to 0.01 and decayed using a poly policy with a power of 0.9. The number of training iterations was set to 15,000 for both LA and Pancreas datasets. To construct spatially decoupled views, we randomly sampled two center positions within the original volume, ensuring that the overlapping region was non-empty and not completely identical.

Evaluation Metrics: We employed four standard metrics for quantitative evaluation: Dice Coefficient (Dice), Jaccard Index (Jaccard), Average Surface Distance (ASD), and 95% Hausdorff Distance (95HD). Ablation study of loss functions

To demonstrate the superiority of CRC loss compared to traditional loss functions, we compared different mutual learning loss functions within the SDRML framework, including Mean Squared Error (MSE), KL Divergence, and Standard Cross-Entropy (CE).

As shown in Table 5, using MSE loss provides smooth consistency constraints but lacks selectivity in blurred boundary regions. Standard CE loss introduces class information but forces the model to fit all pseudo-labels (including erroneous ones), resulting in poor 95HD performance. In contrast, CRC loss filters out low-confidence regions via a dynamic threshold, using only reliable regions for supervision. On the LA dataset, CRC brought a 0.61% Dice improvement over MSE and achieved the best 95HD, proving that the “reliable mutual learning” mechanism effectively mitigates confirmation bias.

Discussion

The proposed SDRML framework has demonstrated state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance across multiple challenging medical image segmentation tasks. In this section, we delve into the underlying mechanisms, the advantages over existing methods, and the limitations of our approach.

Spatial decoupling: breaking the “memory effect” of context

While the relatively fixed positions of anatomical structures in medical images provide prior knowledge for segmentation, they also prone the model to shortcut learning. As mentioned in the introduction, models tend to memorize “position” rather than learn “features.” SDRML employs a spatial decoupling strategy that forces the model to maintain consistency across two translated views that share the same foreground but possess different background contexts. Similar to TraCoCo [13], our experimental results (Table 4) confirm that this strategy significantly reduces the 95HD metric. Unlike TraCoCo, which relies on a single model or standard co-training, we integrate this strategy into a tri-model architecture. In the Pancreas-CT experiments, SDRML performed exceptionally well against the extremely difficult abdominal background. This suggests that when the model is compelled to ignore strong positional priors (e.g., “the pancreas is always anterior to the spine”), it instead learns more robust textural features of the pancreas itself.

Reliable mutual learning: a filter for confirmation bias

Confirmation bias is a persistent ailment in semi-supervised learning. Existing methods, such as CC-Net [16] or UA-MT [11], attempt to mitigate this issue through model divergence or uncertainty maps, yet they remain susceptible to pollution from noisy pseudo-labels. SDRML distinguishes itself by combining input-level perturbation (noise injection) with output-level filtering (CRC loss). Input Level: The auxiliary models receive noise-added images. This not only increases task difficulty, forcing the auxiliary models to learn more robust features, but also naturally generates a prediction distribution distinct from the main model (View Difference), which is the theoretical foundation for the effectiveness of co-training [15]. Output Level: The CRC loss acts as a “soft gate.” Unlike MSE, which forcibly pulls two prediction distributions closer, CRC allows models to maintain divergence in “uncertain” regions and only performs mutual learning in “confident” regions. The comparison in Table 5 clearly demonstrates that this strategy is better suited than MSE for handling the blurred boundaries prevalent in medical images, effectively preventing the cascading amplification of erroneous supervision signals.

Computational cost and inference efficiency

Although SDRML requires maintaining three networks (1 Main Model + 2 Auxiliary Models) during the training phase, resulting in GPU memory usage and training time approximately three times that of a standard V-Net, we exclusively use the Main Model during the inference phase. Specifically, during the training phase, the tri-model architecture increases the GPU memory footprint and training time by approximately 3 × compared to a standard V-Net. However, we emphasize that the inference phase solely utilizes the Main Model. Therefore, the deployment cost is identical to a single backbone network (e.g., inference time per scan on an RTX 3090), ensuring zero additional latency for clinical applications. This represents an efficient strategy of “trading training-time computation for inference-time accuracy.”

Limitations and future work

Despite the excellent performance of SDRML, certain limitations remain:

Hyper-parameter Dependency: The threshold τ in the CRC loss requires adjustment based on dataset difficulty. For extremely challenging tasks (e.g., tiny tumors), an excessively high threshold might lead to a scarcity of supervision signals in the early stages of training. Future work could explore adaptive thresholding strategies (e.g., curriculum learning). For instance, strictly filtering pseudo-labels in the early stages and gradually relaxing the threshold as the model becomes more discriminative could further balance noise suppression and supervision abundance.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed the Spatially Decoupled Reliable Mutual Learning (SDRML) framework to address key challenges in semi-supervised 3D medical image segmentation. Through an innovative spatial decoupling strategy, we successfully mitigated the model’s overfitting to background context. By introducing a mutual learning mechanism incorporating CRC loss and noise perturbation, we effectively suppressed pseudo-label noise and confirmation bias. Extensive experiments on the LA, Pancreas, and ACDC datasets demonstrate that SDRML significantly outperforms existing SOTA methods in few-shot settings, providing a new paradigm and a strong benchmark for semi-supervised learning in the field of medical image analysis.
