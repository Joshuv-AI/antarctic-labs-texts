Impact of improved chicken production systems on household income and dietary diversity in Tigray, Ethiopia: Evidence from an endogenous switching regression model
Authors: Berhe Mekonnen Mengistu, Yohannes Tekle Asfaw, Birhanu Hadush Abera, Melaku Berhe, Berhanu Gebremedhin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chicken farming is an important livelihood activity for households in the Tigray Region of Ethiopia, contributing to income generation and access to animal-source foods. However, robust evidence on the welfare impacts of improved chicken production systems remains limited. This study examined the effects of adopting improved chicken production systems on household income and dietary diversity among chicken-producing households. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 1,005 households across 11 districts in Tigray. Data were collected through structured face-to-face interviews, with annual household income from chicken production and Household Dietary Diversity Scores (HDDS) used as the primary outcome indicators. An Endogenous Switching Regression (ESR) model was employed to correct for potential self-selection bias and estimate the welfare effects of adopting improved chicken production systems on income and dietary diversity outcomes. Results indicated that adoption of improved chicken production systems generated substantial economic and nutritional benefits. Adopting households gained, on average, USD 666 annually compared with the income they would have achieved under traditional production systems, while non-adopting households could potentially increase their annual income by USD 710 if they adopted improved systems. The overall average income gain attributable to adoption was estimated at USD 690 per household. Adoption also improved household dietary diversity, increasing HDDS by 0.52 food groups among adopters and by 0.31 food groups among non-adopters under the counterfactual adoption scenario, with an overall average improvement of 0.42 food groups. Adoption of improved chicken production systems was positively associated with younger household heads, higher educational attainment, larger flock sizes, and improved access to veterinary services, whereas female-headed households and households with larger livestock holdings were less likely to adopt. Overall, improved chicken production systems generated substantial economic and nutritional gains, underscoring their potential to strengthen household livelihoods and food security in Tigray. Expanding access to veterinary and extension services, strengthening producer training, and improving access to affordable, quality production inputs are essential to accelerate adoption and maximize these benefits, particularly among resource-constrained households.

Introduction

Livestock production plays a central role in improving rural livelihoods, food security, and income generation in sub-Saharan Africa. Chickens are among the most widely raised food-producing animals globally, with an estimated population of about 26.6 billion in 2022, exceeding that of all other livestock species. This dominance reflects their strategic role in global food systems as one of the fastest-growing livestock subsectors, contributing to food security, poverty reduction, income generation, and improved nutrition through affordable animal-source foods (FAO, 2024a). Chicken production also supports employment and household income, thereby enhancing economic stability and welfare in rural and peri‑urban communities (Birhanu et al., 2023).

In Africa, chickens dominate the poultry sector, representing 97% of the total poultry population in 2022 and contributing approximately 83% of poultry meat and 99.8% of egg production (Guèye, 2024). Smallholder chicken systems are particularly important for low-income households, offering accessible opportunities for income generation, nutritional improvement, and resilience against livelihood shocks, especially among women and youth (Passarelli, et al., 2022). Moreover, chicken production remains one of the most inclusive livestock enterprises in Africa, characterized by low entry barriers and considerable potential for rapid productivity gains through improved management practices.

In Ethiopia, chicken production is nearly universal among rural and peri‑urban households and represents one of the most widespread livestock activities within smallholder farming systems (Alebachew, et al., 2022; Birhanu, Osei-Amponsah, Yeboah Obese and Dessie, 2023). Despite their widespread presence, per capita consumption remains low, averaging 57 eggs and 2.85 kg of chicken meat per person annually (CSA, 2021). Recognizing the strategic importance of poultry, the Ethiopian government has formulated the National Poultry Development Strategy (2022–2031), which aims to increase the share of chicken meat in total meat consumption from approximately 5% in 2020 to 30% by 2030 (Fekadu, et al., 2023a). The strategy emphasizes improving productivity through enhanced genetics, feed systems, veterinary services, disease control, and value-chain development(Fekadu, Esatu, Takele, Tesfaye, Zewdie, Amssalu, Yirgu, Endawek, Shiferaw and Million, 2023a).

In Tigray, chickens hold significant cultural, economic, and social value, serving as important sources of household income, nutrition, and liquid assets for meeting urgent cash needs and fulfilling cultural and religious obligations (Resom and Belay, 2024). Nevertheless, household consumption of chicken products remains low, averaging only 0.3 eggs per person per week (approximately 15.6 eggs per year), reflecting low productivity, weak market integration, and affordability constraints. Dietary patterns, shaped by cultural, economic, and seasonal factors, further limit access to nutrient-dense foods. Religious fasting practices and seasonal fluctuations in food availability reduce the consumption of animal-source foods, including eggs and chicken meat (Haileselassie, et al., 2022; Mesfin, et al., 2024). Consequently, child nutrition remains a major public health concern, with the prevalence of stunting, wasting, and underweight remaining above critical public health thresholds in many parts of the region (Haileselassie, Redae, Berhe, Henry, Nickerson and Mulugeta, 2022; Shibeshi and Asfaw, 2024). These challenges underscore the need to better understand how improved chicken production systems can contribute to enhancing household income and nutrition in Tigray.

Income from chicken and egg sales contributes significantly to household welfare by enabling the purchase of food, healthcare, and education services for household members. Empirical evidence from Ethiopia indicates that poultry contributes approximately 10–30% of total household cash income in smallholder production systems, depending on management intensity and access to production inputs (Alebachew, Ejigu, Adnie and Gebeyehu, 2022; Fekadu, Esatu, Takele, Tesfaye, Zewdie, Amssalu, Yirgu, Endawek, Shiferaw and Million, 2023a). Studies further indicate that households adopting improved chicken production systems generate substantially higher income than those relying on traditional scavenging systems due to increased egg production, improved growth performance, lower mortality, and larger marketable surpluses (Alebachew, Ejigu, Adnie and Gebeyehu, 2022; Bekuma and Tadesse, 2022). Traditional production systems remain characterized by low productivity, with indigenous chickens producing approximately 18–60 eggs per hen per year, whereas improved exotic breeds can produce 200–250 eggs per hen annually under improved management conditions (Alebachew, Ejigu, Adnie and Gebeyehu, 2022; Bekuma and Tadesse, 2022). This productivity differential contributes to higher household income through increased egg output, improved production efficiency, faster growth rates, and greater marketable surpluses. Despite these advantages, traditional production systems continue to make limited contributions to household income and nutrition, underscoring the importance of improved genetics, better feeding, veterinary services, and strengthened extension support (Abebe, et al., 2023; Markos, et al., 2023; MOA, 2023). Recent evidence further suggests that adoption of improved poultry technologies substantially increases household income, although the magnitude of these gains varies according to production systems, agroecological conditions, and access to complementary services (FAO, 2024b; Sariyev and Zeller, 2023). However, most previous studies have relied on descriptive analyses or conventional econometric methods that do not adequately account for self-selection in technology adoption, thereby limiting the accuracy of estimated welfare impacts, particularly in the Tigray Region.

Despite the recognized role of chicken farming in supporting livelihoods, food security, and nutrition, empirical evidence in Ethiopia and across Africa remains fragmented and methodologically limited. Most existing studies focus on traditional scavenging systems, do not adequately differentiate production systems, or rely on descriptive approaches without rigorously estimating the welfare effects of adoption while accounting for selection bias. Consequently, robust quantitative evidence on the differential impacts of improved versus traditional chicken production systems on household income and dietary diversity remains scarce, particularly in the Tigray Region. This limitation is critical because, without rigorous causal evidence, it is difficult to determine whether the observed improvements in household income and nutrition are attributable to technology adoption or to pre-existing household characteristics. Consequently, policymakers and development practitioners may design interventions that are inefficient or poorly targeted, limiting their effectiveness and scalability. Moreover, the lack of disaggregated evidence across production systems limits understanding of how different technological packages affect livelihood and nutrition outcomes under varying socioeconomic and institutional conditions. Addressing this evidence gap is therefore essential for informing policies aligned with Ethiopia’s National Poultry Development Strategy and related initiatives aimed at improving smallholder productivity, dietary diversity, and household resilience. Therefore, this study examined the welfare effects of improved chicken production systems on household income and dietary diversity among smallholder chicken producers in selected zones of the Tigray Region, Ethiopia. Using an Endogenous Switching Regression (ESR) model, the study accounts for potential self-selection bias associated with adoption decisions and provides more robust estimates of the impacts of improved chicken production systems on household welfare outcomes.

Materials and methods

Description of the study areas

A cross-sectional household survey was conducted from February to August 2025 in four administrative zones of the Tigray Region, northern Ethiopia. The study areas were selected to capture variations in improved and traditional chicken production systems across rural, peri‑urban, and urban settings. The study covered 11 districts (woredas), including Axum and Adwa in the Central Zone; Kilte Awulaelo and Gulo Mekeda in the Eastern Zone; Samre and Hintalo in the Southeastern Zone; and the five sub-cities of Mekelle City Administration, namely Hadnet, Adihaki, Semien, Hawelti, and Quiha (Fig. 1). The three rural zones (Central, Eastern, and Southeastern) comprised six districts and 18 Tabias (The smallest administrative units in the Tigray). In addition, Mekelle City Administration was included to capture urban and peri‑urban chicken production systems that are characterized by relatively greater market access, commercialization, and input utilization. Population data and household sampling frames for each study area were obtained from local administrative offices. Households engaged in chicken production under either traditional extensive systems or improved semi-intensive and intensive systems were considered eligible for inclusion in the study. The selected study areas represent major chicken-producing areas with varying production practices, levels of market integration, and access to veterinary and extension services, thereby providing a suitable setting for evaluating the effects of improved chicken production systems on household income and dietary diversity.Fig. 1Map of the study districts in Tigray Region, Ethiopia.Fig 1 dummy alt text

Sampling strategy

A multi-stage sampling approach was employed. Four zones (Fig. 1) were purposively selected based on their chicken population density and production potential. From each zone, two districts were selected, followed by three Tabias from each district based on the same selection criteria to ensure adequate spatial representation. Within each Tabia, chicken-producing households were randomly selected for interviews. Only households actively engaged in chicken production systems were included in the study. Prior to data collection, informed oral consent was obtained from all respondents, who were encouraged to provide accurate and complete information. Households that declined participation were replaced by other eligible neighboring households willing to participate.

Sample size determination

Sample size was determined using probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling, ensuring that the number of sampled households reflected population size of each study area (Alam, et al., 2015). Data collection procedures and protocols

A semi-structured questionnaire was developed to collect household-level data on chicken production systems, income generation, and dietary diversity. It included both closed- and open-ended questions and was initially prepared in English before being translated into interviewee’s native language, Tigrigna. Enumerators recruited from the selected Tabias were trained to administer the survey using Kobo Toolbox. Prior to the main survey, the questionnaire was pre-tested on 5% of the total sample using households not included in the study. The pre-test evaluated question clarity, logical flow, and completion time, and the feedback obtained was used to refine the questionnaire for clarity, consistency, and relevance. The survey captured comprehensive household characteristics, including age, sex, marital status, education, household size, and occupation. Farm-level attributes included collected comprised landholding size, chicken farming experience, flock size, production system, production purpose, chicken age groups, breed type, and market distance. Institutional factors included access to extension and veterinary services, technical consultancy, and distance to markets and veterinary clinics. Economic variables encompassed income from chicken and egg sales, as well as other income sources. In addition, food consumption data were collected to calculate the Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS).

Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS) was computed by summing the number of food groups consumed by the household during the previous 24 hours. The score ranged from 0 to 11 and was treated as a continuous variable. It is expressed mathematically as:HDDS=∑i=111FGiwhere FGirepresents the ithfood group consumed by the household during the recall period and takes a value of 1 if at least one food item from that food group was consumed, and 0 otherwise.

The index includes 11 standard food groups, with higher HDDS values indicating greater dietary diversity (FAO, 2011).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Households located in the selected study areas and engaged in chicken production for at least one year were eligible. All chickens, regardless of age, sex, breed, production purpose, or production system (traditional or improved), were considered.

Exclusion criteria

Households that did not own chickens or had less than one year of chicken production experience were excluded to ensure a reliable assessment of production and income outcomes. Respondents younger than 18 years were also excluded.

Operational definitions

Chicken production systems

The chicken production systems were classified into two categories (traditional and improved systems) based on FAO poultry production guidelines (FAO, 2014, 2023).

The traditional chicken production system (non-adopters)

The traditional chicken production system was defined as an extensive, scavenging-based management system characterized by minimal external inputs. Chickens depend primarily on scavenging for feed, receive little or no supplementary feeding, are commonly kept in rudimentary or shared shelters, and have limited access to veterinary services, vaccination, and extension services. This system is generally associated with low productivity, high mortality, poor flock health, and limited market participation (Fekadu, et al., 2023b; MOA, 2023; Yesuph, et al., 2024).

The improved chicken production system (adopters)

In this study, the improved chicken production system was defined as a semi-intensive or intensive production system in which households implemented an integrated technology package. The package included improved genetic resources (improved breeds, crossbreeds, or exotic strains), supplementary or commercial feeding, improved housing, routine flock health management (vaccination, treatment, parasite control, and biosecurity), and regular access to veterinary and extension services for technical support on poultry husbandry, nutrition, disease prevention, and flock management (Alebachew, Ejigu, Adnie and Gebeyehu, 2022; Chebo, et al., 2022; FAO, 2023; Urgesa, 2023). The technology package could be obtained through government programs, development projects, cooperatives, private suppliers, or farmers’ own investments; however, households were classified based on the implementation of the production package rather than the source of technologies or inputs.

For the empirical analysis, households that reported practicing semi-intensive or intensive production and implementing the integrated technology package at the time of the survey were classified as adopters and assigned a value of 1. Households practicing traditional scavenging-based production without the integrated package were classified as non-adopters and assigned a value of 0. Therefore, adoption status was treated as a binary treatment variable in the Endogenous Switching Regression (ESR) analysis.

Producer

A household member who is responsible for making key decisions related to chicken production and management, typically the household head or farm manager.

Household dietary diversity score (HDDS)

An indicator that measures the number of food groups consumed by household members in the 24 hours preceding the survey (FAO, 2007; Swindale and Bilinsky, 2006).

Net income from chicken production systems

Total annual revenue from chicken and egg sales minus production and marketing costs, excluding household-level home consumption of poultry products.

Household

A group of individuals living together who share meals and resources and make joint consumption and economic decisions.

Exchange rate

Hypotheses

This study evaluated the effects of adopting improved chicken production systems on household income and dietary diversity using an Endogenous Switching Regression (ESR) model. The analysis was based on the hypothesis that adoption of improved chicken production systems leads to significant improvements in household welfare outcomes compared with traditional production systems. Specifically, the following hypotheses were tested:

H1: Adoption of improved chicken production systems increases household net income among adopters.

H2: Non-adopting households would achieve higher net income if they adopted improved chicken production systems.

H3: Adoption of improved chicken production systems increases dietary diversity among adopters.

H4: Non-adopting households would achieve higher dietary diversity if they adopted improved chicken production systems.

Econometric model specification

Endogenous switching regression (ESR) model

This study examined the effects of adopting improved chicken production systems on household net income from chicken production and household dietary diversity, measured using the HDDS. Because households voluntarily chose whether to adopt improved or traditional chicken production systems, the adoption decision was inherently non-random and could be influenced by both observable characteristics (e.g., education, flock size, asset ownership, and access to extension services) and unobservable factors such as managerial ability, entrepreneurial orientation, and risk preferences. Ignoring this non-random self-selection could result in endogeneity and biased estimates of the welfare effects of improved chicken production (Musyoka, et al., 2025).

To address this potential selection bias, an ESR model was employed, jointly estimating the adoption (selection) equation and separate outcome equations for adopters and non-adopters using full-information maximum likelihood (FIML). The first-stage selection equation models the probability of adopting improved chicken production systems as a function of household demographic characteristics, resource endowment, institutional support, market access, and the exclusion restriction. By allowing the error terms of the selection and outcome equations to be correlated, the ESR framework corrects for both observed and unobserved sources of selection bias and provides consistent estimates of both observed and counterfactual outcomes (Diiro, et al., 2018; Omodara, et al., 2025).

Unlike conventional instrumental variable or treatment-effects models, which generally estimate average treatment effects under more restrictive assumptions, the ESR framework estimates regime-specific outcome equations for adopters and non-adopters, thereby capturing heterogeneity in the welfare effects of improved chicken production systems. Likewise, unlike propensity score matching, which controls only for observable characteristics, the ESR framework simultaneously accounts for selection arising from both observable and unobservable factors, making it particularly suitable for evaluating the impacts of chicken technology adoption when adoption decisions are endogenous (Musyoka, Nyarindo, Alders and Isaboke, 2025). Furthermore, the ESR framework enabled estimation of the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT), average treatment effect on the untreated (ATU), and average treatment effect (ATE), thereby providing a comprehensive assessment of both realized and potential welfare gains from adoption.

Exclusion restriction and model identification

Exclusion restriction and model identification

Identification of the ESR model was achieved through an exclusion restriction (instrumental variable) included in the selection equation but excluded from the outcome equations. In this study, distance to the veterinary clinic was used as the exclusion restriction because it influences households' decisions to adopt improved chicken production systems by affecting access to veterinary advice, vaccination services, disease control, and technical information. Households located closer to veterinary clinics are therefore more likely to adopt improved production systems because they face lower transaction costs in accessing essential poultry health and advisory services. Conditional on adoption status and other observed household characteristics, distance to the veterinary clinic is not expected to directly affect household net income or dietary diversity because its primary role is to facilitate access to poultry production technologies and technical services rather than directly influencing household production or food consumption. Consequently, its influence on household welfare operates indirectly through technology adoption, satisfying the exclusion restriction required for identification of the ESR model.

The validity of the identification strategy was assessed using both theoretical and empirical criteria. A falsification test showed that distance to the veterinary clinic was significantly associated with adoption of improved chicken production systems at the 1% significance level but was not significantly associated with flock size or egg production among non-adopting households, supporting the validity of the exclusion restriction. In addition, the Wooldridge (1995) score test failed to reject the null hypothesis that the instrument was uncorrelated with the error term in the outcome equation, indicating exogeneity. The Anderson-canonical correlation test rejected the null hypothesis of under-identification at the 1% significance level, confirming instrument relevance (Baum, et al., 2007). Similarly, the Cragg–Donald Wald chi-square statistic rejected the null hypothesis of weak identification. Collectively, these diagnostic tests demonstrated that the instrument was both relevant and exogenous, thereby supporting the validity of the identification strategy and strengthening the causal interpretation and robustness of the estimated ESR treatment effects. Furthermore, the consistency between the theoretical justification and the empirical diagnostic tests provided additional confidence that the estimated treatment effects were robust to selection bias and were unlikely to be driven by weak or invalid instruments.

The ESR framework has been widely applied to evaluate the welfare effects of agricultural technology adoption, including poultry production systems, where household income and nutritional outcomes are potentially affected by endogenous adoption decisions (Assa, 2012; Omodara, Oluwasola and Oyebanji, 2025).

Assumptions of the ESR model

The ESR model assumes that the error terms in the selection equation and the two regime-specific outcome equations follow a trivariate normal distribution with zero means and covariance matrix:(3)(uiε1iε0i)∼N((000),(1σ1uσ0uσ1uσ120σ0u0σ02))where uiis the error term in the selection equation, and ε1iand ε0iare the error terms for the outcome equations under the improved and traditional chicken production systems, respectively. The variance of the selection error term was normalized to one because the adoption decision is estimated using a probit model. Since a household cannot simultaneously belong to both production regimes, the covariance between ε1iand ε0iis not identified and is therefore not estimated. The covariance terms σu1and σu0capture the correlation between unobserved factors influencing adoption and those affecting the outcome variables under each production regime. A statistically significant covariance indicates the presence of endogenous self-selection, thereby justifying the use of the ESR model instead of conventional regression models (Di Falco, et al., 2011; Lokshin and Sajaia, 2004).

The ATE can be expressed as a weighted average of ATT and ATU:ATE=P(D=1)×ATT+P(D=0)×ATUwhere: P(D=1)= proportion of adopter households, P(D=0)= proportion of non-adopter households.

Conceptual framework of chicken production systems adoption and welfare outcomes

A conceptual framework for the adoption of improved chicken production systems and welfare outcomes illustrates how household decision-making processes are linked to production systems and animal welfare indicators through interactions among socioeconomic, institutional, and biophysical factors. At the core, the adoption of improved chicken production systems is driven by household characteristics (such as age, income, education, labor availability, and gender of the household head), perceived benefits (e.g., income generation and improved nutrition), and access to enabling resources including credit, extension services, quality production inputs, and veterinary support and market opportunities. These adoption decisions then determine the production system implemented (scavenging, semi-intensive, or intensive), which directly influences management practices such as the use of improved breeds, housing, feeding, disease control, vaccination and biosecurity. These management practices improve flock productivity by increasing egg and meat production and reducing mortality, thereby contributing to improved household welfare outcomes, including higher net income from chicken production and greater HDDS. The framework also recognizes feedback loops: improved household welfare can increase household investment in improved chicken production systems, thereby reinforcing adoption and continued use of improved management practices, while poor production performance or limited access to support services may discourage continued adoption or lead to the abandonment of improved production systems. Data management and analysis

Data were collected using Kobo Toolbox and exported to Microsoft Excel for cleaning and coding. For statistical analysis, the cleaned dataset was then imported into Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, were computed to summarize household sociodemographic characteristics, farm attributes, and income sources. Differences between adopter and non-adopter households were assessed using appropriate statistical tests. The Endogenous Switching Regression (ESR) model was estimated to examine the determinants and effects of adopting improved chicken production systems on household net income from chicken production and household dietary diversity measured by the HDDS. Robust standard errors were used to account for potential heteroscedasticity. Statistical significance was assessed at the 1%, 5%, and 10% levels.

Ethics approval

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the College of Health Sciences, Mekelle University (Approval No. MU-IRB2652/2025). Prior to data collection, participants were informed about the objectives and procedures of the study, and informed consent was obtained from all respondents. Participation was voluntary, and respondents were assured of their right to refuse or withdraw from the study at any time without penalty or adverse consequence. The confidentiality and anonymity of all participants were maintained throughout the study, and the collected data was used solely for research purposes.

Diagnostic tests

Several instrumental variable (IV) diagnostic tests were conducted to assess the validity and strength of the exclusion restriction (Supplementary Table S2). The results provide strong evidence supporting the relevance and validity of the instrumental variable. The first-stage F-statistic (14.87; p = 0.0001) and the Sanderson–Windmeijer F-statistic (14.87) both exceed the conventional threshold of 10, indicating that the instrument is sufficiently strong and rejecting the null hypothesis of weak instruments. The Anderson canonical correlation test (Kleibergen–Paap rk LM χ² = 17.71; p < 0.001) rejects the null hypothesis of under-identification, confirming that the model is identified. Furthermore, the Cragg–Donald Wald F-statistic (16.51) exceeds the Stock–Yogo 10% critical value (16.38), while the Kleibergen–Paap rk Wald F-statistic (14.87) provides additional evidence against weak identification. Finally, the Wooldridge robust score test for overidentifying restrictions fails to reject the null hypothesis of instrument exogeneity (p = 0.0773), indicating that the instruments are not significantly correlated with the error term.

Discussion

The descriptive evidence revealed clear differences between improved and traditional chicken production systems, with adopter households generating higher income from live chicken and egg sales. This finding indicates enhanced productivity, market participation, and commercialization, consistent with recent studies showing that improved and crossbred chicken technologies increase production, sales, and poultry-related income in Ethiopia (Ayalew, et al., 2023b; Mesele, 2023). Furthermore, sustained benefits depend on access to complementary inputs, veterinary services, extension support, and markets (Birhanu and Jensen, 2023; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024).

The average household head was approximately 39 years old, indicating that chicken production is primarily managed by economically active individuals, although younger producers were more likely to adopt improved production systems. This finding was consistent with recent studies from Ethiopia, and Ghana and Tanzania showing that younger producers were generally more receptive to innovation and more likely to adopt improved poultry production technologies and management practices (Kabeta, et al., 2024; Ouma, et al., 2023). Differences in marital status, gender, household size, and educational attainment further suggest that adoption is influenced by household characteristics and human capital. Education enhances access to information, extension services, and technical knowledge, thereby promoting the adoption of improved production systems (Jateno, et al., 2023; Mesele, 2023; Tsegaye, et al., 2024).

Asset endowment also differentiates adopters from non-adopters. Households with smaller livestock holdings were more likely to adopt improved chicken production systems, suggesting that resource-constrained households view improved chicken production as a viable livelihood diversification strategy. Compared with other livestock enterprises, improved chicken production requires relatively low capital investment, limited land, and short production cycles while providing rapid returns through the sale of live chickens and eggs. Consequently, it offers an accessible opportunity to increase household income, improve nutrition, and strengthen household resilience against income shocks. This finding is consistent with recent studies from Ethiopia and Tanzania showing that improved poultry production is a low-entry, income-generating enterprise that strengthens the livelihoods and resilience of resource-constrained rural households (Birhanu and Jensen, 2023; Mponji, et al., 2024; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024).

At the production level, improved chicken production systems are characterized by greater specialization, larger flock sizes, uniform flock structures, and stronger market orientation, reflecting producers’ efforts to enhance productivity, improve efficiency, and meet growing market demand. These systems rely on improved management practices, including better housing, feeding, chicken healthcare, and the use of improved breeds and other inputs. In contrast, traditional production systems are predominantly subsistence-oriented, with smaller flocks, mixed-age structures, and multiple objectives focused on household food security, income supplementation, and risk reduction rather than commercial production. The presence of chickens of different ages and production purposes enables households to spread production and disease risks, maintain a continuous supply of eggs and chicken meat for consumption or sale, and cope with resource constraints under low-input conditions. Collectively, these differences reflect contrasting production strategies and commercialization levels: improved production systems emphasize market-oriented production and productivity gains, whereas traditional systems prioritize household resilience and livelihood security (Birhanu and Jensen, 2023; FAO, 2022; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024).

Access to veterinary services, vaccination, and commercial feed was significantly better among adopters, highlighting the importance of complementary inputs in realizing the full benefits of improved chicken production systems. This finding suggests that adoption extends beyond the use of improved chicken breeds; it also requires complementary technologies, improved management practices, institutional support, and market linkages that collectively enhance productivity, improve flock health, increase production efficiency, and support sustained household welfare gains. Without these complementary inputs, the productivity and economic benefits of improved chicken production systems cannot be fully realized, underscoring the need for a holistic approach to technology adoption (Bist, et al., 2024; Mponji, Cao, Wang and Jia, 2024).

The selection of explanatory variables is grounded in the adoption literature, recognizing that household demographics, asset endowments, and institutional factors jointly influence technology adoption and welfare outcomes (Birhanu and Jensen, 2023; Habtewold and Heshmati, 2023; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024). Household variables, including age, gender, education, household size, livestock ownership, landholding, and off-farm income, capture differences in resource endowments, labor availability, and adoption capacity. Institutional factors, such as access to extension services, veterinary services, credit, and markets, facilitate adoption by reducing information asymmetries and transaction costs while improving production outcomes.

The results are largely consistent with these theoretical expectations. Educational attainment is a strong and significant predictor of adoption, indicating that better-educated households are more capable of accessing and applying technical knowledge, thereby reducing uncertainty and facilitating the adoption of improved chicken production technologies (Mponji, Cao, Wang and Jia, 2024; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024). Likewise, household size positively influences adoption, suggesting that greater family labor availability supports labor-intensive activities, including feeding, housing, health management, and disease control, thereby facilitating the successful implementation of improved chicken production systems(Birhanu and Jensen, 2023; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024).

In contrast, TLU is negatively and statistically significantly associated with the adoption of improved chicken production systems, indicating that livestock-rich households have lower incentives to adopt. This relationship likely reflects competition for key resources, such as labor, feed, and housing space, between large livestock and improved chicken production, as well as the tendency of wealthier households to prioritize investment in their existing livestock enterprises. In addition, larger livestock holdings provide alternative sources of income and food security, reducing the relative economic incentives to invest in improved chicken production technologies (Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024). These findings suggest that improved chicken production is more attractive to resource-constrained households, as it represents a livelihood diversification strategy requiring relatively low initial investment. Rather than complementing existing large livestock enterprises, improved chicken production appears to serve as an alternative income-generating activity, particularly for households with limited livestock assets. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies showing that improved poultry production provides a low-entry opportunity to enhance household income, food security, and livelihood resilience among resource-constrained smallholders (Birhanu, et al., 2024, 2023; Werdofa, et al., 2024).

Flock size is positively and significantly associated with the adoption of improved chicken production systems, indicating that producers with larger flocks are more likely to invest in productivity-enhancing technologies. Larger flock sizes enable producers to achieve economies of scale by distributing fixed costs across more birds, thereby improving the profitability of investments in improved breeds, commercial feed, housing, and animal health services. This finding aligns with technology adoption theory, which suggests that producers adopt new technologies when expected returns outweigh associated costs and risks (Birhanu, et al., 2022, 2024). In contrast, households primarily engaged in egg-layer and dual-purpose systems are less likely to adopt improved production systems, possibly because established production practices, adjustment costs, and uncertainty regarding expected benefits reduce incentives for technological change. This highlights the role of existing production structures and previous investment decisions in shaping adoption pathways. Furthermore, access to inputs alone is insufficient for sustained adoption; complementary extension services, training, veterinary support, and technical assistance are essential to strengthen management capacity and maximizing the benefits of improved chicken production systems, particularly among smallholder producers (Birhanu, Kassie and Dessie, 2024; Sariyev and Zeller, 2023; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024).

The Endogenous Switching Regression (ESR) results provide robust evidence that the adoption of improved chicken production systems significantly enhances household income and dietary diversity after accounting for potential self-selection bias. The significant correlation between the selection and outcome equations confirms endogenous selection, indicating that households self-select into adoption based on both observable and unobservable characteristics. These findings confirm the suitability of the ESR approach and support the causal interpretation of the estimated welfare effects.

The income results reveal a nonlinear relationship between age and net income from improved chicken production. Income initially declines with age but increases at higher ages, suggesting that accumulated experience, resource control, and social networks may enhance management efficiency and market participation among older producers. This finding is consistent with recent evidence from Ethiopia and Kenya, which shows that the benefits of farming experience and accumulated assets become more pronounced over time (Birhanu and Jensen, 2023; Kamau, et al., 2024; Tamasgen, et al., 2025). Educational attainment also influences net income outcomes, although the effects vary across education levels. The negative association between primary education and net income under improved chicken production systems suggests that basic schooling alone may not provide sufficient technical capacity to manage input-intensive chicken production effectively. This finding highlights the importance of practical training, extension support, and continuous learning alongside formal education to fully realize the income benefits of adopting improved technologies (Ayalew, Yinnesu, Misganaw, Molla, Birhan, Chanie, Kindie and Amsalu, 2023b; Birhanu, Kassie and Dessie, 2024; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024).

Landholding size positively influences net income under traditional systems by improving access to locally available feed resources, whereas flock size increases net income across both production systems through economies of scale and more efficient utilization of production inputs (Birhanu and Jensen, 2023; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024). Production objectives also shape economic outcomes: dual-purpose systems enhance net income under traditional conditions by supporting risk diversification but may reduce returns under improved systems, where specialization and technical efficiency are more critical. Likewise, chicken farming experience alone does not ensure higher net income, highlighting the need for continuous knowledge upgrading, extension support, and adaptation to improved management practices (Onyemekihian, et al., 2023; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024).

Income from other livestock has a positive effect on net income under improved production systems, suggesting complementarities between livestock assets and improved chicken production. Unlike studies reporting that livestock ownership may discourage poultry technology adoption because of competition for household resources (Sariyev and Zeller, 2023; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024), our findings suggest that livestock assets ease financial constraints and enable investment in improved breeds, commercial feed, housing, and animal health services. This complementarity may enhance production efficiency and strengthen the economic benefits of improved chicken production systems.

The ESR results further demonstrate that adoption of improved chicken production systems contributes to improved household dietary diversity, although its effects vary according to household characteristics and production strategies.

Age shows a nonlinear relationship with HDDS, indicating that dietary diversity initially declines and later improves as households accumulate experience, assets, and management capacity. Gender disparities remain important determinants of nutritional outcomes. Female-headed households exhibit lower dietary diversity under traditional systems and are less likely to adopt improved production technologies, likely because of limited access to productive assets, credit, extension services, veterinary support, and markets, together with greater labor constraints and restricted decision-making opportunities (Ayalew, et al., 2023a; Iizuka, et al., 2025). These findings underscore the need for gender-responsive interventions that enhance women's access to productive resources, technical support, labor-saving technologies, and market opportunities to promote equitable adoption and maximize the economic and nutritional benefits of improved chicken production systems.

Marital status positively influences dietary diversity under traditional systems, potentially reflecting the benefits of shared labor, pooled household resources, and more efficient allocation of income and food resources. In contrast, the mixed effects of educational attainment on HDDS suggest that formal schooling alone may be insufficient to translate production gains into improved nutritional outcomes. Accordingly, integrating human capital development with nutrition-sensitive interventions, including improved access to extension services, market opportunities, and nutrition education, is essential to maximizing the dietary benefits of improved chicken production systems (Balcha, et al., 2025; Jateno, Alemu and Shete, 2023; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024).

Livestock ownership (measured by tropical livestock units, TLU) positively influences HDDS, with stronger effects observed under improved production systems. This finding suggests complementarity between livestock assets, income generation, and access to diverse animal-source foods, indicating that integrated livestock–chicken production systems can enhance household nutrition outcomes (Mseleku, et al., 2024; Passarelli, Abdelmenan, Tewahido, Demmu, Abreham, Ambikapathi, Gunaratna, Berhane and Fawzi, 2022).

Production objectives also influence dietary outcomes. Layer production is negatively associated with HDDS, suggesting that market-oriented specialization may reduce household consumption of chicken products by prioritizing sales over home consumption. In contrast, dual-purpose production under improved systems enhances dietary diversity by supporting both market participation and household access to poultry products. These findings indicate that increased production alone may not automatically translate into improved nutrition unless complemented by nutrition-sensitive interventions, including nutrition education, improved market access, and strategies that promote household consumption of nutrient-rich poultry products (Mseleku, Chimonyo, Slotow and Ngidi, 2024; Passarelli, Abdelmenan, Tewahido, Demmu, Abreham, Ambikapathi, Gunaratna, Berhane and Fawzi, 2022). Furthermore, off-farm income positively influences HDDS by enhancing households' purchasing power and access to a more diverse diet. Conversely, wage labor income is negatively associated with dietary diversity under improved production systems, likely reflecting time constraints, competing livelihood activities, and household expenditure priorities that limit investment in nutritious diets (Gebre, et al., 2023; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024).

The Endogenous Switching Regression (ESR) framework accounts for potential self-selection bias in adoption decisions and enables the estimation of observed and counterfactual outcomes. The significant correlation coefficients (selection terms) confirm endogenous selection, indicating that households self-select into improved chicken production systems based on both observable and unobservable characteristics. This finding supports the suitability of the ESR approach and strengthens confidence in the estimated welfare effects of adoption.

The ESR treatment effects reveal substantial economic welfare gains from adopting improved chicken production systems. Adoption increases annual household net income by USD 666 among adopters (ATT), while non-adopters could gain USD 710 if they adopted (ATU), yielding an overall average treatment effect (ATE) of USD 690. The larger ATU relative to ATT indicates substantial unrealized income potential, likely reflecting persistent constraints to accessing credit, affordable and high-quality production inputs, veterinary services, and extension support. These findings are consistent with recent evidence from Ethiopia, Tanzania, and Nigeria, demonstrating that sustained adoption depends on integrated support, including access to finance, extension services, veterinary care, markets, and producer training (Alderman, et al., 2022; Birhanu and Jensen, 2023; Birhanu, Kassie and Dessie, 2024). Relative to the average annual household income of USD 11,144 (ETB 639,015), the estimated ATE represents approximately 6.2% of total household income. Moreover, the estimated ATE accounts for about 34.3% of average annual chicken income (USD 2,014; ETB 115,482), indicating that the estimated income gains are not only statistically significant but also economically meaningful. These findings are consistent with evidence from Ethiopia, where adoption of crossbred poultry increased egg sales, household income, and flock productivity (Sariyev and Zeller, 2023). Similarly, recent studies from Ethiopia and other sub-Saharan African countries indicate that improved poultry technologies increase household income by enhancing productivity, reducing mortality, and strengthening market participation when complemented by access to quality inputs, veterinary services, extension support, and market linkages (Birhanu and Jensen, 2023; Birhanu, Kassie and Dessie, 2024; Sariyev and Zeller, 2022; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024).

The estimated income gains are economically credible given the production characteristics of the sampled households. Although the mean flock size (395 chickens) is influenced by a relatively small number of commercial producers, the median flock size of 20 chickens indicates that most producers operate relatively small flocks. Nevertheless, adopters earned significantly higher annual chicken income (USD 3,300.61; ETB 189,272) than non-adopters (USD 923.38; ETB 52,951; p < 0.001), indicating that the income gains were driven primarily by higher productivity from improved breeds, better feeding, housing, disease control, and management rather than flock expansion alone. This finding is consistent with recent evidence showing that combining improved chicken breeds with better husbandry, animal health services, extension support, and market access enhances productivity and economic outcomes among smallholder poultry producers (Birhanu, Kassie and Dessie, 2024; Yesuph, Wordofa and Tefera, 2024).

The ESR results further indicate that adoption of improved chicken production systems significantly enhances household dietary diversity. Specifically, adoption increased HDDS by 0.52 food groups among adopters (ATT), while non-adopters could increase their HDDS by 0.31 food groups under adoption (ATU), resulting in an overall average treatment effect (ATE) of 0.42 food groups. These findings are consistent with recent evidence from Ethiopia and other sub-Saharan African countries showing that improved poultry production contributes to better household diets by increasing the availability of animal-source foods and generating additional income for food purchases (Alderman, Gilligan, Leight, Mulford and Tambet, 2022; Birhanu, Kassie and Dessie, 2024; Mseleku, Chimonyo, Slotow and Ngidi, 2024). Although an increase of 0.42–0.52 food groups may appear modest, it is nutritionally meaningful given the low baseline HDDS (3.65 food groups), representing an 11–14% improvement in household dietary diversity. Adoption of improved chicken production systems enhanced dietary outcomes through both direct access to nutrient-rich animal-source foods and indirect income-mediated pathways. Adopters were significantly more likely to consume eggs (30.6% vs. 17.5%; p < 0.001) and chicken meat (14.5% vs. 7.2%; p < 0.001) than traditional producers, while increased poultry income likely improved access to diverse foods, including legumes, fruits, vegetables, and dairy products. These findings align with evidence from East Africa demonstrating that nutrition-sensitive poultry interventions improve egg consumption and dietary quality, particularly when integrated with nutrition education and behavior-change approaches (Anyona, et al., 2023; Omer, et al., 2022; Sekabira, et al., 2022; Wilson, et al., 2022). Together, these results reinforce the agriculture–nutrition pathway, whereby improved chicken production contributes to dietary diversity through enhanced availability of animal-source foods and increased household purchasing power. However, moderate dietary gains indicate that poultry production alone is insufficient to address nutritional deficiencies; complementary interventions, including nutrition education, market access improvement, and promotion of household poultry product consumption, are required to maximize nutritional benefits.

Limitations of the study

Despite the robustness of the Endogenous Switching Regression (ESR) approach, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, cross-sectional design limits the ability to assess changes in adoption behavior and welfare outcomes over time. Therefore, the estimated effects should be interpreted within the assumptions of the ESR framework and as evidence of welfare differences after accounting for selection bias rather than definitive long-term causal effects.

Second, income and production data were collected using a 12-month recall period and may be subject to measurement error and recall bias, particularly for frequent poultry-related transactions. Although such errors are unlikely to differ systematically between adopters and non-adopters, they may introduce random variation in the estimated outcomes. Similarly, HDDS was measured using a 24-hour recall period, which minimizes recall error but may not fully capture seasonal variation or habitual dietary patterns.

Third, although ESR accounts for both observed and unobserved sources of selection bias, its validity depends on the appropriateness of the exclusion restriction and underlying model assumptions. While diagnostic tests supported the relevance and validity of the selected instrument, residual endogeneity arising from unobserved factors cannot be completely ruled out.

Finally, this study focuses on short-term welfare outcomes and does not capture longer-term effects, such as asset accumulation, productivity dynamics, sustained nutritional improvements, or environmental sustainability. Future longitudinal studies are needed to validate these findings, strengthen causal inference, and assess the persistence of benefits associated with improved chicken production systems.

Conclusion and policy implications

This study provides robust causal evidence that the adoption of improved chicken production systems significantly enhances household welfare among smallholder farmers in Tigray, Ethiopia. Employing an Endogenous Switching Regression (ESR) framework to correct for self-selection bias, the results demonstrate substantial and statistically significant improvements in both net household income and dietary diversity attributable to adoption decisions. The ESR estimates revealed strong and economically meaningful treatment effects. The ATT indicates that adopting households earn approximately USD 666 more annually than they would under traditional production systems, confirming substantial realized welfare gains. The ATU suggests that non-adopters could increase their annual income by about USD 710 per year if they adopted improved systems, highlighting considerable unrealized gains and persistent adoption constraints. The ATE of USD 690 per household further confirms that adoption generates substantial aggregate income gains at the population level. All estimated income effects are statistically significant at the 1% level, reinforcing the robustness of the results.

Comparable improvements are observed for nutritional outcomes. Adoption increases household dietary diversity, with the ATT indicating an average gain of 0.52 food groups in the HDDS among adopters. The ATU suggests that non-adopters could increase their HDDS by 0.31 food groups through adoption, while the ATE of 0.42 food groups demonstrates a consistent positive nutritional effect across the study population. These findings indicate that improved chicken production systems contribute not only to higher household incomes but also to better dietary quality and nutritional security.

The findings generate three key policy implications. First, the substantial ATT and ATU estimates indicate that improved chicken production systems generate both realized and unrealized welfare gains. Expanding adoption through integrated chicken development packages, including improved breeds, quality feed, veterinary services, and strengthened extension support, could therefore deliver significant improvements in rural incomes and nutrition, particularly among households facing adoption constraints. Second, adoption barriers are not solely technological but also socioeconomic. The heterogeneity in adoption determinants suggests that extension and support programs should be tailored to household characteristics such as education, age, gender, and resource endowment. Attention should be given to female-headed and resource-constrained households by improving their access to productive assets, affordable credit, extension services, training, and markets. Third, sustained welfare gains require complementary investments beyond technology adoption. Strengthening rural market infrastructure, input supply systems, veterinary service delivery, and producer–buyer linkages is essential to translate productivity gains into sustained income growth and dietary improvements. Overall, the findings demonstrate that improved chicken production systems represent a high-return investment capable of simultaneously improving household income and nutrition. Coordinated investments in technology dissemination, gender-responsive extension services, institutional support, and market development therefore offer a scalable and evidence-based pathway for enhancing rural livelihoods, food security, and nutritional well-being among smallholder farming systems.
