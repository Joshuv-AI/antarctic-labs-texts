An overview of human inflammasomes: Activation and regulation
Authors: Elisabeth Jäger, Sana Ismaeel, Christian Stehlik, Andrea Dorfleutner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe innate immune system has evolved as the primary response mechanism against microbial infections, tissue damage, and cellular stress. Signals that disrupt homeostasis are sensed by pattern recognition receptors and initiate innate immune responses to restore homeostasis. Some intracellular pattern recognition receptors belonging to the Nod-like receptor, AIM2-like receptor, and pyrin family initiate the assembly of a multiprotein signaling platform called inflammasome. Subsequent protease activation enables maturation and secretion of the proinflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β and interleukin-18 and initiation of a proinflammatory cell death, called pyroptosis. Importantly, inflammasome dysregulation is involved in numerous diseases. While many inflammasomes are evolutionary highly conserved, some species have unique sensor proteins and unique regulatory mechanisms. Recently, significant characteristics of human inflammasomes and their regulation have been identified. These exciting insights into human inflammasome biology will be crucial for our efforts to understand human physiology and to develop inflammasome-based treatment strategies. Here, we highlight distinct features of human inflammasomes.

Introduction

The innate immune system comprises several evolutionary conserved and germline-encoded receptors that recognize specific patterns associated with pathogens (called pathogen-associated molecular patterns [PAMPs]) or with changes in cell metabolism and/or cell damage (called damage-associated molecular patterns [DAMPs]). The receptors, thus called pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), are critical for initiating innate immune responses. There are extracellular, membrane-associated, and cytosolic PRRs initiating distinct signaling pathways. Upon detection of PAMPs or DAMPs, certain cytosolic PRRs have been found to assemble into multiprotein structures, called inflammasomes.1 The inflammasome complex typically consists of at least a sensor protein and a proinflammatory executioner caspase, including caspase-1, caspase-4, and caspase-5, in humans. Caspases are cysteine proteases that are synthesized as inactive proform with an N-terminal caspase recruitment domain (CARD) and get activated by autoproteolytic cleavage upon inflammasome complex formation. Subsequently, active proinflammatory caspases rapidly cleave their substrates, including the inactive proforms of the proinflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-18, as well as gasdermin D (GSDMD), to facilitate their maturation. GSDMD consists of an N-terminal pore-forming domain (GSDMD-N), which is released from the C-terminal inhibitory domain upon cleavage. GSDMD-N has a strong affinity for ionic lipids and self-assembles into 20–25 nm-sized pores in the plasma membrane, resulting in the release of mature IL-1β and IL-18 as well as of other small intracellular DAMPs into the extracellular space to initiate inflammation.2 Eventually, the autoinhibited dimer of the transmembrane protein ninjurin-1 is disrupted, and different mechanisms have been proposed for ninjurin-1 oligomerization and plasma membrane rupture, including punching of holes in a “cookie cutter”–like manner, single filament–mediated pushing away of lipids or a double filament zipper-like mechanism.3–6 The subsequent proinflammatory cell death is called pyroptosis,3 and released DAMPs and cytosolic molecules further fuel inflammation. Additionally, pyroptosis eliminates the intracellular replication niche for invading bacteria to help control infection. However, membrane repair by the ESCRT-III (Endosomal Sorting Complex Required for Transport) machinery counteracts plasma membrane rupture and is crucial for cell homeostasis.7 To avoid unwanted inflammasome activation and pyroptosis, there are multiple control mechanisms, including gene expression, post-translational modification, proteasomal degradation, and subcellular localization of inflammasome components as well as crucial protein-protein interactions, highlighting the importance of timely and controlled activation.8,9

Broadly, inflammasomes are categorized into canonical and noncanonical inflammasomes, which we describe in detail subsequently. While inflammasomes have many evolutionarily conserved functions among species, we are only starting to understand some of the complex differences, not only inflammasomes in different species and cell types, but also unique differences in their regulation. In this review, we want to highlight some of the distinctive features of human inflammasomes and their regulation, which set them apart from other species, especially from mouse, which has been extensively utilized as a model organism to study inflammasome-associated mechanisms and diseases.

Canonical inflammasomes

Canonical inflammasomes exhibit a conserved signaling mechanism and usually consist of a sensor, an adapter, and the proinflammatory caspase-1 as executioner. Intracellular sensors include members of the NOD-like receptor (NLR) and AIM2-like receptor (ALR) families as well as pyrin and CARD8 and detect a structurally diverse set of PAMPs and DAMPs.1 The sensor molecules typically consist of 3 key components: a sensing domain, which detects PAMPs and DAMPs; an oligomerization domain to initiate inflammasome complex formation; and an effector domain for the recruitment of other crucial inflammasome components. For NLRs, the C-terminal leucine-rich repeat domain (LRR) is responsible for sensing DAMPs and PAMPs. The central NACHT (NAIP, CIITA, HET-E, and TP1) domain including the nucleotide binding domain with ATPase function is responsible for ATP binding and initiation of oligomerization. A linker region connects the NACHT to the N-terminal pyrin domain (PYD) in NLRPs or CARD in NLRCs and the PYD- and CARD-containing adaptor protein apoptosis-associated speck-like protein (ASC) is recruited via homotypic domain interactions. Because ASC can recruit caspases via CARD-CARD interactions, it is able to connect the inflammasome sensor with proinflammatory caspases. Canonical inflammasome activation usually happens in 2 steps1: a priming step, which is mediated by TLR-triggered nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling, preparing cell responses by upregulating gene expression and inducing post-translational modifications of inflammasome components2; and an activation step, which triggers inflammasome assembly, activation, and consequently inflammation.1 Depending on the nature of the PAMP or DAMP, specific PRRs determine which inflammasome is activated. A wide variety of pathogenic components, including DNA, double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), flagellin, type III secretion system proteins, and toxins as well as danger-associated perturbations like ion flux, (nano)particles, crystalline structures, and cell death–associated molecules such as heme trigger canonical inflammasome activation.1 Even though humans evolved multiple mechanisms to tightly control inflammasome activation, inflammasome dysregulation and dysfunction have been linked to numerous diseases.1

NLRP3: An inflammasome sensor for cellular stress

The NLRP3 domain organization is typical for members of the NLRP protein family (PYD-NACHT-LRR) and is highly conserved across species, with over 80% identity and almost 90% similarity between human and mouse NLRP3. While human and mouse NLRP3 both form a double-ring cage structure, human NLRP3 assembles a decamer, whereas mouse NLRP3 assembles a dodecamer.10 Contrary to other inflammasome sensors, NLRP3 is activated by a wide variety of PAMPs and DAMPs, including crystalline substances such as monosodium urate, silica, or cholesterol crystals as well as ATP, cytosolic dsDNA, and others.11 The presence of these PAMPs and DAMPs is concomitant with a disturbance in homeostasis or extracellular danger converging into cellular events like potassium efflux, mitochondrial or lysosomal damage-associated reactive oxygen species, PI4P accumulation during trans-Golgi network disassembly or disrupted endosome trafficking, oxidized mitochondrial DNA, or calcium signaling.12–15 Importantly, while human NLRP3 senses bacteria-derived messenger RNA (mRNA), transfer RNA, and ribosomal RNA (rRNA), the mouse NLRP3 inflammasome only responds to bacterial mRNA.16 Because mRNA-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome activation is triggered by the absence of 3′ polyadenylation in bacterial mRNA, the murine and human sensing mechanism may differently distinguish structural differences or the presence or absence of unique nucleic acid modifications among different bacterial RNA components.17 A unique coreceptor or other cofactors may also be required for rRNAs and transfer RNA-triggered inflammasome responses in human macrophages.18 Recently, two structurally very similar proteins, NLRP11 and NLRP12, were associated with the regulation of human NLRP3 activation. While NLRP11 is needed to facilitate full activation of the human NLRP3 inflammasome (Fig. 1),19 NLRP12 is involved in its negative regulation in humans but not in mice.20 Additionally, human but not mouse monocytes can activate an IL-1β-processing NLRP3 inflammasome in response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) through an alternative activation pathway including caspase-8 signaling upstream of NLRP3.21 To better identify differences between human and mouse NLRP3 inflammasome regulation, mice engineered to express NLRP3 with the human or mouse 5′ regulatory promoter region revealed that LPS strongly induces NLRP3 expression from the mouse but not from the human NLRP3 promoter, concomitant with much more IL-1β release in response to LPS/ATP. Interestingly, expression of the D305N gain of function NLRP3 mutant results in chronic inflammation with both promoters but causes aseptic meningitis only with the human promoter.22 Hence, considering the different transcriptional regulation of NLRP3 between species seems to be important for assessing its role in autoinflammatory and autoimmune diseases. In addition, stochastic splicing of human but not mouse NLRP3 results in an isoform lacking the NEK7 binding site, which renders it inactive.23 However, a NEK7-independent pathway requiring IKKβ-mediated recruitment of NLRP3 to PI4P during priming has also been described for human NLRP3 inflammasome activation.24 Although the NLRP3 inflammasome is the most extensively studied inflammasome, most research findings are assumed to translate from mouse to human based on its conservation across species. However, recent studies highlight impactful differences between mouse and human NLRP3 expression and its role in human disease.

NLRP11: An emerging immune regulator

While NLRP11 is absent in mice, it features a similar domain structure as NLRP3 (PYD-NACHT-12x LRRs) and is expressed in immune tissues and reproductive organs in primates.25 Increased NLRP11 expression occurs after stimulation of the type I interferon (IFN-I) pathway,26 NF-κB pathway,27,28 and adenosine receptor signaling in B cells.29 The function of NLRP11 in inflammasome signaling is still controversial. The initial characterization using ectopic expression revealed recruitment of NLRP11 to ASC aggregates30 and NLRP11 also interacts with both ASC and NLRP3 in macrophages to promote NLRP3 inflammasome activation (Fig. 1). In the absence of NLRP11, there is no NLRP3-mediated caspase-1 activation and no IL-1β release. While all NLRP11 domains are necessary to facilitate NLRP3 inflammasome activation, only the NACHT and LRRs are necessary for NLRP3 binding and its oligomerization and the PYD is necessary for ASC binding followed by pro–caspase-1 recruitment.19 However, another study failed to detect NLRP11 recruitment to ASC or any effects on caspase-1 activation.28 On the other hand, the LRR of NLRP11 also interacts with DDX3X, a regulatory protein of the DEAD-box protein family, which results in negative regulation of nigericin-induced inflammasome activation as well as IFNβ production by interfering with IKKε.31 Further, NLRP11 has also been identified as an LPS sensor upstream of the noncanonical inflammasome (discussed subsequently).32 These contradictory observations may be due to experimental and cell type differences, and more studies are necessary to unravel the physiological function of NLRP11 in human inflammasomes.

NLRP1: A protease-activated inflammasome sensor

NLRP1 is the first identified inflammasome sensor,33 and its mechanism and function have been further investigated.34 Human NLRP1 encodes a PYD and a NACHT connected by a disorganized linker region, followed by 6 LRRs, a FIIND (function-to-find-domain), and a CARD. Seven transcript variants have been detected. Although NLRP1 orthologs are found across mammals, the presence of PYD, CARD, and the number of LRR repeats varies. For instance, all 3 mouse Nlrp1 paralogs are missing the PYD.25 Human NLRP1 is mainly expressed in barrier tissues, such as skin, lung, and immune organs and gene mutations are frequently associated with skin disease.35 However, mouse keratinocytes lack NLRP1b expression and consequently NLRP1 inflammasome responses.36 The FIIND domain consists of 2 subdomains, ZU5 and UPA, which undergo autoproteolysis but remain noncovalently associated in an inactive state. Only after proteasomal degradation of the N-terminal domain will the C-terminal UPA-CARD be released for inflammasome assembly (Fig. 1).37 Binding of the dipeptidyl peptidases DPP8/9 to the autoproteolytic site in the FIIND restricts NLRP1 inflammasome activation, but contrary to mice this interaction depends on NLRP1 autoproteolysis in humans to generate the necessary binding interface.38,39 Interestingly, DPP8/9 do not directly cleave NLRP1. Nevertheless, the DPP9 inhibitor Vbp (Val-boro-pro) disrupts the interaction between DPP9 and NLRP1, which accelerates degradation of the N-terminal fragment and UPA-CARD–mediated inflammasome assembly and activation.40,41 Furthermore, human NLRP1 but not mouse NLRP1b inflammasome activation is ASC-dependent.42,43 Although proteolytic cleavage of mouse NLRP1b by lethal factor of Bacillus anthracis lethal toxin was its first identified physiological trigger, lethal factor will not trigger human NLRP1 inflammasome activation. Instead, a diverse range of viral polyprotein 3C proteases induce NLRP1 inflammasome activation in humans.44,45 Thus, NLRP1 is mainly a guard-type sensor, detecting pathogen protease activity instead of binding a PAMP. However, NLRP1 also directly binds long dsRNA of RNA viruses.46 Furthermore, UVB radiation and ribotoxic stress induce ZAKα/p38-mediated hyperphosphorylation of a human-specific disordered linker region and thereby NLRP1 activation in keratinocytes.47–49 In addition, NLRP1 is sensing the cellular redox state of the cell by binding oxidated TRX1, which suppresses NLRP1 activation.50,51 Although the general activation mechanism is similar between mouse and humans, the initial triggers vary with a broader spectrum of stimuli detected by human NLRP1.

CARD8: A truncated NLRP1 inflammasome sensor

CARD8 has no ortholog in rodents and resembles an N-terminally truncated NLRP1, consisting only of the ZU5 and UPA containing FIIND, the CARD, and a short, disordered N-terminal region. CARD8 is expressed in most myeloid cells and is the highest expressed inflammasome sensor in endothelial cells.52 The autoproteolytic activity of the FIIND generates 2 noncovalently linked and therefore inactive fragments: the repressive N-terminal and the inflammasome forming C-terminal fragment. DPP8/9 also interact with CARD8 to prevent N-terminal degradation and inflammasome activation.37,39,53,54 Contrary to NLRP1, the C-terminal fragment of CARD8 does not bind to the active site of DPP9, and therefore the DPP9 inhibitor VbP will not prevent DPP9 interaction with CARD8.53 However, DPP9 inhibitors still activate the CARD8 inflammasome, indicating that the enzymatic activity rather than the interaction prevents inflammasome activation (Fig. 1).39,53 Another small molecule inhibitor, CQ31, inhibits DPP8/9 activity without disrupting the NLRP1-DPP8/9 interaction and will therefore specifically only activate the CARD8 inflammasome.55 The N-terminal fragment of CARD8 controls inflammasome activation by ubiquitin-independent 20S proteasome-mediated degradation of the disordered region and further degradation of the remaining repressive N-terminal fragment in response to cell stress, such as DPP8/9 inhibition, to release the active C-terminal fragment.56,57 Furthermore, viral proteases from coxsackie virus and HIV cleave the N-terminal–disordered region and activate the CARD8 inflammasome also in a proteasome-dependent manner.52,58 Although the CARD8 C-terminus shares structural features with NLRP1, there are distinct differences in the inflammasome complex formation. While NLRP1 recruits ASC, CARD8 directly binds to the caspase-1-CARD and its inflammasome activation is ASC-independent.59 In addition to its function as an inflammasome sensor, CARD8 has also been proposed to inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation.60 The FIIND of CARD8 interacts with NLRP3 and thereby blocks speck formation. However, cryopyrin-associated periodic syndrome (CAPS)–associated NLRP3 mutations prevent binding of CARD8 and therefore escape this regulatory mechanism.60 Interestingly, it was shown that the CARD8 inflammasome can also be activated by DPP9 inhibition in naïve CD4 and CD8 T cells, resulting in pyroptosis, but not IL-1β production, showing that the CARD8 inflammasome is also functional in nonmyeloid cells.61 The mechanism of CARD8 inflammasome activation has common but also unique features compared with NLRP1, and CARD8 might have evolved in humans to provide additional control over inflammasome activation.

NAIP/NLRC4: The needle-sensing inflammasome

NAIP and NLRC4 encode a NACHT and an LRR, but only NLRC4 encodes an N-terminal CARD for inflammasome assembly. Although direct CARD-CARD interactions between NLRC4 and caspase-1 have been demonstrated by cryo-EM, ASC is nevertheless needed for human NLRC4 inflammasome assembly and IL-1β release (Fig. 1).62 Activation of the NAIP/NLRC4 inflammasome occurs in human and mouse cells in response to infection with flagellated Gram-negative bacteria, including Salmonella, Pseudomonas, or Legionella, to restrict bacterial replication through rapid cell death of infected cells.63–67 While human NAIP (hNAIP) functions as the only known sensor for the human NLRC4 inflammasome, mice interestingly encode several functional Naip genes with distinct ligand recognition and expression in different tissues and cell types.66 In particular, the bacterial virulence factors type III secretion system (T3SS) needle, inner rod, and flagellin are sensed by NAIP1, NAIP2, and NAIP5/6, respectively, but in humans the sole hNAIP senses all these various ligands.65,68,69 However, the circumstances for ligand specificity of hNAIP are still poorly understood. While the T3SS needle protein robustly activates hNAIP/NLRC4,70,71 the T3SS inner rod and intracellular flagellin of Salmonella, which are both robustly sensed by mouse macrophages, may fail to activate the human NLRC4 inflammasome.70,72,73 Electron microscopy, structural analysis and biochemical studies confirmed that the needle protein is the only high affinity hNAIP ligand to trigger hNAIP/NLRC4 inflammasome assembly and activation.74 In humans, needle-, inner rod–, and flagellin-mediated NLRC4 inflammasome activation might therefore depend on hNAIP and NLRC4 expression levels and possibly expression of other cofactors, priming and differentiation of monocytes, as well as on differences between specific bacterial strains. Assembly of the hNAIP/NLRC4 inflammasome occurs through a lock and key mechanism after pathogen sensing by hNAIP. Needle binding to hNAIP, which does not encode a lock motif, alters its closed confirmation to expose the hNAIP key for interaction with the lock of closed hNLRC4, which also encodes a key motif. Subsequently, a conformational change of hNLRC4 exposes its key for further interaction with the lock of additional closed hNLRC4 molecules to promote hNLRC4 polymerization and inflammasome assembly. This also explains why hNLRC4 is present at a higher ratio than hNAIP and needle in the NLRC4 inflammasome complex.74

In human macrophages, Salmonella infection activates not only the hNAIP/NLRC4 inflammasomes, but also the NLRP3 and caspase-4/caspase-5 inflammasomes, and the hNAIP/NLRC4 and NLRP3 inflammasomes restrict Salmonella replication.67,71 The Salmonella pathogenicity island 1 (SPI-1) and SPI-2 encode 2 distinct T3SS. While human and mouse NLRC4 inflammasomes are activated by SPI-1–encoded flagellin , rod , and needle proteins, only hNAIP also detects the SPI-2–encoded needle and restricts intracellular Salmonella even in the absence of SPI-1 T3SS.67 Interestingly, human intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) express NAIP/NLRC4 at a much lower level than myeloid cells. Thus, in human IECs the SPI-1 T3SS triggers caspase-4, rather than hNAIP/NLRC4 inflammasome activation to restrict intracellular Salmonella replication. However, mouse IECs have high NAIP/NLRC4 expression and block mucosal traversal of Salmonella to prevent systemic dissemination.75–77

NLRP7: The lipopeptide-sensing inflammasome

NLRP7 is absent from mice but is encoded in humans, and although it has a structure similar to NLRP3, it has initially been reported to inhibit caspase-1–mediated release of IL-1β in overexpression studies.78 Hereditary mutations in NLRP7 show a strong association to the abnormal pregnancy hydatidiform mole,79 and disease-linked NLRP7 mutations impair IL-1β and TNF secretion without affecting IL-1β processing.80 In addition to the described inhibitory role of NLRP7, reports suggest a proinflammatory role of an NLRP7 inflammasome upon bacterial infection. An RNAi screen identified NLRP7 as an inflammasome sensor in human macrophages in response to infection with Gram-positive bacteria. NLRP7 recognizes bacterial acylated lipopeptides, a bacterial cell wall component, leading to ASC-dependent caspase-1 recruitment and activation (Fig. 1). Subsequent maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18 helps to restrict intracellular replication of Listeria and Staphylococcus.81 The ATPase activity of NLRP7 is needed to promote inflammasome responses82 and NLRP7 needs to be deubiquitinated by the deubiquitinase enzyme STAMBP (signal transducing adaptor molecule binding protein) to prevent its degradation. Therefore, inhibition of STAMBP is a potential therapeutic strategy to reduce inflammation.83 Inflammasome activity is also regulated through alternative splicing of the LRR, as shown for human but not mouse NLRP3.23 Similarly, alternative splicing of NLRP7 results in proteins with a different number of LRRs and the isoform with the most LRRs promotes the highest level of IL-1β release.81 Thus, both activating and inhibitory inflammasome roles of NLRP7 have been reported, and therefore further research is needed, especially in the context of disease-linked NLRP7 mutations.

NLRP9: An RNA-sensing inflammasome in IECs

Human NLRP9 encodes a PYD, NACHT, and LRRs and is highly expressed in reproductive organs and IECs.84 There are 3 paralogs in mice, but they share <45% identity to human NLRP9, and only mouse NLRP9b is highly expressed in IECs. Recently, NLRP9 was discovered to play an important role in intestinal immune responses against rotavirus infections by sensing short dsRNA. Significantly, NLRP9 only recruits ASC after rotavirus infection and dsRNA recognition is mediated by DHX9 (DExH-box RNA helicase 9) (Fig. 1).84 Only human NLRP9 but not mouse Nlrp9b interacts with ASC upon rotavirus infection in HEK293T cells.84 However, rotavirus infection of Nlrp9 conventional and conditional IEC knockout mice showed elevated viral load in IECs and more severe pathology compared with wild type mice. In this model, pyroptosis, rather than IL-18, is crucial to contain rotavirus infection.84 Crystal structure studies of the human NLRP9 PYD revealed unique aspects of the molecular mechanism of NLRP9 inflammasome assembly. Contrary to other PYDs, including NLRP3, NLRP1 and ASC, the NLRP9 PYD cannot self-oligomerize, remains monomeric, and also does not colocalize with ASC specks.85,86 N-terminal loop obstruction of interacting surfaces or charge inversion of several residues within the interacting surfaces that generate repulsive forces have been proposed to prevent oligomerization, but post-translational modifications could possibly be needed to promote oligomerization.85,86 In addition, the interaction of a unique C-terminal region of NLRP9 with the LRR is proposed to help maintain an inactive closed conformation to regulate NLRP9 activation.87 While it seems that Nlrp9b is the functional ortholog of human NLRP9 in IEC, it is still unclear if human NLRP9 takes over all functions of the 3 Nlrp9 genes encoded in mice and if Nlrp9a and Nlrp9c also form inflammasomes in other tissues. The molecular mechanism for NLRP9 inflammasome assembly is also still elusive.

PYRIN: The guard-type inflammasome

Pyrin is encoded by the MEFV (Mediterranean fever) gene and consists of an N-terminal PYD and a linker region followed by bZIP, bBox (B), coiled coil (CC), and B30.2 (PRY/SPRY) domains. Interestingly mouse pyrin does not contain a C-terminal B30.2 domain due to a frameshift mutation.88,89 The inactive state is maintained by serine phosphorylation of the linker region by protein kinase N1 and 2 (PKN1/2), to facilitate binding of the 14-3-3 chaperone. Activation of the human pyrin inflammasome has been recently described to proceed in 2 regulatory steps:1 Disruption of 14-3-3 binding to pyrin, for instance through Rho GTPase inhibition preventing PKN1/2 activity2 and suspension of the B30.2 domain-mediated negative regulation, which not only modulates PKN1 binding, but also facilitates inflammasome activation in a steroid hormone catabolite dependent manner (Fig. 1).89–91 Despite the missing B30.2 domain in mice, the pyrin inflammasome is activated in response to RhoA GTPase inhibition. For instance, the bacterial toxins Clostridioides difficile toxins A/B or Yersinia outer proteins target Rho GTPases to prevent actin polymerization and phagocytosis as an evasion mechanism. Hence, pyrin functions as a sensor for perturbed actin cytoskeletal dynamics. Furthermore, pathogenic mutations in the human MEFV gene are linked to autoinflammatory syndromes with high IL-1β levels, including Familial Mediterranean Fever (FMF) and pyrin-associated autoinflammation with neutrophilic dermatosis (PAAND). While FMF-associated mutations are clustered in the C-terminal B30.2 domain of pyrin, PAAND-associated mutations interfere with serine dephosphorylation and 14-3-3 protein binding. Consistent with FMF mutations in the B30.2 domain or PAAND mutations in the phosphorylated linker, pyrin inflammasome activation in FMF patients is largely controlled by phosphorylation, but monocytes from PAAND patients strongly respond to low doses of steroid hormone catabolites.90,91 Interestingly, knock-in of the human B30.2 domain in mice causes a spontaneous inflammatory phenotype, which is further exacerbated by introducing human FMF-associated pyrin mutations in the B30.2. The increased IL-1β and severe autoinflammation are reminiscent of human FMF.89,92

Regulators of the human canonical inflammasome signaling pathway

All inflammasome sensors, the adaptor ASC and the inflammatory caspases contain a PYD (NLRPs, AIM2, IFI16, pyrin), a CARD (NLRCs, caspase-1/4/5/11), or both (ASC) to facilitate the oligomerization, nucleation, and polymerization of the inflammasome platform, which is necessary for caspase activation by an induced proximity mechanism and subsequent processing of IL-1β, IL-18, and the pore-forming GSDMD. The homotypic PYD-PYD or CARD-CARD interactions occur in a sequential manner, resulting in inflammasome assembly. With the evolution of the inflammasome pathway, genes encoding small proteins containing only the PYD or CARD are likely the result of gene duplications from other PYD-containing proteins and were positively selected for regulating inflammasome responses.93 These proteins can interact and competitively bind to inflammasome components to prevent crucial PYD or CARD interactions and interfere with inflammasome assembly. Intriguingly, these proteins are unique to humans and higher primates but are absent in most species including mice, which makes studying their physiological in vivo function and significance quite challenging. However, it implies that another level of inflammasome regulation evolved and is beneficial for humans.

PYD-only proteins

Although PYD-only proteins (POPs) are not encoded in mice, transgenic expression of human POPs recapitulates their inflammasome inhibitory function and ameliorates inflammasome-linked diseases like Muckle-Wells syndrome, gouty arthritis, and sepsis (POP1/PYDC1, POP2/PYDC2) and viral infections (POP3/PYDC5).94–98 POP1 and POP2 have high similarity to ASC and regulate inflammasome responses by binding to ASC, thereby preventing the interaction between inflammasome sensors, such as NLRP3, and the PYD of ASC to prevent caspase-1 activation.94–97,99–102 Additionally, POP2 interferes with IκB kinases upstream of NF-κB and inhibits inflammasome priming.96,97,101,102 Interestingly, these different activities are encoded by different α-helices.103 Despite inhibiting innate immune responses, POP2-expressing mice are more resistant to infection with Francisella and Streptococcus through increased IFN-γ production and improved macrophage function. Hence, POP2 reduces detrimental inflammation while preserving and enhancing protective immunity.96,97 Furthermore, POP1 and POP2 interfere not only with inflammasome assembly by targeting ASC, but also with PYD-containing inflammasome sensor oligomerization and filament polymerization.104 Interestingly, expression of POP1 and POP2 is positively regulated by NF-κB signaling downstream of TLRs and cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-18, resembling a feedforward regulatory loop.97,103 On the other hand, POP3 expression is induced by IFN-I, similar to the dsDNA inflammasome sensors AIM2 and IFI16. POP3 further differs from POP1 and POP2 because it does not bind to ASC, but rather binds to the PYD-containing AIM2 and IFI16. Thus, competitive binding of POP3 specifically prevents ASC recruitment and inflammasome activation.98 Furthermore, POP2 and POP3 promote IFN-I production,98 and therefore also POP3 expression is regulated by a feedforward regulatory loop. Overall, POPs are important regulators of human inflammasome assembly.

Caspase recruitment domain–only proteins

Caspase recruitment domain–only proteins (COPs) are small proteins consisting of just a single CARD, share high homology with the caspase-1 CARD, and likely originated from exon duplication events.93 Three COPs, CARD16 (COP/Pseudo-ICE), CARD17 (INCA), and CARD18 (Iceberg), are encoded within the genomic cluster of inflammatory caspases in humans but are absent in mice. Analogous to the function of POPs, they bind and negatively regulate caspase-1 activation by competitive binding.105–108 In addition to inhibiting caspase-1 dimerization, CARD16 can modestly activate NF-κB and binds to the CARD of ASC. Contraindicative to its described inflammasome inhibitory function, oligomerized CARD16 acts as a scaffold for caspase-1 filament formation and activation.109 However, CARD17 localizes to the tip of caspase-1 filaments and caps them to prevent filament elongation.110–113 While CARD18 inhibits RIPK2-mediated caspase-1 oligomerization,106 it was also shown to promote caspase-1-CARD filament formation.111 Yet, in a FRET assay and upon expression in a human macrophage cell line, all 3 COPs prevented dimerization of caspase-1-CARD and inhibited caspase-1 activation, IL-1β and IL-18 release, and pyroptosis. Accordingly, their knockout enhances caspase-1 activation and IL-1β and IL-18 release.114 Moreover, transgenic expression of human CARD16, CARD17, and CARD18 in mice prevents inflammasome activation and ameliorates uric acid crystal-induced gouty arthritis.114 Importantly, the 3 COPs act through distinct, nonredundant mechanisms thereby adding different layers and control points to human inflammasome regulation.

Noncanonical inflammasome: The LPS-sensing inflammasome

Bacterial LPS is a major PAMP and activates distinct innate immune pathways. Extracellular LPS is recognized by the plasma membrane-bound TLR4/MD2/CD14 complex leading to MyD88-dependent NF-κB activation, which serves as a priming step for inflammasome activation. Furthermore, endocytosed LPS is recognized by endosomal TLR4 leading to TRIF-dependent type I IFN production.115 LPS that reaches the cytosol during infection with Gram-negative bacteria is sensed by the CARDs of human caspase-4/5 or mouse caspase-11, resulting in their oligomerization and activation of the noncanonical inflammasome.116 Subsequently the caspase-4/5/11 substrate, GSDMD, is cleaved, resulting in membrane pore formation and pyroptotic cell death.117–120 Noncanonical inflammasome activation also leads to K+ efflux and thus secondary activation of the NLRP3/ASC/caspase-1 inflammasome and subsequent maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18.118,121 Consequently, the noncanonical inflammasome plays a protective role during bacterial infection to limit bacterial replication.122,123 Unlike the canonical inflammasome, caspase-4/5/11 of the noncanonical inflammasome function as intracellular LPS sensors as well as effector caspases and no additional adaptor is needed.124 Until recently, caspase-4 and caspase-5 were considered the only intracellular receptors for LPS in humans. However, recent reports suggest that guanylate-binding proteins (GBPs) and NLRP11 can also sense cytoplasmic LPS and facilitate its transfer and binding to caspase-4/5, specifically in human macrophages (Fig. 2).

Caspase-4/5

Humans and mice display significant differences in the noncanonical inflammasome because human caspase-4 and caspase-5 evolved through gene duplication of an ancestral gene compared with mice with only 1 ortholog, caspase-11. While caspase-4 is constitutively expressed, caspase-5 is upregulated in response to LPS and IFNγ similar to caspase-11 and they are also structurally and functionally more similar.125 Like the TLR4/MD2/CD14 complex, caspase-4/5/11 also recognize the hydrophobic acetylated lipid A moiety of LPS,124 but there are important differences between human and mouse intracellular LPS recognition. Caspase-4/5/11 all recognize the usually hexa-acylated lipid A, but some pathogens, including Francisella, express tetra-acylated lipid A. While this allows evasion of noncanonical inflammasome responses in mice, caspase-4 evolved in humans to also sense this under-acylated LPS.126 Binding of LPS to caspase-4/5/11 induces their oligomerization,124 and dimerization is sufficient for caspase-4 activation127 through proximity-induced autoproteolysis.128 While caspase-4 is essential for pyroptosis,129–131 the specific role of caspase-5 in sensing LPS independently of caspase-4 and during infection remains poorly understood. Caspase-4/5 can also sense heme, a by-product of red blood cell lysis and a known extracellular DAMP. Although both mediate cytokine release, heme-induced pyroptosis is primarily mediated by caspase-4.132 Cytokine processing and release has mainly been attributed to secondary NLRP3/ASC/caspase-1 inflammasome activation.118,121 However, recent evidence shows that human caspase-4/5, but not mouse caspase-11, can also cleave pro–IL-1β and pro–IL-18 independently of caspase-1. This potentially bypasses the caspase-1–dependent canonical inflammasome activation in response to Gram-negative bacteria infections.127,133,134 Another study confirmed that human but not mouse caspases mediate pro–IL-18 processing and activation, but pro–IL-1β was cleaved into an inactive p27 fragment, rather than a bioactive p17 fragment by caspase-4/5 and caspase-11, thus deactivating IL-1β signaling. Hence, generation of mature IL-1β may be detrimental during infection.135 Furthermore, only human caspase-5 and mouse caspase-11, but not caspase-1/4, were shown to promote pro–IL-1α cleavage and release,136 although contradictory results reported also caspase-4-mediated cleavage of pro–IL-1α.129

Guanylate-binding proteins

IFN plays a crucial role in facilitating noncanonical inflammasome activation by upregulating a family of IFN-inducible GTPases, including GBPs and p47 immunity-related GTPases (IRGs). Both promote pathogen-containing vacuole rupture and recruitment of caspases to pathogen membranes as well as cytoplasmic LPS.137 While mice encode 23 IRGs and 11 GBPs, humans lack functional p47 IRGs but encode 7 GBPs. GBPs consist of an N-terminal catalytic GTPase domain followed by a helical domain for protein and membrane interactions and a C-terminal prenylation motif. Initial recruitment of human GBP1 to the outer surface of vacuole-escapedGram-negative bacteria and to the membrane of pathogen-containing vacuoles facilitates GBP2, GBP3, and GBP4 recruitment and formation of GBP aggregates, which serve as a platform for caspase-4 recruitment and activation followed by pyroptosis.138–142 GBPs function as unique cytosolic PRRs with positively charged residues in the N- and C-terminal regions binding directly to the lipid A moiety of free LPS or LPS in outer membrane vesicles to promote LPS aggregation for efficient caspase-4 activation.139,143,144 Significantly, only human GBP1, and to a much weaker extent GBP2 and GBP3, bind and aggregate LPS to promote pyroptosis and restrict bacterial growth after bacterial infections.138,139,143,144 While under certain conditions, human GBP2 can promote Gram-negative bacteria-induced pyroptosis in the absence of GBP1, most evidence supports that GBP1 is required to liberate LPS for the subsequent recruitment of other GBPs.143 Although colocalization of some mouse GBPs with LPS has been observed, there is currently no evidence that they directly bind to LPS. In addition to assembling a GBP platform on pathogen-containing vacuolar membranes, GBPs can also directly kill Gram-negative bacteria by disrupting the LPS O-antigen barrier and destabilizing the rigidity of the outer membrane.144 Hence, human GBP1 is an LPS-sensing pattern recognition receptor that likely supports LPS binding and activation of caspase-4 by assembling a GBP scaffold to facilitate caspase-4 clustering, oligomerization, and activation.

NLRP11

NLRP11 not only contributes to NLRP3 activation,19 but also functions as intracellular LPS sensor by binding to cytoplasmic LPS.32 It also binds to caspase-4 and caspase-1 but not to caspase-5, and promotes efficient caspase-4 activation, GSDMD cleavage, and pyroptosis in response to intracellular LPS or Shigella infection.32 Therefore, NLRP11 may facilitate responses particularly if only small amounts of LPS are present during bacterial infections by bringing LPS molecules in close proximity to caspase-4 for efficient binding. Because mice lack NLRP11, this may explain the increased sensitivity of humans to LPS and Gram-negative bacteria infections.

Regulators of the human noncanonical inflammasome

To control noncanonical inflammasome activation, the expression of its components is regulated. Caspase-5 and caspase-11, but not caspase-4, are LPS- and IFN-inducible and IFN regulatory factors (IRFs) emerged as crucial transcriptional regulators of caspase-4 expression.125,145 While IRF2 directly binds to the promotor of caspase-4 and is necessary for LPS-induced pyroptosis, IRF1 was able to compensate for the absence of IRF2 in the presence of IFN-γ. Interestingly, in mouse macrophages IRF2 also regulates GSDMD but not caspase-11 expression.146 However, in humans IRF2 may have some cell type specific effects, since it regulates GSDMD expression in endothelial cells but caspase-4 expression in macrophages.145,146 Because the noncanonical inflammasome has a pivotal role in defending against intracellular pathogens, it is not surprising that bacterial pathogens developed evasion mechanisms. Enterohemorrhagic E. coli prevent caspase-4 activation and pyroptosis in epithelial cells through their T3SS effector NleF, which prevents the dimerization and subsequent activation of caspase-4.147 Similarly, Shigella secretes a T3SS effector OspC3, which also binds to caspase-4 to prevent its dimerization and activation.148 Shigella and Yersinia secrete E3 ubiquitin ligases to ubiquitinate and consequently degrade GBPs. Specifically, Shigella IpaH9.8 targets hGBP1 and Yersinia YspE1 and YspE2 target hGBP1, hGBP2, hGBP5 and hGBP6 to evade the noncanonical inflammasome in human cells.149,150 Shigella also encodes the virulence factor IpaH7.8, which targets human GSDMD for ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby preventing pyroptosis and the loss of its intracellular replication niche. Interestingly, amino acid changes in the LRR motif of N-terminal GSDMD prevent IpaH7.8 from targeting mouse GSDMD,151 showcasing the evolutionary competition between bacterial virulence factors and host defense mechanism.

Conclusions

Inflammasomes contribute to crucial innate immune responses to pathogenic infections or disturbances of cellular homeostasis by facilitating IL-1β and IL-18 release and pyroptosis. Although inflammasome assembly, activation, and the consequent cellular responses are highly conserved, evidence supports significant differences among species. For instance, songbirds do not encode ASC and caspase-1, which arguably are the inflammasome core components.152 Interestingly, bats show a generally dampened NLRP3 inflammasome response due to decreased expression, a novel splice variant and altered LRR, which allows to tolerate high viral loads.153 Further examples for the adaptation of the innate immune system to environmental factors include commensal microbes. Because defects in inflammasome components, such as hereditary mutations and impaired regulation contribute to or cause a wide spectrum of inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, as well as other immune-linked disorders, extensive research efforts over the past two decades aimed to elucidate the fundamental principles of inflammasome biology and to establish the core concepts of inflammasome signaling. While human inflammasome research often relies on genetically manipulated human cancer cell lines, such as the monocytic cell line THP-1 or on primary human cells derived from peripheral blood, emerging access to inducible pluripotent stem cell–derived immune cells and inducible pluripotent stem cell–derived or primary organoids from healthy donors or patients with different inflammatory disorders will allow consideration of crosstalk between different cell types. We expect that these approaches will better complement in vivo studies to drastically improve our understanding of human disease mechanisms and help to identify disease-specific and even patient-specific drug targets for tailored therapeutic approaches. To study inflammasome activation in vivo, mouse models are most frequently used due to the technical gene manipulation advances that allow knocking out of inflammasome proteins and regulators and due to the well-established tools for determining inflammasome activation by measuring proinflammatory mediators in body fluids and in affected tissues. To gain deeper insights into human disease development and to possibly harness mechanistic findings into novel intervention strategies for future clinical applications, it is critical to develop and utilize appropriate models that reflect human disease.154 Because most in vivo disease models are performed in mice, it is particularly important to consider key aspects of human inflammasomes as for instance differences in ligand affinity, domain architecture, coreceptors, and regulators as summarized in Fig. 3. Improved CRISPR technologies allow the generation of humanized mice by replacing a mouse gene with the human ortholog or by adding human specific domains to the mouse gene but changing a single inflammasome component may not be sufficient to recapitulate human disease. While mouse disease models help to gain insights into conserved functions and mechanisms of inflammasome activation and regulation, significant discrepancies between humans and mice have been observed. For instance, disease severity of CAPS, caused by different mutations in the Nlrp3 gene, is reversed in mice compared with humans, which further highlights the need for improved models to study human-specific aspects of inflammasomes and related diseases. Particularly, based on the already identified differences within regulatory mechanisms, the transfer of a single human gene into a mouse may not always recapitulate its regulation and function. For instance, replacing mouse with human NLRP3 will still not reflect human NLRP3 responses in the absence of crucial coreceptors and regulators found in human such as NLRP11 and NLRP12. NLRP11 is necessary for NLRP3 inflammasome activation even in NLRP3 autoactivating disease mutant–expressing human cells. This could explain the discrepancy between disease severity of human NLRP3 mutants in the mouse for CAPS, as different mutations could cause activation in an NLRP11-dependent or NLRP11-independent manner, but still requires further investigation. Humanized mouse models utilizing human bone marrow transfer are very suitable to study inflammasome pathways and their intervention in myeloid cells, but they might not properly reflect systemic functions, including interactions with and functional consequences on stromal and parenchymal cells. To address those concerns, recent efforts succeeded in creating mice (huNSG-QUAD) with complete and fully functional human immune systems and functional human NLRP3 inflammasome signaling.155

Although mouse models can be utilized for a wide variety of inflammasome-linked diseases, limitations apply especially when studying human-specific inflammasomes or human-specific pathogens. Suitable models may depend on whether it is beneficial to suppress or enhance inflammasome activity, and eventually inducible models can be used to avoid developmental defects and tissue-/cell type–specific targeting. In conclusion, human inflammasome research is rapidly developing, and human-specific models hold great promise to unlock many yet unexplored areas, paving the way for exciting new discoveries.
