Study Protocol: Homelessness and Health: Integrating Structural, Social, and Behavioral Pathways
Authors: Lesley Jo Weaver, L. Zachary DuBois, Mackenzie Ní Flainn, J. Josh Snodgrass
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis protocol paper describes the methods of a multi‐year study examining individual, community, and structural‐level influences on health among people experiencing homelessness in a mid‐sized Oregon city. We draw on biocultural theory and method to examine three distinct social ecologies of homelessness in the study setting: unsheltered homelessness, temporarily sheltered homelessness, and permanent supportive or transitional housing. Our mixed‐method approach incorporates epidemiological‐style questionnaires, biomarkers sampled via finger prick blood and hair, and in‐depth case study interviews to holistically examine the pathways that connect homelessness and physical and mental health, and to compare outcomes across the three social ecologies of homelessness. Preliminary results are now emerging and are being used in public advocacy as well as research publications.

Introduction

Homelessness reached record highs across the USA in 2024, the last year for which the federal government reported data (de Sousa and Henry 2025). The challenges of homelessness are no longer confined to specific demographic groups clustered in urban centers, but now impact large and small cities and people of varied demographic backgrounds across the country. Recent legislation such as the 2024 Grants Pass v. Johnson Supreme Court ruling empowered municipalities to enforce camping bans regardless of the lack of availability of shelter beds, effectively criminalizing unsheltered homelessness. Amidst the current housing affordability and cost‐of‐living crises, and in the wake of the COVID‐19 pandemic, more Americans than ever before are contending with housing insecurity and its consequences.

Homelessness is a recognized public health crisis (Willison 2021). Virtually every facet of health that is measured, whether clinically or in research, is worse among people experiencing homelessness (PEH) than among those that are housed; these inequities span measures of mental health, chronic diseases, sleep quality and quantity, substance use, infections of all types, experiences of dis/ability, and are also reflected in premature mortality (Hwang et al. 2001; Toro et al. 1999; Westbrook and Robinson 2021). As in other parts of the US, the housing crisis in Oregon has resulted in a population of PEH with a diverse set of unmet health needs, many caused or exacerbated by the physical embodiment of internalized social inequality and marginalization (Alexander et al. 2022; Byrne et al. 2021; Hodgetts et al. 2007; Lyon‐Callo 2000). Within these contexts of marginalization, during pilot phase interviews, PEH routinely emphasized the crucial importance of social connections with peers and mentors in homeless communities.

Efforts aiming to address health among PEH often focus narrowly on individual‐level behaviors (e.g., illicit drug use) as predictors of health risk or targets for behavioral intervention, without sufficient consideration of social relationships or broader sociopolitical structures and contexts (e.g., Folsom et al. 2005). This individualistically‐focused paradigm is problematic for at least two reasons. First, it leads to “blind spots” in the literature that themselves reflect a lack of understanding of the multiple pathways that lead from environmental configurations to poor health among PEH, nor those that might attenuate health risks. Second, interventions designed to benefit PEH often force new social configurations and lack evidence about the optimal design, timing, and intensity of those interventions for wellbeing. A collective lack of consideration of the importance of social ties and impact of social disruptions among homeless communities constrains evidence‐based policy development. Concern about social disruption may also discourage individual PEH from accessing certain shelter arrangements if rules limit guests or force other kinds of social reconfiguration (Magwood et al. 2019).

This paper describes the study protocol for a multi‐year NSF‐funded project (BCS‐2317286), part of the Homelessness Policy and Health working group; https://blogs.uoregon.edu/weaverlab/houselessness‐research/ at the University of Oregon. The study design offers an alternative, more holistic approach for examining drivers of poor health among PEH that takes individual, social, and structural factors into account. The overall goal of the study is to merge a social‐ecological theoretical model and a mixed‐methods biocultural approach to investigate lived experiences of health and illness across the spectrum of homelessness in Oregon.

In this study, we identify three main social‐ecological niches that pattern social and structural exposures for PEH: (1) sleeping unsheltered on the streets alone or in small groups (including tent or car camping); (2) sleeping in temporary shelters, such as safe sleep sites; and (3) sleeping in permanent supportive housing. The first setting—formally known as “unsheltered homelessness” by the US Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) is generally the most resource‐scarce but least limiting, meaning that people may have pets, actively use drugs, and/or may have legal system involvement without being barred from those spaces. Unsheltered spaces also offer little security and are technically illegal (although enforcement varies by location), so people are often required to move frequently and are subject to theft, repeated citation or incarceration, and interpersonal violence. Temporary shelters, meanwhile—dubbed “sheltered homelessness” by HUD—typically offer more resources and security but may have barriers to entry that exclude people with pets, active substance use, and/or legal system involvement. They also often involve restrictions that limit residents' social interactions, which can be a major deterrent for people who would otherwise prefer to be sheltered (Magwood et al. 2019). Finally, permanent supportive housing (PSH) combines stable housing, rental assistance, and voluntary supportive services such as case management and onsite care teams. PSH is generally considered the “gold standard” for addressing chronic homelessness (Aubry et al. 2020; Rog et al. 2014), but it tends to be the most rule‐constrained and limited in quantity.

The study objectives are as follows:

Objective 1: Understand relevant structural and social characteristics associated with the three identified socioecological contexts of homelessness in the study site.

Objective 2: Compare individual characteristics, social relationships, structural drivers of marginalization, mental health, and physical health across the three social ecologies of homelessness.

Objective 3: Investigate multivariate relationships between the three social ecologies of homelessness and mental/physical health, including allostatic load as an overall measure of “wear and tear” across physiological systems.

Objective 4. Assess the differential contribution of individual, social, and structural variables to health.

Ethics

The study procedures were co‐developed with an expert panel of community partners working in homelessness who provided guidance on best practices for trauma‐informed biospecimen collection, appropriate and desirable compensation levels, and special considerations for anonymity. These were then presented for further refinement during a series of focus group discussions with people currently experiencing houselessness in 2022–2023. Focus group participants advised on methods and approach and provided essential feedback on additional supports provided for study participants in the form of medical consultation and care, survival supplies, harm reduction supplies, and basic food.

This phase of relationship building and pilot work helped the team establish sensitivity to the context and trust within homeless communities and service providers, who are rightfully protective of the vulnerable communities in which they work and live. Following their guidance, we obtained IRB approval for participants to provide verbal informed consent only and be compensated via cash instead of gift cards. All study procedures were pre‐approved by the authors' Institutional Review Board (#00000840). Given that many people in this population have legal system involvement, we also obtained a Certificate of Confidentiality from the National Institutes of Health, which protects researchers from being compelled to disclose identifying information about research participants in response to subpoenas or other legal demands. All study team members received standard human subjects research ethics training, plus enhanced training led by MNF in trauma‐informed approaches to research, bystander intervention, de‐escalation, self‐defense, and naloxone administration.

Phase 1 participants are assigned a study number, and Phase 2 participants are invited to choose a pseudonym. Participants are compensated $30 cash for each interview or questionnaire (including both Phase 1 and 2) and $20 cash for hair and blood biospecimen collection during Phase 1. These levels and methods of compensation were deemed appropriate and desirable by the expert panel. During and separately from data collection sessions, the research team is gathering survival and harm reduction supplies from community partners, donations, and private purchase, and distributing these to anyone in need both during and outside data collection sessions, whether or not they are a study participant.

As the study results begin to emerge, we are assembling a new expert committee of people with lived experience of homelessness to advise on data interpretation, dissemination venues and plans, and to provide co‐authorship and dissemination participation opportunities for interested study participants.

Methods and Analysis

Setting

The study is being conducted in a mid‐sized Oregon city that recently reported one of the nation's highest per‐capita homelessness rates (HUD 2021). In 2025 over 10 000 people participated in the county's Homeless Response System (Health and Human Services 2025). Available data suggest a sharp local increase in total numbers of PEH during the COVID lockdowns of 2020, while nationally, there was a significant rise in unsheltered homelessness and a slight decrease in shelter use during this time, driven largely by West Coast states like Oregon (Henry et al. 2022). Oregon currently has an acute housing crisis; between 2020 and 2022 alone, rental fees increased across Oregon by over 15%, while population growth and slow housing production dramatically reduced the vacancy rate to one of the lowest in the US (State of the State's Housing Report 2024).

Recruitment and Sampling

We are recruiting study participants during local service provider health events, and through our own pop‐up clinics and outreach visits at service centers and local parks. We are also recruiting participants in partnership with local permanent supportive housing facilities.

We are using a quota convenience sampling strategy to recruit a sample of 401 PEH (Table 1), to date, divided across the three social ecologies of homelessness described above. Data collection is ongoing, but the current sample size exceeds the minimum requirement of 146 individuals to detect small effects at an alpha level of 0.05 with 15 predictors in a hierarchical linear multiple regression model (an a priori power calculation using G*Power 3.1.9.7). People who are 18 years of age or over, who have been without housing for at least 30 days, and who are able to give informed consent are eligible to participate. People younger than 18, who have been without housing for less than 30 days, or whose ability to understand and respond to questions is limited for any reason are ineligible for participation. If they wish, those ineligible for participation are still eligible to receive biomarker‐based tests and a health consultation with an EMT, nurse, or physician and referral to local free‐of‐cost healthcare providers.

Data Collection

We are administering a series of questionnaires and collecting minimally invasive finger prick blood and hair samples to assess stress and health biomarkers (DeCaro and Helfrecht 2022; McDade et al. 2007; Madimenos et al. 2022; Snodgrass 2022; Worthman and Costello 2009). We are also working with a subset of 80 individuals from the original data collection who indicated that they are willing to participate in follow‐up visits and in‐depth qualitative interviews to develop ethnographic case studies.

Timeline

Data collection began in November 2023 and, at the time of this writing in June 2026, is nearing completion. The study protocol is being published now (instead of earlier in the research process) because AJHB only began publishing study protocols in late spring 2026.

Phase 1: Questionnaire, Biomarkers, and Policy Analysis (Expected Completion July 30, 2026)

To address objective 1, we are conducting structured interviews based around a questionnaire. The interview guide includes questions about PEH's experiences of incarceration, police interactions, perceptions of changes in social freedom when moving between socio‐ecological settings, any existing restrictions on people's ability to spend time with people they wish, and assessments of the positive and negative impacts of their current and most recent previous living situations. We are also collecting homelessness‐related policy documents and media accounts reporting on policy specific to Oregon. Policy changes with relevance for the study population include actions such as police sweeps, changes to the legality of public land camping, and decriminalization of drugs, all of which are occurring in the study site. These materials provide context for the research and its findings, as well as touchpoints for discussion during case study interviews with PEH about their experiences of these structural‐level influences.

To address objectives 2–4, the questionnaire includes validated scales assessing individual‐, social‐, and structural‐level variables hypothesized to impact physical and mental health (Table 2, below). These include social connectedness, perceived stress, resilience, food insecurity, symptoms of depression and anxiety, drug use, stressful life events, self‐reported physical health, Medicaid enrollment, health behaviors, frequency of forced or voluntary moving, and demographic information.

At the time of the questionnaire, we are also collecting minimally‐invasive biomarkers for allostatic load and overall health assessment (DeCaro and Helfrecht 2022; McDade et al. 2007; Madimenos et al. 2022; Snodgrass 2022; Worthman and Costello 2009). These include blood pressure, glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), a lipid panel (total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, triglycerides), hemoglobin measured using finger prick blood tests and point‐of‐care instruments, C‐reactive protein (CRP) using dried blood spots, and hair cortisol.

Data collection occurs outdoors in public parks, at service centers, and at permanent supportive housing buildings year‐round on a weekly or monthly basis. Sessions are typically staffed by 6 individuals handling consent, interviews, and biomarkers as a stepwise process, adapting a model developed across other field sites by JJS and colleagues (Madimenos et al. 2022; McClure et al. 2013; Snodgrass et al. 2010, 2026). Phase 2: Case Studies of Lived Experience of Homelessness (Expected Completion September 30, 2026)

Throughout the fieldwork, case studies are being developed during at least one follow‐up visit with a subset of approximately 80 people from the original sample of 401 individuals. These consist of semi‐structured interviews and participant observation of everyday life in participants' social worlds. Interviews elicit details of their lives and personal histories, daily routines, perceptions of the quality and nature of their relationships with friends, street kin, and family, and perceptions of how their social settings have changed over time. As results emerge from the policy materials, we are incorporating questions about experiences of specific policy changes into case study interviews. We are also asking about prior experiences with service providers and housing options, and prior experiences of exploitation and/or mistreatment by people in positions of power. These case studies will provide a broadened perspective on how the social dynamics captured in the Phase 1 data appear in everyday life and in participants' wider social contexts. These interviews address Objectives 1–4.

Key Outcome Variables

Depression and Anxiety Symptomology

The Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 and Generalized Anxiety Disorder Assessment‐7 are used to assess symptoms of depression and anxiety. They consist of 9 and 7 questions, respectively, with four Likert‐style response categories scored 0–3 whose responses are summed to create scores that reflect the number and severity of symptoms in the last 4 weeks. PHQ‐9 scores can range from 0 to 27, and GAD‐7 scores can range from 0 to 21.

Allostatic Load

To assess AL, we are measuring each participant's blood pressure and heart rate as indices of cardiovascular function using an automated device (Omron 3 or Omron HEM‐907XL, Hoffman Estates, IL); glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) as a measure of metabolic function and approximate 3 month average blood sugar (A1cNow+, Whitestown, IN); C‐reactive protein (CRP) as a measure of chronic inflammation and immune function from dried blood spots collected by finger prick using standard procedures (McDade et al. 2007); and finally, we are collecting a hair sample for hair cortisol analysis to assess endocrine function in the Global Health Biomarker Lab at the University of Oregon. CRP and cortisol are measured using colorimetric enzyme linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA; BioTek ELX808/EL808 microplate readers, Winooski, VT). These markers assess the four key physiological systems involved in maintaining allostasis and have been successfully used in a prior community‐based study in of stigma, marginalization, and health among transgender and nonbinary people in Oregon to assess allostatic load (DuBois and Juster 2022). Allostatic load will be calculated by summing the parameters for which a participant falls into the highest risk quartile for their lived gender (Singer et al. 2004) (Table 3).

Physical Health

To further assess physical health in addition to allostatic load and ensure that study participation is beneficial for participants, we measure blood hemoglobin using the HemoCue Hb201+ point‐of‐care instrument and glucose and lipid profile using the Cardiocheck PA instrument with finger prick blood spots. Those measures serve as the basis for a free health consultation with volunteer medical staff and will be used in analysis as secondary health indicators in addition to stress and allostatic load measures.

Self‐Reported Health

We assess self‐reported health using the Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System‐2 (PROMIS‐2), a two‐item instrument that asks people to rate their health as poor, fair, good, or excellent and state to what extent they face challenges in everyday physical activities such as walking, climbing stairs, and carrying groceries. These items will be treated separately in analysis.

Key Predictor Variables

Social Connection

We assess social connection using the Social Connectedness Scale‐Revised, an instrument consisting of 20 items that assess a subjective sense of how close one feels in their relationships with the social world, and which has been used in other studies with PEH (Dwyer et al. 2023; McCay et al. 2011). Participants provide Likert‐style responses to each item (1 = strongly agree to 6 = strongly disagree). Responses are summed (and reverse‐scored as needed, since some items are worded negatively) to produce a final score that can range from 20 to 120, with higher scores reflecting a greater feeling of connectedness.

Health Behaviors

We assess individual‐level health behaviors using the Health Behaviors Checklist, a widely used instrument that assesses both health‐promoting behaviors such as exercise as well as risk‐avoiding behaviors such as using seatbelts in vehicles (Hampson et al. 2019). The original HBC consists of 39 items. We adapted the instrument to exclude items related to behaviors in households and drug use (which we assess separately), leaving 27 items. Responses are summed to produce an overall health behaviors score.

Alcohol and Other Substance Use

While we originally planned to assess alcohol and other substance use with standard scales such as the Drug Abuse Screening Test and the Alcohol Use disorders Identification Test (Allen et al. 1997; Skinner 1982; Yudko et al. 2007), they were deemed highly stigmatizing by our expert panel of PEH and community harm reduction workers. Instead, we ask open‐ended questions about alcohol, cannabis, and other drug use and use frequency. We also ask about experiencing overdoses.

Food Insecurity

We assess food insecurity using a modified version of the US Department of Agriculture's Adult Food Security Survey Module, which has been used successfully in past studies of PEH in the USA (O'Campo et al. 2017).

Stressful Life Events

To account for the potential confounding influences of stressful life events on mental health, we administer the Life Stressor Checklist‐Revised (Wolfe et al. 2012), a scale assessing experience of 30 stressful items that are summed to create a life stressor score that can range from 0 to 30. It has been used successfully with US‐based homeless populations (Alexander et al. 2022).

Resilience

We use a modified version of the Brief Resilience Scale to assess individual‐level resilience to hardship, or the ability to “bounce back” in response to stressful events (Smith et al. 2008). The BRS consists of six questions, one of which we excluded for brevity, resulting in a 5‐item scale whose responses are summed (and reverse‐scored as needed) to create a resilience score ranging from 5 to 25.

Demographics

The questionnaire records participants' age, gender identity, self‐reported racial identity, education level, parental status, employment status, date of first homelessness, and duration of current living situation.

Structural Violence Variables

We ask about lifetime frequency of arrest and incarceration, frequency of involuntary moves in the last 6 months, and police interactions in the last year.

Structural Restriction Variables

We assess restriction due to structural sources by asking about change in social freedom upon moving from their last to their present socioecological setting, whether participants have current legal action against them that restricts their ability to access essential services or structures, as well as positives and negatives associated with each of their last two living situations.

Analytic Plan

All qualitative analyses, including interview and policy analyses, are being conducted in Dedoose 9.0.78. All quantitative analyses are being conducted in R Studio v. 2026.05.0 + 218 and SPSS 29.0.2.0, with an alpha level of 0.05 for hypothesis testing. Before all analyses are performed, statistical assumptions are being checked and appropriate values being reported. In addition, relevant descriptive statistics are being calculated and presented. The rate of significance will be considered to evaluate risk of type 1 error due to multiple comparisons.

Dissemination

Because homelessness is a pressing social issue with significant public interest, the dissemination plan contains a public‐facing component and an academic component.

First, to facilitate public‐facing dissemination, the research team has produced an op‐ed article about cuts to federal food assistance and their impacts on PEH published in 2026 in Science Politics (Albiani et al. 2026). We have also presented mid‐stream results to community partners and stakeholders around the city and the state, including local neighborhood associations, the county housing authority, the university, the state science and industry museum, and a state‐wide consortium of homeless service providers. In April 2026, we held a share‐back meeting at the local library where all past participants were invited for a meal and a presentation of preliminary study findings. Public outreach is ongoing and will include future stakeholder presentations and a public education component.

In addition to this community‐based dissemination, the study is producing traditional scholarly outputs through academic conference presentations and peer‐reviewed academic publications, which will be placed in open‐access journals whenever possible to facilitate public access to research results.

Strengths and Challenges

This work, to our knowledge, is the first biocultural study of homelessness and its relationship to health. It is also the first to incorporate ethnographic case studies alongside quantitative analyses of experience, physiology, and health. It is therefore uniquely positioned to provide much‐needed basic research on the embodied stress associated with housing insecurity in the United States, and its impacts on physical and mental health. Beyond those basic findings, this research has the potential to transform our understanding of connections between the lived experience of homelessness and ill health—a taken‐for‐granted relationship whose constituent pathways are poorly understood.

This work is also unique in its ability to compare the degree to which structural variables measuring the impact of policy on people's lives predict health outcomes, as opposed to individual or interpersonal variables. It challenges the assumption that solutions to homelessness should focus on individuals as the site of action for change. Understanding the mechanisms that endanger or protect PEH at the individual, interpersonal, and structural levels is crucial for constructing meaningful programs that address the root of homelessness, rather than providing a temporary fix or worse, doing harm.

Challenges include data collection in outdoor environments, recruitment and consent, and incomplete data records. Temperature extremes at times require creative solutions to point‐of‐care instrument temperature regulation, such as using space heaters connected to a generator in winter or placing instruments on ice packs during summer. Recruitment and consent prove challenging at times because there is generally very high demand for participation, and the research team cannot always accommodate everyone who wishes to be involved. Occasionally, potential participants are unable to give informed consent while experiencing altered states of consciousness due to substance ingestion and/or neurodiversity; in cases where people are unable to respond to questions, we decline their participation. Finally, we have many incomplete records since people often wish to participate more than once, and sometimes skip sections of the questionnaire, which is quite long. The study team is doing our best to prevent repeated participation, but this occurs occasionally. The overall study refusal rate is very low, but the duration and complexity of the data collection process means that missingness occurs frequently. Analyses based on a smaller subsample and/or data imputation will be used to deal with missingness.
