Diagnosis of metastatic prostate cancer by supraclavicular lymph node excision in a patient with persistent macroscopic hematuria: A case report
Authors: Vladimir Drozda, Zuzana Cierna, Michal Harmanos, Marian Cvik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 72-year-old man, who was under regular urological care, was diagnosed with advanced prostate carcinoma, based on a biopsy of a supraclavicular lymph node. Despite rising PSA levels, the patient had negative results on digital rectal examination, negative repeat prostate biopsy, and prostate MRI showed no signs suggestive of malignancy. A PSMA PET/CT scan, which was not indicated for the patient during the diagnostic proces, would have facilitated the diagnostic workup, particularly in detecting metastases. This case highlights the need for a broad diagnostic approach and the use of all available diagnostic methods in the diagnosis of malignant diseases.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most commonly diagnosed cancer in men, with 1.466,680 new cases and 396,792 deaths worldwide in 2022.1 Acinar adenocarcinoma accounts for up to 95% of prostate malignancies.2 Prostate cancer is mostly located in the peripheral zone, less frequently in the central zone of the gland.3, a, b Clinical manifestations may include hematuria and recurrent lower urinary tract infections. In advanced stages of the disease, which account for about 13% of cases, bone pain due to the presence of metastases, coagulopathy and thrombocytopenia are encountered. The prognosis of this malignancy is heavily dependent on the presence and extent of metastases. The most common sites of prostate cancer metastases are regional lymph nodes, bones, liver and lungs.4 However, the proportion of patients with atypical sites of metastases is not negligible. Supraclavicular lymphadenopathy as the initial presentation of metastatic prostate cancer is particularly uncommon and easily misdiagnosed.4,5 Supraclavicular nodal metastases are most frequently encountered in gastrointestinal malignancies, primary head and neck tumors or lymphoproliferative disorders.6 However, an increasing number of cases of primary prostate adenocarcinoma spreading to the supraclavicular region have been reported, raising suspicion of this diagnosis.7 We present the case of a man with repeatedly negative prostate biopsies, who was diagnosed with prostate carcinoma based on the supraclavicular lymph node biopsy. Our case highlights the need for a broad diagnostic approach in situations, where there is a high suspicion of prostate carcinoma, yet the prostate biopsy is negative.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 72-year-old man who was diagnosed with advanced prostate cancer based on histopathological analysis of metastasis in an unusual site. The patient was followed up by an urologist in 2019 and 2020 with serum PSA values of 1.24 ng/ml and 1.93 ng/ml, respectively, with a physiological digital rectal exam (DRE) of the prostate. In January 2021, the patient suffered from acute prostatitis with a culture detection of Enterococcus faecalis. Serum PSA was not tested during antimicrobial treatment of prostatitis.

At the next routine urological screening exam in February 2023, the serum PSA value was 39.43 ng/ml, but the DRE revealed no suspicion of abscess or cancer. Therefore, an MRI of the prostate was performed in March 2023 (Fig. 1). The radiological report described an enlarged prostate protruding into the bladder and PI-RADS 2 and PI-RADS 3 nonspecific lesions in the central portion and apex of the gland, but without evidence of carcinoma. A blood test in March 2023 revealed a progression of serum PSA levels to 62.68 ng/ml and prostate biopsy was indicated. A transrectal ultrasound-guided random prostate biopsy was perfomed in May 2023 under antibiotic coverage with Gentamicin. In representative punctures of prostatic tissue were prostatic glands without signs of malignancy. Based on a negative biopsy and an unsuspicious MRI, the outpatient urologist recommended a follow-up examination in three months. PSMA PET/CT scan was not indicated. In June 2023, the patient underwent a neurological examination for the vertebral and rib pain in the thoracic spine with a recommendation to schedule an MRI of the thoracic spine. At the end of August 2023, an attack of macroscopic hematuria occurred with a decrease in the blood count from 137 g/l to 106 g/l, and the serum PSA level progressed significantly to 1443.50 ng/ml, which led to acute hospitalization at the Department of Urology of the University Hospital in Trnava at the beginning of September 2023. During institutional care, staging CT of the chest, abdomen, and small pelvis was performed, revealing metastatic foci in the lung parenchyma measuring 7–17 mm, more numerous in the right lung segments, hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy, a pathological supraclavicular mass on the left measuring 33 mm, pathological retroperitoneal and pelvic lymphadenopathy with lymph node packets, enlarged prostate, thickened bladder wall, and multiple lytic bone metastases. Maximum hemostatic therapy was initiated in collaboration with a hematologist, as the clinical condition progressed to severe coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, and secondary posthemorrhagic anemia of moderate severity. Despite treatment, the hemogram dropped to 89 g/l and acute transurethral coagulation of bleeding sites presumed to be in the prostate or bladder was indicated. The surgical findings were dominated by a picture of hyperplastic middle lobe of the prostate with varicose bleeding remodelling. The entire bladder mucosa showed no signs of pathological ulceration, telangiectasia, or petechial lesions. The middle lobe of the prostate was resected and the bleeding sites were coagulated. The resected tissue was sent for histopathological examination. The result was without signs of malignancy in completely processed and immunohistochemically examined material. The immunophenotype of the prostatic gland epithelium was AMACR negative surrounded by p63 positive basal cells. The tissue was focally necrotic, hemorrhagic, and heavily infiltrated with inflammatory polymorphonuclear leukocytes. The urethral epithelium was regular without dysplasia.

On the sixth postoperative day, hematuria recurred with a decrease in the blood count to 70 g/l, and a second endoscopic transurethral coagulation and simultaneous excision of supraclavicular lymph node on the left side were indicated. The result of histopathological examination was metastasis of prostate cancer to the left supraclavicular lymph node, confirmed by positive immunohistochemical markers specific to prostate tissue (PSA, AMACR, ERG) (Fig. 2). Due to histopathological confirmation of prostate cancer metastasis, consideration of numerous metastases in the skeleton and significant lymphadenopathy of the pelvis and retroperitoneum, treatment of metastatic prostate cancer was initiated with Degarelix LHRH antagonist from the group of androgen deprivation drugs. The patient experienced rapid stabilization of coagulation parameters, normothrombocythemia, and normalization of the blood count without the need for repeated blood transfusions. Almost three years after diagnosis, the patient is currently in the stage of hormone-sensitive metastatic prostate cancer with combination therapy consisting of androgen deprivation therapy and androgen receptor pathway inhibitors. Due to the presence of bone metastases, the patient is being treated with the monoclonal antibody Denosumab in combination with analgetics.

Discussion

Adenocarcinoma of the prostate has an indolent course, which may be difficult to diagnose in its early stages. Metastatic prostate cancer commonly involves the axial skeleton and the regional lymph nodes, followed by the lungs, bladder, liver and adrenal glands. Regional lymph node dissemination most frequently occurs to the obturator nodes, along with the perivesical, hypogastric, iliac, presacral and para-aortic nodes.5

Supraclavicular and cervical nodal metastasis from distant primaries beyond the neck and thorax are mostly associated with gastrointestinal malignancies such as gastric cancer.4 The left supraclavicular lymph node, classically described as Virchow's node, is recognized for its association with distant metastasis. It was first documented in 1848 by pathologist Rudolf Virchow, referring to carcinomatous involvement of the left supraclavicular lymph node secondary to metastatic malignancy, particularly from the abdomen and pelvis.8 Right supraclavicular nodal metastases are mostly observed in head and neck and thoracic malignancies and are extremely rare in infradiaphragmatic malignancies.4,5

Cervical lymph node metastasis occurs in only about 0,4% of patients with prostate cancer.9 The mechanism of metastasis to the supraclavicular lymph node is explained by the anatomical structure of the vascular and lymphatic system: from regional lymph nodes as obturator and pelvic ones, through the retroperitoneal lymph nodes, cysterna chyli, thoracic duct, where the lymphatic system reunites with the systematic circulation at the left subclavian vein. Therefore, prostate cancer can spread to the left jugular trunk via retrograde spread with left-sided supraclavicular lymph node metastasis. Prostate cancer supraclavicular lymph node metastases on the right-side have been described almost exclusively in cases of concurrent left-sided metastases.4,10 A recent study by Wan et al. summarizes published cases of supraclavicular and cervical metastases from prostate carcinoma, comprising 41 left-sided cases, 4 right-sided cases, and 3 bilateral cases.9 Supraclavicular nodal metastasis is asociated with increased serum PSA level, higher Gleason score and ISUP grade 5.4

A digital rectal examination is one of the standard screening tests for the prostate. In our patient, the result of this examination was within normal limits, despite high serum PSA levels and the presence of distant metastases. Similar cases of patients with advanced prostate cancer, supraclavicular lymphadenopathy, and a negative DRE have been published.8,11

Since the carcinoma mainly affects the central zone of the hyperplastic gland, it may not be detected by needle biopsy. In patients with high serum PSA levels and a negative result in terms of malignancy from a prostate biopsy, one option is to collect prostate tissue using a transurethral endoscopic approach.3,12 In rare cases where even transurethrally collected samples do not detect a malignant tumor, the key option is to examine the metastatic site, e.g., subcutaneous lesion or tissue from lymphadenopathy.

PSMA PET/CT is a new sensitive and specific radiological ability to identify metastatic prostate cancer using radiolabeled ligand 68Ga-PSMA-11, which binds to a transmembrane glycoprotein PSMA that is overexpressed in nearly all prostate cancer cells.3,4 According to EAU Guidelines 2021 and NCCN Guidelines 2021, PSMA PET/CT is recommended for initial staging of intermediate, high risk and very high risk prostate cancer where available due to its superiority compared to conventional imaging methods.4

In our case, the patient was diagnosed with advanced prostate cancer based on a biopsy of a supraclavicular lymph node, despite having undergone screening examinations for prostate cancer. The last serum PSA level test showing a normal value was performed in 2020. According to European guidelines, the follow-up interval for a patient without clinical symptoms and with negative PSA and DRE is set at every three years; the patient therefore underwent a urological examination in February 2023, at which time the serum PSA level was 39.43 ng/ml. Between February 2023 and August 2023, the patient underwent a series of medical examinations with an outpatient urologist, a radiologist, and a neurologist, with negative DRE, repeat negative prostate biopsy, and non-suspicious MRI.

During outpatient monitoring of the patient, a PSMA PET/CT scan, which could have led to the detection of the primary prostate carcinoma as well as metastatic lesions suitable for biopsy, was not indicated. In the subsequent course of events, the patient had to be urgently admitted to the hospital for inpatient care due to severe macroscopic hematuria with a decrease in blood cell counts, circulatory instability caused by blood loss, and progressive thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy. Unfortunately, the hospital where the patient was admitted does not have the capability to perform this crucial CT scan on-site. Given the patient's serious clinical condition, it was not possible to transport him to the nearest PET-CT facilities in neighboring cities. A CT scan performed during hospitalization revealed numerous metastatic lesions.

Conclusion

Diagnosing malignancies, including prostate cancer, which can remain asymptomatic even at an advanced stage, always presents a challenge. Rising serum PSA levels, despite a negative prostate biopsy and an MRI scan, necessitate a broader diagnostic approach. In such cases, one must also consider the rare possibility of metastatic spread to non-regional lymph nodes, specifically supraclavicular and cervical nodes, which are accessible to visual inspection and palpation. In this context, a basic physical examination of the lymph nodes could become part of the assessment in specialized outpatient clinics. In addition, it is necessary to utilize multiple imaging modalities, including PSMA PET/CT, which offers significantly higher sensitivity and specificity in identifying prostate cancer compared to conventional imaging methods such as MRI, CT, and bone scintigraphy.
