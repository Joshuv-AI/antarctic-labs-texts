Mutual benefit of cloud manufacturing system and customers through integration of scheduling, order acceptance and fairness under a maintenance strategy
Authors: Ali Salmasnia, Maedeh Abbaszadeh, Zohre Kiapasha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, the Cloud Manufacturing System (CMS) has received increasing attention as a customer-oriented process for the production of customized products. To increase the market share of the CMS, it’s inevitable to take customer satisfaction into account. Consequently, this study simultaneously addresses both customer satisfaction and CMS satisfaction. In this context, a multi-objective mathematical model is developed for the scheduling of tasks to the CMS, focusing on five core characteristics: (1) setting the customized product price under a mutual benefit policy, (2) designing a maintenance program to reduce machine failure rate and costs, (3) ensuring fairness among customers, (4) providing an order acceptance policy, and (5) factoring in the costs related to earliness and tardiness. The performance of the proposed model was assessed through three comparative experiments: (a) the mutual benefit strategy’s effectiveness, (b) the impact of the provision of the maintenance program, and (c) the effect of tardiness and earliness.Keywords: Cloud manufacturing system, Mutual benefit, Scheduling, Maintenance strategy, Order acceptance, FairnessSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In today’s competitive environment, customer expectations for customized products with optimal price and quality are rising, posing challenges for manufacturers. Resource sharing among companies is a potential solution, though geographical dispersion complicates integration. Advances in IT and IoT have accelerated globalization, allowing efficient resource and technology exchange. Cloud manufacturing has emerged as a service-based model that integrates distributed production capabilities to respond to customer needs1. The key players include the customer, the CMS, and the service provider.

In cloud manufacturing, limited capacity and strict deadlines make accepting all tasks impractical, as delays result in fines2. To maximize revenue, systems must selectively accept a subset of tasks based on processing and transportation costs. Therefore, integrating scheduling and order acceptance (SOA) is crucial for determining the optimal set of tasks. Despite its importance, research on order acceptance in the CMS is limited.

Scheduling is vital in CMS to optimize task sequencing and their allocation to service providers3–9. Vahedi-Nuri et al.10 integrated order acceptance, pricing, and scheduling to enhance utility and fairness via a multi-objective model. Zhou et al.11 minimized delivery times; Yuan et al.12 prioritized time, cost, quality, and capability dynamically. Chen et al.13 considered completion deadlines and tardiness, whereas Jafarnejad Ghomi et al.14,15 optimized cost, time, and tardiness using PSO/GA for load balancing and service selection. Collectively, these studies confirm that scheduling is a cornerstone of CMS efficiency. Given the rising resource heterogeneity in cloud manufacturing, optimal scheduling is not optional.

In CMS studies, the focus is on task assignments without sequence, known as planning-like studies presented by16–20. Delaram and Fatahi Valilai19 proposed a model to optimize task management. Zhou et al.21 used an improved genetic algorithm to address various customer tasks. Liu et al.22 introduced a multitasking model for CMS based on time, cost, and transportation. Ahn and Hoor23 aimed to minimize tardiness penalties and task completion costs while maximizing quality. Akbaripour et al.24 developed a mixed-integer planning model focusing on quality, time, and cost. Zhou et al.25 tackled dynamic multitasking challenges, and Cao et al.26 framed a single-task problem as a multicriteria decision problem with criteria including time, quality, cost, and service.

Customer satisfaction in the CMS hinges on final profit—revenue minus cost—where revenue directly depends on pricing strategies for customer tasks. The CMS must adopt optimized pricing approaches to balance profitability and satisfaction. Crucially, scheduling and pricing decisions are interdependent and must be addressed simultaneously to improve decision-making and ensure system success. Yet, research on their integrated optimization in CMS remains limited. Wang and Wen27 and Liu et al.28 addressed the integration of pricing into CMS scheduling, providing a foundational framework for this underexplored area.

A core CMS objective is coordinating tasks among service providers to meet customers’ specified delivery deadlines, as any deviation—tardiness or earliness—incurs escalating penalties. Therefore, accounting for these penalties is essential for optimizing operational efficiency and minimize system costs. Studies conducted by10,13–15,23 incorporated delay penalties into their respective models, highlighting the significance of addressing such costs in optimization problems.

Machines deteriorate over time and require preventive maintenance (PM) to reduce failure rates and associated costs. To enhance the efficiency of CMSs, it is crucial to integrate maintenance activities with production scheduling, given their interdependence. Additionally, machine failures necessitate repair and maintenance, making it essential to include maintenance time in task scheduling. While limited research addresses machine failures and availability constraints within CMSs, related studies in other fields provide valuable insights.

The interplay between scheduling, order acceptance, pricing, and maintenance in CMSs underscores the need for a comprehensive model integrating these dimensions. The existing literature predominantly examines these topics in isolation or overlooks them, with limited studies addressing their integration. The ultimate goal of the research is to maximize the mutual utility of the CMS and its customers by protecting the benefits of both parties and minimizing the gap between the top-performing customer’s utility and that of the rest, ensuring fair treatment for all. Problem definition

This study focuses on a CMS in which there is a collection of customers in different geographical locations. Besides, a number of independent factories have different companies in various locations that take part in the CMS. refers to the set of machines in factory f, which—like other manufacturing resources in CMS—is provided by platform-registered factories and companies. These factories possess specialized capabilities to fulfill customer tasks. Customers can explore the platform’s features and capabilities before initiating the planning phase, enabling them to submit tasks with detailed requirements and specifications. The term “task” is used interchangeably with “order” or “job,” referring to customer requests. Once a task is submitted, it is executed by machines within the factories. As multiple machines are capable of processing each task, scheduling becomes a critical component of the process.

The primary aim of SOA is to optimize the utilization of CMS’s limited resources through an acceptance/rejection policy for customer tasks. Decisions are based on factors such as due dates, processing times, task revenue, and associated costs, to maximize profits while balancing production costs and revenues. Tasks are rejected when their costs exceed the price that customers are willing to pay, or when CMS capacity constraints limit acceptance. Pricing follows a mutual benefit principle: the quoted price is set above the CMS’s incurred cost, which inherently reflects factory and machine-specific characteristics such as processing capability, location, and maintenance intensity, yet remains below the customer’s maximum acceptable cost. This dual-bound interval enables capacity-aware, selective order acceptance while preserving fairness and system sustainability. The proposed model incorporates customer satisfaction, which is based on minimizing total costs, and CMS satisfaction, focused on maximizing profits. Fairness is also emphasized to ensure system sustainability. Customer utility is defined as a normalized measure of satisfaction, reflecting how much the actual cost incurred falls below the customer’s maximum acceptable cost. Using the maximum observed utility as the fairness reference ensures that no customer is left significantly worse off relative to the best-served one, promoting equity without requiring absolute equality. CMS utility is similarly scaleless based on its profit relative to a feasible best-case benchmark, enabling meaningful, scale-free trade-offs among the three objectives. Thus, the model is guided by three objectives: maximizing the summation of customer utilities, minimizing disparities in customer utility, and maximizing CMS utility, fostering equitable and mutually beneficial outcomes for all stakeholders.

Scheduling tasks and maintenance activities are one of the most critical problems in a CMS. The majority of scheduling techniques for preventative maintenance assume machine availability throughout the planning period. However, this idealized view rarely holds in actual production settings, where machine availability is frequently disrupted by unexpected failures, planned PM interventions, or degradation from continuous use. Machines often experience downtime due to failures, PM activities, or operational fatigue. Consequently, determining an optimal schedule for maintenance activities is essential to minimize maintenance costs. The objective is to allocate tasks effectively across machines while optimizing PM activities. The integrated model seeks to minimize maintenance costs, including both PM expenses and costs associated with minimal repairs. Machine failures are classified into two categories in this problem:

Sudden breakdowns, often referred to as catastrophic failures, may occur in manufacturing equipment, resulting in considerable financial losses and risks. In these cases, minimal repairs return machines to an operational state but do not change the hazard rate value, leaving them in an “as bad as old” state.

Solution approach

The proposed model involves NP-hard optimization problems that often require significant computational time for exact methods, making them impractical for complex cases. Factors such as a high number of constraints, decision variables, and conflicting objectives contribute to this complexity. Therefore, we opted for multi-objective meta-heuristic algorithms, which are effective tools for finding suitable solutions in such challenging scenarios.

Multi-objective function optimization algorithm

A wide range of practical challenges in the industry involve multi-objective optimization, where conflicting objectives mean that improving one may worsen another. Unlike single-objective optimization, these problems yield a set of solutions rather than one. The aim is to obtain the Pareto front, i.e., the set of non-dominated solutions that outperform all other alternatives.

Non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm (NSGA-II)

NSGA-II is a high-performance evolutionary algorithm for solving multi-objective problems. Its key advantages include effective handling of multiple objectives, faster ranking solutions, and reduced computational complexity compared to earlier methods. Crowding distance is used to obtain a more uniform Pareto solution front. The offspring population is generated from the parent population in the initial step of NSGA-II through tournament selection. The two generated populations from crossover and mutation operators are merged to form the entire population of size 2N, where N denotes the population size. the combined population is then classified using the non-dominated sorting procedure. The new population is constructed by sequentially incorporating solutions from non-dominated fronts according to their dominance rank. When the final front exceeds the available capacity, a crowding sort procedure is applied to preserve diversity among the chosen individuals. In NSGA-II, solution representation plays a key role and is commonly formulated as a sequence (or vector) of discrete or continuous decision variables. Furthermore, a computational performance analysis is conducted. Several small-scale problem instances are successfully solved to optimality using GAMS with the epsilon-constraint method. However, as the problem size increases to a medium scale, the solution time dramatically increases, and in many cases, GAMS fails to find an optimal solution within a reasonable time frame (e.g., less than a few hours). As illustrated in Fig. 7; Table 5, NSGA-II consistently delivers high-quality solutions in significantly less time, even for large-scale instances. In contrast, GAMS struggles with scalability and is unable to solve medium- to large-sized numerical instances within an acceptable computational time. These findings justify the use of NSGA-II for complex, large-scale multi-objective optimization problems, especially when a practical trade-off between solution accuracy and computational efficiency is required.

Managerial insights

Based on the computational results and sensitivity analyses, the following practical recommendations are proposed for cloud manufacturing managers to enhance system profitability, and customer satisfaction:

Adopt a mutual-benefit pricing policy: Rather than pursuing maximum CMS profit unilaterally, set product prices based on a fair trade-off between customer total cost and CMS utility. This balanced approach prevents customer churn and builds long-term trust.

Adopt a mutual-benefit pricing policy: Rather than pursuing maximum CMS profit unilaterally, set product prices based on a fair trade-off between customer total cost and CMS utility. This balanced approach prevents customer churn and builds long-term trust.

Integrate PM into CMS scheduling: Managers should prioritize PM scheduling even when it adds short-term cost, because it significantly reduces unexpected failures, minimizes repair downtime, and stabilizes delivery performance, especially under tight due-date constraints.

Integrate PM into CMS scheduling: Managers should prioritize PM scheduling even when it adds short-term cost, because it significantly reduces unexpected failures, minimizes repair downtime, and stabilizes delivery performance, especially under tight due-date constraints.

Treat MR and PM as complementary, not alternatives: Removing MR and PM reduces profit. Both are essential; PM delays failure occurrence, whereas MR ensures continuity when failures inevitably occur. A layered reliability strategy is non-negotiable for high-availability cloud manufacturing.

Conclusions and directions for future research

This study proposes a multi-objective optimization model that integrates order acceptance, scheduling, maintenance activities, and pricing decisions within a cloud manufacturing environment. The model aims to maximize the utility of the CMS and customers under the existence of fairness among the customers. The applicability of the proposed model is illustrated through a numerical case study, while a NSGA-II-based metaheuristic is developed to obtain non-dominated (Pareto) solutions. Comparative studies using four criteria—average improvement percentage, misleading value, set coverage, and average distance from the ideal point—demonstrate the model’s effectiveness against existing literature. The comparisons showed that a combined optimization approach yields the highest utility. Implementing a maintenance program led to a 153.43% increase in cloud manufacturing profit, a 31.48% reduction in customer costs, and a 66.88% decrease in the total difference between the highest-utilizing customers and others. Preventive maintenance resulted in a 114.93% profit boost for cloud manufacturing, a 19.41% decrease in customer costs, and a 63.8% reduction in the total difference between the customer’s utility with the highest utility and other customers. Minimal repairs achieved a 101.87% increase in cloud manufacturing profit, an 11.01% reduction in customer costs, and a 63.14% decrease in in the total difference between the customer’s utility with the highest utility and other customers. Additionally, incorporating tardiness and earliness in the model showed a 143.5% growth in CMS profit, a 34.41% saving in customer costs, and a 68.96% reduction in utility differences between the customer’s utility with the highest utility and other customers.

Based on the identified research gaps, the following suggestions are made for future model development in this context:

In cloud manufacturing systems, treat each order as a task with multiple subtasks, recommending the use of logistics services to transition between them.

Future modeling should incorporate factors affecting customer satisfaction, such as early failures, product availability, and quality, in addition to life cycle costs.

Explore the static multi-machine scheduling problem in several levels of preventive maintenance, such as single machine, unrelated parallel machine, job shop, and flow shop environments.
