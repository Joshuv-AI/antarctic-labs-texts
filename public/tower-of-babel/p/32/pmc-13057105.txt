Extraction and characterization of biomaterials from pork bones: a potential alternative of pineapple by-product extract for commercial proteases
Authors: Van-Ba Hoa, Won-Seo Park, Jun-Sang Ham, In-Seon Bae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to develop cost-effective methods for extracting biomaterials from pork bones. Collagen and chondroitin sulfate (CS) were extracted from pork bones using a pineapple by-product extract (PE) at 5% (v/w) or commercial proteases (pepsin and Alcalase) under identical conditions. The use of PE resulted in a higher collagen yield than pepsin (3.77% vs. 1.42%). The CS concentration extracted with PE was comparable to that obtained using Alcalase (11.98 mg/g vs. 11.37 mg/g). Protein pattern analysis indicated that collagens extracted using either PE or pepsin were type I collagen. 1H NMR analysis showed that CS extracted using either PE or Alcalase contained both CS-C and CS-A units. Among 28 peptides identified, 7 peptides (Gly-Arg, Glu-Gly-Arg, Ala-Val-Gly, Leu-Ala, Leu-Val-Gly, Phe-Ala-Gly-Gly, and Gly-Pro-Leu-Gly-Pro-Hyp-Gly-Ala-Pro-Gly) and 4 peptides (Leu-Pro, Leu-Thr-Gly, Gly-Pro-Leu-Gly-Pro-Hyp-Gly-Ala-Pro-Gly, and Gly-Pro-Ala-Gly-Pro-Val-Gly-Pro-Val-Gly) were found only in the collagen extracted with pepsin and PE, respectively. Notably, peptide hydrolysates derived from PE-extracted collagen exhibited higher free radical scavenging and ferrous ion-chelating activities than those obtained from pepsin-extracted collagen. Overall, PE emerges as a cost-effective and efficient alternative to commercial enzymes for extracting biomaterials from pork bones.

Introduction

Pork is the second most consumed type of meat worldwide (Kim et al. 2024). Bones account for more than 10% of the weight of each pork carcass, resulting in the generation of substantial quantities of this by-product by the pork industry each year. However, only a limited proportion of these by-products is directly utilized for human consumption (MarSolís et al. 2021). In recent decades, advances in technology and materials science have stimulated efforts toward the comprehensive utilization of meat by-products (Liu et al. 2015). Collagen and chondroitin sulfate (CS) are among the most valuable biomaterials found in animal bones. Collagen, which accounts for approximately 30% of the total protein content in the animal body, plays a crucial role in maintaining the structural integrity and mechanical properties of cells and connective tissues (Liu et al. 2015). Among the various collagen types, type I collagen is abundantly present in bones, skin, and tendons. Structurally, it consists of two α1 chains (130–120 kDa) and one α2 chain (120–110 kDa) that intertwine to form a right-handed triple-helical structure (Liu et al. 2015). Due to their nutritional value and biological functionality, collagen-derived products from animal sources are now widely used in the food and pharmaceutical industries (Ahmad et al. 2024). Previous studies have demonstrated that daily collagen supplementation can significantly improve skin health and exert anti-aging effects (Addor et al. 2018).

Chondroitin sulfate (CS) is a negatively charged polysaccharide belonging to the family of sulfated glycosaminoglycans (GAGs). It is abundantly present in cartilage and the extracellular matrix of human and animal joints, where it is covalently linked to a core protein (Bougatef et al. 2024; Shi et al. 2014). CS is composed of repeating disaccharide units consisting of glucuronic acid (GlcA) and N-acetyl-D-galactosamine (GalNAc). Based on its sulfation pattern, CS is classified into several types, including GlcA-GalNAc-4-O-sulfate (CS-A), GlcA-GalNAc-6-O-sulfate (CS-C), GlcA-2-O-sulfate-GalNAc-6-O-sulfate (CS-D), and GlcA-GalNAc-4,6-O-disulfate (CS-E) (Shi et al. 2014). According to the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR), CS is recommended as a symptomatic slow-acting drug for the treatment of osteoarthritis (Shen et al. 2023). In many countries, CS is currently prescribed in combination with glucosamine sulfate to enhance the therapeutic efficacy in osteoarthritis management (Simental-Mendia et al. 2018). At present, approximately 80% of commercial CS products available on the market are produced in China, primarily sourced from marine organisms (Faheem et al. 2024).

To date, several methods have been developed for extracting collagen and CS from various animal sources. In principle, the extraction process involves the removal of unwanted components, weakening of chemical bonds, and disruption of structural linkages. Recently, to enhance extraction efficiency, commercial proteases have been widely employed to cleave cross-linkages among the α-chains (in the case of collagen extraction) and the covalent bonds between the core protein and CS (in the case of CS extraction) (Liu et al. 2015; Bougatef et al. 2024; Faheem et al. 2024; Cao et al. 2020). However, these commercial enzymes are often costly, leading to increased extraction expenses and higher prices for the final products. Therefore, the development of more efficient and cost-effective extraction methods is essential to reduce input costs, lower final product prices, and ultimately improve economic returns.

Plants such as papaya, pineapple, kiwifruit, and ginger are rich sources of diverse proteases, including papain, bromelain, actinidin, and zingibain. Among these, pineapple is a particularly abundant source of bromelain (Mohd-Azmi et al. 2023). Bromelain is widely distributed throughout various parts of the pineapple plant, including the crown and fruit (peel and core) (Venetikidou et al. 2025), and exhibits a strong capacity to hydrolyze polypeptide bonds (Gagaoua et al. 2021). Pineapple is extensively cultivated in many countries, ensuring a stable and sufficient global supply. Moreover, the pineapple processing industry generates millions of tons of by-products annually (Chaudhary and Singh 2024). Notably, the pineapple core, which comprises approximately 16% of total fruit by-products, is a rich source of bromelain (Chaudhary and Singh 2024). Utilizing enzyme extracts derived from pineapple by-products to convert meat by-products into value-added biomaterials for food and pharmaceutical applications represents a promising strategy for enhancing economic returns. This study aimed to develop efficient methods for utilizing pork bones and to characterize the chemical and biological properties of collagen and CS derived from this by-product.

Materials and methods

Materials

Pork bones (leg and backbone) from a commercial breed collected at a processing plant (Wanju, Korea) were used. Acetic acid, NaOH, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), NaCl, pepsin (from porcine gastric mucosa, EC 3.4.23.1, Cat. No. P7000), bromelain (from pineapple stem, EC 3.4.22.32, Cat. No. B5144), Alcalase 2.4 L (from Bacillus licheniformis, EC 3.4.21.62, Cat. No. P4860), type I collagen, chondroitin sulfate, chondroitinase ABC, D-glucuronolactone, DEAE-Sephadex, and reagents for bioactivity assays, including butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA) and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Chemicals used for agarose gel electrophoresis and SDS-PAGE were obtained from Bio-Rad (USA). Fresh pineapple fruits were purchased from a local supermarket in Jeonju (Korea).

Bone powder preparation

The bone samples were removed from fat, lean, and fascia, cut into thin pieces, soaked in liquid nitrogen for 5 min, and ground into fine powder using a grinder (Hanil Electric Co., Korea). The bone powders were dried at 40 °C for 24 h before use.

Pineapple by-products extract (PE) preparation, and proteolytic activity assay

After removal of the edible flesh, the pineapple by-product (core portion) was collected, ground using a blender, and filtered through a double layer of cheesecloth. The resulting filtrate was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 20 min, and the crude extract (hereafter referred to as pineapple extract, PE) was collected. The proteolytic activity of PE was assessed using the colorimetric methods described by Cruz et al. (2020) and Fissore et al. (2023). The assay conditions (e.g., temperature and pH) were similar to those used for collagen and CS extraction in this study. The molecular weight of bromelain present in the PE was determined by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). In addition, the dosage of PE (5%, v/w) used for collagen and CS extraction was selected based on preliminary evaluations at various concentrations to ensure extraction efficiency comparable to or greater than that achieved using commercial proteases.

Extraction of collagen

Pepsin-soluble collagen (PSC) extraction

PSC was extracted following the method described by Cao et al. (2020) with minor modifications. Briefly, bone samples (100 g) were treated with 900 mL of 0.1 M NaOH for 48 h to remove non-collagenous proteins. The treated bones were then rinsed with tap water for 5 min and drained. Subsequently, the samples were demineralized in 0.25 M EDTA at a 1:10 (w/v) ratio for 2 days, followed by rinsing with tap water and draining. The demineralized bones were soaked in 0.5 M acetic acid (pH 2.8) at a 1:10 (w/v) ratio for 48 h, after which pepsin was added at a concentration of 0.5% (w/w), and incubation was continued for an additional 48 h at 4 °C. Following enzymatic hydrolysis, the samples were centrifuged at 6000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and salted with NaCl to a final concentration of 2 M/L. The precipitated collagen was recovered by centrifugation at 17,000 × g for 50 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellets were redissolved in a minimal volume of 0.5 M acetic acid and dialyzed using a 7 kDa molecular weight cut-off membrane against 20 volumes of distilled water for 48 h. Finally, the dialyzed samples were freeze-dried and stored for subsequent analyses.

Pineapple by-products extract soluble collagen (PESC)

For PESC extraction, all procedures and conditions were identical to those used for the PSC extraction, except that PE was added at 5% (5 mL per 100 g of sample) relative to the sample weight. Following hydrolysis, collagen was collected and precipitated as described above. The extraction yields of PSC and PESC were calculated as the weight of freeze-dried collagen divided by the wet weight of the initial sample, multiplied by 100.

Extraction of chondroitin sulfate (CS)

CS was extracted following the method of Nakano et al. (2012) with suitable modifications. After washing with tap water and draining, bone samples (100 g each) were placed in zipper bags containing five volumes of 0.1 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 8.0), to which either 2 mL of Alcalase or 5 mL of PE was added. The bags were tightly sealed and incubated in a water bath at 55 °C for 8 h, a temperature considered optimal for bromelain activity (Gagaoua et al. 2021). After extraction, the samples were filtered through cheesecloth, and the filtrates were deproteinized by adding trichloroacetic acid to a final concentration of 7%, followed by incubation for 12 h at 4 °C. The solidified fat layer on the surface was manually removed, and the samples were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant containing CS was collected and precipitated with absolute ethanol to a final concentration of 60%, and left overnight. After centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min at 10 °C, the resulting pellets (crude CS) were collected, freeze-dried, and subjected to further purification. Characterization of pork bone collagen

Protein pattern of pork bone collagen

The protein pattern of collagen was analyzed using sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Specifically, the collagen at a concentration of 6 mg/mL was mixed with sample buffer and boiled for 5 min, and 15 µL of each sample was loaded on 4–20% precast gradient gels for 90 min at 110 V. The gels were stained with R-250 Coomassie brilliant blue for 1 h and destained for 2 h using the R-250 destaining kit.

Amino acid composition

The amino acid composition was analyzed after hydrolyzing 50 mg of lyophilized collagen in 5 mL of 6 N HCl at 110 °C for 24 h, using a high-speed amino acid analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi Co., Tokyo, Japan).

Collagen peptide hydrolysates preparation and antioxidant activity assays

Collagen hydrolysates were prepared by hydrolyzing the PSC and PESC (100 mg each) with the same PE to generate smaller peptides. Briefly, each sample was dissolved in 5 mL of ultrapure water, mixed with 200 µL of PE, and hydrolyzed at 50 °C for 5 h. Following hydrolysis, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected, freeze-dried, and used for antioxidant activity assays and peptide sequencing.

The antioxidant activities of the peptide hydrolysates were evaluated using DPPH radical scavenging and ferrous ion-chelating assays. Samples and the standard (butylated hydroxyanisole, BHA) were prepared at various concentrations, and the assay procedures were performed as previously described (Hoa et al. 2016). The Fe2+ chelating activity was calculated as: 1 − (Asample/Acontrol) × 100. EC50 values (the effective concentration at which ferrous ions are chelated or DPPH radicals are scavenged by 50%) were also calculated using interpolation from linear regression analysis.

Peptide sequencing

For sequencing, lyophilized collagen hydrolysates (1 mg) were dissolved in 1 mL of ultrapure water and then filtered through a 0.2 μm syringe filter. Samples (10 µ) were injected into a peptide mapping column (2.1 × 100 mm, 2.7 μm) (Agilent) connected to an ultra-performance liquid chromatography (Vanquish UPLC, Thermo Scientific, USA) coupled with a tandem mass spectrometer (Q Exactive Plus, Thermo Scientific, USA). Peptide separation was performed at 30 °C with a flow rate of 100 µL/min using mobile phases A (H₂O/formic acid, 100:0.1 v/v) and B (acetonitrile/formic acid, 100:0.1 v/v). Elution was carried out using a gradient of 2–98% solvent B over 31 min, followed by 2–98% solvent A over 15 min. The mass spectrometry settings included a full scan range of 200 to 1500 m/z, MS2 fragmentation at 200 m/z, an isolation window of 1.6 m/z, and resolutions of 70,000 and 17,500 for MS and MS2, respectively. Peptides were identified from mass spectra using de novo sequencing software (Thermo FreeStyle, version 1.8).

Purification and concentration determination of CS

The lyophilized crude CS (dissolved in 2 mL distilled water) was applied to a pre-packed DEAE-Sepharose column, and chromatography was performed using a Gradient Pump Liquid Chromatography (Bio-Rad). Elution was carried out at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min using a linear gradient of NaCl (50 mM to 2.0 M) in 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.3). Fractions (2 mL each) were collected using a fraction collector (Bio-Rad) and screened for CS using the glucuronic acid carbazole reaction (Cesaretti et al. 2003). Fractions containing glucuronic acid were combined, precipitated with alcohol, centrifuged at 3000×g to recover the CS (as described above), and lyophilized. Finally, the CS concentration (mg/g wet bone sample) was determined using the same procedure (glucuronic acid carbazole reaction), calculated by multiplying the glucuronic acid content by the values of molecular weight difference (Korea Food and Drug Administration 2008) as follows: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{CS}\, \mathrm{concentration} = \text{Glucuronic acid content} \times 1.1023 \times 2.593$$\end{document}

In which: 2.593 is the molecular weight of CS/molecular weight of glucuronic acid, and 1.1023 is the molecular weight of glucuronic acid/molecular weight of standard D-glucuronolactone.

Chondroitin sulfate migration pattern

To confirm the absence of other GAGs (e.g., dermatan sulfate), the CS samples were digested with chondroitinase ABC before electrophoresis. For digestion, CS samples (50 µg each) were dissolved in 50 µL of Tris–acetate buffer (pH 8.0), followed by adding 20 µL of chondroitinase ABC (0.1 mU/µL). The reaction was carried out at 37 °C for 5 h. Both non-digested and digested CS samples (10 µL each) were subsequently loaded onto a 0.6% agarose gel prepared with 0.04 M barium acetate buffer (pH 5.6) and electrophoresed at 150 mA for 180 min, as described by Im et al. (2009).

1H NMR spectroscopy

The structural integrity of CS was examined using a 1H NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany) operating at 600 MHz. For analysis, approximately 10 mg of lyophilized CS was dissolved in 1 mL of D₂O. Chemical shifts (ppm) were interpreted with Delta NMR Processing and Control Software (JEOL Delta v6.0, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS software (version 7.1; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The General Linear Model procedure was applied, with the extraction method treated as a fixed factor and the measured data as dependent variables. Differences between means were evaluated using Duncan’s multiple range test, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Yield of collagen and CS

In this study, the proteolytic activity of PE was determined under the same conditions used for collagen and CS extraction. The proteolytic activity of PE was 1.18 ± 0.02 and 3.33 ± 0.59 U/mg protein at 4 °C and 55 °C, respectively, indicating that enzymatic activity increased with temperature (Fig. 2A). Previous studies have similarly reported that bromelain exhibits activity over a wide range of pH values and temperatures (Gagaoua et al. 2021). SDS-PAGE analysis showed that the molecular weight of bromelain present in PE was nearly identical to that of commercial bromelain (Fig. 2B). Consistent with this finding, a previous study reported a molecular weight of 24.5 kDa for bromelain isolated from pineapple juice (Sarawanan et al. 2025).

Regarding collagen extraction, the use of PE resulted in a significantly higher yield (3.77%) compared with pepsin (1.42%) (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). Commercial proteases are commonly employed to disrupt intra- and intermolecular covalent cross-links in native collagen, thereby facilitating the release of collagen chains. Pepsin preferentially cleaves peptide bonds in the telopeptide regions of collagen molecules (Liu et al. 2015), whereas bromelain is capable of hydrolyzing peptide bonds at multiple sites, including those adjacent to aromatic, basic, and hydrophobic residues (Otte et al. 1997; Kumar et al. 2023). Under identical extraction conditions, the observed differences in collagen yield can therefore be attributed to variations in cleavage specificity between the enzymes. Previous studies have demonstrated that bromelain exhibits higher enzymatic activity than pepsin under the same hydrolysis conditions (Otte et al. 1997). Cao et al. (2020) reported collagen yields ranging from 4% to 11% from bovine bones extracted with pepsin, while Dhakal et al. (2018) reported a collagen yield of 9.41% from chicken feet extracted using papain.

For CS extraction, similar concentrations were obtained for both the CE and PE (11.37 vs. 11.98 mg/g, respectively) (Fig. 2E). CS typically exists as proteoglycans within the extracellular matrix, and cleavage of the covalent bonds between CS and core proteins is essential for its release (Shi et al. 2014). In practice, protein hydrolysis using commercial proteases is the most commonly employed approach for CS extraction. Nakano et al. (2012) extracted CS from chicken biomass using commercial proteases and reported a CS concentration of 213 mg/g sample.

Electrophoretic patterns of collagen and CS

The electrophoretic patterns of collagen and CS are shown in Fig. 3. Collagen extracted using either pepsin or PE exhibited two distinct bands with molecular weights of approximately 130–120 kDa and 120–110 kDa, corresponding to the α1- and α2-chains of type I collagen, respectively (Fig. 3A). This result is consistent with previous reports using various raw materials extracted with pepsin (Cao et al. 2020). In addition, several lower-molecular-weight bands ranging from approximately 75 to 20 kDa were observed in PESC. Bromelain is known to possess a broad range of cleavage sites (Otte et al. 1997). Therefore, based on the SDS-PAGE results, it can be inferred that bromelain in PE cleaves not only specific peptide bonds within the triple-helical region but also those in the telopeptide domain.

The electrophoretic pattern of CS is presented in Fig. 3B. A single band was observed for both CS samples extracted using either PE or Alcalase, and their migration patterns were similar to that of the CS standard. Moreover, no bands were detected after treatment with chondroitinase ABC, indicating the susceptibility of the extracted CS to enzymatic digestion. These results suggest that the CS products extracted from pork bones consist of pure glycosaminoglycans. Similarly, Nakano et al. (2012) reported that CS extracted from chicken biomass was susceptible to chondroitinase ABC digestion.

1H NMR of CS

The 1H NMR spectra exhibited signals mainly in the ranges of 1.8–2.1 ppm and 3.2–4.8 ppm (Fig. 4). As reported in previous studies, signals in the range of 1.9–2.1 ppm correspond to the methyl protons of GalNAc. Signals at approximately 3.3, 3.5, and 3.6 ppm are assigned to the H2, H3, and H5 protons of GlcA, respectively, whereas signals at 3.7, 3.8, and 4.05 ppm correspond to the H2, H6, and H4 protons of GalNAc. Signals observed at approximately 4.1 and 4.6 ppm are attributed to the H4 and H6 protons of GalNAc-4SO₄ and GalNAc-6SO₄, respectively (Bougatef et al. 2024; Im et al. 2009).

The 1H NMR spectra of CS extracted using either PE or Alcalase exhibited highly similar profiles. Both CS products showed characteristic signals at 1.97–2.02 ppm and at 3.33, 3.48, and 3.50 ppm, corresponding to the methyl proton of GalNAc and the H2, H3, and H5 protons of GlcA, respectively. Signals corresponding to the H2, H6, and H4 protons of GalNAc were observed at 3.76, 4.05, and 3.81 ppm, respectively. Notably, two signals at approximately 4.15 and 4.63 ppm were detected, which correspond to the H4 and H6 protons of GalNAc-4SO₄ and GalNAc-6SO₄, respectively. Based on these 1H NMR spectral features, it can be confirmed that the CS products extracted from pork bones using both PE and Alcalase contained CS-A and CS-C units (Shen et al. 2023). CS is recommended by EULAR as a symptomatic slow-acting drug for the treatment of osteoarthritis and has also been increasingly applied in the regeneration of bone, cartilage, joint, and hepatic tissues (Shen et al. 2023).

Amino acid composition and peptide sequences of pork bone collagen

The amino acid compositions of PSC and PESC are presented in Table 1. In both collagen products, Gly was the most abundant amino acid, accounting for approximately 23.70–24.17% of the total amino acids, followed by Pro (11.15–13.30%) and Glu (13.39–13.77%). These results are consistent with previous reports on collagen extracted from various sources using pepsin (Dhakal et al. 2018; Jeevithan et al. 2015). Gly, Pro, and Hyp are the three major amino acids that constitute the structural units of collagen molecules (Liu et al. 2015). No significant differences in amino acid composition were observed between the PSC and PESC (p > 0.05).

A total of 28 peptides were identified from the collagen hydrolysates (Table 2). Among these, 17 peptides were detected in both collagen products, whereas 7 peptides (Gly-Arg, Glu-Gly-Arg, Ala-Val-Gly, Leu-Ala, Leu-Val-Gly, Phe-Ala-Gly-Gly, and Gly-Pro-Leu-Gly-Pro-Hyp-Gly-Ala-Pro-Gly) were found exclusively in the PSC-derived hydrolysate, and 4 peptides (Leu-Pro, Leu-Thr-Gly, Gly-Pro-Leu-Gly-Pro-Hyp-Gly-Ala-Pro-Gly, and Gly-Pro-Ala-Gly-Pro-Val-Gly-Pro-Val-Gly) were identified only in the PESC-derived hydrolysate. These differences can be attributed to variations in enzyme cleavage specificity; for example, pepsin preferentially cleaves peptide bonds adjacent to Leu and Phe residues, whereas bromelain cleaves peptide bonds adjacent to aromatic, basic, and hydrophobic amino acid residues (e.g., Gly) (Kumar et al. 2023).

Bioactivities of peptides are strongly influenced by their amino acid composition and length. Hydrophobic amino acids (Ala, Val, Met, Ile, Leu, Phe, Pro, Trp, Tyr, and Gly) and aromatic amino acids (Phe, Trp, and Tyr) are known to play key roles in peptide bioactivities (Liu et al. 2016). In addition, short peptides consisting of 2–20 amino acids generally exhibit stronger biological activities (Brij et al. 2014). In the present study, all identified peptides possessed these key characteristics, suggesting their potential applications in the functional food and biomedical industries.

Antioxidant activity of collagen hydrolases

The antioxidant activities of collagen hydrolysates are presented in Fig. 5. Regarding the DPPH assay, the PESC-derived hydrolysate exhibited stronger antioxidant activity (EC₅₀ = 0.76 mg/mL) than the PSC-derived hydrolysate (EC₅₀ = 0.82 mg/mL). Similarly, the PESC-derived hydrolysate showed higher ferrous ion-chelating activity (EC₅₀ = 0.77 mg/mL) compared with the PSC-derived hydrolysate (EC₅₀ = 0.83 mg/mL). Nevertheless, the antioxidant activities of both hydrolysates were lower than that of the positive control (BHA; EC₅₀ = 0.65–0.69 mg/mL).

The antioxidant activity of protein hydrolysates is strongly influenced by amino acid composition. Aromatic and hydrophobic amino acids can interact with free radicals through electron or hydrogen donation and can chelate metal ions (Liu et al. 2016). In addition, peptides enriched with hydrophobic amino acids react more readily with lipid-soluble free radicals (Zhang et al. 2020). Peptides containing arginine at the C-terminus have also been reported to exhibit strong antioxidant activity (Li et al. 2007). According to the peptide sequencing results (Table 2), most identified peptides contained hydrophobic amino acid residues (e.g., Gly) at their terminal positions, and some possessed Arg at the C-terminus. These structural features may underlie the antioxidant activities observed in both PSC- and PESC-derived hydrolysates. Furthermore, differences in peptide composition, particularly in the content of hydrophobic amino acids such as Leu, Gly, Ala, Pro, Met, and Val, may contribute to the enhanced antioxidant activity observed in PESC-derived hydrolysates (Ranathunga et al. 2006). In addition, variations in proteolytic patterns arising from differences in protease size, active-site geometry, and tolerance to protein structure can lead to differences in peptide structural properties and/or released amino acid composition, ultimately resulting in differences in bioactivities (e.g., antioxidant activity) (Nongonierma and FitzGerald 2017). Lee et al. (2012) reported an EC₅₀ value of 0.632 mg/mL for the DPPH scavenging activity of duck skin gelatin hydrolyzed with collagenase.

Free radicals are generated through normal metabolic processes as well as environmental exposures, and excessive accumulation can disrupt the body’s antioxidant defense systems, leading to oxidative stress and the development of various diseases (Sarmadi and Ismail 2010). Accordingly, the consumption of natural antioxidants, including pork bone-derived products and their collagen peptide hydrolysates, may help protect against oxidative damage.

Conclusion

In this study, cost-effective methods were developed for extracting collagen and CS from pork bones using PE, and their efficiency was compared with that of commercial proteases. Under identical extraction conditions, PE demonstrated superior performance by yielding higher amounts of collagen and comparable CS concentrations relative to commercial proteases. A large number of peptides with lengths ranging from 2 to 12 amino acids were identified in the collagen hydrolysates. Notably, peptide hydrolysates derived from PESC exhibited significantly higher antioxidant activity than those derived from PSC. Collectively, these findings indicate that PE represents a cost-effective and efficient alternative to commercial proteases for extracting biomaterials from livestock bones. However, further studies are required to validate the biological activities of the identified collagen peptides and CS and to assess their potential applications in the functional food and pharmaceutical industries.
