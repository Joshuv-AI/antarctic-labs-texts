Sonographic Assessment of Effectiveness of Ultrasound-guided Interventions for Soft-tissue Pain in Foot and Ankle
Authors: Sarankumar Balasubramanian, Ankur Goyal, Shivanand Gamanagatti, Raju Sharma, D. Vijay Kumar, Vikrant Manhas, Vijay Kumar, Poonam Coshic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Ultrasound (US)-guided interventional procedures are used to relieve soft-tissue pain in the foot and ankle. We evaluated the utility of US (including Doppler and elastography) in the response assessment of patients undergoing interventional procedures for soft-tissue pain in the foot and ankle. We also compared the imaging parameters with clinical scoring systems (Visual Analog Scale [VAS] score [rest pain] and American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society [AOFAS] [function] scores).Methods:Consecutive patients with treatable soft-tissue conditions of the foot and ankle underwent US-guided interventional procedures. The patients were followed up at the 1st, 3rd, and 6th months after intervention for VAS (0–10) and AOFAS (0–100) scores and US (B mode, Doppler, and Elastography) parameters.Results:Forty-one patients were screened, of which 24 underwent US-guided procedures for 31 conditions. Group I (tendinopathies/plantar fasciitis) (n = 20) showed significant short-and long-term improvements after tendon fenestrations (improvements of 45.7%, 56.2%, and 78.9%, respectively, in VAS [P < 0.001] and 58.6%, 72.8%, and 97%, respectively, in AOFAS [P < 0.001] at 1, 3, and 6 months follow-up). The volume of the abnormal hypoechoic region showed a significant reduction (48.8% and 77.3% at the 3rd and 6th months, respectively, P < 0.001) with an increase of elasticity value by 113%, 174%, and 236% at the 1st, 3rd, and 6th months, respectively. There was a good correlation between changes in VAS and the volume of the hypoechoic area at first (P = 0.02 and rho = 0.50), third (P = 0.01 and rho = 0.58), and 6th months (P < 0.001 and rho = 0.77) follow-ups. In Group II (bursitis/ganglion) (n = 7), there was significant improvement of VAS (P < 0.05) and AOFAS (P < 0.05) after 1, 3, and 6 months of steroid injection (46.2% and 59.8%, 62.5% and 82.2%, and 77.7% and 109%, respectively). On imaging, the volume of the bursa/ganglion showed a considerable reduction of 50.7% at the 1st month postintervention (P < 0.01).Conclusion:US-guided fenestration was effective in treating tendinopathies, while steroid injections were helpful in bursitis/ganglion cysts. US parameters significantly improved and correlated with the clinical response.

INTRODUCTION

The incidence of foot and ankle soft-tissue pain is rising due to the increasing participation of the general population in recreational activities and causes significant morbidity.[1] Plantar fasciitis has a prevalence of about 10% in the general population and is associated with work-related long-standing hours, obesity (body mass index >30 kg/m²), and reduced ankle dorsiflexion.[123] Chronic tendinopathies and plantar fasciitis occur due to chronic repetitive micro-injuries and show degenerative changes with fewer inflammatory cells and an increase in the noncollagenous matrix.[4] Other conditions such as Achilles tendinopathy, retrocalcaneal bursitis, ganglion cyst, and sinus tarsi syndrome also cause significant pain and restriction of motion.

Novel interventional radiological treatment options like dry needling and platelet-rich plasma injection have shown significant responses in cases of plantar fasciitis and Achilles tendinopathies.[56] Local corticosteroid injections are a safe and effective treatment option for conditions such as ankle impingement, retrocalcaneal bursitis, plantar fasciitis, tenosynovitis, Morton’s neuroma, and sinus tarsi syndrome.[78]

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are often used to quantify the impact of an intervention.[9] A PROM tool for evaluating pain is the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) from 0 to 10. A clinical outcome score known as the American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) score is frequently used in the clinical research to evaluate the functional outcome of patients with foot and ankle problems.[10]

The role of ultrasonography is not only limited to diagnosing these conditions but is also helpful in guiding injection procedures.[11] Ultrasound (US)-guided interventions are more accurate as compared to blind injections in the delivery of treating agents to the exact site of the disease. US can also assess the distribution of drugs in the affected area.[12]

US-guided foot and ankle soft-tissue procedures have been the subject of a few research studies,[10111213141516171819] most of which have focused primarily on the clinical outcome. The radiological findings in the follow-up of these cases have not been properly studied. Advanced techniques like elastography (which may give insights into the improvement in stiffness of tendons) have also not been explored. The purpose of this study was to comprehensively evaluate the utility of US (including Doppler and elastography) in the response assessment of patients undergoing interventional procedures for soft tissue pain in the foot-ankle and compare the imaging parameters with clinical scoring systems (VAS score [rest pain] and AOFAS [function] scores).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A prospective study was conducted from July 2020 to December 2022, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, after approval by the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, New Delhi (Reference number. IECPG-350/22.07.2020, dated 05/08/2020), and informed consent was obtained from all recruited patients. Consecutive patients aged between 18 and 60 years, presenting with foot and ankle pain, were found to have treatable soft-tissue conditions with a lack of response to medical management/physiotherapy (tried for at least 3 months), were recruited, and underwent US-guided interventional procedures. The patients were followed up at the 1st, 3rd, and 6th months after the intervention for pain response using VAS (0–10), and functional assessment by AOFAS (0–100) scores and US (B mode, Doppler, and Elastography) parameters were also recorded. Patients who were not willing to participate in the study/come for follow-up, patients with significant cardiovascular, hepatic, or renal disease/uncontrolled diabetes mellitus/Previous history of local site trauma or surgery/known allergy to injection materials were excluded from our study. Vascular malformations and tumors, bone and joint interventions, and suspected abscesses for drainage were not included. US parameters such as long axis, short-axis dimension (LAD and SAD), and volume of the hypoechoic abnormal area of tendon/fascia/bursa/ganglion, presence of tear, thickness, cortical irregularity, probe tenderness, Doppler color signal, and mean elasticity value (MEV) of the abnormal area of tendon/fascia were assessed using a high-frequency linear US probe 4–15 MHZ on the Aixplorer Supersonic Imagine US machine, France. Care was taken to avoid anisotropy in the tendon/fascia by keeping the angle of the probe perpendicular to the visualized structure. The same abnormal area was measured at each follow-up by recording the exact site of the same at the time of presentation. Using shear-wave elastography, a Qbox with a 2-mm diameter region of interest (ROI) was placed over the most hypoechoic area of the tendinosis/fasciitis region at each follow-up. The same hypoechoic area was particularly assessed by placing an ROI in the subsequent follow-ups.

Injection technique and procedure

To numb the area of interest, the patients were instructed to apply a Toplap patch gel containing a mixture of lidocaine and prilocaine (2.5% and 2.5%) 45–60 min before the planned procedure. Under sterile aseptic precautions, using a high-frequency linear US probe 4–15 MHZ of the SuperSonic Imagine Aixplorer US machine, France, or linear probe 3–16 MHZ of Samsung Model RS80 EVO US machine, Germany, and by using a 23G hypodermic needle, a maximum of 4 ml of local anesthetics in a 1:1 ratio (2% lignocaine and 0.5% Bupivacaine) were injected into the skin and subcutaneous plane. With the same puncture, the needle was advanced into the abnormal soft tissue structures, and 15–20 fenestrations were done into the most hypoechoic part of the tendon/plantar fascia for tenotomy. The Achilles tendon was fenestrated along its longitudinal axis by a lateral approach along the side of the tendon (prone position of patient), while the plantar fascia was fenestrated along its short axis using a needle inserted along the medial aspect of the fascia. For conditions like bursitis and ganglion cyst, 40–80 mg of methylprednisolone acetate was injected after aspiration of contents, using an 18 G needle. Procedural information like needle size, number of fenestrations (in dry needling), amount of methylprednisolone injected, and postprocedure complications, if any, were noted down. If patients had been taking nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)/aspirin, the same was discontinued 5 days before the procedure and up to 1 month after the procedure.

Post-procedure instructions

Following injection, manual compression was applied for 5–10 min, and in the meantime, the patients were observed for any acute complications. All patients were advised to use RCE (rest, compression, and elevation) for the first 48 h postintervention. Paracetamol 500–650 mg was prescribed for discomfort rather than NSAIDs, as the latter may interfere with platelet function and potentially hamper the treatment mechanism. After 1 week, patients were sent to the physiotherapy unit for stretching and pain-relief exercises. The standard rehabilitation protocol was followed as per the pathology of the disease.

Follow-up

After the injection procedure, response evaluation was assessed through the VAS score (pain) and AOFAS score (function). This was done at 4 weeks, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks postintervention. US grayscale, Doppler, and Elastography were also done during these visits. Clinical response grading: None (<25%), mild (25%–50%), and excellent (>50%) improvement in VAS and AOFAS scoring. The proportion of patients showing significant response (>25% improvement in VAS/scoring systems) was also assessed at respective intervals. Similarly, imaging response was also graded as none (<25%), some (25%–50%), and marked (>50%) improvement in US imaging parameters such as thickness of tendon/fascia, volume of hypoechoic/hyperechoic part, LAD and SAD, and mean elasticity value as well. All of these parameters were assessed at all three interval follow-ups. Analgesic requirements were documented at each follow-up. If the patient took paracetamol more than or equal to 2 g/day for ≥3 days per week for more than 2 weeks, that would also be considered as treatment failure (in addition to <25% response in both functional scoring and VAS score). Complications including skin atrophy, weakness, and pain lasting more than 48/72 h after the procedure were also noted.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed by using Stata 14.0 software (Statacorp LLC, Texas, United States), and nonparametric tests were used. Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The Wilcoxon signed–rank test was used to compare the changes in quantitative variables from 1st, 3rd, or 6th month follow-ups with respect to the baseline. The Marginal homogeneity test was used to assess the changes in categorical variables like tenderness or echogenicity at two different time points. Fisher’s exact test was used to find the association between binary categorical variables like the probe tenderness. Spearman’s rank correlation test was used to estimate the correlation between quantitative variables since our sample was small and not normally distributed. P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 41 patients were screened, of whom 24 patients were finally included in our study [Flowchart 1]. These patients underwent US-guided procedures for 31 conditions in 26 feet (5 patients had 2 different conditions in the same foot). The mean age was 43.3 ± 14.1 years (range 21–72), and the male-to-female ratio was 0.8:1.

At the presentation, all patients had probe tenderness. Eighteen out of 20 patients showed resolution of probe tenderness 1 month after tenotomy, and none had any recurrence of probe tenderness at the short- and long-term follow-ups (P < 0.0001). The mean thickness of the abnormal tendon/fascia showed a significant reduction at the 6th-month follow-up (P < 0.001) [Table 1].

At the 3rd-month follow-up, the echogenicity of the tendon significantly improved from a hypoechoic area at baseline to a heteroechoic area in 11 out of 17 patients (P = 0.004) and to a hyperechoic tendon in 10 out of 17 patients (P = 0.0007) at the 6th-month follow-up.

At the start of the study, 17 patients had grade 0 color signals, and one patient each had grade 1, grade 2, and grade 3 color signals. Three out of 20 patients showed neovascularity at the 1st month follow-up, and the remaining 17 patients’ color signals had stayed unchanged (P = 0.39, insignificant). Only one patient demonstrated a grade 2 signal at the 6-month follow-up.

In the 1st, 3rd, and 6th months, the mean elasticity value (MEV) of the tendon/fascia showed a significant mean percentage increase of 113%, 174%, and 236%, respectively. Statistical significance was demonstrated only at the 3rd month (P = 0.03) follow-up. Elasticity color, as well as its dispersion (homogeneous/heterogeneous), showed no significant change at follow-ups as compared to baseline (P > 0.1). At 3 months, pain relief significantly correlated to the increase in the mean elasticity value (P = 0.018 and rho = 0.93) [Figure 4d]. In comparison to baseline, the corresponding MEV effect sizes (95% CI) in the 1st, 3rd, and 6th months were 0.777 (9.5, 64.4), 0.9 (50.4, 154.8), and 0.92 (83.8, 218), respectively. DISCUSSION

Majority of the patients (87.5%) showed some response to the US-guided treatment. The proportion of patients showing excellent response increased with time in both groups. In Group 1 (chronic tendinopathies), ultrasonographic guidance enabled precise targeting of the abnormal hypoechoic part, and significant improvements in both clinical (pain and function) and imaging parameters were seen postintervention. Housner et al.[13] also found significant improvement in the VAS score at both the 1st- and 3rd-month follow-ups. A long-term response was seen by Rastegar et al.,[19] who found a significant reduction in the VAS score from 6.41 ± 0.83–0.69 ± 0.93 at the 1-year follow-up (P < 0.005) following dry needling of chronic tendinopathy.

There was a significant correlation between clinical and imaging improvements at all follow-ups. However, the imaging response occurred at the long-term follow-up, while the clinical response occurred earlier, at even 1st-month follow-ups. Almost all patients demonstrated resolution of tenderness. The echogenicity and thickness of the abnormal tendon also improved with time. A notable progressive increase was observed in the mean elasticity value of the abnormal region of tendon/fascia from the 1st to the 6th-month follow-ups and correlated with pain improvement. Statistical significance was demonstrated only at the 3rd month (P = 0.03) follow-up. This result may reflect structural healing occurring within 3 months, which is why the elasticity value increased (i.e., regaining the normal stiffness of the tendon). However, we could not ascertain the lasting effect of this finding because of the small sample size.

Yeo et al.[17] observed insignificant changes in the AP diameter of the tendon with a mean thickness of 9.2 ± 2 mm and 9.2 ± 1.9 mm at pre-and post-procedure, respectively (P > 0.05). At 6 and 12 weeks after dry needling, El Mallah et al.[18] discovered a similar negligible improvement in plantar fascia thickness.

There is a paucity of the literature regarding the correlation between clinical and imaging response after US-guided soft-tissue interventions of the foot and ankle. The few previous studies on this subject have included only a few US parameters like the thickness of the plantar fascia or the LAD of ganglion cyst; however, we did a comprehensive US assessment including Doppler and elastography apart from grayscale parameters such as LAD, SAD, volume of abnormal area, and thickness. No prior studies have assessed the elastographic changes in chronic tendinopathies of the foot and ankle. In our study, we discovered that the volume of the abnormal hypoechoic region of tendinopathy positively correlated with the alleviation of pain at all three follow-ups. A significant positive correlation was also observed between the decrease in pain at the 6th month and the reduction in the LAD of the hypoechoic region of tendinopathy. There was a significant correlation between the increase in mean elasticity value and pain alleviation at the 3rd-month follow-up.

Unlike blind injection, US guidance allowed us to precisely deliver steroids to the affected region in cases of retrocalcaneal bursitis and ganglion cysts. In Group II, after methylprednisolone injection, the clinical scoring system considerably improved at short- and long-term follow-ups. The VAS score’s short-term response was compatible with the study conducted by Boone et al.,[20] where they found an excellent response (reduction by 7–10 points) in 37% of cases and a good response (drop by 4–6 points) in 26% of cases at short-term follow-up poststeroid injection in retrocalcaneal bursitis.

The size of the bursitis/ganglion cysts demonstrated short-term improvements. The functional improvement and longest dimension/volume of bursitis/ganglion cysts showed significant inverse correlation at the short-term follow-ups. This implied that steroids were beneficial in the short term for reducing pain and improving structural abnormality; however, more patients need to be studied to assess the long-term effects of steroid injection in these situations. At initial follow-ups, patients showed resolution of probe tenderness and remained stable up to 6 months after injection.

US-guided foot and ankle soft-tissue procedures have only been the subject of a small number of research studies, the majority of which focused primarily on the clinical outcome. The radiological findings in the follow-up of these cases have not been properly studied. Ours is the only study to date that has comprehensively assessed grayscale US parameters such as LAD, SAD, volume of abnormal region, color Doppler grades, elastography color, and mean elasticity values (MEV) after US-guided interventions. In addition, we discovered that these imaging parameters showed improvement that significantly correlated with the clinical response parameters. In our study, the clinical improvement preceded imaging changes in most of the cases. This highlights the fact that structural improvement takes time even though patient well-being may improve earlier. The implications of this finding are that the postprocedure rehabilitation is equally important to have a sustained relief. Our study’s limitations include a small sample size, lack of a control group//quality of life measures, and lack of long-term follow-up. Because of small heterogeneous nature, no definite conclusion could be drawn from Group III. Our study provides an objective US basis of clinical improvement with foot and ankle interventions. The results are especially important for those patients who may not show an early clinical response. If such patients show US improvement, then a wait and watch approach may be more appropriate rather than proceeding onto next intervention/surgery.

CONCLUSION

Dry needling/tenotomy of chronic tendinopathies was found to be effective in relieving pain and tenderness, as well as improving functional mobility in the foot and ankle. In the long run, tenotomy was found to improve the US morphology as well as the elasticity values of the damaged tendon/fascia, although the clinical benefit was seen earlier than the imaging response. In diseases such as retrocalcaneal bursitis and ganglion cysts, US-guided steroid injection provided a good short-term relief, with imaging response and clinical improvement occurring at the same time. Clinical improvement was found to correlate with the sonographic reduction in the size of the abnormal region in both groups.
