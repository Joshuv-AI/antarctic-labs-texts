Growth Hormone Therapy Restores Fertility in a Woman With Isolated Growth Hormone Deficiency From Chronic Sheehan Syndrome
Authors: Yeung-Ae Park, Alon Talmor, Rita Upreti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSheehan syndrome is a rare condition of pituitary necrosis following postpartum hemorrhage, hypovolemia, and/or associated shock, presenting with lactation failure and acute hypopituitarism immediately postpartum; chronic hypopituitarism can manifest months to years later. We present a patient with chronic Sheehan syndrome with isolated growth hormone (GH) deficiency presenting with secondary infertility in whom GH replacement led to natural conception, demonstrating reversibility of infertility. A 38-year-old female patient was referred for secondary infertility on a background of postpartum hemorrhage and inability to breastfeed postpartum 4 years earlier. During workup for secondary infertility, her investigations revealed an isolated GH deficiency, confirmed on a glucagon stimulation test, and a partially empty sella and a thin, 1.6-mm pituitary gland on magnetic resonance imaging, supportive of chronic Sheehan syndrome. Other fertility investigations were unremarkable. Before GH replacement, the patient had 1 unsuccessful cycle of in vitro fertilization. After 2.5 months of GH replacement, the patient achieved natural conception. Individuals with a history of postpartum hemorrhage, agalactorrhea, and symptoms potentially indicative of hypopituitarism should be investigated for chronic Sheehan syndrome. In pituitary pathology, GH deficiency should be considered as a potential contributor to infertility, which can be treated and potentially reversed with GH replacement.Keywords: growth hormone deficiency, hypopituitarism, infertility, Sheehan syndrome

Introduction

Sheehan syndrome is a rare condition of pituitary necrosis following postpartum hemorrhage, hypovolemia and associated shock [1], classically presenting with lactation failure immediately postpartum [2]. Although delayed presentations of chronic Sheehan syndrome occurring months to years later are more common in developed countries where most births take place in hospitals [1], acute hypopituitarism following postpartum hemorrhage remains a significant issue in developing countries where home births are more frequent [3]. The typical order of affected anterior hormones in most pituitary conditions, including Sheehan syndrome, is growth hormone (GH), prolactin, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) [1]. We present a case of isolated GH deficiency secondary to chronic Sheehan syndrome presenting with secondary infertility and successful natural conception upon GH replacement.

Case Presentation

A gravida 3, para 1, 38-year-old woman was referred for secondary infertility with previously all spontaneous natural conceptions. The patient and her partner presented with difficulty conceiving for more than 12 months despite regular, timed intercourse. Four years prior, her vaginal delivery was complicated by a retained placenta requiring manual removal with a postpartum hemorrhage of 900 mL. Following the procedure, a hemoglobin of 83 g/L (SI: 5.15 mmol/L) (reference range, 98-137 g/L [SI: 6.08-8.50 mmol/L]) was recorded, and this was further complicated with an anaphylaxis to rocuronium, requiring inotropic support in intensive care. Notably, she was unable to produce breastmilk postpartum and had irregular menstruation with 40- to 80-day cycles in contrast to her previous regular 28-day cycles. She had noted decreased energy levels and a “flat” affect since this time. Four months postpartum, the patient was diagnosed with primary autoimmune hypothyroidism. Resumption of menses and lack of symptoms suggestive of hypocortisolism were reported to be reassuring and no other pituitary panels were performed immediately postpartum.

Diagnostic Assessment

During evaluation of secondary infertility, on examination, her blood pressure was normal without a postural drop. Her weight was 67 kg, and her body mass index was 26.2 kg/m2. Her biochemistry revealed normal prolactin 4.7 ng/mL (SI: 101 mIU/L) (reference range, 2.8-28.2 ng/mL [SI: 60-600 mIU/L]), and intact hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axes (Table 1). The patient was euthyroid on thyroxine 75 micrograms daily. At the time of her primary thyroid diagnosis, her TSH was 9.98 μIU/mL (SI: 9.98 mIU/L) (reference range, 0.4-4.0 μIU/mL [SI: 0.4-4.0 mIU/L]) with free thyroxine (fT4) of 0.44 ng/dL (SI: 5.6 pmol/L) (reference range, 0.70-1.48 ng/dL [SI: 9.0-19.0 pmol/L]) and positive anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies (>1300 IU/mL [SI: > 1300 kIU/L]) (reference range, < 60 IU/mL [SI: < 60 kIU/L]). Interestingly, her insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) levels were low on repeat tests at 83.5 ng/mL (SI: 6.39 nmol/L) (reference range, 134.1-468.6 ng/mL [SI: 10.27-35.88 nmol/L]) and 77.1 ng/mL (SI: 5.9 nmol/L) (reference range, 185.4-482.3 ng/mL [SI: 14.2-36.9 nmol/L]).

A glucagon stimulation test confirmed GH deficiency with the maximum stimulated GH of 0.68 ng/mL (SI: 0.02 nmol/L) (reference range, > 3.0 ng/mL [SI: > 0.22 nmol/L]). Subsequently, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the pituitary revealed a partially empty sella and a thin, 1.6-mm pituitary gland (Fig. 1). Her ferritin level was unremarkable. Other investigations, including sexually transmitted infection screen, hemoglobin A1C, androgen concentrations, karyotype, pelvic ultrasound, and partner's investigations, including semen analysis, were unremarkable. Her anti-Müllerian hormone was 0.88 ng/mL (SI: 6.3 pmol/L) (reference range, 0.14-7.45 ng/mL [SI: 1.0-53.5 pmol/L]) and antral follicle count was 10 on her pelvic ultrasound scan. Given the previous history of postpartum hemorrhage and anaphylaxis requiring inotropic support, GH deficiency, and partial empty sella, the patient was diagnosed with an isolated adult-onset GH deficiency likely secondary to Sheehan syndrome. No other reversible pathologies for infertility were found. The patient's hypothyroidism was thought to be a primary thyroid pathology given its trajectory, response as expected in TSH to thyroxine dose adjustments, and the predicted order of affected hormone axes in Sheehan syndrome. However, in patients with suspected central hypothyroidism, ACTH deficiency should be excluded before thyroxine treatment [4].

Treatment

The patient was commenced on somatropin 0.2 mg subcutaneous injection daily for GH replacement. The patient's IGF-1 level gradually increased, reaching 9.1 nmol/L after almost 8 weeks of therapy and was in the reference range for her age and sex in early pregnancy. With GH replacement, the patient noticed a marked improvement, particularly in energy levels, mood, and general well-being.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Prior to GH replacement, the patient had tried for conception with timed intercourse for 2 years with no success. With ovarian stimulation, the patient had one cycle of in vitro fertilization (IVF) with 11 eggs retrieved and 3 eggs fertilized; however, none reached the blastocyst stage. After 2.5 months of GH replacement, the patient achieved natural conception. Repeat pituitary panel since GH replacement was unremarkable. Given the anticipated increase in placental GH production, the patient's GH replacement was decreased at 20 weeks gestation and ceased at 24 weeks gestation. Her baby was born healthy at term. GH replacement therapy was recommenced postpartum.

In summary, we present a rare case of isolated GH deficiency secondary to chronic Sheehan syndrome from postpartum hemorrhage 4 years prior, presenting with secondary infertility and successful natural conception upon GH replacement.

Discussion

Sheehan syndrome is a rare condition of pituitary necrosis, typically following postpartum hemorrhage, hypovolemia, and associated shock. It affects up to 5/100 000 births and is more common in developing countries [1]. During pregnancy, the pituitary volume increases principally due to hyperplasia of lactotrophs [5] and can lead to compression of its vasculature [6]. Consequently, hypovolemia makes anterior cells susceptible to ischemia, causing partial to pan-hypopituitarism [7]. The typical order of affected anterior hormones, from early to late, is GH, prolactin, FSH, LH, ACTH, and TSH [1], as reflected in our patient. In contrast, the posterior pituitary has a separate blood supply less sensitive to hypovolemia or associated shock [7]. Hence, arginine vasopressin deficiency is rare [8].

The diagnosis of Sheehan syndrome is not always immediately postpartum. In the acute setting, failure to lactate is common [2]. Hypocortisolemia may be challenging to distinguish from hypotension and tachycardia associated with hypovolemia and associated shock from postpartum hemorrhage. However, hypocortisolemia can be suggested by hyponatremia and hypoglycemia [1]. Prompt management with corticosteroids is crucial, and the possibility of Sheehan syndrome should always be considered in women with postpartum hemorrhage. In our patient, her need for inotropic support was related to anaphylaxis, and she has never had any sign of hypocortisolism.

Radiologically, acute Sheehan syndrome is characterized by an enlarged pituitary with an acute central infarction without hemorrhage [9]. In the chronic setting, Sheehan syndrome can be diagnosed due to symptoms of hypopituitarism, such as fatigue, weakness, weight loss, cold intolerance, and concentration difficulties, months to years after the pathologic hemorrhage [1]. On an MRI, an empty sella is characteristic in 70% of patients, and a partially empty sella (as seen in our patient) is seen in 30% [10]. Patients with Sheehan syndrome are treated with lifelong replacement of the deficient hormones. Autoimmune hypophysitis, the most common cause of hypophysitis, should be considered as a differential in postpartum women with hypopituitarism in the presence of autoimmune disease, immune checkpoint inhibitor use [11] and pituitary stalk thickening on imaging [12]. In our patient, although there was a history of Hashimoto thyroiditis, there was no evidence of pituitary stalk thickening to suggest autoimmune hypophysitis.

Infertility is the failure to establish a clinical pregnancy after 12 months of regular and unprotected timed sexual intercourse [13]. Overall, 1 in 6 couples experience infertility [14]. Its etiology is broad; one-third is due to female reproductive factors, one-third to male reproductive factors, and one-third to combined male and female reproductive factors or unknown [14]. In women with unexplained infertility, in a small cohort study of 25 patients, 2 patients (8%) were found to have GH deficiency [15]. In our experience, this percentage is considerably higher than typically observed clinically, and further research is needed to confirm these findings.

Nevertheless, GH deficiency is likely an under-recognized etiology of infertility. GH and IGF-1 have been associated with signaling for reproductive function, ranging from folliculogenesis, ovarian steroidogenesis, oocyte maturation, and embryo implantation [16]. GH upregulates the expression of FSH and LH receptors, thereby increasing the responsiveness of granulosa cells to gonadotropins [17]. Additionally, GH has been associated with promoting endometrial receptivity and pregnancy via implantation facilitation [18]. Furthermore, GH gene mutation and GH deficiency have been associated with diminished ovarian reserve, poor ovarian response during assisted reproductive technology, and implantation failure [16]. Reduced blastocyst aneuploidy has been associated with GH supplementation during ovarian stimulation in women with advanced maternal age [19], and in women younger than 40 with prior pregnancy failures with aneuploidy rates higher than 50% [20], without a history of GH deficiency. The hypothesized mechanism is that GH supplementation may reduce meiotic division errors associated with failed fertilization [20] and aneuploidy during oocyte development, thereby decreasing the incidence of aneuploid blastocysts [19]. A Japanese patient with pituitary dwarfism achieved successful pregnancy after 4 years of GH therapy recommencement in adulthood [21]. In 4 eugonadal patients with GH deficiency and infertility, GH replacement therapy achieved successful conception, suggestive of the clinical role of GH in oocyte fertilization and establishment of pregnancy [22]. In our patient, while she was GH-deficient, none of the fertilized oocytes progressed to the blastocyst stage, and preimplantation genetic tests were unable to be performed. Our patient's ability to naturally conceive after a short duration of GH replacement despite a significant history of secondary infertility, including the unsuccessful cycle of IVF, highlights the potential for reversibility of infertility with GH replacement in patients with GH deficiency. In fertility treatment, the use of adjuvant GH therapy in patients without GH deficiency, with an attempt to improve age-related decreases in assisted reproductive technique efficiency [16] is controversial and not our practice. In individuals with established GH deficiency and infertility, however, GH replacement is likely to enhance fertility outcomes [15], as demonstrated in our patient.

In addition to its fertility benefits, GH replacement can lead to multisystem health benefits. Adult-onset GH deficiency results in a clinical picture characterized by insulin resistance, with atherogenic lipid profiles, central adiposity, weakness, osteoporosis, fatigue, depression, and a 2-fold increase in premature cardiovascular mortality [23]. In these individuals, GH replacement has been demonstrated to increase lean body mass and bone mass, reduce fat mass, and improve physical function, cardiovascular risk profile, mood, and energy, resulting in reduced use of health services [24]. It is important to recognize that a condition of untreated GH deficiency can mask other concurrent forms of hypopituitarism, particularly central hypothyroidism and hypocortisolism, due to its multifaceted interaction and overlapping clinical presentation with other pituitary hormone deficiencies [25]. Thus, it is important to re-evaluate the pituitary hormone panel upon GH replacement and for titration of GH therapy [26].

In conclusion, individuals with a history of postpartum hemorrhage, agalactorrhea, and symptoms potentially indicative of hypopituitarism should be investigated for Sheehan syndrome. In pituitary pathology, not only HPG, HPA, and hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axes dysfunction, but also GH deficiency, should be considered as potential contributors to infertility. GH deficiency should be considered in such patients, which can be treated with GH replacement with potential improvement of fertility.
