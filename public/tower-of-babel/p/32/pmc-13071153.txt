SHIP-1 adapter functions mediate recruitment of FCRL1 to the BCR and inhibition of ERK
Authors: Malory M Wolfe, Rut Tallon Arranz, Elizabeth L Zenni, Timothy J Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFCRL1 is a plasma membrane coreceptor on B cells that has been shown to potentiate B cell receptor (BCR)–driven calcium flux and negatively regulate ERK phosphorylation in a GRB2 and tyrosine-dependent manner. Of the proteins that associate with FCRL1, the recruitment of the inositol phosphatase SHIP-1 is GRB2 dependent, implicating SHIP-1 in FCRL1-mediated ERK regulation. Using immunoprecipitation and Western blotting, it was found that a proline-rich region in the C-terminus of SHIP-1, rather than its N-terminal SH2 domain, mediates recruitment of SHIP-1 to Y281 in FCRL1 in a manner dependent on GRB2. Interestingly, Y281 and GRB2 were also required for FCRL1 colocalization to the BCR. Translational fusions between tyrosine-mutated FCRL1 and the SH2 domain of SHIP-1 were sufficient to drive both colocalization of FCRL1 with the BCR as well as the ERK-inhibitory activity of FCRL1. Our data are consistent with a function for SHIP-1 as an adapter between FCRL1 and the BCR signalosome, and that this adapter function is responsible for BCR/FCRL1 colocalization after BCR stimulation and modulation of ERK signaling.

Introduction

B cell receptor (BCR) signaling is a critical process that regulates the activation, differentiation, and survival of B cells, which are essential for an effective immune response. When a B cell encounters its cognate antigen, the B cell receptor (BCR) undergoes aggregation, triggering a series of intracellular signaling events.1,2 Upon aggregation, the signal is propagated through the activation of Src-family tyrosine kinases, FYN, LYN, and BLK, in B cells.3 These phosphorylate immunoreceptor tyrosine–based activation motifs in the cytoplasmic tails of Igα and Igβ, resulting in the recruitment of SYK and the propagation of downstream signaling.4–6 One of the key signaling pathways activated downstream of BCR activation is the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK signaling pathway. The phosphorylation of ERK results in the downstream activation of transcription factors like ELK1, CREB, and PAX5, which play an essential role in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival by activation of essential gene transcription.7–9

Tight regulation of downstream signaling is essential for B cell development, selection, and activation to mount an effective response and minimize the risk of autoimmunity or lymphoma. One such regulator is the SHIP-1, which has been described to negatively regulate BCR signaling by catalyzing the PI-3K product PtdINs-3,4,5P3 into PtdIns-3,4P2.10,11 The structural elements of SHIP-1 include a SH2 domain on the N terminus, the inositol phosphatase domain flanked by a PH-related domain and an overlapping C2 domain, and a C-terminus comprising an unstructured proline-rich region (PRR).12 In B cells, SHIP-1 is recruited through its SH2 domain to the phosphorylated immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif in FcγRllb, negatively regulating BCR signals when antigen-specific antibody is already present.11,13 In addition to recruitment by inhibitory receptors, SHIP-1 can be recruited to the membrane through adapter proteins including SHC, GRB2, CIN85, DOK-3, and others,14–17 involving its SH2 domain as well as other motifs within its C-terminal unstructured region.

We previously demonstrated the recruitment of SHIP-1 to FCRL1 in a GRB2-dependent manner.18 FCRL1 is a member of a family of immunoregulatory proteins homologous to classical Fc receptors.19,20 Human FCRLA, FCRL3, FCRL4, and FCRL5 have all been observed to interact directly with immunoglobulins, either on the surface or within B cells,21–25 and interactions between HLA-DR and FCRL6 have also been observed.26 The remaining members of the family, FCRL1, FCRL2, and FCRLB, remain orphan receptors. Human FCRL1 comprises 3 extracellular Ig-like domains and 2 immunoreceptor tyrosine–based activation motifs, and has been shown to enhance calcium flux and proliferation after BCR engagement.27–29 In mice, FCRL1 has been shown to enhance calcium flux and formation of the BCR signalosome under the same conditions in which it can negatively regulate ERK activation, showing that it is doing more than simply modulating BCR signal strength.18,27,30 This study sought to determine whether the inhibition of ERK activation was the result of SHIP-1 recruitment to the BCR. Surprisingly, we found that the adapter functions of SHIP-1, rather than the phosphatase activity, account for the inhibition of ERK by FCRL1 in addition to recruitment of FCRL1 to the BCR cap.

Methods

SHIP-1 translational fusion proteins

Translational fusions of SHIP-1 domains and eGFP were generated for immunoprecipitation experiments. Based on reference sequence (NM_010566.3), eGFP was fused to the C-terminus of the SH2 domain of SHIP-1 (amino acids 1–120), and the N-termini of the phosphatase (amino acids 108–886) and the PRR (amino acids 900–1191). Fusions were prepared by overlap extension polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and ligated into pLenti-CMV-GFP-Puro vector (gift from Eric Campeau and Paul Kaufman; Addgene #17448),31 replacing the eGFP with the fusion. Sequences were verified prior to use by Sanger or Oxford Nanopore sequencing. A20.IIA1.6 (hereafter referred to as A20) cells were transduced as previously described,18 then placed under selection with 3 µg/mL puromycin prior to sorting based on GFP expression with a BD FACSMelody Cell Sorter. Stable GFP expression was later confirmed through flow cytometry on BD LSR-II flow cytometer, and sizes of constructs were verified through Western blotting. Fusions of the phosphatase domain with GFP exhibited instability and toxicity and were not used for further experiments.

A site-directed mutated FCRL1 with all cytosolic tyrosines changed to phenylalanine (6YF)18 was used to generate translational fusions of SHIP-1 protein domains and FCRL1. Overlap extension PCR was used to fuse the C-terminus of FCRL1.6YF to the SHIP-1 SH2 domain (amino acids 1–120), phosphatase domain (amino acids 108–886), and PRR (amino acids 900–1191). PCR products were ligated into pLenti-CMV-GFP-Neo vector (gift of Eric Campeau and Paul Kaufman; Addgene #17447).31 A20 cells lacking endogenous FCRL1 expression (A20ΔFcrl1)18 were transduced and grown under selection in 1 mg/mL G418. Transduced cells were stained for FLAG epitope tag expression (clone L5; BioLegend) and sorted with a BD FACSMelody Cell Sorter. Protein surface expression was regularly verified through flow cytometry and fusion protein sizes were confirmed by Western blotting.

Co-immunoprecipitations and Western blotting

Transduced A20 cells (2 × 107 per sample) were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min in unsupplemented RPMI 1640 with 2 mM pervanadate. Cells were lysed in PBS containing 1% Triton X-100 (v/v), 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM orthovanadate, and 1× SIGMAFAST Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). Streptavidin-coated magnetic beads (120 µg per sample; New England Biolabs) were preincubated for 1 h at 4 °C with 3 µg of biotinylated anti-FCRL1 (Miltenyi Biotec), anti-human IgG1 (isotype control; Miltenyi Biotec), or anti-CD22 (Miltenyi Biotec). Lysates were incubated with antibody bound beads for 2 h at 4 °C with rotation. Samples were washed 3 times in lysis buffer at 4 °C. Samples were then eluted by resuspending beads in 1× sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) loading dye for 10 min at 60 °C. For immunoprecipitations of GRB2, transduced A20 cells (1.5 × 107 per sample) were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min in unsupplemented RPMI 1640 with 2 mM pervanadate, and cells were lysed as described previously. Protein G agarose resin (GenScript) was preincubated for 1 h at 4 °C with 3 µg of either anti-Grb2 (C-7; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) or rat anti-mouse IgG1 A85-1 (Isotype control; BD Pharmingen). Lysates were incubated with antibody bound beads for 2 h at 4 °C with rotation. Samples were washed 3 times in lysis buffer at 4 °C. Samples were then eluted in 1× SDS-PAGE loading dye for 10 min at 60 °C.

For Western blotting, samples in SDS-PAGE loading dye were denatured and reduced by heating at 95 °C with 2-mercaptoethanol. Samples were loaded on a SurePAGE 4% to 12% Bis-Tris Plus gel (GenScript), electrophoresed in Tris-MOPS-SDS running buffer, transferred to a 0.2 µm nitrocellulose membrane, and immunoblotted. Immunoblots were probed overnight at 4 °C with 0.5 µg/mL anti-GRB2 (Cell Signaling Technology), 0.5 µg/mL anti-FCRL1 (clone 350G10—described previously18 anti-GFP (Cell Signaling Technology), and biotinylated anti-CD22 (clone E8I1F; Cell Signaling Technology) diluted in 1× Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 with 1% bovine serum albumin. Horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 hour at 4 °C, including 0.1 µg/mL anti-mouse IgG1 (Cell Signaling Technology), 0.1 µg/mL anti-rat IgG (Cell Signaling Technology), and 0.1 µg/mL streptavidin (BioLegend), were used according to the isotype and host species of primary antibodies. Blots were developed using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged using a ChemiDoc gel imager (Bio-Rad). Densitometry was performed using Image Lab (Bio-Rad).

Intracellular flow cytometry

For measurement of ERK1/2 activation in transduced cell lines, 2 × 106 A20 cells per sample were equilibrated to 37 °C for 20 min. Cells were then stimulated with 400,000 Dynabeads M-450 Tosylactivated magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) conjugated to anti-κ light chain antibody (187.1; Bio X Cell) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were stimulated at 37 °C for 10 min then fixed in 1.4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min. Cells were stained with Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated anti-phos-ERK 1/2 (clone 269434; R&D Systems) for 30 min at room temperature. Flow cytometry data were acquired on an LSRII flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo version 10 software (BD Biosciences). Geometric mean fluorescence intensities were calculated following gating on live, singlet populations.

Confocal microscopy

Primary B cells for imaging were obtained by disaggregating cells from the spleen of wild-type 129/SvImJ and Fcrl1−/− mice. A20 B cells were generated as described previously or previously.18 Prior to activation, primary cells were treated with anti-CD16/CD32 blocking antibody (2.4G2, Bio X Cell). Cells (2 × 106 per sample) were activated with 2 µg/mL purified anti-mouse IgD (11-26c2a; BioLegend), F(ab′)2 goat anti-mouse IgM (Jackson Immunology) or goat F(ab′)2 anti-mouse Ig (Southern Biotech) for 10 min at 37 °C in RPMI 1640. Washed cells were fixed for 10 min at room temperature in 4% paraformaldehyde, then blocked and permeabilized for 30 min in PBS with 0.1% v/v Triton and 2% bovine calf serum at room temperature. Cells were stained with 2 µg/mL of biotinylated anti-mouse CD307a (FCRL1, REA566; Miltenyi Biotec) for 30 min at room temperature. Secondary antibodies were used according to the isotype and host species of the primary antibodies, including Alexa Fluor 647 anti-mouse IgM (RMM-1; BioLegend), Alexa Fluor 647 anti-mouse IgD (11-26c2a; BioLegend), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG2a (Invitrogen), Alexa Fluor 555 donkey anti-goat IgG (H + L) (OR97402; Invitrogen), Alexa Fluor 594 anti-Rab7A (W16034A; BioLegend), and streptavidin Alexa Fluor 633 (Invitrogen) or Dylight 488 or (Columbia Biosciences) as indicated. Cells were washed in PBS, resuspended in ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Invitrogen), and imaged using the Zeiss LSM 710 laser scanning confocal microscope using the 63× objective and 1 AU pinhole setting to produce optical sections of estimated thickness, 0.7 to 1.0 µm. Primary murine B cells were analyzed using Zeiss ZEN 3.5 software by manually adding regions of interest (ROIs) around the BCR cap (defined as the area of polarized BCR accumulation) and the opposite region of the cell membrane with no visible cap. For each ROI, the mean fluorescence intensity was measured in arbitrary units. BCR Cap versus outside BCR regions were compared with each other. For cell lines, total colocalization analysis was performed using Coloc2 plugin in ImageJ version 1.54f (National Institutes of Health). Mander’s M1 coefficient—representing the fraction of FCRL1 (green) signal overlapping with BCR (red) signal—was calculated without applying thresholds. A minimum of 20 cells per condition were imaged and analyzed from at least 2 independent experiments.

Graphing and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 software (GraphPad Software). A significance level cutoff of P < 0.05 was used in all experiments. Statistical tests are defined in the figure legends.

Results

The PRR of SHIP-1 is recruited to FCRL1 through GRB2

We previously observed that GRB2 binds FCRL1 at tyrosine 281 (Y281), and SHIP-1 is recruited to FCRL1 in a GRB2-dependent manner.18 SHIP-1 and GRB2 both contain SH2 domains, and FCRL1 has 6 cytoplasmic tyrosines, at least 2 of which are phosphorylated. This allows the potential for simultaneous recruitment of SHIP-1 and GRB2 to FCRL1. Alternatively, SHIP-1 contains a C-terminal PRR with multiple PxxP motifs that could allow recruitment of SHIP-1 to GRB2 through one or both of the GRB2 SH3 domains. To distinguish between these possibilities, we constructed translational fusions between GFP and the individual domains of SHIP-1 and expressed them in A20 cells using the lentiviral expression vector pLenti-CMV-Puro (Fig. 1A–C). Co-immunoprecipitation assays were conducted to identify which domain of SHIP-1 was recruited. Following immunoprecipitation of FCRL1, Western blotting showed that the PRR, but not the SH2 domain of SHIP-1, coprecipitated as evident from a band of approximately 59 kDa (Fig 1D). Because A20IIA1.6 cells do not contain FcγRIIb, co-immunoprecipitations with CD22 were performed to confirm the correct folding and phosphotyrosine-binding activity of the SHIP-1 SH2 domain.32 To test whether the PRR of SHIP-1 could be recruited to FCRL1 indirectly through GRB2, immunoprecipitations were performed with an anti-GRB2 antibody. Consistent with our earlier findings, the PRR::GFP fusion but not the SH2::GFP fusion could be recruited by GRB2 (Fig. 1E). Taken together, these results indicate that SHIP-1 is recruited to FCRL1 through an interaction with the PRR and one of the SH3 domains of GRB2.

Colocalization of FCRL1 to the BCR is GRB2 dependent

Previous studies using total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy have demonstrated colocalization of FCRL1 and IgM on the surface of B cells following activation of the BCR.30 To more fully characterize this colocalization phenomenon, FCRL1 and the BCR were imaged by confocal microscopy in splenocytes after antibody-induced BCR crosslinking. Confocal images of primary cells show the formation of an antibody-induced cap during activation. Though FCRL1 was not directly engaged with any antibody reagent or similar crosslinker, FCRL1 is shown to localize spontaneously to the BCR cap (Fig. 2A). This is measured by quantification of fluorescence intensity of FCRL1 localized within and outside of the capped BCR, which was defined manually by adding ROIs around the BCR cap (defined as the area of polarized BCR accumulation) and the opposite region of the cell membrane with no visible cap (Fig. 2B). This confirms the ability of FCRL1 to colocalize with the BCR during activation30 and shows that colocalization of FCRL1 with the BCR is not restricted to IgM-containing BCRs. For further confirmation that the accumulation seen is intracellular, RAB7a was stained to examine BCR and FCRL1 colocalization (Fig. S1A). We found overlap in imaging and no difference in the Mander’s overlap coefficients (MOCs) of the BCR or FCRL1 with RAB7a indicating the intracellular localization of these proteins (Fig. S1B).

To understand the mechanism by which FCRL1 colocalizes with the BCR, Fcrl1−/− A20 cells (ΔFcrl1) with rescued expression of FCRL1 mutants were used to interrogate which amino acids are essential for FCRL1 recruitment to the BCR. Cell lines included A20.ΔFcrl1 cells expressing FLAG-tagged wild-type FCRL1 or FLAG-tagged site-directed mutants of FCRL1 Y281F, Y297F, or one in which all tyrosines were ablated (6YF). Following stimulation, FCRL1.Y297F colocalizes with the BCR in a manner similar to wild-type FCRL1, and FCRL1 and the BCR appear to be endocytosed together. However, FCRL1.Y281F and 6YF mutations exhibit a different localization pattern whereby FCRL1 moves toward the BCR cap but does not fully colocalize with it. Instead, FCRL1 localizes to an area on the periphery of the BCR cap and appears to be endocytosed separately from the BCR (Fig. 3A). Total colocalization was measured and quantified by Mander’s overlap coefficient (MOC), the 6YF and Y281F mutants had an MOCs of 0.7 and 0.6, respectively, which was significantly lower than wild-type, confirming the requirement of Y281 for recruitment of FCRL1 to the BCR (Fig. 3B).

GRB2 is an important adapter protein in B cell signaling and is recruited to FCRL1 through Y281.18,28 To determine if the colocalization of the BCR with FCRL1 is dependent on GRB2, A20 cells with Cas9-mediated knockout of Grb2 (A20.ΔGrb2) were stimulated with anti-BCR antibodies as described previously. GRB2-deficient cells demonstrated incomplete localization of FCRL1 to the BCR cap, with the formation of the cap being disorganized in the absence of GRB2 (Fig. 4). These results indicate that the full colocalization of FCRL1 with the BCR is dependent on GRB2.

Adapter functions of SHIP-1 connect FCRL1 to the BCR and ERK regulation

Because SHIP-1 is recruited to FCRL1 in a manner dependent on GRB2 recruitment to Y281 on FCRL1,18 and the PRR of SHIP-1, not the SH2 domain, is recruited to FCRL1, also through Y281, we hypothesized that other domains or motifs in SHIP-1 are available to serve as an adapter to recruit FCRL1 to the BCR complex. Therefore, we examined if any of the individual domains of SHIP-1 are involved in the colocalization of FCRL1 with the BCR. To bypass the requirement for complex formation between FCRL1 and GRB2, translational fusions of individual domains of SHIP-1 and tyrosine-mutated FCRL1.6YF were generated and expressed in A20.ΔFcrl1 cells. These included separate fusions of FCRL1 and the SHIP-1 SH2 domain (6YF::SH2), the phosphatase-C2 region (6YF::PHOS), and the PRR (6YF::PRR) (Fig. 5A–C). When transduced into FCRL1-deficient A20 cells, the fusion proteins had comparable levels of expression to that of endogenous FCRL1 in the parental A20 cell line (Fig. S2). Following BCR activation, the FCRL1.6YF::SH2 fusion colocalized with the BCR; however, the 6YF::PHOS and 6YF::PRR fusions demonstrated only partial movement toward the BCR, remaining on the periphery BCR cap like 6YF (Fig. 5D). Quantification of the colocalization of the PHOS and PRR fused FCRL1 with the BCR shows significantly lower MOC values when compared with the wild-type of 6YF::SH2 fusion (Fig. 6B). This indicates that the SH2 domain of SHIP-1 drives colocalization of FCRL1 with the BCR. Combined with the data showing that the PRR of SHIP-1 interacts with FCRL1 through GRB2, these results indicate that GRB2 and SHIP-1 form a bridge linking FCRL1 to the BCR signaling complex.

Our previous studies demonstrated that FCRL1 could negatively regulate ERK phosphorylation after BCR ligation, however, the mechanism by which this occurs remains unknown.18 To investigate whether regulation of ERK activation is dependent on the phosphatase or adapter activity of SHIP-1, the A20.ΔFcrl1 cells expressing the 6YF fusions were stimulated through the BCR. Intracellular flow cytometry was used to determine which domain of SHIP-1 is involved with ERK regulation. It was found that the FCRL1.6YF::SH2 fusion protein was able to recapitulate the ERK-inhibitory phenotype of expressing wild-type FCRL1. Additionally, 6YF::PHOS and 6YF::PRR constructs gave relatively similar or intermediate levels of ERK phosphorylation as the 6YF protein (Fig. 6). This result indicates that it is the adapter function of SHIP-1, rather than the phosphatase activity, that produces ERK inhibition through FCRL1.

Discussion

SHIP-1 is a multifunctional protein with adapter and catalytic functions.12 Its role in the immune response is classically considered that of a crucial negative regulator controlling the activation of lymphocyte signaling.2,11,33 However, it is likely that its role is more than simply immunosuppression, as pharmacological disruption of SHIP-1 activity can have divergent effects on cellular activation and survival, with SHIP-1 activity enhancing activation of the AKT/mTOR pathway while genetic ablation of SHIP-1 suggests a role in suppressing the Ras/ERK pathway.34–36

FCRL1 plays multiple regulatory roles in BCR signaling, including modulation of ERK phosphorylation and enhancing calcium flux.18,27,30 Our previous studies demonstrated that the recruitment by FCRL1 of the inositol phosphatase, SHIP-1, and the modulation of ERK phosphorylation after BCR stimulation are both dependent on GRB2. Based on this, we hypothesized that the ERK inhibitory activity of FCRL1 would result from the phosphatase activity of SHIP-1, thereby reducing downstream activation of the Ras pathway after BCR stimulation. Instead, we observed that the phosphatase domain of SHIP-1 was dispensable for ERK inhibition, and the fusion of FCRL1 and the SH2 domain of SHIP-1 was sufficient for the ERK-inhibitory activity of FCRL1. This leaves the precise mechanism of FCRL1-mediated ERK inhibition unresolved but strongly implies the recruitment of as-yet-unidentified proteins in producing this phenomenon.

In addition to the ERK inhibitory activity of SHIP-1, our findings show a novel adaptive role of SHIP-1 in the recruitment of FCRL1 to the B cell synapse during B cell activation. Following antigen recognition, the BCRs are able to form microclusters for assembly of the signalosome allowing for the activation of Src family kinases that can propagate signaling.1,30 Previous studies into the localization of FCRL1 during BCR signaling found colocalization of FCRL1 and IgM following BCR stimulation.30 Our experiments confirmed that FCRL1 colocalizes with the IgM following induced aggregation of the BCR complex We note that this colocalization is independent of BCR isotype, as IgG and IgD showed a similar effect, likely precluding a direct interaction between surface immunoglobulin and FCRL1. Instead, our data support a model in which there is a two-stage recruitment of FCRL1 to the BCR synapse. First, there is a tyrosine-independent recruitment of FCRL1 to the periphery of the cap. Whether this occurs through the activity of the extracellular, transmembrane, or cytoplasmic domains of FCRL1 remains to be determined. As the BCR aggregates mature into a synapse and become endocytosed, FCRL1 is relegated to the periphery of the synapse unless Y281 is phosphorylated and recruits GRB2 and SHIP-1. Because SHIP-1 recruitment is dependent on GRB2 and the PRR of SHIP-1, the available data favor a model in which the SH2 domain of GRB2 directly interacts with phospho-Y281, and 1 or 2 PxxP motifs in the PRR of SHIP-1 are recruited by one or both of the SH3 domains of GRB2.14 This leaves the SH2 domain of SHIP-1 available to interact with proteins of the BCR signalosome and complete the recruitment of FCRL1 into the BCR synapse (Fig. 6B). Multiple candidate interacting proteins have been identified which could play this role, including actin-associated proteins or Igα.17

Once in the synapse, the FCRL1/GRB2/SHIP-1 complex may nucleate the recruitment of additional molecules to the signalosome. We and others have previously observed the recruitment of GRAP, SOS1, and c-Abl by FCRL1,18,30 and SHIP-1 binds a host of proteins that perform and regulate critical functions of antigen-receptor signaling, including Ras and cytoskeleton-dependent functions.17,37 Future studies will focus on understanding the mechanism and purpose of FCRL1 entry into the B cell synapse.
