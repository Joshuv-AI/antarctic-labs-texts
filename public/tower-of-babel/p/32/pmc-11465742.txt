Influence of compliance and resistance of the test lung on the accuracy of the tidal volume delivered by the ventilator
Authors: Zheng-Long Chen, Yu-Zhong Yan, Hong-Yi Yu, Qiu-Bo Wang, Wei Wang, Ming Zhong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLarge variations in respiratory system compliance and resistance may cause the accuracy of tidal volume (VT) delivery beyond the declared range. This study aimed at evaluating the accuracy of VT delivery using a test lung model to simulate pulmonary mechanics under normal or disease conditions.MethodsIn vitro assessment of the VT delivery accuracy was carried out on two commercial ventilators. Measurements of the inspired and expired VT from the ventilator and FlowAnalyser were compared to evaluate the separated and combined influences of compliance and resistance on the delivered VT accuracy. To do this, the errors of five delivered volumes (30 ml, 50 ml, 100 ml, 300 ml, and 500 ml) were checked under 29 test conditions involving a total of 27 combinations of resistance and compliance.ResultsFor the tested ventilator S1 with a flow sensor near the expiratory valve, the average of expired VT errors (ΔVTexp) in three measurements (4 test conditions for each measurement) correlated to test lung compliance (r=-0.96, p = 0.044), and the average of inspired VT errors (ΔVTins) correlated to compliance (r = 0.89, p = 0.106); for the tested ventilator S2 with a flow sensor located at the Y piece, no clear relationship between compliance and ΔVTexp or ΔVTins was found. Furthermore, on two ventilators tested, the current measurements revealed a poor correlation between test lung resistance and ΔVTins or ΔVTexp, and the maximum values of ΔVTexp and ΔVTins correspond to the maximum resistance of 200 cmH2O/(L/s), at which the phenomenon of the flap fluttering in the variable orifice flow senor was observed, and the recorded peak inspiratory pressure (Ppeak) was much higher than the Ppeak estimated by the classical equation of motion. In contrast, at the lower resistance values of 5, 20, 50 and 100 cmH2O/(L/s), the recorded Ppeak was very close to the estimated Ppeak. Overall, the delivered VT errors were in the range of ± 14% on two ventilators studied.ConclusionsDepending on the placement site of the flow sensor in the ventilator circuit, the compliance and resistance of the test lung have different influences on the accuracy of VT delivery, which is further attributed to different fluid dynamics effects of the compliance and resistance. The main influence of compliance is to raise the peak inspiratory pressure Ppeak, thereby increasing the compression volume within the ventilator circuit; whereas a high resistance not only contributes to elevating Ppeak, but more importantly, it governs the gas flow conditions. Ppeak is a critical predictive indicator for the accuracy of the VT delivered by a ventilator.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-024-03294-1.

Background

The ventilator is the most common life sustaining device in hospitals, used for respiratory support in patients with respiratory failure, surgical anesthesia, and emergency resuscitation [1]. The accuracy of the ventilator’s tidal volume settings and display values is a crucial component of its basic performance. The typical values of tidal volume were 6 to 8 mL/kg predicted bodyweight in a low tidal volume ventilation strategy [2]. From the patient’s perspective, substantial evidence suggests that improper settings of ventilator tidal volume can cause volutrauma and barotrauma, leading to respiratory system diseases and affecting long-term respiratory outcomes [3, 4].

However, the claimed accuracies of tidal volume may vary among different ventilator manufacturers, and the testing methods for the control accuracies may also exhibit a varying degree of differences. For instance, during the accuracy testing of volume-controlled breath type, manufacturers generally use passive test lungs or bags as substitutes for the human natural lungs [5, 6]. Evidently, the respiratory system resistance (R) and compliance (C) of clinically mechanically ventilated patients are very different from those of test lungs or bags, considering various physiological or pathological conditions. For example, data from references [7, 8] showed that respiratory system compliance and resistance in adults with normal lung condition were approximately 50 mL/cmH2O and 10 cmH2O/(L/s), respectively; for a full-term infant, the typical values of compliance and resistance were about 5 mL/cmH2O and 50 cmH2O/(L/s). In fact, the international standard for critical care ventilators (ISO 80601-2-12:2020) provides detailed requirements and guidelines for the accuracy testing of tidal volume to ensure consistency in the detection methods across different manufacturers [9, 10].

Unfortunately, there is little literature available on the rationale for this standard from aspects of both clinical practice and engineering design. To be specific, what criteria are used to determine various combinations of lung compliance and airway resistance as specified in ISO 80601-2-12:2020? In fact, several previous studies validated that the accuracy of displayed values of tidal volumes were related not only to respiratory impedance, but to the placement location of the proximal flow sensor [11–13]. The aim of this study was therefore to address the following questions: (1) how do compliance and resistance of test lungs affect the accuracy of controlled and displayed tidal volumes? (2) do the type and placement location of the flow sensors impact the accuracy of tidal volume delivered or exhaled? and (3) whether or not the accuracies of controlled and displayed tidal volumes still remain within the declared range when commercial ventilators encounter an extreme compliance and/or resistance?

Methods

Test platform

According to the recommendation of the international standard ISO 80601-2-12:2020, a test platform [9, 10] was constructed to investigate the influence of compliance and resistance of test lungs on the accuracy of controlled and displayed tidal volume of the ventilator (Fig. 1). Inspired or expired tidal volumes of the test lung were measured by a medical FlowAnalyser (PF-300, IMT Analytics, Switzerland) located after the Y piece of the ventilator circuit. The outlet of the FlowAnalyser was attached to one end of a resistance component via a short and flexible piece of tubing fused to the approximately sized connector, while the other end of the resistance component was connected directly to the inlet of the test lung. The FlowAnalyser can measure bidirectional airflow and its tidal volume range is ± 10 L, with an accuracy of ± 2% of the actual reading. This accuracy is higher than the permissible errors in set or displayed tidal volume specified in the ISO 80601-2-12:2020, which states that for a delivered volume greater than 50 mL, the measurement accuracy shall be within ±(4.0 mL + 15% of the actual volume expired through the patient-connection port) [9].

Two test lung models were used. One model, Adult/Infant Training and Test Lung (Michigan Instruments, MI, USA), was used to simulate the small compliance values (1, 3, and 5 mL/cmH2O).

Another model was custom-designed rigid test lungs (Shenzhen Yice Medical Test Co., Ltd, Guangdong, China) for the simulation of relatively large compliance values (10, 20 and 50 mL/cmH2O). This kind of test lung is based on the Boyle’s law to simulate lung compliance [14], that is, for gas inside an airtight and rigid container, gas pressure is inversely proportional to gas volume given an isothermal compression process.

Two commercial ventilators, CARESCAPE R860 (GE HealthCare, Chicago, Illinois, United States) and Bellavista 1000 (Vyaire Medical, Irvine, California, USA), numbered as S1 and S2 respectively, were selected in the study. Both ventilators can provide mechanical ventilation for adult or pediatric patients. The tidal volume settings of CARESCAP R860 ranged 100 to 2000 mL in adult mode, 20 to 300 mL in pediatric mode. The available tidal volume range of Bellavista 1000 was 300 to 2500 mL for adult patients and 30 to 500 mL for pediatric patients. The declared accuracy of tidal volume delivery was ± 10% of setting for the ventilator S1 and ±(2 mL + 15% of setting) for the ventilator S2. The ventilator S1 measured the expired tidal volume at the expiratory valve, whereas the ventilator S2 measured it at the Y piece. During the following test procedures, gas volume and flow rate reported by the ventilators or the FlowAnalyser are expressed at BTPS (body temperature and ambient pressure saturated with water vapor).

During the above testing, we noticed particularly that the readings on the ventilator and/or the FlowAnalyser were somewhat drifting even with an identical tidal volume setting each time we shutdown these equipment and restarted them several hours later. Therefore, we performed three repeated tests totally on three different days to ensure the reliability of the results. After testing the separate influence of compliance and resistance on the accuracy of fixed tidal volume, we assessed the accuracy of the delivered tidal volumes over the range of 30 to 500 mL with two ventilators, using the test lungs with different combinations of compliance and resistance [9]. The specific test protocol are shown in in Supplementary Table 1. The test procedures are similar to steps 1 to 6 described above. Note that the pediatric ventilator circuits and flow sensors were used when the ventilators provided the intended delivered tidal volumes of 50 and 30 mL (rows 9–21 in Supplementary Table 1), and the adult ventilator circuits and flow sensors were used for the tidal volumes of 300 and 500 mL (rows 1–8 in Supplementary Table 1).

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The relationship between percentage errors in the delivered tidal volume and compliance and resistance of test lungs was evaluated by Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Comparison of groups for combined influence compliance and resistance on the expired tidal volume errors was carried out using two-way analyses of variance (ANOVA) for repeated measures with the Tukey correction for multiple comparisons as appropriate. The data we compared have an approximately Gaussian distribution and an equal variance. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA, www.graphpad.com). A p value lower than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, the influence of test lung compliance and resistance on the accuracy of the delivered tidal volume was evaluated in vitro on two typical commercial ventilators under well-controlled conditions. Our results showed that for the tested ventilator S1with a flow sensor near the expiratory valve, both inspired and expired VT errors correlated to test lung compliance, whereas for the tested ventilator S2 with a flow sensor located at the Y piece, no such a clear relationship was found. Furthermore, both ventilators exhibited poor correlation between test lung resistance and the errors of VT delivery. At the high resistance value of 200 cmH2O/(L/s), the recorded \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{peak}$$\end{document} was much higher than the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{peak}$$\end{document} estimated by the classical equation of motion. In contrast, at the lower resistance values of 5, 20, 50 and 100 cmH2O/(L/s), the recorded \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{peak}$$\end{document} was very close to the estimated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{peak}$$\end{document}. Overall, the errors of VT delivery were in the range of ± 14% on two ventilators studied.

In fact, the effect of compliance and resistance on the accuracy of VT delivery depends on two key factors: the type of flow sensor and the site of flow measurement. The most common flow sensor technologies used in commercially available ventilators are: differential pressure flow sensors, hot wire anemometers and ultrasonic flowmeters [15]. Each kind of the flow sensor is based on the different working principle, and thus has a specific sensitivity throughout the flow range. Among them, the ventilator S1 tested currently used hot wire anemometers and the ventilator S2 adopted differential pressure flow sensors for the flow measurement. The functional principles of these two flow sensors are schematically shown in Fig. 6 [16, 17].

Inside variable orifice flow sensor, there is a flap made of flexible sheet materials like plastic (left in Fig. 6). The flap, cut in the middle, creates an opening which enlarges with the increase in the flow rate. By appropriately selecting elasticity and optimizing shape of the flap, variable orifice flow sensors are allowed possessing a linear relation between flow and the differential pressure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varDelta\:P$$\end{document}. Of note, the configuration of variable orifice flow sensors are susceptible to changes in flow patterns, resulting in resonant vibration or flutter of the flaps. Hot wire anemometers encompass a thin electrically heated platinum wire. As gas flows across the platinum wire, heat is transferred from upstream to downstream (right in Fig. 6). This cooling effect of the gas flowing across the heated wire results in changes in electrical resistance which are proportional to the mass of the gas flowing through and can be measured by an electronic circuit. It should be noted that the output voltage –gas flow relationship of hot wire anemometers is non-linear. Thereby it features a high sensitivity at low flow rate and a low sensitivity at high flow rate. One of the main drawbacks is possible zero drifts due to gas temperature changes.

From the viewpoint of flow sensors principles, the working range of the flow sensor is a crucial factor affecting the accuracy of the delivered tidal volume. According to the classical equation of motion [18, 19], the maximal inspiratory pressure (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{peak}$$\end{document}) on volume-controlled ventilation can be estimated by the equation:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{peak}=\frac{{V}_{T}}{{C}_{rs}}+{R}_{aw}\frac{d{V}_{T}}{dt}+PEEP$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{V}_{T}$$\end{document} is the delivered tidal volume, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{rs}$$\end{document} is the compliance of respiratory system, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{aw}$$\end{document} is the airway resistance, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{d{V}_{T}}{dt}$$\end{document} is the inspiratory flow that is usually set at a constant value on volume-controlled ventilation, and PEEP is positive end-expiratory pressure, representing the baseline tension of the lung. Equation of motion shows that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{peak}$$\end{document} is determined concurrently by airway resistance and respiratory system compliance. Note that the higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{peak}$$\end{document} is, the more volume is lost because of the distensibility of the ventilator circuit, and thereby the greater error in the delivered tidal volume.

When the preset tidal volume is achieved, the ventilator switches to the expiratory phase and the patient passively exhales. Expiratory flow (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\dot{{V}_{E}}$$\end{document}) is determined by the following equation:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\dot{{V}_{E}}=-\frac{{P}_{EL}}{{R}_{aw}}{e}^{-t/{(R}_{aw}{C}_{rs})}$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{EL}$$\end{document} is the elastic pressure of the respiratory system at the beginning of exhalation and equals the sum of the driving pressure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{{V}_{T}}{{C}_{rs}}$$\end{document} and PEEP [20]. Also note that the peak expiratory flow rate at time t = 0 is dependent on the elastic pressure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{EL}$$\end{document} and airway resistance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{aw}$$\end{document}. In addition, only when the flow source creates laminar flow conditions do Eqs. (1) and (2) hold true. A narrow diameter, accessory fittings or the like in the flow path near the flow sensor may worsen flow conditions, resulting in the occurrence of turbulence and jetting [21], and therefore affecting measurement accuracy of flow sensors.

Influence of compliance on the delivered tidal volume errors

During the tests, we found that the recorded \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{peak}$$\end{document} increased accordingly as compliance of test lungs decreased. Using the Eq. (1) and the recorded inspiratory flow \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\dot{V}}_{I}$$\end{document}, we further made a rough estimation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{peak}$$\end{document} (columns 5 and 8 in Table 2) in tests listed in the first four rows of Table 1, which were very close to the recorded \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{peak}$$\end{document} (columns 4 and 7 in Table 2) on the two ventilators. This comparison provides a reasonable explanation for the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varDelta\:VT}_{ins}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varDelta\:VT}_{exp}$$\end{document} in Fig. 2. The ventilator S1 measured flow signals inside the ventilator, but the FlowAnalyer measured flow signals immediately after the Y piece. A decreased compliance resulted in an increased \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{peak}$$\end{document}, the more volume was compressed and lost in the ventilator circuit, therefore yielding the greater negative bias errors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varDelta\:VT}_{ins}$$\end{document} and the greater positive bias errors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varDelta\:VT}_{exp}$$\end{document} [6]. Although the ventilator S1 allows for a compensation formula to minimize the effects of circuit compliance, practically speaking, satisfactory compensation can be difficult. First, circuit compliance may change over time due to water vapor condensation or the circuit leak. Second, some uncontrolled factors, for example, variations in the breathing circuit setup (e.g., humidifiers, water traps, heat and moisture exchangers and other devices), are difficult to include into such a compensation formula. In contrast, the ventilator S2 measured flow signals at the site of the Y piece, therefore no significant correlation between compliance and errors in the delivered tidal volume was observed.

Influence of resistance on the delivered tidal volume errors

During the tests, we noted that the recorded \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{peak}$$\end{document} (columns 4 and 7 in Table 3) increased accordingly as resistance of test lungs increased. and4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{aw}={K}_{1}+{K}_{2}\dot{V}$$\end{document}

Actually, we have observed the phenomenon of flutter of the flap (see Supplementary Video 1) in the variable orifice flow senor on the tested ventilator S2 whenever a large resistance of 200 cmH2O/(L/s) was added into the ventilator circuit. It was likely that jetting flow, creating by the narrow diameter of resistance component, induced vibration of the flap [21]. Likewise, jetting flow may well degrade the measurement accuracy of the hot wire flow sensor on ventilator S1 [24].

Furthermore, a higher resistance implies a larger time constant [20, 25]. Take resistance = 200 cmH2O/(L/s) and compliance = 10 mL/cmH2O for example, the calculated time constant is the product of resistance and compliance equal to 2 s. Considering the expiratory time of 1.2 s in the tests (Table 1), this suggests that the end expiratory flow will not reach zero, as observed in Fig. 7. Under this condition, the ventilator and FlowAnalyser will give different expiratory volumes due to the different integration time length for flow signals.

Taken together, the nonlinear pressure drop-flow relationship of the airways is important factor in explaining large errors of VT delivery as observed in Fig. 3. In clinical practice, this nonlinearity can also help us understand the mechanism of expiratory flow limitation commonly present in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or severe asthma [26]. In expiratory phase, the airway resistance may increase due to bronchospasm, secretions and airway compression, resulting to higher expiratory flow rate and lower airway wall support pressure exerted by the gas. As such, airways will collapse when the flow reaches a certain velocity, limiting further increases in expiratory flow. At this time, an increase in patient’s expiratory effort will have limited effect on lung emptying due to a larger expiratory time constant or airway closure. Thus, a part of gas was trapped in the airways or alveoli, causing a larger expiratory VT errors.

Combined influence of compliance and resistance on the delivered tidal volume errors

As shown in the left panel of Fig. 4, when the tidal volume setting was adjusted from 50 mL to 30 mL, the signs of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varDelta\:VT}_{ins}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varDelta\:VT}_{exp}$$\end{document} changed in the opposite direction on the ventilator S1, indicating similar results as compared with the ventilator S2 (right panel of Fig. 4). This fact proved again that the site of the flow sensor is a crucial factor affecting the error of delivered volume. As to why \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varDelta\:VT}_{exp}$$\end{document} in the group with compliance = 1 mL/cmH2O (Fig. 5) was significantly smaller than that in the other two groups, we speculated that the reason may be related to the difference in the elastic pressure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{EL}$$\end{document} of test lungs. With the same tidal volume set of 30 mL, the larger compliance was, the lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{EL}$$\end{document}, and the smaller peak expiratory flow in the expiratory phase. It was very likely that the flow sensors have lower resolution and accuracy in this low range. Alternatively, due to the existence of a dead space [27] between the ventilator flow sensors and FlowAnalyser, when the peak expiratory flow was too small, there may be more volume staying in the dead space that couldn’t be sensed by the downstream ventilator flow sensor.

Limitations of the study

Some potential limitations of this laboratory study must be addressed when extrapolating the results to clinical settings. First, although the FlowAnalyser used in the study was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s recommendations before data collection, it still has a ± 2% uncertainty for each measured tidal volume. Second, in clinical scenarios respiratory system resistance and compliance vary dynamically with respiratory rate and are non-linear. The total resistance in mechanical ventilation includes circuit resistance, endotracheal tube resistance and airway resistance. Using a static resistance and compliance instead may partly weaken the validity of our findings. Third, the effects of leak around the tracheal tube or within ventilator circuit itself are not taken into account [10, 28]. A leak in the expiratory limb could underestimate the expired volume displayed by the ventilator. In fact, the controllable gas leakage is just the advantage of the in vitro measurements. Finally, a further study is required to evaluate the impact of the different airflow patterns or ventilation modes (e.g., volume guarantee, pressure regulated volume control) on the accuracy of the delivered tidal volumes [29].

Conclusions

On the basis of the test findings from two commercial ventilators, we have found that regardless of types of flow sensors or their placement site in the ventilator circuit, variations in respiratory system compliance and resistance have a significant impact on the accuracy of the delivered tidal volume, particularly when the intended tidal volume is less than 100–150 mL. The main influence of compliance is to raise the peak inspiratory pressure, thereby increasing the compression volume within the ventilator circuit; whereas a high resistance not only contributes to elevating the peak inspiratory pressure, but more importantly, it governs the gas flow conditions (laminar, turbulent or the like). Peak inspiratory pressure is a critical predictive indicator for the accuracy of the tidal volume delivered by a ventilator.
