Failure modes and effects analysis of LINAC-based stereotactic arrhythmia radioablation categorized by segmental targets
Authors: Jing Li, Yongchang Wu, Qingyong Chen, Hao Guo, Ran Luo, Long Bai, Weige Wei, Yanmei Hao, Hang Yu, Guyu Dai, Xiangyu Zhang, Qing Xiao, Qing Yang, Sen Bai, Guangjun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStereotactic arrhythmia radioablation (STAR) is an emerging treatment for refractory or recurrent arrhythmias. Compared with conventional stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), STAR involves greater complexity in target delineation, motion management, and organs at risk (OARs) protection, yet it lacks established consensus clinical guidelines, and workflow-specific risk analyses remain limited.ObjectiveTo develop a C-arm linear accelerator (LINAC)-based STAR workflow and perform a failure modes and effects analysis (FMEA) with fault tree analysis (FTA), supplemented by an exploratory segment-based anatomical proximity analysis.MethodsA multidisciplinary team constructed a process map for C-arm LINAC-based STAR and identified potential failure modes across the workflow. Risks were scored using occurrence, severity, and detectability to calculate risk priority numbers (RPNs), and FTA was used to analyze causal pathways. In a predefined exploratory imaging subgroup of eight patients with ventricular tachycardia and simulation-acquired coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA), we divided the left ventricle according to the 17-segment model and measured the minimum distances from each segment to adjacent OARs as an anatomical surrogate of exposure likelihood.ResultsSeventy-nine failure modes were identified, of which 17 were classified as high risk. The highest-risk failure modes were concentrated in target delineation and motion-related steps, including inaccurate multimodal image registration (RPN 432), improper motion evaluation/management (RPN 336), and diagnostic error of arrhythmia substrate definition (RPN 320). In the exploratory imaging subgroup (n = 8), segmental spatial patterns were moderately consistent across patients, with most standard deviations of minimum distance below 2.0 cm, although variability remained for several segment-OAR relationships. Segments 4, 5, and 10 were each located within 2 cm of both the stomach and esophagus, indicating relatively higher anatomical proximity-related risk.ConclusionIn this FMEA of C-arm LINAC-based STAR, the principal high-risk workflow steps were concentrated in substrate definition, image registration, and motion management. The segment-based analysis provides an exploratory anatomical risk-mapping framework for OAR awareness. Further multicenter studies are needed to refine workflow risk prioritization and evaluate the clinical relevance of the segment-based anatomical findings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13014-026-02883-0.

Introduction

Stereotactic arrhythmia radioablation (STAR) is an innovative application of stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) for the treatment of refractory arrhythmias. Emerging clinical evidence suggests that STAR is a feasible treatment option for selected arrhythmias, including ventricular tachycardia (VT), atrial fibrillation, premature ventricular contraction, ventricular fibrillation, and atrial tachycardia [1–3]. STAR delivers precise high-dose radiotherapy to arrhythmogenic substrates non-invasively, providing a promising therapeutic option for patients who have failed conventional therapies, such as antiarrhythmic drugs and catheter radiofrequency ablation [4].

A standard clinical workflow for STAR has not yet been established in the published results, and challenges remain in terms of equipment and technique. First, accurate target volume delineation is essential, which is based on electroanatomic mapping (EAM) or non-invasive electrocardiographic imaging to identify arrhythmogenic substrates. Multimodal images, such as 4-dimensional computed tomography, cardiac magnetic resonance, and/or positron emission tomography, are fused to define the planning target volume (PTV) [5]. The unclear anti-arrhythmic mechanism of ionizing radiation [6, 7], the diagnostic accuracy of arrhythmogenic substrates [8], and the margin setting during PTV generation are key factors for treatment effectiveness [9]. Second, motion management is crucial for the complex PTV movement due to cardiorespiratory motion, thereby preventing off-target irradiation. Current methods, such as gating and tracking techniques based on the internal target volume, can cause dose uncertainties [10, 11]. Third, STAR planning is different from tumor radiotherapy. It needs new strategies for beam technique, prescription methods, and the trade-offs between target coverage and critical structures, including organs at risk (OARs) and cardiac substructure [12]. STAR is a conceptual and methodological integration of multidisciplinary diagnosis and treatment. It carries different risks than conventional SBRT and therefore warrants a dedicated risk analysis.

To reduce radiotherapy-related incidents, failure modes and effects analysis (FMEA) was introduced to radiotherapy in 2008 [13]. FMEA is used to systematically identify possible errors throughout the radiotherapy treatment process and assess their clinical impact. This approach helps direct limited resources to maximize patient care quality. Based on the methodology and nomenclature outlined in the TG-100 report by the American Association of Physicists in Medicine [14], FMEA has been applied to various radiotherapy workflows [15–17], but a dedicated analysis for STAR is lacking.

In this study, a multidisciplinary team was formed to identify risks in C-arm linear accelerator (LINAC)-based STAR according to the TG-100 framework and to develop a structured FMEA/FTA approach for safe and effective treatment. Risks related to the equipment, workflow, and multidisciplinary procedures involved in the diagnosis and treatment of ventricular arrhythmia were systematically evaluated. In addition, we performed an exploratory anatomical analysis using the American Heart Association 17-segment model to describe segment-specific proximity patterns between potential ventricular targets and adjacent OARs [18], with the aim of supporting anatomical risk awareness.

Materials and methods

Clinical trial overview and patient selection for analysis

This FMEA was conducted based on data from a single-arm phase I/II trial of STAR for ventricular arrhythmia at our institution. The original trial protocol and this secondary analysis were approved by the Ethics Committee on Biomedical Research of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No: 2020 (Scientific Review No. 516)), and informed consent was obtained from all participants [3, 19]. A workflow-based FMEA/FTA was conducted for the overall STAR process.

From this cohort, a detailed anatomical proximity analysis was performed in a predefined imaging subset. This exploratory analysis aimed to describe anatomical proximity patterns. Eight patients were selected based on the following criteria: (1) diagnosis of VT, and (2) availability of coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) images acquired on a CT simulation scanner. Failure modes identification, risk scoring, and prioritization

A multidisciplinary team with direct experience in STAR treatment conducted the FMEA. First, we mapped the entire STAR clinical workflow, from patient referral to treatment delivery, into a detailed process tree. For each step, potential failure modes (FMs) were systematically identified.

Risk quantification was performed according to the TG-100 methodology [14, 15]. Each FM was evaluated using three parameters on a 1–10 scale: Occurrence (O), the estimated probability of the failure; Severity (S), the clinical impact on the patient if the failure goes undetected and uncorrected; and Detectability (D), the likelihood that existing quality assurance (QA) systems would detect the failure [20].

To enhance objectivity and reliability, we used a two-stage structured scoring process. First, thirty experts representing five disciplines (cardiac electrophysiology, radiation oncology, nursing, medical physics, and radiation therapy) independently assigned preliminary O, S, and D scores to each failure mode. These preliminary ratings were used to reveal variability in interpretation and potential individual bias [21], and their consistency across experts was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). In the second stage, the multidisciplinary team jointly reviewed each failure mode. For items with discrepant preliminary ratings, participants explained the rationale underlying their scores with reference to the TG-100 definitions, the institutional workflow, and the most clinically appropriate interpretation of the failure mode. The facilitator then led discussion until the group agreed on a single O, S, and D score for that failure mode, without additional weighted voting or formal adjudication [22, 23]. Final scores were therefore assigned by consensus rather than by mathematical aggregation [24, 25]. The risk priority number (RPN) was calculated by multiplying O, S, and D (RPN = O × S × D) [14]. Following the generation of RPNs, a threshold was determined to identify high-risk FMs requiring mitigation. FMs ranked in the top 10% or with a consensus S score ≥ 8 were classified as high-risk and were included in the subsequent analysis [26]. A fault tree analysis (FTA) was performed to delineate the potential causes and effects for each failure. Specific mitigation strategies were formulated for high-risk FMs. For each strategy, the team re-estimated the Occurrence (O') and Detectability (D') after implementing the proposed actions. The anticipated effect of each proposed action on the O and D scores was estimated by the team, allowing for the calculation of a post-mitigation RPN to quantify the projected risk reduction.

Segmental target definition

The STAR treatment was typically prescribed as 25 Gy in a single fraction, which increased the risk of radiation exposure to OARs compared to conventional radiotherapy. Since the substrate for ventricular arrhythmia can originate from any myocardial segment, we adopted the standardized 17-segment model of the left ventricle (LV) to systematically define potential target regions and assess OAR proximity [10, 27]. For the eight patients selected as described above, their CCTA images were utilized. These images were obtained using a retrospective electrocardiogram-gated protocol on a 128-slice CT scanner (Revolution CT, GE Healthcare, Boston, MA, USA). The axial scans were performed during an exhaled breath-hold phase and reconstructed into 10 phases (from 0% to 90%) with a slice thickness of 2.5 mm.

In the diastolic images (phase 0%), we rotated the coronal, sagittal, and transverse planes to match the LV short-axis, 2-chamber, and 4-chamber views. Along the long axis of the LV, we labeled a point at the mitral annulus as “base” marker and another point at the first slice above the apex as “apex” marker, where the contrast-enhanced lumen was visible. The distance between the two markers was divided into three parts, separating the LV wall into apical, middle, and basal regions. The boundaries for targets 1 to 16 were defined at specific angles on the short-axis view. The 17th target included the LV wall from the true apex to the “apex” marker.

O × S score for OAR proximity risk

OARs adjacent to the LV were contoured for this analysis, including the esophagus, trachea, left bronchus, stomach, and liver. The O coefficient was assigned based on the minimum distance from each OAR to the segmental targets, serving as a simplified anatomical surrogate for the geometric likelihood of dose exposure. Specifically, O was categorized as 9 for a minimum distance of ≤ 2 cm, 6 for 2–5 cm, and 3 for > 5 cm. In this segmental analysis, the occurrence score did not represent an empirically observed event rate or dosimetric probability but was instead derived from the minimum segment-to-OAR distance as a geometric surrogate for OAR irradiation risk.

The S coefficient reflected the clinical severity of a potential overdose and was determined using single-fraction OAR dose constraints from the AAPM TG-101 report [28], which provides a widely recognized conservative standard for single-fraction, high-dose treatments. This approach aligns with the current clinical practice adopted in multicenter STAR studies such as RAVENTA and the STOPSTORM.eu consortium, where established SBRT constraints serve as the benchmark in the absence of STAR-specific guidelines [29, 30].

For the segment-specific anatomical analysis, we used an O × S framework as a simplifying methodological choice rather than a full FMEA [15, 31]. Within this framework, a separate D component was not explicitly included, as pre-treatment plan review and verification were assumed to provide broadly comparable detectability across segments, thereby limiting its discriminative value. Accordingly, the resulting scores should be interpreted as relative anatomical risk indicators.

Results

All enrolled patients were treated with a single fraction of 25 Gy prescribed to the PTV. The treatment was delivered using a C-arm LINAC (Edge, Varian Medical Systems) with SBRT techniques. The process map is illustrated in Fig. 1. Treatment planning was performed using the Eclipse treatment planning system (Version 13.5, Varian Medical System Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). Comprehensive motion management was employed, using optical surface imaging (OSI) for all patients and supplementing with a real-time position management respiratory gating system for cases with substantial target motion [11]. The MOSAIQ Integrity Platform and MOSAIQ were used for informatization management. Machine- and patient-specific QA was performed, and treatment was delivered with image-guided radiotherapy, including electronic portal imaging device (EPID) and on-board cone beam CT (CBCT).

The FTA was performed to delineate potential causes and effects; the complete FTA charts are provided in Supplemental Figure S1.

Table 2 lists 17 FMs (21.5% of the total) that were classified as high risk. This group comprised 13 high severity FMs (S ≥ 8) and 7 high RPN FMs, with an overlap of 3 FMs meeting both criteria. These projected RPN’ values and the median decrease of 50.0% (IQR: 37.5%-67.9%) represent theoretical risk reductions and may inform future prospective evaluation.

We retrospectively collected CCTA images from eight patients, delineated 17 targets and OARs using Slicer (Version 5.6.2), and calculated the minimum distance from each of the 17 targets to the OARs. This subgroup analysis was intended to characterize anatomical proximity patterns. The mean and standard deviation (SD) for high-risk OARs were presented in Table 4. Despite heterogeneity in underlying cardiac anatomy across the eight patients, the majority of standard deviations were below 2.0 cm, suggesting a moderately consistent spatial pattern within this cohort. Although the overall segmental pattern was broadly preserved across patients, inter-patient variability was more pronounced for several inferior and apical segment-OAR relationships. The greatest dispersion was observed for the stomach at segments 14, 15, and 17, and for the esophagus at segments 13 and 17, whereas several bronchial and tracheal distances were comparatively more stable.

To estimate segment-specific OAR toxicity risk, we applied an O × S framework. The O coefficient was assigned according to the minimum distance between each LV segment and adjacent OARs as a simplified geometric surrogate of exposure likelihood: O = 9 for a minimum distance of ≤ 2 cm, O = 6 for 2–5 cm, and O = 3 for > 5 cm [23]. The S coefficient was assigned according to the relative clinical severity of exceeding single-fraction OAR dose constraints derived from TG-101, which served as the primary benchmark in the absence of STAR-specific consensus constraints. We then used O × S to compare the relative anatomical risk of different segment-OAR relationships. Relative O × S scores differed most prominently for the esophagus, stomach, and left bronchus across ventricular segments, with the highest relative scores concentrated in inferobasal and inferolateral regions, as shown in Fig. 2. In the exploratory imaging subgroup, post-treatment gastritis was observed in one patient (case 3) with an inferior and inferolateral wall target.

Discussion

This study provides a structured, workflow-based risk analysis of C-arm LINAC-based STAR, identifying distinct vulnerability clusters. The highest-risk failure modes were concentrated in arrhythmia substrate definition, multimodal image registration, and motion management, highlighting the workflow steps most in need of risk mitigation.

These observations are consistent with prior STAR literature emphasizing the technical complexity of substrate delineation and multimodal image integration. The integration of EAM with CT and MRI has long been recognized as a challenging step in achieving accurate and reproducible target definition in STAR [5, 9, 27]. Our results extend prior observations by adding a workflow-based identification and prioritization of high-risk failure modes. Inaccurate image registration and substrate misidentification were among the most frequent high-risk failure modes, reflecting ongoing concerns regarding precision and standardization in substrate delineation. This is consistent with the broader evolution of STAR practice, where workflow heterogeneity and platform-dependent differences remain important barriers to standardization [32].

Motion-related risks were also prominent in our analysis. This is consistent with prior studies demonstrating that both respiratory and cardiac motion can substantially affect target localization, margin selection, and dose delivery in STAR [10, 11, 33]. Robust motion assessment is therefore particularly important in single-fraction STAR. Our FMEA further indicates that motion uncertainty may translate into concrete vulnerabilities across multiple workflow steps, from target definition to treatment planning and image guidance, rather than remaining only a theoretical geometric or dosimetric concern.

In a separate exploratory imaging subgroup, we used a 17-segment anatomical framework to describe segment-specific proximity patterns between potential ventricular targets and adjacent OARs. Its purpose was to provide a simple geometric overview of where certain ventricular segments are more likely to lie close to specific extracardiac OARs. The observation that inferobasal and inferolateral segments, including segments 4, 5, and 10, frequently lie close to the stomach and esophagus is anatomically intuitive. This finding places a familiar anatomical relationship into a standardized segment-based framework that may support early anatomical risk awareness during patient selection and pre-treatment decision-making. This approach is directionally consistent with published target-mapping and contouring guidance, which has emphasized the utility of the 17-segment model for communication, target localization, and cross-disciplinary planning, as well as the importance of careful delineation of adjacent critical structures [27, 34]. Based on a small sample of eight patients, this segmental analysis should be regarded as hypothesis-generating with respect to anatomical proximity patterns [32, 35].

From a workflow perspective, the present findings suggest that the most important opportunities for risk mitigation lie upstream, particularly in substrate definition, multimodal image registration, motion evaluation, and OAR delineation. For cases involving ventricular segments in close proximity to gastrointestinal OARs, additional planning vigilance may be warranted, including careful soft-tissue assessment during simulation and image guidance, secondary verification of adjacent OAR contours, and prioritization of steep dose gradients where feasible. These considerations are consistent with broader STAR planning experience and with ongoing efforts to refine motion-aware treatment strategies and contouring standards [30, 33, 34]. However, these implications should be interpreted as workflow-level considerations rather than evidence of improved clinical outcomes.

Several limitations should be emphasized. First, the anatomical subgroup was small, and the segment-specific framework was intended only for exploratory risk mapping. Second, the FMEA/RPN process remains semi-quantitative and consensus-based, despite the use of a structured multidisciplinary scoring process and assessment of inter-rater consistency. Third, the projected reductions in RPN after mitigation represent expert-estimated effects rather than prospectively validated improvements. Fourth, this analysis was developed for a C-arm LINAC-based STAR program and does not capture the potentially different risk profile of alternative delivery systems. In addition, technical issues relevant to STAR, such as CBCT image-quality limitations and metal artifacts, remain important sources of uncertainty in imaging and planning and may require dedicated mitigation strategies [36–38]. Accordingly, our findings support structured risk awareness and workflow prioritization, but not validation of a predictive toxicity model or definitive guidance for practice change.

Conclusion

This study provides a workflow-based FMEA/FTA for C-arm LINAC-based STAR and identifies high-risk steps concentrated in substrate definition, multimodal image registration, and motion-related management. It also describes an exploratory segment-based anatomical framework that may support early anatomical risk assessment. Together, these findings may help centers strengthen workflow risk awareness and support earlier risk-informed clinical decision-making. Further multicenter studies integrating dosimetry, motion assessment, and clinical outcomes are warranted to refine workflow risk prioritization and better define the clinical relevance of the segment-based anatomical framework.
