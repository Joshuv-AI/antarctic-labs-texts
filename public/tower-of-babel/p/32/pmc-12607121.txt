Rapid detection of synthetic pigments in black tea using hyperspectral imaging technology and machine learning
Authors: Zhihan Wang, Weijie Peng, Zhengrui Tian, Lin Zhu, Mengqi Guo, Zhiwei Chen, Hewei Meng, Chunwang Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we propose a novel approach that integrates hyperspectral imaging with machine learning to address the challenge of rapid and non-destructive detection of artificial pigment adulteration in Wuyi Congou tea. Compared with traditional chemical methods, this method enables the identification of pigment types, quantification of adulteration rate, and visualization of pigment distribution. A hyperspectral dataset of black tea adulterated with three synthetic pigments (Tartrazine, Carmine, and Sunset Yellow) was constructed, supporting the establishment of qualitative identification and quantitative prediction models. The qualitative model achieved an accuracy greater than 98 %, while the quantitative model yielded a predictive correlation coefficient (Rp) greater than 0.95 and root mean square error of prediction (RMSEP) less than 0.025. These results validate the feasibility and effectiveness of this approach for detecting and quantifying pigment adulteration in black tea, providing new possibilities for tea quality and safety inspection.Keywords: Synthetic pigments, Hyperspectral imaging, Machine learning, Chemometrics, Qualitative analysis, Quantitative analysis

Introduction

Production and processing of tea should adhere to the fundamental principles of being natural and additive-free. However, some businesses, in pursuit of higher profits, still add synthetic pigments to tea to enhance its appearance and other sensory qualities. This affects not only the quality of the tea, but also fair competition in the market and poses a potential threat to consumer health. Studies have shown that long-term intake of Allura Red AC may exacerbate experimental colitis by modulating intestinal flora and serotonin pathways (Kwon et al., 2022). Barrows and Wallin (2016) noted that the main current pigment-safety issues are currently inconsistent regulatory standards, potential health impacts, the development of natural alternatives, and the need to detect illegal pigments. Li, Elliott, et al. (2024); Li, Shen, et al. (2024) examined tea-fraud incidents from 2011 to 2023 and summarized nine forms of tea fraud, detection and analysis techniques, and preventive control measures. These studies suggest the urgent need to establish efficient and reliable methods for detecting pigment adulteration.

In recent years, spectroscopic techniques, especially hyperspectral imaging (HSI), have been widely used in the field of food-adulteration detection due to their ability to reflect the quality of a sample by analyzing the absorption of functional groups of molecules. This technique has the advantage of fusing image and spectral information with high spatial and spectral resolution (Gowen et al., 2007). Yoon and Ekramirad (2024) analyzed existing food-inspection problems using machine-learning-assisted multispectral and HSI techniques. Faqeerzada et al. (2020) achieved online identification of almonds and apricot kernels using near-infrared (NIR) and image-processing techniques. Khan et al. (2020) detected Sudan red adulteration in red chili peppers in cooking oil using HSI. Yang, Creedon, et al. (2021); Yang, Dong, et al. (2021) detected dye molecules, melamine, and different classes of pesticides in food products using Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectra (SERS). Phillips and Abdulla (2023) constructed a honey adulteration dataset based on HSI and achieved highly accurate identification of adulterated honey through feature-smoothing techniques and machine learning models. Meanwhile, Moskowitz and Yakes (2023) investigated the use of a portable vibration spectrometer for analyzing the authenticity of food products. In the field of tea detection, Yang, Creedon, et al. (2021); Yang, Dong, et al. (2021) examined the physicochemical properties of black tea during fermentation using HSI. Gong et al. (2024) developed an enhanced support vector regression based on the Pied Kingfisher Optimization Algorithm (PKO-SVR) model for the accurate detection of tea polyphenol content using HSI; and Li, Elliott, et al. (2024); Li, Shen, et al. (2024) used HSI for the quantitative analysis and visualization of catechin content in green tea samples stored at different temperatures. Jiang et al. (2025) detected the addition of lead chrome green (LCG) to tea using NIR spectroscopy.

Despite the remarkable results achieved by applying spectroscopic techniques to detect adulteration in food and tea, few studies have used HSI to target black tea adulterated with synthetic pigments. Traditionally, pigment adulteration in food has been detected using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). Li et al. (2020) used HPLC to detect synthetic food pigments in beverages. Yuan and Wang (2024) used Reverse-Phase High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (RP-HPLC) to simultaneously determine nine synthetic pigments in food, namely lemon yellow, amaranthine red, indigo, carmine, sunset yellow, brilliant blue, seductive red, erythrosine, and seductive red. However, these methods are usually limited by complicated sample pretreatment, a long detection period and an inability to perform online non-destructive testing. It is difficult to realize the visual analysis of the spatial distribution of pigment in the sample. In recent years, optical methods such as NIR and Raman spectroscopy have been used to detect adulteration in tea. Luo et al. (2023) used NIR to detect sugar adulteration in black tea and developed partial least squares regression (PLSR) and support vector regression (SVR) models. The optimal model was found to have high accuracy, with an Rp of 0.924 in the prediction set. Li et al. (2023) achieved detection of pigment-adulterated tea samples (Sunset Yellow and Tartrazine) using SERS. However, its ability to distinguish between multiple mixed pigments is limited, and factors such as sample particle size and light scattering greatly affect its quantitative accuracy. Currently, research into the rapid, non-destructive visual detection of multiple types of artificial pigment adulteration in black tea is lacking. In this paper, we present a non-destructive detection model for black tea adulteration based on HSI. The model uses a combined machine-learning method to extract the spectral information of key bands, and qualitatively and quantitatively analyses the adulterated black tea. This realizes identifying the type and amount of adulteration and distribution visualization. The application scenarios for HSI in rapid nondestructive testing of tea adulteration are expanded, and a theoretical foundation is laid for the development of portable tea-quality testing equipment.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The experiment was conducted in the laboratory of the Tea Research Institute of Shandong Academy of Agricultural Sciences, with black tea samples of Wuyi Congou tea prepared in 2024. The artificial pigments utilized in this study were Tartrazine (C16H9N4Na3O9S2, 85 %), Carmine (C16H8N2Na2O8S2, AR), and Sunset Yellow (C16H10N2Na2O7S2, 87 %), which were procured from MACKLIN. Fig. 2 illustrates the molecular formulae of the three pigments. The electronic scales used Kaifeng high-precision jewellery-weighing technology, with a known accuracy of 0.001 g ± 0.005 g. Ten 100 g samples of black tea were prepared, one of which was the control sample (pure tea). The remaining nine samples were divided into three groups: Sunset Yellow, Tartrazine, and Carmine. These samples were prepared with adulteration concentrations of 0.1 %, 0.2 %, and 0.3 %. Fig. 1 shows the experimental workflow.

Hyperspectral detection systems and data acquisition

We used the FS-13 HSI system (CHN Spec, China), with a spectral coverage of (391–1001) nm to collect spectral data. Image information was acquired via line scanning (push-broom). The system comprised a hyperspectral camera, a stage, a uniform-illumination light source, an electronically controlled mobile platform, and supporting control software. Prior to the experiment, we opened FigSpec Scan and connected the hyperspectral camera and mobile platform to the computer to ensure normal, stable operation of the equipment. The camera parameters were then set and the platform's moving speed was set to 20 mm/s. The lens aperture was adjusted to achieve the appropriate exposure intensity. Finally, black-and-white calibration was performed and all parameters were kept constant throughout the acquisition process to ensure the accuracy and repeatability of the spectral data. During sample preparation, we spread the processed samples evenly in a standard mold. After lightly pressing and flattening them to maintain consistent thickness and surface flatness, we removed the mold and placed the samples on a mobile platform for image acquisition. To reduce spectral error caused by uneven surfaces, the samples were poured back into the mold and flattened again after each image frame was acquired, ensuring uniform and representative data. We prepared a total of 10 groups of samples with different treatments and allocated them into a calibration set and a Predictive set in a 7:3 ratio. Ultimately, 270 valid hyperspectral data samples were obtained. Table 1 presents the specific quantities of the various samples. In addition, we performed separate spectral acquisitions to obtain the spectral characteristics of the Sunset yellow, Tartrazine, and Carmine adulterated pigments.

Spectral pre-processed

To improve the stability of the model and the availability of spectral data, Multiplicative Scatter Correction (MSC) (Rinnan et al., 2009) and Detrended Fluctuation Analysis (DFA) were used to pre-processed the raw hyperspectral data (Kantelhardt et al., 2002). MSC corrected the baseline drift and bias of each sample using one-way linear regression with the mean spectra. DFA was utilized as a supervised dimensionality reduction technique to construct in-class and out-of-class scatter matrices, and to extract discriminants that optimally differentiate between various adulteration types for the purposes of feature compression and data visualization.

Characteristic wavelength selection

To extract key variables from high-dimensional spectra, we combined Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Lasso regression for feature selection. PCA achieves unsupervised dimensionality reduction by retaining the main variance information of the data through linear projection, while Lasso sifts out the redundant features in a regression framework with the introduction of L1 regularization; these features provided good sparsity and interpretability. Each method has its own advantages and provides high-quality feature subsets for subsequent modelling.

Model establishment and evaluation indicators

We established the adulteration-type and adulteration-rate detection models. For qualitative identification of adulteration classification, we used three supervised learning models: Random Forest (RF) (Probst et al., 2019), Support Vector Machine (SVM) (Campbell, 2020), and Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) (Barker & Rayens, 2003). For quantitative analysis of adulteration rate, we developed a regression model with feature selection, in which the evaluation metric RMSEP was the standard deviation of the residuals, mean absolute error (MAE) was the mean absolute error, and Rp explained the proportion of variance. Based on the performance of each model on the calibration and predictive set, we combined the results of each evaluation metric and selected the optimal model.

Software

Spectral and image information was collected using FigSpec Scan, the regions of interest were extracted with Envi5.6, and the data was pre-processed, feature-selected, model-constructed, and visualized with Matlab2021b (The Mathworks Inc., Massachusetts, USA).

Results and discussion

MSC and DFA pre-processed

The mean reflectance characteristics of the pure tea samples and samples doped with the three pigments are shown in Fig. 3a. A significant disparity was identified between the pure and pigment-doped tea samples at (500–600) nm and (800–900) nm. Notably, the Carmine-doped tea samples manifested a pronounced absorption peak near 600 nm, whilst the Tartrazine and Sunset Yellow-doped tea samples demonstrated divergent reflectance trends after 700 nm. These disparities in band intensity can be attributed to the distinct light-absorption behaviors of the functional groups contained within the pigment molecules. In combination with Fig. 2, Sunset Yellow (C16H10N2Na2O7S2) contains an azo group (-N=N-) and an aromatic ring, whose π → π* transition produces absorption in the visible region at approximately (480–520) nm, which is superimposed on the water solubility characteristics of its sulphonic acid group. Tartrazine (C16H9N4Na3O9S2) also has an azo bond and several carboxyl/sulphonic acid groups in its structure, with the main absorption occurring at (420–480) nm. However, when it interacted with polyphenols in tea samples, a change in reflectance occurred in the (550–700) nm band, even after the complex reaction. Carmine (C16H8N2Na2O8S2) contains two azo groups and multiple carboxyl and hydroxyl groups, resulting in a pronounced selective absorption within the (580–620) nm range and leading to the formation of distinctive absorption peaks. This was evident as a characteristic trough at 600 nm. The presence of these functional groups leads to the manifestation of a unique ‘spectral fingerprint’ for each pigment, thereby providing an essential spectral foundation for the classification and identification of adulteration. The results following MSC pretreatment were evident, as demonstrated in Fig. 3c. MSC effectively eliminated the spectral baseline drift caused by particle size and scattering effects, thereby accentuating the spectral characteristics. The pre-processed spectra demonstrated higher signal-to-noise ratios and clearer peak shapes in the range of (500–1000) nm. Notably, the characteristic peaks of the Carmine-doped tea samples at 600 nm were more significant, further enhancing the quality of the input data for the classification model.

PCA analysis

As illustrated in Fig. 4a, the raw spectra after PCA downscaling exhibited discernible clustering tendencies in the spatial domain. Notably, PC1 alone accounted for an impressive 84.2 % of the total variance, underscoring the substantial disparities among the various classes in terms of their spectral characteristics. However, we observed that the samples doped with Tartrazine and Sunset Yellow exhibited overlap in the PC2 direction. This finding suggests the potential for baseline drift or scattering interference to affect the integrity of the raw data. As illustrated in Fig. 4b, the pre-processed data obtained from MSC exhibited substantial disparities, accompanied by a notable enhancement in PCA clustering, augmented intra-class aggregation, and a more pronounced convergence of sample points between pure samples and various adulteration types. It was demonstrated that the dispersion of carmine-doped samples was reduced, which confirmed that MSC effectively eliminated the noise caused by particulate scattering. Furthermore, it was established that the inter-class separation was improved, and PC1 (63.7 %) and PC2 (22.6 %) together achieved a clear separation of the four classes of samples. It was particularly noteworthy that Tartrazine and Sunset Yellow were completely distinguished in the PC2 direction. After pre-processed, the proportion of variance explained by PC1 decreased from 84.2 % to 63.7 %, while the proportion explained by PC2 increased from 9.5 % to 22.6 %. This indicates that the MSC optimized the discriminative structure of the multidimensional data by transferring some key features from PC1 to PC2. The MSC pre-processed PCA results provide ideal inputs for classification models, with high intra-class consistency and high inter-class discrimination directly improving model accuracy.

Establishment of qualitative classification model

In this study, we utilized three mainstream machine-learning algorithms, namely SVM, RF, and PLS-DA, to model the classification and recognition of black tea samples adulterated with various synthetic pigments. Table 3 give, the primary evaluation metrics, including classification accuracy, recall, and F1 score, obtained by the three models for the calibration and predictive set. Fig. 5 provides a visual representation of the confusion matrix, showcasing the classification outcomes of the three models. The findings indicate that all three models demonstrated effective calibration set performance, with both the RF and PLS-DA models attaining 100 % classification accuracy, recall, and F1 score. This suggests their capacity to accurately identify various types of adulteration during the training phase. The SVM model, though it had a slightly lower accuracy (97.88 %) and F1 score (96.59 %) compared to the first two models, also exhibited strong fitting ability. With regard to the specific parameters, the number of decision trees utilized in the RF model was 100, the kernel function parameters of the SVM model were c = 512, g = 0.0039, and the principal component scores (PCs) of the PLS-DA model were 10. In terms of the classification effect on the predictive set, the PLS-DA model maintained a 100 % accuracy rate and F1 scores. However, the results were overly idealized and there is a risk of overfitting in high-dimensional data scenarios. Overfitting occurs when a model learns too accurately from the training samples, which can impede the its capacity to generalize to new samples (Lang et al., 2021). Conversely, the accuracy of the RF model in the predictive set exhibited a marginal decline to 98.77 %, while the F1 score registered at 99.07 %, demonstrating slight variability but maintaining a high plateau, and thus showing commendable generalization capability and stability. Conversely, the SVM model demonstrated variability in its performance within the predictive set, exhibiting a decline in accuracy to 97.53 % and an F1 score of 96.02 %. This phenomenon could be attributed to the heightened sensitivity of the SVM model to parameter settings, resulting in diminished robustness when confronted with high-dimensional, noisy data. In summary, all three models have strong classification and identification ability. The PLS-DA model offers certain advantages in hyperspectral dimension compression by extracting latent variables and maximizing the correlation between explanatory and response variables (Fordellone et al., 2018), but the potential risk of overfitting still needs to be noted. The RF model, by virtue of its highly efficient and stable modelling capability and excellent generalization performance, demonstrated better comprehensive performance, especially in applications oriented towards the detection of actual tea-adulteration types. The generalization of the RF model was more prominent, and thus we considered it the optimal choice for a classification and recognition model in this study.

It is important to note that the classification results are closely related to the molecular structure of the pigments and the nature of their chromophores. As outlined in Section 3.1, Carmine displays a pronounced absorption peak at (580–620) nm, corresponding to the π → π* transition between the azo group (-N=N-) and the aromatic ring (Dapson, 2005). This property facilitates its clear distinction from other pigments in PCA space. Tartrazine exhibits a main absorption peak at (420–480) nm, which overlaps with the interaction of tea polyphenols, and also shows significant differences at (550–700) nm (Barrows & Wallin, 2016); Sunset Yellow has a strong absorption peak at (480–520) nm, and the sulfonic acid group confers water solubility, influencing its reflectance characteristics in the near-infrared region (Wojciechowski & Szadowski, 2000) (Alghamdi et al., 2021). In accordance with the previously mentioned spectrochemical principles, the classification of the model can be elucidated as follows: Firstly, the disparities in these characteristic peaks have been shown to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio following MSC preprocessing, thereby augmenting the model's sensitivity to molecular structural variations. Secondly, the characteristic peaks of different pigments generate ‘spectral fingerprint’ distinctions within the spectral space. These distinctions typically manifest as principal variance components oriented in distinct directions within the low-dimensional projection space of PCA. Consequently, the classification algorithm is capable of differentiating between them with a relatively high degree of accuracy. Carmine displays distinct absorption peak positions and intensities in comparison to azo groups. This characteristic generally renders it more independent in feature space, facilitating easier distinction. In contrast, lemon yellow and sunset yellow exhibit comparable degrees of conjugation and absorption ranges. In conditions of high noise or scattering effects, there may be partial overlap between these two dyes, which could explain why certain models exhibit slightly higher misclassification rates when differentiating between them. It is evident that the model's high classification accuracy is contingent not solely on the statistical algorithm itself, but also on the interaction between the distinctive ‘spectral fingerprint’ of pigment molecules and the tea matrix. In the field of honey adulteration detection, Phillips & Abdulla, 2023employed an HSI + ML model to detect adulteration of honey with sugar, achieving a cross-predictive accuracy of 96.39 %. In a study on the adulteration of juice, NIR + RF/LDA was employed to detect mixed juice adulteration, achieving a predictive set accuracy of 98 %, an SVR regression model Rp = 0.989, and RMSEP ≈ 1.683 (Calle et al., 2022). In comparison with the extant literature, the RF model in this study exhibited superior classification performance in the black tea system, particularly with regard to differentiating between chemically similar Tartrazine and Sunset Yellow.

Establishment of the quantitative pigment detection model

As shown in Table 4, the four models were evaluated based on their performance on the calibration and predictive set. The DFA-LASSO-LM model exhibited notable advantages across all three pigment assays, with Rp = 0.9631 and RMSEP = 0.0229 for Carmine detection, Rp = 0.9561 and RMAE = 0.0194 for Tartrazine detection, and Rp = 0.9597 and RMSEP = 0.0230. Fig. 6 shows, the regression-prediction results of the model for different adulteration amounts of the three types of adulterated pigments. First, an examination of all subplots revealed a clear linear trend in the distribution of the predicted points of green (pure tea), light (low adulteration) to dark (high adulteration) samples. The majority of points were found to be in close proximity to the diagonal line, suggesting that the model exhibited not only a high degree of fit (Rp) but also minimal error (RMSEP). Second, the adulteration gradients in the calibration and predictive set exhibited clear differentiation, with distinct intervals between the predicted values of the samples at the four adulteration rates (0, 0.1 %, 0.2 %, and 0.3 %), suggesting that the model has a robust capability to discern subtle variations and is well-suited for the detection of fine-grained adulteration quantities in practical applications. Furthermore, the predictive set (Fig. 6b, d, and f) revealed that the predicted points remained highly concentrated on the target, even in data not involved in the training set. This finding is notable for its absence of systematic deviation or outliers, which further demonstrates the model's superior generalization performance.

The superior performance of DFA-LASSO-LM can be attributed to the elimination of most of the scattering interference by DFA and the selection of feature wavelengths by LASSO regression. The three strategies are integrated to construct a hybrid modelling path that combines physical mechanism-driven learning and statistical learning. DFA first performs spectral difference processing on the hyperspectral data, which significantly suppresses the background scattering and noise interference caused by factors such as sample thickness, surface roughness, and light-intensity fluctuation. This process improves the effective information signal-to-noise ratio. This results in the characteristic absorption peaks of pigment molecules becoming more pronounced. Examples of these molecules include Carmine (600 nm), Tartrazine (430 nm), and Sunset Yellow (500 nm). LASSO regression uses 10-fold cross-predictive to determine the optimal regularization parameter λ, selecting the optimal value by minimizing the MSE path. The features corresponding to the non-zero coefficients are then retained in order to approximate the feature dimension, thereby avoiding the overfitting problem and improving the model's sparsity and interpretability (Fig. 7 for the selected feature bands). The key wavelengths retained by LASSO feature selection closely correspond to the maximum absorption regions of the aforementioned chromophores, thereby demonstrating the chemical interpretability of the wavelength selection. The quantitative model's good linearity and low error can be explained by Beer-Lambert's law: within the absorbable wavelength range, changes in the reflected spectrum are approximately proportional to the pigment content in the sample. Consequently, when the model selects wavelengths precisely at the absorption peak or sideband of the pigment, a linear relationship between concentration and spectral signal emerges. This makes it possible to establish a stable quantitative mapping using LM linear regression methods. The LM linear regression partially performs the numerical mapping between the features and the adulteration amount, thereby ensuring the simplicity and efficiency of modelling. In comparison with the detection of synthetic pigments in milk (RMSEP ≈ 0.035) (Aqeel et al., 2025), the RMSEP in this study was significantly lower in the black tea matrix, indicating that the model has stronger interference suppression capabilities in complex matrices. In summary, DFA-LASSO-LM has been demonstrated to achieve high accuracy and low error in statistical terms. Furthermore, it has been shown to capture the unique spectral response generated by the interaction between pigment molecules and tea leaf matrices in chemical terms. The final regression model demonstrates high accuracy, stability and interpretability, which provides a robust technical foundation for the subsequent construction of online or on-site tea adulteration detection systems.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed a model for the identification and quantitative detection of adulteration in black tea with the addition of three synthetic pigments: Sunset Yellow, Tartrazine, and Carmine. The model was constructed using HIS technology in combination with spectral pre-processed and feature-selection methods. The experimental results demonstrate that:

(1)The HSI is an effective method of capturing the spectral features of tea samples with different types of color adulteration. The key identification band is concentrated in the range of (500–700) nm, which provides strong support for the characteristic response of pigment molecules in the visible region.

Despite the positive results achieved, this study is not without its limitations. First, the experiments were conducted within the confines of a laboratory setting, where the light source and sampling environment were relatively optimal, a condition that may deviate from the actual tea production and distribution processes. Second, the study exclusively encompassed three types of synthetic dyes, omitting a more extensive array of adulterants, such as Chrome Green and Amaranth. Furthermore, the study did not address complex cases of mixed adulteration, such as the simultaneous addition of Carmine and Sunset Yellow in black tea, which to some extent limits the model's general applicability. Furthermore, the study concentrated on a particular tea variety, Wuyi Congou tea, with a comparatively modest sample size and concentration range. This restriction, to a certain extent, limits the model's capacity for extrapolation. We plan to pursue this research in a number of directions. First, the spectral database of pigments and adulterants must be expanded to encompass a greater number of potential illegal additives, and detection strategies for multi-component mixed adulteration must be explored. Second, the experiment should be expanded to encompass a wider range of tea varieties and sample concentrations. This will enhance the model's generalization capability and adaptability. Then, this study aims to construct a real-time online detection system, using embedded hardware and portable imaging devices, with a view to advancing the practicality of this technology in tea processing and quality detection. Finally, we plan to develop lightweight and efficient algorithms, with a view to achieving fast identification under resource constraints and promoting the migration of adulteration-identification algorithms to edge computing and mobile terminals. In summary, the present study validates the feasibility and effectiveness of the HSI and machine-learning-based approach in the expeditious detection of color adulteration in black tea. This provides theoretical support and technical pathways for non-destructive and online tea quality and safety detection. This study provides a demonstration case for the construction of an intelligent and efficient tea quality and safety inspection system. Furthermore, it offers a robust option for sustainable development of the tea industry and for consumer safety.
