TACKLE concussion study: an observational study of post-concussion biomarker and neuroimaging progression in adolescent male school sports-related concussion
Authors: Xuan Vinh To, Brett Robinson, Gary Mitchell, Warren McDonald, Paul Cumming, Jessie Wood, Teresa Mammone, Tracey Evans, Jason Ross, Fatima Nasrallah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionSports-related concussion (SRC) is an established research topic in the context of sport professionals suffering from mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI), but there is scant investigation of SRC arising in school-aged athletes. Effective management of SRC in adolescents is especially dependent on obtaining an understanding of its pathophysiology and the multifaceted nature of recovery. In this planned observational study, we shall investigate the associations among multimodal data comprising blood-based and saliva-based biomarkers, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), quantified susceptibility imaging (QSM), resting state functional connectivity MRI (rsfMRI) and cognitive testing in school rugby players with a conventional diagnosis of concussion. Our objective is to map out a natural history of the post-concussion injury and recovery process as measured by diverse biomarkers.Methods and analysisThis prospective cohort study will enrol 450 male adolescents who participate in sports (including rugby, basketball and swimming). We shall quantify blood biomarker levels (total tau, neurofilament light, glial fibrillar acidic protein and ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase-L1), white matter integrity on DTI, cerebral venous oxygen saturation on QSM, connectivity metrics on rsfMRI and cognitive performance after SRC. We conduct measurements at pre-injury baseline measure and post-SRC at four to five pivotal times: day 1 (day of injury), 3, 6, 13 and 21 (if symptoms persist) post-concussion. Using mixed-effects and trajectory modelling, we shall assess biomarker trajectories.We have secured ethical approval for this study from The University of Queensland’s Human Research Ethics Committee, Queensland. We shall inform participants and/or their guardians verbally and in writing of the study’s scope and procedures as a condition for informed consent. The dissemination of findings shall entail peer-reviewed publications and presentations at national and international conferences and via research and clinical networks. Completion of this study should provide a clearer understanding of anatomic and functional outcomes in adolescents with sports-related concussion.Benefits of the studyThe multimodal investigation of a cohort of adolescents suffering from concussion in the context of community sports should offer broad insight into the effects of mTBI on the developing brain.Keywords: Brain Injuries, Rehabilitation medicine, Neurological injury

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a significant global public health issue, with considerable mortality and loss of productivity as its consequences.1 In Australia, there were an estimated 275 cases of TBI per 100 000 in 2016,2 whereas New Zealand (relevant to the Australian context due to close geographical proximity and similar socio-economic conditions) reported a rate of 790 per 100 000, of whom 95% qualified as mild TBI (mTBI).3 The term mTBI frequently refers to a structural injury of the brain, whereas in common parlance, concussion implies a functional disturbance without evident findings in imaging,4 which might be erroneously dismissed by players and medical professionals as a benign event without long-term consequences for brain health.5 This may be especially problematic in the context of adolescent sports-related head injuries, which occur frequently among contact sport players in rugby union (henceforth known as rugby), rugby league and Australian Football League. mTBI in children potentially imparts a risk for cognitive impairments6 in association with subtle and persistent microstructural changes involving the white matter, which may persist after the resolution of overt symptoms.7 Previous research has suggested cognitive vulnerability irrespective of the incidence of seasonal concussions in teenage contact sport athletes, compared with comparison groups not participating in contact sports.8 Furthermore, affective symptoms were common occurrences in adolescents recovering from sports-related concussion and had significant associations with the delay before return-to-play (RTP).9 One of the contemporary goals for concussion management is to accurately diagnose a concussion and determine the correct RTP point.

Given such considerations, the Concussion in Sport Group (CISG) has developed a tool for the assessment of concussion in sport, the Sport Concussion Assessment Tool (SCAT), which is applicable for adolescents aged 13 years and older and adults.10 However, the assessment tools entail relatively basic metrics of symptom reporting, gait, coordination, speech and cognition, which may not be sensitive enough to capture subtle signs of an injury. World Rugby developed the Head Injury Assessment (HIA) protocol based on the SCAT documents and is a process of multiple assessments on the day of injury (HIA1 form immediately after injury and HIA2 form conducted 3 hours post injury) and the subsequent 2 days post injury (HIA3 forms).11 Such tools are an adjunct to clinical assessment of concussion by medical professionals and provide a standardised assessment that assists in managing injured athletes. Nonetheless, current sideline assessment tools have a conspicuous lack of objective metrics of the severity of the injury. Even when clinical imaging is used, neither CT scan nor conventional structural MRI is highly sensitive for detecting the generally subtle brain abnormalities following mTBIs.12 13 Indeed, detection of any such brain structural defects would necessarily call for reclassification of the injury to a higher grade of TBI. This scenario led to the adoption of more advanced MRI techniques, especially in relation to white matter integrity,14 15 functional connectivity and blood oxygenation changes.16,18 Similarly, there is no consensus about which among the various blood biomarkers of nervous system injury might be most sensitive in the context of adolescents with sports-related concussion.19

In this presentation of the Targeting Amateur Concussions using KnowLedge from neuroimaging and biomarkers to Enhance diagnosis and prevent long-term sequelae of Concussions (TACKLE) protocol design, we outline our plan to undertake a comprehensive multimodal and longitudinal analysis of mTBI markers in a cohort of adolescents experiencing head trauma during participation in contact sports. The main aim of this investigation is to characterise the post-concussion trajectories of a set of brain-specific blood and saliva biomarkers accompanying the symptomatic recovery. A secondary aim is to chart post-concussion trajectories of advanced neuroimaging metrics—including diffusion tensor imaging, quantitative susceptibility mapping and resting-state functional connectivity. Another secondary aim is to evaluate whether biological recovery, as measured by biomarkers, parallels, precedes or lags clinical recovery.

Hypothesis and research questions

Primary hypothesis: relative to baseline and/or low-impact reference controls, concussion is associated with time-dependent changes in blood-derived, saliva-derived and MRI-derived biomarkers, with the largest deviation occurring in the acute and subacute post-injury period and gradual normalisation toward baseline in subsequent follow-ups.

Exploratory research questions: given the high dimensionality and autocorrelating structure of multimodal outcome measures (clinical measures, blood and saliva biomarkers and advanced MRI metrics), we will address the following exploratory questions using latent multi-trajectory subgroup discovery approaches designed to characterise heterogeneity in multiple longitudinal variables.

Do distinct multimodal recovery phenotypes exist, including patterns where biological recovery is broadly concordant with clinical recovery and patterns where biological recovery is discordant (preceding or delayed)?

Methods

The Queensland Brain Institute (Brisbane, Australia), World Rugby (Dublin, Ireland), Rugby Australia (Sydney, Australia) and Trajan Scientific (Melbourne, Australia) conferred in the protocol design. Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics committee at The University of Queensland, Australia (2021/HE002696). This protocol manuscript was prepared according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology’s (STROBE’s) Extension for Sport Injury and Illness Surveillance reporting guideline.20

Study design

This is a 3-year prospective, observational, longitudinal study in male school students aged 14–19 years. Recruitment began at the end of 2022 and occurred alongside competition seasons in early 2023 and is still currently ongoing at the time of this manuscript preparation. Enrolled participants will be assessed for a baseline while they are engaging in low-impact physical exercise, provided they have not had an injury within the previous 8 weeks. During a period of high-impact physical exercise or at the time of a suspected head-knock, the follow-up of the post-concussion assessment will be initiated. The follow-up assessment will begin at day 1 (day of injury), 3, 6, 13 and 21 (if symptoms persist) post-concussion. At each time point (and including baseline): (1) an MRI scan will be attempted to measure structural and functional alterations in the brain (excluding day 1); (2) a blood sample and saliva sample will be collected to measure biomarkers; and (3) clinical and cognitive assessments will be conducted with the HIA protocol21 22 and the Immediate Post-concussion Assessment and Cognitive Testing (ImPACT) tool,23 at intermittent time points at baseline and post-injury day 3. Study participants and recruitment

In total, 450 male school students, 14–19 years of age, will be recruited from 10 high schools located in metropolitan areas across South East Queensland, Australia, including Brisbane, Toowoomba and the Gold Coast. Based on current epidemiological data for adolescent rugby athletes, it is anticipated that approximately 62–64 participants (out of 450 enrolled) will sustain a sport-related concussion. During the study period, we require at least 62 rugby concussions, a minimum of 31 rugby non-concussion comparisons, 31 basketball and 31 swimmer comparisons. Participants will be screened using the following inclusion criteria and categorised into four groups: (1) students participating in rugby (a group for concussed or non-injured rugby comparison groups), or basketball or swimming (a physically demanding and low to no-risk comparison group); and (2) able to undergo an MRI scan. Exclusion criteria are any manifest neurological or intellectual disabilities, or self-reporting of drug or alcohol dependence. Exclusion criteria for participation in the MRI-related data collection procedure include carriage of MRI-incompatible metalware such as heart pacemakers, claustrophobia or unwillingness to participate in the MRI procedure. Should a participant decide to enrol in the study, they and/or parents or guardians can express their interest in participating through an online Google form, prompting the clinical research coordinator to contact them for a discussion of their eligibility. A participant meeting the exclusion criteria for participation in the MRI-related data collection procedure does not automatically exclude them from other components of the study, including bio-sampling, clinical assessments and cognitive evaluations. Once deemed eligible, we shall send the electronic parental consent, participant assent and adult forms via the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap)-based secure web application.

Participants will be allocated to one of three groups:

Concussion group: Active rugby players who sustain a concussion, as determined by clinical assessment using the World Rugby HIA process (using HIA1 and HIA2 on the day of injury and HIA3 2 days post injury).11

Injury surveillance procedures

To ensure comprehensive monitoring of head impacts and potential concussions during training and competition, a multi-tiered surveillance system is implemented as per the school sports guidelines. Game officials and coaching staff are instructed to observe play closely and report any suspected head injuries using standardised incident reporting forms. In addition, some games and selected training sessions are video recorded, providing an objective record for subsequent review by trained research personnel to identify unreported or subtle head impact events. Players are also educated at enrolment on the importance of self-reporting and are required to notify the coaching staff of any head impact or concussion-related symptoms during and after sessions or directly notify the research team. All reported or observed incidents were documented and followed-up with assessments in accordance with the study protocol. This integrated approach—combining real-time observation, video verification and participant self-reporting—was designed to minimise missed events and ensure accurate injury documentation throughout the study period and participant enrolment.

Study procedures

Data Collection: Assessment measures will be performed at different time points as per the schematic provided in figure 1. All data regarding prior history of concussions/head injuries will be collected from school records as per ethics agreements. Any injuries that are incurred during training will also be captured, and participants will be followed-up in line with the study protocol and timelines. Data collected as part of the study will include the following.

Demographic data: Participants will provide demographic information via REDCap, including date of birth, age when starting their sport, duration in sport, position (rugby only), weight, height, mental health diagnoses, current medications, central nervous system conditions, migraine or headache history, prior concussion diagnoses and involvement in other potentially concussive activities.

Neurocognitive/clinical assessments: This will entail the ImPACT and the World Rugby HIA process following injury. The ImPACT assessment is a Food and Drug Administration-approved, computerised medical test that measures cognitive and motor performance in tests of memory, motor speed, reaction time and impulse control, and which takes 20 min for completion. The World Rugby HIA process is a standardised tool for evaluating concussions designed for use by medical professionals.

Study-specific blood biospecimens: Will be collected from each participant, consisting of 25 mL of blood (two 10 mL K2 EDTA tubes, one 5 mL serum-separating tube and a finger prick lancet collection) at each time point. Each collection shall consist of two 10 mL K2 EDTA tubes and one 5 mL serum-separating tube (SST) (Becton Dickinson), plus an 80 μL sample collection in a Mitra clamshell (Neoteryx). The K2 EDTA tubes (and likewise the SST tubes) are centrifuged at 1900×g for 10 min to separate plasma and buffy coat, which are then stored separately at −80°C for later handling and analyses, including DNA extraction. These samples will be analysed for biomarkers using the Quanterix Simoa platform to investigate glial fibrillar acidic protein (GFAP), ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase isozyme L1 (UCH-L1), total tau protein levels, phospho-tau neurofilament-light (NfL), amyloid β 1-40/1-42 (Aβ40/42),24 and neural-derived exosomes for biomarker profile.25 Any remaining blood samples will be stored at The University of Queensland, Queensland Brain Institute. Each participant will provide samples of capillary blood via finger prick (Accu-chek Safe-T-Pro Plus lancet), with storage in a Mitra device at −80°C for a later, phase 2 industry studies. Saliva samples shall be gathered using a DNA/RNA Shielded SafeCollect kit (Zymo Research) and stored at −80°C for the assessment of microRNA changes following concussion.26

Neuroimaging: MRI scans will take place at QScan Radiology clinics in Queensland or at the Medical Imaging Service at Toowoomba Base Hospital, Toowoomba, on 3T Philips scanners. The MRI protocol has been harmonised across all scanners in consultation with Philips headquarters and co-designed with QScan Radiology. Radiologists and data analysis personnel will be blinded when performing assessments of the MRI images. Scan acquisitions include anatomical T1-weighted structural imaging, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and Neurite Orientation Density and Dispersion Index (NODDI) for assessment of white matter integrity, quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) for quantification of cerebral microbleeds, and cerebral venous oxygen saturation, and resting state functional connectivity MRI (rsfMRI) for identifying the strength of brain-wide regional connectivity. The total scan duration will be 30 min. For details of the MR parameters, see table 1.

Return-to-play decisions for all participants will be made by physicians as part of standard clinical care, in accordance with World Rugby’s current guidelines for adolescent athletes. These guidelines mandate a minimum 21-day stand-down period following a diagnosed concussion, providing a standardised and evidence-based framework for RTP clearance. This uniform criterion minimises variability in clinical decision-making and ensures participant safety. The timing of RTP and physician assessments will be documented for all cases to allow transparency and facilitate analysis of any deviations from the standard protocol.

In addition to the 3-day post-injury clinical assessments, all participants undergo a mandatory RTP review between days 17 and 19 following concussion. This review is conducted either by the participant’s general practitioner or by the study physician, in accordance with World Rugby guidelines. The review includes evaluation of persisting symptoms and clinical clearance prior to RTP, ensuring that recovery is adequately monitored beyond the initial assessment period.

Primary and secondary outcomes

Primary outcomes are the longitudinal trajectories of RTP decisions, HIA scores and plasma GFAP and NfL levels following a concussion. RTP is defined as standard clinical care decision by the participant’s physician, in accordance with World Rugby’s current guidelines, and is recorded as an observed clinical outcome, contextualising other findings with reference to current practice. Baseline reference comparisons for HIA, GFAP and NfL trajectories are determined based on data availability and in order of comparability to the post-concussion state: matched individual pre-injury baseline, pre-injury baselines within the rugby cohort and baselines within the low-impact athletes.

Secondary outcomes include: (1) characterisation of individual biomarker trajectories following concussion, including plasma and saliva-derived biomarkers and MRI-derived metrics, across all assessment time points and time to normalisation for each individual biomarker. Biomarker values will be referenced to predefined baseline sets according to data availability and comparability, similar to the approach used for primary outcome; (2) exploratory latent multi-trajectory subgroup discovery: identification of distinct joint recovery phenotypes of clinical symptoms (HIA and RTP) and other biological measures (GFAP, NfL, MRI-derived metrics); (3) exploratory analysis on factors associated with the phenotypes: evaluation of associations between recovery phenotype membership (or posterior probabilities) and pre-injury and exposure-related factors (eg, age, years of play, playing position, weight, height, medical histories and prior concussion diagnoses) and acute injury context and severity indicators captured shortly after injury.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis for primary hypothesis: longitudinal trajectories of plasma GFAP, NfL, UCH-L1, total and phospho-tau, Aβ40/42, HIA scores and RTP decisions following sports-related concussion will be analysed using separate mixed-effect models for each. Continuous outcomes (GFAP, NfL, UCH-L1, total and phospho-tau, Aβ40/42 and HIA scores) will be modelled using linear mixed-effects modelling, and the binary outcome (RTP) will be modelled using generalised linear mixed-effects modelling with a binary logistic regression link. For each of the outcome variables, three separate models will be fitted according to the baseline reference set: matched individual pre-injury baseline, pre-injury baselines within the rugby cohort and baselines within the low-impact athletes. For models based on matched individual pre-injury baselines and rugby cohort baselines, the primary fixed effect is the time since injury. For models based on low-impact athlete comparisons, fixed effects include time since injury, group and time×group interaction. For time since injury is modelled as a categorical variable with baseline as the reference level, statistical inference across follow-up time points will be adjusted for multiple comparisons using false discovery rate correction. Fixed-effect estimates will be reported with 95% CIs.

Statistical analysis for exploratory research question: given the high dimensionality (large number of variables at multiple time points) and correlation structure of the multimodal outcome measures, we will conduct exploratory model-based phenotyping for characterisation of the heterogeneity in joint clinical-biological recovery patterns. These analyses are exploratory and hypothesis-generating; findings will be interpreted using effect sizes, uncertainty and clinical plausibility rather than formal hypothesis testing.

We will identify the latent multi-trajectory recovery subgroups using a finite mixture of multivariate linear regression models fitted to repeated multivariate outcome profiles.32 33 The repeated measures will be arranged in long format, and component-specific regression mean structures will be used to capture differences in trajectory shape over time and within-component dependence among outcomes will be accommodated through component-specific covariance structures. Candidate models with varying numbers of latent components were estimated by likelihood-based expectation-minimisation (EM) optimisation with multiple initialisations,34 and the number of components will be determined using information criterion-based model selection that broadly balances fit, parsimony and cluster separation, including Bayesian information criterion-type (BIC), integrated complete likelihood-type (ICL) and approximate weight of evidence-type (AWE) criteria.35,37 The trajectory analysis method can handle missing data, with the assumption that the data are missing at random (MAR).38 Existing statistical tests for the nature of missing data, for example, Little’s test39 or modified Hawkins’ test,40 only assess whether missing data follows missing completely at random (MCAR); if the test returns an invalid MCAR assumption, there is no test to distinguish between the missing not at random or MAR scenarios. Nevertheless, due to the mild nature of the TBIs in this study, participant attritions are unlikely due to non-random attrition41 where the participant suffers such a debilitating injury that they cannot be followed-up, and thus the nature of missing data are more likely due to ‘administrative error’, which is a plausible cause for MAR or MCAR data.42 Prior TBI studies often deem the MAR assumption of their data as ‘plausible, albeit not testable’.43

This analysis assumes that the data can be described by relatively distinct latent clusters with different joint recovery trajectories or phenotypes (eg, concordant or discordant biological and clinical recovery). Nevertheless, latent cluster discovery-type analysis can overfit and extract ‘fictitious’ groups, especially given the chosen sample size. We will impose a priori constraints and present multiple complementary diagnostics when choosing and interpreting the number of components. Candidate solutions will include a single component model (ie, all clinical and biological trajectories of all participants charted together) and multiclass models of no more than three potential components, unless other criteria strongly support a much higher number of components. The prior-classification BIC-type, ICL-type and AWE-type criteria will be used to assess whether a model with more than one cluster is supported. To further ensure that the classification solution reflects meaningful heterogeneity across components rather than poor and poorly separated clusters, we will apply several prespecified post-classification criteria. The first criterion is minimum cluster size—the smallest cluster should contain at least five participants. The second set of criteria includes a set of posterior classification quality measures: fifth percentile and average posterior probability (APPs >0.7 across all clusters are considered indicative of adequate classification44), relative entropy45 46 (which ranges between 0 and 1, with values closer to 1 indicating higher accuracy and confidence in participants into a hard cluster based on the posterior probabilities, with >0.8 commonly interpreted as having less classification uncertainty47), and mismatch (which is the difference between the estimated probability of a cluster membership and the proportion of participants classified into that cluster based on the highest posterior probability—a mismatch close to 0 suggests an adequate fit and more than 1% indicating potential issues46). The third criterion is the clinical interpretability of the chosen latent cluster discovery solution: plotting of the estimated mean trajectory for each clinical measure or biological biomarker of each cluster in the chosen solution—plots and graphs of mean trajectories provide easier and more intuitive interpretation of joint-trajectories and multi-trajectories latent cluster discovery.48 If the criteria favour a separation solution with number of components=1, we will report that the data do not support distinct recovery phenotypes under this modelling approach.

Interpretation of the recovery clusters will be primarily descriptive: we will plot and present the cluster size and trajectories (in terms of medians/10–90th percentile ranges or means/SD) for each outcome measure (clinical and biological) in each cluster. Components will only be labelled and interpreted as recovery ‘phenotypes’ (eg, concordant vs discordant/delayed/preceding biological recovery relative to clinical recovery), only when most of the specified classification quality criteria indicate high classification quality and clinical coherence. As this type of analysis is exploratory and partially data-driven, none of the aforementioned criteria (BIC, ICL, AWE, cluster size, APPs, relative entropy, mismatch and clinical interpretability) had a universally agreed-on cut-off, nor should they be the sole determinant of the solution suitability and thus should be jointly interpreted.46 Consistent with published guidelines on trajectory modelling, the different identified clusters or phenotypes should not be interpreted as literally distinct entities, but rather as simplifying statistical approximations of the more complex reality.38

For further exploratory analysis based on the recovery phenotypes results, we will assess associations between phenotype membership (or posterior membership probabilities) and prespecified baseline/exposure/injury-context factors using multinomial regression or probability-weighted regression. Candidate factors include age, years of play/age of sport initiation, position/training exposure, prior concussion history, medical history and/or acute context of injury or concussion severity indicators captured shortly post-injury. For clarification and minimisation of statistical circularity and interpretation, association analysis with recovery phenotype will focus on pre-injury, exposure and other injury severity indicators that are not used to define the recovery phenotypes in the trajectory analysis. When and if early post-injury clinical or biological biomarkers are examined, they will be interpreted as prognostic factors rather than causal determinants of phenotype membership. Statistical results of this analysis will be reported as effect estimates with 95% CI (eg, multinomial relative risk ratios/ORs, or marginal changes in predicted phenotype probabilities from posterior-probability-weighted models). Sample sizes used in each analysis and treatment of missing data (eg, with multiple imputation according to our Contemporaneous Outcomes Multiple Imputation (KOMI) framework49) will be documented. The results will be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, with emphasis on effect size, uncertainty and clinical coherence rather than statistical significance based on hard boundaries of recovery phenotypes.

Power calculation

Sensitivity power analyses in G*Power (V.3.1.9.6, Kiel University, Germany) were conducted. For the following specification: t-tests (means: difference between two dependent means (matched pairs)), two-tailed, α=0.05, with n=62 concussed participants providing matched baseline and follow-up data, the study has 80% power to detect an effect size of dz=0.36. For the following specification: t-tests (means: difference between two independent means (two groups)), two-tailed, α=0.05, with n=62 concussed participants compared with n=31 non-concussed rugby participants providing pre-injury baselines, the study has 80% power to detect an effect size of dz=0.55. Likewise, with n=62 concussed participants and n=62 low-impact athlete controls, the study has 80% power to detect an effect size of dz=0.45.

Discussion

Key results

Diagnosis of concussion often relies on self-reported symptoms rather than objective markers. In this protocol, we outline our project to characterise the post-concussion trajectories of objective biometric indices, including fluid biomarkers and advanced MRI metrics, in association with symptom-based assessments. The decision to RTP after a head injury calls for new approaches, especially in the context of school sports and younger athletes, and this study aims to be among the first steps towards a more objective set of criteria. For example, while about 80% of American collegiate athletes were asymptomatic by day 14 after a concussive injury, and had returned to play by day 24, one-half of the ‘slow recovery’ group (the remaining 20% or 10% of the total) had not recovered fully at 6 months post-injury.50 There was an association between lower early (first available score within 48 hours post-injury) symptom severity scores and faster recovery, which was likely a result of a self-selection process whereby those with less severe concussions returned sooner.50 Other research shows that brain imaging findings can persist despite symptomatic recovery 1–2 months post-concussion.7

A number of large prospective studies have addressed the diagnosis and management of concussion/mTBI in the context of junior sports, placing their main emphasis on themes such as clinical symptoms and their evolution, or the use of imaging and blood biomarkers.51 52 The American National Collegiate Athletic Association and Department of Defence Concussion Assessment, Research and Education Consortium53 represents an extremely comprehensive and broad examination of concussion, mainly emphasising collegiate athletes and military service members, and extending over the interval of 1 day to 2 weeks post-injury. The Australasian Paediatric Head Injury Rules Study54 and a comparable Scandinavian study55 mostly emphasised refinement of diagnostic criteria for head injuries to guide acute clinical decision-making, such as whether to order CT scans or reclassify the severity of head injuries.

The TACKLE study is distinct in its intention to capture post-injury clinical and diagnostic data within an adolescent cohort, following the clinical trajectory at multiple time points extending from the day of injury to the recovery phase. We thereby seek to examine the effects of concussions on the adolescent brain, which may present specific vulnerabilities due to its ongoing development and plasticity, through a multi-biomarker approach. Our approach integrates neuroimaging techniques with biomarker quantification to enrich understanding of the effects of mTBI. Our multimodal data and follow-up approach aim to enhance our understanding of outcomes, potentially leading to new criteria for assessment of mild brain injury and decisions about RTP.

Limitations

This study included only male participants because recruitment was centred on an adolescent rugby union pathway in Queensland that, at the time of study conception (2021) and commencement (late 2022), had limited and still-emerging structured participation opportunities for female athletes in the same age-grade and competition format. For example, the inaugural Queensland 16–18 years state team was established in July 2022, at the 50th anniversary of the Schoolboys Rugby League.56 Queensland Rugby Union launched its Schoolgirl Super 7s Series in 2025.57 Consequently, recruitment of a sufficiently sized comparable female cohort would have been very limited. Exclusion of female athletes has been a major issue in sport-related concussion research,58 and in this study context, female athletes have also been excluded from the sport itself. Future studies should prioritise inclusion of female athletes as participation and competition pathways expand, in order to improve generalisability and address the historical inequity.

We designed the TACKLE study to capture baseline measures of relevant variables through a cross-sectional approach in a naturalistic setting, attendant on recruitment of a population of student athletes at risk for head injury during the follow-up interval. While this study design presents advantages for statistical analysis, our recruitment may entail a selection bias for rugby players at particular risk for sustaining an mTBI. However, our broad recruitment of participants with multimodal data at baseline will provide an important normative database for this and any future studies. The planned number of participants in the comparison has sensitivity to detect moderate effect size only, and the sensitivity will be reduced even further after multiple comparison corrections required for the multiple comparisons in the primary hypothesis and research question. The multi-outcome trajectory exploratory analysis was designed to jointly interpret and extract potentially distinct recovery phenotypes from multiple available variables, but this has other limitations. This design was suitable for multiple variables but has the risk of extracting ‘fictitious’ clusters and requires a balance between being a simplifying model for facilitation of interpretation and reflecting complex reality. Ethics approval was obtained for collection of saliva samples but there was insufficient funding for extensive testing of saliva biomarkers; we planned to biobank the saliva samples, alongside leftover blood samples for potential future studies/collaborations.

While the present study is not designed to identify a standard procedure for concussion management, the expected results should inform a future programme aiming to develop recommendations with broad application and implications in student athletics. We note that defining criteria for ‘complete recovery’ would properly call for extensive follow-up extending over years or decades, and TACKLE results shall constitute an important step towards achieving that goal. Results of our prior preclinical investigations in a rodent TBI model show cross-modal differences in the recovery of MRI markers59; in translation to the present case of student athletics, we aim to establish standard procedures for obtaining objective measures of recovery and for risk assessment.

Generalisability: The study’s focus on a high-risk activity (rugby) in a specific age group may limit the generalisability of findings to broader populations or non-athletic contexts.
