Perivascular space volume measurement at the inferior basal ganglia on cranial ultrasound in neonates: associations with germinal matrix hemorrhage and brain maturation
Authors: Se Won Oh, Arum Choi, Dayeon Bak, Jimin Kim, Yoonho Nam, Hyun Gi Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of this study was to assess the feasibility of cranial ultrasound (CUS)–based perivascular space (PVS) volume quantification in neonates, evaluate its relationship with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)–based PVS volume measurements, explore associations with germinal matrix hemorrhage (GMH), and examine longitudinal changes with maturation.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center study included neonates who underwent CUS with PVS volume measurements at term-equivalent age (TEA) between November 2024 and March 2025. Neonates were grouped into those with GMH and controls. Group differences were analyzed and longitudinal changes of CUS-based PVS according to maturation were evaluated using generalized estimating equations in relation to corrected-gestational age (CGA). In neonates with both CUS and MRI at TEA, corresponding PVS volumes were compared using Spearman correlation and Bland-Altman analysis.ResultsA total of 89 neonates were included. Among 19 neonates with GMH (10 males, median interquartile range, gestational age [GA]: 260 [247 to 269] days) and 70 controls (46 males, GA: 265 [254 to 274] days), those with GMH had larger CUS-based PVS volumes (0.052 [0.041 to 0.065] cm3 vs. 0.041 [0.030 to 0.054] cm3, P=0.024). Longitudinal analysis revealed increasing CUS-based PVS volume with CGA in both GMH (0.26 mm3/day, P=0.020) and control groups (0.17 mm3/day, P=0.026). CUS-based PVS volume correlated with MRI-based PVS volume (r=0.453, P=0.026) and showed good agreement on Bland-Altman analysis.ConclusionCUS-based neonatal PVS volume quantification was feasible, correlated with MRI, reflected GMH-related changes, and increased with maturation. CUS-based PVS volume quantification has the potential to serve as a valuable marker of glymphatic function in neonates.Keywords: Perivascular spaces, Cranial ultrasound, Neonate, Germinal matrix hemorrhage, Glymphatic system

Introduction

The glymphatic system, a brain clearance mechanism, plays a vital role in maintaining brain health by facilitating the removal of metabolic waste and the distribution of nutrients [1]. A key component of the glymphatic system is the perivascular space (PVS), which acts as a conduit for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and interstitial fluid transport [2,3]. The development of the PVS and CSF movement within it depends on the maturation of the choroid plexus for CSF production and the blood-brain barrier [4]. Previous studies have demonstrated the significance of assessing PVS volume in neonates, revealing developmental changes related to age [5] or hypoxic-ischemic injury [6]. In older children, larger PVS volumes have shown association with neurologic disorders such as autism [7] or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder [8]. Until now, the evaluation of PVS and their association with the glymphatic system has primarily been studied using brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Cranial ultrasound (CUS) is a noninvasive imaging technique widely used in neonatal care to assess brain structures and detect abnormalities. Unlike brain MRI studies [5–8], however, volumetric assessment of PVS has not been conducted using CUS. Instead, past studies focused the visibility of PVS on CUS in neonates, a finding commonly referred to as lenticulostriate vasculopathy [9,10]. Lenticulostriate vasculopathy is characterized by increased echogenicity of the penetrating lenticulostriate vessels that supply the basal ganglia and portions of the internal capsule [11]. In the past, lenticulostriate vasculopathy has been interpreted as mineralized PVS [12–14], but more recent studies suggest that it represents brain injury rather than a direct inflammatory process [15]. On the other hand, PVS in neonates are predominantly observed in the basal ganglia rather than the white matter, highlighting the lenticulostriate region as a key area for evaluating neonatal PVS and potentially reflecting maturational processes. Therefore, establishing the feasibility and reliability of CUS for PVS assessment comparable to MRI would offer a significant advantage, as CUS is routinely and repeatedly performed in neonates requiring cranial monitoring.

Germinal matrix hemorrhage (GMH) is one of the frequent forms of intracranial hemorrhage observed in preterm infants [16], which can be detected using CUS. GMH originates in the germinal matrix, a richly vascularized and cellular region located in the subependymal area near the lateral ventricles [17]. The germinal matrix is a densely cellular and highly vascularized region situated beneath the ependymal germinal zone, serving as a site for cell migration during brain development [17]. The proliferation and differentiation of neurons with distinct function in the fetus depend on specific regions of the germinal matrix vasculature [18]. Previous studies in mouse models have demonstrated that GMH induces an inflammatory response and astrogliosis, which subsequently impair glymphatic function [19,20], suggesting a potential link between GMH and disruption of the glymphatic system in neonates. However, the relationship between GMH and glymphatic function in neonates remains unexplored.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the feasibility of CUS-based PVS quantification, to examine its relationship with MRI-based PVS volume, to explore its association with the presence of GMH in neonates, and to investigate longitudinal changes in CUS-based PVS volume in relation to maturation.

Materials and Methods

Compliance with Ethical Standards

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. CUS images from one university hospital (Eunpyeong St. Mary’s Hospital, The Catholic University of Korea) were used. This study was approved by the institutional review boards of the hospital (Eunpyeong St. Mary’s Hospital: PC25RISI0029). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective study design.

Study Sample

The study population consisted of patients who underwent CUS between November 2024 and March 2025. All CUS were performed for clinical purposes based on the neonatologist's decision. The clinical indications included suspicion of brain injury or neurological symptoms.

The inclusion criterion was neonates who underwent CUS during the study period (November 2024 to March 2025). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) absence of CUS at term-equivalent age (TEA; 37–42 weeks corrected gestational age [CGA]), (2) CUS without PVS measurement, (3) follow-up rather than initial CUS at TEA, and (4) any CUS abnormality other than grade 1 GMH. A total of 89 initial CUS examinations performed at term-equivalent age were included. We divided the neonates into those with GMH (with GMH group) and without GMH or other abnormality on CUS (without GMH group). All neonates in ‘with GMH group’ had grade I GMH [21], which is subependymal bleeding limited to germinal matrix. Neonates with GMH grade 1 on either the right or left side, detected at any time up to or at TEA, were classified into the GMH group. For longitudinal analysis, additional CUS scans before or after the primary exam (the first CUS at TEA) were included. For example, if a neonate underwent CUS at 36, 37, and 38 weeks of CGA, the primary examination was defined as the scan at 37 weeks, as this represented the first CUS obtained at TEA. The scans performed at 36 and 38 weeks were then considered additional CUS examinations for the longitudinal analysis. Follow-up of CUS in our institution was typically every 7 days until discharge, with clinical adjustments. Neonates were typically discharged around term-equivalent age, depending on their clinical condition. Some neonates were followed up on outpatient basis.

To verify the CUS-based PVS volume, we aimed to provide MRI-based PVS volume as the gold standard. For this, we selected a subgroup of patients from the total of 89 neonates to investigate the relationship between PVS volume measured by CUS and brain MRI at TEA. Twelve neonates were finally included to assess the relationship between CUS-based and MRI-based PVS volumes.

We collected demographic information of the included neonates. Clinical information for the neonates, including gestational age (GA) at birth, sex, CGA on CUS, birth weight, mode of delivery, and Apgar scores (1 minute and 5 minutes), was collected.

PVS Volume on CUS

CUS was performed by a single pediatric radiologist with 15 years of experience (H.G.K.), using an ultrasound system (LOGIQ E10, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). For PVS measurement, a 2–9 MHz linear transducer was used to capture coronal and sagittal views. The anterior fontanelle was used as the acoustic window for image acquisition, and gain was adjusted using the standard neonatal cranial preset with minor modifications to optimize visualization of deep structures.

The selection of the PVS region on CUS was guided by a previous study that visualized PVS on 7T MRI [22]. That study demonstrated that the inferior aspect of the putamen contains dilated PVS, in contrast to the more distal PVS that appear as linear structures. To align with these findings, we selected the inferior putaminal region for volumetric PVS evaluation, as this area can be reliably assessed in three dimensions. We further inferred that our measurements corresponded to the inferior portion of the PVS pathway, supported by the presence of lenticulostriate arterial flow on Doppler ultrasonography (Fig. 2). Based on this selection and assumption, the basal ganglia PVS measurement was part of routine CUS practice in our institution during the study period.

An example of the CUS-based measurement of PVS volume is illustrated in Fig. 3. In the coronal plane, the largest hyperechogenic structure located at the caudal and lateral regions of the basal ganglia was identified. The height and width were measured in this coronal view. The corresponding structure was then identified in the sagittal view, and its length was measured from the anterior to posterior margin. PVS volume was calculated using the ellipsoid formula: length×width×height×0.523. Measurements were performed separately for the right and left sides. Depending on the analysis, either the mean of bilateral values (for group comparisons) or individual hemisphere values (for correlation with MRI-based PVS volume) were utilized.

PVS Volume on MRI

All MRI data were acquired using a 3T scanner (Magnetom Vida, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). A 3D T2-weighted sequence, sampling perfection with application-optimized contrasts using different flip angle evolution (SPACE), was obtained using the following parameters: repetition time/echo time=4,000 ms/562 ms; 0.8 mm isotropic voxel size; and a variable flip angle. All neonates were fed, wrapped, and placed in a MedVac infant immobilizer (CFI Medical, Fenton, MI, USA) for magnetic resonance scanning. ITK-SNAP software was used for manual segmentation. A pediatric radiologist with 15 years of experience (H.G.K.) performed primary manual segmentation of the PVS. Segmentation was performed on both sides, right and left. An example and detailed description of the MRI-based PVS segmentation are provided in Supplementary Text 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1.

Interobserver Variability

PVS volume on CUS was measured by a pediatric radiologist and trained researchers. Both the individual side measurements and the average of the two were used for the interobserver variability analysis. Interobserver variability for PVS volume measurement on MRI was not assessed, as our previous research on the same cohort demonstrated excellent agreement (intraclass correlation coefficients [ICC], 0.96; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.88 to 0.99) [23]. A detailed description of the interobserver variability assessment is provided in the Supplementary Text 1.

Statistical Analysis

To assess the relationship between PVS volume measured by CUS and brain MRI, Spearman correlation was performed. Bland-Altman plot was used to evaluate the agreement between the two methods and to identify any proportional bias. Neonatal characteristics between the two groups of neonates with and without GMH were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test or Fisher’s exact test. For the longitudinal data, we included only patients with repeated measurements (≥2 observations) for analysis. We used generalized estimating equations (GEE) to analyze the relationship between PVS volume, CGA, and GMH status, accounting for repeated measurements. The model included CGA, GMH status, and their interaction as predictors. We also calculated the CGA effect for the GMH group using linear combinations. Interobserver reliability was assessed using ICC with a two-way random-effects model examining consistency. ICC values were interpreted according to established criteria: poor (<0.40), fair (0.40–0.59), good (0.60–0.74), and excellent (≥0.75) reliability. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the geepack and multcomp packages or GraphPad Prism version 10.2.2 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Analyses were performed by H.G.K and A.C., who have 15 and 10 years of statistical experience, respectively.

Results

PVS Volumes on CUS and MRI

There were 12 neonates (8 males, median [interquartile range (IQR)], GA: 246 [234 to 272] days; CGA at CUS: 267 [262 to 279] days) who had both CUS and brain MRI performed at TEA. The median (IQR) interval days between CUS and MRI was 4 (2–7) days. CUS-based PVS volume showed correlation with MRI-based PVS volume (r=0.453; P=0.026) (Fig. 4A). The Bland-Altman analysis demonstrated good agreement between PVS volume measurements obtained using CUS and MRI with a mean bias of 0.024 cm3. The limits of agreement ranged from –0.004 cm3 to 0.052 cm3. No evidence of proportional bias was observed (Fig. 4B).

Interobserver Variability

CUS-based PVS volume showed excellent interobserver agreement: right PVS (ICC, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.71 to 0.86), left PVS (ICC, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.98 to 0.99), and average of both sides PVS (ICC, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.91 to 0.96).

Association of GMH with CUS-Based PVS Volume

There were 19 neonates with GMH (10 males, median [IQR], GA: 260 [247 to 269] days, CGA at CUS: 271 [264 to 279] days) and 70 control neonates without GMH (46 males, GA: 265 [254 to 274] days, CGA at CUS: 271 [263 to 276] days) (Table 1). There was no evidence of differences in sex distribution, GA, CGA at CUS, delivery method, or Apgar scores between the two groups (all P>0.05). When CUS-based PVS volume between the groups with GMH and control neonates were compared, GMH group had significantly larger PVS volumes than controls (GMH vs. controls, 0.052 [0.041 to 0.065] cm³ vs. 0.041 [0.030 to 0.054] cm³; P=0.024) (Fig. 5A). Longitudinal Change of CUS-based PVS Volume According to CGA

A total of 158 time points from 57 neonates were evaluated to assess the longitudinal change in PVS volume according to CGA on CUS. The CGA ranged from 229 to 357 days. GEE models revealed a positive association between CUS-based PVS volume and CGA (Fig. 5B). This association was consistent in both the GMH group (estimated coefficient, 0.26 mm3 per CGA day; P=0.020) and control group (estimated coefficient, 0.17 mm3 per CGA day; P=0.026). When assessing the association between the presence of GMH and CUS-based PVS volume with respect to CGA, no evidence of relationship was found (P=0.502).

Discussion

PVSs, increasingly recognized as markers of glymphatic system function, have been predominantly studied using MRI. However, their volumetric assessment using CUS in the neonatal brain remains unexplored, despite CUS being a widely used, noninvasive, and portable imaging modality in neonates. In this study, we were able to quantify PVS volume on neonatal CUS and identified a relationship with MRI-based PVS volumes (coefficient, 0.453; P=0.026). Additionally, CUS-based PVS volume differed between neonates with and without GMH (P=0.024). Longitudinal analysis further revealed a positive association between CUS-based PVS volume and CGA at the time of imaging.

Our study showed that PVS volume could be quantified by measuring its width, height, and length in coronal and sagittal views on CUS. The CUS-based PVS volume demonstrated a correlation with the gold standard, the MRI-based PVS volume. The spatial distribution of PVS in the basal ganglia closely aligns with the course of the lenticulostriate arteries [22]. It is noteworthy that the region of interest in our study differs from the conventional areas examined in previous CUS studies of lenticulostriate vasculopathy [9-11] or MRI studies of PVS scoring that focus on the PVS areas above the level of the anterior commissure [24,25]. We specifically targeted the caudal portion of the basal ganglia, the origin site of the lenticulostriate arteries. This region of CUS-based PVS correlates more with basal ganglia PVS that were automatically quantified from 3D T2-weighted MRI images, as those volumes included the caudal portion of the basal ganglia [5,26]. Notably, our Bland-Altman analysis revealed a positive mean difference, indicating that CUS tended to overestimate PVS volume compared to MRI. This overestimation is likely due to the use of a conventional volume calculation method that assumes a spherical structure, which does not accurately reflect the irregular morphology of the PVS. In addition, the interval between the two examinations may also have contributed to the observed differences. Although a good correlation was observed between CUS-based and MRI-based PVS volume measurements, this correlation does not necessarily imply biological equivalence between the two modalities. Histologic validation is required to confirm that these distinct measurement approaches reflect similar biological entities.

When we compared the CUS-based PVS volume between neonates with and without GMH, GMH group showed larger PVS volume compared to those without GMH. In adults, enlarged PVS have been studied as risk factors for intracranial hemorrhage [27]; however, few studies have shown hemorrhage to be a cause of PVS enlargement. Our findings highlight a potential link between GMH and dysfunction of the glymphatic system. The enlarged PVS in the GMH group may reflect impaired glymphatic flow due to hemorrhagic damage or secondary inflammation in the periventricular region. GMH results in significant structural and functional disruption within the germinal matrix, including vascular fragility, blood-brain barrier breakdown, and subsequent neuroinflammation [17]. These pathological processes could obstruct CSF clearance pathways, leading to PVS dilatation. Additionally, iron overload and thrombin toxicity, both emphasized in the molecular mechanisms of GMH, are known to promote fibrosis and edema [17], further compromising glymphatic circulation. GMH induces significant astrocyte proliferation, evidenced by elevated glial fibrillary acidic protein expression in a mouse study [19]. Since astrocytes play a central role in regulating the glymphatic system, particularly through their endfeet that express aquaporin-4 channels [2,28], this observation may also imply that glymphatic function is compromised following GMH. However, as GMH grade 1, which constituted the comparative cohort in our study, is generally associated with limited clinical implications, the mechanisms linking GMH grade 1 to PVS enlargement should be interpreted with caution and require further validation in future studies.

The association between CUS-based PVS volume and CGA is consistent with previous studies on glymphatic imaging markers in neonates [5,29], suggesting developmental progression of glymphatic function. In a previous study, automated segmentation of PVS volume was performed, and the volume was normalized by the basal ganglia volume to calculate the fraction of PVS [5]. This fraction of MRI-visible basal ganglia PVS showed a negative correlation with postmenstrual age at the time of the MRI scan [5]. In the study, however, there was no significant relationship between the absolute PVS volume and postmenstrual age at MRI scan [5]. Since our measurement focused on a portion of the basal ganglia PVS, excluding the cranial part (i.e., the distal segments of the lenticulostriate vessels), a direct comparison with the previous study using MRI-based PVS volume is not possible. Nevertheless, because our study utilized longitudinal data on PVS in neonates, it may provide a more reliable assessment of the positive association between CUS-based PVS volume and CGA compared to previous cross-sectional studies.

Our study has several limitations. First, we were unable to reliably assess interobserver variability in CUS-based PVS volume measurements, as all scans were performed by a single radiologist. Given that the orientation of the CUS, specifically the coronal and sagittal sections, can influence the measured PVS volume, interobserver variability remains an important limitation and should be addressed in future studies with multiple CUS performers. We believe that acquiring cine images in both coronal and sagittal planes, rather than relying on selected static images, may reduce variability in image selection among operators. Second, while neonates without GMH were used as the control group, they were not entirely free of other pathological conditions. Previous studies have shown that MRI-visible PVS volume differs between preterm and term neonates [5]. Since our GMH group included both preterm and term neonates, we similarly included both preterm and term neonates in the control group. There was no evidence of differences in demographic or birth history characteristics between the two groups, supporting the use of neonates without GMH as a reasonable control group. Nonetheless, conducting a prospective study with a cohort of neonates with minimal or no pathological conditions would further strengthen the validity of our findings. Third, the PVS region selected in our study represents the most caudal (and thus most proximal) portion of the basal ganglia PVS pathway, which may make direct comparison with prior studies challenging, as previous work has largely focused on more cranial (distal) regions. It remains unclear whether this caudal region can serve as a glymphatic imaging marker that corresponds well with findings from other studies, or whether this region possesses its own distinct physiological role. Future research is needed to clarify these possibilities. Fourth, our longitudinal evaluation of CUS-based PVS volume, is inherently limited to the age range during which CUS is feasible. In our study, the maximum CGA on CUS for longitudinal assessment was 357 days (approximately 11 months). As CUS image quality depends on probe availability and spatial resolution, both of which decline with increasing age, older infants may derive limited benefit from this evaluation, indicating that CUS-based PVS assessment is confined to a relatively narrow developmental window.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the feasibility of quantifying PVS volume using CUS in neonates. We found a significant correlation between PVS volumes measured on CUS and MRI, supporting the potential of CUS as a reliable, noninvasive tool for assessing glymphatic markers in the neonatal brain. Moreover, CUS-based PVS volumes were significantly higher in neonates with GMH compared to those without, suggesting a link between GMH and glymphatic dysfunction. The positive association between longitudinal CUS-based PVS volume and CGA highlights the developmental progression of glymphatic function as captured through CUS. Future research should investigate the clinical significance of CUS-based PVS volume assessment in larger, diverse, and prospective cohorts, including neonates with a range of pathological conditions such as hypoxic-ischemic injury.

Key points

Neonates with germinal matrix hemorrhage (GMH) had larger perivascular space (PVS) volumes than those without GMH (P=0.024). Longitudinal analysis of neonates showed increasing cranial ultrasound (CUS)–based PVS volume with corrected gestational age at CUS in both GMH (P=0.020) and control groups (P=0.026). CUS-based PVS volume showed correlation with magnetic resonance imaging–based PVS volume (r=0.453, P=0.026).
