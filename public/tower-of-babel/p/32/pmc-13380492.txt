Single-cell and longitudinal transcriptomics-guided engineering of FZD1-targeting precision nanotherapy against osteosarcoma cancer stem cells
Authors: Zhiyu Chen, Zimeng Li, Wanli Yu, Huanhuan Huang, Junhong Chen, Yunsheng Jiang, Xu Zhou, Ling Zhong, Hongmei Zhuo, Yukun Jia, Qiaochu Li, Yang Huang, Cheng Chen, Jianxiang Zhang, Zhengxue Quan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteosarcoma is the most prevalent primary malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents. Osteosarcoma cancer stem cells (OCSCs) critically drive tumorigenesis and progression through their self-renewal capacity and therapy resistance. Understanding mechanisms maintaining OCSC stemness and identifying key therapeutic targets are thus critical for effective intervention. Here, we systematically analyzed OCSC transcriptomic features using single-cell and longitudinal RNA-sequencing, identifying transcription factor 7 like 1 (TCF7L1)-associated transcriptional activity in relation to OCSC stemness and frizzled class receptor 1 (FZD1)-associated Wnt/β-catenin signaling as a putative upstream pathway. FZD1 expression significantly correlated with clinical malignancy progression in osteosarcoma. Leveraging these transcriptional signatures, we screened and validated homoharringtonine as a potent therapeutic agent against osteosarcoma and OCSCs. To enhance efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity, an FZD1-targeted, pH/glutathione dual-responsive nanoplatform was engineered to achieve precise drug delivery to OCSC-derived tumors via high-affinity binding of UM206 peptide to FZD1, concurrently enabling tumor microenvironment-triggered homoharringtonine release in acidic, glutathione-rich niches. This integrated approach characterizes OCSC-associated transcriptional regulation and supports further development of OCSC-targeted precision nanotherapies for osteosarcoma.Keywords: Osteosarcoma, Cancer stem cells, Frizzled class receptor 1, Homoharringtonine, Targeted nanomedicine

Introduction

Osteosarcoma, the most prevalent primary malignant bone cancer in children and adolescents, exhibits high aggressiveness and metastatic propensity. While chemotherapy can increase 5-year survival for localized osteosarcoma to approximately 60%, therapeutic progress has stagnated over four decades. Survival rates for metastatic or recurrent cases persist below 30% [1]. Extensive intratumoral heterogeneity of osteosarcoma drives this therapeutic challenge by promoting invasiveness, drug resistance, and treatment failure [2]. Such heterogeneity-fueled recurrence and metastasis constitute primary causes of poor prognosis [3,4], necessitating novel strategies to address osteosarcoma heterogeneity and improve clinical outcomes [5]. The complexity and heterogeneity of osteosarcoma are intimately associated with osteosarcoma cancer stem cells (OCSCs), a critical subpopulation defined by self-renewal capacity and asymmetric division [6]. As principal drivers of osteosarcoma heterogeneity, OCSCs centrally mediate tumor initiation, invasion, metastasis, and recurrence [[7], [8], [9]]. Unlike non-stem cancer cells, OCSCs frequently reside in quiescent or dormant states that enhance survival under stress and confer profound resistance to chemotherapy and apoptotic stimuli [10,11]. By upregulating stemness-associated genes, activating epithelial-mesenchymal transition, enhancing DNA repair mechanisms, and other related pathways, OCSCs potentiate cancer aggressiveness and therapeutic resistance, ultimately driving relapse and treatment failure [12]. However, the molecular signatures and functional mechanisms governing OCSCs remain inadequately defined. Systematic characterization of OCSCs is thus essential to advance osteosarcoma biology understanding and develop effective targeted therapies that address intratumoral heterogeneity challenges and improve patient outcomes.

Precise identification of OCSCs and characterization of their molecular traits are imperative. Remarkably, groundbreaking advancements in high-throughput technologies, particularly single-cell sequencing, have revolutionized molecular profiling of cancer stem cells [13,14]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has been extensively applied to decode osteosarcoma heterogeneity. This enables identification of chemotherapy-resistant transcriptional signatures [15], elucidation of complex immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments [16,17], development of differentiation trajectory-based classifications [18], and characterization of cancer-associated fibroblast roles in osteosarcoma recurrence and prognosis [19]. However, systematic scRNA-seq investigation of stemness-related characteristics in osteosarcoma remains limited. Stemness-focused scRNA-seq can resolve OCSC programs, thereby refining diagnosis and enabling rational design of targeted therapies.

Beyond identifying and characterizing OCSCs, targeting these cells presents dual challenges and therapeutic opportunities. A major obstacle is intrinsic resistance of OCSCs to conventional therapies, demanding discovery of effective agents. Recent advances leveraging gene expression patterns for small-molecule drug screening have accelerated drug discovery and repurposing [20,21], demonstrating broad translational potential [[22], [23], [24], [25], [26]]. This approach offers novel strategies for developing OCSC-targeted therapeutics. Beyond drug discovery, efficient delivery remains critical. Identification of novel targets and tumor microenvironment features, such as pronounced acidosis and elevated glutathione (GSH) levels in OCSCs [15,27,28], provides new opportunities for precise drug delivery. Microenvironment-guided targeted delivery and controlled drug release have emerged as essential strategies for improving therapeutic outcomes [29]. Integrating advanced drug screening technologies with intelligent nanoscale delivery systems thus shows significant potential to enhance antitumor efficacy and address OCSC-associated therapeutic challenges in osteosarcoma.

This study aimed to delineate cancer stemness-associated transcriptional signatures in osteosarcoma and to develop corresponding targeted therapeutic strategies. We found that transcription factor 7 like 1 (TCF7L1) activity is strongly correlated with osteosarcoma stemness. Furthermore, we discovered frizzled class receptor 1 (FZD1) as an upstream Wnt/β-catenin-associated modulator linked to TCF7L1 transcriptional activity, supporting FZD1 as a potential therapeutic target for stemness-directed intervention. Leveraging OCSC transcriptional profiles, we screened and validated homoharringtonine (HHT), a small molecule drug, as a potent therapeutic agent against both non-stem osteosarcoma cells and OCSCs (Fig. 1a). To enable precise OCSC targeting and tumor microenvironment-responsive drug delivery, an FZD1-directed, pH/GSH dual-responsive HHT nanotherapeutic platform was engineered. This nanotherapy demonstrated robust efficacy in suppressing OCSC-derived tumors (Fig. 1b).

Schematic illustration of stemness marker identification in osteosarcoma, drug screening, and development of a tumor microenvironment-responsive, targeted nanotherapy. a, Identification of FZD1 as a stemness-associated target in osteosarcoma and screening of a potent antitumor drug HHT. b, Engineering of a tumor microenvironment-responsive nanotherapy (HAU) for osteosarcoma/OCSCs treatment by encapsulating HHT in a pH/GSH dual-responsive, FZD1-targeted nanocarrier. Upon specific accumulation in osteosarcoma/OCSCs, HAU enables enhanced cellular internalization and stimuli-triggered drug release, thereby effectively reducing tumor stemness and showing potent anti-tumor activity against OCSC-derived tumors.

Results

Increased transcriptional activity of TCF7L1 is associated with cancer stemness features in osteosarcoma

To dissect the heterogeneity and transcriptional characteristics in osteosarcoma, scRNA-seq data from 17 human osteosarcoma samples, including 145,198 cells, were integrated. Following batch correction, a single-cell atlas of osteosarcoma was obtained (Fig. S1a), with the integrated data demonstrating high quality (Fig. S1b–d). Eight major cell types in osteosarcoma were identified using classical marker genes (Fig. 2a and b), and the top five differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell type were visualized in a heatmap (Fig. S1e). All eight cell types were present in both non-metastatic and metastatic osteosarcoma, with notable changes in the proportions of the “osteosarcoma cell” population (Fig. 2c and Fig. S1f–g).

Focusing on the heterogeneity, a subpopulation analysis of osteosarcoma cells was then performed. Cell cycle quiescence is recognized as one of the characteristics of OCSCs [7,30]. However, in data analysis, a strong background signal from the cell cycle may obscure the underlying fundamental causes for the tumor cell heterogeneity, thereby masking transcriptional features related to cancer stemness. Therefore, the interference from the cell cycle was first removed (Fig. S2a–b). Because OCSCs represent only a small fraction of osteosarcoma cells (approximately 1%-5%) [31], we re-clustered the osteosarcoma-cell compartment at high resolution after cell-cycle regression and Harmony batch correction, yielding 24 transcriptomic subclusters (Fig. 2d and Fig. S2c). These subclusters were used to infer stemness states rather than to define fixed biological lineages.

Cancer stemness represents a dynamic continuum rather than a binary state [32]. Therefore, two more suitable dimensionality reduction methods, diffusion map and force-directed graph, were employed to observe the continuous cellular state transitions in the osteosarcoma subpopulations (Fig. 2e and f). The pseudotime trajectories were calculated for osteosarcoma subpopulations and the differentiation potential was assessed (Fig. S2d–h). By analyzing the derivative of the differentiation potential-pseudotime curve, we identified the pseudotime value (0.46) corresponding to zero slope in the first derivative as the point of most rapid reduction in osteosarcoma differentiation potential (Fig. 2g). Osteosarcoma cells with pseudotime values below 0.46 were exclusively from the osteosarcoma subpopulation 12 (designated as subcell_12), which exhibited the highest differentiation potential, representing 2.02% of the total osteosarcoma cell population. Among the previously reported osteosarcoma stemness-related markers [12,[33], [34], [35], [36]], only SOX2 and PROM1 (CD133 gene) were found to be specifically upregulated in subcell_12, suggesting that, at the transcriptional level, the elevated expression of PROM1 and SOX2 reflects increased cancer stemness (Fig. S2i). The expression-pseudotime curves demonstrated that both PROM1 and SOX2 were highly expressed before the pseudotime 0.46, while nearly zero thereafter (Fig. 2h and i). Additionally, subcell_12 exhibited relatively high stemness based on CytoTRACE2 scores in both diffusion map and force-directed graph (Fig. 2j and k). To validate these findings at the cellular level, sphere formation assays were performed under serum-free, non-adherent conditions to selectively enrich pre-existing stem-like subpopulations from human osteosarcoma cell lines, including HOS, 143B, and MNNG/HOS (MN/H). Compared with parental osteosarcoma cells, these sphere-enriched cells displayed markedly higher mRNA and protein levels of PROM1 (CD133) and SOX2 (Fig. 2l–m and Fig. S3). Based on these results, we propose that subcell_12 represents OCSCs, characterized by the specific high expression of PROM1 and SOX2.

Further, transcription factor (TF) activity analysis was performed using pySCENIC to investigate OCSC transcriptional characteristics. Regulon-based TF activity was distinguished from RNA expression by adding the “(+)” designation to active TFs (e.g., SOX2 for RNA, SOX2(+) for TF activity). Correlation analyses were conducted between the activity scores of all TFs and SOX2(+) activity, as well as between the activity scores of all TFs and SOX2 expression levels (Fig. 2n). TCF7L1(+) was found to rank fourth in correlation with SOX2(+) transcriptional activity and sixth in correlation with SOX2 expression levels (Fig. S4). The highest variance score (0.809) was exhibited by TCF7L1(+) when grouped by OCSCs (subcell_12), indicating that TCF7L1(+) serves as the primary contributor (80.9%) to OCSC stemness, while its direct contribution to metastasis is minor (variance score 0.0014, 0.14%) (Fig. 2o). Consistent with the density localization of PROM1 and SOX2 observed in both UMAP and diffusion map analyses, TCF7L1 expression density was similarly concentrated in subcell_12 (Fig. 2p). Violin plots further revealed elevated expression levels of PROM1, SOX2, and TCF7L1 in OCSCs, with enhanced transcriptional activities of SOX2(+) and TCF7L1(+) also being detected in OCSCs (Fig. 2q–u).

Subsequently, time-dependent RNA-seq analysis was performed on osteosarcoma cells during sphere-forming enrichment for OCSCs at days 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7 in vitro (Fig. S5). Principal component analysis indicated that the transcriptional variations were primarily driven by the cultivation duration of OCSCs rather than differences among osteosarcoma cell lines (Fig. 2v). A progressive increase in the number of DEGs was observed with extended OCSC sphere-forming enrichment, indicating that time-dependent transcriptomic alterations were induced in osteosarcoma cells (Fig. 2w and Fig. S5a). TF activity analyses of time-dependent RNA-seq data revealed a progressive increase in both SOX2(+) and TCF7L1(+) activities with extended OCSC culture duration (Fig. 2x and y).

Previous studies have demonstrated that TCF7L1 exerts bidirectional transcriptional regulatory effects, i.e., transcriptional repression occurs when the Wnt pathway remains inactive, while transcriptional activation of downstream targets is promoted upon Wnt activation [37,38]. Therefore, pathway enrichment analyses were performed. Upregulated trend genes (Cluster 5) based on time-dependent RNA-seq were significantly enriched in the Wnt signaling pathway (Fig. S5b–c). Additionally, a strong positive correlation was observed between the gene set variation analysis (GSVA) scores based on Hallmarks for Wnt/β-catenin signaling and prolonged OCSC culture duration (Fig. S5d). Consistent with these observations, significant activation of the Wnt pathway was detected in subcell_12 by scRNA-seq analysis (Fig. S6). Accordingly, the underlying mechanisms responsible for TCF7L1 activation were further investigated.

FZD1 as a critical marker for stemness and poor prognosis in osteosarcoma

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted using time-dependent RNA-seq data to further identify stemness-related targets in osteosarcoma (Fig. S7). Subsequently, DEGs were screened by comparing osteosarcoma tissues with paired adjacent normal tissues (Fig. S8). The results of both analyses were then intersected with genes exhibiting concurrent elevation in scRNA-seq and upregulation in time-dependent RNA-seq (Fig. 3a), identifying five key genes including FBLIM1, FZD1, LAMC1, MAGED2, and SLC7A8. Their mRNA expression levels increased temporally in time-dependent RNA-seq analysis (Fig. 3b and Fig. S9a). Notably, FBLIM1, FZD1, and SLC7A8 exhibited subcell_12-specific overexpression (Fig. S9b). Further analyses revealed that only FZD1 was significantly upregulated in osteosarcoma tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues, with further elevation in metastatic samples (Fig. S9c–d). Moreover, high FZD1 mRNA expression correlated with poor clinical outcomes, reducing both overall survival and metastasis-free survival (Fig. 3c–d and Fig. S9e–f). At single-cell resolution, the UMAP plot illustrates the FZD1 expression density across osteosarcoma subpopulations (Fig. 3e).

Validation experiments confirmed significantly elevated mRNA and protein expression of FZD1 and TCF7L1 in OCSCs, while CTNNB1 (β-catenin gene) transcript levels remained unchanged (Fig. 3f–i and Fig. S10a-b). Consistently, scRNA-seq analysis detected no subcell_12-specific expression of CTNNB1 (Fig. S10c). Notably, OCSCs exhibited increased total β-catenin protein, accompanied by elevated non-phosphorylated β-catenin (np-β-catenin) and reduced phosphorylated β-catenin (p-β-catenin) (Fig. 3j and Fig. S10d–f). This RNA-protein discordance is consistent with enhanced β-catenin stabilization in OCSCs, although the present gain-of-function evidence does not by itself establish the upstream mechanism. In xenograft models, OCSCs formed significantly larger and heavier tumors than HOS cells, even at a lower inoculation density of 1 × 106 cells (Fig. S10g–i). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed prominent multipolar and asymmetric divisions with heterogeneous nuclear sizes in OCSC-derived tumors (Fig. S10j). Additionally, sustained overexpression of FZD1 and SOX2 was observed in OCSC subcutaneous tumor tissues (Fig. S10k).

Further, we performed immunohistochemistry using osteosarcoma tissue microarrays to assess the correlation between FZD1 and clinical characteristics. Normal bone and bone marrow tissues exhibited low FZD1 expression (Fig. 3k). In contrast, elevated FZD1 expression significantly correlated with advanced tumor node metastasis (TNM) stages in osteosarcoma patients (Fig. 3l). A progressive increase in the proportion of high FZD1 expression was observed from T1N0M0 (5%) through T2N0M0 (29%) to T3N1M0 (60%) (Fig. S11a). Histological analysis of four paired paraffin-embedded specimens confirmed notably elevated FZD1 protein in osteosarcoma versus adjacent muscle (Fig. S11b), with stage IV expression markedly exceeding stages I and II. Collectively, these results indicate that FZD1 may serve as a critical marker for clinical malignant progression of osteosarcoma.

To evaluate the functional relationship between FZD1 and osteosarcoma stemness, we performed loss-of-function validation by knocking down FZD1 using siRNAs. Among the three designed siRNAs targeting FZD1, RT-qPCR validation identified si-FZD1-3 as the most effective knockdown construct (Fig. S12a). Therefore, si-FZD1-3 was selected for subsequent functional assays in HOS cells. Correspondingly, expression of the stemness markers SOX2 and PROM1 decreased substantially after FZD1 knockdown, while TCF7L1 remained unchanged (Fig. S12b–e). FZD1 depletion also markedly suppressed OCSC sphere formation, reducing both the sphere number and size (Fig. S12f–h). Western blot analysis confirmed these findings, showing reduced levels of SOX2 and CD133 upon FZD1 depletion (Fig. S12i–n). Mechanistically, FZD1 knockdown markedly decreased nuclear β-catenin accumulation, as shown by immunofluorescence staining and quantitative analysis (Fig. S12o–p). However, TCF7L1 protein expression was not markedly changed after FZD1 knockdown (Fig. S12q–r). Western blot analysis further showed that FZD1 knockdown increased total GSK-3β levels and reduced its inhibitory phosphorylation at Ser9, which was accompanied by increased phosphorylation of β-catenin at Ser33/Ser37/Thr41 and decreased total β-catenin protein levels (Fig. S12s-z). These loss-of-function data support a role for FZD1 in β-catenin stabilization and nuclear accumulation, while the mechanism by which endogenous FZD1 overexpression promotes β-catenin stabilization in OCSCs requires further validation. Together, these findings support an important role of FZD1-associated β-catenin-dependent signaling in OCSC stemness maintenance.

Moreover, to further validate the functional contribution of FZD1 at the receptor/activity level in osteosarcoma stemness, we inhibited its protein function using a FZD1-specific antagonist peptide UM206. After incubation with 100 μg/mL UM206 for 24 h, HOS cells maintained >70% cell viability (Fig. S13a), indicating minimal cytotoxicity. Of note, this supraphysiological concentration was used as a stringent limit test to exclude intrinsic cytotoxicity of the FZD1-targeting peptide UM206, rather than to simulate physiological exposure. Notably, pretreatment with UM206 significantly reduced the size and number of OCSC spheres in sphere-formation assays (Fig. 3m–n and Fig. S13b–c). UM206 downregulated FZD1 and CTNNB1 mRNA without affecting TCF7L1 (Fig. 3o–q), concomitantly suppressing Wnt downstream gene AXIN2 and OCSC-related genes SOX2 and PROM1 (Fig. 3r–t). Interestingly, UM206 induced internalization of membrane-localized FZD1, showing cytoplasmic co-localization after 2 h of co-incubation (Fig. 3u and Fig. S13d). This ligand-receptor internalization likely represents an essential mechanism by which UM206 inhibits the Wnt pathway. Collectively, these results indicate that elevated FZD1 expression in OCSCs is associated with Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation, TCF7L1-mediated transcriptional activity, and upregulation of the downstream stemness markers SOX2 and PROM1 (Fig. 3v).

Transcriptional profiling identifies BRD-K76674262 as a highly potent therapeutic against osteosarcoma/OCSCs

Although UM206 inhibits osteosarcoma stemness by targeting FZD1, its direct tumoricidal efficacy remains limited. Consequently, identifying a cytotoxic agent with potent tumor-killing capacity is imperative. Leveraging the transcriptional profiles of osteosarcoma and OCSCs, we employed the Connectivity Map (CMap) to screen small-molecule compounds with more potent efficacy against osteosarcoma and OCSCs. To satisfy CMap data input requirements, intersectional gene analysis was first conducted (Fig. S14a). These signature genes were upregulated in osteosarcoma and further elevated in OCSCs (Fig. S14b–c). By screening 25,177 compounds via CMap, BRD-K76674262, i.e., homoharringtonine (HHT) was identified as the top-ranked agent capable of potentially reversing the transcriptional signature of OCSCs (Fig. 4a and Fig. S14d).

To evaluate antitumor efficacy of HHT, osteosarcoma cell viability was determined at 24 h post-treatment using cisplatin (DDP) as a positive control. It was found that the half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of HHT was 0.11 μg/mL in HOS cells, which was significantly lower than that of DDP (3.66 μg/mL) (Fig. 4b and Fig. S15a–c). Similarly, HHT exhibited low IC50 values in both 143B and MN/H cells (0.08 and 0.25 μg/mL; Fig. S15d–g). Following the established sphere-formation protocol (Fig. 3m), DDP showed limited inhibition of OCSC spheres, while HHT significantly suppressed sphere formation (Fig. 4c–e). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) revealed substantially elevated nuclear expression of SOX2 after treatment with 3.7 μg/mL DDP for 24 h. In contrast, substantial reductions in nuclear SOX2 levels were observed in the HHT (0.1 μg/mL) and UM206 (100 μg/mL) treatment groups (Fig. 4f and Fig. S16). Notably, DDP upregulated mRNA levels of SOX2, PROM1, CTNNB1, and AXIN2 in HOS cells, consistent with enhanced Wnt/stemness-associated transcriptional programs.

Conversely, UM206 significantly reduced mRNA levels of FZD1, SOX2, PROM1, CTNNB1, and AXIN2, consistent with its direct blockade of FZD1-mediated Wnt signaling. HHT also downregulated FZD1, PROM1, CTNNB1, and TCF7L1 mRNA levels, although it did not significantly alter SOX2 or AXIN2 expression (Fig. S17a–f). Notably, despite the relatively unchanged SOX2 mRNA levels following HHT treatment, its nuclear protein levels were markedly decreased. This discordance suggests that HHT may inhibit cancer stemness at least partially through post-transcriptional mechanisms, a possibility that will be further investigated below. Next, direct cytotoxic effects of HHT on OCSC spheres were examined. Flow cytometry quantification indicated that HHT induced apoptosis in >60% of cells, while DDP induced only 20-35% apoptosis in OCSCs (Fig. 4g and Fig. S17g–i). Further live/dead staining confirmed that cytotoxicity of HHT on OCSC spheres was dose-dependent (Fig. 4h–i and Fig. S18).

Moreover, in vivo antitumor efficacy of HHT was evaluated in parental HOS cell-derived subcutaneous tumor models (Fig. 4j), with HHT demonstrating a dose-dependent effect. All HHT groups exhibited superior tumor growth suppression compared to DDP (Fig. 4k and l). Significant reduction in tumor weight was observed for HHT at doses higher than 0.5 mg/kg (Fig. 4m). Histopathological analysis revealed extensive necrosis and apoptotic signals in tumors treated with HHT at doses of 0.5 and 2.5 mg/kg, concomitant with significant suppression of proliferation markers Ki-67 and PCNA (Fig. S19). However, prolonged administration of 2.5 mg/kg HHT induced progressive toxicity in nude mice, manifested as body weight loss and kyphosis development (Fig. 4n and Fig. S20a). At this high dose, HHT caused significant hematopoietic toxicity evidenced by the reduced spleen index as well as diminished white blood cell (WBC) and red blood cell (RBC) counts, alongside elevated hepatic and renal indices indicating hepatorenal impairment (Fig. S20b–i). Taken together, while HHT exhibits potent anti-osteosarcoma activity in vivo, its therapeutic window is constrained by dose-limiting hematopoietic and hepatorenal toxicities at relatively high doses (≥2.5 mg/kg).

Preparation and characterization of FZD1-targeted pH/GSH dual-responsive nanoparticles (NPs) for tumor-specific HHT delivery

Building upon these promising findings, we further hypothesize that tumor-specific delivery of HHT can maximize therapeutic efficacy while mitigating systemic toxicity. Capitalizing on distinctive pathophysiological characteristics of OCSCs, such as FZD1 overexpression, elevated GSH levels, and acidic microenvironments, we engineered an intelligent nanocarrier for targeted HHT delivery and stimuli-responsive release in osteosarcoma and OCSCs. Specifically, the high-affinity FZD1-targeting peptide UM206 was used as the active targeting unit. Molecular docking confirmed effective binding of UM206 to the FZD1 extracellular domain, which was preserved following conjugation with 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[methoxy(polyethylene glycol)-2000] (DSPE-PEG), an FDA-approved, PEGylated phospholipid derivative commonly employed in nanotherapeutic formulations (Fig. 5a and Fig. S21a). Molecular dynamics simulations over 100 ns revealed stable binding geometries and consistent interaction patterns for both FZD1/UM206 and FZD1/UM206-conjugated DSPE-PEG (DPU) complexes (Fig. 5b and Fig. S21b–f). Both UM206 (Kd = 2.0 × 10−22 M) and DPU (Kd = 1.6 × 10−25 M) exhibited very low binding free energies with FZD1 (approximately −30 kcal/mol), indicating strong noncovalent interactions with FZD1 (Fig. 5c). Consequently, DPU was synthesized by direct covalent conjugation and confirmed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (Fig. 5d and Fig. S22). Subsequently, a pH/GSH dual-responsive carrier material was engineered to enable tumor-specific hydrolysis and controlled HHT release in the OCSC microenvironment. We designed an acetalated disulfide-bridged β-cyclodextrin dimer (defined as AcsCD), with synthetic procedures detailed schematically (Fig. S23a–c). The structural validation of disulfide-linked β-cyclodextrin dimer (ssCD) and AcsCD was performed using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry and FTIR/1H NMR spectroscopy. Quantitative analysis of the 1H NMR spectrum indicated that the OH-6 groups in β-cyclodextrin were nearly 100% acetalated, while the OH-2 and OH-3 groups were substituted at approximately 65% (Fig. S23d–f). Additionally, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) quantification based on the retention time and peak area integration revealed that the purity of ssCD and AcsCD reached 99.56% and 99.97%, respectively (Fig. S23g). As expected, real-time 1H NMR spectroscopy demonstrated triggerable hydrolysis of AcsCD under acidic conditions (pH 6.5 or pH 5.5) as well as in the presence of 10 mM and 20 mM GSH (Fig. S24).

Then four distinct HHT-loaded NPs were synthesized using either AcsCD or a non-responsive control material poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA). These included two non-responsive controls, i.e., non-targeted HHT/PLGA NPs (HP) and FZD1-targeted HHT/PLGA-UM206 NPs (HPU) (Fig. 5e), and two responsive systems, i.e., non-targeted HHT/AcsCD NPs (HA) and FZD1-targeted HHT/AcsCD-UM206 NPs (HAU) (Fig. 5f). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation revealed spherical morphology for all NPs, with UM206 surface coating visible on targeted formulations following DPU incorporation (Fig. 5g–j). Targeted NPs exhibited slightly larger hydrodynamic diameters (HPU, 193 nm ± 3 nm; HAU, 221 nm ± 9 nm) relative to corresponding non-targeted counterparts (HP, 177 nm ± 5 nm; HA, 212 nm ± 3 nm) (Fig. 5k–n), while all maintained negative ζ-potential values (Fig. 5o). The drug loading content of HHT was determined to be 14.0% for HP, 13.0% for HPU, 12.9% for HA, and 12.8% for HAU (Fig. S25a).

In vitro release tests showed that the non-responsive nanotherapy HPU remained stable with minimal drug release under elevated GSH concentrations, acidic pH, or combined conditions (Fig. 5p). Drug release from HAU accelerated significantly upon either pH reduction or GSH elevation, with higher GSH concentrations further enhancing release kinetics at constant pH (Fig. 5q). Notably, under dual-stimuli conditions (pH 5.5 and 10 mM GSH), HAU achieved approximately 90% drug release within 12 h and nearly complete release by 36 h. These results demonstrate that HAU exhibited desirable pH/GSH dual-responsive drug release behaviors, while HPU was insensitive to both stimuli. To understand release features of NPs under different conditions, typical release kinetic models, including zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, Korsmeyer-Peppas, and logistic models, were applied to fit the cumulative release profiles (Fig. S25b–f). The first-order and logistic models exhibited the best fit, indicating that drug release is primarily a passive diffusion process driven by the drug concentration gradient. At pH 7.4, NPs showed no pH responsiveness, resulting in negligible drug release. Under this condition, cumulative release curves were well described by the first-order and Higuchi models. At pH 6.5 and pH 5.5, the acidic environment led to release exponent values below 0.43 in the Korsmeyer-Peppas model, indicating that the release behavior was governed by Fickian diffusion. The release curves exhibited a rapid initial phase followed by a slow later phase and were consequently well fitted by the first-order model. In the presence of GSH, AcsCD-derived NPs exhibited an accelerated release profile. In this case, the Korsmeyer-Peppas model provided a good fit, suggesting non-Fickian diffusion. Under combined low-pH and GSH conditions, the dual-responsive NPs showed more pronounced and rapid drug release, with the logistic model providing the best fit to the release data. Overall, AcsCD-based NPs are capable of triggered, rapid drug release upon exposure to low pH and high GSH concentrations.

Moreover, all nanoformulations displayed exceptional colloidal stability, as evidenced by negligible changes in the mean diameter and polydispersity index of HP, HPU, HA, and HAU NPs over 48 h of incubation in serum-containing PBS (Fig. S25g–j). In addition, cyanine5 (Cy5)-loaded NPs were prepared for subsequent fluorescence imaging studies (Fig. S26).

Enhanced targeting and uptake of NPs by osteosarcoma and OCSCs via UM206-mediated FZD1 targeting

To evaluate enhancement effects of the FZD1-targeting unit DPU on cellular uptake in osteosarcoma cells or OCSCs in vitro, non-responsive Cy5-labeled NPs, namely Cy5/PLGA NPs (CP) and Cy5/PLGA-UM206 NPs (CPU), were initially utilized. CLSM observation revealed time-dependent uptake of both NPs in HOS cells, with CPU showing superior internalization efficiency (Fig. 6a and Fig. S27). Cytoplasmic distribution of CPU was detectable at 2 h post-incubation, intensifying at 4 and 8 h. By contrast, notable cytoplasmic localization of CP was delayed until 4 h. Flow cytometry confirmed significantly higher mean fluorescence intensity in CPU-treated cells versus CP from 0.5 h onward, with both NPs showing time-dependent uptake kinetics (Fig. S28a–c). To further confirm that UM206-mediated internalization is FZD1 receptor-dependent, HOS cells transfected with si-FZD1-3 were incubated with CAU. FZD1 knockdown significantly reduced CAU internalization compared to FZD1-expressing control cells, demonstrating that the enhanced uptake of UM206-functionalized NPs is specifically attributable to FZD1-mediated active targeting (Fig. S28d–e). In OCSC spheroids, 3D CLSM imaging indicated stronger fluorescence signals in the CPU group compared to the CP group. Similarly, FZD1-targeted Cy5/AcsCD-UM206 NPs (CAU) demonstrated enhanced uptake compared to Cy5/AcsCD NPs (CA) (Fig. 6b and Fig. S29a). Flow cytometric quantification revealed progressively increasing Cy5-positive OCSC populations over time, where targeted NPs (CPU and CAU) consistently outperformed non-targeted NPs (CP and CA) at all intervals (Fig. 6c–f and Fig. S29b). Notably, NPs uptake occurred for nearly all cells in the OCSC spheroids for DPU-functionalized NPs (CPU and CAU) by 8 h. These results validate the role of UM206-derived DPU in enhancing NP internalization in osteosarcoma cells and OCSCs in vitro.

Subsequently, an OCSC-inoculated subcutaneous tumor model was established in nude mice to evaluate in vivo targeting and distribution of different NPs. In vivo fluorescence imaging showed predominant hepatic accumulation of Cy5 signals in 0.5 to 3 h after intravenous (i.v.) injection across all groups (Fig. 6g and h). While hepatic fluorescence progressively decreased, FZD1-targeted NPs (CPU and CAU) exhibited significant tumor accumulation at 6 h post-injection. Conversely, minimal fluorescence was detected at tumor sites in free Cy5 and non-targeted NPs (CP and CA) groups. Tumor fluorescence at the OCSC-derived tumor site subsequently intensified, peaking at 24 h before declining. After 72 h, tumor tissues were excised and ex vivo imaging confirmed high fluorescence intensities in CPU and CAU groups (Fig. 6i and j). Moreover, quantitative analysis across tumors and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) revealed that tumor accumulation in both CPU and CAU groups was markedly higher than that in other organs, further confirming their preferential tumor-targeting capability (Fig. S30). Further CLSM observation of tumor cryosections showed negligible fluorescence in the Cy5 group, while CP and CA groups exhibited non-cellular cavity distribution of Cy5 signals. By contrast, CPU and CAU displayed robust intracellular Cy5 signals co-localized with phalloidin-stained cytoskeleton, showing greater intensity and extensive distribution (Fig. 6k and l). These results suggest that non-targeted NPs accumulation in OCSCs tumors occurs primarily via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect [39], while DPU-mediated active targeting significantly enhances tumor-specific delivery and cellular internalization.

In vitro anti-tumor effects and transcriptomic analysis of mechanisms underlying therapeutic efficacy of HHT nanotherapies

At 24 h after treatment, cytotoxicity was significantly increased in the HAU group compared with the other four groups at HHT concentrations varying from 0.001 to 1 μg/mL (Fig. 7a). Notably, HHT-loaded AcsCD NPs (HA and HAU) exhibited stronger cytotoxic effects against HOS cells than other groups at 0.1 ∼ 10 μg/mL. IC50 analysis indicated negligible impact of PLGA NPs (HP and HPU) on HOS cell viability, while AcsCD NPs substantially reduced IC50 values, with HA at 0.042 μg/mL (2.5-fold lower than free HHT) and HAU at 0.014 μg/mL (7.6-fold reduction versus free HHT) (Fig. S31a). At 0.1 μg/mL HHT, 48 h treatment significantly reduced OCSC spheroid volumes across treatment groups compared to the control group, with HPU, HA, and HAU exhibiting the most pronounced reductions (Fig. 7b). TUNEL staining confirmed extensive apoptosis in HAU-treated OCSC spheroids, contrasting with limited apoptotic cells in other groups (Fig. 7c). Multiplex immunofluorescence analysis revealed strong membrane-localized FZD1 and nuclear TCF7L1 expression in the control group, while their levels were variably reduced across all treatment groups (Fig. 7d). Notably, DPU-functionalized NPs (HPU and HAU) induced marked suppression of both proteins. It is worth noting that blank PLGA NPs, blank AcsCD NPs, and AcsCD degradation products all exhibited negligible cytotoxicity across concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 μg/mL (Fig. S31b–d), confirming that the enhanced anti-tumor effects of HA and HAU are attributable to the HHT payload rather than carrier-induced toxicity. Furthermore, pretreatment with L-buthionine-(S,R)-sulfoximine (L-BSO, a GSH synthesis inhibitor) or bafilomycin A1 (BafA1, a lysosomal acidification inhibitor) significantly attenuated HAU-induced cytotoxicity (Fig. S31e), verifying that the anti-tumor activity of HAU relies on intracellular GSH- and pH-triggered drug release rather than non-specific mechanisms. Collectively, these results demonstrate that HAU exerts the most potent in vitro anti-tumor and anti-stemness effects among all tested formulations.

To further elucidate the molecular basis underlying the anti-tumor and anti-stemness effects of free HHT and HAU, RNA-seq was performed for HOS cells treated with PBS (Control), free HHT, or HAU for 24 h. Principal component analysis revealed distinct clustering of the three groups, indicating that both treatments markedly reprogrammed the transcriptomic landscape of osteosarcoma cells, with HAU inducing a transcriptional profile distinct from that of free HHT (Fig. S32a). Volcano plot analysis further confirmed extensive transcriptional alterations across all three pairwise comparisons, reflecting overlapping but non-identical responses to the two treatments (Fig. S32b–d). Unbiased KEGG-based GSEA showed that HAU significantly suppressed multiple tumor-associated pathways, including pathways in cancer, focal adhesion, tumor growth factor-β signaling, and most prominently the Wnt signaling pathway. By contrast, free HHT elicited broadly similar but weaker transcriptional changes, particularly for the inhibition of Wnt pathway activity (Fig. S32e). To quantitatively assess Wnt pathway activity, GSVA was performed, which revealed a progressive decline in Wnt signaling activity from the Control group to the HHT group and further to the HAU group (Fig. 7e). Consistently, at individual gene levels, HAU significantly downregulated the mRNA expression of FZD1, TCF7L1, CTNNB1, and the canonical Wnt target AXIN2 (Fig. S32f–i). Although free HHT also significantly reduced the expression of FZD1, TCF7L1, and CTNNB1, it failed to induce a significant change in AXIN2 expression. Comparatively, HAU triggered robust AXIN2 suppression, indicating that HAU more comprehensively disrupts downstream Wnt transcriptional output compared to free HHT.

Furthermore, to evaluate the overall impact on cellular stemness, we calculated the mRNA expression-based stemness index (mRNAsi) [40], a transcriptomic metric quantifying the stem-like state of tumor cells. mRNAsi scores decreased progressively from the Control group to the HHT group and further to the HAU group, and HAU treatment nearly eliminated the stemness-associated transcriptional state of osteosarcoma cells (Fig. 7f). Consistent with this trend, HAU-treated cells exhibited lower PROM1 expression than HHT-treated cells (Fig. S32j). Notably, HHT markedly increased SOX2 mRNA levels, whereas HAU restored SOX2 transcription to near-baseline values (Fig. S32k). Meanwhile, HAU produced stronger suppression of SOX2 protein expression than free HHT (Fig. S32l–m). In addition, both HHT and HAU significantly upregulated the pro-apoptotic gene BAX and downregulated the anti-apoptotic gene BCL2, with HAU exerting superior regulatory effects (Fig. S32n–o). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HAU more effectively inhibits the stemness transcriptional program and activates apoptotic signaling in osteosarcoma cells relative to free HHT.

In vivo antitumor efficacy and mechanistic validation of HAU in OCSC-derived tumors

Given the potent inhibitory effects of HAU on OCSCs, its cytotoxic efficacy, and significant tumor-targeting capability, we further evaluated its in vivo therapeutic performance against OCSC-derived tumors. First, a subcutaneous tumor model was established using OCSCs cultured from HOS, followed by different treatments (Fig. 7g). Control tumors exhibited rapid progression with large yet uniform volumes, accompanied by mortality in two nude mice on days 22 and 26, respectively (Fig. 7h and i). All treatment groups demonstrated anti-tumor activity attributable to the potent anticancer activity of HHT. Notably, HAU exerted the most significant anti-OCSC effects, showing significantly reduced tumor volumes and weights versus other groups, along with superior size uniformity indicating exceptional therapeutic consistency (Fig. 7h–j). It is worth noting that HAU treatment induced distinct tumor volume regression initiating at day 12, a response absent in other groups.

During treatment, no statistically significant intergroup differences in body weight changes were observed (Fig. S33a). Preliminary safety assessment revealed that free HHT significantly reduced WBC and RBC counts in nude mice through hematological analysis. Conversely, all NP formulations maintained physiological WBC levels, with active-targeting nanotherapies exhibiting RBC-protective effects (Fig. S33b–c). Platelet counts, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), creatinine (CREA), and urea levels remained stable across groups. Although aspartate aminotransferase (AST) showed slight reduction post-treatment, no intergroup statistical differences were observed (Fig. S33d–h). Furthermore, histological examination showed structural effacement of splenic tissue boundaries in the free HHT group, suggesting potential bone marrow suppression. No splenic abnormalities or pathological alterations in major organs (heart, liver, lungs, and kidneys) were detected in nanotherapy-treated mice (Fig. S33i). Consequently, while a single administration of 0.5 mg/kg HHT adversely affected hematopoietic function, drug-loaded NPs, particularly the active targeting nanotherapy HAU, demonstrated markedly reduced systemic toxicity, underscoring their therapeutic safety profile.

To further evaluate the anti-OCSC efficacy of HAU beyond HOS-based models, a subcutaneous tumor model was established using 143B cell line-derived OCSCs, with survival outcomes monitored over two months (Fig. 7k). During the initial 21-day treatment period, HPU, HA, and HAU groups exhibited suppressed tumor growth, with HAU demonstrating the most pronounced suppression (Fig. 7l). In contrast, the control, HHT, and HP groups showed rapid tumor progression. Following the final administration, tumor growth rates accelerated across all groups. However, 80% (4/5) of HAU-treated mice maintained slower growth kinetics. Survival analysis revealed comparable mortality in the control, HHT, and HP groups, while treatment with HPU, HA, and HAU significantly prolonged survival. Notably, HAU achieved statistically superior survival benefits versus all other treatments (Fig. 7m). These results validate the potent anti-OCSC activity of HAU in two OCSC xenograft models and highlight its promising potential for osteosarcoma therapeutic application.

Moreover, subcutaneous OCSC tumor tissues were employed to further evaluate therapeutic effects of HAU and elucidate its underlying mechanisms. H&E staining revealed intact structure with densely arranged tumor cells for the model group, whereas focal necrosis was observed in free HHT and HP groups. HPU, HA, and HAU groups exhibited enlarged necrotic zones, with HAU demonstrating the most extensive necrosis, with only minimal residual tumor cells retaining normal morphology (Fig. 8a). Immunohistochemical analysis of Ki-67 and PCNA on serial sections confirmed anti-proliferative effects across all treatment groups. Among them, HAU showed nearly complete ablation of proliferating cells (Fig. 8b and c). Consistently, HAU induced the most extensive apoptotic signaling (Fig. 8d and Fig. S34). Meanwhile, Western blot analyses further demonstrated maximal BAX upregulation and maximal BCL-2 suppression in the HAU group (Fig. S35). These results collectively substantiate that HAU elicits the most potent anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects against OCSCs in vivo.

To determine whether HAU suppresses OCSC stemness in vivo, multiple immunofluorescence analyses were performed to assess stemness-related marker expression in OCSCs. Control tissues exhibited widespread expression of SOX2, CD133, FZD1, and TCF7L1. Compared to the control, free HHT and all nanotherapy treatments significantly downregulated SOX2, with HPU and HAU demonstrating particularly robust suppression (Fig. 8e–f and Fig. S36). For FZD1, all nanotherapy groups outperformed free HHT, particularly those containing the FZD1-targeting DPU unit (Fig. 8e and g, and Fig. S36). Notably, HAU nearly abolished FZD1 expression. While free HHT only mildly inhibited TCF7L1 and CD133, all NPs significantly suppressed these markers, with HPU, HA, and HAU showing notably potent downregulation (Fig. 8e and 8h–i, and Fig. S36). In addition, decreased protein expression of FZD1 and TCF7L1 was observed in tumor tissues following treatment with various NPs. Notably, β-catenin levels were significantly reduced in HA and HAU groups compared to other groups (Fig. 8j and Fig. S37). Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis revealed downregulation of OCSC signature genes (SOX2, PROM1) across all treatment groups, with the most pronounced suppression occurring in the HAU group (Fig. 8k and l). Together, these results indicate that HAU treatment potently induces apoptosis in OCSC-derived tumors and strongly suppresses their stemness (Fig. 8m).

Discussion

OCSCs have been recognized as key drivers of osteosarcoma progression and therapeutic resistance. However, the specific molecular regulatory networks and effective drug targets remain poorly understood [7]. This study presents new findings by integrating scRNA-seq data from osteosarcoma with time-dependent RNA-seq analysis, revealing for the first time that FZD1-driven Wnt/β-catenin signaling and associated TCF7L1 transcriptional activity may represent a key regulatory mechanism governing OCSC stemness maintenance. Importantly, this mechanism was significantly correlated with advanced clinical staging and poor patient prognosis. Through CMap reverse screening, we identified HHT as a potential therapeutic agent, and its anti-OCSC efficacy was confirmed by comprehensive in vitro and in vivo studies. Subsequently, we developed an innovative HHT-based nanotherapy (i.e., HAU) by combining UM206 peptide-mediated FZD1 targeting with a tumor microenvironment-responsive delivery system, thereby optimizing in vivo therapeutic efficacy of HHT while minimizing its systemic toxicity. This approach establishes a closed-loop system spanning from target discovery in osteosarcoma/OCSCs to precision drug delivery. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that the engineered HHT nanotherapy can effectively target OCSCs, potently suppress cancer stemness, and exhibit remarkable tumor-killing efficacy. Furthermore, HAU treatment significantly prolonged survival in animal models while reducing systemic toxicity, supporting a promising strategy to address OCSC-associated therapeutic challenges in osteosarcoma management.

The sustained activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in osteosarcoma has been extensively documented [41]. We found that the subcell_12 subpopulation, comprising only 2.02% of osteosarcoma cells, displayed the highest differentiation potential alongside elevated SOX2/PROM1 expression, with concomitant pronounced Wnt pathway activation, aligning with previous studies [8]. Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanisms driving Wnt pathway activation in OCSCs remain elusive. The FZD family, consisting of seven-transmembrane Wnt receptors with both extracellular and intracellular domains, plays a pivotal role in regulating Wnt signal transduction [42]. Our study identified FZD1 as a crucial mediator of OCSC stemness maintenance. siRNA-mediated FZD1 knockdown impaired sphere-forming capacity and reduced SOX2 and CD133 protein expression in OCSCs, indicating a functional role of FZD1 in maintaining stem-like properties. Mechanistically, FZD1 depletion reduced nuclear β-catenin accumulation, decreased inhibitory phosphorylation of GSK-3β at Ser9, and increased degradation-associated phosphorylation of β-catenin at Ser33/Ser37/Thr41, providing evidence for attenuated β-catenin stabilization. These results suggest that FZD1 is linked to TCF7L1 activity primarily through canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling rather than through direct regulation of TCF7L1 expression. Nuclear β-catenin may enhance TCF7L1-dependent transcriptional output as a co-activator [37,38], offering a plausible explanation for the increased TCF7L1 regulon activity observed in OCSCs. Nevertheless, this interpretation remains inferential in the absence of TCF7L1 reporter or ChIP assays. Together, these findings substantiate that FZD1-associated Wnt/β-catenin signaling contributes to OCSC stemness partly through enhanced TCF7L1 transcriptional activity, while the direct transcriptional regulation of SOX2 or PROM1 remains to be fully defined.

Clinically, FZD1 upregulation correlated strongly with advanced osteosarcoma progression and unfavorable patient outcomes. For targeted inhibition, we employed a high-affinity FZD1-binding peptide UM206 [43], which effectively suppressed Wnt pathway activation. Our findings not only corroborate these observations but also further demonstrate that UM206 triggers rapid internalization of the FZD1-UM206 receptor-ligand complex in OCSCs, effectively blocking Wnt signaling and impairing tumor sphere formation. It is noteworthy that FZD1 membrane dynamics may also be modulated by co-regulatory factors such as RNF43/ZNRF3, which promote Frizzled receptor ubiquitination and endocytic turnover to fine-tune Wnt signaling intensity [44]. Whether such mechanisms are relevant in OCSCs warrants future investigation. Additionally, FZD1 overexpression has been implicated in multiple malignancies such as neuroblastoma and breast cancer, where it contributes to drug resistance and aggressive phenotypes [[45], [46], [47]]. Our findings underscore the important functional role of FZD1 in governing osteosarcoma stemness and position it as a promising therapeutic target, though the upstream regulatory machinery of FZD1 receptor homeostasis in OCSCs remains to be fully resolved. Further investigations should explore whether FZD1 inhibition similarly disrupts stemness maintenance in other cancers.

Leveraging the well-established utility of CMap in anticancer drug discovery [22,[48], [49], [50]], we employed CMap reverse matching to identify HHT as a highly cytotoxic agent. HHT demonstrated potent tumoricidal activity against both non-stem osteosarcoma cells and OCSCs, while effectively impairing sphere formation capacity of OCSCs. Notably, the paradoxical enrichment of stemness markers observed upon DDP treatment is consistent with prior reports that cytotoxic chemotherapy can activate Wnt/β-catenin-driven stemness programs, thereby expanding the OCSC pool [8]. This mechanism may partly account for the high recurrence rates observed in patients following standard chemotherapy. The capacity of HHT to suppress multiple Wnt/stemness targets thus positions it as a rational complement to conventional cytotoxic regimens, with the potential to target chemotherapy-enriched OCSCs, although this possibility requires validation in dedicated chemotherapy-resistant or post-treatment residual tumor models. As a known protein synthesis inhibitor, HHT exerts its effects by binding to ribosomes and preventing aminoacyl-tRNA access to the elongation site [[51], [52], [53]]. Interestingly, our RNA-seq data revealed a compensatory upregulation of SOX2 mRNA in the HHT-treated group. Combined with our validated Western blot and immunofluorescence data showing a sharp decrease in SOX2 protein levels, we hypothesize that HHT-mediated ribosomal blockade likely leads to rapid depletion of short-lived stemness proteins like SOX2. In response, cells may trigger a profound compensatory transcriptional activation of SOX2. However, these accumulated mRNAs cannot be translated into functional proteins due to the ribosomal inhibition. This inferred “transcription-translation uncoupling” suggests that HHT intervenes in the stemness network at the post-transcriptional level. Despite remarkable therapeutic potential of HHT against osteosarcoma/OCSCs, our studies revealed that administration of 2.5 mg/kg HHT induced substantial toxicity in nude mice. This limitation likely stems from its potent cytotoxicity, non-specific biodistribution, and poor aqueous solubility [54], which currently constrain its clinical dosing and applicability for solid tumor treatment.

Beyond suppressing OCSC stemness, effective osteosarcoma treatment must also minimize systemic toxicity. Effective tumor-targeted drug delivery is paramount for enhancing therapeutic outcomes while reducing off-target effects. NPs inherently accumulate at tumor sites by the EPR effect [55,56], and active targeting strategies can further enhance tumor-specific accumulation and reduce non-specific distribution, thereby improving both treatment efficacy and safety [57,58]. Consequently, we developed active targeting nanocarriers by peripherally introducing the UM206 peptide. Compared to non-targeted NPs, UM206-functionalized NPs exhibited superior FZD1-targeting capability, enabling precise localization in OCSCs. In addition to targeting efficiency, intelligent stimulus-responsive drug release represents another critical design consideration. Characteristic low pH combined with elevated GSH levels in the tumor microenvironment provides the rationale for our dual-responsive nanocarrier design [[59], [60], [61]]. The engineered FZD1-targeted, pH/GSH dual-responsive HHT nanotherapy HAU maintained excellent stability under physiological conditions while showing rapid drug release in acidic, GSH-rich microenvironments. Furthermore, carrier biocompatibility and inhibitor-based experiments collectively confirmed that the anti-tumor efficacy of HAU is predominantly driven by intracellularly released HHT rather than carrier-derived toxicity or non-specific mechanisms. Importantly, this responsive release property did not compromise in vivo targeting specificity of NPs to OCSCs. Both in vitro and in vivo studies confirmed that UM206 decoration is the dominant factor for tumor accumulation of NPs, and ex vivo imaging-based quantitative analysis of biodistribution profiles further showed reduced nonspecific distribution in major organs, supporting improved in vivo targeting specificity. Notably, HAU demonstrated potent antitumor effects in mouse models of osteosarcoma. Moreover, the low FZD1 expression observed in normal bone and bone marrow tissues, together with the reduced nonspecific distribution in major organs and the absence of obvious histological or hematological toxicity, suggests a limited off-target risk for HAU under the current experimental conditions. Nevertheless, because FZD1 may be expressed in certain physiological contexts, more comprehensive profiling of FZD1 expression across normal tissues and long-term safety evaluation are required before clinical translation. Notably, our engineered targeted nanomedicine HAU not only addresses the systemic safety and delivery limitations of free HHT, but also more effectively suppresses the compensatory Wnt/stemness-associated transcriptional response through synergistic actions of HHT-mediated ribosomal inhibition and UM206-mediated FZD1/Wnt pathway blockade, as evidenced by the more comprehensive suppression of Wnt/stemness-associated transcriptional programs in RNA-seq analysis.

Several limitations should be noted. First, because all in vivo experiments were performed in immunodeficient nude mice, the effects of HAU on adaptive immunity and the tumor immune microenvironment could not be fully assessed [62]. Although differences in myeloid and lymphoid populations were observed between metastatic and non-metastatic samples, and recent advances in osteosarcoma immunotherapy underscore the importance of the tumor immune microenvironment [63], this study focused on tumor-cell-intrinsic OCSC programs. Therefore, the prognostic contribution of these immune populations and their functional interactions with OCSCs, including co-culture models, warrant further investigation. Second, although OCSC-derived xenografts confirmed the tumor-forming capacity of OCSCs and FZD1 knockdown supported its involvement in stemness maintenance, further validation using limiting-dilution transplantation, orthotopic models, and independent scRNA-seq cohorts is required to substantiate OCSC tumor-initiating capacity, relevance to the bone microenvironment, and cross-cohort robustness. Third, therapeutic comparisons did not include a standard MAP (i.e., high-dose methotrexate, doxorubicin, and cisplatin) positive-control arm. Therefore, the current data support the anti-OCSC activity and favorable safety profile of HAU relative to the tested controls, but do not establish superiority over first-line clinical regimens. In addition, possible effects of AcsCD degradation products on stemness-marker expression, intracellular GSH levels, long-term in vivo safety, and biodistribution warrant further evaluation. Finally, future studies employing TCF7L1 reporter assays or ChIP-based approaches will be essential to delineate the mechanistic link between FZD1-associated Wnt/β-catenin signaling and TCF7L1 transcriptional activity in OCSCs.

Conclusion

In summary, our study suggests that FZD1-associated Wnt/β-catenin signaling and TCF7L1-mediated transcriptional activity contribute to OCSC stemness maintenance, as revealed by transcriptional cartography of OCSCs. Building on these findings, we developed HAU, an FZD1-targeted, pH/GSH dual-responsive nanotherapeutic for selective delivery of HHT to OCSCs. This nanotherapy leverages the FZD1-targeting peptide UM206 for OCSC-specific localization, integrated with a tumor microenvironment-responsive nanocarrier that enables spatiotemporal control of drug release. The synergistic design enables selective HHT accumulation and triggered release in OCSCs. Quantitative analysis of biodistribution profiles confirms preferential tumor localization and reduced off-target organ exposure. Consequently, relative to free HHT, this nanotherapeutic strategy achieves potent OCSC eradication, suppresses primary tumor growth, delays the tumor-burden-defined endpoint in subcutaneous xenograft models, and markedly reduces systemic toxicity. Collectively, our findings establish a promising OCSC-targeted nanotherapeutic approach capable of overcoming stemness-associated treatment resistance and expanding the paradigm of solid tumor-directed nanotherapies.
