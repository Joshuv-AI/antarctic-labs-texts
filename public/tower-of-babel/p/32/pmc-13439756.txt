Risk factors for persistent postpartum low back and pelvic pain: a prospective cohort study focusing on trunk muscle mass, delivery mode, and prepregnancy physical activity
Authors: Katsuhito Hayashi, Megumi Koga, Kotaro Takazawa, Ichiro Yasuhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPersistent low back and pelvic pain (pp-LBPP) is a common postpartum condition with important implications for maternal function and quality of life. However, its underlying mechanisms remain insufficiently understood. While reduced trunk muscle mass (TMM) has been associated with chronic low back pain in nonpregnant populations, its relevance in the postpartum period, characterized by unique physiological and biomechanical changes, has not been prospectively evaluated, highlighting a critical knowledge gap in postpartum musculoskeletal health. This study aimed to examine the association between early postpartum TMM and pp-LBPP and to identify independent risk factors.MethodsThis prospective observational study enrolled pregnant women with LBPP at 35 weeks’ gestation. TMM was measured at four weeks postpartum using bioelectrical impedance analysis and indexed by height (TMM-I, kg/m²). Persistent pain was assessed at four months postpartum. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate associations between pp-LBPP and maternal, obstetric, and behavioral variables.ResultsAmong 209 participants, 48% reported pp-LBPP at four months postpartum. Multivariate analysis identified older maternal age (aOR 1.10; 95% CI 1.03–1.18), primiparity (aOR 2.53; 95% CI 1.27-5.02), cesarean delivery (aOR 3.29; 95% CI 1.49-7.26), and lack of pre-pregnancy athletic activity (aOR 2.03; 95% CI 1.02-4.01) as independent risk factors. TMM-I was not significantly associated with pp-LBPP.ConclusionsIn this prospective postpartum cohort, early postpartum TMM was not independently associated with persistent pain, suggesting that muscle quantity alone may not explain pain persistence after childbirth. Instead, obstetric and modifiable behavioral factors were significant contributors. These findings highlight the importance of preventive strategies, including pre-pregnancy physical activity and targeted postpartum care, in this understudied population, shifting the focus from muscle quantity to modifiable clinical and behavioral determinants.

Introduction

Pregnancy-related low back and pelvic pain (LBPP) is a prevalent musculoskeletal condition affecting a substantial proportion of women during and after pregnancy [1]. The condition is associated with functional impairment, reduced quality of life, and the potential transition to chronic pain [2]. While most cases of LBPP resolve within the first three months postpartum, an estimated 15% persist beyond this period [3], constituting a notable burden for affected individuals and healthcare systems [4].

Several studies in nonpregnant populations have suggested that low trunk muscle mass (TMM) may contribute to spinal instability and chronic back pain [5]. However, its relevance in postpartum populations remains unclear, particularly in the context of persistent postpartum LBPP (pp-LBPP). The physiological and biomechanical changes during pregnancy and delivery, including rapid weight gain and hormonal effects on ligaments and muscle tone, may exacerbate musculoskeletal vulnerability [6, 7]. Despite this, few studies have investigated whether postpartum TMM levels are associated with the persistence of LBPP following childbirth.

In addition to physiological measures such as TMM, various obstetric and behavioral risk factors have been implicated in postpartum pain, including parity, delivery mode, and pre-pregnancy physical activity [1, 8, 9]. A better understanding of these risk factors may inform preventive strategies and help clinicians identify women at high risk for persistent symptoms. Therefore, this prospective study aimed to determine whether early postpartum TMM is independently associated with pp-LBPP and to identify other clinical and perinatal predictors of persistent symptoms.

Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted at the National Hospital Organization Nagasaki Medical Center in Japan between July 2020 and January 2022. Pregnant women with self-reported LBPP at 35 weeks’ gestation were invited to participate. Inclusion criteria included age ≥ 20 years, singleton pregnancy, and the presence of LBPP at enrollment. Exclusion criteria were a history of spinal disease, delivery at other facilities, incomplete clinical data, or absence from postpartum follow-up visits.

All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the institutional research ethics board and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Definition and assessment of LBPP

LBPP was defined anatomically as pain located between the 12th rib and the gluteal folds, between the posterior iliac crest and gluteal fold, or near the symphysis pubis (Fig. 1) during ordinary daily activities, including postural changes such as bending or standing. Participants were asked about the presence of pain at baseline and follow-up visits (four weeks and four months postpartum). We defined the pp-LBPP as LBPP at four months postpartum, because musculoskeletal recovery typically stabilizes by that time after parturition [3]. Although lumbar and symphyseal pain may have different pathophysiological bases, in accordance with previous epidemiological studies [3, 8], we collectively defined these as LBPP to capture the overall clinical burden.

Pain intensity was measured using an 11-point numerical rating scale (NRS), where 0 represented no pain and 10 indicated the worst possible pain. Persistent pain was defined as pain continuously reported from late pregnancy through four months postpartum. Women whose pain disappeared completely and then reappeared later were not considered to have persistent pain.

Measurement of trunk muscle mass

TMM was assessed at four weeks postpartum using a multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analyzer (MC-780 A-N; Tanita Corp., Tokyo, Japan). TMM index (TMM-I) was calculated as TMM (kg) divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²). This index was used to account for body size variations across individuals.

Covariates

Baseline and perinatal characteristics were extracted from clinical records and self-administered questionnaires. Variables included maternal age, parity, pre-pregnancy BMI, gestational weight gain, delivery mode, breastfeeding status, history of LBPP before pregnancy, history of athletic activity (participation in sports after age 20), and habitual pre-pregnancy exercise (defined as physical activity ≥ 30 min twice weekly). Regarding cesarean procedure, because Pfannenstiel incision was used in more than 95% of cases and vertical incision in fewer than 5%, we did not consider those cesarean techniques as a confounder. Habitual physical activity was defined as engaging in exercise lasting ≥ 30 min per session, at least twice per week for one year, according to the definition of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan [10].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were compared using t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, and categorical variables using chi-square tests. Logistic regression was used to assess the association between TMM-I and pp-LBPP, defined as continued LBPP at four months postpartum. We also conducted an additional analysis using NRS at four months as a continuous variable in logistic regression. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate models were included in multivariate models. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All analyses were conducted using EZR (version 1.37).

Results

Among 690 screened women, 209 met inclusion criteria and completed follow-up. Of these, 101 women (48%) reported pp-LBPP at four months postpartum (Fig. 2). The NRS (interquartile range) of the intensity of LBPP in women with LBPP was 5 (4–6), 4 (3–5) and 4 (3–5) during pregnancy, four weeks postpartum and four months postpartum, respectively.

Although the absolute TMM was higher in women with pp-LBPP, the TMM-I did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.065) (Table 1). TMM-I was not independently associated with pp-LBPP (Table 2). Analysis using NRS at four months as a continuous variable in logistic regression did not alter the main findings.

Discussion

This prospective study identified key obstetric and behavioral factors associated with pp-LBPP in women who experienced pain during late pregnancy. Nearly half of our participants reported persistent pain at four months postpartum, a prevalence consistent with previous population-based studies reporting rates between 30% and 50% [3, 8]. These findings highlight that pp-LBPP is not a transient postpartum symptom but a major contributor to long-term musculoskeletal morbidity and functional limitation in women of reproductive age.

Although low trunk muscle mass (TMM) has been recognized as a contributor to spinal instability and chronic low back pain in nonpregnant populations [5], our results did not demonstrate an independent association between postpartum TMM and pp-LBPP. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, the postpartum period involves marked physiological fluctuations—fluid redistribution, rapid weight loss, and hormonal changes such as relaxin-mediated ligamentous laxity—that can affect both muscle tone and impedance-based estimates of muscle mass [6, 7]. Second, the quantity of trunk muscle may be less critical than its functional quality, such as neuromuscular coordination and deep stabilizer activation, in maintaining spinal and pelvic stability after childbirth. This interpretation aligns with prior work emphasizing the role of impaired motor control and delayed muscle recovery, rather than absolute muscle mass, in postpartum pain persistence [11]. Furthermore, although bioelectrical impedance analysis provides a practical and noninvasive tool, its accuracy in estimating postpartum muscle composition remains limited compared with imaging-based techniques such as MRI or DXA. Future studies using multimodal approaches to assess both muscle morphology and function would better elucidate these relationships.

In contrast, several obstetric and behavioral variables showed significant associations with pp-LBPP. Primiparity and cesarean section were among the strongest predictors, supporting the notion that both mechanical strain and surgical recovery influence musculoskeletal outcomes. Primiparous women may be particularly vulnerable because of unconditioned pelvic support structures and limited adaptation to postural adaptation to the physical demands of childcare [12]. The association between cesarean section and persistent pain is also biologically plausible. Abdominal wall incision and altered trunk muscle recruitment may delay restoration of core stability and restrict mobility in the early postpartum period, thereby perpetuating pain [13]. Similar associations between cesarean delivery and delayed functional recovery have been reported in other setting [13], emphasizing the need for early mobilization and tailored physiotherapy following surgical delivery.

Importantly, lack of pre-pregnancy athletic activity emerged as a modifiable risk factor. Women without prior athletic experience were approximately twice as likely to develop pp-LBPP. This finding is consistent with previous large-scale cohort studies showing that higher physical activity levels before pregnancy, particularly those involving trunk and pelvic stabilization, reduce the risk of pregnancy-related pelvic girdle pain [9]. Regular exercise may enhance muscle strength, proprioception, and body awareness, thereby providing protective effects against pregnancy-induced biomechanical stress. These results reinforce the importance of promoting physical fitness and core conditioning among women of reproductive age, not only for obstetric outcomes but also for musculoskeletal health.

Taken together, our findings suggest that postpartum musculoskeletal recovery is multifactorial, integrating biological, biomechanical, and behavioral components. Emerging evidence indicates that women with persistent pain exhibit altered body perception and fear of movement, which may contribute to central sensitization and chronic pain syndromes [11]. Although psychological and caregiving factors were not evaluated in our study, these domains likely interact with physical determinants and should be considered in future research. The present findings underscore the need for a multidisciplinary approach involving obstetricians, physiotherapists, and rehabilitation specialists to address the complex determinants of pp-LBPP.

From a clinical perspective, these results highlight several opportunities for early intervention. Screening for high-risk women—those undergoing cesarean delivery, primiparas, or women with low habitual physical activity—could enable targeted postpartum physiotherapy and education. Exercise-based rehabilitation focusing on pelvic and trunk stabilization may facilitate functional recovery and reduce long-term pain burden, consistent with evidence from systematic reviews demonstrating the benefits of targeted exercise therapy during and after pregnancy [14]. Public health programs encouraging pre-pregnancy physical activity may also contribute to primary prevention.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include its prospective design, use of objective muscle mass measurement, and standardized pain assessments. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the bioelectrical impedance method may be influenced by postpartum fluid shifts, potentially reducing measurement accuracy. Second, a substantial proportion of enrolled participants were lost to follow-up, which may introduce selection bias. In addition, we did not perform an a priori sample size calculation because this was an exploratory prospective study. However, post hoc evaluation indicated adequate power (over 0.8) to detect significant associations for the main outcomes. We also should mention that we did not investigate factors associated with childcare burden, which may have affected the pp-LBPP, even though parity did not differ between the groups. Finally, the sample was derived from a single tertiary center in Japan, limiting generalizability.

Conclusion

Nearly half of women with LBPP during late pregnancy reported persistent symptoms at four months postpartum. While TMM was not associated with pain persistence, cesarean delivery, primiparity, and lack of pre-pregnancy physical activity were significant risk factors. These findings emphasize the importance of promoting pre-pregnancy exercise and integrating early postpartum rehabilitation to support recovery and prevent chronic pain.
