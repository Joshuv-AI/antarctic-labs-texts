The Y-Loop for Double-Row Rotator Cuff Repair: An All-Arthroscopic Technique
Authors: Yan Yan, Chen Chen, Chao Fang, Gang Yu, Di Wu, Ying-Ming Wang, Qi-Chun Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rotator cuff tear is a prevalent shoulder condition that poses unique challenges for surgeons. Several locking suture methods based on the concept of single- or double-row fixation have been described and reported to address rotator cuff tears. However, not all methods yield acceptable clinical outcomes. In this article, we describe an all-arthroscopic double-row over-locking suture technique inspired by the H-loop and lark-loop techniques. This method uses a high-strength over-locking suture to create a horizontal stitch configuration that mimics the natural structure of the rotator cuff cable, thereby achieving a tear-resistant loop and initial tendon-to-bone fixation. Final fixation is accomplished using a suture anchor at the greater tuberosity. This technique is easy to perform, requires no additional sutures or stitches, and is particularly suitable for patients with small- to medium-sized rotator cuff tears or tendons of poor quality.

Rotator cuff tear is a prevalent shoulder disorder, accounting for over 50% of all shoulder injuries and serving as the leading cause of shoulder pain and functional impairment.1 The incidence of rotator cuff tear increases with age, with the prevalence exceeding 30% in populations older than 60 years.2 Although conservative treatment can alleviate symptoms to some extent, it fails to achieve structural restoration of the rotator cuff or halt disease progression. Currently, arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) represents the primary surgical method for rotator cuff tear.1,3 Multiple factors influence postoperative tendon-bone interface healing, including age, inflammatory status, rehabilitation compliance, endocrine disorders, and other factors. However, the predominant determinant remains surgical technique.4,5

Multiple arthroscopic techniques exist for rotator cuff repair, including single-row fixation, double-row (DR) constructs, suture-bridge configurations, and interlocking suture methods such as double-locked stitches,6 the modified cow-hitch technique,7 parachute repairs,8 rip-stop reinforcement,9 shoelace constructs,10 Chinese knot stitches,11 the H-loop technique,12 and bilayer lasso-loop repairs.13 Despite these technical innovations, structural failure rates persist at clinically significant levels (≥20%), particularly in patients with degenerative tears with compromised tendon integrity or poor tissue quality.14,15 To minimize retear rates after rotator cuff repair, sports medicine specialists have described diverse suture-locking strategies. However, these technical variations inherently entail distinct learning curves, particularly for methods involving complex suture configurations or supplemental fixation devices—requirements that elevate surgical proficiency demands and prolong operative duration. Generally speaking, surgeons—especially those in the early training phases—show a significant preference for simplified yet biomechanically robust repair techniques with minimized procedural steps.

Therefore, in this article, we describe a suture technique, the Y-loop technique, that resembles the configuration of the shape of the letter Y. The Y-loop technique is an original all-arthroscopic DR over-locking suture technique inspired by the H-loop and lark-loop techniques.12,16 This technique is easy to perform, requires no additional sutures or stitches, and is particularly suitable for patients with small- to medium-sized rotator cuff tears or tendons of poor quality.

Surgical Technique

Patient Positioning and Landmark Identification

After receiving general anesthesia combined with a brachial plexus block, the patient is placed in the beach-chair position for shoulder arthroscopy. The position is established by elevating the trunk 45° to 60° with simultaneous hip flexion to 40° to 50° and knee flexion to 20° to 30°, maintaining the operative shoulder beyond the table edge. The head is stabilized neutrally using a foam headrest to prevent cervical hyperextension. A posterior pad is placed at the medial border to optimize glenohumeral exposure. In addition, the arm is positioned in 20° of forward flexion and 40° of abduction using a hydraulic arm holder. Arthroscopic Portal Placement

The primary posterior portal is established via a 1.5-cm incision positioned inferomedial to the acromion’s posterolateral angle (approximately 1 cm medial and 2 cm distal). Diagnostic arthroscopy is initiated through this portal using a 30° arthroscope to assess intra-articular pathology. Anterior portal placement uses needle localization lateral to the coracoid apex. The standard lateral portal is positioned 2 cm distal to the acromial edge along the supraspinatus tendon longitudinal axis. Supplemental accessory portals may be created as dictated by pathology complexity (Fig 1).

Evaluation of Glenohumeral Joint

The glenohumeral joint is examined through the posterior portal. Then, the anterior portal is established to determine whether the long head of the biceps tendon and subscapularis tendon are torn or inflamed (Fig 2). If arthroscopy reveals injury or inflammation of the long head of the biceps tendon or an associated SLAP lesion, further surgical intervention is performed based on the patient’s age and athletic demands. Should a subscapularis tendon tear be identified, additional fixation at its lesser tuberosity insertion site is warranted. Subacromial Decompression and Rotator Cuff Repair Preparation

After completion of intra-articular procedures in the glenohumeral joint, the arthroscope is transitioned through the posterior portal into the subacromial space. Under direct visualization, a lateral working portal is established. With an arthroscopic shaver (Smith & Nephew) and radiofrequency ablator (Smith & Nephew), inflamed subacromial bursal tissue is systematically excised to achieve complete exposure of the rotator cuff and the undersurface of the acromion with concomitant hemostasis. If significant osteophyte formation and subacromial impingement are identified, an arthroscopic burr (Smith & Nephew) is used to perform acromioplasty.

Subsequently, an anterolateral portal is created. Through combined lateral and anterolateral portals, the rotator cuff tear dimensions, configuration, and tendon mobility under tension are evaluated. The tendon footprint is meticulously prepared using an arthroscopic bone cutter, followed by microfracture awl penetration into the footprint bed to create multiple perforations until bleeding occurs—a critical step to enhance vascular recruitment and tendon-to-bone healing biology (Fig 3).Fig 3Representative arthroscopic view of a right shoulder in the beach-chair position with a 30° arthroscope. Y-Loop Technique for Rotator Cuff Repair

Visualization portal selection—either anterolateral or lateral—is determined by rotator cuff footprint dimensions (Video 1). Primary anchor insertion commences with a triple-loaded suture anchor (Smith & Nephew) positioned at a 45° angle toward the rotator cuff footprint, immediately adjacent to the articular margin at the tear center. All 6 suture strands (from 3 sutures) are sequentially passed through the rotator cuff tissue more than 10 mm medial to the lateral tear edge, ensuring an equidistant strand distribution. Under lateral portal visualization, the strands are tagged with numbers 1 to 6 sequentially from posterior to anterior. Critically, paired strands (strands 1 and 2, strands 3 and 4, and strands 5 and 6) must originate from the same suture (Fig 4).Fig 4Intra-articular arthroscopic image of a right shoulder from the lateral viewing portal with a 30° arthroscope. Postoperative Rehabilitation

Postoperative rehabilitation mandates protective sling immobilization for 4 to 6 weeks, permitting passive range of motion exclusively. The affected extremity is maintained in adduction (15° to 30° of abduction) with neutral rotation and elbow flexion. During weeks 6 to 12, rehabilitation advances to active-assisted motion with scapular-plane restrictions. Resistive training and weight-bearing activities are deferred until at least 12 weeks postoperatively.

Discussion

With accelerating global population aging, rotator cuff tear has emerged as the predominant etiology of shoulder pain and functional impairment.1 Nonoperative interventions—including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, acupuncture, physical therapy, and intra-articular corticosteroid injections—typically show limited clinical efficacy in moderate to severe tears.3,17 Consequently, ARCR has become the gold standard. However, ARCR retear rates remain unacceptably high (≥20%), with contributing factors affecting healing.4,5 Mounting evidence indicates that surgical technique and fixation method constitute the primary determinants of structural integrity.4,14

Contemporary ARCR techniques predominantly derive from single-row or DR fixation principles. The ideal construct should provide (1) biomechanical stability, (2) maximal footprint coverage, (3) anatomic restoration, (4) technical efficiency, and (5) minimal implant burden. Interlocking suture techniques demonstrably enhance repair integrity without compromising tissue quality.18 However, complex configurations necessitate steep learning curves and prolong operative times. Crucially, repair should restore the native anatomy beyond mere tear closure.11 In 1993, Burkhart et al.19 first established the biomechanical significance of the rotator cable in preventing retears. The H-loop technique of Guo et al.12 achieved favorable outcomes by using additional suture to replicating cable mechanics. Building on this foundation, we developed the interlocking-suture Y-loop technique—integrating interlocking sutures with cable simulation. This construct creates continuous transverse stabilization through linked suture loops, complemented by longitudinal fixation via lateral-row anchors, thereby establishing a suture-bridging “lock-stitch” system.

Our prototype uses triple-loaded medial anchors, in which interlocking loops connect 3 knots into a transverse cable. The technique adds only 2 looping steps to conventional DR repairs, resulting in rapid skill acquisition. Adaptable to 2-suture DR, 4-suture DR, and double–lateral row fixation, the Y-loop technique holds promise for other soft-tissue repairs (e.g., meniscus and Achilles tendon). Pearls and Pitfalls of Y-Loop Technique

Anatomic studies confirm a predominantly longitudinal rotator cuff fiber orientation.20 Conventional knotted DR techniques create isolated fixation points, risking transverse fiber disruption. Knotless techniques improve articular surface smoothness but risk longitudinal suture cutting. The H-loop technique introduced transverse stabilization, yet it increased technical complexity by adding suture. The Y-loop technique minimizes transverse stress without extra suture, while the Y-configuration optimizes pressure distribution across the tendon-bone interface, potentially enhancing healing biology. Advantages and Disadvantages of Y-Loop Technique

In general, the Y-loop technique synergizes interlocking sutures with cable simulation to create a biomechanically optimized, technically efficient construct. Early evidence supports its safety and efficacy for the treatment of degenerative small- to medium-sized tears. However, rigorous biomechanical validation and multicenter randomized controlled trials are warranted to assess long-term tendon-bone healing outcomes.
