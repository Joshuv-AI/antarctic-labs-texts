Clinically Relevant Blood Pressure Elevation During Apnea Testing for Brain Death Determination: Association With Circulating Catecholamines
Authors: Geon Hwee Jeon, Dong-Keun Hyun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo characterize hemodynamic changes during apnea testing for brain death determination and evaluate their clinical relevance while exploring associations with circulating catecholamines.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 27 patients who underwent apnea testing between January 2020 and March 2024. Each patient underwent two apnea tests, during which heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured at baseline and within 1–7 minutes after testing. Peak post-test values were compared with baseline values. Plasma catecholamines were measured before and after the first test in a subset of 13 patients.ResultsBoth apnea tests were associated with significant increases in cardiovascular parameters. Median SBP increased from 123.5 mmHg (interquartile range [IQR], 109.2 to 142.0) to 147.5 mmHg (IQR, 128.0 to 178.8; p<0.001). Median HR increased from 81.0 beats/min (IQR, 72.0 to 96.0) to 90.0 beats/min (IQR, 75.2 to 102.2), and DBP increased from 70.0 mmHg (IQR, 62.0 to 81.0) to 82.5 mmHg (IQR, 70.5 to 99.0) (all p<0.001). Post-test SBP exceeded 140 mmHg and 160 mmHg in 61.1% and 38.9% of tests, respectively. Peak mean arterial pressure increased to 105.5 mmHg (IQR, 89.3 to 125.7), exceeding 90 mmHg and 100 mmHg in 72.2% and 57.4% of tests, respectively. In subset analysis, epinephrine increased modestly, whereas norepinephrine and dopamine remained unchanged.ConclusionApnea testing consistently induced clinically relevant increases in blood pressure and HR without corresponding elevations in circulating catecholamines, suggesting additional mechanisms may contribute to these hemodynamic responses during apnea testing.

INTRODUCTION

Brain death, defined as the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem, requires standardized and reproducible assessments for accurate determination, particularly in the context of organ donation.112728) Among these procedures, the apnea test remains a cornerstone examination because it directly evaluates brainstem-mediated respiratory drive under controlled hypercapnia.4112328) The absence of spontaneous respiratory effort under these conditions supports the determination of brain death with a high degree of confidence.11252728)

During apnea testing, clinicians commonly observe abrupt increases in blood pressure and heart rate (HR). Such hemodynamic instability has been associated with test interruption or failure.81415) These responses have been attributed to transient catecholamine surges—epinephrine, norepinephrine, and dopamine—related to autonomic dysregulation and neuroendocrine stress, although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.2622) Hemodynamic fluctuations during testing may alter vascular resistance and cardiac output, raising concerns regarding their potential effects on donor organ stability and graft outcomes.11621)

The organization of the central autonomic and cardiovascular regulatory pathways provides a potential mechanistic framework for these responses.37) Experimental studies suggest that loss of brainstem control may trigger reflex activation of catecholaminergic pathways, including the A5 cell group, leading to sympathetic hyperactivity and acute hypertension.624) Although these findings are largely derived from animal models, they may partially explain the transient hypertensive responses observed clinically.624) In addition, transient sympathetic discharges following acute brain injury may contribute to early hemodynamic instability.5) However, clinical data remain limited, and prior studies have been constrained by small sample sizes and heterogeneous methodologies.815)

Current guidelines emphasize the prevention and management of hypotension during apnea testing but provide limited guidance regarding hypertension, despite its frequent occurrence.41128) This gap is clinically relevant because sustained blood pressure elevation may adversely affect organ perfusion and graft viability.11316)

In this context, catecholamines were selected as candidate mediators because they play a central role in acute autonomic and cardiovascular stress responses.622) Therefore, this study aimed to characterize hemodynamic changes during apnea testing for brain death determination, evaluate their clinical relevance, and explore their association with circulating catecholamines.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 27 consecutive patients who underwent apnea testing for brain death determination at our institution between January 2020 and March 2024. Patients who met the clinical criteria for brain death evaluation and had complete hemodynamic recordings during apnea testing were included. In accordance with the institutional protocol, all enrolled patients successfully completed the standardized evaluation and demonstrated positive results on both the first and second apnea tests, confirming the irreversible absence of spontaneous respiration. Demographic and clinical variables were collected from medical records, including age, sex, cause of brain injury, and vasopressor use at the time of apnea testing. Vasopressor administration was recorded for each test, and concurrent use of multiple agents was permitted.

Apnea testing procedure

Apnea testing was performed in mechanically ventilated patients after preoxygenation with 100% oxygen for at least 10 minutes. Oxygen was delivered through the endotracheal tube during testing. The test was continued until the arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide increased by ≥50% from baseline.

In accordance with the institutional protocol for brain death determination, all patients underwent two apnea tests to confirm the absence of spontaneous respiration. Vital signs, including HR, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), were recorded immediately before each test, and serially within 1–7 minutes after testing. Because the number and timing of post-test measurements varied among patients, the peak post-test value for each parameter was defined as the highest value recorded during the observation period and compared with the corresponding pre-test baseline value.

Catecholamine measurements

Beginning in August 2022, plasma catecholamines (epinephrine, norepinephrine, and dopamine) were measured immediately before and after the first apnea test. Patients were included in the catecholamine analysis if both pre- and post-test samples were available and analyzed using the same assay platform. To ensure consistency, patients with incomplete sampling or those analyzed using an earlier assay version with a narrower detection range were excluded. Values exceeding or below the assay detection limits were assigned the corresponding threshold values for statistical analysis. Serial post-test sampling was not performed to avoid interference with the standardized brain death determination protocol.

Statistical analyses

Because each patient underwent two apnea tests, hemodynamic measurements were analyzed on a per-test basis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR), defined as the interval between the 25th and 75th percentiles, or mean ± standard deviation, as appropriate. Paired comparisons of pre- and post-test values for HR, SBP, DBP, and catecholamine levels were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Effect sizes were calculated as r=Z/√N for significant comparisons.

Associations between changes in vital signs and catecholamine levels were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) because of the small sample size and potential non-normal distribution of variables. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were calculated as supplementary analyses.

To account for potential confounding by vasoactive support, exploratory multivariate linear regression analyses were performed using post-test catecholamine levels as dependent variables and baseline catecholamine levels and vasopressor dosages (norepinephrine, phenylephrine, and vasopressin) as independent variables. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using pre–post changes (Δ) in catecholamine levels as dependent variables. Results are presented as β coefficients with standard errors and 95% confidence intervals. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Inha University Hospital (IRB No. 2021-12-034). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because anonymized patient data were used for analysis.

The primary causes of brain injury were intracerebral hemorrhage (n=11), subarachnoid hemorrhage (n=5), subdural hemorrhage (n=3), hanging (n=3), and other causes (n=5). In the subset of 13 patients with available biochemical data, plasma catecholamine concentrations showed a dissociation from the marked blood pressure elevations observed during apnea testing (TABLE 3). Plasma epinephrine levels increased modestly from a median of 26.5 pg/mL (IQR, 9.1 to 60.3) to 31.8 pg/mL (IQR, 11.2 to 75.9), with a median change (Δ) of 1.6 pg/mL (IQR, 0.3 to 8.5; p=0.033). In contrast, plasma norepinephrine levels remained unchanged, increasing from a median of 1,680.6 pg/mL (IQR, 401.2 to 3,172.5) to 1,718.3 pg/mL (IQR, 435.3 to 2,929.9) (Δ median; 17.7 pg/mL; IQR, −50.0 to 96.0; p=0.075). Exploratory multivariate analysis

In exploratory multivariate regression models adjusted for baseline catecholamine concentrations, vasopressin dosage was significantly associated with higher post-test epinephrine levels (β=1.73, p=0.001). No significant associations were observed for norepinephrine or phenylephrine dosages. In sensitivity analyses using pre–post changes (Δ) in catecholamine levels as dependent variables, no vasopressor dosage was significantly associated with changes in catecholamine concentrations (all p>0.05).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the cardiovascular and neurohormonal effects of apnea testing in patients undergoing brain death determination, with a particular focus on the relationship between hemodynamic responses and circulating catecholamines. Consistent with previous observations, both apnea tests were associated with significant increases in HR and blood pressure, confirming that apnea testing imposes a substantial cardiovascular burden.

Although previous studies have reported similar hemodynamic responses, few have simultaneously evaluated biochemical and cardiovascular parameters within the same cohort.815) Prior reports have described transient hypertension and tachycardia during apnea testing; however, hypotension may also occur in a subset of cases, highlighting the need for individualized hemodynamic monitoring and support.81529)

Despite these pronounced cardiovascular responses, plasma catecholamine concentrations showed no consistent pre–post changes. Only epinephrine demonstrated a modest, but statistically significant increase, whereas norepinephrine and dopamine remained unchanged. These findings contrast with the conventional assumption that hypertension during apnea testing is primarily driven by systemic catecholamine surges.622)

Several mechanisms may explain this dissociation. First, catecholamine release following brainstem injury is highly dynamic and transient; therefore, the two-point sampling design may not have captured short-lived fluctuations.622) However, serial sampling was intentionally avoided to prevent interference with the standardized brain death determination protocol, consistent with current guidelines.428) Second, the baseline neurohormonal milieu in patients with brain death is influenced by vasopressor therapy, systemic inflammation, and potential adrenal exhaustion, which may attenuate measurable catecholamine responses.101219) Third, exogenous vasoactive agents, particularly vasopressin, may modulate vascular tone independently of endogenous sympathetic activity.113) In addition, circulating catecholamine levels may not accurately reflect sympathetic neural activity because cardiovascular responses are often mediated by local neurotransmitter release at sympathetic nerve terminals.7)

Correlation and multivariate analyses further support this interpretation. Changes in HR and blood pressure showed no meaningful association with catecholamine fluctuations, suggesting that the observed hemodynamic responses are unlikely to be explained solely by systemic catecholamine activation. In the multivariate analysis, vasopressin dosage was significantly associated with higher absolute post-test epinephrine levels. Clinically, patients requiring concurrent vasopressin therapy represented a subgroup with severe hypotension that could not be adequately stabilized with norepinephrine monotherapy alone. Therefore, these higher absolute epinephrine levels may not solely represent an endogenous stress response, but may instead reflect the combined or confounding pharmacologic effects of multiple high-dose exogenous vasopressors. Nevertheless, the absence of significant associations when analyzing pre–post changes (Δ) suggests that although intensive vasopressor support may alter baseline neurohormonal status, it does not fundamentally mask or modify the acute cardiovascular responses consistently triggered by controlled hypercapnia during apnea testing. This finding is consistent with donor management studies showing that vasopressin may stabilize blood pressure without inducing sympathetic overstimulation.20) Given the limited sample size, these multivariable analyses should be interpreted as exploratory.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the abrupt hemodynamic responses observed during apnea testing cannot be fully explained by systemic catecholamine changes alone. This phenomenon underscores the complexity of residual autonomic activity in patients with brain death and highlights the importance of careful hemodynamic monitoring during apnea testing.18) From a clinical perspective, these results identify a gap in current brain death protocols. Existing guidelines provide detailed recommendations for the prevention and management of hypotension but offer limited guidance regarding hypertension during apnea testing.91728) Given the consistent occurrence of blood pressure elevation, explicit recommendations for monitoring and managing hypertensive responses may be warranted. Persistent hypertension may increase myocardial oxygen demand, contribute to cardiac injury, and impair microvascular perfusion in donor organs, potentially compromising graft quality.162126) Early recognition and targeted management, including the use of short-acting antihypertensive agents, may help mitigate these risks without interfering with the diagnostic process and may improve donor stability.

Some limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size, particularly in the catecholamine subset, may have limited statistical power and increased susceptibility to variability. Consequently, catecholamine values may have been skewed, and the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Catecholamine measurements were obtained only during the first apnea test and at two time points, precluding assessment of transient or cumulative hormonal responses. In addition, substitution of values outside assay detection limits may have introduced bias. Furthermore, this study did not evaluate transplant outcomes, antihypertensive interventions, or post-procurement organ function; therefore, the clinical implications of the observed hemodynamic changes remain uncertain. Additional studies are required to determine their potential effects on donor management and transplant outcomes. Finally, because this was a retrospective single-center study, the generalizability of the findings is limited. Nonetheless, the physiological mechanisms identified are likely applicable to other settings, given the standardized nature of apnea testing protocols.

CONCLUSION

Apnea testing in patients undergoing brain death determination was consistently associated with marked increases in blood pressure and HR without corresponding elevations in circulating catecholamines. These findings suggest that circulating catecholamine measurements alone do not fully explain the hemodynamic responses observed during apnea testing. Nevertheless, the observed increases in blood pressure and HR were consistent and clinically apparent. Recognition and monitoring of hypertension during apnea testing should be considered in future brain death protocols.
