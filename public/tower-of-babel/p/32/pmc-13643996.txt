Effects of a staged postoperative blood pressure management protocol on cerebral perfusion stability and neurological outcomes after carotid artery stenting: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Mengyuan Li, Xiaona Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarotid artery stenting (CAS) carries a risk of postoperative cerebral perfusion instability. This study evaluated a multidisciplinary staged blood pressure (BP) management protocol on cerebral hemodynamics and neurological outcomes.MethodsRetrospective analysis included 67 CAS patients: control group (n = 33) received conventional BP management; intervention group (n = 34) received staged BP protocol. Cerebral hemodynamics were assessed using transcranial Doppler (TCD), including middle cerebral artery mean flow velocity (MCA-Vm) and pulsatility index (PI). Baseline, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h values were recorded. Preoperative MRI/MRA were reviewed for impaired perfusion surrogates. NIHSS and Barthel Index (BI) were evaluated.ResultsIntervention group showed more stable MCA-Vm and PI. MCA-Vm peaked at 24 h (72.3 ± 14.8 cm/s) then stabilized or decreased to 64.8 ± 10.4 cm/s at 72 h. PI decreased from 0.94 ± 0.18 to 0.86 ± 0.14 in the intervention group versus 0.92 ± 0.21 to 0.78 ± 0.19 in controls. Fewer BP target failures occurred (17.6% vs. 45.5%; P = 0.019), and rescue antihypertensive therapy was less frequent (17.6% vs. 42.4%; P = 0.034). NIHSS at discharge was lower (1 [IQR 1–3] vs. 3 [2–4]; P = 0.009) and BI at 30 days higher (91.5 ± 7.6 vs. 84.3 ± 10.4; P = 0.002). A small subset exhibited postoperative MCA-Vm reduction, indicating heterogeneous hemodynamic responses.ConclusionStaged postoperative BP management was associated with stable TCD-derived cerebral hemodynamics and improved neurological outcomes after CAS. Findings reflect surrogate hemodynamic changes rather than absolute cerebral blood flow, emphasizing individualized, monitored BP management.Keywords: Carotid artery stenting, Staged blood pressure management, Cerebral perfusion, Multidisciplinary care, Neurological outcomes, Hyperperfusion syndrome

Introduction

Carotid artery stenosis (CAS) is an important cause of ischemic stroke and may produce chronic cerebral hypoperfusion distal to the affected vessel [1, 2]. CAS is a minimally invasive alternative to carotid endarterectomy, particularly for patients at increased surgical risk because of unfavorable anatomy, previous neck surgery or irradiation, or substantial medical comorbidity [3]. The widespread adoption of CAS has been facilitated by technological advancements in stent design, embolic protection devices, and procedural techniques, which have collectively contributed to improved safety profiles and clinical outcomes [4].

After CAS, abrupt restoration of flow to chronically hypoperfused tissue may produce cerebral hyperperfusion, whereas perioperative hypotension or excessive BP reduction may cause hypoperfusion and ischemia. These contrasting complications are partly related to impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation and make postoperative BP management clinically challenging [5, 6].

Postoperative blood pressure (BP) control is therefore essential. Hypertension increases the risk of cerebral hyperperfusion, but excessive BP reduction may compromise cerebral perfusion, particularly in patients with severe stenosis, poor collateral circulation, or impaired cerebrovascular reserve [7, 8]. A management strategy must therefore limit excessive pressure while avoiding clinically important hypoperfusion. The concept of staged blood pressure management represents an emerging paradigm that addresses the temporal dynamics of cerebrovascular adaptation following revascularization. Rather than maintaining static blood pressure targets throughout the postoperative period, this approach recognizes that the brain’s tolerance for perfusion changes evolves over time as autoregulatory mechanisms gradually recover [9]. The theoretical framework underlying staged management is grounded in the understanding that cerebral vessels require a finite period to readjust their contractile tone and restore normal reactivity to changes in perfusion pressure. Dynamic cerebral autoregulation is frequently impaired in severe carotid artery disease and may improve after carotid revascularization, including CAS. However, the optimal timing and magnitude of postoperative BP liberalization remain uncertain [10]. This temporal model of staged intervention aligns with the physiological recovery trajectory and offers a rational basis for developing nursing protocols that dynamically adjust blood pressure goals according to the postoperative phase.

Multidisciplinary collaboration is essential for the implementation and success of postoperative blood pressure management after CAS, with nursing surveillance serving as a key component of bedside protocol delivery. Physicians establish individualized blood pressure targets, prescribe antihypertensive or vasoactive therapy, and make treatment-escalation decisions, whereas nurses perform scheduled hemodynamic monitoring, administer prescribed treatment, evaluate neurological status, document protocol adherence, and promptly notify the treating physician when predefined escalation criteria are met [11]. The continuous presence of nursing staff at the bedside facilitates real-time observation of neurological changes that may herald the onset of perfusion-related complications, including subtle alterations in consciousness, motor function, or speech that precede more overt manifestations of hyperperfusion syndrome [12]. Furthermore, nursing education and patient engagement represent essential components of comprehensive blood pressure management, as patients who understand the rationale for blood pressure control and recognize warning symptoms demonstrate improved adherence to monitoring protocols and earlier reporting of concerning symptoms following discharge [13]. Despite the recognized importance of coordinated postoperative care, evidence remains limited regarding structured multidisciplinary protocols that combine phase-specific blood pressure targets, physician-directed treatment, standardized nursing surveillance, transcranial Doppler monitoring, and predefined escalation pathways in patients undergoing CAS.

This study evaluated whether a multidisciplinary staged postoperative BP management protocol was associated with greater stability of TCD-derived cerebral hemodynamic parameters and more favorable neurological outcomes after CAS. We hypothesized that phase-specific BP targets combined with standardized monitoring and escalation procedures would reduce hemodynamic variability compared with conventional postoperative management.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary hospital’s Neurology and Cerebrovascular Intervention Center in China. Medical records of patients who underwent carotid artery stenting between August 1, 2024, and August 31, 2025, were systematically reviewed. The study compared outcomes between patients managed using a multidisciplinary staged postoperative blood pressure management protocol and those who received conventional postoperative blood pressure management. The staged postoperative blood pressure management protocol was introduced as a multidisciplinary quality-improvement initiative in February 2025, creating a natural comparison between the protocol-based and conventional management periods.

All CAS procedures were performed by experienced interventional neurologists using self-expanding carotid stents (Precise Pro, Cordis) with distal embolic protection devices (FilterWire EZ, Boston Scientific). Pre-dilatation was performed in 30 (44.8%) patients and post-dilatation in 28 (41.8%) patients. Staged angioplasty was conducted in 5 (7.5%) high-risk patients to minimize hyperperfusion risk. Vasopressors (dopamine or norepinephrine) were administered postoperatively if systolic blood pressure dropped below 100 mmHg. Bradycardia (heart rate < 50 bpm) occurred in 6 patients (9.0%) and hypotension (systolic BP < 90 mmHg) in 5 patients (7.5%).

Participants

Inclusion criteria were as follows: age 18 years or older; diagnosis of symptomatic or asymptomatic internal carotid artery stenosis of 70% or greater confirmed by digital subtraction angiography; successful completion of CAS procedure; availability of complete perioperative records including hemodynamic monitoring data and neurological assessments.

For exploratory purposes, patients were retrospectively classified into a higher-risk subgroup for hyperperfusion based on the presence of at least one of the following criteria: severe carotid stenosis (> 85%), contralateral carotid occlusion, evidence of impaired cerebrovascular reserve on preoperative imaging surrogate markers (reduced ipsilateral arterial visualization on MRA or ipsilateral FLAIR hyperintense vessel signs), poor collateral circulation, or preoperative hemodynamic compromise (low baseline MCA-Vm). Subgroup analyses were planned to examine whether the effects of staged BP management differed in this higher-risk population.

Exclusion criteria included emergency CAS for acute stroke; pre-existing severe neurological deficits (NIHSS > 15); contraindications to transcranial Doppler examination; concurrent intracranial pathology; incomplete follow-up data; and mortality within 24 h of the procedure unrelated to cerebrovascular complications.

The sample size was determined based on a power analysis assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.7) for the primary outcome of cerebral perfusion stability index, with alpha set at 0.05 and power at 0.80. The calculation indicated a minimum requirement of 30 patients per group. A total of 67 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis, with 34 patients in the intervention group and 33 patients in the control group. Preoperative brain MRI and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) performed within 14 days before CAS were retrospectively reviewed when available. Because single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), computed tomography perfusion (CTP), and acetazolamide-based cerebrovascular reserve testing were not routinely performed during the study period, quantitative preoperative cerebral blood flow, cerebrovascular reserve, and misery perfusion data were unavailable for most patients.

Multidisciplinary staged postoperative blood pressure management protocol

The multidisciplinary staged postoperative blood pressure management protocol consisted of three phases corresponding to the expected temporal pattern of cerebrovascular adaptation following CAS.

The protocol was jointly developed and supervised by an interventional neurologist, a vascular neurologist, and a clinical nurse specialist. The interventional neurologist was responsible for perioperative procedural decisions and the initial postoperative blood pressure prescription. The attending vascular neurologist reviewed each patient’s neurological status, cerebral perfusion findings, and blood pressure response and approved any adjustment of blood pressure targets or medication regimens. Bedside nurses performed scheduled blood pressure measurements, administered prescribed antihypertensive or vasoactive medications, conducted standardized neurological assessments, documented protocol adherence, and activated the escalation pathway when predefined thresholds were reached. The clinical nurse specialist served as the protocol coordinator and was responsible for staff training, weekly fidelity audits, feedback, and communication between the nursing and medical teams. Nurses did not independently prescribe medications or modify blood pressure targets.

Phase I (0–24 h postoperative): The physician-prescribed blood pressure target was < 120/80 mmHg. Bedside nurses performed continuous monitoring and titrated intravenous nicardipine or labetalol within the physician-authorized dose range according to the standardized protocol. Any dose requirement beyond the authorized range, persistent target failure, hypotension, bradycardia, or neurological deterioration prompted immediate physician review. Vital signs were recorded every 15 min during the first 6 h, then hourly. Neurological assessments using NIHSS were performed every 2 h. The reference baseline TCD examination was performed within 24 h before CAS. Follow-up TCD examinations were conducted at 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after CAS whenever technically feasible. Postoperative percentage changes in MCA-Vm were calculated relative to the preoperative value. An MCA-Vm increase exceeding 50% from baseline triggered enhanced nursing surveillance and immediate review by the vascular neurology or interventional team; subsequent blood pressure target adjustment and medication modification were made by the responsible physician.

Phase II (24–72 h postoperative): Blood pressure targets were gradually liberalized to below 130/85 mmHg. Monitoring frequency was reduced to vital signs every 2 h and neurological assessments every 4 h. Transcranial Doppler assessments were performed twice daily. Patient education regarding symptom recognition and blood pressure self-monitoring was initiated.

Phase III (72 h to discharge): Blood pressure management transitioned to oral antihypertensive medications targeting below 140/90 mmHg. Monitoring frequency aligned with standard ward protocols. Comprehensive discharge education was provided, including home blood pressure monitoring instructions and warning signs requiring immediate medical attention. The control group received routine postoperative care, including blood pressure monitoring every 4 h, neurological assessments twice daily, and physician-directed blood pressure treatment without phase-specific targets or a standardized multidisciplinary escalation pathway. Transcranial Doppler assessments were performed only when clinically indicated.

Preoperative neuroimaging and hemodynamic surrogate assessment

Preoperative brain MRI and MRA examinations performed within 14 days before CAS were retrospectively reviewed by two neuroradiologists who were blinded to treatment group and postoperative outcomes. Because SPECT, CTP, positron emission tomography, and acetazolamide challenge examinations were not routinely available, quantitative cerebral blood flow, cerebrovascular reserve, oxygen extraction fraction, and misery perfusion could not be systematically evaluated.

As surrogate markers of preoperative hemodynamic impairment, the reviewers assessed: (1) laterality of intracranial arterial visualization on three-dimensional time-of-flight MRA, defined as reduced signal intensity or reduced visualization of the ipsilateral middle cerebral artery branches compared with the contralateral side; and (2) ipsilateral FLAIR hyperintense vessel signs, defined as focal, linear, or serpentine hyperintensities along cortical or leptomeningeal vessels distal to the treated carotid stenosis. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Preoperative imaging was available for 61 of 67 patients (91.0%). Reduced ipsilateral arterial visualization on MRA was identified in 14 patients (20.9%), and ipsilateral FLAIR hyperintense vessel signs were identified in 12 patients (17.9%). Six patients without complete preoperative MRI/MRA sequences were classified as having unavailable imaging-marker data and were not assumed to have negative findings.

Transcranial doppler ultrasonography protocol

Transcranial Doppler (TCD) ultrasonography was performed using a 2-MHz pulsed-wave Doppler transducer (DWL Multi-Dop X4, Compumedics, Germany) by certified vascular technologists who had completed standardized training with a minimum of 100 supervised examinations. The middle cerebral artery (MCA) was insonated through the temporal acoustic window with patients positioned supine at a 30-degree head elevation. Optimal signal acquisition was achieved by adjusting the probe position and angle to obtain the highest amplitude signal with characteristic spectral waveform morphology. Measurements were obtained at a depth of 50–60 mm from the temporal window, and consistent insonation depths were maintained across serial examinations for each patient.

The primary TCD parameters recorded included mean flow velocity (MCA-Vm), peak systolic velocity (PSV), end-diastolic velocity (EDV), and pulsatility index (PI), calculated using the formula PI = (PSV - EDV) / MCA-Vm. Each measurement was performed in triplicate with a minimum of five consecutive cardiac cycles recorded, and the mean value was used for analysis. Baseline TCD was performed within 24 h preoperatively, and “baseline” refers to the preoperative MCA measurement. Postoperative TCD assessments were conducted at 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after CAS. At each timepoint, MCA-Vm and PI were recorded, and concurrent blood pressure (systolic and diastolic) was documented. Absolute MCA-Vm and PI values, as well as delta values relative to baseline, were calculated. PI values at 24 and 48 h were included to provide a more complete evaluation of cerebral hemodynamics. An MCA-Vm increase exceeding 100% from baseline was regarded as a marker of marked hyperperfusion, whereas a decrease of ≥ 20% was considered indicative of potential hypoperfusion. An increase of > 100% was regarded as a TCD marker of marked hyperperfusion, whereas a decrease of ≥ 20% was regarded as a potential hypoperfusion pattern. The cerebral perfusion stability index was calculated as the percentage of postoperative measurements remaining within the predefined clinically acceptable range after exclusion of measurements meeting the marked hyperperfusion or hypoperfusion criteria. Quality control measures included inter-rater reliability assessment with an intraclass correlation coefficient exceeding 0.85 for all primary parameters.

Multidisciplinary training, nursing competency, and protocol fidelity

Before protocol implementation, all bedside nurses in the neurovascular unit completed a standardized training program consisting of 8 h of didactic instruction and 16 h of supervised clinical practice. Physicians and vascular technologists attended a 2-hour multidisciplinary protocol briefing focused on blood pressure targets, treatment-escalation criteria, transcranial Doppler interpretation, and communication responsibilities. Training modules covered the pathophysiology of cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome, blood pressure measurement techniques, neurological assessment using standardized scales, and protocol-specific monitoring and documentation requirements. Competency was assessed through written examination and practical demonstration, with a minimum passing score of 85% required for independent practice.

Protocol fidelity was monitored through weekly audits of nursing documentation, including verification of monitoring frequency adherence, appropriate blood pressure target ranges for each phase, and completeness of neurological assessments. A dedicated clinical nurse specialist served as the protocol coordinator, conducted weekly documentation audits, provided ongoing education and feedback, and reported adherence concerns to the responsible neurologist and multidisciplinary quality-improvement team. Nursing staff-to-patient ratios were maintained at 1:2 during Phase I and 1:4 during Phases II and III to ensure adequate monitoring capacity.

Although nursing surveillance was central to bedside implementation, clinical decision-making remained multidisciplinary. Nurses were responsible for monitoring, protocol-based medication administration within prescribed parameters, neurological assessment, documentation, patient education, and escalation of abnormal findings. Physicians retained responsibility for determining blood pressure targets, prescribing or changing medications, interpreting clinically significant TCD abnormalities, and initiating rescue treatment.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes assessed cerebral perfusion parameters using transcranial Doppler ultrasonography, including middle cerebral artery mean flow velocity (MCA-Vm), pulsatility index (PI), and a cerebral perfusion stability index calculated as the percentage of measurements within the target physiological range during the first 72 h.

Secondary outcomes included neurological function assessed by the NIHSS, a 15-item scale quantifying stroke-related deficits with scores ranging from 0 (no deficit) to 42 (severe impairment) [14]. The modified Rankin Scale (mRS) evaluated functional disability on a 7-point scale from 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (death) [15]. The Barthel Index (BI) measured activities of daily living independence with scores from 0 to 100, where higher scores indicate greater independence [16]. All neurological assessments were performed by trained nursing staff who had achieved certification in NIHSS administration through the National Institutes of Health online training program. Inter-rater reliability was established through periodic comparison assessments, with kappa coefficients exceeding 0.80 for categorical outcomes.

Complications included cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome, asymptomatic cerebral hyperperfusion, postoperative hypoperfusion, ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, and hemodynamic instability requiring intervention. Hemodynamic instability was defined as bradycardia with a heart rate < 50 beats/min, hypotension with systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg, or a sustained deviation from the phase-specific systolic blood pressure target requiring active treatment. Active treatment included intravenous fluid administration, atropine, vasopressor therapy, intravenous antihypertensive rescue therapy, or urgent physician-directed adjustment of the prescribed blood pressure target. Ischemic lesions were evaluated using postoperative MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), performed routinely within 24–48 h after CAS. Symptomatic ischemic lesions were defined as new focal neurological deficits corresponding to the location of DWI hyperintensity, whereas asymptomatic lesions were defined as new DWI hyperintense foci without any neurological symptoms. Causes of NIHSS worsening were categorized as: symptomatic ischemia, new infarction detected on MRI-DWI, hemodynamic ischemia related to perioperative hypotension or bradycardia, or hyperperfusion-related neurological symptoms. Clinical correlation was performed by two independent neurologists, and disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Postoperative hypoperfusion was defined as a reduction in ipsilateral MCA-Vm of ≥ 20% from the preoperative baseline at any postoperative assessment, particularly when accompanied by new neurological symptoms, a reduction in systemic blood pressure, or imaging evidence of delayed cerebral perfusion or watershed ischemia. Patients with a smaller decline in MCA-Vm were reviewed individually but were not classified as having definite hypoperfusion.

Data collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records by trained research nurses using a standardized data collection form. Information collected included demographic characteristics, medical history, stenosis characteristics, procedural details, serial blood pressure measurements, nadir and peak systolic blood pressure, antihypertensive medication use, vasopressor use, intravenous fluid administration, atropine use, rescue management, percentage of readings within the prescribed target range, reasons for blood pressure target failure, neurological assessments, and 30-day outcomes. All data extractors were blinded to the study hypotheses. Double data entry was performed for 20% of randomly selected records to verify accuracy, with an error rate below 1% considered acceptable.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Version 26.0 for Windows (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between the protocol and control groups using independent-samples t tests. Ordinal variables and continuous variables with non-normal distributions, including NIHSS score, mRS score, and carotid stenosis severity, were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as number and percentage and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The mRS was additionally analyzed as an ordinal outcome and using the prespecified dichotomizations of 0–1 versus 2–6, 0–2 versus 3–6, and 0–3 versus 4–6. Because of the small number of patients in the higher mRS categories, Fisher’s exact test was used for these dichotomous comparisons. All comparisons were performed between the protocol and control groups. Repeated measures ANOVA was employed to analyze temporal changes in cerebral perfusion parameters. A two-tailed P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Of 84 patients who underwent CAS during the study period, 67 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Seventeen patients were excluded due to incomplete monitoring data (n = 8), emergency procedures (n = 5), or lost to follow-up (n = 4). The intervention group comprised 34 patients and the control group 33 patients.

This subgroup had a higher prevalence of severe stenosis (≥ 85%), reduced ipsilateral MRA visualization, and FLAIR hyperintense vessel signs compared with lower-risk patients. Reduced ipsilateral intracranial arterial visualization on MRA was observed in 14 patients (20.9%), and ipsilateral FLAIR hyperintense vessel signs were observed in 12 patients (17.9%). Cerebral perfusion parameters

Transcranial Doppler monitoring revealed significant differences in cerebral perfusion stability between groups, as illustrated in Fig. 2 and summarized in Table 2. The intervention group greater cerebral perfusion stability, with a stability index of 87.2 ± 8.4%, compared with 76.8 ± 11.2% in the control group (P = 0.003). Cerebral perfusion parameters

Repeated measures ANOVA revealed a significant group-by-time interaction effect for MCA-Vm (F = 4.28, P = 0.018), indicating that the temporal pattern of velocity changes differed between groups. As depicted in Fig. 2, the intervention group showed a more gradual increase and earlier stabilization of flow velocities, whereas the control group demonstrated greater variability and a slower return toward baseline values. However, postoperative flow responses were not uniform across all patients. Four patients showed a decrease in ipsilateral MCA-Vm of ≥ 20% from the preoperative baseline: one patient in the intervention group and three patients in the control group. Three of these decreases were transient and were not accompanied by neurological deterioration. One patient in the control group developed transient contralateral limb weakness during an episode of hypotension and was classified as having symptomatic postoperative hypoperfusion. Because of the small number of events, no formal risk-factor analysis was performed.

Neurological function outcomes

NIHSS scores were analyzed as ordinal, non-normally distributed data, as shown in Fig. 3 and Table 3. The intervention group had lower NIHSS scores at discharge than the control group [median (IQR), 1 (1–3) vs. 3 (2–4); P = 0.009], and this difference remained at 30 days [1 (0–2) vs. 2 (1–3); P = 0.014].

Neurological function outcomes

At 30 days, the median mRS was 1 (IQR, 0–1) in the intervention group and 1 (IQR, 0–2) in the control group (P = 0.08 by Mann–Whitney U test). Favorable outcomes defined as mRS 0–1 occurred in 26 of 34 patients (76.5%) in the intervention group and 18 of 33 patients (54.5%) in the control group (P = 0.075). The corresponding proportions for mRS 0–2 were 94.1% and 81.8% (P = 0.150), while those for mRS 0–3 were 97.1% and 90.9% (P = 0.356). None of these dichotomized mRS comparisons reached statistical significance. Barthel Index scores were significantly higher in the intervention group at both discharge (85.4 ± 9.8 vs. 78.6 ± 12.4, P = 0.016) and 30-day follow-up (91.5 ± 7.6 vs. 84.3 ± 10.4, P = 0.002).

Complications

The incidence of complications is summarized in Table 4. Odds ratios were not calculated due to small event numbers and zero counts in some outcomes; therefore, no forest plot is presented. All reported percentages should be interpreted as descriptive, exploratory estimates. Marked postoperative cerebral hyperperfusion, defined by compatible clinical symptoms and/or an MCA-Vm increase of > 100% with supportive imaging findings, was identified in 1 patient (2.9%) in the intervention group and 5 patients (15.2%) in the control group. Clinical cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome occurred in 1 patient in the intervention group and 4 patients in the control group. One additional patient in the control group had an asymptomatic MCA-Vm increase of > 100% with mild cerebral edema on imaging and was classified as asymptomatic cerebral hyperperfusion rather than clinical hyperperfusion syndrome. Because of the small number of events and the absence of systematic quantitative perfusion imaging, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Postoperative complications

Postoperative MRI-DWI identified one symptomatic ischemic lesion in the control group, which manifested as transient contralateral motor weakness and corresponded to a new infarct on imaging. No additional asymptomatic ischemic lesions were detected in either group. NIHSS worsening in the control patient was attributed to symptomatic ischemia associated with perioperative hypotension (hemodynamic ischemia). No additional NIHSS worsening was observed. Causes were classified as: symptomatic ischemia, new infarction on MRI-DWI, hemodynamic ischemia due to hypotension/bradycardia, or hyperperfusion-related neurological symptoms.

Blood pressure control metrics

Analysis of blood pressure control showed that the intervention group had greater adherence to phase-specific targets and lower blood pressure variability than the control group. Although mean postoperative SBP was higher in the control group, this group also experienced more frequent transient hypotensive or hypertensive excursions, target-range failures, and rescue interventions, as illustrated in Fig. 4 and quantified in Table 5.

The percentage of time with blood pressure within target range was 82.4 ± 11.6% in the intervention group compared to 64.8 ± 18.4% in the control group (P < 0.001). Mean systolic blood pressure during the first 24 h was lower in the intervention group (124.6 ± 8.2 mmHg vs. 138.4 ± 14.6 mmHg, P < 0.001).

Blood pressure variability, measured as the standard deviation of systolic blood pressure readings, was significantly lower in the intervention group (8.6 ± 3.2 vs. 14.8 ± 5.6, P < 0.001), reflecting more stable hemodynamic control throughout the postoperative period (Table 5; Fig. 4). Intravenous antihypertensive therapy was used in 24 patients (70.6%) in the intervention group and 18 patients (54.5%) in the control group (P = 0.212), reflecting the stricter early postoperative blood pressure target in the intervention group. Vasopressors were required in 2 patients (5.9%) and 5 patients (15.2%), respectively (P = 0.259). Rescue intravenous antihypertensive treatment for sustained SBP above the prescribed target was required in 6 patients (17.6%) in the intervention group and 14 patients (42.4%) in the control group (P = 0.034). Any rescue intervention, including intravenous fluids, atropine, vasopressors, or urgent antihypertensive treatment, was required in 5 patients (14.7%) and 12 patients (36.4%), respectively (P = 0.053). Failure to remain within the prescribed SBP target for more than 20% of monitored readings occurred in 6 patients (17.6%) in the intervention group and 15 patients (45.5%) in the control group (P = 0.019).

Discussion

The present study suggests that a multidisciplinary staged postoperative blood pressure management protocol is associated with improved cerebral perfusion stability and neurological outcomes following carotid artery stenting. The protocol combined physician-defined blood pressure targets, standardized nursing surveillance, transcranial Doppler assessment, and predefined multidisciplinary escalation pathways. This coordinated approach was associated with fewer marked postoperative hyperperfusion events and greater stability of TCD-derived flow velocity. However, because quantitative preoperative and postoperative cerebral blood flow examinations were not systematically performed, the findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence of changes in absolute cerebral perfusion.

The observed improvement in cerebral perfusion stability index represents a clinically meaningful outcome that reflects the effectiveness of phase-specific blood pressure management. Previous research has established that cerebrovascular autoregulation is impaired following carotid revascularization, rendering cerebral blood flow passively dependent on systemic arterial pressure [17]. Moulakakis and colleagues reported that approximately 7.5% of patients experience hyperperfusion following CAS, with symptomatic presentations occurring in 1–2% of cases [18]. The 15.2% rate of marked hyperperfusion events in the control group was higher than the reported incidence of clinical cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome. This difference may partly reflect our broader composite classification, which incorporated marked TCD velocity elevation and supportive imaging findings in addition to clinical symptoms. When restricted to clinically symptomatic cases, the event rate was lower. Severe carotid stenosis, symptomatic presentation, impaired collateral circulation, and preoperative hemodynamic compromise may also have contributed; however, these mechanisms could not be fully evaluated because quantitative CBF and cerebrovascular reserve measurements were unavailable. Quantitative cerebral perfusion imaging and cerebrovascular reserve assessment are preferred for identifying patients at risk of postoperative hyperperfusion. Such examinations were not routinely performed in this retrospective cohort. We therefore evaluated available preoperative MRA and FLAIR images as exploratory surrogate markers of hemodynamic compromise [19, 20]. Reduced visualization of ipsilateral intracranial arterial branches on time-of-flight MRA may reflect reduced regional blood flow, while FLAIR hyperintense vessel signs may represent slow antegrade flow or leptomeningeal collateral circulation. These markers may assist in identifying patients with impaired hemodynamics when SPECT or CTP is unavailable, but they are indirect and should not be considered equivalent to quantitative CBF, cerebrovascular reserve, or oxygen extraction fraction measurements. Accordingly, the imaging-marker findings in the present study were used for descriptive risk characterization rather than definitive diagnosis of misery perfusion.

The lower incidence observed in the intervention group—2.9% compared with 15.2% in the control group—suggests that coordinated blood pressure management, incorporating systematic nursing surveillance and timely physician-directed treatment, may mitigate hyperperfusion risk. Among the total cohort, 22 patients (32.8%) met criteria for higher risk of postoperative hyperperfusion, including 12 in the intervention group and 10 in the control group. These high-risk patients had a greater prevalence of severe stenosis (≥ 85%), reduced ipsilateral MRA visualization, and FLAIR hyperintense vessel signs, reflecting impaired cerebrovascular reserve or suboptimal collateral circulation. Notably, the reduction in hyperperfusion incidence in the intervention group occurred despite the inclusion of these high-risk patients, indicating that the staged multidisciplinary BP protocol may be particularly beneficial in patients predisposed to cerebral perfusion instability. Although the sample size was insufficient for formal subgroup statistical testing, descriptive analyses suggest that individualized phase-specific BP targets and vigilant monitoring could reduce hyperperfusion events in patients with severe stenosis, contralateral occlusion, poor collateral flow, or preoperative hemodynamic compromise. Inclusion of lower-risk patients in the current cohort may dilute the observed effect, highlighting the potential value of risk-stratified prospective studies to clarify the true impact of individualized postoperative BP management. Given the retrospective design and small number of events, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

The temporal pattern of cerebral perfusion changes observed through transcranial Doppler monitoring provides insights into the physiological response to staged blood pressure management. Abou-Chebl and colleagues established that intensive blood pressure treatment targeting less than 140/90 mmHg for standard patients and less than 120/80 mmHg for high-risk patients significantly reduces intracerebral hemorrhage following CAS [21]. The most physiologically relevant finding was that gradual liberalization of the SBP target after the first postoperative day was not accompanied by a progressive increase in MCA-Vm. In the intervention group, mean SBP increased from 124.6 ± 8.2 mmHg during the first 24 h to 131.8 ± 9.4 mmHg during 24–72 h, whereas MCA-Vm peaked at 24 h and subsequently decreased from 72.3 ± 14.8 cm/s at 24 h to 68.5 ± 11.2 cm/s at 48 h and 64.8 ± 10.4 cm/s at 72 h. This divergence between rising SBP targets and stabilizing or declining MCA-Vm suggests that cerebral flow became progressively less pressure-passive during the early postoperative period.Several physiological mechanisms may explain why MCA-Vm did not continue to increase despite staged SBP liberalization. Immediately after CAS, chronically dilated distal arterioles may have limited capacity to constrict, making cerebral blood flow relatively pressure-dependent. During the subsequent 24–72 h, gradual recovery of vascular smooth-muscle tone, reduction in compensatory arteriolar dilation, normalization of endothelial reactivity, and redistribution of collateral flow may partially restore cerebral autoregulation. As autoregulatory function improves, moderate increases in systemic pressure may be buffered by cerebrovascular constriction rather than being transmitted directly into progressive increases in cerebral flow velocity.

The observed stabilization of MCA-Vm should not be interpreted as definitive proof of restored autoregulation because dynamic autoregulatory indices, quantitative cerebral blood flow, and cerebrovascular reserve were not directly measured. Nevertheless, the temporal dissociation between SBP and MCA-Vm provides indirect physiological support for early recovery of autoregulatory capacity after revascularization. This finding represents an important rationale for staged rather than fixed postoperative blood pressure targets.

Clinically, prolonged strict SBP lowering may not be necessary in all patients once serial TCD values, neurological findings, and blood pressure variability have stabilized. Continuing an excessively low target after the period of greatest hyperperfusion risk could expose patients with poor collateral circulation or residual hemodynamic impairment to cerebral hypoperfusion. Therefore, relaxation of the SBP target should not be based on elapsed time alone but should be individualized according to stable MCA-Vm and PI trends, absence of hyperperfusion symptoms, neurological stability, and lack of recurrent hypotensive or hypertensive excursions. Yoshimura and colleagues similarly demonstrated that staged angioplasty approaches can prevent hyperperfusion syndrome, supporting the conceptual framework of graduated hemodynamic changes [22].

Postoperative cerebral hemodynamic responses were heterogeneous rather than uniformly hyperperfusive. Although most patients showed an early increase in ipsilateral MCA-Vm, four patients met the operational criterion for postoperative hypoperfusion, including one symptomatic patient with transient neurological deterioration during hypotension [23, 24]. These low-flow responses may reflect procedure-related hypotension or bradycardia, excessive antihypertensive treatment, impaired collateral recruitment, distal embolization, intracerebral steal, or delayed microvascular adaptation. The coexistence of hyperperfusion and hypoperfusion patterns indicates that a uniform low SBP target may not be appropriate for all patients. Serial assessment of MCA-Vm, PI, systemic blood pressure, and neurological status is therefore necessary to distinguish patients who require continued protection from hyperperfusion from those in whom further BP reduction may threaten cerebral perfusion.

The superior neurological outcomes observed in the intervention group, including lower NIHSS scores and higher Barthel Index values, carry important implications for patient quality of life and healthcare resource utilization. These findings align with research by van der Heyden and colleagues who demonstrated that cerebral perfusion parameters improve in approximately 50% of patients following carotid stenting, with the degree of improvement correlating with clinical outcomes [25]. The multidisciplinary protocol may have supported this beneficial effect by optimizing the hemodynamic environment during the critical early postoperative period, with nurses providing continuous bedside surveillance and physicians directing individualized treatment adjustments. The approximately 7-point difference in Barthel Index scores at 30-day follow-up represents meaningful improvement in activities of daily living independence that impacts patient autonomy and caregiver burden.

Comparison with contemporary blood pressure management guidelines indicates that the staged protocol aligns with evolving recommendations while incorporating structured nursing surveillance into a multidisciplinary clinical pathway. The 2023 European Society of Hypertension guidelines emphasize individualized blood pressure targets and the avoidance of rapid reductions that may compromise cerebral perfusion [26]. Castro and colleagues demonstrated that dynamic cerebral autoregulation assessed by transcranial Doppler can guide blood pressure management decisions, with impaired autoregulation identifying patients requiring more conservative targets [27]. Our protocol operationalized these principles through standardized monitoring intervals, physician-defined treatment targets, nurse-activated escalation pathways, and multidisciplinary responses to hemodynamic changes. Nurse-activated escalation refers to notification and initiation of predefined safety procedures rather than independent modification of medication prescriptions or blood pressure targets. The integration of transcranial Doppler monitoring at protocol-specified timepoints provides objective perfusion data that complements clinical neurological assessment.

Within this multidisciplinary framework, the role of nursing surveillance in early complication detection merits particular emphasis. Kim and colleagues reported that experienced nurses demonstrate superior ability to detect early neurological deterioration, with nursing experience exceeding four years associated with more than two-fold higher detection rates of stroke lesion progression [28]. The staged protocol formalized nursing surveillance through predefined monitoring frequencies that intensified during the highest-risk periods, while ensuring that abnormal findings prompted physician assessment and multidisciplinary treatment decisions. The control group had a higher mean SBP but also greater SBP variability, more frequent target failure, and greater use of rescue therapy. This apparent discrepancy reflects the limitation of relying on mean SBP alone: a patient may have a high average SBP while simultaneously experiencing transient hypotension, hypertensive peaks, or rapid fluctuations. The lower frequency of rescue treatment and target failure in the intervention group therefore suggests greater hemodynamic consistency rather than simply lower blood pressure. However, because the difference in the composite hemodynamic instability outcome did not reach statistical significance, this observation should be interpreted cautiously.

The original emphasis on nursing reflected the central role of bedside nurses in delivering the monitoring-intensive components of the protocol. Nurses performed the majority of scheduled blood pressure measurements and neurological assessments, administered medications within physician-prescribed parameters, provided patient education, and initiated escalation when safety thresholds were exceeded. However, the intervention was not exclusively nursing-driven. Blood pressure targets, medication prescriptions, interpretation of clinically relevant perfusion changes, and rescue-treatment decisions remained under physician responsibility. We have therefore revised the terminology throughout the manuscript to describe the intervention as a multidisciplinary staged postoperative blood pressure management protocol, while retaining a specific description of nursing responsibilities as a key implementation component.

The mechanistic pathway linking staged blood pressure management to improved outcomes likely involves multiple complementary mechanisms. First, prevention of extreme blood pressure elevation during the immediate postoperative period protects against mechanical stress on newly revascularized vessels and chronically vasodilated cerebral microvasculature [29]. Second, the gradual liberalization of blood pressure targets during Phase II and III may facilitate physiological recovery of autoregulatory function by providing incrementally increasing hemodynamic challenges within a safe range. Third, intensive monitoring may facilitate early recognition of both excessive increases and clinically important decreases in cerebral flow velocity. This bidirectional surveillance is important because strict blood pressure reduction intended to prevent hyperperfusion could aggravate hypoperfusion in patients with impaired collateral circulation or persistent autoregulatory dysfunction. Lieb and colleagues emphasized that early identification and careful monitoring constitute the most important aspects of hyperperfusion syndrome prevention [30].

Several important clinical implications emerge from our findings. First, the staged blood pressure management protocol may be incorporated into routine multidisciplinary postoperative care, although implementation requires staff education, clearly assigned responsibilities, physician availability for escalation, and ongoing protocol-adherence monitoring. Second, transcranial Doppler monitoring, though not universally available, provides valuable objective data that enhances clinical decision-making; institutions lacking this capability may adapt the protocol using surrogate measures of perfusion status. Third, the emphasis on patient education during Phase II positions patients for successful transition to outpatient management, potentially reducing readmission rates and improving long-term blood pressure control.

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, quantitative preoperative and postoperative cerebral perfusion examinations, including SPECT, CTP, arterial spin-labeling perfusion imaging, acetazolamide cerebrovascular reserve testing, and oxygen extraction fraction assessment, were not routinely performed. Consequently, TCD-derived MCA-Vm should be regarded as a surrogate hemodynamic measure rather than a direct measurement of regional cerebral blood flow, and misery perfusion could not be determined. Second, retrospective assessment of MRA laterality and FLAIR hyperintense vessel signs was possible only in patients with adequate preoperative imaging and may be affected by image-acquisition parameters and reader interpretation. Third, the small number of hyperperfusion and hypoperfusion events precluded reliable multivariable or subgroup analysis. The retrospective design also introduces potential selection bias and limits causal inference. The single-center setting may restrict generalizability to institutions with different patient populations, procedural volumes, or nursing staffing models. The relatively modest sample size, while adequate for detecting moderate effect sizes, may have limited power to identify differences in rare complications. The comparison between historical and intervention periods introduces temporal confounding related to unmeasured improvements in care quality or patient selection. Future prospective multicenter randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these findings and establish the cost-effectiveness of staged blood pressure management protocols.

In conclusion, a multidisciplinary staged postoperative blood pressure management protocol was associated with greater cerebral perfusion stability and more favorable neurological outcomes in patients following carotid artery stenting. The protocol was associated with stable TCD-derived cerebral hemodynamics despite gradual SBP liberalization, suggesting that fixed prolonged BP restriction may not be necessary once cerebral flow velocity and neurological status have stabilized. Because cerebral responses were heterogeneous and some patients exhibited postoperative hypoperfusion, BP targets should be individualized using serial hemodynamic and neurological assessments. The protocol integrates physician-directed treatment with structured nursing surveillance, standardized neurological assessment, transcranial Doppler monitoring, and predefined escalation pathways. Prospective multicenter studies are required before this multidisciplinary approach can be recommended as a standard component of post-CAS care.
