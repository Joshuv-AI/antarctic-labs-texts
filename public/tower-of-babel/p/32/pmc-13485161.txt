Vertical Growth of Facial Soft Tissues: A Longitudinal Three‐Dimensional Facial Growth Study
Authors: Signe Silinevica, Amir Nassereldine, Gundega Jakobsone, Jinjun Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis longitudinal cohort study aimed to assess changes in vertical facial landmarks in individuals aged 10 to 18 years and identify sex differences in growth patterns.Materials and MethodsOne hundred and one individuals (males, n = 56; females, n = 45) were observed. Three‐dimensional facial surface images were acquired using stereophotogrammetry every 6 months. The linear distances between various facial structures, with a focus on vertical facial features, were measured and plotted over time, along with the confidence intervals, creating growth curves. To illustrate growth rates, the relative changes between consecutive measurement periods (mm/year) for males and females were analysed. Age‐related average facial features are presented in radar charts.ResultsAt the beginning of the observation period, males exhibited a longer lower face, and the difference in face length increased by 7.6 mm. At age 11, nose lengths were nearly identical between sexes; however, by age 12, a marked divergence in growth emerged, leading to longer noses in males. Between the ages of 10 and 17, the upper lip in males grew at approximately twice the rate observed in females. The first growth peak for the lower face in males corresponded to that of females at age 11.5, later males had two more peaks at ages 14 and 17.5.ConclusionsThe lower, middle, and upper faces exhibited distinct growth patterns. Although sexual dimorphism was evident before age 10, it became more pronounced and increased during the observation period.

Introduction

Growth of the human face is a multidimensional and dynamic continuum. Thus, the clinician must understand the amount and direction of expected growth for facial structures. Traditionally, facial growth has been investigated using cephalograms [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. The North American growth samples have been integrated into the Craniofacial Growth Consortium Study (CGCS) to establish updated growth standards and individualised predictive models [7]. The CGCS investigators acknowledged limitations of historical radiographs, particularly due to variable tissue density leading to soft‐tissue overexposure in earlier records and the need for non‐linear magnification correction across cephalometric images [7]. The cephalograms represent the growth of the skeletal structures and might not be appropriate for describing facial growth because it is also seen as the changes that affect external facial features. Although it had been stated earlier [8] that growth of hard and soft tissues is not strictly correlated, other studies have suggested a closer relationship between the soft and hard tissues of the face [9]. More recently, three‐dimensional facial scanning has been employed to evaluate longitudinal facial changes. These methods use landmarking techniques [10, 11, 12, 13, 14] or global and discrete local facial shapes [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20], which can be sequenced by reregistering a series of faces.

Some of the reported facial scan samples are longitudinal [10, 11, 16, 20], whereas others are cross‐sectional [14, 15, 17, 19, 21] or mixed longitudinal [22, 23]. Most longitudinal growth studies have relatively small sample sizes, with the largest including only 87 individuals [20].

Although sexual dimorphism in the adult facial morphology is apparent [14, 15, 17, 19, 21], the studies have shown indecisive conclusions about development of facial dimorphism. Ferrario et al. [22] found that, within each age group, linear distances were significantly larger in boys than in girls, with some exceptions coinciding with the earlier female growth spurt. Angular measurements, however, did not show a corresponding sexual dimorphism. On the other hand, Kesterke et al. [14] concluded that craniofacial dimorphism emerged through distinct developmental trajectories during growth, and the definitive adult male and female facial shapes that were present after puberty resulted from numerous distinct developmental changes occurring at earlier stages. Different studies have suggested varying timing for growth spurts in males and females, and these may also differ among distinct regions of the face [12, 20].

With the increasing clinical availability of three‐dimensional imaging, contemporary studies are increasingly focused on evaluating treatment‐related soft‐tissue changes. Accordingly, a longitudinal investigation of soft‐tissue growth would provide a valuable reference for both clinical practice and research.

Thus, this longitudinal cohort study aimed to [1] determine the evolution of changes in vertical facial landmarks for individuals aged 10–18 years and [2] identify the sex differences in growth dynamics for vertical facial parameters.

Materials and Methods

In this prospective cohort study of a Caucasian population, 300 invitations were dispatched randomly to individuals born in 2002 and who were listed in the database of the Riga Stradins University Institute of Stomatology. Of these invitations, 181 individuals responded, and 126 consistently participated. At the beginning of the study, 55 participants were excluded due to either meeting the predefined exclusion criteria or expressing unwillingness to continue participation Figure S1. The exclusion criteria comprised a history of craniofacial trauma, craniofacial anomalies, facial disfigurement, clinically evident facial asymmetry, or prior initiation of orthodontic treatment involving functional appliances or maxillary expansion. Scanning sessions were conducted every 6 months, spanning from the ages of 10–18 years (T1–T17). Owing to pandemic‐related restrictions, one session (T16) had to be cancelled. A total of 101 individuals (56 males and 45 females) were included in the study, all of whom did not miss more than two consecutive scanning sessions up to session 16. Sixteen participants took part in the final scanning session Figure S1. Seventeen individuals or 16,8% underwent minor orthodontic treatment carried out by fixed appliances.

The facial images were captured by the same operator using the 3dMDface system scanner (3dMD, Atlanta, USA) in natural head posture. A single calibrated examiner identified and recorded the landmarks as described by Farkas [24]. The mid‐endocanthion point (men) is determined by the left and right inner canthi. This point is considered a relatively consistent landmark, with the inner canthi only showing a slight increase of 1.6 and 1.9 mm for males and females, respectively, within the age range of 9–16 years [24].

Statistical Analyses

Prior to the reliability assessment, the calibration procedure was performed by a single operator, who placed the landmarks twice, with a 7‐day interval, on 30 randomly selected 3D images. It was repeated multiple times until the measurement error was within 1 mm as determined by Dahlberg's error calculation and the intraclass correlation coefficient higher than 0.997. The procedure used to evaluate intra‐examiner error in the present study has been described in detail in an earlier publication [13].

At first, a histogram of the percentages of attendances for both males and females were constructed. The percentages were calculated with respect to the maximum number of male (66) and female (60) attendance throughout the entire duration. A polynomial regression of order 10 was used to model the facial features in relation to age. The regression results are presented with an estimated confidence interval of 90%.

To illustrate the growth rates, the data reflecting the relative growth between two adjacent participating periods, i.e., mm/year, for both males and females were processed. Afterwards, the same polynomial regression of order 10 was applied to fit the growth rate data. The average facial features with respect to ages are plotted in the radar charts.

Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to assess the associations between body mass index (BMI), orthodontic treatment history, and gender with the changes in facial measurements over the time between 10 and 14 years of age and 14 and 17 years of age. Facial variables served as dependent variables, while BMI, orthodontic treatment history, and gender were entered as independent predictors.

Since participant drop‐out was observed, the algorithm employed linear interpolation to estimate the missing data. The method has been described in detail in a previous study [13].

Intra‐Operator Reliability

In the calibration results, Dahlberg's error analysis indicated that the glabella on the y‐axis showed the lowest precision; however, none of the measurement errors exceeded 1.0 mm. The intraclass correlation coefficients ranged from 0.997 to 1.000.

Some measures (men‐pr and men‐sn) were comparable for boys and girls at the beginning of the observation period but showed clear separation later (Figure 3 and Table 2). By age 17.3 years, this distance increased to 9.01 mm in males and 5.09 mm in females (Table 2). Growth Rates

The growth rates are presented in Figures S2 and S3.

The males had two growth spurts (at 11.5 and 14 years of age) assessed by the men‐pg changes at 2.5 and 3 mm/year, respectively Figure S2. The lower lip had similar growth spurts, whereas the upper lip (men‐ls) appeared to follow the growth dynamics of the nose Figure S2. The nose (men‐prn) exhibited a steady increase in the growth rate, reaching the maximum (2 mm/year) at age 13.5 Figure S3.

The females had a maximum rate of growth of 2.3 mm/year at 11.6 years, as assessed by the men‐pg change, which then gradually decreased to 0 at 15 years Figure S3. The upper lip (sn‐ls) and nose demonstrated a continuous decrease in the growth rates.

The Linear Regression

The regression model for changes between 10 and 14 years of age indicated that sex had the most consistent and significant effect (p < 0.001) across all craniofacial parameters except the upper lip (sn–ls). Neither BMI nor orthodontic treatment history demonstrated statistically significant associations with any of the analysed variables. The explanatory power of the models was moderate, with R2 values ranging from 0.15 for men–n to 0.38 for men–sn.

For the period between 14 and 17 years of age, the regression model showed that sex accounted for 64% of the variance in the increase in nose length (p = 0.002) and 44% of the variance in lower‐lip change (p = 0.020). BMI and orthodontic treatment history did not exhibit significant effects on any of the evaluated facial changes.

Discussion

The mean intra‐examiner error was generally < 0.5 mm; however, the following coordinates exceeded this value: g Y, 0.61; n Y, 0.52; and pg. Y, 0.69. The variance due to landmarking errors as a proportion of total mean variation was less than 3.4% (men‐n, 3.42%; men‐prn, 1.60%; men‐sn, 3.57%; men‐ls, 2.93%; men‐li, 2.15%; men‐pg, 3.35%). The positional accuracy of the majority of reference points is consistent with findings reported in previous studies [25, 26]. In the present study, a landmark‐based analysis was applied. Three‐dimensional surface scanning also allows for surface‐based methods, which are particularly important in very young children, where rapid and substantial changes in facial form occur over short periods of time [27]. Surface‐based analysis, together with landmark‐based analysis, has been used for the assessment of facial asymmetry [28], however, in the present study, facial asymmetry was not evaluated. In the context of growth assessment, linear distance changes in facial parameters are easier to apply in clinical practice and research.

A small percentage of individuals (16.8%) had previously undergone orthodontic treatment with fixed appliances. Patients requiring growth‐modification therapy, as well as those with facial disfigurement or asymmetries, were excluded from the study. This approach allows us to refer to the sample as having normal facial proportions, although defining this term in such studies is challenging [7], particularly because skeletal patterns of the individuals were not assessed. The results of the multivariate regression analysis revealed that a history of orthodontic treatment did not predict changes in the facial variables either between ages 10 and 14 or between ages 14 and 17. Moreover, the effects of the treatment on the midline points analysed in this study are likely minimal, as facial characteristics such as facial height, inter‐ocular distance, and the relative prominence of the lips are largely determined by genetic factors [29].

Various trends have been observed in growth among the different facial structures. For the lower face, vertical growth ceased at around the age 14 in females. For males, growth slowed down at age 15. Our observations are in line with those of most studies [6, 20, 22]. However, Bulygina et al. [30] reported an earlier growth cessation in females by age 12, whereas other studies have suggested more prolonged growth for females with regard to some facial parameters [5, 13]. The lengthening of the lower face could be due to the pubertal growth of the mandible [2, 5, 30]. The extended growth of the facial length in males aligns with the finding of a previous study, which observed substantial percentage increases for males aged 6–18 years across all vertical dimensions, ranging from 32% to 40% [5]. In the same study, females experienced less vertical measurement increases by approximately 19%–26%. Snodell et al. [31] reported similar findings from their longitudinal growth studies.

For the middle face length as recorded by the men‐sn distance, both males and females reached a plateau at about the same age of 15 and 14.5 years, respectively. On the contrary, Ferrario et al. [22] reported an increase in the n–sn distance up to 17 years of age in males. Previous studies [11, 16] have also noted that boys exhibit more prominent lips across all age groups, and this prominence tended to increase with age. The men‐prn distance showed a clear separation during pubertal growth starting at 12, leading to longer noses in males, a phenomenon also documented in other studies [5, 14, 29].

The evolution in men‐prn may indicate an overall midface advancement. Matthews et al. [19] identified more nuanced shape alterations along the dorsum of the nasal bridge and nasal tip in males. This has led to a subtle narrowing and downwards inclination of the nasal tip in males. In addition, subtle shape changes were observed in the upper lip and philtrum: the upper lip region projected outward in males. In the present study, differences in the upper lip length were observed between males and females throughout the observation period. A clear separation in growth for lips was noted between males and women at 12.6 years, resulting in more inferiorly and anteriorly placed lips in males. Similar observations have been previously reported [10, 19]. Moreover, nearly no increase in the upper lip length either for males or females was recorded. Several cephalometric studies [5, 6] have suggested that there is hardly any increase in the vertical length of the upper lip after age 9. Blanchette et al. [6] found that > 93% of the upper lip growth length is accomplished by age 11. Bergman et al. [32] recorded a 1‐mm increase in the upper lip length between ages 12 and 16 years. Notably, the length of males' lips increased by approximately 6 mm as measured at men‐ls, allowing for the greater protrusion of incisors compared to females.

Growth Rates

The landmarks related to the upper, middle, and lower face had different growth trends. In females, one distinct growth peak was observed for the landmarks of the upper and lower face, while the middle face structures grew steadily. Three growth peaks were observed for all mid‐endocanthion‐based landmarks in males. A previous study reported multiple peaks of growth both for males and females have been found in a previous study [13]. However, they differed from the findings of the present study in terms of the timing and intensity of sampling. Welsh males arrived at the maximum peak of 5.4 mm/year for men‐pg compared with the maximum of 3.4 mm/year observed in the present study. Instead, the maximum growth rates at the peaks were comparable among Latvian, Finnish, and Welsh populations, ranging from 2.2 to 2.8 mm/year [13].

Very few studies have provided the rates of facial growth in children [2, 4]. These studies produced smooth curves for growth rates due to the employed methodology. However, Lewis et al. [2] noted that some individuals had multiple growth spurts of the mandible. Moreover, Buschang et al. [4] observed changes in the direction of growth velocity curves three times between ages 10 and 15 years. The peak adolescent velocity was approximately 1.6 times greater for boys than for girls, resulting in a difference of 4 mm in mandibular length. In the present study, a difference of 6 mm in men‐pg was obtained through three different growth peaks in males, though with the same intensity as that of female growth.

Several studies have stated that growth spurts occur later in boys than in girls, with a difference of approximately 1–2 years [4, 19]. In the present study, the onset of the first growth spurt in males coincided with the growth spurt of females. Koudelova et al. [20] reported similar overlapping growth spurts.

Some parameters (men‐pr and men‐sn) already showed sex differences at the beginning of the observation period, whereas others developed during the pubertal growth spurt. Several studies have reported that the differences in the forehead, buccal region, and chin were evident before puberty, challenging the view that sex differences result from pubertal hormone levels [17, 19].

In the present sample, at age 14, six males were classified as obese, while four females and two males were classified as overweight. Ferrario et al. showed that obesity may lead to changes in facial structure, although they did not find significant differences in the linear facial measurements between obese and normal‐weight individuals [33]. The linear regression analysis in the present study revealed that BMI had non‐significant effects on the measured facial changes. In contrast, previous cross‐sectional cephalometric studies have shown that increased BMI is associated with greater facial dimensions and protrusion by age 14 [34] or age 16 [35]. A mixed‐longitudinal study [23] investigated the association between three‐dimensional facial changes and height and weight gain in children. The authors demonstrated that weight gain had a stronger relationship (explaining 25% of the variance) with increases in facial dimensions than height gain (10% of the variance), indirectly indicating an influence of BMI on accelerated facial growth. However, the same research group later suggested that BMI was not associated with altered facial growth in a non‐obese population [36].

Limitations of the Study

This study has some limitations. First, the analysed data were obtained from participants representing just one distinct population. Richmond et al. [13] reported facial growth spurt differences in different populations of Caucasian origin. Moreover, Claes et al. [18] estimated that ancestry accounted for 9.6% of the variation in facial surface shapes. A genome‐wide association study [37] identified five loci (PRDM16, PAX3, TP63, C5orf50 and COL17A1) that are involved in the determination of the human face in Europeans. Population‐specific differences in craniofacial growth have been documented, including distinct growth‐prediction curves in Korean compared with American cohorts [38]. Some general developmental patterns may be applicable across groups; for example, gender‐related growth differences tend to be consistent, with males exhibiting greater growth magnitudes and a later pubertal peak (approximately 12–16 years in males versus 10–14 years in females) [39]. However, other investigations have reported contrasting patterns of sexual dimorphism compared with our results [40].

The most substantial reduction in the sample size occurred following the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic. A total of 81 participants remained enrolled up to scanning session 16; however, this session was cancelled due to pandemic‐related restrictions. This decline in participation was likely attributable to the extraordinary circumstances of the pandemic, which may have led individuals to deprioritize their involvement. Additionally, some individuals had relocated to other cities or countries for educational purposes. Despite the attrition, the remaining sample retained a random distribution of characteristics. Moreover, the effects of the treatment on the midline points analysed in this study are likely minimal, as facial characteristics such as facial height, inter‐ocular distance, and the relative prominence of the lips are largely determined by genetic factors [29].

Clinical Implications

It has been shown that patients with a vertical growth pattern tend to mature earlier than those with other facial types [3, 6]. In the treatment of open bite, both the patient's inherent growth pattern and the anticipated timing of the vertical facial growth spurt should therefore be carefully considered. Unfortunately, our sample could not be subdivided by vertical growth type, which limits direct comparisons across growth patterns. The results of the present study indicate that vertical growth in females has largely ceased by approximately 14 years of age, whereas males continued to exhibit vertical growth across all measured parameters. These sex‐based differences highlight the need for retention strategies tailored specifically for male and female patients. Additionally, the upper lip showed minimal growth between the ages of 10 and 17, a finding that aligns with previous reports [5, 6, 36] and should be taken into account when planning the extrusion of the upper incisors, as excessive incisor display cannot be compensated for by natural soft‐tissue growth during adolescence.

Conclusions

Different growth dynamics in the lower, middle, and upper face have been observed. The lower face in males was longer at the beginning of the observation period, and because of more intensive growth peaks, the difference in the face length (men‐pg) increased to 7.6 mm. The first growth peak of males is nearly the same as that of females.

At age 11, the nose lengths were nearly identical in both males and females; however, a distinct divergence in growth became evident at age 12, resulting in longer noses in males at the end.

Between the ages of 10 and 17, the upper lip in males grew at approximately twice the rate observed in females.

The men‐g distance exhibited a consistent and similar increase for both males and females.
