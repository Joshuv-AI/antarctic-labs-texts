Introduction

Sudan, a low-income country in Africa, faces a weak healthcare system and high malaria prevalence. The epidemiology of malaria is influenced by emerging resistance of parasites and vectors, humanitarian crises caused by political instability and climate changes, such as rainfall seasons, flooding and population movement.1 2 Since April 2023, severe conflict has left 11 million individuals in urgent need of medical care, with over two-thirds of hospitals in affected regions non-functional. The remaining ones face imminent closure due to shortages of medical staff, supplies, water and electricity.3 From 2023 to June 2024, 1,621,164 malaria cases have been documented across 15 states.4

Even before the conflict, malaria represented a significant burden. In 2018 and 2019, more than 63,000 cases were reported annually in a single state, with children under 5 years of age accounting for a large proportion.5 The escalation to over 1.6 million cases nationally during the current conflict reflects a dramatic surge, highlighting how instability has amplified transmission and undermined control efforts.

The aim of this manuscript is to explore the role of public awareness and education in supporting Sudan’s malaria vaccine rollout, particularly in conflict-affected and displaced populations where misinformation, healthcare disruption and limited access to reliable information remain key barriers.

Malaria vaccine and the Sudan context

The WHO recommends two malaria vaccines (RTS, S and R21) to prevent Plasmodium falciparum malaria in children in endemic regions, focusing on areas with moderate-to-high transmission.6 7 The vaccine is advised from 5 months of age, given in four doses, with a fifth dose in areas of highly seasonal transmission.7 The malaria vaccine was introduced in Sudan in October 2024, initially covering 15 localities in Gedaref and Blue Nile states and benefitting over 148 000 children. The goal is to expand to 129 localities across Sudan in 2025 and 2026.8 The current malaria vaccines lower the incidence of uncomplicated malaria by about 40%, severe malaria by 30% and overall mortality by 13%. Widespread use could significantly save the lives of tens of thousands of young children each year.6 7

However, Sudan’s rollout is taking place in the midst of a severe humanitarian crisis. It can be assumed that challenges such as misinformation, limited awareness and logistical barriers may affect uptake in some areas. The programme relies heavily on the Expanded Programme on Immunization, with support from non-governmental organisations (NGOs) and community health workers, yet conflict-related damage to infrastructure, population displacement and workforce shortages could undermine sustainability and equitable distribution.9 Similar issues were observed with measles vaccination in Sudan, where hesitancy was driven by cultural beliefs, misinformation and provider-related factors suggesting that malaria vaccine acceptance may also be fragile without strong community engagement.10

Beyond acceptance, the operational realities of vaccine delivery in conflict settings must also be considered. Although systematic reports from Sudan’s malaria vaccine rollout are not yet available, it can be reasonably assumed that conflict-related insecurity, fragile infrastructure and limited cold-chain capacity present significant obstacles to distribution. Similar challenges have been documented in other conflict-affected countries. For instance, the COVID-19 vaccine rollout in Yemen faced severe delivery interruptions due to active frontlines and fragmented authorities, with health workers often operating under direct security threats.11 In contrast, South Sudan—despite being the least electrified country and facing extreme heat—has shown that resilient cold chain systems, supported by Gavi and partners, can sustain vaccine programmes through solar refrigeration, innovative stock monitoring and outreach to hard-to-reach areas.12 These experiences suggest that Sudan’s malaria vaccine programme will likely require both security-sensitive delivery models and investment in robust cold chain innovations to ensure continuity under fragile conditions.

Experiences from other African countries suggest that maintaining community trust, supporting health workers and ensuring the fourth-dose completion are among the key challenges in achieving high and sustained uptake.13 These lessons point to additional risks in Sudan, where the health system has already been deeply disrupted by conflict.9

Taken together, while the malaria vaccine represents a groundbreaking tool with great promise, its full potential in Sudan will depend on addressing these systemic, social and logistical challenges alongside the rollout.

The role of public awareness and education

Public awareness is vital for the successful rollout of the malaria vaccine in Sudan. Informed communities are more likely to accept immunisation, reducing hesitancy and correcting misconceptions. However, misinformation and cultural beliefs have been documented as barriers to vaccine acceptance in Sudan, particularly with measles vaccination, where religious and cultural perceptions strongly influenced caregiver decisions.10 This suggests that similar challenges may arise for the malaria vaccine, especially in rural and displaced communities where awareness remains low due to limited media coverage, insufficient funding and minimal local involvement.

Displaced populations face unique challenges, with restricted access to accurate information and healthcare services. Evidence from low- and middle-income countries shows that education campaigns tailored to local cultural and socio-economic contexts—such as involving community leaders, integrating local languages and addressing caregivers’ concerns—significantly improve vaccine acceptance.10 14 Lessons from COVID-19 highlight the importance of tailored strategies for vaccine hesitancy, especially in low-literacy populations and among caregivers.14

Importantly, gender dynamics play a crucial role. In Sudan, as in many similar settings, mothers and female caregivers are often the primary decision-makers for childhood immunisation.15 Designing education and outreach programmes that directly engage women, particularly in displaced and rural communities, could enhance vaccine uptake and long-term trust in immunisation services. Evidence from low- and middle-income countries indicates that while women strongly advocate for vaccination, they often face barriers such as limited decision-making power, restricted mobility and financial constraints, whereas men who usually hold greater authority in household decisions may demonstrate higher levels of hesitancy due to misinformation and traditional beliefs.16 This highlights the importance of empowering women, engaging men and ensuring gender-sensitive approaches when planning vaccination strategies in Sudan to achieve equitable and sustained uptake of the malaria vaccine.

Community engagement fosters trust and active participation, with early, context-specific communication and collaboration with local leaders proving effective in similar contexts. Using health volunteers to educate underserved populations can directly address concerns and misconceptions.17

Building on this, strengthened and inclusive education initiatives with explicit attention to gender roles, cultural beliefs and the needs of displaced populations are essential for bridging the gap between vaccine availability and uptake. This will be critical to ensuring equitable coverage and advancing Sudan’s fight against malaria.

Challenges in raising awareness

Several interrelated challenges limit community engagement and awareness about the malaria vaccine in Sudan. Cultural beliefs and widespread misinformation contribute to vaccine hesitancy, with some parents fearing that vaccines may harm their children. Low health literacy, poor infrastructure and insecurity further restrict access to reliable information and services.14 18 The ongoing conflict has severely disrupted the health system, causing a reduction in national vaccination coverage and leaving rates as low as 30% in conflict-affected areas.8

Additionally, recurrent health emergencies such as outbreaks of other infectious diseases, combined with limited access to healthcare facilities, divert both attention and resources away from malaria vaccination efforts.17

Successful strategies for public engagement

To ensure the success of the malaria vaccine rollout in Sudan, effective strategies for public engagement must be implemented. Mobile radio campaigns, mosque-based outreach and community gatherings are particularly feasible in the current conflict setting, where internet access and health services are limited. While social media can be useful, poor connectivity in rural and conflict-affected areas necessitates greater reliance on these traditional methods.

Collaborating with trusted community leaders, healthcare workers and local NGOs is crucial in building trust and combating misinformation. Evidence shows that community-driven strategies tailored to education level, age and gender significantly improve acceptance of vaccination programmes.19 Meta-analyses also confirm that ‘fit-for-purpose’ engagement—rather than a ‘one-size-fits-all’ strategy—maximises effectiveness.20 Experiences from other crises further suggest that community champions can strengthen trust and address misinformation at grassroots levels.21

Conclusion and call to action

The fight against malaria in Sudan depends heavily on the success of public awareness and education initiatives. Addressing misconceptions and providing clear, accurate information are crucial to overcoming vaccine hesitancy. Priority should be given to practical strategies such as mobile radio messaging, mosque-based outreach, deployment of community health volunteers and collaboration with trusted local leaders. These approaches are more feasible in Sudan’s fragile context and can strengthen trust while extending vaccine coverage.

Key stakeholders—including the Sudanese Ministry of Health, UNICEF, Gavi and the WHO country office—must take coordinated responsibility for advancing these efforts. By aligning resources and strategies, they can ensure that the malaria vaccine reaches the most vulnerable populations, particularly in rural and displaced areas.

Immediate, collective action is needed to safeguard the future of Sudan’s population, reduce the burden of malaria and advance the nation’s health goals.
