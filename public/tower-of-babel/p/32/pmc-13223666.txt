Weak economic evidence for WHO’s essential cancer medicines for Africa
Authors: Aron E van der Steege, James Humuza, Cyprien Shyirambere, Hans Hogerzeil, Rob M P M Baltussen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe WHO Model Lists of Essential Medicines (MLEM and MLEMc) guide national medicine selection, particularly in low-income and lower middle-income countries (LMICs). Concerns exist about the adequacy and transferability of economic evidence supporting recent cancer medicine additions.MethodsWe systematically examined modifications to cancer medicines on the WHO MLEM(c) between 2017 and 2023 and assessed their adoption into National Essential Medicines Lists (NEMLs) and NEMLs for children (NEMLcs) of African LMICs (2019–2024). Economic evidence was classified as cost or cost-effectiveness data and transferability to African LMIC contexts was evaluated.ResultsOf 160 modifications, 11% included cost data and 9% cost-effectiveness evidence. All identified cost-effectiveness studies showed low transferability to African LMIC settings. Among 46 African LMICs, 23 NEMLs and 6 NEMLcs were available. Median adoption of new cancer medicines was 23% for NEMLs and 13% for NEMLcs.ConclusionRecent WHO MLEM(c) modifications to cancer medicines are rarely supported by robust, context-specific economic evidence yet several have been adopted in African LMICs. Stronger requirements for economic evaluations are essential to ensure the affordability, sustainability and policy relevance of WHO essential medicine recommendations.

Introduction

Access to essential medicines is central to achieving universal health coverage (UHC), especially in low-income and lower middle-income countries (LMICs).14 According to the WHO, these are medicines that satisfy the priority healthcare needs of a population and are intended to be available in functioning health systems at all times, in appropriate dosage forms, of assured quality and at prices individuals and health systems can afford.5

To support countries in achieving these goals, the WHO publishes a Model List of Essential Medicines (MLEM) and, since 2007, a separate MLEM for Children (MLEMc).6 Countries may use the WHO MLEM(c) as a reference when compiling their own National Essential Medicines Lists (NEML) and NEMLs for children (NEMLc), thereby serving as a key instrument to optimise medicine selection, guide procurement and promote equitable health outcomes.710

The WHO Expert Committee on the Selection and Use of Essential Medicines, an independent body of experts appointed by WHO, is responsible for reviewing and updating these Model Lists. Every 2 years, the Committee evaluates applications for the addition, amendment or deletion of medicines, applying selection criteria, including disease prevalence and public health relevance, scientific evidence of efficacy and safety, and comparative cost and cost-effectiveness.5 Historically, this process has resulted in a focus on affordable, generic medicines.11 12 However, recent years have seen a notable shift towards the inclusion of high-cost patented medicines, particularly for cancer.11 12

This development poses new challenges for LMICs, especially in Africa, where cancer mortality has now surpassed that of malaria and HIV.1315 While the inclusion of these new cancer medicines in NEMLs could potentially improve access and outcomes, their high costs raise concerns regarding sustainability, affordability and the opportunity costs within already constrained health budgets.61622 Furthermore, the addition of expensive cancer medicines to the MLEM(c) has triggered debate about the adequacy of the evidence base supporting these decisions, and their consistency with the definition and selection criteria of essential medicines as outlined by the WHO.23 In particular, concerns have been raised regarding the lack of robust, context-specific economic evaluations.23 Without such evidence, the inclusion of costly medicines risks encouraging national policymakers to prioritise interventions that may not be affordable or the most cost-effective in their setting.

Given the MLEM(c)’s influential role in shaping national policy and procurement decisions, it is critical to understand the extent to which recent modifications to these lists are supported by robust economic evidence. Such insights can inform equitable and sustainable health policy decisions and support progress towards UHC.14

This study systematically examines modifications made to cancer medicines on the WHO MLEM(c) between 2017 and 2023, evaluating the extent to which these changes were supported by economic evidence and assessing the transferability of cost-effectiveness evidence to the context of African LMICs. It further examines the adoption of these newly added cancer medicines into NEML(c)s across African LMIC. By doing so, it aims to provide critical insights into the strength of the economic foundations underpinning global essential medicines governance and its implications for health policy in resource-limited settings.

Methods

This study conducts a systematic document analysis of all modifications related to cancer medicines on the WHO MLEM(c) between 2017 and 2023. Additionally, it assessed the extent to which African LMICs adopted these modifications into their NEMLs and NEMLs for children (NEMLc).

Search strategy and selection criteria

Information on modifications to the MLEM(c) was extracted from WHO Expert Committee reports on ‘The Selection and Use of Essential Medicines’ published in 2017, 2019, 2021 and 2023 on the WHO’s open-access website.52427 These reports detail the Expert Committee’s decisions regarding the inclusion or removal of medicines, indications, dosages and routes of administration. NEML(c)s were identified through a systematic search of three sources: (1) official government or Ministry of Health websites, (2) the WHO repositories and (3) targeted grey-literature searches using Google. Searches were performed in English and in the official language(s) of each country. For Google searches, we combined the terms essential medicines list, national essential medicines list, list national medicines or list national medicines essential with the name of the country (eg, ‘National essential medicines list Mali/Liste Nationale des Médicaments Essentiels Mali). The same search logic was applied on government and WHO websites. Only NEML(c)s published between 2019 and 2024 were included, ensuring that the lists reflect decisions made following the most recent MLEM(c) updates and are not outdated. Economic and demographic data were sourced from the WHO Global Health Observatory, The Global Economic Monitor and the Global Health Expenditure Database.2830 The World Bank website was used to identify African LMICs and the total population of each country in 2023. Cost-effectiveness thresholds for each country were estimated and projected to 2024 using the method proposed by Pichon-Riviere and colleagues, based on 2021 economic data.31 This method provides a country-level cost-effectiveness threshold using easily accessible and widely available data, offering a more empirically grounded and context-specific estimate compared with conventional approaches such as the 1%–3% of GDP per capita rule.

Data extraction

For each MLEM(c) modification, the following information was extracted: the type of modification (new medicine, new indication and new dosage form or new route of administration) and the presence of supporting economic evidence. Economic evidence was classified into two categories: information related to costs and information related to cost-effectiveness (cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) estimates or incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs)). Sources cited as evidence were assessed based on four criteria: relevance to the intended indication, relevance to the intended age group (all ages vs up to and including 12 years), relevance to the healthcare systems of African countries and whether the reported QALY or ICER fell below the estimated cost-effectiveness threshold for African LMICs. For national adoption, cancer medicines listed in NEMLs and NEMLcs were identified and compared with the WHO MLEM(c) editions from 2017, 2019, 2021, and 2023.

Medicines were considered aligned if they corresponded to a medicine listed on the MLEM(c) or its therapeutic alternative, regardless of variations in formulation, strength, indication or priority classification assigned by individual countries. The alignment rates of new cancer medicines on the MLEM(c) into NEML(c)s were assessed using the MLEM(c) published at least one calendar year prior to each NEML(c), which served as the reference list. Thus, 100% alignment means the NEML(c) includes all WHO-listed cancer medicines or their accepted therapeutic alternatives.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the data. We calculated the proportion of MLEM(c) modifications supported by economic evidence, the proportion of citations that met context-relevant criteria, the mean cost-effectiveness threshold and the alignment rates of cancer medicines across national lists. Frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables, while medians and IQRs were used for continuous variables. All analyses were conducted in Microsoft Excel (LTSC 2021). Double data extraction was performed to ensure accuracy, and discrepancies were resolved by re-examining the original sources.

Results

Between 2017 and 2023, the WHO Expert Committee made 160 modifications related to cancer medicines across the MLEM(c), with an almost equal distribution between the MLEM and MLEMc (table 1 and online supplemental table S1). The most common type of modification was the addition of a new indication to an existing medicine (65.8% for MLEM and 69.1% for MLEM), followed by the addition of a new medicine (22.8% for MLEM and 21.0% for MLEMc), and finally, the addition of a new dosage or route of administration to an existing medicine (11.4% for MLEM and 9.9% for MLEMc). Of the modifications to the MLEMc, 58.2% of the additions of a new medicine were already listed on the MLEM, 25% of the additions of a new indication corresponded to indications already listed on the MLEM, and 12.5% of the additions of a new dosage or route of administration corresponded to dosages or routes already listed on the MLEM. Economic evidence was inconsistently provided to support these modifications. Among MLEM modifications, 41.8% included quantitative cost information, whereas only 30.9% of MLEMc modifications did so. Quantitative cost-effectiveness evidence was presented for only 8.9% of MLEM modifications and 2.5% of MLEMc modifications.

Of the 36 sources cited to support claims related to cost-effectiveness, 17 were focused on monotherapy interventions of essential cancer medicines and could be assessed in detail. Among these, only 3/17 (17.7%) fully matched the intended WHO MLEM(c) indication. Just one study (5.9%) was done in the context of LMICs and also met the cost-effectiveness threshold. None of the sources was fully appropriate regarding patient age group (table 2). Regarding the availability of national lists, 23 of the 46 African LMICs (50.0%) had a publicly available NEML published between 2019 and 2024, while six countries (13.0%) had an NEMLc (table 3, online supplemental table 3a,b). The regional distribution of available lists showed a predominance in Eastern, Western and Middle Africa, with limited representation from Southern and Central Africa. Analysis of national lists revealed that countries included a median of 43 cancer medicines for an NEML (IQR 29–52) and 22 for NEMLc (IQR 11–35). The median alignment between MLEM(c) and NEML(c) was 59.4% (IQR: 39.1%–68.8%) for NEMLs and 47.7% (IQR: 24.4%–60.2%) for NEMLcs. Between 2017 and 2023, the adoption rate of newly added cancer medicines from the WHO MLEM(c) into national lists was 23.1% for NEMLs and 12.5% for NEMLcs. Incorporation rates were higher among countries whose national lists were more recently updated. Additionally, a median of 11.1% of cancer medicines on the NEMLs and 1.9% on the NEMLcs were not included in the WHO MLEM(c).

Discussion

This study evaluated the extent to which recent modifications to cancer medicines on the WHO MLEM and MLEMc were supported by economic evidence and assessed their adoption into the NEML and NEMLc of African LMICs. The findings show that, despite WHO selection criteria requiring consideration of economic evidence, particularly cost-effectiveness analyses, such evidence relevant to African settings was rarely provided for modifications made between 2017 and 2023. Moreover, while several cancer medicines newly added to the WHO MLEM(c) were incorporated into national lists, overall adoption rates remained modest.

The limited provision of economic evidence aligns with previous research identifying weaknesses in the evidentiary basis supporting WHO MLEM recommendations.23 Our analysis extends this concern by highlighting that even when economic evidence is cited, it is rarely specific to the indications, age groups or healthcare contexts of African LMICs. This lack of context-specific data challenges the applicability of global recommendations to national decision-making processes. It also reflects prior findings regarding the unaffordability of cancer medicines listed on the MLEM and concerns about their applicability in LMICs.121621 32 33

These findings should be interpreted in the context of the institutional constraints of the WHO EML process. As noted by Jenei et al, the Expert Committee is unable to assess country-specific budget impacts due to substantial variation in medicine prices across countries.12 This constitutes a structural limitation of the WHO EML process that shapes how economic evidence can be generated, assessed and reported at the global level.

NEML(c)s are developed through heterogeneous processes across African LMICs, differing in governance, stakeholder involvement and the use of economic evidence. Against this background, our analysis shows that several newly listed cancer medicines from the MLEM(c) have been incorporated into national lists despite limited relevance of economic evidence and their high costs. These adoption patterns suggest that countries selectively integrate WHO recommendations based on local priorities and resource constraints, with observed lags between WHO listing and national adoption potentially reflecting cautious decision-making or limited institutional capacity.34

In this context, countries have often relied on cost-effectiveness evidence generated in high-income settings, sometimes adjusted using Purchasing Power Parity or similar transferability assumptions, to inform national prioritisation. For example, Rwanda’s recent health benefit package revision applied a prioritisation process for cancer services within its Community-Based Health Insurance scheme, drawing on international sources such as the Tufts Cost-Effectiveness Analysis Registry due to the absence of local economic evaluations.35 While this pragmatic approach enabled structured decision-making, it also illustrates the central challenge identified in this study, namely the continued dependence on economic evidence with limited transferability to African LMIC contexts.

The inclusion of expensive cancer medicines on the WHO MLEM(c) without robust supporting economic evidence raises concerns about equity and sustainability. In LMIC settings, the adoption of high-cost interventions risks diverting scarce resources away from more cost-effective health interventions, exacerbating existing health inequities. Ensuring that the WHO MLEM(c) continues to serve as a reliable guide for UHC efforts in resource-constrained contexts requires strengthening the rigour of evidence requirements, particularly with respect to economic evaluations and affordability assessments.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the analysis was restricted to publicly available WHO reports and national lists and therefore may not capture internal deliberations or unpublished country updates. Second, the assessment of cost-effectiveness studies used a limited number of variables and did not constitute a comprehensive economic evaluation and only relied on studies cited in the reports. Third, local factors such as medicine procurement prices, healthcare infrastructure and political influences, which may affect adoption decisions and prices, were not systematically analysed. Fourth, the analysis was limited to modifications, excluding rejected applications, hence no conclusions can be drawn regarding the full scope of the decision-making dynamics. Finally, the study focused exclusively on cancer medicines and does not account for broader trends across other therapeutic areas. Despite these limitations, this study offers novel insights into the relationship between global essential medicines recommendations and national adoption patterns of new cancer medicines in African LMICs. It highlights critical gaps in the current processes and offers evidence to support calls for reform in the WHO MLEM selection methodology.

We recommend that the WHO Expert Committee revises its application and selection procedures to require submission of formal, context-relevant economic evaluations, including cost-effectiveness analyses. In addition, stronger scrutiny should be applied to ensure that evidence is applicable across a range of healthcare settings, particularly LMICs. National policymakers are encouraged to critically assess the suitability of WHO-listed medicines for their specific contexts, making greater use of local health technology assessment processes to guide decisions. Future research should explore the broader political, institutional and financial dynamics that shape national adoption decisions and evaluate the real-world health outcomes associated with the inclusion of high-cost medicines in essential medicines lists. Such work will be crucial for informing strategies to strengthen the role of the MLEM as a tool for promoting equitable, evidence-based health systems globally and ultimately supporting progress towards UHC.
