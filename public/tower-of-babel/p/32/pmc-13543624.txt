Demographic and laboratory characteristics of neurosyphilis: a retrospective cohort study from a tertiary dermatosis hospital
Authors: Yun He, Ai-Hong Gong, Hui-lin Zhang, Yu-Yan Chen, Xiao-Liang Liang, Bing-Lei Li, Lian-Jiang Huang, Cheng-Zhi Lv, Ai-Li Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeurosyphilis, a severe manifestation of syphilis caused by Treponema pallidum invasion into the central nervous system, exhibits diverse clinical subtypes. Detailed, subtype-specific analyses of neurosyphilis within regional populations are needed to complement broad epidemiological data.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 90 Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)-negative neurosyphilis patients from a tertiary dermatosis hospital in Dalian, China (2023–2024). Demographic information and laboratory indicators were collected, and differences across subtypes (asymptomatic, meningeal, meningovascular, general paresis) of neurosyphilis were analyzed.ResultsAsymptomatic neurosyphilis was the most prevalent subtype (46.67%, n = 42). The cohort was primarily composed of middle-aged patients (40–59 years, 63.33%, n = 57). Notably, 3.33% (n = 3) of patients were as young as 20–29 years (the youngest being 21 years old). most of whom had a low educational level (76.67%, n = 69) and were unemployed or inactive (66.67%, n = 60). Of the 90 patients, 5 (5.56%) presented with neurological symptoms, while the remaining 85 were identified through other means (incidental discovery, routine screening, or partner notification). Serum rapid plasma reagin (RPR) titers showed no significant differences among subtypes, and more than half of the patients (52.22%, n = 47) presented with titers ≤ 1:16. Additionally, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) RPR titers and protein levels increased significantly with clinical severity, from the lowest in asymptomatic neurosyphilis to the highest in general paresis. A moderate positive correlation was observed between serum and CSF RPR titers.ConclusionsThis study reveals a trend toward asymptomatic and younger-onset neurosyphilis, primarily observed in middle-aged, low-educated, and unemployed individuals. Most cases were detected incidentally or through screening rather than neurological symptoms, indicating a high risk of underdiagnosis. Furthermore, over half of the patients had serum RPR titers ≤ 1:16, suggesting that using a threshold of > 1:16 for lumbar puncture would miss more than half of the cases. CSF RPR and protein levels increased with clinical severity, highlighting their critical role in differential diagnosis.

Introduction

Syphilis is a sexually and vertically transmitted infection caused by the spirochete Treponema pallidum and remains a global public health challenge with a resurgent incidence over the past two decades [1–3]. According to data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) of the United States, the number of neurosyphilis cases increased by 32% between 2020 and 2021 [4]. Treponema pallidum spreads rapidly and can invade the central nervous system (CNS) within days or weeks. In early syphilis, 25%–60% of patients experience such CNS invasion. Most of these patients recover spontaneously [5–7]; however, about 30% of those with CNS invasion develop persistent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) abnormalities [6, 8]. Importantly, despite these CSF abnormalities, many patients remain asymptomatic and are thus diagnosed with asymptomatic neurosyphilis—the most common form of the disease. Asymptomatic neurosyphilis occurs predominantly in early syphilis but can also be seen in late syphilis. Symptomatic neurosyphilis occurs mostly in patients with late syphilis, while some cases also occur in patients with early syphilis. These include meningeal neurosyphilis (usually within 1 year after infection, but may occur later), meningovascular neurosyphilis (usually 5–12 years after infection, but can also occur in early syphilis), and parenchymal neurosyphilis (usually more than 15 years after infection), such as general paresis (average 15–20 years after infection) and tabes dorsalis (average 20–25 years after infection) [9, 10]. The rarity of general paresis and tabes dorsalis in the modern antibiotic era explains why contemporary cohorts differ from historical reports.

While the clinical distinctions between neurosyphilis subtypes are well-established, their relative prevalence and clinical-demographic correlates exhibit considerable geographic and population-specific heterogeneity, as reflected in contemporary studies [11, 12]. Such disparities may be influenced by regional differences in host factors, screening intensity, antimicrobial exposure, and HIV co-infection status. Moreover, the serological diagnosis of late neurosyphilis can be challenging, as serum RPR titers may decline or even become negative, complicating case identification [13, 14]. Furthermore, many clinical and epidemiological analyses, often constrained by sample size or diagnostic categorization, tend to aggregate neurosyphilis cases into broad groups [15, 16], thereby obscuring subtype-specific variations in demographic, serological, and CSF profiles. A detailed, subtype-focused analysis within well-defined regional contexts is therefore essential to clarify the local clinical spectrum and inform targeted diagnostic strategies.

Therefore, we conducted a retrospective analysis of 90 patients diagnosed with neurosyphilis at Dalian Dermatosis Hospital between January 2023 and December 2024, systematically comparing the demographic and laboratory characteristics across different subtypes. Our findings aim to delineate subtype-specific patterns to facilitate early identification and clinical management in this region, to contribute critical regional data to a more comprehensive understanding of neurosyphilis heterogeneity in China, and to provide a reference for targeted prevention and control strategies regionally and globally.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study enrolled patients diagnosed with neurosyphilis at Dalian Dermatosis Hospital (a tertiary specialized hospital and the municipal center for sexually transmitted disease (STD) control) between January 2023 and December 2024. In accordance with the US CDC and European guidelines for syphilis management [14, 17], the primary screening population consisted of two sources: (1) syphilis patients with neurological symptoms; and (2) those without neuropsychiatric symptoms but who experienced serological treatment failure after standard antitreponemal therapy (e.g., a <4-fold decline in serum RPR titer at 6–12 months post-treatment or persistent serofast status). HIV serostatus was determined on serum samples collected concurrently with CSF. A total of 90 HIV-negative neurosyphilis patients were ultimately included. Demographic information and relevant laboratory indicators were collected and systematically analyzed.

Diagnostic criteria

The diagnosis of neurosyphilis was established based on integrated clinical and laboratory criteria, with reference to published US and European guidelines [14, 17]. A diagnosis of neurosyphilis was considered in patients with syphilis of any stage who presented with neurological, ocular, or otic symptoms, or in those without such symptoms but with serological treatment failure. The diagnosis was confirmed by at least one of the following CSF abnormalities: (1) a reactive CSF non-treponemal test (e.g., rapid plasma reagin; RPR); (2) an elevated CSF white blood cell (WBC) count (> 5 cells/µL); or (3) elevated CSF protein levels (> 500 mg/L). A negative CSF treponemal antibody result effectively excludes neurosyphilis, whereas a positive result alone is not diagnostic due to the possibility of passive antibody transfer from the blood. The symptomatic neurosyphilis subtypes were classified according to the clinical criteria of the multicenter trial protocol [18]; asymptomatic neurosyphilis was defined separately based on the absence of neurological symptoms in the presence of CSF abnormalities. Patients were excluded from the study for any of the following reasons: return visits, absence of lumbar puncture, incomplete medical records, HIV co-infection, other central nervous system diseases (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease), or non-syphilitic central nervous system infections.

Laboratory tests

All the serum and CSF samples of the participants were analyzed in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications. The RPR test was conducted using commercial kits (Shanghai Kehua Bio-Engineering Co., Ltd.) to detect non-treponemal antibodies, while treponemal antibodies were identified using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (InTec Products, Inc., Xiamen, China). HIV antibody and antigen (HIV Ag + Ab) were detected in serum using a Sysmex HISCL5000 fully automated chemiluminescence analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan). CSF protein quantification was performed on a Dirui CS-1300 biochemical analyzer (Dirui Industrial Co., Ltd., Changchun, China), and CSF WBC counts were determined using a Sysmex XN-1000 hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) in body fluid mode.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared across subtypes using one-way ANOVA, with post-hoc Bonferroni tests for pairwise comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared across subtypes using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction when the overall test was significant. Correlations between serum and CSF parameters were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis, and the correlation strength was interpreted as follows: |rₛ| = 0.00–0.30, negligible; 0.30–0.50, low; 0.50–0.70, moderate [19]. No missing data were present for any of the variables included in the analysis. Figures were generated using GraphPad Prism software 9.5 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The demographic characteristics of the study participants

This study included a total of 90 patients diagnosed with neurosyphilis at Dalian Dermatosis Hospital. Asymptomatic neurosyphilis was the most prevalent clinical subtype, comprising nearly half of the cohort (46.67%, 42/90), followed by meningovascular neurosyphilis (18.89%, 17/90), general paresis (18.89%, 17/90), and meningeal neurosyphilis (15.55%, 14/90) (Fig. 1A). The mean age of the patients was 50.87 ± 10.70 years, with 55.56% (n = 50) being male and 44.44% (n = 40) female. The cohort was predominantly middle-aged, with the largest groups being 50–59 (38.89%, 35/90) and 40–49 (24.44%, 22/90) years old. Notably, three patients (3.33%) were as young as 20–29 years old, with the youngest being 21, while only two (2.22%) were in the 70–79 age group. The male-to-female ratio varied across age groups: 0.50:1 (20–29 years), 2.00:1 (30–39 years), 1.75:1 (40–49 years), 0.84:1 (50–59 years), 1.69:1 (60–69 years), and 1.00:1 (70–79 years) (Fig. 1B). In terms of educational attainment, the majority (76.67%, n = 69) had a high school education or below, while 23.33% (n = 21) had college or higher education. Employment status analysis indicated that 33.33% (n = 30) were actively employed and 66.67% (n = 60) were unemployed or inactive. As for the reported source of syphilis infection, 10.00% (n = 9) were attributed to stable partners, 16.67% (n = 15) to casual partners, and 73.33% (n = 66) were of unknown origin. The routes of neurosyphilis diagnosis were varied: only 5.56% (n = 5) were diagnosed due to neurological symptoms, while 44.44% (n = 40) were incidentally detected during visits for other diseases, 45.56% (n = 41) were identified through routine screening, and 4.44% (n = 4) were diagnosed following partner notification. Further analysis indicated that asymptomatic neurosyphilis was primarily detected through routine screening (71.44%, 30/42). In contrast, meningeal neurosyphilis, meningovascular neurosyphilis, and general paresis were most frequently diagnosed incidentally during visits for other diseases, accounting for 57.14% (8/14), 76.47% (13/17), and 88.24% (15/17) of cases, respectively. Regarding prior syphilis treatment history, among the 90 patients, 46 (51.11%) had received treatment before, while 44 (48.89%) had never been treated. Across subtypes, the proportion of untreated patients was highest in the general paresis group (12/17, 70.59%), followed by the meningeal group (9/14, 64.29%), the meningovascular group (10/17, 58.82%), and the asymptomatic group (13/42, 30.95%) (Table 1). In summary, this cohort was primarily composed of middle-aged patients, with a notable proportion of young adults, who mostly had a low educational level and were unemployed or inactive. Asymptomatic neurosyphilis was the most prevalent subtype, and the vast majority of cases were identified incidentally or through screening. Moreover, nearly half of the patients (48.89%) had no prior syphilis treatment, with the untreated rate varying considerably across subtypes (ranging from 30.95% in the asymptomatic group to 70.59% in the general paresis group).

The characteristics of serum RPR titer in patients with neurosyphilis

All enrolled patients were seropositive for treponemal antibodies. The distribution of serum RPR titers is shown in Fig. 2A: the most common titer was 1:32, observed in 27 patients (30.00%), followed by 1:16 in 20 patients (22.22%). Only one case exhibited a titer of 1:1, and two cases showed a titer of 1:128. Using a cutoff titer of 1:16 for stratification, 52.22% (47/90) of patients presented with titers ≤ 1:16, while 47.78% (43/90) had titers > 1:16. The ratio of patients with RPR titers > 1:16 to those with titers ≤ 1:16 across neurosyphilis subtypes was as follows: 0.56:1 (15/27) for asymptomatic neurosyphilis, 1.33:1 (8/6) for meningeal neurosyphilis, 0.70:1 (7/10) for meningovascular neurosyphilis, and 3.25:1 (13/4) for general paresis (Fig. 2B). The median serum RPR titer for the overall cohort was 1:16 (IQR: 1:8–1:32). Subtype-specific median titers were as follows: asymptomatic neurosyphilis, 1:16 (IQR: 1:8–1:32); meningeal neurosyphilis, 1:32 (IQR: 1:8–1:32); meningovascular neurosyphilis, 1:16 (IQR: 1:8–1:64); and general paresis, 1:32 (IQR: 1:16–1:64). No statistically significant differences in serum RPR titers were observed among the neurosyphilis subtypes (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2C). Collectively, serum RPR titers were not significantly associated with neurosyphilis subtypes, and more than half of the neurosyphilis patients in this cohort presented with titers ≤ 1:16.

The characteristics of cerebrospinal fluid RPR titer, protein, and WBC count in neurosyphilis patients

All patients in this study tested positive for treponemal antibodies in CSF. The distribution of CSF RPR titers is shown in Fig. 3A: a titer of 1:4 was the most common, observed in 29 patients (32.22%), followed by CSF RPR negative in 22 patients (24.44%); a titer of 1:16 was the least frequent, found in only one patient (1.11%). When CSF RPR titers were categorized as negative or positive (≥ 1:1), 75.56% (68/90) of neurosyphilis patients exhibited positive CSF RPR results. The ratio of CSF RPR-positive to negative cases across subtypes was as follows: asymptomatic neurosyphilis, 1.63:1 (26/16); meningeal neurosyphilis, 6:1 (12/2); meningovascular neurosyphilis, 4.67:1 (14/3); and general paresis, 16:1 (16/1) (Fig. 3B). The median CSF RPR titer for the entire cohort was 1:2 (IQR: 1:1–1:4). Subtype-specific median CSF RPR titers were as follows: asymptomatic neurosyphilis, 1:1 (IQR: negative to 1:2); meningeal neurosyphilis, 1:4 (IQR: 1:1–1:4); meningovascular neurosyphilis, 1:4 (IQR: 1:2–1:4); and general paresis, 1:4 (IQR: 1:2–1:8). Statistical analysis revealed significant differences in CSF RPR titers among the four neurosyphilis subtypes. Post-hoc comparisons confirmed that the asymptomatic neurosyphilis group had significantly lower median CSF RPR titers than the general paresis group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3C).

The median CSF protein level among all neurosyphilis patients was 608.15 mg/L (IQR: 504.00–777.63) (Normal CSF protein range 150–500 mg/L). CSF protein levels differed significantly across the four neurosyphilis subtypes. Specifically, the asymptomatic neurosyphilis group had a median CSF protein level of 537.95 mg/L (IQR: 466.65–652.67), which was significantly lower than that in the meningovascular neurosyphilis group (709.00 mg/L; IQR: 564.50–947.35) (P < 0.01) and the general paresis group (787.00 mg/L; IQR: 604.00–1022.55) (P < 0.001). The meningeal neurosyphilis group (552.00 mg/L; IQR: 507.75–796.25) also showed a significantly lower median CSF protein level compared to the general paresis group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3D). When CSF protein levels were categorized as normal (≤ 500 mg/L) or elevated (> 500 mg/L), the majority of patients (75.56%, 68/90) exhibited elevated levels, while 24.44% (22/90) remained within the normal range. The ratio of elevated to normal CSF protein levels by subtype was as follows: asymptomatic neurosyphilis, 1.47:1 (25/17); meningeal neurosyphilis, 3.67:1 (11/3); meningovascular neurosyphilis, 16:1 (16/1); and general paresis, 16:1 (16/1) (Fig. 3E).

The median CSF WBC count for the entire neurosyphilis cohort was 21.00 cells/µL (IQR: 9.50–56.00) (Normal CSF WBC count: ≤5 cells/µL). Subtype-specific median WBC counts were as follows: asymptomatic neurosyphilis, 16.50 cells/µL (IQR: 5.50–43.75); meningeal neurosyphilis, 28.50 cells/µL (IQR: 18.50–63.50); meningovascular neurosyphilis, 22.00 cells/µL (IQR: 8.00–70.00); and general paresis, 39.00 cells/µL (IQR: 17.00–62.00). No statistically significant differences in CSF WBC counts were observed among the subtypes when analyzed as a continuous variable (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3F). When CSF WBC counts were dichotomized into normal (≤ 5 cells/µL) or elevated (> 5 cells/µL), the majority of patients (72.22%, 65/90) exhibited elevated counts, while 27.78% (25/90) fell within the normal range. The ratio of elevated-to-normal CSF WBC counts across neurosyphilis subtypes was as follows: asymptomatic neurosyphilis, 1.47:1 (25/17); meningeal neurosyphilis, 13:1 (13/1); meningovascular neurosyphilis, 2.4:1 (12/5); and general paresis, 7.5:1 (15/2) (Fig. 3G). Overall, these results show that CSF abnormalities (notably RPR titers and protein levels) correlated with clinical severity, with the asymptomatic group being the mildest and the general paresis group the most severe.

Correlations among laboratory indicators in neurosyphilis patients

To evaluate correlations among laboratory indicators in neurosyphilis patients, Spearman correlation analysis was performed. A moderate positive correlation was observed between serum and CSF RPR titers (rₛ = 0.504, P < 0.001; Fig. 4A). The serum RPR titer showed a low positive correlation with CSF WBC count (rₛ = 0.415, P < 0.001; Fig. 4B), but no significant correlation was found with CSF protein level (rₛ = 0.146, P = 0.171; Fig. 4C). Furthermore, the CSF RPR titer was lowly positively correlated with both CSF WBC count (rₛ = 0.423, P < 0.001; Fig. 4D) and CSF protein level (rₛ = 0.364, P < 0.001; Fig. 4E). A statistically significant but negligible correlation was identified between the CSF protein level and the CSF WBC count (rₛ = 0.233, P < 0.05; Fig. 4F). These findings suggest a moderate correlation between serum and CSF RPR titers. Nonetheless, the overall low strength of most correlations underscores that serum RPR alone cannot fully capture the complexity of central nervous system involvement in neurosyphilis.

Discussion

Neurosyphilis, a central nervous system infection caused by Treponema pallidum, can develop at any stage of syphilis and presents with highly heterogeneous clinical manifestations. The study retrospectively analyzed demographic and laboratory characteristics of 90 HIV-negative neurosyphilis patients in a tertiary specialized hospital. This study cohort revealed that neurosyphilis predominantly presents as an asymptomatic infection, frequently identified incidentally in middle-aged individuals with lower socioeconomic status. The presence of younger patients underscores its expanding clinical spectrum. Critically, over half of the cases would be missed if lumbar puncture were restricted to patients with serum RPR > 1:16, highlighting the limitation of relying on serological titers alone for diagnosing neuroinvasion. The strong association between CSF abnormalities (RPR and protein) and clinical severity confirms the essential role of CSF analysis in disease staging and differential diagnosis. Collectively, these findings enhance our understanding of the demographic and laboratory characteristics of neurosyphilis and may contribute to the development of more targeted diagnostic and public health strategies for this neurologically invasive stage of syphilis.

A systematic review of reported neurosyphilis cases in China indicated that parenchymal syphilis constituted the predominant clinical manifestation (57.3%), followed by asymptomatic neurosyphilis (16.7%) [20]. Data from Shanghai Skin Disease Hospital (also a tertiary dermatology hospital) revealed that the predominant type of neurosyphilis was asymptomatic neurosyphilis, accounting for 59.9% of the entire clinical sample [21]. This aligns closely with our findings and underscores the pivotal role of specialized dermatology centers in identifying occult neurosyphilis cases. Regional differences in neurosyphilis subtype distribution are presumably driven by variations in syphilis screening practices and access to care. Asymptomatic neurosyphilis, lacking typical symptoms, depends heavily on systematic screening—71.43% of asymptomatic neurosyphilis cases in this study were detected via routine screening, indicating better identification of such occult cases in regions with robust screening programs. In contrast, general paresis, a common parenchymatous subtype often misdiagnosed as mental illness, was incidentally found in 88.23% of cases (not via targeted screening). This highlights a “symptom-oriented bias” in neurosyphilis detection. Specifically, symptomatic subtypes are identified via clinical visits, whereas asymptomatic ones require proactive screening. In line with the literature, our data showed that nearly half of the patients (48.89%) had no prior syphilis treatment, with the highest untreated rate in the general paresis group (70.59%) and the lowest in the asymptomatic group (30.95%), further supporting that lack of prior antisyphilitic therapy is an important risk factor for symptomatic neurosyphilis [22, 23]. Critically, these findings support routine syphilis serological testing in patients with unexplained cognitive or psychiatric symptoms to reduce misdiagnosis of general paresis-like subtypes, although the diagnostic accuracy of this approach remains to be validated in controlled studies. Additionally, previous studies have established advanced age as a significant risk factor for neurosyphilis, with age over 45 years being identified as an independent risk factor in syphilis patients [24–27]. While our findings confirm that the majority of patients were over 45, a notable proportion (16.67%, 15/90) were under 40, with the youngest patient being 21 years old. This finding challenges the conventional view of neurosyphilis as being a disease solely associated with advanced age. Consequently, there is a pressing need to adapt clinical screening strategies and enhance public health education targeting younger populations to facilitate early diagnosis and timely intervention.

The serum RPR titer is widely used as a serological marker to evaluate syphilis disease activity and monitor treatment response. Previous studies have consistently reported a significantly higher median serum RPR titer in patients with neurosyphilis compared to those with non-neurosyphilitic syphilis [15, 16, 28]. Furthermore, among neurosyphilis patients, symptomatic cases exhibit a significantly higher median RPR titer than asymptomatic cases [29]. Although the present study did not include a non-neurosyphilis control group, our analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in serum RPR titers across the four neurosyphilis subtypes. Prior research has proposed a serum RPR titer > 1:32 as a predictive marker for neurosyphilis in HIV-negative individuals [30, 31], whereas in HIV-coinfected syphilis patients, a serum Toluidine Red Unheated Serum Test (TRUST) titer ≥ 1:16 serves as a key predictor of neurosyphilis [24]. In alignment with these findings, the European Guidelines on Syphilis recommend CSF analysis to exclude asymptomatic neurosyphilis in HIV-positive patients with serum RPR titers > 1:32 [14]. Notably, in our cohort, more than half (52.22%, 47/90) of confirmed neurosyphilis cases presented with serum RPR titers ≤ 1:16, indicating that using a serum RPR titer of ≤ 1:16 as a predictor would miss more than half of neurosyphilis cases. This observation underscores the importance of extending clinical suspicion for neurosyphilis beyond high-titer patients to include those with low serological titers. Therefore, the diagnosis of neurosyphilis should be based on a comprehensive evaluation that integrates clinical presentation, serological testing, and CSF analysis to reduce the risk of underdiagnosis.

It should be acknowledged that for asymptomatic early syphilis patients, the clinical and prognostic value of abnormal CSF findings remains uncertain, and CSF examination has not been shown to improve clinical outcomes. Thus, lumbar puncture decisions in these patients should be made cautiously, considering these uncertainties. Our findings provide a clear correlation between CSF profiles and the clinical severity of neurosyphilis. The most compelling evidence for this association comes from the analysis of CSF protein levels. We observed a stepwise increase in median CSF protein concentrations across the disease spectrum, from the asymptomatic form, through meningeal and meningovascular involvement, to the most severe parenchymal form, general paresis. This progressive elevation strongly suggests that the degree of blood-brain barrier disruption and intrathecal inflammation intensifies with clinical progression. This gradient of CSF abnormality was further reinforced by the RPR titers. The significantly lower median CSF RPR titer in asymptomatic neurosyphilis compared to general paresis indicates a lower antigenic load or a less robust humoral immune response within the central nervous system. The fact that the majority of patients across all subtypes were CSF RPR-positive (75.56%) confirms the critical role of this test in diagnosis. However, the graded increase in both CSF RPR titers and protein levels from asymptomatic to symptomatic subtypes provides a more nuanced picture than a simple positive/negative dichotomy, highlighting that the degree of these abnormalities carries important clinical and pathological implications. Interestingly, while CSF WBC counts were also frequently elevated, they did not differ significantly among subtypes. This suggests that pleocytosis is a common but non-specific indicator of neuroinflammation. In contrast, the magnitude of CSF protein elevation and CSF RPR titer might be relatively more reflective of the underlying pathological burden and tissue damage in neurosyphilis, although this notion requires further validation. Notably, although a moderate correlation was observed between serum and CSF RPR titers—supporting the role of serum RPR as an indirect marker of intrathecal activity—the overall strength of this association was limited. This underscores the inadequacy of relying solely on serological testing to diagnose neuroinvasion. Clinically, this is of particular importance, as patients with low or moderate serum RPR titers may still exhibit neurosyphilis, potentially leading to underdiagnosis if CSF analysis is omitted.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective investigation with a relatively small sample size (n = 90). Additionally, because our hospital serves as a municipal STD control center with active screening protocols, asymptomatic cases may be overrepresented and severe symptomatic cases underrepresented relative to neurology settings, which warrants caution when extrapolating our findings. Second, the cross-sectional nature of the data and the lack of longitudinal follow-up limit our ability to elucidate the natural progression between neurosyphilis subtypes—such as the rate of evolution from asymptomatic to symptomatic forms—or to evaluate the long-term impact of subtype-specific therapeutic strategies. Third, we used CSF RPR instead of the recommended CSF Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) due to reagent unavailability in China. Given the higher false‑negative rate of RPR, the true neurosyphilis prevalence may be underestimated. Fourth, the absence of a control group comprising syphilis patients without neurological involvement prevents direct assessment of the specificity of the observed CSF profiles for neurosyphilis diagnosis and staging. Fifth, our cohort was limited to four major neurosyphilis subtypes and did not include other forms such as ocular or otosyphilis. This is likely due to the specialized focus of our dermatology hospital, where patients with primary ocular or auditory symptoms typically seek initial care in ophthalmology or otology settings. Future studies would benefit from a multi-center design that includes these specialties to obtain a more representative sample.

In summary, this study demonstrates that neurosyphilis in Dalian, China, exhibits a trend toward asymptomatic presentation and younger onset, predominantly affecting middle-aged individuals with lower education levels and high unemployment. Most cases were identified incidentally or through screening rather than by neurological symptoms, indicating a substantial risk of underdiagnosis. Importantly, reliance on a serum RPR titer > 1:16 as the threshold for lumbar puncture would have missed over half of the confirmed neurosyphilis cases. Conversely, CSF RPR titers and protein levels showed a significant positive correlation with clinical severity, underscoring their essential role in accurate diagnosis and stratification. Our findings not only contribute critical data to understanding the neurosyphilis spectrum in China but also provide valuable insights for elucidating global heterogeneity in the disease and refining management approaches worldwide.
