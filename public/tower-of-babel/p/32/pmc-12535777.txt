Probiotics: a supplement to the gluten-free diet in celiac disease
Authors: Hasna Ait Said, Lahcen Elmoumou, Bouchra Rherissi, Nadia El Kadmiri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Celiac disease is an enteropathy caused by intolerance to gluten, with genetic and environmental factors playing a key part in its pathogenesis. This review addresses the use of the gluten-free diet as the primary treatment for patients with celiac disease and the use of probiotics as an adjunctive therapy. The gluten-free diet is the sole treatment for preventing symptoms and allowing the inflamed intestinal mucosa to recover. The gluten-free diet, however, also has some very significant challenges like its complexity, the need for ongoing monitoring by dieticians, and the risk of contamination with gluten even in gluten-free labelled foods.At the same time, the growing interest in the use of probiotics as an adjunct therapy in celiac disease management is based on the hypothetical contribution of the gut microbiota to the pathophysiology of the disease. It has been hypothesized that gut dysbiosis could be involved in the development and maintenance of celiac disease symptoms. Probiotics, in particular the genera Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, are among the promising adjuvants for the improvement of gut health. These beneficial microorganisms may play an essential important, crucial role in the breakdown or modification of gluten polypeptides and in the reduction of intestinal epithelial cell damage caused by gliadin.While probiotic use is not a replacement for a gluten-free diet, it can have additive benefits by assisting in the preservation of a healthy balance of gut microbiota and lessening some chronic gastrointestinal symptoms.

Introduction

Celiac disease (CeD) is increasingly recognized as a chronic autoimmune enteropathy that is genetically determined and triggered by exposure to gluten. Its prevalence is currently estimated to be around 1 in 266 people worldwide (1). The clinical classification of CeD includes several distinct forms. The classic symptomatic forms are characterized by symptoms such as diarrhea, bloating, and dyspepsia, with or without malabsorption. Atypical forms have less obvious and variable symptoms, while asymptomatic or silent forms have no gastrointestinal symptoms (2).

During proteolytic digestion in the intestinal lumen, the prolamins present in cereals such as wheat (gliadin), barley (hordein), and rye (secalin) are broken down into a family of polypeptides rich in glutamine and proline (3). These polypeptides are responsible for the inappropriate immune response that characterizes CeD. Peptides rich in glutamine and proline are particularly resistant to degradation by digestive enzymes, allowing them to penetrate the intestinal mucosa. Once in the small intestine, these peptides trigger an immune response mediated by T lymphocytes, which recognise these fragments as foreign elements. This immune response leads to chronic inflammation and damage to the intestinal wall, resulting in the symptoms and complications associated with CeD (3).

To manage this immune response, the current management of CeD relies on a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet (GFD) (4). However, although the Codex Alimentarius allows up to 20 parts per million (ppm) of gluten in foods, some patients do not respond satisfactorily to a strict GFD (5). In fact, a minority of people diagnosed with CeD continue to experience persistent symptoms and histological abnormalities despite adherence to a GFD. These patients may also experience symptomatic relapses despite adherence to treatment, and in some cases, the disease progresses to a more severe form known as refractory sprue (6).

In addition to genetic predisposition, more and more studies are highlighting the importance of environmental factors in the development of CeD, particularly the crucial role of the gut microbiota (7). Research suggests that changes in the gut microbiome in individuals carrying the HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8 genes may influence the way gluten is processed in the intestinal lumen (8). These changes may affect intestinal barrier permeability and innate and adaptive immune responses, contributing to the development or exacerbation of gluten-sensitive enteropathy (9). This increased understanding of the interaction between the gut microbiota and CeD has led to the exploration of potential strategies to improve the clinical management of this condition. Among these strategies, the use of probiotics is emerging as a promising option. Because of their ability to modulate the gut microbiome, probiotics could play an adjuvant role in the dietary management of CeD. They could help to restore a healthy microbial balance, strengthen the intestinal barrier, and influence immune responses, offering a complementary approach to strict GFD (10). This review aims to discuss the GFD for patients with CeD and the use of probiotics as an emerging therapy for this condition.

Gluten-free diet in celiac disease

A lifelong GFD is the recommended treatment for people suffering from CeD (11). These individuals follow a rigorous GFD, which means avoiding all foods containing this protein. This includes the gluten present in wheat (notably gliadin), barley (hordeins), oats (avenins), rye (secalins), and other closely related cereals (12). Because of the dietary restrictions imposed by the GFD, it is recommended that those concerned include other nutritious sources in their diet, such as vegetables, fruit, meat, fish, as products that are specifically gluten-free. What's more, in response to growing demand for products suitable for people on a GFD, the food industry has dramatically increased production of foods labeled "gluten-free". Indeed, in 2016, retail sales of gluten-free foods topped $15.5 billion, more than double the amounts seen in 2011 (13). To guarantee transparency and consumer safety, the FDA has specific regulations regarding the labelling of 'gluten-free' products. Under these regulations, a product must meet specific regulatory requirements in order to be labelled as 'gluten-free', 'without gluten' or similar. Specifically, a product is considered 'gluten-free' if it contains less than 20 ppm of gluten. The aim of this standard is to ensure that products labelled as such contain enough gluten to be safe for people with CeD or gluten sensitivity (14).

Even when following a strict GFD, it is virtually impossible to eliminate the risk of gluten contamination, as many foods contain hidden gluten, such as sausages, soups, soy sauce, and ice cream. What's more, even gluten-free products can contain traces of gluten due to cross-contamination. This occurs when foods containing gluten are processed, stored, or prepared in the same facilities as gluten-free products, resulting in residues that can contaminate foods claimed to be gluten-free. Indeed, a meta-analysis by Guennouni et al. showed that 15.12% of gluten-free foods tested were contaminated with gluten, with concentrations exceeding 20 mg/kg. Naturally gluten-free foods were much more affected (28.32%) than labelled gluten-free products (9.52%) and meals served in food services (4.66%) (15). Syage et al. demonstrated that even in individuals with CeD adhering to a strict gluten-free diet, regular ingestion of small amounts of gluten can still occur, enough to trigger symptoms and cause intestinal damage. In this study, adult celiac patients maintained a GFD while their gluten exposure was objectively assessed through the detection of immunogenic peptides in stool and urine. The results showed that, on average, participants unknowingly consumed between 150 and 400 mg of gluten per day. Although this amount is much lower than in a typical standard diet, it remains sufficient to sustain intestinal inflammation and hinder mucosal healing (16).

Efficacy of gluten-free diet in celiac disease

Extensive studies have shown that a GFD can be very effective in restoring the structure of the small intestine, especially in children. In fact, when a strict GFD is followed, 95% of children can recover normal small bowel histology within two years (17). This recovery is particularly remarkable in young patients, who seem to respond more quickly and effectively to the diet. In adults, the recovery process appears to be longer and less predictable. Research shows that only 34% of adult patients achieve complete mucosal recovery after two years on a GFD. However, this rises to 66% after five years (18). However, there is some evidence to suggest that older patients, particularly those over the age of 60, may have difficulty achieving full recovery even when following a strict gluten-free diet. In this age group, it has also been found that despite strict adherence to the diet for 24 months, complete recovery of the intestinal mucosa may remain partial, both endoscopically and histologically (19).

In general, CeD is considered to be a permanent intolerance to gluten. However, some patients can discontinue the GFD without symptoms or clinical signs. In this context, Hopman et al set out to determine whether CeD patients can develop gluten tolerance. The study included 77 patients, 53 of whom were confirmed to have true CeD. The results suggest that the development of gluten tolerance is possible in some CeD patients. In particular, normal intestinal mucosa was observed in four of the eight patients who consumed gluten, suggesting that these patients may have developed some tolerance. A better understanding of the mechanisms of this tolerance may help to identify patients for whom a lifelong GFD may not be necessary. This could pave the way for personalized therapeutic approaches and more flexible management of CeD by identifying cases where the diet can be adapted to individual gluten tolerance (20).

Gluten-free diet and gut microbiota

GFD is partially effective at rebuilding intestinal microbiota in patients with CeD. Although the dietary elimination of gluten is necessary, essential to reduce symptoms and heal intestinal lesions, it is not always enough to completely restore a healthy, balanced intestinal microbiota. According to Palm et al, the institution of a GFD had several notable effects on the composition of gut microbiota. The diet resulted in a significant reduction in populations of beneficial gut bacteria, such as Bifidobacteria and Lactobacilli, that play a role in gut health and immunity. Concurrently, it has encouraged a greater proliferation of potentially pathogenic bacteria, specifically Bacteroides, Enterobacteria, and Escherichia coli, which have negative impacts on digestive health. This microbial composition shift has also resulted in a decrease in the capacity of fecal samples to stimulate host immunity (21) robustly. Similarly, Nadal et al. observed a reduction in the populations of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium compared with Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli and Bacteroides and in children with CeD, whether untreated or treated with a GFD. These observations suggest that this dietary approach may not only reduce populations of beneficial bacteria but also promote an increase in Enterobacteria, which are markers associated with the active phase of CeD (22).

Probiotics in celiac disease

Recent advances in our understanding of the pathophysiology of CeD have led to the development of a number of innovative and promising therapeutic approaches. These include treatments such as zonulin inhibitors, genetically modified gluten, therapeutic vaccines, tissue transglutaminase inhibitors, and, more recently, probiotics (23). According to the WHO/FAO, probiotics are live micro-organisms which, when consumed in sufficient quantities, offer health benefits to the host (24). These benefits can manifest themselves through several mechanisms, including: (i) the production of inhibitory substances such as organic acids, bacteriocins and hydrogen peroxide (ii) the blocking of pathogen adhesion sites, (iii) the degradation of toxin receptors, (iv) competition for available nutrients, and (v) modulation of the immune response (25).

Probiotics have shown potential to influence the prognosis of CeD by modifying the gut microbial profile. By promoting the growth of beneficial microbes and restoring a healthier balance in the duodenal microbiota, probiotics may help alleviate symptoms and improve the host immune response (Figure 1). To assess the efficacy of probiotics in the context of CeD, several in vitro studies have been conducted to observe their effects on cell cultures and experimental models. These studies have investigated how probiotics modify the interaction between intestinal microbes and the intestinal mucosa. At the same time, clinical trials have been carried out to determine the real impact of probiotic administration on CeD patients.

Some probiotics have been shown to break down or modify gluten polypeptides, which could be important for people with gluten sensitivity or CeD. A study conducted by De Angelis and colleagues found that VSL#3 probiotic, a freeze-dried preparation containing approximately 450 billion cells per sachet, was particularly effective at hydrolyzing gliadin polypeptides compared to other commercial probiotic products. VSL#3 includes a diverse combination of strains at very high concentrations (10¹¹ CFU/g), including Streptococcus thermophilus, L. acidophilus, Lactobacillus casei, and L. delbrueckii ssp. bulgaricus, L. plantarum, Bifidobacterium infantis, B. longum, and B. breve. This diverse mix exerts potent enzymatic activity, breaking down gluten peptides into smaller fragments that are potentially less immunogenic and less toxic (26). The epitopes involved in gluten-related disorders, such as CeD, contain a high proportion of proline residues. Proline is unique among the 20 amino acids because of its cyclic structure, which imposes specific constraints on the structure of peptides and proteins (27). This particular conformation makes gluten peptides extremely resistant to hydrolysis. VSL#3's ability to target these resistant peptides is therefore particularly valuable. In comparison, other probiotics such as Oxadrop (containing Bifidobacterium infantis, Lactobacillus acidophilus, Lactobacillus brevis, and Streptococcus thermophilus), Florisia (containing Lactobacillus brevis, Lactobacillus salivarius subsp. salicinius, and Lactobacillus plantarum), and Yovis (including Bifidobacterium breve, Bifidobacterium infantis, Bifidobacterium longum, Lactobacillus acidophilus, Lactobacillus casei, Lactobacillus plantarum, and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus, Enterococcus faecium, and Streptococcus salivarius subsp. thermophilus) were less effective at degrading gliadin polypeptides. These results suggest that VSL#3 may offer a significant advantage in the treatment of gluten-related disorders due to its superior ability to modify gliadin polypeptides (26).

Some strains of Lactobacilli and Bifidobacteria are relevant to improving gut health. Extensive studies of intestinal microbiota, e.g., via fecal and duodenal biopsy analysis, have shown that these strains are capable of reducing symptoms in CeD patients on GFD. Olivares et al evaluated the potential effects of Bifidobacterium longum CECT 7347 in children recently diagnosed with CeD. Thirty-three children were enrolled in a randomized double-blind, double-masked placebo-controlled clinical trial. They were given a daily capsule of B. longum CECT 7347 (10^9 colony-forming units) or placebo (excipients) for three months on a GFD. It was noted that the probiotic group had a reduction of intestinal inflammation and sIgA levels in fecal samples compared to the placebo group. In addition, children given the probiotic had better growth parameters, peripheral lymphocyte subset alterations, and reduced levels of TNF (28). Furthermore, a study carried out by Primec and collaborators evaluated the supplementation effect of Bifidobacterium breve BR03 (DSM 16604) and B. breve B632 (DSM 24706) on children with CeD following a gluten-free diet. In this trial, individuals were administered either a placebo or a mix of the two probiotic strains at a dose of 2 × 10⁹ CFU per day for three months. The study demonstrated significant correlations between some microbial groups and inflammatory markers: Verrucomicrobia, Parcubacteria, and some unidentified phyla of bacteria and Archaea correlated positively with TNF-α, indicating their potential role in the inflammatory process of the disease. Moreover, Proteobacteria were highly associated with fecal short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) levels. Probiotic supplementation decreased certain of these populations, such as Verrucomicrobia, Synergistetes, and Euryarchaeota, which were negatively associated with SCFAs, suggesting their possible implication in microbiota imbalance. Synergistetes and Euryarchaeota could also play a role in ensuring an anti-inflammatory status in the healthy intestine, making them valid targets to recover the intestinal microbiota in celiac patients (29). Using the same strains, a randomized, double-blind, double-masked, placebo-controlled clinical trial was conducted in children with CeD who were already stabilized on a GFD. For three months, participants received either daily supplementation with Bifidobacterium breve BR03 and B632 (2 × 10⁹ CFU/day) or a placebo. The main parameters evaluated included TNF-α production, as well as other markers of inflammation and the composition of the gut microbiota. The results showed that taking B. breve led to a significant reduction in TNF-α production compared to the placebo. However, the authors did not observe any significant clinical changes in terms of gastrointestinal symptoms, probably due to the fact that the children were already on a GFD and relatively asymptomatic at baseline. Favorable changes in the composition of the microbiota, including an increase in Bifidobacteria, were also reported. The authors conclude that B. breve may have an anti-inflammatory effect in the intestines of children with CeD, suggesting a potential role as an adjunct to a GFD. However, its long-term clinical impact remains to be confirmed (30).

Similarly, another double-masked, randomized, placebo-controlled study was conducted in adults with CeD who had not yet stabilized on a GFD. Participants received either B. infantis NLS at a dose of 1 × 10⁹ CFU/day or a placebo for six weeks. Intestinal biopsies were performed before and after the intervention to analyze the expression of α-defensin-5, an antimicrobial peptide produced by Paneth cells and considered a marker of innate intestinal immunity. The results showed that probiotic treatment led to a significant reduction in the expression of α-defensin-5 in the intestinal mucosa, suggesting a potential effect of modulating the innate immune response. However, despite this biological change, no notable clinical improvement in symptoms or quality of life was observed in patients compared to the placebo group. The authors conclude that B. infantis NLS may influence specific intestinal immune mechanisms in active CeD, but that these biological effects do not necessarily translate into short-term clinical benefit (31). In the same context, a study conducted by Smecuol et al. examined the effect of Bifidobacterium infantis NLS supplementation in adults with active CeD, in addition to a gluten-free diet. Participants received 1 × 10⁹ CFU/day for three weeks as part of a randomized, double-blind, double-masked, placebo-controlled trial. The evaluation focused on changes in gastrointestinal symptoms, quality of life, and specific biological parameters. The results showed a significant improvement in digestive symptoms in the probiotic group, particularly for indigestion (P = 0.0035), constipation (P = 0.0483), and reflux (P = 0.0586), compared to the placebo group. However, no significant changes were observed in inflammatory markers or intestinal mucosal histology. Nevertheless, a trend toward a decrease in disease-specific antibodies was observed: the final/baseline ratios of tissue transglutaminase IgA and deamidated gliadin IgA concentrations were lower in the probiotic group, with marginal statistical significance (P = 0.055 for tTG IgA and P = 0.181 for DGP IgA) (32). As part of a systematic review, Harnett et al. included several clinical trials using various strains such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Bifidobacterium breve, and Lactobacillus plantarum, as well as combined formulations. Treatment durations ranged from a few weeks to several months, and doses varied from 10⁸ to 10¹¹ CFU/day. The parameters evaluated included intestinal permeability, immune response, clinical symptoms, serological biomarkers, and histological parameters. The aggregated results showed no significant benefit compared to placebo, regardless of the strain or protocol used. The authors attributed this lack of a significant effect to the wide methodological heterogeneity of the studies, including sample size, strains, doses, and intervention durations. They emphasized the need for more homogeneous and standardized protocols to rigorously evaluate the efficacy of probiotics in CeD (33). Consistently, a systematic review of seven randomized controlled trials conducted in healthy adults found no effect of probiotic supplementation on the composition of the fecal microbiota in terms of α diversity, richness, or evenness, compared to placebo. However, a single study reported a significant change in the overall structure of the fecal bacterial community, as reflected by β diversity, compared to placebo. These results, obtained in different clinical settings, reinforce the idea that the effects of probiotics remain difficult to demonstrate robustly due to the variability of the methodologies used (34).

Despite their promising effects, the use of probiotics in immunocompromised patients or those with severe comorbidities requires particular caution. Several studies have reported cases of opportunistic infections associated with the administration of probiotics, including Bacteremia and Fungemia linked to strains such as Lactobacillus spp. or Saccharomyces boulardii (35). These adverse events, although rare, highlight the importance of a rigorous assessment of the benefit-to-risk ratio before prescribing probiotics, particularly in immunocompromised patients, those with implanted medical devices, or those with central catheters (36).

In vitro studies have shown that certain strains of lactobacilli are able to reduce the toxicity of gluten epitopes. When added to sourdough, these specific strains effectively lyse gluten peptides rich in proline and glutamine, reducing the concentration of gluten to less than 10 ppm, which is considered a safe level for sensitive individuals. At the same time, fungal proteases intervene by further degrading the gluten into smaller, less immunogenic peptides. This dual approach - lysis by lactobacilli and degradation by fungal proteases - results in a significant reduction in toxic epitopes, making wheat-based products potentially safer for people with coeliac disease (37). Four strains of lactobacilli isolated from the proximal gastrointestinal tract of pigs (L. ruminis, L. johnsonii, L. salivarius, and L. amylovorus) showed the greatest, most excellent, andmost remarkable ability to degrade gluten peptides (38). Encouraging results were also obtained in a study investigating the immunogenicity of wheat flour pre-treated with selected Lactobacilli (L. brevis 14G, L. alimentarius 15M, L. hilgardii, and L. sanfranciscensis 7A) and fungal proteases. This evaluation was carried out both in vitro, using intestinal T cell lines, and in vivo in 20 CeD patients who had undergone a short-term gluten challenge. The results showed that hydrolyzed wheat flour failed to activate T cell lines in the intestinal mucosa of celiac patients. Consistent with in vitro findings, no significant increase in INF-γ secretion was observed in patients consuming hydrolyzed wheat flour compared to healthy controls (39).

Recently, a study was conducted using an animal model of gliadin-induced enteropathy to observe whether Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (L.GG) could have beneficial effects. Specifically, this research investigated the protective effects of L.GG against gliadin-induced enteropathy digested with pepsin-trypsin by analysing jejunal tissue samples taken from Wistar rats. The probiotic effects were assessed by examining the architecture of the intestinal mucosa, the expression of intercellular junction proteins, and the metabolism of polyamines. The results show that L.GG protects the intestinal mucosa of rats against damage caused by gliadin digested by pepsin-trypsin by preventing a decrease in the expression of intercellular junction proteins (40). Similar beneficial effects were observed with the probiotic Bifidobacterium longum CECT 7347, which reduced the production of inflammatory cytokines and TNF-α in the enteropathy model. An increase in CD4+, CD4+/Foxp3+, and CD8+ T cells was observed in sensitised and gliadin-fed animals. However, administration of B. longum CECT 7347 decreased CD4+ and CD4+/Foxp3+ cell populations while increasing CD8+ T cell populations. These effects suggest that B. longum CECT 7347 may moderately attenuate specific changes in jejunal structure (41). In addition, Saccharomyces boulardii KK1 strain, a probiotic isolated from the commensal flora, also improved the development of enteropathy in association with a reduction in CD71 expression in epithelial cells and local cytokine production (42). In view of the crucial role of probiotics in regulating immune responses, it is essential to note that Lactobacillus spp. and Bifidobacterium lactis strains can significantly modulate both innate and adaptive immune responses in DQ8 transgenic mice (43). Additionally, the commensal probiotic strain Bifidobacterium longum NCC2705 produces a serine protease inhibitor with immunomodulatory properties (44). Notably, administration of Lactobacillus casei has been shown to inhibit cyclooxygenase activity in HLA-DQ8 transgenic mice sensitized to gliadin. This results in villous blunting, deregulation of TNF-α expression, and a reduction in gliadin-specific IL-2 production (45). All these findings suggest that integrating probiotics into therapeutic strategies may offer innovative solutions for the management of CeD and other gluten-related disorders. However, further studies are needed to confirm their efficacy and optimize their clinical use.

Although the studies cited present a wide variety of methodologies, including in vitro experiments, animal models, and clinical trials in humans, this heterogeneity complicates direct comparisons of results. It limits the scope of conclusions that can be drawn. However, the positive effects observed in animal models reinforce the hypothesis that certain probiotics, whose clinical efficacy is beginning to be demonstrated, could help modulate gluten-induced inflammation and thus alleviate clinical symptoms in patients with CeD.

Conclusion

People with CeD often experience an imbalance in their gut microbiota, even when strictly adhering to a GFD. Because of this persistent imbalance, the integration of probiotics as an adjunct therapy to the GFD may offer significant benefits in the management of CeD.
