Conservative management of extensive maxillary adenomatoid odontogenic tumor with a customized cyst plug: a case report emphasizing differential diagnosis and bone regeneration
Authors: Zuwen Ma, Guangwei Chen, Huilin Wu, Weifa Li, Libin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis case report describes a large maxillary adenomatoid odontogenic tumor (AOT) initially misdiagnosed as a dentigerous cyst (DC) and highlights the postoperative use of a customized cyst plug to manage a palatal bone defect without bone grafting.Case presentationA 13-year-old girl presented with anterior maxillary swelling and a unilocular radiolucent lesion associated with an impacted canine. Background

AOT is a relatively rare benign odontogenic tumor, first described by Steensland in 1905 [1]. AOT accounts for approximately 3–7% of all odontogenic tumors and is characterized clinically as a slow-growing [2], well-demarcated lesion predominantly affecting adolescents, especially in the maxillary cuspid region [3]. Although rare, Philipsen et al. suggest that AOT ranks fourth among all odontogenic tumors in terms of frequency [4]. The precise pathogenesis of AOT remains unclear; however, it is widely accepted that it originates from the reduced enamel epithelium associated with unerupted teeth [5, 6]. Clinically and radiographically, AOT frequently resembles odontogenic cysts, particularly DC, resulting in misdiagnosis rates reported between 20–30% [5]. Such diagnostic confusion can lead to inappropriate surgical approaches, increasing the risk of postoperative complications, particularly due to the fragility of the thin palatal bone plate. This case report presents the clinical management of a 13-year-old female patient with extensive maxillary AOT, emphasizing the differential diagnosis between AOT and DC, exploring the efficacy of conservative surgical approaches, and highlighting the role of a personalized cyst plug in facilitating effective postoperative bone regeneration.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 13-year-old girl presented with right maxillary swelling that had first been noticed more than 3 months earlier. According to the clinical history, the swelling had gradually become more apparent before presentation. One day before referral, CBCT performed at another hospital revealed a cystic lesion in the right maxilla, and the patient was referred to our department for further management.

Clinical examination

According to the contemporaneous clinical record, asymmetry of the maxillofacial region, no boils or eczema on the surface skin, no obvious abnormality in the opening type and degree of opening, no obvious abnormality in the bilateral temporomandibular joints and facial nerve function. Intraoral examination showed permanent teeth, general oral hygiene, normal overjet and overbite, and a swelling of about 4.0 cm×3.8 cm in size extending from the right maxillary central incisor to the right second molar region. On palpation the swelling was firm, slightly tender, clear boundary, and slight redness and swelling of the gingiva. The pulp vitality of the related teeth were normal. TM = 0°. Because preoperative clinical photographs were unavailable, these findings could not be visually documented in the present report.

Auxiliary examination

CBCT showed a well-defined cystic lesion of approximately 4.0 × 3.8 cm in the right maxilla, with low-density areas and internal punctate calcified foci. The lesion was confined to the right maxilla and extended to the maxillary sinus, nasolacrimal duct, and the roots of teeth #16, #15, #14, and #12. An impacted tooth (#13) was located within the lesion, close to the nasolacrimal duct (Fig. 1). Based on the clinical and radiographic findings, the provisional preoperative diagnosis was DC associated with the impacted tooth #13 in the right maxilla.

Diagnostic and therapeutic procedures

The lesion was initially diagnosed preoperatively as a DC. No separate preoperative aspiration was performed in this case. Under local anesthesia, open decompression and extraction of the impacted tooth (#13) were performed. A square palatal incision was made over the lesion, and a mucoperiosteal flap was elevated. The palatal bony wall was then removed using an ultrasonic bone cutter to create access to the lesion. After exposure of the cystic cavity, yellowish fluid was released, and the lesional tissue was carefully curetted. During the procedure, the cyst wall was noted to be unusually thick and firm, raising suspicion that the lesion might not represent a simple odontogenic cyst. The impacted tooth was subsequently exposed and removed, and the cavity was irrigated thoroughly with sterile saline. After trimming of the bone margins and achievement of hemostasis, iodoform gauze was placed, and the wound was closed with silk sutures. Because of the atypical intraoperative appearance, part of the lesional tissue was submitted for histopathologic examination. Microscopically, the epithelial cells were arranged in duct-like or tubular structures, consistent with AOT (Fig. 2). The final pathologic diagnosis was AOT of the right maxilla.

Sutures were removed 7 days after surgery, and the wound healed uneventfully. At the 20-day follow-up, a through-and-through bony defect was noted in the palatal wound (Fig. 3A). Continued follow-up was arranged, and a cyst plug was placed for postoperative management of the defect (Fig. 4). At the 6- and 18-month follow-up visits, progressive healing of the palatal defect was observed. By 18 months after surgery, the defect had healed completely, and the patient reported no discomfort (Fig. 3B–C). CBCT at the final follow-up showed complete closure of the surgical site, and electric pulp test (EPT) of the teeth in the involved region remained normal (Fig. 5).

Discussion and conclusion

Diagnostic pitfalls between AOT and DC

The differential diagnostic features of follicular AOT and DC, together with their correlation to the present patient at different diagnostic stages, are summarized in Table 1. The histogenesis of AOT remains incompletely understood. Current evidence generally supports an origin from odontogenic epithelium, particularly remnants of the dental lamina and, in some cases, the reduced enamel epithelium associated with unerupted teeth [7]. In follicular AOT, which is the most common subtype and is frequently associated with impacted canines, origin from the lining epithelium of a DC or from cystic transformation of odontogenic epithelium has also been proposed [8]. In contrast, DC are developmental cysts that originate from the degeneration of the REE after crown formation [9]. This intrinsic difference determines their differences in biological behavior and pathological features. In terms of clinical presentation, patients with AOT usually present with a slow-growing, painless swelling with an average duration of up to 2–3 years [10]. In the present case, the patient came to the clinic with a complaint of a 3-month history, which is relatively rare in AOT and may have contributed to the initial misdiagnosis. In contrast, DC grows relatively quickly and may be accompanied by signs of infection. It is noteworthy that AOT exhibits a significant gender predisposition with a prevalence twice as high in females as in males, whereas there is no significant gender difference in DC [11].

Differential diagnosis between follicular AOT and DC in the present case

In this case, unilocular radiolucency accompanied by calcified dots or contained teeth demonstrated by CBCT, is a typical manifestation of AOT. These calcified foci correspond histologically to amyloid deposits within the tumor. In contrast, DC usually shows homogeneous translucent shadows and rarely calcifications. In addition, AOT usually has thicker walls (> 2 mm) and is not as closely related to the impacting teeth as DC. In this case, the atypical relationship of the lesion to the obstructing #13 teeth was one factor that led to the initial misdiagnosis. Histopathologic examination is the gold standard for confirming the diagnosis of AOT. In the present case, an abnormally thickened cyst wall was observed intraoperatively and was immediately sent for histopathological examination, a worthwhile practice. The characteristic pathologic manifestations of AOT are the formation of a duct-like structure by the tumor epithelium surrounded by spindle cells [12]. Notably, approximately 20–30% of AOT cases are initially misdiagnosed as DC, a statistic suggesting the clinician’s need for vigilance [5].

Rationale for conservative management

The mainstay of treatment for AOT is surgical excision or curettage [13]. Conservative local excision is usually sufficient because AOT is usually peritumoral and slow-growing, and non-invasive. For larger AOTs, open decompression may be considered to reduce the size of the tumor before resection [14]. In the present case, due to a preoperative misdiagnosis of a DC, open decompression surgery was used, but after abnormal thickening of the cyst wall was detected intraoperatively, the plan was promptly adjusted to thoroughly scrape the diseased tissue and send it for pathological examination, which is in line with the principle of treatment for AOTs. In adolescent patients, surgery requires special consideration for the protection of the permanent dental embryo and the impact on jaw development [13]. In this case, despite the involvement of the lesion in the nasolacrimal duct and maxillary sinus region, the adjacent pulp viability was successfully preserved through delicate operation (ultrasonic bone knife debridement, avoiding excessive stripping of soft tissues), and the 18-month postoperative follow-up showed normal root development.

Clinical justification for the customized cyst plug

AOT occurs in the maxillary anterior region, where the bone plate is thin and is prone to intraoperative cavitation defects [12, 14]. The successful application of a personalized cyst plug in this case promotes the healing of the postoperative bone defect and provides an important reference for the management of similar cases. The effect of open decompression is related to smooth drainage, and the cyst plug can keep the drainage port open, balance the pressure inside and outside the capsule, and promote the therapeutic effect [15].

Through in-depth analysis, we found that the combination of cyst plugs after open decompression surgery to prevent premature healing of the wound is a mainstream practice, and the patient in the present case has been wearing a cyst plug for 18 months after the surgery [16]. The cyst plug can ensure the effect of continuous drainage of capsule fluid, to avoid premature healing and formation of a dead space. It also prevents food from entering the cavity, makes cleaning easier for the patient, increases compliance, decreases swelling, and reduces the risk of secondary infections and postoperative complications [15]. Compared with invasive methods such as bone grafting, cyst plug therapy is less invasive and less costly, and is particularly suitable for adolescent patients in their growth period. From a biomechanical point of view, the cyst plug provides a stable mechanical environment for the defect area [17]. Relevant studies have shown that a cyst piug can improve the mechanical conditions for postoperative defect healing by dispersing occlusal forces and reducing stress concentration at the defect margin [18].

Limits of inference from a single adolescent case

Because this is a single-case observation without a comparator, no causal relationship can be established between use of the customized cyst plug and the favorable bone healing observed during follow-up. In particular, spontaneous healing and the relatively strong regenerative potential of an adolescent patient may have contributed substantially to the outcome [19]. Moreover, radiographic bone fill alone does not establish the underlying biological mechanism of healing. A further limitation of this report is the absence of a baseline true maxillary occlusal radiograph, which reduced conventional two-dimensional documentation of cortical expansion and clinical–radiographic correlation. Accordingly, its principal value lies in demonstrating a feasible conservative management approach for a large AOT initially misdiagnosed as DC and in suggesting that customized postoperative support may be considered in selected patients with similar defects. Further case series and comparative studies are needed to determine whether plug-assisted management offers reproducible benefits over alternative approaches, including spontaneous healing alone and graft-based reconstruction.

In summary, this case emphasizes that large follicular AOTs may closely mimic DCs on initial clinical and radiographic assessment, and that intraoperative vigilance with histopathologic confirmation remains essential for accurate diagnosis. It also shows that, in a carefully selected adolescent patient, conservative postoperative management of a palatal defect using a customized cyst plug was associated with satisfactory healing without bone grafting. Nevertheless, because this report describes only a single case, the observed outcome should not be interpreted as evidence of efficacy. Rather, this case provides a clinically relevant and graft-sparing management example that may inform future study in similar settings.
