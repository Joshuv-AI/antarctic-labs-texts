Multi-omics analysis of the maize ear diameter mutant3 (zmed3) provides insights into female inflorescence development
Authors: Jing Liu, Tianxiao Yang, Zhuoya Gao, Jianxin Li, Tuan Li, Jialei Ou, Yalan Li, Shuaishuai Zhang, Yinuo Wang, Huiling Xie, Weihua Li, Jihua Tang, Pengshuai Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe ear is a crucial component of grain yield in maize. Studying the mechanisms underlying ear development is therefore essential for genetic improvement and molecular breeding programs. Among various yield-influencing factors, ear development plays a central role, as it is orchestrated by the inflorescence meristem. This meristematic structure directly regulates key yield-related traits, including ear size, kernel number, and row arrangement.ResultsHere, we analyzed the zmed3 mutant, which shows flattened ear tip and disordered kernel rows, and is a single recessive mutation isolated from the Lx9801 breeding population. Using integrated transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analyses at the 4 mm stage of developing ears, we identified 1,589 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), 181 differentially accumulated proteins (DAPs), and 122 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) in zmed3 mutants compared with normal siblings. These global omics changes were primarily associated with central carbon metabolism. Mutant zmed3 inflorescence meristems (IMs) were initially enlarged, switched to a more fasciated pattern, and finally led to impaired spikelet meristems (SMs). Transcriptomics suggested activation of the jasmonic acid signaling pathway, potentially affecting spikelet cell elongation. Proteomics indicated disruption of the MAPK signaling pathway, likely affecting spikelet cell polarity. Metabolomics demonstrated deficiencies in the tricarboxylic acid cycle and phenylpropanoid synthesis pathway, which in turn alter meristem cells differentiation and cell wall remodeling. Multi-omics integration uncovered a regulatory network involving cell cycle initiation, jasmonic acid signaling, and metabolic flux homeostasis, and pinpointed several candidate genes for future functional characterization.ConclusionsOur study not only identifies potential molecular mechanisms underlying maize ear development but also pinpoints precise targets for genetic improvement. These findings deepen our understanding of inflorescence biology and provide a theoretical framework for optimizing yield-related traits, thereby offering actionable insights for the design of molecular breeding strategies to enhance maize productivity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07439-0.

Introduction

As a globally vital food crop, maize (Zea mays L.) exhibits grain yield predominantly constrained by its ear morphology, where structural parameters such as kernel row number directly set the yield ceiling [1]. Flattened ears (fasciation) in maize represent a prevalent morphological variation characterized by the transformation of the ear axis from circular to a flat, band-like shape. This variation is often coupled with irregular kernel arrangement and an abnormal increase in row numbers, potentially enhancing yield [2]. Therefore, elucidating the genetic regulatory mechanisms underlying the development of flattened ears and the theoretical basis of their morphological formation holds critical scientific and practical significance for the genetic improvement of maize yield traits.

The molecular mechanisms underlying maize ear flattening have been investigated, revealing roles for multiple genes and signaling pathways [3]. Key regulators include the FASCIATED EAR (FEA) gene family. Among these, FEA2 [4] (a CLV2-type LRR receptor-like kinase) inhibits meristem proliferation through the CLAVATA-WUSCHEL (CLV-WUS) negative-feedback loop; its disruption causes enlarged meristems, thickened ears, and disordered kernel rows [5]. Complementing this pathway, the FEA3 receptor perceives CLE peptides (e.g., ZmFCP1) secreted from differentiating organ primordia [6]. Recently identified FEA5 (phosphatidylcholine 2-acylhydrolase) integrates lipid signaling with auxin responses: FEA5 mutants exhibit broadened meristems, chaotic kernel patterning, and dysregulated hormone dynamics, revealing novel lipid-hormone crosstalk [7]. Parallel mechanisms include FEA4, which maintains meristem size through direct activation of KN1 ortholog ZmZMM2 [8]. Others, such as FASCIATED EAR1 (Fas1), have also been reported to be related to the morphology of panicle meristem [9]. Additionally, RAMOSA (RA) genes (e.g., RA3) link trehalose metabolism to branching architecture [10]. Other genes, such as THICK TASSEL DWARF1 (TD1) [11] mutations, cause flattened ears, thickened tassels, and increased tassel branching density; BARREN INFLORESCENCE1 (BIF1) [12] and BARREN INFLORESCENCE2 (BIF2) [13] mutations result in missing or abnormally branched male and female spikelets; Teopod 1 (TP1) [14] mutations manifest as increased tillering, narrow leaves, smaller ears, and reduced tassel branching; Tasselsheath4 (TSH4) [15] mutation causes cluster formation of bracts at the base of the tassel, elongation of the inflorescence, and elongation of the ear. Different genes affect multiple phenotypes, leading to abnormal development of maize ears. Nevertheless, the systemic regulatory network that drives normal ear growth and development and maintains regular kernel row arrangement is still unclear.

Therefore, this study took the naturally occurring mutant zmed3, characterized by flattened ear development, as the research object. By integrating transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs), differentially accumulated proteins (DAPs), and metabolites (DAMs) in the zmed3 mutant. By analyzing the key pathways and regulatory networks involved in these changes from multiple perspectives, identifying candidate genes regulating the abnormal ear development of zmed3, providing a theoretical basis for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying the formation of the zmed3 mutant phenotype, and offering precise targets for high-yield molecular breeding in maize.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The zmed3 mutant originated as a natural variant in maize inbred line Lx9801. Homozygous mutant plants were crossed with B73 pollen to generate F1 hybrids. These F1 plants were backcrossed as males to zmed3 females to establish the BC1F1 population. All materials were field grown at Yuanyang Experimental Station, Henan Agricultural University (35.11°N,113.95°E, 2024) using standard maize cultivation protocols.

Phenotypic observation

Immature maize ear at the 2-mm and 4-mm developmental stages were dissected. Morphological differences between these stages were initially assessed using stereomicroscopy. Subsequently, ears at the 4-mm stage were examined in greater detail using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). At maturity, BC₁F₁ ears were phenotyped for rachis architecture (spherical or flattened) with segregation ratios analyzed by χ2 test in Microsoft Excel.

Transcriptome

Total RNA was extracted from 4 mm developing ear samples of maize zmed3 and Lx9801 (three biological replicates per group) using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen). RNA quality (RIN), quantity (≥ 2 μg), and concentration (≥ 300 ng/μL) were assessed using an Agilent 2100 system, and genomic DNA contamination was checked via agarose gel electrophoresis. Libraries were constructed using the VAHTSTM Stranded mRNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme), including mRNA enrichment with Oligo dT beads, RNA fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, end repair, adapter ligation, PCR amplification, and circularization to generate single-stranded circular DNA libraries. Qualified libraries were sequenced on the MGI high-throughput platform, producing 150-bp paired-end reads with a total depth of 6 GB. Raw sequencing data (FASTQ) were filtered using SOAPnuke (v2.1.0) and bbduk to remove adapters, poly-A/poly-G tails, and low-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the maize B73 reference genome (v4) using STAR software (≤ 2 bp mismatches), and gene expression levels were quantified using FeatureCounts. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with the edgeR package (FDR ≤ 0.05, |log2FC|≥ 1). Log-transformed and centered expression data were analyzed by PCA using SIMCA software (v18.0.1).

Proteome

Fresh ears of Lx9801 and zmed3 were harvested at the transcriptome-corresponding developmental stage in biological triplicates (n = 3). Samples were ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized with L3 lysis buffer (1% SDS, 7 M urea, 2 M thiourea) using ice-cold ultrasonication for debris removal. Proteins were purified by cold acetone precipitation overnight, then redissolved in 8 M urea. After reduction with 5 mM DTT (37 °C, 45 min) and alkylation with 11 mM iodoacetamide (15 min, light-protected), tryptic digestion (Promega) was performed at 37 °C overnight. Peptides were desalted using a C18 column (Millipore) and quantified with Pierce™ peptide assay kits. Peptides were separated via a NanoElute UHPLC system with a 60-min gradient (2% to 80% B; 300 nL/min) using mobile phases 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B). Mass spectrometry was performed on a timsTOF Pro2 in ddaPASEF mode: positive ion detection (m/z 100–1700), ion mobility (1/K0: 0.7–1.4 Vs/cm2), quadrupole isolation width (2–3 Th), and collision energy scaled with ion mobility (20–59 eV). Raw data were processed using FragPipe (IonQuant module) for MaxLFQ label-free quantification. Filtering criteria included removal of non-specific peptides and contaminants, and retention of proteins with ≥ 1 unique peptide. Differentially accumulated proteins (DAPs) were identified with the edgeR package (|log2FC|≥ 1, FDR ≤ 0.05). The expression matrix was log-transformed and centralized using SIMCA software, followed by PCA. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA; R package) was used to construct co-expression modules (β = 12, minModuleSize = 30). Protein–protein interactions (PPI) were analyzed using the STRING database (confidence score > 400), and functional annotations were derived through BLAST alignment against the maize B73 genome (v4).

Metabolome

Fresh ears of Lx9801 and zmed3 were harvested at the transcriptome-corresponding developmental stage in biological triplicates (n = 3). Metabolite extraction and analysis used a tissue sample (about 100 mg) ground in liquid nitrogen. After extraction and centrifugation with 80% methanol aqueous solution, the supernatant was diluted to 53% methanol concentration and centrifuged for the second time. The supernatant was subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis by a Shimadzu UFLC HPLC system combined with an Applied Biosystems 4500 QTRAP mass spectrometer (positive ion mode, controlled by Analyst 1.6 software). The identification of metabolites follows the Level 1 standard of Metabonomics Standards Initiative (MSI), and is confirmed by two orthogonal parameters: the accurate mass error is controlled to be less than 5 ppm, and the retention time of reference standard (± 0.2 min) or MS/MS fragment spectrum of the same system analysis is matched, and MSn fragment spectrum is provided for isomer/isobaric substance for structural analysis; Blank samples and dilution studies were used to eliminate pollutants, the original data, processing parameters and database (mzCloud, mzVault, Masslist) search criteria were ensured to be transparent, and new metabolites were stored in public databases such as MetaboLights as required. The quantitative analysis was normalized by QC samples (compounds with CV > 30% were eliminated), combined with mass deviation of 5 ppm, signal intensity deviation of 30% and minimum intensity threshold filtering, and the molecular formula was predicted by molecular ions and fragment ions and matched with the database.

In the data analysis stage, the outliers are filtered by IQR, and the peak area data with a missing value ≤ 50% is retained, and the missing value is filled with the half minimum value. After normalization by total ion current (TIC), SIMCA software is used for logarithmic transformation and centralized pretreatment, and PCA is carried out in turn to explore the data structure. Through cross-validation, permutation test, and an external validation set, we avoided over-fitting, and determined the differential metabolites by VIP score (> 1.0) combined with Benjamini–Hochberg corrected P value (FDR ≤ 0.05). GO and KEGG enrichment analysis uses agriGO v2.0 and KEGG tools, takes FDR ≤ 0.05 as the threshold, constructs Venn diagram and PCA through Biocloud platform, realizes the visualization of enrichment bubble diagram and hierarchical clustering thermogram with the help of online tools, Morpheus and TBtools, and analyzes the correlation of metabolic pathways in combination with biological background.

GO and KEGG analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted on the identified Differentially Abundant Proteins (DAPs), DEGs, and Differential Metabolites (DAMs) using agriGO v2.0 (http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/agriGOv2/index.php) and KEGG (https://www.kegg.jp/) software [16]. Analyses were categorized into Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC), with significance defined by FDR ≤ 0.05. Venn diagrams and PCA were generated using the Bemis cloud platform (www.biocloud.net), and enrichment bubble plots were created using online software (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn). Clustering heatmaps were visualized with Morpheus (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus) and TBtools (https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools) [17].

Multi-omic data integration

A nine-quadrant diagram (|log2FC|≥ 1) was created to visualize gene expression changes in the transcriptome and proteome of Lx9801 and zmed3 based on joint proteomics and metabolomics analysis using R (v3.5.1) software. KEGG enrichment analysis of multi-group data was performed using the Omicshare website, and a Venn diagram was generated to identify common KEGG pathways. Genes identified in both the proteome and transcriptome were co-located based on significantly enriched KEGG pathways.

RT-qPCR

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed using a SYBR Green I-based assay. Reactions were prepared with the ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Cat# Q312). The UBQ (UBIQUITIN) gene served as the reference gene, amplified using the specific primer pair TTCTTTGAACGCGTCCTCAC (forward) and AACAGTGACGAAGGCACAAC (reverse). Amplification and real-time fluorescence detection were conducted on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System. Each experiment included three biological replicates, with three technical replicates per biological sample. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial 95 °C denaturation step for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 58 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 15 s. A dissociation curve analysis was subsequently performed by gradually increasing temperature from 60 °C to 95 °C with continuous fluorescence acquisition. Gene expression quantification was monitored via fluorescence thresholds, with relative expression levels calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Results

Phenotypic analysis

A natural mutant, zmed3, was identified in the elite inbred line Lx9801. Comparative microscopic analysis identified developmental anomalies in the zmed3 female inflorescence relative to the wild type. Upon reaching a gynoecium length of 2 mm, the inflorescence meristem (IM) of zmed3 exhibited significant degeneration and enlargement (Fig. 1A). As the gynoecium extended to 4 mm in length, the floral meristem (FM) progressively displayed flattening and structural disorganization (Fig. 1B-C). This mutant phenotype exhibited stability throughout the reproductive growth stage (Fig. 1D). Genetic crosses of zmed3 to the standard inbred line B73 yielded F1 progeny exhibiting normal female spike development, thereby confirming recessive inheritance. Backcrosses of F1 plants produced 2354 plants with the normal phenotype and 2290 displaying the zmed3 mutant phenotype, a segregation ratio (1:1) consistent with a recessive monogenic trait (χ2 = 0.8820 < 3.841, p = 0.05; Fig. 1E). Throughout all base positions, sequencing quality scores (Q-values) were consistently within acceptable limits (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). Base composition analysis confirmed near-expected A:T and C:G ratios, indicating high-quality sequencing data (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation between Lx9801 and zmed3 samples (Supplementary Fig. 1 C). Inter-group correlation analysis revealed greater intra-genotype similarity compared to inter-genotype similarity, thereby supporting the reliability of the data for downstream analysis (Fig. 2A). We identified 1,589 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), comprising 1,026 up-regulated and 563 down-regulated genes (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 1). Proteomic analysis

To investigate proteome remodeling during ear development, quantitative LC–MS/MS proteomics was performed in both zmed3 and Lx9801 at the 4-mm gynoecium stage. High-resolution mass spectrometry identified 101,684 peptides corresponding to 11,157 proteins, with 9,028 proteins robustly quantified across both lines (Supplementary Fig. 2 A). Analysis of peptide-protein relationships revealed an inverse correlation between unique peptide count per protein and protein identification frequency, confirming stringent identification of high-abundance proteins (Supplementary Fig. 2B). PCA demonstrated clear separation between Lx9801 and zmed3 proteomes, reflecting genotype-specific abundance patterns (Fig. 3A). Intra-group correlation analysis showed exceptional reproducibility (Pearson's r > 0.95 all replicates), while marked inter-group divergence confirmed suitability for comparative analysis (Fig. 3B). Applying stringent cutoffs, we identified 181 differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) which include 45 up-regulated and 136 down-regulated in zmed3 (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Table 2). Metabolomic analysis

To investigate metabolite dynamics during ear development, we performed metabolomic analysis of zmed3 and Lx9801 at 4 mm ear length stage. Following rigorous quality control filtering, 22,839 metabolites were retained, including 256 secondary metabolites (Supplementary Fig. 3 A). PCA demonstrated clear separation between Lx9801 and zmed3 samples (Fig. 5A), indicating significant differences in their metabolic profiles. Volcano plot identified 60 significantly upregulated and 62 downregulated metabolites (FDR < 0.05, Fig. 5B). Hierarchical clustering heatmap analysis further revealed distinct patterns of upregulated and downregulated metabolites, emphasizing the distinct metabolomic profiles between the two genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 3B). These shifts underscore metabolic adjustments during female ear development, which is a critical adaptive mechanism for enhancing fitness in response to developmental stresses. KEGG metabolomic pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to amino acid metabolism (including aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and various amino acid biosynthetic pathways), small molecule metabolism (e.g., coenzyme A biosynthesis), ABC transporters, and integrated metabolism (Fig. 5C). Integrated multi-omics analysis

To dissect the hereditary basis of defective ear development in maize, we performed integrated transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic profiling of the zmed3 mutant characterized by flattened ear architecture and irregular inflorescence patterning. Initial Nine-Quadrant analysis demonstrated pervasive discordance between transcriptional and translational regulation, with 48 genes exhibiting up-regulated transcription but down-regulated protein expression, and 173 genes showing inverse down-regulated transcription/up-regulated protein profiles. Conversely, 366 and 28 gene-protein pairs displayed coordinated up- or down-regulation, respectively (Fig. 6A), indicating synchronous genomic-proteomic control in a subset of developmental regulators. Further KEGG analysis revealed that major metabolic pathways, including glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, galactose metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, tyrosine metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and cyanogenic amino acid metabolism, play significant roles in the panicle morphological development of zmed3 (Fig. 6B). Candidate gene mining

In the co-analysis, the Zm00001d041772, Zm00001d002258, Zm00001d043607, Zm00001d043348 and Zm00001d042353 genes were up-regulated or down-regulated in the KEGG pathway (Table 1). To verify these findings, we conducted RT-qPCR tests on the roots, stems and leaves of Lx9801 and zmed3 female spikes at the 4 mm developmental stage (Supplementary Table 3). Taking Lx9801 as the control group for each tissue type, the results showed that Zm00001d042353 was significantly upregulated in both the leaves and female flower spikes of zmed3. (Fig. 6C, Table 1). Discussion

Phenotypic characteristics of zmed3 mutant across developmental stages

The zmed3 mutant, derived as a natural recessive mutation in the elite inbred line Lx9801, exhibits temporally regulated developmental anomalies. At the 2-mm stage of ear development, morphological alterations in the ear become apparent. By the 4-mm stage, these progress to pronounced flattening and structural disorganization, ultimately resulting in mature ears with flattened apices and disordered grain arrangement. This phenotype shows similarities to known maize mutants affecting ear development, particularly those disrupting meristem maintenance and pattern formation. For instance, the FEA4 mutant displays thickened ears and disrupted grain row patterning due to abnormal regulation of inflorescence meristem size [14], while the COMPACT PLANT2 (CT2) mutant ear is flattened, and the male spikelet is increased [18]. These parallels suggest that zmed3 may function by modulating key regulatory pathways during developmental transitions.

Multi-omics analysis uncovers the molecular regulatory network of zmed3 mutant

Transcriptomic profiling revealed significant alterations in jasmonic acid (JA) signaling and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades. Notably, the tasselseed1 (ts1) mutant demonstrates that reduced endogenous JA levels impair stamen development, a defect reversible upon exogenous JA application [11]. JA and its derivatives critically influence floral organ fate determination in maize, with elevated JA levels promoting pistil abortion [19]. Furthermore, the MKK3-MPK6 cascade, activated by JA in Arabidopsis, exerts negative control over ATMYC2/JIN1 expression, as evidenced by genetic analyses using gain- and loss-of-function mutants [20]. Collectively, these findings underscore the centrality of JA in mediating zmed3-dependent ear morphogenesis. Additionally, dysregulation of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (marked by elevated ferulic acid and reduced sinapic acid) and glutathione metabolism indicate metabolic and energetic imbalances in the mutant. These integrated observations emphasize the critical role of metabolic homeostasis and its complex interplay with signaling pathways in maize ear architecture.

Functional characterization of the candidate gene Zm00001d042353 and its regulatory potential

The candidate gene Zm00001d042353 encodes sucrose-phosphate synthase, which governs sucrose availability during plant growth and fiber elongation. In Arabidopsis, its homolog contributes to cell wall biosynthesis, and loss-of-function mutants exhibit impaired secondary wall synthesis in seed epidermal cells, notably reducing radial wall height and disrupting structural integrity. This further induces aberrant cell morphologies, including decreased cell area, irregular shapes (compromised hexagonal morphology), and broader angular variations, with blurred boundaries and fusion phenotypes between adjacent cells [16]. These data strongly suggest that misregulation of this gene compromises cell wall architecture in the developing ear, ultimately driving the morphogenetic defects observed in zmed3.
