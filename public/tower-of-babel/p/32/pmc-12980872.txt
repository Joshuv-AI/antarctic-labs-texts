Reactive carbonyl species in health and chronic disease: from methylglyoxal to an integrative network of metabolic regulation
Authors: Shu Li, Peter Paul Nawroth, Jens Kroll
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prevalence of chronic diseases is increasing dramatically, but the metabolism, particularly the pervasive carbonyl stress that accompanies many of these conditions, is rarely considered a potential cause. Reactive carbonyl species are spontaneously generated through a variety of endogenous metabolic reactions, and contain highly reactive carbonyl groups. Methylglyoxal, a prime reactive carbonyl, has been linked to cardiovascular disease, diabetes and its complications, obesity, chronic kidney disease, and ageing. Its detoxification is mainly regulated by the glyoxalase system; however, surprisingly, studies in mice, zebrafish and drosophila with a knockout of glyoxalase 1 showed viable animals with only minor metabolic phenotypes. Importantly, compensatory mechanisms for other potential methylglyoxal-detoxifying enzymes, including aldehyde dehydrogenases and aldo reductases, were identified in glyoxalase 1 knockout animals. Subsequent knockout studies of different Aldehyde-Dehydrogenases and Aldo-Keto-Reductases have demonstrated that Glyoxalase 1 does not solely regulate the metabolism of reactive carbonyl species and organ functions. Instead, other reactive carbonyl species, together with their corresponding detoxification enzymes, exhibit distinct organ susceptibility. These detoxifying enzyme systems are interconnected at multiple levels in a complex and redundant manner, and their dysregulation can lead to chronic pathological conditions. Conceptually, the review aims to focus on future cardiovascular research investigating the specificity of different reactive carbonyl species to their respective detoxification systems and the interplay and organ-specific regulation of these detoxification pathways. The future goal is to develop reactive carbonyl species profiles and markers of inadequate detoxification in order to identify new patient subgroups. Another future challenge will be to establish reactive carbonyl species profiles and corresponding enzyme system activities as biomarkers for predicting, diagnosing and monitoring chronic diseases in translational and clinical contexts. Ultimately, we suggest to develop potent and specific reactive carbonyl species scavengers, as well as detoxifying enzyme activators, and define new patient subgroups with different treatment needs and prognoses.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Over the past decades, the prevalence of chronic diseases has increased dramatically, which impose substantial health and economic burdens on both individuals and society, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes and its complications, chronic kidney disease, and age-related systemic functional decline [1–4]. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023 (GBD 2023) reports, modifiable risk factors such as high blood pressure, smoking, high blood sugar, and obesity/overweight (high body mass index) account for approximately half of the global mortality and morbidity [5]. Although current clinical management spans antihypertensive, weight loss and glucose-lowering therapy strategies, few interventions can truly stop disease progression or reverse tissue damage [6–8].

A major limitation in our understanding of many prevalent Western diseases is evident from the fact that these diseases are believed to be defined by a single or only a few disease mediators, which are mostly measured as risk predictors in serum. However, clinical experience demonstrates that there must be many unknown subtypes, since some patients succumb quickly to the disease while others are resistant. Some respond well to therapy, while others do not. All of the most common causes of death and loss of quality of life in the Western world are diseases in which metabolic changes have been observed. However, the inability to precisely predict the course of the disease and treatment response in each patient indicates that commonly diagnosed and treated risk factors only partially address the cause of the disease.

This conceptual review follows the hypothesis that in order to understand the metabolic de-arrangements underlying changes in vascular dysfunction, altered lipid and glucose metabolism, and insulin resistance, a deeper investigation of the molecular abnormalities below these classical risk predictors is required. One approach is to study the small molecules generated as byproducts during energy flux. These encompass reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive carbonyl species (RCS).

Growing mechanistic evidence indicates that RCS act as key metabolic intermediates linking nutrient overload, oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular dysfunction to various chronic disorders [9–11]. They not only drive multiple pathological processes but may also contribute to heterogeneity in therapeutic responses [12]. For instance, pharmacometabolomic studies in early-phase Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients have demonstrated that baseline urine metabolite profiles, including citric acid, myoinositol, and hippuric acid, can stratify patients into metformin responders and non-responders [13]. Similarly, a pilot trial reported that high-dose pyridoxamine add-on treatment may benefit a subtype of schizophrenia with enhanced carbonyl stress [12]. Despite clear evidence that metabolic characteristics vary between diseases and influence how they respond to treatment, RCS are not adequately incorporated into current conceptual and therapeutic frameworks for chronic diseases. This gap largely reflects persistent methodological challenges, including the reliable quantification of the diverse spectrum of RCS, the instability of RCS and the limited availability of interventions whose effects can be attributed predominantly to RCS reduction rather than to concurrent or alternative mechanisms. As a result, a coherent, cross-disease perspective is still lacking.

The aim of this conceptual review is to integrate current knowledge on the generation, detoxification and accumulation of reactive metabolite species, from methylglyoxal (MG) to other RCS, including acrolein (ACR), malondialdehyde (MDA), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), acetaldehyde (AA), trans,trans-2,4-decadienal (tt-DDE) and 2-hexadecenal (2-HD), in order to evaluate their interconnected and disease-specific roles in major chronic disorders. By establishing a cross-disease conceptual framework centered on carbonyl stress, we will highlight unresolved scientific questions and outline what is needed to enable more precise and effective future interventions.

The definition of RCS and its function in chronic diseases

RCS are a large group of biological compounds containing highly reactive carbonyl groups [14], and are generated through a variety of endogenous metabolic reactions as well as from exogenous sources [15]. Endogenous production encompasses a combination of nonenzymatic reactions, such as lipid peroxidation, amino acid oxidation, and glycation, as well as enzymatic pathways, most prominently the polyol pathway and glycolysis. Figure 1 displays the chemical structure of the RCS most commonly found in biological samples, which was obtained from the PubChem Compound database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Exogeneous sources of RCS exposure include industrial pollutants, automobile exhaust gas, cigarette smoke, organic pharmaceutical chemistry and food processing [14, 16, 17]. RCS are electrophilic which make them react with the nucleophilic groups in macromolecules such as proteins, nucleic acids, and aminophospholipids. Higher concentrations of RCS lead to the irreversible modification and formation of multiple adducts and cross-links, collectively named advanced glycation end products (AGEs) and advanced lipoxidation end products (ALEs). At lower concentrations, RCS promotes reversible modification of nucleophilic macromolecules, stimulates proliferation in aortic smooth muscle cells and K562 cells, and protects against bacterial, viral, and fungal infections, as well as oxidative stresses [14, 15, 18, 19]. The most common AGEs are methylglyoxal-derived hydroimidazolone 1 (MG-H1), N(ε)-carboxyethyl-lysine (CEL), N(ε)-carboxymethyl-lysine (CML) and argpyrimidine (AP). The most prevalent ALEs are 4-HNE- and MDA-adducted proteins. These AGEs and ALEs can impair protein structure and function [20].Fig. 1Illustration of structure of classical RCS

RCS and the resulting ALEs/AGEs have been implicated in multiple chronic diseases, with excessive RCS accumulation emerging as a hallmark of cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome (MS), T2DM, and neurodegenerative diseases. RCS-driven protein and lipid modifications play a central role in cardiovascular disease by promoting endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and atherosclerotic plaque progression [21–23]. In MS and T2DM, elevated MG, ACR and 4-HNE contribute to insulin resistance, β-cell dysfunction, and vascular complications [23–27]. In the kidney, carbonyl stress correlates with mesangial expansion and glomerulosclerosis, contributing to CKD [28]. Moreover, in neurodegenerative diseases including Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease, carbonyl-mediated protein aggregation and mitochondrial dysfunction are recognized contributors to neuronal loss [21, 29]. In addition, activation of RAGE, the receptor for AGE, further amplifies cell signaling transduction pathways by triggering various signaling pathways and leading to cellular dysfunction [14, 15]. Importantly, research has shown the organ-specific susceptibility to RCS, suggesting the carbonyl damage markers and detoxification capacity for RCS differ across tissues [30, 31]. Therefore, suppressing RCS formation, enhancing their detoxification, or blocking downstream signaling is essential to eliminate the organ-specific carbonyl stress and preserve cellular function.

One major strategy for reducing RCS involves the direct trapping of RCS using nucleophilic scavengers such as aminoguanidine and pyridoxamine, penicillamine, anserine and hydrazine-based compounds to form stable adducts [32, 33]. Another major pathway involves enhancing endogenous enzymatic detoxification, including Glyoxalase 1 and 2, Aldehyde-Dehydrogenases (ALDHs), and Aldo-Keto-Reductases (AKRs) [34, 35]. Antioxidants and metal chelators can suppress glycoxidation, glucose autoxidation, and lipid peroxidation, thereby reducing the formation of RCS precursors [36]. Crosslink breakers which could remove the already formed AGE cross-links, such as alagebrium (ALT-711), are able to help reverse tissue stiffness and vascular dysfunction in experimental diabetes [23, 32]. Finally, blocking RAGE signaling using soluble RAGE (sRAGE) or small-molecule RAGE antagonists interrupts downstream inflammatory cascades [15, 35], and recent article in experimental diabetes highlighted the importance of estradiol in reducing the reactive metabolite levels and protecting the diabetic retina [37]. Moreover, Together, these strategies could alleviate carbonyl stress, when one RCS species is measured alone, but failed in the complex system of a metabolic disease. This points to a yet incomplete understood network regulating RCS on the level of production and detoxification.

Methylglyoxal and its degrading enzyme glyoxalase as a prime candidate of RCS

The classical view

MG is the most important and highly reactive α- oxoaldehyde compound that is generated in various groups of organisms, from plants to human [38, 39]. The main endogenous source of MG in mammalian cells is its formation as a by-product of glycolysis and the autoxidation of glucose. Other endogenous sources of MG are catabolism of threonine and acetone, lipid peroxidation and degradation of glycated proteins [40]. MG is also present exogenously, with particularly high concentrations found in Manuka honey [41]. Under physiological conditions, MG concentrations are typically low, ranging from 50–150 nM in human plasma and approximately 1–4 μM in mammalian and plant cells [42]. When metabolic flux is increased or detoxification capacity is impaired, the MG levels rise substantially [24]. Increased levels of MG exert profound biological effects due to its electrophilic capacity to modify proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. Protein glycation by MG preferentially targets arginine and lysine residues, leading to the formation of MG-H1and N(ε)-carboxyethyl-lysine (CEL) that represent the most abundant AGEs in vivo [43–45]. Prominent targets include abundant and long-lived proteins such as albumin, hemoglobin, collagen, and crystallins, thereby affecting protein structure and function across tissues [38, 45–49]. Glycation-derived free adducts generated during protein turnover are primarily eliminated via renal excretion [45, 50]. Accumulating evidence implicates MG and MG-derived AGEs as contributors to the pathogenesis of multiple chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease, obesity, diabetes and its complications, neurodegenerative disorders, chronic kidney diseases, aging, immune dysregulation, and carcinogenesis [38, 42, 45, 50, 51]. Similarly, MG was described as an independent marker associated with the severity and progression of sepsis and its ability to induce capillary leakage by disrupting proteins that form the endothelial barrier in vitro [33].

Notably, the effect of MG displays dose-dependent duality known as hormesis [38, 42, 43, 52, 53]. Low doses of MG are beneficial for maintaining cellular viability, enhancing stress adaptation, slowing aging, and preventing from cellular damage [43, 53]. They may also explain the potential benefits of Manuka honey [54]. In contrast, high concentrations of MG cause significant cytotoxicity, trigger inflammation, disturbs DNA repair and mitochondrial function, impair proteostasis, and ultimately promote cellular dysfunction or cell death [38, 44, 55, 56]. The dual role of MG highlights the need for further research and a more critical evaluation of the existing literature. All too often, an elevation is interpreted as the cause of a disease despite a lack of data proving causality. This one-sided view of MG and its actions has generated an oversimplified picture of RCS biology. This is evident in cancer research, where MG may either facilitate tumor growth and progression or enhance the sensitivity of cancer cells to treatment regimens, depending on the metabolic context and concentration [38, 42, 44, 52].

Increased levels of MG are predominantly detoxified by the glyoxalase system, which consist of glyoxalase 1 (GLO1) and glyoxalase 2 (GLO2). Together, these two enzymes convert MG into its end product D-lactate, via the formation of the intermediate hemithioacetal (HTA) and S-D-lactoylglutathione (SDL) [24]. The glyoxalase system was first discovered and described in 1913 by Dakin and Dudley [57]. Subsequently, extensive research has investigated its involvement in chronic diseases, diabetes, vascular disease and related disorders, and found that the GLO1 activity is significantly reduced in diabetic and obese mice [58–60]. In addition, overexpression of GLO1 in diabetic rats has been shown to reduce the accumulation of AGEs and oxidative stress [61, 62]. While this fits to the current hypothesis of MG as a driver of metabolic disease, the elevation is surprisingly little compared to the striking reduction of GLO1 activity, pointing to other detoxification mechanisms.

Lessons from glo1 knockout studies

Initially, it was believed that GLO1 knockout (KO) mice could not survive due to the expected high level of MG accumulation, but this had not been proven experimentally [63]. Nevertheless, GLO1 KO animals have been successfully generated and are viable in mice [64, 65], zebrafish [66] and drosophila [67]. Specifically, one GLO1 KO mouse model was viable without a pronounced phenotypic defect, displaying slightly increased MG-H1 levels in liver, reduced anxiety-like behavior and increased depression-like behavior [64]. Another GLO1 KO mouse model was also viable, but failed to show elevated MG or MG-H1 levels under hyperglycemic conditions [65]; drosophila GLO1 KO accumulated MG levels, and became obese, insulin resistant and hyperglycaemic [67]; and GLO1 KO zebrafish accumulated moderate MG levels, developed normal glucose metabolism but became insulin resistant when stressed with high-calorie intake (Table 1) [66]. Organ alterations linked to reactive carbonyl species and their detoxifying enzyme systems

We and others have identified compensatory mechanisms for other potential MG-detoxifying enzymes, including ALDHs and AKRs, which were identified in GLO1 KO mice and GLO1 KO zebrafish, but not in GLO1 KO drosophila [65–67]. These findings support the hypothesis that higher-developed organisms have evolved to compensate for a potential loss of GLO1 function, thereby highlighting the key role of GLO1 in MG detoxification and health. The consequences may be far-reaching. Since each detoxifying enzyme is distributed differently throughout the body, these signatures may indicate organ dysfunction, which may be more or less relevant to some patients. This is important for diseases such as atherosclerosis and diabetes, which, according to current understanding, affect the whole body. However, it is well known that some patients develop diabetic retinopathy first, while others develop nephropathy. The same is true for atherosclerosis, since some patients develop peripheral atherosclerosis more prominently than others, while others develop central or cardiac atherosclerosis. The data also suggests that MG and other RCS, along with their detoxifying enzyme systems, are interconnected. Subsequently, this would require to analyze the RCS profiles and their detoxifying enzymes in chronic diseases in animal models, and the complex analysis and intervention must also be considered in clinical applications. Therefore, identifying the interconnected regulatory network of different RCS is mandatory for future studies, and the first published data on this topic will be discussed later [68]. In addition to global deletion models, partial GLO1 knockdown models in rodents and C. elegans demonstrated that moderate reductions in GLO1 activity are sufficient to increase MG-derived AGEs levels, promote glucose intolerance, nephropathy and lifespan shortening [69–71]. Genetic studies in humans have helped to detect GLO1 polymorphisms associated with various diseases, including diabetes, diabetic vascular complications and schizophrenia [72–75].

Pathways affecting the activity of glyoxalase

The identification of upstream regulators of GLO1 is also an emerging area of research because different levels of GLO1 activity can have a significant impact on effective MG concentrations. Post-translational modifications play a key role in modulating GLO1 function. Phosphorylation of Thr107 mediated by calmodulin-dependent kinase II delta (CaMKIIδ), and Tyr136 mediated by glucose-responsive kinases alter the KM and Vmax of GLO1, protein content and thus cellular MG-detoxification capacity, integrating GLO1 activity into the regulation of diabetes, aging and cancer [76, 77]. Additionally, S-nitrosylation of GLO1 in cooperation with glutathione (GSH) have been shown to decrease enzymatic activity [78, 79]. Building on this mechanistic insights, pharmacological strategies have targeted both MG itself and the glyoxalase system in diabetes and related metabolic diseases. MG scavengers include aminoguanidine, pyridoxine, curcumin, metformin, N-acetylcysteine (NAC), anserine, or L-carnosine [33, 38, 80, 81]. In parallel, GLO1 inducers such as the trans-resveratrol/hesperetin combination have been explored experimentally and clinically, where GLO1 induction improved mildly glycemic control, insulin resistance and vascular function in overweight and obese subjects [44, 82]. However, the clinical relevance of these findings remains yet to be proven.

How interconnected the network of detoxification enzymes acts and how little is currently known about their functions has been revealed by in vivo studies using GLO2-deficient mice and zebrafish. The studies revealed organismal viability and normal redox homeostasis under baseline conditions, yet identified previously unexpected roles of GLO2 in regulating tissue-specific glucose metabolism [83, 84].

Limitations of the common view on detoxification

First of all, many of the studies referring to an association of MG or MG-H1 levels with diseases are not only correlative, but in addition the transient changes seen are not at all in the range needed to induce in vitro and in vivo any biological effects. Many in vitro studies using excessively high concentrations of MG have identified several types of cellular damage induced by MG [85, 86]. However, these concentrations almost never occur in chronic diseases. Therefore it is not surprising, that GLO1 KO studies and several clinical studies in patients with diabetes and prediabetes or even ketoacidosis have mostly failed to repeat the effects seen in vitro and to identify markedly increased and persistent MG concentrations, as well as pronounced, causative alterations in organ functions in vivo [64–67, 84, 87, 88]. Consistently, long-term feeding of mice with MG was reported to be not toxic and did not induce renal diseases and diabetes [89]. Furthermore, studies aimed at reducing MG only in animal models and patients have had limited success [90, 91], suggesting that inhibiting MG alone is not a sufficient therapeutic strategy. This observation fits well to the concept of a yet only partly understood network of several enzymes which are in different organs in different ways responsible to control the balance of various RCS. Together, these observations led to the development of the 'two/multiple-hit hypothesis' of organ complications in chronic diseases. This hypothesis proposes that organ damage only occurs when at least two or more of the following factors are altered (Fig. 2): altered RCS profiles, dysregulated expression and activities of detoxification enzymes, and nutritional, environmental, inflammatory and genetic factors and aging [66]. For example, GLO1 KO zebrafish exhibited a modest 30% increase in MG concentrations yet remained metabolically and phenotypically normal. However, excessive food consumption led to insulin resistance, hyperglycemia and activated retinal angiogenesis in the mutants [66]. In this example, the MG increase represents the first hit, while the high-calorie intake represents the second hit. Multiple RCS as regulators of health and chronic diseases

Based on GLO1 KO studies performed in mice and zebrafish, which were viable and mostly normal, additional enzymes potentially involved in MG detoxification have been identified, most notably ALDHs and AKRs [64–67]. Single KO studies targeting individual ALDH and AKR members have revealed distinct RCS as the preferred substrates of specific enzymes. However, MG levels remained normal in these single ALDH and AKR mutants [26, 27, 92, 93]. Thus, the limited view on MG only has misled the field. The novel view presented in this review opens not only towards the multiple number of enzymes involved and their cross reactivity, but also to a much wider range of different RCS. Acrolein: biogenesis, molecular reactivity, pathological outcome, and detoxification mechanisms

ACR is a widely recognized α,β-unsaturated aldehyde and one of the most toxic lipid peroxidation–derived aldehydes [94]. Endogenously, ACR is generated primarily during lipid peroxidation, but also from polyamine metabolism and myeloperoxidase-mediated reactions under conditions of oxidative stress, inflammation and hyperglycemia. Exogenously, ACR exposure derives from cigarette smoke, heated cooking oils, chemotherapeutic drugs, air pollution, and other environmental sources [95, 96]. The strong electrophilicity of ACR enables it to react with nucleophilic residues, such as amino acid, nucleic acids, and phospholipids, leading to the formation of ACR-derived ALEs [96–98]. It has been identified that such covalent modifications disrupt the function of these biomacromolecules and result in apoptosis and mutations [99, 100]. The most extensively characterized ALEs is Nε-(3-formyl-3,4-dehydropiperidino)lysine (FDP-lysine), and the other adducts include propano-arginine derivatives, histidine–ACR adducts, and cysteine–ACR conjugates [96, 101]. Various studies have indicated that the accumulation of ACR contributes to the onset and progression of cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, pathological angiogenesis, and neurodegenerative diseases, largely through ACR-derived ALEs formation, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammatory activation, endothelial dysfunction and neuronal damge [26, 102–104].

Multiple enzymatic systems participate in the cellular defense against ACR [26, 105, 106]. AKRs and ALDHs effectively detoxify the ACR. The AKR family members, such as AKR1A1A, AKR7A3, AKR7A5, AKR1B3, reduce ACR to less reactive alcohol derivatives [26, 105, 107, 108]. ALDH family members, such as ALDH3A1, ALDH1A1, ALDH2, catalyze the oxidation of ACR and its conjugates [97, 109–113]. Additional antioxidant defensive systems, including glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), ceruloplasmin, hemoxygenase and GSH, further contribute to protecting against cellular damage [34, 114]. Beyond endogenous enzymes, a growing number of small molecules scavengers have been applied in conditions such as diabetes, diabetic retinopathy, Parkinson’s diseases, and spinal cord contusive injury, which include 2-hydrazino-4,6-dimethylpyrimidine (2‑HDP), L-carnosine, hydralazine (HDZ), dimercaprol, pyridoxamine, and phenelzine [103, 114–118]. Strategies that enhance the body's natural detoxification processes are also being investigated. These include the pharmacological activation of ALDH2 by ALDA-1, and stimulation of ALDH3A1 by ALDA-341 and ALDH-89 [34]. In addition, constitutive androstane receptor (CAR) activators and pregnane X receptor ligands have been shown to markedly upregulate of ALDH and AKR gene expression in tissues of experimental rodent models [114, 119]. Finally, the utilization of gene therapy may also provide an effective approach of enhancing ALDH and AKR expression and activity in the diabetic retina owing to the development of adeno-associated viruses vectors in the eye [120, 121].

Altogether, ACR is one of the most reactive lipid peroxidation–derived aldehydes and exerts profound toxic effects across multiple cell types and organs. Other lipid peroxidation-derived aldehydes, such as 4-HNE, MDA, also substantially contribute to carbonyl stress and therefore require highly consideration.

4-Hydroxynonenal: biogenesis, molecular reactivity, pathological outcome, and detoxification mechanisms

4-HNE is the other highly reactive α,β-unsaturated aldehyde generated predominantly during the peroxidation of ω-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids, and is widely regarded as a secondary lipid peroxidation products [94]. It functions both as a marker of oxidative stress and a mediator of cellular dysfunction through the formation of stable adducts with proteins, DNA and lipids [122, 123]. Physiological concentrations of 4-HNE in human blood and serum was estimated to be about 0.05–0.15 uM [124, 125], whereas pathological conditions can elevate its levels significantly to more than 100 uM [126]. High levels of 4-HNE correlate with a large number of pathological diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative diseases, metabolic syndrome and cancers [27, 127–129]. Although produced in many tissues, 4-HNE accumulation is particularly detrimental to pancreatic β cells, which possess limited antioxidant capacity and relatively low expression of several detoxifying enzymes. Consequently, excessive 4-HNE promotes β-cell dysfunction and loss, impairs insulin secretion and may contribute to the development of diabetes [130, 131].

To counteract 4-HNE toxicity, organisms rely on several detoxification pathways mediated by ALDHs, AKRs and GSTs [127]. ALDH3A1 has been identified as a major 4-HNE-detoxifying enzyme in zebrafish [27], while other isoforms, such as ALDH1A1, ALDH2, and ALDH5A, also contribute to its oxidation and clearance [132–136]. Members of the AKRs family (AKR1A1, AKR1B1, AKR1B10, AKR1C3) and GSTs family (GSTA4, GSTP1) likewise play prominent roles in reducing or conjugating 4-HNE [137–141], thereby mitigating its cytotoxic and mutagenic effects. However, β cells have relatively low expression of some of these enzymes, which may partially explain their vulnerability to carbonyl stress [130]. In addition to enzymatic pathways, several endogenous and exogenous small molecules, including L-carnosine, L-glutamine, 2,4-dinitrophenyhydrazine and hydralazine, can directly scavenge 4-HNE [27, 142, 143]. Moreover, ALDH activator such as ALDA-341 enhance endogenous 4-HNE detoxification [144].

Malondialdehyde: biogenesis, molecular reactivity, pathological outcome, and detoxification mechanisms

MDA is an end product of lipid peroxidation and a marker of oxidative stress, with increased levels frequently observed during ferroptosis [145, 146], and it also exhibits mutagenic and toxic properties [147, 148]. In addition to non-enzymatic lipid peroxidation, MDA is generated as a side product during the biosynthesis of thromboxane A2 [149]. As a major precursor of ALEs, MDA interacts with nucleophilic residues in protein, lipids and nucleic acids, generating various MDA–derived adducts that contribute to carbonyl stress, inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately damaging cells and organs [150]. MDA is most toxic to cardiomyocytes, vascular endothelial cells, neurons and hepatocytes cells, primarily through the formation of pathogenic MDA–LDL adducts and the mutagenic DNA lesion M1dG (3-(2-deoxy-β-d-erythro-pentafuranosyl)pyrimido[1,2-α]purin-10(3H)-one deoxyguanosine) [151–154]. In the context of endothelial (dys)function, MDA is also linked to injury of the glycocalyx and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), which further leads to atherogenesis [155, 156].

In contrast to 4-HNE, the enzymatic detoxification of MDA is mediated mainly by AKRs, particularly AKR7A5 [157]. In line with this, MDA has been reported to induce aldehyde reductase expression and activity, suggesting the adaptive protective role of ALR under carbonyl stress [158]. Beyond enzymatic defenses, pharmacological and antioxidant strategies have been explored to reduce MDA burden [159]. Classical antioxidant enzymes (CAT, and SOD) further reduce ROS generation and indirectly lower MDA levels [160].

Acetaldehyde: biogenesis, molecular reactivity, pathological outcome, and detoxification mechanisms

AA is a highly reactive and toxic aldehyde that can bind to protein, DNA and other cellular macromolecules [161]. While alcohol consumption represents the predominant source in humans, AA is also generated endogenously from MDA metabolism under oxidative stress, and is exogenously present in foods, cigarette smoke, beverages and environmental exposures [162–165]. AA can react synergistically with MDA to generate hybrid DNA and protein adducts, such as MDA–AA adducts, which are deleterious to the liver and heart [166, 167]. Through these modifications, AA contributes to genomic instability, carbonyl stress and altered cellular signaling, thereby promoting the development of carcinomas, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes and neurodegenerative disorders [92, 168–170].

Given its high reactivity and pronounced genotoxicity, efficient detoxification of AA is essential to attenuate its accumulation and prevent carbonyl stress–induced cellular damage [171, 172]. In mammalian cells, AA is primarily oxidized to acetate by ALDHs, with ALDH2 playing an important role in mitochondrial AA clearance [173, 174]. In addition, studies in ALDH2.1 KO zebrafish have shown that AA accumulation contributes to the hypoglycaemia and retinopathy [92]. ALDH2, other ALDH isoforms, including ALDH1A1, ALDH1B1 and ALDH1A3, also contribute to AA elimination [109, 168, 175, 176]. In addition, small molecules such as 2-aminobenzamide and hydralazine act as scavenging agents for AA [177–179].

trans,trans-2,4-decadienal (tt-DDE): biogenesis, molecular reactivity, pathological outcome, and detoxification mechanisms

tt-DDE is an endogenous secondary medium-chain aldehyde generated from ω-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid peroxidation during oxidative stress and ferroptosis. It is also encountered exogenously through dietary exposure, particularly from thermally oxidized cooking oils and food processing [180, 181]. Extensive studies have shown that tt-DDE induces covalent modifications of proteins, including cytochrome c, Hsp90 and 14-3-3ζ [182, 183], as well as DNA [184], thereby exerting broad biological effects in diabetes, atherosclerosis, cancers and Alzheimer's disease [93, 182, 185, 186].

Cellular detoxification of tt-DDE relies primarily on ALDH enzymes, GSH conjugation and small molecular scavengers [93, 182]. Research in ALDH9A1B-deficient zebrafish has demonstrated that tt-DDE accumulation causes microvascular damage and glucose metabolism impairment through insulin resistance [93]. Other ALDH isoforms, such as ALDH3, were reported to oxidize medium- and long-chain aldehydes into non-toxic fatty acids, suggesting a potential role in tt-DDE detoxification [187]. In addition, GSH-mediated conjugation has been reported as a major cellular defense mechanism against alkenals and alkadienals [188]. Meanwhile, nucleophilic scavengers, such as L-carnosine and NAC reduce tt-DDE levels and reverse the tt-DDE-induced alterations [93, 181].

2-Hexadecenal: biogenesis, molecular reactivity, pathological outcome, and detoxification mechanisms

2-HD is an unsaturated long-chain fatty aldehyde. The major source of 2-HD is the irreversible cleavage of sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) by S1P lyase (S1PL) during sphingolipid catabolism [188, 189]. 2-HD possesses a wide spectrum of biological activity through the covalent modification with proteins and DNA. Cohen et al. demonstrated that 2-HD induces apoptosis by covalently binding to Cys126 of the pro-apoptotic BCL-2-associated X protein, and triggering the permeabilization of the outer mitochondrial membrane in vitro [190, 191]. In parallel, solid evidence has indicated that translocase of the outer mitochondrial membrane complex, which is essential for mitochondrial protein import and proteostasis, represents a main target of 2-HD, ultimately resulting in programmed cell death [192]. Taking these facts into account, 2-HD formation is associated with the multiple pathologies, including cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative diseases, and immunological disorders [193–195].

It has been shown that the long-chain fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase ALDH3A2 (also known as FALDH) catalyzes the oxidation of 2-HD to hexadecenoic acid and/or hexadecanoic (palmitic) acid, whereas ALDH3B1 has been reported to exhibit in vitro activity toward 2-HD [195–199]. Up to now, relatively little attention has been paid to cellular detoxification strategies for 2-HD. Therefore, further studies are required to elucidate the regulatory and defense mechanisms involved in 2-HD metabolism.

Lessons from multiple RCS as regulators of chronic diseases

In summary, recent studies have shown that a limited view of one RCS and one detoxification enzyme is insufficient for understanding disease and developing new approaches to the therapy of metabolic and vascular disorders. In the future, we will need to study several RCS and several detoxification enzymes in parallel in order to understand the network that ultimately determines the generation and outcome of carbonyl stress. These enzyme systems are interconnected at multiple levels in a much more complex and partially redundant manner, and their dysregulation can lead to far more pathological consequences than originally believed. Further investigations into the specificity of different RCS to their respective detoxification systems, as well as the interplay between various RCS and their corresponding detoxification systems, are crucial for identifying novel mechanisms involved in chronic diseases, and for developing more effective preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Interdependence of RCS in regulating health and chronic disease

Double knockout (DKO) animal models that target different RCS-detoxifying enzymes have shown, that there is indeed a yet incomplete understood network of detoxification enzymes and RCS. While these studies are still very limited, they have already demonstrated that the combined loss of different detoxification enzymes can amplify carbonyl stress, increase MG-H1 and induce tissue-specific organ alterations. Thus, they support the hypothesis that different RCS and their enzymes act cooperatively rather than independently.

Insights from DKO animal models

In a recent GLO1/ALDH3A1 DKO zebrafish model, the combined deficiency led to increased levels of the MG-derived adduct MG-H1. This was accompanied by impaired glucose homeostasis and exaggerated activation of angiogenesis in the hyaloid and retinal vasculature, and thickening of the glomerular basement membrane in the kidneys. From a mechanistic perspective, MG-H1 accumulation led to a reduced proteasome activity. In turn, the downregulation of proteasome activity further exacerbated the MG-H1 accumulation, forming a feedback loop that ultimately resulted in hyperglycemia and activated retinal angiogenesis [200]. The effect on all the other RCS species mentioned above needs yet to be studied. However, similar concepts emerge in the context of neurodegenerative and ocular disease [68, 201]. In a mouse model, ALDH1A1/ALDH2 DKO exhibited age-dependent deficits in motor performance, as assessed by gait analysis and by performance on an accelerating rotarod. Although a significant increase in 4-HNE levels was observed, the study warrants deeper mechanistic validation linking specific aldehyde species to neuronal dysfunction [201]. In the eye, ALDH1A1/ALDH3A1 DKO mice displayed increased lens opacification, along with elevated levels of 4-HNE- and MDA-adducted proteins, as well as reduced proteasome activity. This supports a model in which combined ALDH loss compromises defense against lipid peroxidation–derived aldehydes and promotes cataract formation [68]. Collectively, these DKO studies suggest that the coordination of multiple detoxification enzymes is crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis. They also highlight the importance of investigating the mechanisms underlying chronic diseases within the context of interacting RCS and their corresponding detoxification systems. Further study is also required into the consequences of the accumulation of multiple RCS, such as MG and ACR, and their respective detoxification enzymes (for example GLO1, AKR1A1A and AKR7A3). A limitation of the field is that these experimental models are mostly studied at the level of one organ only. Future research must therefore address a parallel study of all organs and pathways in order to gain a complete picture of the role of RCS and their detoxification enzymes.

Genetic GLO1 models in a disease-specific context

Although DKO models for interrogating interactions among multiple RCS remain limited, an alternative line of investigation has emerged that focuses on MG-detoxifying enzyme deficiencies within specific disease contexts and under conditions of impaired posttranslational repair, with supportive and contradictory findings [202–204].

In atherosclerosis, genetic manipulation of GLO1 has largely had neutral effects, highlighting the complexity of carbonyl stress regulation in vivo. In ApoE KO mice with human GLO1 overexpression, MG levels remained unchanged and there were no differences in aortic root lesion size or plaque phenotype between mice with and without GLO1 overexpression, suggesting that enhancing GLO1 activity alone is insufficient to affect atherogenesis [202]. Similarly, GLO1 knockdown in the ApoE KO mice did not result in increased MG-derived AGEs levels and had no major short-term effects on the energy expenditure or atherosclerotic plaque formation [203]. Together, these findings indicate that MG detoxification via GLO1 may be counteracted by compensatory pathways in the atherosclerotic context. In contrast, the combined loss of GLO1 and DJ-1 in cellular systems revealed more pronounced accumulation of MG-H1 and carboxyethyl-arginine (CEA), accompanied by enhanced MG-mediated histone arginine modification [204]. Taken together, these studies emphasize the importance of investigating deficiencies in RCS-detoxifying enzymes within specific disease contexts and under various pathological conditions.

Unresolved questions and future perspectives

Over the past decades, a wide spectrum of RCS, ranging from MG to lipid peroxidation–derived aldehydes, has been identified and extensively characterized with respect to their sources, chemical reactivity, detoxification enzyme, and disease associations. These studies have firmly established carbonyl stress as a hallmark of chronic metabolic, cardiovascular, renal and neurodegenerative diseases. Accumulating evidence indicates that the onset and development of chronic diseases do not dependent on individual RCS solely, but rather on the multiple RCS and the coordinated function of their detoxifying enzyme systems. While numerous RCS have been investigated, their interconnected detoxification networks, compensatory mechanisms and context-dependent regulation remain mostly unknown. This suggests that future research should shift its primary focus from individual RCS and individual enzymes toward the integrated systems that control their homeostasis.

Perspectives in basic research

A key consideration for RCS and their detoxifying enzyme systems is the functional consequences of the different binding affinities of specific RCS to their respective enzymes. As RCS can bind to different detoxifying enzymes with different affinities, it is necessary to determine the conditions under which detoxification occurs and the functional consequences if one enzyme fails. Investigating the complex networks of detoxifying enzymes is also crucial, as is determining how the loss of one enzyme can be compensated for by others, and the organ alterations that this may induce. It also seems likely that there are RCS that are currently unknown and uncharacterized; in silico prediction approaches, combined with experimental validation could facilitate the identification of novel RCS and their corresponding detoxifying enzymes. Meantime, in silico modeling can be performed to anticipate the candidate catalytic or substrate-interaction sites of the detoxifying enzyme [205].

Another important future area for research is the organ- and tissue-specific regulation of RCS detoxifying enzymes, which can be achieved by using conditional and cell-specific KO models in specific organs, which will help to resolve why certain tissues exhibit particular vulnerability to carbonyl stress [206]. Studies should also identify the cellular localization and molecular targets of RCS and their detoxifying enzymes, as many RCS-induced post-translational, DNA, and lipid modifications remain unidentified, and their cellular and physiological consequences are poorly understood. In addition, little is known about the upstream regulators at post-translational, epigenetic and transcriptional levels of detoxifying enzymes, and the primary inducers must be identified under different pathological conditions. This will enable causative experiments in animal models and pave the way for therapeutic applications in patients with chronic diseases.

Perspectives in translational and clinical research

Some clinical studies have already demonstrated the clinical relevance of RCS in chronic diseases [40]. However, establishing RCS profiles is not yet standard practice for diagnosing chronic diseases or making therapeutic decisions. While determining RCS in plasma and urine is favorable, it should be noted that RCS formation and detoxification mainly occur in solid tissues, such as liver, muscles, and kidneys, and that urine and plasma samples can only provide correlative, rather than causative information. While incorporating solid tissues into RCS analysis is advisable, this is also associated with additional ethical approvals, workload, feasibility and adaptation to analytical measurements. In addition, as RCSs are relatively unstable, it is recommended that the modifications induced by RCSs in the clinical sample are also determined, rather than determining the RCSs alone. Most importantly, there is an urgent need to develop novel, potent and specific RCS scavengers, as well as RCS detoxifying enzyme activators based on the mechanistic pathways, and translate to individuals. The future challenge lies in establishing different RCS profiles and their corresponding enzyme system activities as biomarkers for predicting and diagnosing chronic diseases, monitoring disease progression, determining patient subgroups, and developing therapeutic interventions and remission.
