FPECGNET: A deep learning framework based on federated learning and prototype learning for interpretable ECG classification with privacy
Authors: Jiang He, Jie He, Jin Xiong, Song He, Yu Chen, XinWei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUnder everyday operational conditions, data privacy regulations prohibit sharing information between hospitals or institutions, contributing to the emergence of data silos. This scarcity of data hinders improvements in model performance. Another major obstacle to AI applications in medicine is the lack of interpretability, where the decision-making process of models cannot be reflected. We propose a deep learning framework based on federated learning and prototype learning that simulates the reality of hospital data silos while endowing the model with interpretability. Using the publicly available PTB-XL dataset, we divided it into three subsets by category and trained the model using the federated learning framework, achieving superior performance on these subsets. When tested on the aggregated model using the PTB-XL dataset, it demonstrated performance comparable to the current state-of-the-art model.

Introduction

Deep learning technology has developed rapidly in recent years and gradually permeated numerous fields, such as healthcare, industrial manufacturing, and financial analysis. In the medical field, deep learning has achieved significant progress in complex tasks like medical image analysis, physiological signal recognition, and disease prediction due to its powerful feature extraction capabilities. However, the large-scale application of artificial intelligence in healthcare still faces many challenges, with the two most prominent issues being the privacy and sharing restrictions of medical data, as well as the lack of interpretability in deep learning models. For example, Ding et al. (2024) conducted a systematic review of deep learning methods in personalized electrocardiogram (ECG) diagnosis,1 pointing out that cross-patient generalization capability is one of the key challenges in current ECG intelligent diagnosis research. Significant differences exist among patients in terms of ECG signal morphology, physiological characteristics, and disease manifestations, which often makes it difficult for models trained on one dataset to maintain stable performance across other patient populations. Since different medical institutions possess data from diverse patient groups, integrating multi-institutional data resources while protecting privacy would help build ECG intelligent diagnostic models with stronger generalization capabilities. For instance, Jiang et al. (2025) provided a systematic review of sensors based on photoelectric volume pulse wave (PPG) and artificial intelligence algorithms,2 highlighting that deep learning methods are widely applied in various physiological signal analysis tasks, including heart rate monitoring, blood oxygen estimation, and disease risk assessment. This demonstrates that interpretable and secure AI frameworks hold significant value not only for ECG analysis but also for broader intelligent monitoring of physiological signals. Meanwhile, the development of medical AI is evolving from simple signal classification and disease prediction to more complex clinical decision-making and medical operation scenarios. For instance, Yao et al. (2025) noted in their review of AI technologies in endovascular robotic-assisted surgery3 that future medical AI systems must simultaneously possess safety, interpretability, and cross-system collaboration capabilities. In this context, constructing intelligent medical models that combine privacy protection and interpretability is crucial for advancing the application of AI in real-world clinical settings.

First, medical data are highly sensitive. Due to strict privacy protection regulations,4 hospitals and medical institutions are typically unable to directly share patient data. This situation leads to the dispersion of medical data across different institutions, resulting in the phenomenon known as “data silos.” Data silos not only limit the scale of data available for training deep learning models but also exacerbate the imbalance in data distribution. In real-world medical settings, different hospitals often possess specific diagnostic and therapeutic advantages in certain disease areas, leading to a pronounced bias in data distribution. For example, Kocak et al. pointed out in their research that some specialized hospitals may have a large number of cases in certain disease categories, while other types of cases are relatively scarce.5 Such imbalanced data distribution can easily lead to overfitting during the training of deep learning models,6 thereby reducing the generalization ability of the models in practical clinical applications.

To address the challenges of medical data privacy and data silos, federated learning (FL) has been proposed and gradually applied in the field of medical artificial intelligence. FL, a distributed machine learning framework first introduced by Google in 2016,7,8 aims to enable collaborative training of shared models among multiple parties while ensuring user data privacy and security. Its core concept involves not directly exchanging local data but allowing participating entities (such as different hospitals, clinics, or medical institutions) to train models locally, with only model parameters or gradients being uploaded to a central server for aggregation. Through this approach, FL can fully utilize massive heterogeneous data resources distributed across various institutions without disclosing sensitive data. Consequently, FL is considered a critical technical solution for resolving medical data silos and enhancing model generalization capabilities.9 However, existing FL research primarily focuses on model performance and privacy protection. For instance, Kuba et al. trained multiple ECG datasets using residual networks and the FL paradigm,10 demonstrating the advantages and feasibility of cross-dataset training. Nevertheless, attention to model interpretability remains relatively limited.

The lack of interpretability in deep learning models poses another major challenge for their application in healthcare. Although attention-based visualization methods can highlight specific ECG data segments the model focuses on during predictions,11 these approaches typically provide only partial explanations, failing to reveal how the model makes concrete diagnostic decisions based on holistic signal features. In clinical practice, physicians often require a comprehensive understanding of the model’s decision-making rationale to enhance the credibility and acceptability of its predictions.12,13 Therefore, improving model interpretability while maintaining performance has become a critical research focus in medical artificial intelligence.

Prototype learning (PL) has been recognized in recent years as a machine learning paradigm with strong interpretability.14 Its core concept involves learning a set of representative “prototype vectors” for each category in the feature space, where the model classifies samples by calculating distances between them and their respective prototypes. Compared to traditional deep classifiers, PL offers advantages, such as structural simplicity, fewer parameters, and strong interpretability. The prediction process can be intuitively explained through the similarity relationships between “samples and prototypes,” enabling physicians to understand the rationale behind the model’s disease classification. This has made it widely popular in medical image tasks, including brain tumor detection, oral cancer diagnosis, and breast cancer classification.15,16,17 Additionally, PL performs well in small-sample learning and imbalanced category tasks, while supporting flexible expansion of new categories. However, existing PL research typically relies on centralized data training. For instance, Yong et al. proposed a graph neural network (GNN) model based on prototype interpretation, PAGE,18 to explain what the underlying GNN model learns for graph classification. Another example is Sahil Sethil et al.’ s protoecgnet, which implements prototype residual network (PRN) training with a focus on interpretability19 but lacks considerations for medical data privacy protection and cross-institutional collaborative training.

Current research demonstrates that although FL effectively addresses data privacy and data silo issues, it lacks interpretability. Conversely, PL, though highly interpretable, struggles with multi-organizational data collaboration and privacy protection requirements. To bridge this research gap, this paper proposes an ECG intelligent diagnostic framework (FPECGNET) combining FL and PL (Figure 1,see STAR Methods). We selected the PTB-XL ECG dataset20 for relevant experiments, as ECG is a critical clinical tool for recording the electrophysiological activity of the heart during each beat. It reflects cardiac rhythm, conduction function, and myocardial ischemia, hypertrophy, and injury, making it widely used in the diagnosis and monitoring of cardiovascular diseases. Cardiovascular diseases are one of the leading causes of death globally,21 including arrhythmias, myocardial infarction (MI), and heart failure. Due to its non-invasive, low-cost, rapid acquisition, and real-time monitoring capabilities, ECG data holds significant value in clinical diagnosis, treatment, and health management. Among numerous ECG databases, PTB-XL is currently one of the largest and most comprehensively labeled 12-lead ECG datasets.20 It contains over 20,000 records, covering diagnostic labels for various cardiac diseases and supports multi-level research from diagnostics to rhythmology and morphology. This dataset not only provides a reliable data source for clinical research but also offers a standardized platform for training and validating deep learning models. Therefore, research based on ECG, particularly PTB-XL data, is of great significance for advancing early detection, intelligent diagnosis, and personalized treatment of cardiovascular diseases.

In terms of model architecture, we adopted ResNet18 as the primary network framework. Since He Kaiming et al. proposed residual networks in 2015,22 this architecture has become one of the most influential network architectures in deep learning, widely applied in tasks, such as image classification, speech recognition, and medical data analysis. Residual networks effectively mitigate gradient vanishing and network degradation during deep network training by introducing “residual connections.” ResNet18 offers advantages, such as small parameter size, good training stability, and strong transferability, making it highly suitable for deployment in FL environments. Additionally, the structure of residual blocks facilitates integration with other mechanisms, such as PL layers or attention mechanisms. In this paper, we introduce a prototype layer after the ResNet18 feature extraction network and optimize prototype vectors through a composite prototype loss function, thereby constructing an interpretable ECG classification model (Figure 4). Finally, we embed this PRN into a FL framework to achieve collaborative training in multi-agent environments.

In conclusion, the proposed FPECGNET integrates FL with PL to enhance model interpretability and generalization while safeguarding medical data privacy. This approach not only establishes a technical pathway for intelligent ECG diagnosis but also provides valuable insights for developing interpretable and secure medical AI research.

We designed multiple experiments to validate our model’s performance. First, we conducted experiments on various partitioned data subsets. We performed distributed training using the FL approach with the PRN framework, comparing it against centralized training using the PRN. Subsequently, we performed FL training and centralized training on the entire dataset (71 labels). Finally, we removed the prototype layer and trained directly using ResNet18 as a baseline. Notably, we simulated real-world hospital data scenarios with overlapping regions. Regional medical data analysis revealed that smaller geographic areas exhibited more severe patient data overlap. In actual healthcare systems, patient populations across different hospitals are typically not entirely independent.23 Due to referrals, follow-ups, or cross-institutional examinations, the same patient may receive care at multiple medical institutions, resulting in varying degrees of data overlap. During FL, we trained with 3 clients, randomly assigning 0.6 of the training set to each. Compared to directly partitioning the dataset into three equal parts, this more realistic data division accelerated model convergence and enabled faster attainment of high performance. We employed F1 score and macro-AUROC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) as performance metrics to evaluate training methods and model quality.

Analysis of experimental results

Overall, the performance of different branch models under the FL framework shows certain differences. Specifically, both the two-dimensional branch models (Morphology and Global) under the FedAvg and FedOpt aggregation strategies significantly outperform the one-dimensional Rhythm branch model in both AUC and F1 score metrics. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to differences in model input formats and feature extraction capabilities. By converting ECG signals into two-dimensional representations and employing two-dimensional convolutional architectures for feature extraction, the two-dimensional models can capture joint temporal and morphological changes in ECG signals across a larger receptive field compared to one-dimensional convolutional models. Consequently, they generate richer and more stable feature representations. Therefore, when identifying morphological information, such as ST-T changes, QRS morphology abnormalities, or MI-related features, the two-dimensional models typically demonstrate superior discriminative capabilities, resulting in higher AUC and F1 score values.

From the perspective of aggregation strategies in FL, FedOpt consistently outperforms FedAvg in both data subsets (Table 1) and the full dataset. This performance gap is closely related to the non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) characteristics of ECG data. In data subsets, the limited number of categories (16 for rhythm and 3 for global diagnosis) results in relatively concentrated sample distribution and low inter-client data heterogeneity. Under these conditions, FedAvg’s simple mean aggregation achieves stable convergence with good performance. In contrast, morphological branches contain more categories (52 types) with highly uneven sample distribution, where different clients may lack certain rare categories or exhibit significant proportion variations. This leads to substantial differences in gradient directions across clients, making FedAvg’s aggregation susceptible to non-IID effects. FedOpt introduces adaptive optimizers (e.g., Adam or momentum-enhanced SGD) on the server side to update the global model, effectively mitigating gradient conflicts and convergence issues caused by non-IID. Consequently, in highly non-IID morphological branches, FedOpt can more effectively integrate client information and enhance performance.

Discussion

This study proposes a training framework for ECG classification that integrates FL with PL, with systematic experimental validation conducted on the PTB-XL dataset. Experimental results demonstrate that this training strategy not only achieves stable performance improvements in the global model but also delivers superior classification performance across client-side branch models. Compared to centralized PL approaches, the proposed federated training framework exhibits enhanced performance metrics including AUC and F1 score, validating its effectiveness and feasibility in multi-label ECG classification tasks.

From an application perspective, this method alleviates the prevalent data silo issue in the field of medical artificial intelligence to some extent. FL enables multiple healthcare institutions to collaboratively model without compromising patient privacy by sharing model parameters rather than raw data across different organizations, thereby enhancing model generalization capabilities on larger datasets. Simultaneously, the introduction of PL mechanisms allows models to represent representative ECG patterns through prototype vectors, providing a degree of interpretability for model predictions. This combination of “F + PL” ensures that the model not only maintains privacy protection but also delivers more transparent and understandable predictive insights for clinical decision-making.

In terms of model architecture, we implemented multiple enhancements and explorations for the network. For instance, we introduced the CBAM (Convolutional block attention module) attention mechanism24 between residual blocks to strengthen the model’s focus on critical feature regions. However, experimental results demonstrated that while this attention module improved the model’s expressive capacity, it also incurred additional computational overhead. Notably, it failed to deliver significant performance gains in the current task and even showed slight performance degradation in some experiments. Furthermore, we conducted comparative analysis of different federated optimization strategies. Experiments revealed that training local branch models using the FedOpt strategy improved model performance on certain metrics, which may be attributed to the uneven data distribution among clients.

Beyond optimizing the training strategy, we further investigated how varying data scales affect model performance. Experimental results demonstrate that when clients have access to more training samples, the model shows significant improvement in multi-label classification tasks. This indicates that richer data enables the model to learn more stable and discriminative feature representations. Additionally, during the model fusion phase, we modified the original fusion architecture by adding an extra linear layer after the fusion classifier to enhance feature representation between different branches. While this improvement delivers measurable performance gains across most experimental settings, it does not substantially alter the model’s optimal performance ceiling.

In summary, this study highlights the training advantages of combining FL with PL. FL enables cross-institutional data collaboration while safeguarding medical data privacy, whereas PL provides excellent model explainability through prototype representations. Visual analysis of prototype vectors (t-SNE) reveals that features of different categories form more distinct update paths in the feature space, further explaining the improvement in model performance. In the future, as medical data scales continue to expand and cross-institutional collaboration becomes increasingly necessary, this learning framework that integrates privacy protection and explainability is expected to play a significant role in more clinical scenarios, thereby ensuring the safety and reliability of medical artificial intelligence in practical applications.

Model effectiveness analysis

Overall, training datasets using FL not only addresses the data scarcity and security concerns that PL may encounter. Moreover, FL accelerates prototype vector updates to some extent by leveraging parallel training across multiple clients, thereby enhancing model performance. To visually illustrate the update trends of prototype vectors under different training approaches, we employed t-SNE for visualization. t-SNE is a nonlinear dimensionality reduction and visualization algorithm,25 proposed by Laurens van der Maaten and Geoffrey Hinton in 2008. It is commonly used to map high-dimensional data (ranging from tens to thousands of dimensions) into 2D or 3D spaces, enabling human observers to intuitively perceive data distributions and clusters. Prototype vectors represent typical high-dimensional data, hence our selection of t-SNE visualization here.

We selected the rhythm branch as an example, which contains 16 categories, each comprising 5 prototypes. We averaged the prototypes of each category at the same time to obtain a single prototype, then recorded the 16 single prototypes every five epochs. Since the model’s output in PL depends on similarity scores between input data and prototype vectors, prototype vector updates significantly impact model performance. Limitations of the study

This study still exhibits certain limitations. Firstly, the FL experiment conducted in this study was carried out in a simulated environment, rather than a real distributed medical system. Although the data were divided into multiple clients to simulate the data distribution across different institutions, this approach still struggles to fully reflect the disparities in equipment, data quality, and patient demographics among various hospitals in real clinical scenarios. Consequently, future work is warranted to further validate this method in a genuine multi-center medical setting.

Secondly, this study primarily conducts experiments based on the publicly available PTB-XL ECG dataset. Despite its large scale and standardized annotations, the data distribution still cannot fully represent real clinical environments. Additionally, the client data partitioning in this study remains relatively simple, without fully considering the potentially severe non-IID issues that may exist in real-world medical scenarios, such as label distribution skew and sample imbalance across different institutions. These factors may affect the performance and convergence stability of the FL model.

Finally, although this study combines the prototype network with the FL framework to enhance the feature representation ability and interpretability of the model, it still does not fully consider the communication efficiency and system overhead issues in practical deployment, such as communication delays and computational capability differences between clients. In addition, the clinical interpretability of the model still needs further verification through the assessment of professional doctors. Therefore, future research will further explore more efficient federated prototype learning strategies and conduct prospective validation on real clinical data.

Method details

Federated learning has gained significant traction in the medical field in recent years due to its distributed training capabilities, which effectively alleviate data scarcity issues.7 Particularly for rare disease cases, its application has garnered increasing attention. However, nearly all medical federated learning studies appear to have downplayed the importance of interpretability. Merely employing attention mechanisms to highlight specific segments falls far short of addressing this critical need.

Prototype learning research in medicine is currently on the rise. Following the introduction of ProtoPNet,26 prototype learning gained widespread attention. The most recent work on ECG prototype learning is ProtoECGNet by Sahil Sethi et al.,19 which implemented training of prototype residual networks and achieved promising performance when compared against relevant black-box models. However, their work focused solely on model interpretability without employing federated learning for training to further address medical model interoperability challenges.

Inspired by these approaches and reflecting on the practical dilemmas of medical AI, I decided to integrate federated learning with prototype learning. My experiments revealed that federated learning enhances the updating of prototype vectors, yielding synergistic results when combined.

In our study, we employed a prototype residual neural network to classify ECG recordings. We utilized an existing federated learning algorithm from the FLOWER community and integrated it with ProtoECGNet. We investigated and compared multiple existing training methods, contrasting their performance against centralized training. This section first provides a detailed overview of the Prototype Residual Network architecture. This includes the underlying residual network, the branching architecture of the intermediate layers, the federated learning training process, and the final model fusion. We then describe how the baseline residual network model integrates with prototype learning, detailing the update process for prototype vectors. We aim to clearly convey the underlying principles and techniques involved in classifying ECG data using prototype learning and federated learning.

Model architecture

From the highest level perspective, the model employs federated learning for training. To facilitate experimentation, we utilized three clients, with each federated learning session using the same model (specific branch models) across all three clients. We conducted multiple federated learning training sessions based on partitioned datasets. Across three training cycles, a total of nine clients participated in a phased approach. Each branch input identical 12-lead ECG data with a shape input of (1×12×1000), but applied distinct label subsets. Every three clients fused to generate a composite branch model, and these three branch models were then merged to produce the final model.

From a foundational perspective, (1) the federated learning training of the 1D Rhythm branch primarily handles data exhibiting temporal anomalies. The base model employs a typical ResNet18 architecture, first converting input data into (12×1000) format before training to output (512×1) features—without performing classification. Instead, a prototype layer is added atop the ResNet18 model, updated through residual network outputs. (2) For the 2D Morphology and 2D Global branches, the base model also employs the ResNet18 architecture. Input data is (1×12×1000), with model output being (512×1×32), though their prototypes differ. The 1D Rhythm branch and 2D Global branch utilize a global prototype (covering all time steps across the entire timeline, performing a single comparison against the entire ECG feature sequence to capture rhythm or overall morphology). The 2D Morphology branch employs a partial prototype (covering only a small time window, e.g., 3 latent units ≈ 0.94 seconds of ECG signal), which then moves along the latent feature map’s timeline using a sliding window approach. This avoids comparing against the global feature and instead compares against local segments, making it more suitable for capturing waveform morphology.19

Branch models are trained as underlying clients in federated learning. Model parameters and prototype vectors (converted using nn.parameter) are aggregated to the central server through the federated learning framework. We employed two distinct federated learning strategies (FedAvg and Fedopt) for aggregation and compared their performance. Three servers generate three aggregated branch models, which are then fused to produce the final ECG classification model. The model architecture is illustrated in Figure 1.

Experiment settings

Data acquisition

We utilized publicly available datasets from PhysioNet,27 specifically the ptb-xl dataset—a large collection comprising 21,799 clinical 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) from 18,869 patients.20 Each ECG recording lasts 10 seconds, with sampling frequencies categorized into 500Hz and 100Hz groups. The raw waveform data was annotated by up to two cardiology specialists, who assigned one or more diagnoses to each ECG. These diagnoses were derived from 71 distinct ECG statements compliant with the SCP-ECG standard. These 71 distinct statements are further categorized into three types based on classification criteria: rhythm statements, morphological statements, and diagnostic statements. To address data distribution imbalance, the 71 ECG data types can also be grouped into five superclasses.

Dataset grouping

We adopted the classification scheme proposed by Sasil et al.,19 which categorizes the 71 labels into three clinically meaningful prototypical categories based on the type of visual reasoning required for diagnosis: (1) Rhythm-based diagnoses, comprising 16 cases. (2) Morphology-based diagnoses, comprising 52 cases. (3) Global-based diagnoses, comprising 3 cases. We performed 10-fold cross-validation on these datasets, using folds 1–8 as training data and fold 9 as the validation set. The final 10-fold was designated as the test set. This evaluation method ensures optimal utilization of training data while minimizing the variability introduced by single-fold partitioning, thereby providing a more stable and objective assessment of the model’s performance.

Evaluation metrics

This study primarily employs macro-AUROC and F1-score to evaluate the model. Macro-AUROC is calculated by separately determining the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) for each diagnostic label and then averaging the AUCs across all labels. This metric is insensitive to classification thresholds and comprehensively reflects the model’s overall ability to distinguish between positive and negative samples under different discriminative thresholds, making it highly applicable in medical diagnostic tasks with imbalanced category distributions.28 In the ROC curve, TPR (True Positive Rate) represents the true positive rate, while FPR (False Positive Rate) represents the false positive rate; TP and TN denote the numbers of true positive and true negative samples, respectively, and FN and FP represent the numbers of false negative and false positive samples, respectively.(Equation 1)AUC=∫01TPR(x)dFPR(x)

The F1-score is the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, which comprehensively evaluates a model’s ability to reduce false positives and false negatives. In multi-label classification tasks, the F1-score is first calculated for each sample or category, and then averaged as the final result. Compared to using Precision or Recall alone, the F1-score provides a more comprehensive reflection of a model’s overall discriminative performance under multi-class and multi-label conditions, making it widely used in the performance evaluation of medical diagnostic models.(Equation 2)PRE=TPTP+FP,SEN=TPTP+FN,F1=2·PRE·SENPRE+SEN

Federal average algorithm

The federated averaging (FedAvg) algorithm was introduced to address data privacy challenges7 while enabling collaborative training of global models. Originally designed for mobile devices, FedAvg allows a federation of participating devices (clients) to jointly train models while preserving privacy. In this approach, clients compute updates to the global model and transmit them to a server, which performs model averaging. Crucially, FedAvg ensures clients never share raw data with each other or the server.

To implement FedAvg, we adopted the federated learning framework from the Flower community (Algorithm 1).29 After introducing the federated learning framework, we modified the code to integrate with the prototype network and address server-client interaction challenges. The algorithm communicates in rounds. In our experiments, we used three clients and one server. Initially, all four models are initialized. At the start of each round, the server transmits the global model parameters to the three clients. The three clients train their models independently without interference. After completing one training round (5 epochs), each client sends its model parameters to the server. Adaptive federated optimization

In federated learning scenarios, when client data exhibits non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) characteristics,30 the classic FedAvg algorithm is often susceptible to client drift. This drift causes local models to gradually deviate from the global optimum during updates, thereby slowing the convergence of the global model and potentially compromising final performance. To mitigate this issue, Reddi et al. proposed the FedOpt algorithm31 as an extension of FedAvg. The core idea of FedOpt is to model the federated learning process as a distributed optimization problem and introduce advanced adaptive optimization methods on the server side (such as variants like Adagrad,32 Adam,33 and Yogi34). Prototype learning algorithms

Prototype learning first initializes prototype representations for each category, mathematically expressed as a set of prototype vectors. This single or multiple vector set undergoes updates during training. Input training data is first converted into feature vectors via a feature extractor (in this paper, a residual network). The distance between these feature vectors and the prototype vectors is then computed using a loss function, driving the update of the prototype vectors. During final validation or testing, the distance between the feature vectors extracted from the data and the prototype vectors is calculated. The similarity is then used to determine which category the sample belongs to.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Prototype updates and loss function

In this model, classification primarily relies on comparing the output from ResNet with prototype vectors. First, let the input ECG image be denoted as x. After feature extraction through the ResNet backbone network, we obtain its feature representation f(x) ∈ ℝd. In the prototype layer, a predefined set of prototype vectors P = {p1, p2, …, pk} is utilized, where each prototype vector pⱼ ∈ ℝd represents a typical feature pattern of a specific category. To quantify the similarity between sample features and prototypes, we employ cosine similarity to calculate their relationship, expressed as(Equation 3)sj=cos(f(x),pj)=f(x)·pj‖f(x)‖2‖pj‖2=∑i=1dfi(x)pj,i∑i=1dfi(x)2∑i=1dpj,i2

Through the above calculation, we can obtain the similarity score vector between the sample and all prototypes.(Equation 4)S=[s1,s2,…,sK]

The vector is then fed into the classification layer as logits to generate prediction probabilities for each category.

To simultaneously ensure classification performance and the discriminative power of prototype representation, we employ a composite prototype loss function for model optimization. This loss function is designed based on the ProtoECGNet approach. The total loss comprises five components: multi-label classification loss, prototype clustering loss, prototype separation loss, prototype orthogonal constraint loss, and contrastive learning loss. The overall loss function can be expressed as(Equation 5)L=LBCE+λ1Lcluster+λ2Lseparation+λ3Lorth+λ4Lcontrast

The framework incorporates: LBCE (binary cross-entropy loss for multi-label classification) to optimize predictive performance; Lcluster (clustering loss) to encourage closer alignment between sample features and their corresponding class prototypes in feature space; Lseparation (separation loss) to maintain sufficient distance between class prototypes; Lorth (orthogonal constraint loss) to reduce redundancy among prototypes and promote diverse discriminative patterns; and Lcontrast (contrastive learning loss) to enhance feature consistency within classes while widening class boundaries. The default weight coefficients (λ1=0.004, λ2=0.0004, λ3=250, λ4=300) balance the contributions of each loss function during optimization.

During backpropagation, the composite loss function computes gradients not only for the feature representation f(x) to update ResNet18’s backbone parameters, but also propagates gradients to the prototype vector P. This process gradually aligns the prototype with the feature distribution of its corresponding class while distancing it from other classes, ultimately generating a prototype representation that demonstrates both strong discriminative power and interpretability.
