Evaluating Bacterial Viability in Faecal Microbiota Transplantation: A Comparative Analysis of In Vitro Cultivation and Membrane Integrity Methods
Authors: Ivana Cibulková, Veronika Řehořová, Marek Wilhelm, Hana Soukupová, Jan Hajer, František Duška, Helena Daňková, Monika Cahová
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFaecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is a developing therapy for disorders related to gut dysbiosis. Despite its growing application, standardised protocols for FMT filtrate preparation and quality assessment remain undeveloped. The viability of bacteria in the filtrate is crucial for FMT's efficacy and for validating protocol execution. We compared two methods—in vitro cultivation and membrane integrity assessment—for their accuracy, reproducibility and clinical applicability in measuring bacterial viability in frozen FMT stool filtrate.MethodsBacterial viability in stool filtrate was evaluated using (i) membrane integrity through fluorescent DNA staining with SYTO9 and propidium iodide, followed by flow cytometry and (ii) culturable bacteria counts (colony‐forming units, CFU) under aerobic or anaerobic conditions.ResultsUsing different types of samples (pure bacterial culture, stool of germ‐free and conventionally bred mice, native and heat‐treated human stool), we refined the bacterial DNA staining protocol integrated with flow cytometry for assessment of bacterial viability in frozen human stool samples. Both the membrane integrity‐based and cultivation‐based methods exhibited significant variability in bacterial viability across different FMT filtrates, without correlation. The cultivation‐based method showed a mean coefficient of variance of 30.3%, ranging from 7.4% to 60.1%. Conversely, the membrane integrity approach yielded more reproducible results, with a mean coefficient of variance for viable cells of 6.4% ranging from 0.2% to 18.2%.ConclusionBacterial viability assessment in stool filtrate using the membrane integrity method offers robust and precise data, making it a suitable option for faecal material evaluation in FMT. In contrast, the cultivation‐dependent methods produce inconsistent outcomes.Keywords: bacterial viability, cultivation, faecal microbiota transplantation, membrane integrity

Introduction

The recent progress in sequencing methods led to the shift in the perception of a multicellular eukaryotic organism defined solely or predominantly by its own genome to the idea of a holobiont, composed of the eukaryotic ‘macro’ organism accompanied by a highly variable resident microbiota (the bacteria, archaea, viruses and fungi) [1]. Along with this paradigm change, there are various efforts to modulate the microbiome through dietary changes, prebiotics, probiotics and faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), the latter showing notable promise in altering gut microbiota composition significantly [1, 2, 3].

The precise explanation of the clinical effect of FMT remains incompletely understood. It appears that a crucial aspect involves the competition between ‘healthy’ live commensal bacteria from the donor for space and nutrients with ‘harmful’ pathogens in the recipient's gut, thereby suppressing the growth of pathogens. However, newer evidence has suggested that the effectiveness of FMT is likely not solely attributed to the restoration of intestinal bacteria per se but also to a myriad of other factors within the stool components such as bacteriophages, intestinal epithelium, dead microorganisms and their metabolic products, which potentially mediate the mechanism of its action [1, 2].

At present, FMT is officially recognised for treating recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) in the US and EU [3, 4, 5]. Beyond CDI, FMT's efficacy extends to conditions like hepatic encephalopathy, inflammatory bowel disease (particularly ulcerative colitis), irritable bowel syndrome, neurological disorders and the transmission of multidrug‐resistant organisms [6, 7, 8]. Over 460 clinical trials are underway, exploring its potential across various health issues, including obesity and multiple sclerosis [9]. In 2019, Europe reported 1874 hospital‐based FMT procedures for both routine and research purposes [8]. Despite its established role, particularly in CDI, standardised guidelines for FMT application are lacking [10]. It is evident that the method of the preparation and storage of FMT filtrate influences its final composition, particularly the abundance and diversity of viable microbiota [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. International consensus offers best practice recommendations [6, 16, 17]; however, guidelines for preparation as well as requirements for the final quality and composition of stool filtrate, especially concerning bacterial viability, are not uniformly defined yet.

Regulatory perspectives on FMT vary globally; some nations regulate the stool product as a pharmaceutical, while others classify it alongside human cell and tissue products. In the USA, Canada, France and the Czech Republic, FMT filtrate is considered an investigational drug, necessitating standardisation for safety and efficacy [18]. Bacterial viability is a key characteristic influencing both therapeutic effectiveness and protocol adherence [15, 19]. Thus, identifying a straightforward, dependable and cost‐effective method for assessing bacterial viability is imperative for FMT centres and stool banks to function effectively and within the regulatory framework.

This study aims to establish a precise and practical approach for assessing bacterial viability in stool filtrate, facilitating its integration into standard operating procedures by FMT centres and biobanks.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

The study design and workflow are shown in Figure 1. First, we used a standard gating strategy for distinguishing different cell populations based on SYTO9 and PI staining based on a blank sample (containing only water and fluorescent dyes) and a pure E. coli bacterial culture (Step I). As the next step, we used a mixture of live and heat‐treated E. coli cultures in defined ratios to confirm that the method accurately reflects the ratio of live to dead bacteria (Step II). We then proceeded to evaluate the resolving power of the LIVE/DEAD™ BacLight™ Bacterial Viability Kit for use in stool samples, for which it is not primarily intended (Step III). For this purpose, we used (i) stool samples from germ‐free (GF) and conventionally reared (CONV) mice to verify whether the staining method can effectively distinguish bacteria from the autofluorescence of non‐bacterial elements in heterogeneous components of the stool; (ii) frozen human stool filtrate, either thawed only or thawed and heat‐treated, to evaluate the sensitivity of the kit in distinguishing live and dead bacteria in stool and (iii) frozen human stool filtrate thawed and stained under aerobic or anaerobic conditions aiming to test the effect of exposure to atmospheric oxygen during the staining process on bacterial viability. After confirming the accuracy of the flow cytometry method for stool samples, we applied it to quantify viable bacteria in an FMT filtrate frozen for 1 year and compared this method with standard cultivation of the same FMT filtrate using colony‐forming units (CFU) calculation (Step IV).

Design of the study.

Donor Characteristics and Human Stool Processing

Human biological samples were collected during the FEBATRICE study, approved by the Ethics Committee for Multi‐Centric Clinical Trials of FNKV University Hospital (Decision No. KH/40/00/2021). Donors underwent screening as per the FEBATRICE clinical trial protocol (NCT05430269). Altogether 13 stool samples were obtained from nine donors (2 females, 7 males), median age of 36 years. (min 25; max 42), median BMI 23.7 (min 17.6; max 27.8). Stool collection from donors for this study began on March 1, 2022 and recruitment ended on June 1, 2022. The study protocol is in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 amendment) and all the donors gave a written, prospective informed consent. Stool from donors, devoid of diarrhoea and free from mucus or blood, was processed within 4 h post‐defecation following the standard operating procedure [20]. Samples were diluted in sterile 0.9% NaCl, homogenised with a dedicated hand blender (Mulinex), filtered for uniformity, mixed with 10% glycerol by volume and stored at −80°C for up to 1 year.

Mice Stool Sample Processing

The stool samples from germ‐free (GF) C57Bl6/J mice were obtained from the colony bred at the Gnotobiology Laboratory Institute of Microbiology of the CAS, Novy Hradek, CR and treated in a sterile way. The samples from conventionally bred (CONV) C57Bl6/J mice were obtained from the breeding facility IKEM, Prague. All samples were kept at −20°C. Prior to the experiment, mouse faeces (100 mg) were thawed at room temperature for 10 min, diluted in 0.9% NaCl 1:4 (wt/wt), homogenised by vigorous vortexing and glycerol (10% wt/wt) was added.

Viability Determination in Pure Bacterial Culture

Overnight culture of E. coli ATCC 10536 (CCM 3988) (50 mL of LB medium, 37°C, 200 RPM) was harvested by centrifugation. The pellet was resuspended in 10 mL 0.9% NaCl solution and then diluted by the decimal series to a final concentration of 1:20,000. One half of the sample was left (live bacteria) and the other half was treated at 70°C for 10 min (heat‐treated bacteria). Before staining, mixtures with different proportions of live and heat‐treated cells were prepared.

Viability Determination in Human Stool Samples Using Flow Cytometry

Aerobic Conditions

Frozen FMT filtrates were thawed at 36°C for 10 min and diluted by the decimal series to a final concentration of 1:10,000. If required, in some experiments where it was necessary to increase the number of dead bacteria, these samples were pretreated by incubation at 80°C for 15 min. For evaluation by flow cytometry samples were further mixed with fluorescent dyes (SYTO9 and PI) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Anaerobic Conditions

In some experiments, the dilution and staining procedure was performed under anaerobic conditions. The saline solution of 9 mL was dispensed into sterile glass vials, bubbled with nitrogen for 15 min and sealed with a rubber stopper with an aluminium rim. The absence of oxygen was checked by resazurin. The FMT filtrate prepared in the standard manner was thawed (closed vessel, 10 min, 36°C). After thawing, the vessel was opened and the content was drawn with an 18G needle into a syringe and 1 mL of the suspension was injected with a needle through the rubber cap into a vial with degassed saline (dilution 1:10). The content of the vial was vortexed and the same process was repeated until we reached a dilution of 1:10,000. An aliquot (500 μL) of the diluted suspension was transferred to another tube and the stains (SYTO9 and PI) were added. The tube was placed in a 50 mL falcon which was inflated with N2 gas, closed with a screw cap and sealed with a parafilm. The sample was stored in this way until measured on a flow cytometer. The aerobic and anaerobic dilution scheme is shown in Figure 2.

Procedure for aerobic and anaerobic staining of FMT stool filtrate samples for the assessment of bacterial viability using flow cytometry.

Flow Cytometry

LIVE/DEADTM BacLightTM.Bacterial Viability Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), for microscopy and quantitative assays, cat.no. L7012, was used to determine the viability of bacteria in faecal samples according to manufacturer instructions [21]. Briefly, 500 of mixture, 0.75 of SYTO9 and 0.75 μL of propidium iodide (PI) were mixed together. The mixture was incubated for 15 min in the dark at room temperature and then subjected to analysis. The samples were measured within the 15 and 30 min after adding the fluorescent dyes. A total volume 100 μL of diluted sample was added to a flow cytometry analysis tube. All samples were analysed in triplicate on a 4‐laser BD LSR II flow cytometer with the excitation 488 and emission 525/50 nm (= 500–550 nm) for SyTO9 and 561 excitation and 610/20 nm emission (= 600–620 nm) for propidium iodide.

Cultivation of Stool Microbiota

A total of 13 FMT filtrates were cultured aerobically and anaerobically. Frozen FMT filtrates were thawed at 36°C for 10 min. Each sample (300 μL) was resuspended in 3 mL of 0.9% NaCl, diluted by decimal series up to final dilution 1:104 and inoculated onto blood agar for aerobic cultivation (24 h) and Schaedler agar for anaerobic cultivation (48 h). The number of colony‐forming units (CFUs) was counted and adjusted relative to 1 mL of the original FMT filtrate. All tests were performed in triplicates.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Mann–Whitney t‐test to identify statistically significant differences between different protocols (Prism v5.0, GraphPad). Descriptive statistics were calculated and reported as means and standard deviations. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Optimisation of Staining Procedure for Stool Samples

Kits designed for assessing bacterial viability are typically validated for in vitro‐grown bacterial cultures. Given that stool samples constitute a markedly different matrix, we tailored the testing protocol establishing a reliable strategy necessitated differentiating bacterial cells from sample debris and background noise. First, we established a baseline signal using a blank sample with deionised water and both fluorescent dyes to depict and quantify false‐positive events caused by noise, impurities and aggregates in assay reagents. Figure 3 shows the blank baseline of the ‘reagents only’ fingerprint where deionised water is in place of samples. We clearly show and quantify the fraction of false‐positive events being processed in each sample due to the presence of noise in the optical and electronic system, impurities in deionised water and aggregates in SYTO9 and PI reagents which are present in all samples at equal quantities. The gating strategy has already been established on the pure culture of E. coli allowing us to define four viability quadrants based on SYTO9 and PI fluorescence, categorising cells as dead, dying or damaged, viable or of unknown status [22, 23].

Baseline signal determination using a blank sample with deionised water and both fluorescent dyes.

To confirm the accuracy of this assay, we analysed mixtures of live (L) and heat‐treated (T) E. coli cultures. Heat treatment reduced the number of viable bacteria in the sample from 98% to 39%. In mixtures of different ratios, the results demonstrate a proportional increase in non‐viable cells with an increased proportion of heat‐treated bacteria, affirming the method's ability to distinguish between viable and non‐viable cells (Figure 4).

Evaluation of the Resolving Power of the Method on Stool Samples

Stool samples inherently contain heterogeneous, autofluorescent components, such as bile derivatives, not accounted for in the standard staining methods. Our main concern was the autofluorescence of non‐bacterial elements in the FITC channel, where SYTO9‐stained bacteria are determined. Since GF stool samples from humans are unobtainable, we used stool from germ‐free (GF) mice, devoid of bacteria and compared this with stool from conventionally bred (CONV) mice, containing both bacterial and non‐bacterial elements (Figure 5). In the stool of GF mice, we observed two orders of magnitude the lower number of fluorescent events than in the stool of CONV mice, most of them categorised as unknown, which could be attributed to non‐bacterial autofluorescence or staining artefacts. This strategy allowed us to confirm that the commercially available kit designed for different purposes (bacterial culture) works with sufficient resolution on different matrices, that is, stool.

Quantification of bacteria in faecal samples of GF and CONV mice. (A) and (B) Germ‐free mice; (C) and (D) Conventional mice and (E) Quantification of the signal (absolute counts).

The consistency of our method was evaluated also on human stool samples, comparing native and heat‐treated samples (Figure 6). The percentage of live bacterial cells was significantly reduced by heat treatment (53.5 ± 0.7 vs. 1.4 ± 0.1), while the percentage of dead bacteria increased accordingly (21.1 ± 0.8 vs. 68 ± 1.4) with a low coefficient of variance (CV) across measurements, indicating that flow cytometry is a reliable tool for assessing faecal transplant viability.

Each measurement was performed in five technical replicates. The lines represent the medians, the numbers indicate the median CV (%).

Impact of Staining Conditions on Bacterial Viability

A significant proportion of intestinal bacteria are anaerobic and preparing the sample for analysis (approximately 2 h) on the laboratory bench could reduce the viability of the anaerobic bacteria and significantly bias the results. To assess the effect of oxygen atmosphere during sample handling, we used two frozen aliquots from the same human donor and performed the staining procedure under both anaerobic and aerobic conditions. The number of viable bacteria decreased by 8% under aerobic compared to anaerobic conditions (49.6 ± 5.3 vs. 57.3 ± 5.5%). The percentage of dead cells (25.2 ± 7.8 vs. 13.1 ± 6.6%) and damaged cells (5.5 ± 2.3 vs. 8.0 ± 3.1%) was higher under aerobic conditions, but the percentage of an unknown material was higher under anaerobic conditions (20.4 ± 5.8 vs. 17.1 ± 3.2%) (Figure 7). None of these differences reached statistical significance. Overall, the aerobic processing of the stool sample leads to a slight decrease in the percentage of living cells compared to the anaerobic procedure. This fact should be taken into account when evaluating the results.

Quantification of the impact of aerobic and anaerobic laboratory staining procedures on the viability of bacteria in human FMT stool filtrate sample. (A) Anaerobic protocol; (B) Aerobic protocol and (C) Signal quantification.

Viability in Donor FMT Filtrates

FMT filtrates stored for a year at −80°C were analysed for bacterial viability using both in vitro cultivation and membrane integrity methods (Figure 8). The flow cytometry results showed higher estimates of bacteria in the same stool filtrate compared with the cultivation technique (Figure 9). By flow cytometry, the average viable cell count was 3.1 ± 1.2 × 1010 cells/mL of the stool filtrate. Using the cultivation technique, the average number of detected viable bacteria was 4.6 ± 4.6 × 108 and 5.9 ± 8.3 × 106 CFU/mL stool filtrate under anaerobic and aerobic conditions, respectively. Importantly, we observed significant intra‐sample variability, with an average CV of 27.1% (min 7.4; max 60.1%) for aerobic cultivation and 33.6% (min 12.4; max 58.1%) for anaerobic cultivation. Flow cytometry outperformed cultivation in consistency, with a markedly lower median CV for the fraction of viable cells in mL stool filtrate (average CV 6.4% for viable cells; min 0.19%; max 18.2%) (Figure 10).

Discussion

In our investigation, we sought a method to accurately assess bacterial viability within faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) suspensions. We optimised a commercially available kit, originally designed for pure bacterial cultures, to evaluate membrane integrity in stool samples. We have demonstrated that the resolving power of the LIVE/DEADTM BacLightTM. Bacterial Viability Kit applied to different samples than the manufacturer intended, such as stool filtrate, is well applicable with consistent results. The main finding of this study is that this approach yields more reliable data than standard cultivation methods, making it a preferable technique for evaluating the quality of the material for FMT according to bacterial viability assessment. Based on international guidelines, FMT is the recommended treatment option with a high cure rate in recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) [3, 4, 5, 24]. Its efficacy has been repeatedly confirmed in the administration by the traditional upper route (nasojejunal tube) or lower route (enema or colonoscopy), but also in the form of capsules, which hold great potential for routine use [2, 25]. Interestingly, the use of fresh, frozen or freeze‐dried lyophilised filtrate is equally effective [6, 23, 26, 27, 28, 29]. However, many times a positive clinical effect after FMT has been described in other indications such as hepatic encephalopathy, inflammatory bowel disease (especially ulcerative colitis), irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), etc., where the FMT method is so far limited to research settings or emergency use in the absence of alternative therapeutic options [6, 30, 31, 32]. Despite numerous stool banks and extensive research into FMT, standardised protocols remain elusive, with practices varying internationally. Some countries are moving to classify FMT filtrate as a pharmaceutical, prompting a push towards standardisation [18]. Stool, a biological substance, presents a complex challenge for characterisation due to its varied components [33, 34]. Recent literature shows that the effect of FMT is mediated by living bacteria but also by non‐living substances such as microbiota fragments, metabolites and probably many others [2, 33, 35]. Although there are studies emphasising the significance of diversity and quantity of live bacteria in FMT filtrate on its clinical effect [6, 14, 19, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40], consensus on a dependable method to quantify viable bacteria is lacking [12, 22, 23, 41, 42, 43].

Traditional cultivation methods are often considered the gold standard for assessing cell viability. Several studies have attempted to evaluate the number of viable bacteria in FMT filtrate using serial dilution and plating techniques followed by counting CFU per millilitre or gramme of filtrate [2, 11, 12, 22, 23, 44]. However, it is known that cultivation methods are not able to detect a substantial part of bacteria living in the gut, so it is evident that they do not guarantee that uncultivable bacteria are non‐viable and determining the quality of FMT filtrate based on the number of cultivable bacteria is therefore misleading [11, 41, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49].

In our experiment, we used a quantitative analysis of viable bacteria in FMT filtrate using a culture technique which showed a significant difference between aerobic and anaerobic conditions (p = 0.001) providing highly variable results.

Culture‐independent techniques, while bypassing culturability issues, have their own limitations [41]. Fluorescent staining for membrane integrity, typically performed with flow cytometry, is a commonly adopted culture‐independent method [50, 51, 52]. Nevertheless, its effectiveness can vary across different bacterial taxa and its application to the heterogeneous matrix of stool presents additional challenges [12, 22, 23, 43].

Although there have been several studies that have assessed the viability of bacteria in stool using live/dead tests by flow cytometry [22, 23, 53, 54], their results are not consistent and have not been validated. For this reason, we decided to investigate whether the method actually measures bacterial cells and whether it does not overestimate the results. For this purpose, we used faeces from germ‐free (GF) mice, which are sterile but contain heterogeneous components that could potentially affect the measurements. For comparison, we used the stool of conventional (CONV) mice. From a strict point of view, the stool of GF mice is not an absolutely perfect substitute for free‐of‐bacteria human stool as it potentially contains substrates which might be converted by microbes and is devoid of some microbial metabolites but still it is the best possible material available. By comparing the measurements, we found that the stool samples from GF mice give orders of magnitude lower signals than those from CONV mice with no significant autofluorescence. Based on these results we conclude, that the Live/Dead Viability kit could be used in combination with the stool matrix.

Since anaerobic bacteria make up the majority of faecal microbiota, they may die during the preparation of the FMT filtrate [12, 13] and the type of FMT filtrate processing, whether aerobic or anaerobic, could influence the viability results [12, 14, 15, 55, 56]. Therefore, we used two frozen aliquots from the same human donor and performed the staining procedure both under anaerobic or aerobic conditions. Our results show that the number of live bacteria decreased by approx. 8% under aerobic conditions compared with anaerobic conditions. This difference does not reach statistical significance, suggesting that aerobic conditions are suitable for routine viability assessment due to ease of manipulation. Nevertheless, aerobic manipulation with the stool sample results in a slight decrease in the percentage of live cells and this fact should be kept in mind when evaluating the results.

Since one of the main objectives of our study was to compare the determination of bacterial viability in FMT filtrate by culture methods and flow cytometry, we compared the absolute number of viable bacteria detected by both methods. In our hands, the quantitative outcomes from flow cytometry are higher than from cultivation methods. This could be explained by the different scopes of both methods—FCM detects all viable cells while the cultivation method is restricted only to bacteria, which could grow outside the gut. We believe that this is the main reason for the difference, but further studies are certainly needed, especially with regard to analysing the taxonomic composition of FC‐sorted fractions and bacterial colonies grown under aerobic and anaerobic conditions. Reproducibility is a crucial aspect of any laboratory method and our study presents a significant step in addressing the variability associated with cultivation‐dependent viability assessments. We observed high variability within samples using this method, with a mean CV of 30.3%. In contrast, the membrane integrity approach demonstrated remarkable consistency, with a mean CV of 6.4% for viable cells.

Some limitations of our study should be noted. The viable bacterial counts determined by flow cytometry and aerobic and anaerobic cultures are not complemented by data on specific bacterial species. Taxonomical analysis of bacterial microbiota in original stool filtrate and microbiota cultured under aerobic and anaerobic conditions based on some of the NGS methods is needed to understand the complex composition of viable microbiota in FMT filtrate. Furthermore, these data could produce additional correlations with the results of fluorescent dye assays. We are further aware of the fact that GF mice stool is not a perfect substitute for human stool free of bacteria for several reasons—mice and human diets differ significantly, GF stool is devoid of some important bacterial metabolites. Nevertheless, it is still the best available option for discrimination of potential autofluorescent contamination originating from faecal components and to our best knowledge, such a study had not been performed before. However, in this study, we did not compare mouse stool with human stool but evaluated the stool composition of two different groups of mice (GF and SPF).

It should be also mentioned that our results were determined on only 13 stool samples. Further validation of the accuracy of the method on larger sample volumes will be required.

Our research contributes to this field by being the first to compare two methods for assessing bacterial viability in faeces. We have developed and validated a flow cytometry‐based method for assessing bacterial viability in FMT filtrates, providing a reliable and efficient alternative to traditional cultivation methods. We have verified that bacterial viability in stool can be evaluated very accurately and with excellent reproducibility using cell integrity assessment combined with flow cytometry, unlike conventional culture‐based viability assessment, which yields inaccurate results. It highlights the suitability of the membrane integrity method for routine assessment of stool transplant quality, crucial for confirming the correct preparation protocol and the resulting clinical effect of the FMT filtrate. Standardising FMT preparations is of particular importance as FMT becomes increasingly recognised for its therapeutic potential and biobanks are expanding worldwide.

In conclusion, our findings recommend flow cytometry for evaluating bacterial viability in FMT, due to its accuracy and reliability over cultivation methods without the need for specialised equipment or extensive technical knowledge and could thus become suitable for routine assessment of transplanted stool quality. We advocate for its inclusion in the standard evaluation of FMT filtrate to determine the number of viable bacteria, enabling its use in routine practice at FMT centres and stool banks.
