New perspective on epigenetic modification and immunotherapy in endometrial cancer
Authors: Haoqian Zhang, Dandan Shen, Nanshan Lin, Li Yang, Chenchen Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is a heterogeneous gynecological malignancy. Epigenetic dysregulation promotes the initiation, progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance of EC. Aberrant DNA methylation, histone methylation, histone acetylation, and chromatin remodeling affect key EC-associated pathways and help shape distinct molecular subtypes, including POLE-mutated and mismatch repair-deficient tumours. In this review, we summarize the major epigenetic alterations implicated in EC, with emphasis on DNA methylation regulators, histone-modifying enzymes, and emerging epigenetic targets such as DNMTs, HDACs, LSD1, EZH2, PRMTs, and BET proteins. We further highlight the rapidly evolving immunotherapy landscape in EC, including clinically established or practice-changing regimens such as pembrolizumab-, dostarlimab-, durvalumab-, and lenvatinib-based strategies, particularly in advanced or recurrent disease. In parallel, we discuss investigational epigenetic therapies, including DNMT inhibitors, HDAC inhibitors, LSD1 inhibitors, EZH2-targeted agents, PRMT inhibitors, and BRD4-directed approaches, which are being explored to restore tumour suppressor pathways, enhance tumour immunogenicity, and improve sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade. By integrating EC-specific epigenetic mechanisms with current immunotherapy advances and ongoing combination strategies, this review provides a clinically oriented perspective on biomarker-guided precision treatment for EC.

Introduction

Endometrial Carcinoma (EC) is a gynecological malignancy originating from the endometrial epithelium, accounting for approximately 5.9% of all cancers in women [21, 70, 79]. Although 67% of the patients can be diagnosed at an early stage and the 5-year overall survival rate (OS) is relatively high (81%), the prognosis of metastatic disease is poor (with a 5-year survival rate of only 16%), and extrapelvic recurrence often occurs [58]. The survival outcomes for recurrent endometrial cancer vary significantly: the OS for pelvic recurrence is 55%, while the OS for extrapelvic recurrence is only 17% [8]. The prognosis is related to factors such as stage, molecular subtype, age and treatment interval. Although advances in early detection and molecular classification have improved risk stratification in EC, advanced and recurrent disease remains a major therapeutic challenge. Historically, chemotherapy was the main systemic treatment for advanced or recurrent endometrial cancer, underscoring the persistent unmet need for more effective therapeutic strategies [72]. More recently, however, the treatment landscape has evolved rapidly with the introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors. Early clinical benefit from immunotherapy was most evident in biomarker-selected subgroups, particularly MSI-H/dMMR tumours, but pivotal phase III trials have now shown that immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with carboplatin and paclitaxel can improve outcomes in the first-line treatment of primary advanced or recurrent endometrial cancer. In 2024, pembrolizumab plus carboplatin/paclitaxel followed by pembrolizumab maintenance was approved regardless of mismatch repair status on the basis of KEYNOTE-868/NRG-GY018, which demonstrated a progression-free survival benefit, although overall survival was immature at the time of approval. In parallel, durvalumab plus chemotherapy was approved for dMMR primary advanced or recurrent disease, and dostarlimab plus carboplatin/paclitaxel demonstrated a statistically significant overall survival benefit in the overall population in RUBY Part 1. Against this rapidly changing clinical background, there is growing interest in strategies that can further enhance immunotherapy responsiveness, among which epigenetic regulation has emerged as a particularly promising area of investigation.

The term “epigenetics” was coined by Conrad Waddington in 1942, referring to heritable changes in gene function that occur without altering the DNA sequence [88]. The advancements in molecular biology have defined epigenetics as a discipline that focuses on the dynamic changes in chromatin state. Its regulatory mechanisms consist of five aspects: DNA/histone/RNA modifications, chromatin remodeling, and non-coding RNA [160]. These processes dynamically activate or inhibit genomic segments in response to signals, and transfer regulatory information beyond the DNA sequence Epigenetic regulation involves three types of enzymes: writers, readers and remodelers. DNA methylation is negatively correlated with gene expression: regions with low methylation promote gene activity, while regions with high methylation inhibit gene activity. Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) exert epigenetic regulatory effects by binding to DNA, RNA, or proteins, altering chromatin structure or post-transcriptional processes. Non-coding RNAs fine-tune cellular pathways through interactions with proteins, RNA, or lipids, linking epigenetic changes to disease progression.

Immunotherapy serves as the therapeutic foundation for cancer by augmenting antitumour immunity. PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors block key tumour immune evasion pathways, reinvigorating antitumour T-cell function within the tumour microenvironment [106, 113]. The MSI-H/dMMR subtype of EC demonstrates enhanced responsiveness to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, underscoring its significant clinical utility [127].

Increasing evidence suggests that dysregulation of epigenetic regulatory factors plays a crucial role in the occurrence and development of EC [5, 49, 97]. However, currently, most of the epigenetic targeted therapies for endometrial cancer are still in the early (I/II stage) clinical trial phase. This review aims to bridge this gap by integrating the current latest understandings of the epigenetic mechanisms of endometrial cancer and the clinical trials of targeted drugs, and to envision its future therapeutic potential. Firstly, we provided an overview of the epigenetic regulation of endometrial cancer, and then delved into the approved epigenetic drugs. Next, we systematically reviewed the progress of immunotherapy, focusing on subtype-specific responses and emerging trends. Finally, we highlighted the ongoing clinical trials that integrate epigenetic and immunotherapy approaches, emphasizing their potential for transforming the prognosis of refractory EC.

Epigenetic modification and cancer

Chromatin is a polymer complex composed of DNA and histones, forming the structural and functional framework of the eukaryotic genome. The basic unit of chromatin, the nucleosome, is composed of histones (H2A, H2B, H3, H4) wrapped around the DNA [60, 150]. The covalent modifications of chromatin components, such as post-translational modifications of histones (PTM) and DNA methylation, dynamically regulate their functions and genetic characteristics [112, 135]. Epigenetics describes the process by which gene expression is regulated through mechanisms that do not rely on changes to the DNA sequence [92, 112]. The key epigenetic processes such as DNA methylation regulate chromatin structure through non-covalent interactions [23, 117]. These modifications form specific binding domains, recruit regulatory proteins, thereby enabling subsequent chromatin remodeling, and fine-tuning DNA-related processes such as transcription and replication [120]. Histones acquire diverse post-translational modifications at specific residues. Lysine residues undergo acetylation, ubiquitination, SUMOylation, and methylation. Methylation manifests as mono-, di-, or tri-methylation states. Additional modifications include arginine methylation or citrullination, proline isomerization, and phosphorylation. Phosphorylation occurs at serine, threonine, and tyrosine residues. Each type of modification has distinct biological effects [83]. For example, H3K27 trimethylation, also called H3K27me3, recruits polycomb repressive complexes. This induces chromatin compaction and concurrent transcriptional silencing through coordinated histone modification [7]. Methylation of arginine regulates chromatin accessibility by altering charge or recruiting recognition proteins. The functional consequences of post-translational modifications are context-dependent in both spatial and temporal dimensions. Identical chemical marks or modifications occurring during divergent cell cycle phases can yield diametrically opposed biological outcomes. Histone modifications regulate the higher-order structure of chromatin by recruiting non-histone proteins or by altering nucleosome stability [33]. Phosphorylation introduces negative charges, thereby disrupting the interaction between histones and DNA during mitosis and apoptosis, and coordinating chromatin remodeling during gametogenesis [147].

DNA methylation is of great significance in eukaryotes, mainly occurring on the cytosine residues within the 5′-CpG islands. In humans, over 80% of CpG sites are methylated, accounting for approximately 1% of all nucleotides [10, 62]. CpG island methylation plays a dual role in transcriptional regulation and tumour development: promoter methylation can silence tumour suppressor genes (such as CDKN2A), while genome-wide hypomethylation can promote genomic instability and activation of oncogenes [71]. DNA methylation induces gene silencing through two mechanisms: (1) blocking the interaction between transcription factors and DNA, and recruiting methyl-binding domain (MBD) proteins (such as MeCP2), which assemble into chromatin condensation complexes (such as Polycomb repressive complexes) [38]. These processes trigger the malignant transformation of cells by disrupting the normal pathways of cell differentiation and apoptosis.

Epigenetic modifications are dynamic and reversible processes that are regulated by specific enzymes. These enzymes can be classified into three functional categories [9]. The enzyme writes in the epigenetic marks by catalyzing the addition process [25]. Erasers remove these modifications to reset chromatin states [54]; Readers recognize specific markers, such as the binding of BRD to acetylated lysine, thereby recruiting downstream effector molecules. A deeper understanding of these enzymes has revealed their roles in disease mechanisms and therapeutic targeting, such as the application of FDA-approved HDAC inhibitors and BET inhibitors in tumour treatment [6, 77, 155].

Epigenetic dysregulation orchestrates malignant transformation by subverting six core cellular processes: aberrant proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, invasive capacity, metastatic dissemination, therapy resistance, and tumour heterogeneity evolution [24, 156].

One of the notable characteristics of cancer is the overall hypomethylation of DNA, which leads to genomic instability and activation of oncogenes, while also causing local hypermethylation of tumour suppressor gene promoters like CDKN2A [30, 98]. Aberrant methylation patterns emerge early during tumourigenesis, prominently affecting enhancer regions. These distal regulatory elements critically facilitate transcription factor and co-regulator binding. Their disruption dysregulates transcriptional networks essential for oncogenic progression [3, 40, 57].

Aberrant histone modifications drive cancer progression through multiple mechanisms. For example, the PML-RARA oncogenic fusion protein recruits histone deacetylases (HDACs), epigenetically silencing tumour suppressor genes through targeted deacetylation to drive acute promyelocytic leukemia pathogenesis [51, 86]. The overexpression of EZH2, which is responsible for the deposition of inhibitory H3K27me3 marks, is associated with poor prognosis in various cancers [76]. Reduced levels of activating histone mark correlate with clinically aggressive breast cancer subtypes. Concurrently, recurrent mutations in histone-modifying enzymes, particularly the KDM6A demethylase, drive oncogenesis through epigenetic deregulation [84, 123]. These epigenetic abnormalities, combined with DNA methylation defects, provide operable targets for treatment, such as the FDA-approved HDAC and EZH2 inhibitors.

Role of epigenetic modification in endometrial cancer

Endometrial cancer

EC is a common type of gynecological cancer. Its incidence rate is second only to that of cervical cancer, accounting for 20% to 30% of all gynecological cancers. In many high-income countries, it even surpasses that of cervical cancer. The main risk factors include age and obesity. Obesity promotes carcinogenesis through an inflammatory microenvironment, which is characterized by elevated levels of IL-6 and TNF-α and immune dysfunction [22, 89]. Postmenopausal progesterone deficiency can exacerbate the estrogen dominance caused by obesity, promoting malignant transformation through unopposed estrogen receptors [104]. Additionally, estrogen also overactivates the PI3K pathway, enhancing cancer cell proliferation and OS, thereby further increasing the risk of cancer [90]. Through genomic analysis of the genomes of 373 cases of EC, the TCGA identified four molecular subtypes: POLE hypermutated type, MSI-H type, copy number low (CN-L) type, and copy number high (CN-H) type [119]. This classification is helpful for diagnosing stratification and precise treatment, such as immunotherapy for MSI-H tumours or PARP inhibitors for the CN-H subtype [128]. Although surgery remains the preferred treatment option, for young patients who wish to preserve their fertility, or for those with other diseases, high surgical risks, or metastatic conditions, surgery has many limitations. Patients with advanced EC face a high mortality rate due to limited treatment options, highlighting the urgent need to develop new targeted therapies and combination treatment regimens.

In endometrial cancer, epigenetic dysregulation has been proven to be a key driver of tumour progression [65, 144]. The treatment for these abnormalities holds significant therapeutic potential, especially in cases of advanced and recurrent diseases where there is an urgent unmet clinical need. For instance, tumours with POLE mutations exhibit high mutability and may respond to immunotherapy; while the overexpression of EZH2 in high-risk subtypes can serve as a target, for example, using inhibitors such as tazemetostat. Combining epigenetic therapies with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) or PI3K-AKT pathway inhibitors may help overcome therapeutic resistance in advanced EC. Many of these regulators have been associated with more aggressive disease, immune escape, or reduced sensitivity to treatment. Examples such as EZH2, LSD1, HDACs, DNMTs, and m6A-related factors suggest that these alterations converge on pathways relevant to TP53, PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling, endocrine response, and tumour immunogenicity. DNA methylation in EC

DNA methylation is one of the earliest and most clinically relevant epigenetic alterations in endometrial cancer (EC). Rather than serving only as a general hallmark of malignancy, aberrant promoter methylation in EC directly affects key tumour suppressor genes and hormone-responsive pathways involved in tumour initiation, subtype stratification, and treatment response. Among these events, hypermethylation of the MLH1 promoter is particularly important, as it is frequently detected in atypical complex hyperplasia and EC and leads to loss of mismatch repair function and microsatellite instability (MSI) [28]. This alteration has clear clinical significance because it links epigenetic silencing to the dMMR/MSI-H phenotype, which is relevant to immune checkpoint blockade. In addition, elevated promoter methylation of ADCYAP1 and HAND2 in benign endometrial biopsies has been associated with an increased future risk of EC, supporting the use of DNA methylation as an early predictive biomarker [74, 85]. DNA methylation also contributes to treatment resistance through silencing of apoptosis-related genes and hormone-related pathways, including progesterone receptor B (PR-B), thereby promoting cell survival and loss of hormonal responsiveness [107, 111].

The methylation landscape in EC is shaped in part by abnormal activity of DNA methyltransferases. DNMT1 and DNMT3B are overexpressed in EC, although their expression patterns differ across histological subtypes, indicating subtype-specific methylation programmes [140]. Studies indicate that DNMT1 and DNMT3B promote the silencing of tumour suppressor genes through aberrant promoter methylation, thereby supporting tumour progression. Recent research highlights that the non-coding RNA FAM83H-AS1 recruits DNMT1 to the CDO1 promoter, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis and promoting tumour cell survival [129]. Importantly, these abnormalities may be reversible. The DNMT inhibitor RG108 can reactivate MLH1 through demethylation, restore mismatch repair activity, and induce apoptosis in EC cells [145]. Furthermore, the combined use of DNMT inhibitors and histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACis) has been shown to synergistically reactivate silenced tumour suppressor genes—such as APC—downregulate anti-apoptotic signalling, and enhance chemotherapy sensitivity; this supports DNMT-targeted therapies as a promising component within combination treatment strategies for EC [141, 148].

DNA demethylation pathways are also disrupted in EC. The TET family proteins, which catalyze the conversion of 5mC to 5hmC, show subtype- and context-dependent alterations: TET1 and TET2 are generally downregulated in EC, whereas TET3 is relatively increased. Reduced TET1/TET2 expression is associated with advanced FIGO stage, poor differentiation, lymph node metastasis, and shorter survival, while altered TET3 expression is linked to aggressive clinicopathological features [18]. Mechanistically, re-expression of TET1 can inhibit EC cell viability by increasing 5hmC levels in the APC promoter region and restoring APC expression. In addition, TET3 has been implicated in the regulation of EMT-related genes, including TWIST1 and ZEB1, suggesting a role in invasion and migration [17, 143]. TET1 and TET2 have also been implicated in the regulation of cancer stemness-associated factors such as NANOG, SOX2, and OCT4, which may contribute to progesterone resistance in EC [149]. TET2 expression has been reported to correlate positively with activated CD8+T cells and dendritic cells, suggesting that DNA demethylation status may influence the immune microenvironment in EC [126]. In this context, pharmacologic modulation of the TET/5hmC axis, including metformin-related effects on TET2 and 5hmC through AMPK signalling, may have therapeutic relevance.

DNA methylation readers promote transcriptional silencing in endometrial cancer. These proteins act synergistically with methylated DNA and chromatin regulators to maintain a repressive transcriptional state. This is unequivocally demonstrated by the silencing phenomenon observed in PR-B, where the binding of MeCP2 to the PR-B promoter results in the loss of progesterone responsiveness [16]. Furthermore, UHRF1 is capable of binding to PRMT5 to form a complex that promotes tumourigenesis. This UHRF1–PRMT5 complex enhances aberrant DNA methylation and chromatin remodeling processes in EC [115]. In summary, current findings indicate that DNA methylation in endometrial cancer is not merely a ubiquitous epigenetic mechanism. Rather, it is a clinically significant and potentially targetable process that regulates the expression of tumour suppressor genes, hormonal signalling, and chromatin states that promote tumourigenesis.

Histone methylation in EC

In EC, histone methylation not only alters chromatin marks but also remodels transcriptional programmes associated with tumour behaviour. Studies have confirmed that these changes can affect cellular proliferation, invasion, hormone-responsive growth, and resistance to treatment. Among currently known regulatory factors, EZH2 has been relatively well-studied and holds promise for broad clinical application. EZH2 is frequently overexpressed in EC and is associated with a poor prognosis. EZH2 serves as the catalytic core of the PRC2 complex, contributing to the establishment of a repressive chromatin state. This repressive chromatin state can suppress the expression of tumour suppressor genes. In EC, there is an interaction between EZH2 and DNMT3B, which can inhibit TCF3 (a transcriptional activator of p21) and promote cell proliferation [35]. This observation is particularly important because it connects histone methylation with dysregulated cell cycle progression mediated by the TP53/p21 axis. The TP53/p21 axis is a key regulator in high-risk EC, especially in tumours with extensive copy-number alterations or aberrant p53 signalling. Similar phenomena are observed among other regulators of histone methylation. Aberrant H3K9 methylation mediated by G9a/EHMT2 exhibits a more pronounced phenotype. Mechanistically, this is primarily established through the suppression of epithelial differentiation programmes, such as E-cadherin. NSD2 is an H3K36 methyltransferase that is also upregulated in endometrial carcinoma. High expression of NSD2 is associated with high tumour grade, myometrial invasion, and poor prognosis in endometrial carcinoma. Existing studies indicate that NSD2 plays a role in tumour progression and is linked to stem cell-associated characteristics. Furthermore, SETD8 catalyzes H4K20 methylation. By influencing the p53 pathway, SETD8 plays a role in the proliferation and anti-apoptotic mechanisms of EC. Overall, existing research findings indicate that the interpretation of histone methylation in EC differs from that in other malignancies; rather, it should be considered within the context of its specific molecular subtypes and tumour biology. It is inappropriate to treat it merely as a generalized epigenetic phenomenon.

LSD1/KDM1A is overexpressed in endometrial adenocarcinoma and has been linked to aggressive clinicopathological features, including high histological grade and lymph node metastasis [121]. Preclinical studies have shown that the use of HCI-2509 (an LSD1 inhibitor) reduces the survival rate of poorly differentiated type II endometrial cells. HCI-2509 also disrupts cell cycle progression and promotes apoptosis. Mechanistic studies showed that HCI-2509 increased histone methylation marks and upregulated LSD1 target genes such as HMOX1 and CDH1. The drug also disrupted cell-cycle progression, caused S-phase accumulation, and induced apoptosis. In an orthotopic KLE xenograft model, daily treatment with HCI-2509 led to tumour regression in 5 of 9 mice. These findings suggest that HCI-2509 has preliminary in vivo activity and acceptable tolerability. Overall, HCI-2509 appears to be a promising therapeutic candidate for aggressive EC. However, the current evidence is still early and needs further validation in more translational and patient-derived models [121]. Histone methylation is also tightly linked to estrogen-driven and PI3K-pathway-dependent EC biology. In β-estradiol (E2)-driven EC, E2 upregulates LSD1 expression, catalyzing H3K9me2 demethylation at the cyclin D1 promoter. This epigenetic reprogramming derepresses cyclin D1 transcription, modulating the GPR30/PI3K/AKT pathway to accelerate tumour cell proliferation. LSD1 inhibition may improve the efficacy of mTOR-targeted therapy in EC. This strategy is supported by the close link between epigenetic regulation and PI3K/Akt/mTOR signalling. In EC, mTOR inhibitors alone often show limited activity because they can induce compensatory Akt reactivation. KDM1A/LSD1 appears to contribute to this escape mechanism by regulating mTORC2- and Akt-related genes. Its inhibition increases repressive H3K9me2 at the mTORC2 promoter, weakens mTOR signalling, and blocks rapamycin-induced Akt feedback activation. Accordingly, combined KDM1A and mTOR inhibition shows synergistic anti-tumour effects, including reduced cell viability, survival, migration, and tumour growth in multiple preclinical EC models [125].

Arginine methyltransferases are emerging as additional EC-relevant regulators. PRMT3 promotes resistance to ferroptosis, whereas its inhibition destabilizes GPX4 mRNA through a METTL14- and m6A-dependent mechanism, thereby enhancing lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic cell death [131]. Importantly, PRMT3 inhibition has shown synergy with anti-PD-1 therapy in EC xenograft models, providing a proof-of-concept that histone/arginine methylation pathways can be exploited to improve immunotherapy efficacy. PRMT4, PRMT5, and PRMT6 have likewise been implicated in EC cell survival, migration, ERα signalling, and AKT/mTOR activation, although their immunologic relevance remains less well defined [47].

Overall, histone methylation in EC converges on a limited number of disease-relevant programmes: repression of tumour suppressor networks, enhancement of estrogen-dependent proliferation, facilitation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling, and promotion of aggressive phenotypes. Therapeutically, these findings support continued exploration of EZH2, LSD1, SETD8, and PRMTs as candidate targets in EC. Among these, EZH2- and LSD1-directed approaches appear especially attractive because they intersect with TP53-related cell-cycle control, endocrine responsiveness, and potential combination strategies with targeted therapy or immunotherapy. For POLE-mutated EC, direct evidence for a defining histone methylation lesion remains limited; however, given the highly inflamed phenotype of this subtype, it will be important to determine whether histone methylation modulators can further enhance antitumour immunity in this setting.

Histone acetylation in EC

In EC, histone acetylation is closely associated with hormone-responsive transcription, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway, and immune evasion. MOF (KAT8) is capable of acetylating H4K16. MOF appears to exert tumour-suppressive effects by activating transcriptional programmes that restrict proliferation. It may also modulate ERα-dependent signalling through receptor acetylation [139].

Among the histone deacetylases in EC, HDAC1, HDAC2, and HDAC3 are frequently overexpressed. The aberrant expression of HDAC1/2/3 is associated with unfavorable clinicopathological features. Currently, HDAC3 holds significant research potential in EC. HDAC3 can suppress STING transcription—thereby attenuating innate immune signalling and anti-tumour immune responses—through chromatin remodeling associated with ERα. Consistent with this view, apicidin inhibits the proliferation of EC cells and promotes apoptosis by downregulating HDAC3/4 [2]. This may be particularly significant within the current therapeutic landscape, as enhancing responsiveness to immunotherapy remains a critical clinical objective.

Histone deacetylation plays a significant role in the pathogenesis and progression of EC. The activation of the PTEN/AKT/mTOR signalling axis—a typical signalling pathway in EC—is highly correlated with histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6). HDAC6 is highly expressed in EC [159]. Given that PTEN loss plays a pivotal role in the biology of endometrial cancer, aberrant HDAC6 acetylation may elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying the core molecular vulnerabilities of this tumour type. Current research indicates that several serine/threonine kinase inhibitory proteins—such as SIRT1, SIRT2, and SIRT7—are associated with autophagy, stem cell properties, drug resistance, and the loss of PTEN stability [43, 44]. The only caveat is that the strength of the supporting evidence and the disease specificity associated with the various members of this family vary. Based on current research findings, a potentially more effective approach would be to focus attention on acetylation regulators associated with the PTEN/PI3K signalling pathway, hormone resistance, or immunosuppression. Providing a detailed description of every individual member of the HDAC family would not be a prudent choice.

Acetylation readers exert a certain degree of influence on the biological processes of EC. BRD4 is a member of the BET family that is overexpressed in EC and promotes oncogenic transcriptional programmes. In estrogen receptor-positive endometrial cancer, BRD4 binds to distal estrogen response elements marked by H3K27ac. Upon binding, BRD4 promotes the elongation of RNA polymerase II and enhances the production of ERα-dependent enhancer RNAs, ultimately driving estrogen-driven transcription and tumour proliferation [87]. We are highly interested in this mechanism, as it directly links chromatin acetylation to hormonal responsiveness in the endometrium. The application of the BRD4 inhibitor JQ1 inhibits the proliferation of EC cells. JQ1 primarily affects the c-Myc and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathways. Therefore, BET family proteins represent potential therapeutic targets for EC [95].

In summary, histone acetylation in EC is not merely an ordinary transcriptional mechanism. In EC, histone acetylation also serves as a regulatory factor for endocrine signal transduction, PTEN-related oncogenic pathways, and the interaction between tumours and the immune system. For example, histone deacetylase inhibitors can induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. Furthermore, histone deacetylase inhibitors can remodel the immune microenvironment via the HDAC3-STING axis, which serves as the theoretical basis for their combination with PD-1/PD-L1 blockers [13]. In addition, therapeutic approaches targeting BRD4 may disrupt estrogen-driven enhancer activity. BRD4 also attenuates proliferative signalling in estrogen receptor-positive endometrial cancer. Although it has not yet been definitively confirmed that the direct regulation of FGFR2 or POLE via acetylation constitutes a mechanism in EC. The broader chromatin environment surrounding these molecular subsets warrants further investigation.

m6A modification machinery in EC

The main m6A modifying enzymes that undergo changes in EC are METTL3 and METTL14. Reduced expression of METTL3 is associated with poor prognosis in EC and is also linked to decreased infiltration of CD8+T cells [50]. Overexpression of METTL3 can inhibit tumour proliferation/migration, enhance the activity of CD8+T cells in vitro and in vivo, and stabilize NLRC5 through a m6A-YTHDF2-dependent mechanism, thereby enhancing anti-tumour immunity [152]. Approximately 70% of endometrial cancers exhibit reduced global m6A methylation, often associated with reduced METTL3 expression or METTL14-related alterations, rather than a 70% prevalence of the METTL14 R298P mutation itself. Recent mechanistic studies suggest that the METTL14 R298P missense mutation can alter substrate preference and contribute to aberrant m6A deposition, thereby enabling oncogenic transcriptome reprogramming. The absence of METTL3/METTL14-mediated m6A deposition would make transcripts such as IGFBP4 unstable, exacerbating the progression of EC [81].

In EC, the main m6A erasure enzymes are FTO and ALKBH5. Overexpression of FTO is associated with a lower OS, while ALKBH5 stabilizes IGF1R mRNA and activates the oncogenic signalling pathway, thereby promoting the invasion of EC. Under hypoxic conditions, ALKBH5 works in synergy with HIF-1α/2α to maintain the stemness of endometrial cancer stem cells (ECSC) through SOX2 mRNA demethylation, and enhances their tumour-initiating ability [12, 118].

The main m6A reading proteins closely related to the pathogenesis of EC are YTHDF1 and YTHDF2. Their dysregulation can disrupt the stability of mRNA. The impaired translation of IGFBP4 driven by YTHDF1 will reduce the tumour suppressive effect [66]. The defect in PAPPA mRNA degradation mediated by YTHDF2 leads to an increase in PAPPA protein levels, thereby promoting the progression of EC [152].

Therapeutically targeting the METTL3/YTHDF2/NLRC5 axis augments antitumour immune surveillance, positioning this signalling pathway as a viable clinical target [137]. Meanwhile, the METTL14 mutation and the ALKBH5-HIF-driven pathway highlight the central role of m6A dysregulation in the occurrence of EC and immune evasion. Targeting these mechanisms may improve therapeutic efficacy by restoring m6A homeostasis and reactivating immune recognition.

Chromatin remodeling in EC

SMARCA2 (BRM) and SMARCA4 (BRG1) are ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling factors in the SWI/SNF complex. They regulate nucleosome positioning through DNA-stimulated ATPase activity. SMARCA2 drives chromatin remodeling, while SMARCA4 binds to acetylated histones (H3/H4) to activate transcription. These proteins are frequently absent in dedifferentiated endometrial carcinoma (DEC), which is an aggressive subtype characterized by a mixture of low-grade endometrial-like cells and undifferentiated cell components. In rare cases of poorly differentiated endometrial tumours, the absence of SMARCA4/2 is associated with high-grade histological features, treatment resistance, and poor prognosis [31, 105].

BRD7 is a chromatin remodeling regulatory factor that promotes the nuclear translocation of β-catenin by interacting with histone modifications. miR-200c inhibits BRD7, facilitating the shuttling of β-catenin from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, and upregulating oncogenes [96].

Immunotherapy in EC

Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) represents a critical class of targeted therapy and immunotherapy. ICB molecules were important in maintaining self-tolerance by mediating negative regulation of immune responses [136]. However, tumours exploit this mechanism by aberrantly regulating immune checkpoint expression in the tumour microenvironment, thereby evading antitumour immunity. As outlined in Table 2, PD-1/PD-L1- and CTLA-4-directed therapies currently represent the core immunotherapeutic strategies in EC, whereas additional checkpoints such as LAG-3, TIGIT, and VISTA remain largely investigational. PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 in EC

The main immune checkpoint targets in EC treatment include PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, while other immune checkpoints such as TIM-3 and LAG-3 have also gradually gained attention. According to TCGA, EC is classified into four molecular subtypes: POLE-mutated, MSI-H/dMMR, CN-L, and CN-H, each with distinct prognostic and therapeutic characteristics [82].

POLE-mutated and MSI-H/dMMR tumours exhibit a high tumour mutational burden (TMB), which drives neoantigen generation and enhances immunogenicity, making them particularly sensitive to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. Although PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 remain the principal immunotherapy targets in EC, emerging immune checkpoints such as TIM-3 and LAG-3 are under investigation to broaden the scope of immunotherapeutic benefit [124].

PD-1-directed therapies have shown significant efficacy in molecularly defined subtypes of EC. In biomarker-selected recurrent or advanced disease, pembrolizumab demonstrated durable activity in MSI-H/dMMR EC, while dostarlimab showed clinically meaningful antitumour activity in the dMMR cohort of GARNET. Retifanlimab also demonstrated notable efficacy in MSI-H/dMMR EC, with an objective response rate of 51.3% and a median duration of response not yet reached in POD1UM-101 cohort H [27, 59, 75, 94, 99]. More recently, immune checkpoint inhibitors have moved into the first-line treatment of primary advanced or recurrent EC in combination with carboplatin and paclitaxel. Pembrolizumab plus carboplatin/paclitaxel followed by pembrolizumab maintenance demonstrated progression-free survival benefit in KEYNOTE-868/NRG-GY018, leading to approval regardless of mismatch repair status, although overall survival remained immature at the time of reporting. In RUBY Part 1, dostarlimab plus carboplatin/paclitaxel showed marked benefit in dMMR/MSI-H tumours and a statistically significant overall survival benefit in the overall population. In DUO-E, durvalumab plus chemotherapy showed benefit in dMMR primary advanced or recurrent EC, whereas maintenance durvalumab plus olaparib should be regarded as an exploratory strategy rather than an established standard. In AtTEnd, atezolizumab plus carboplatin/paclitaxel showed clear benefit in dMMR tumours, whereas the final overall survival analysis in the all-comer population was not statistically significant [19]. In addition, pembrolizumab combined with lenvatinib significantly improved progression-free and overall survival in previously treated advanced EC, particularly expanding treatment options for pMMR/MSS disease.

PD-1/PD-L1-directed agents are central to EC immunotherapy. Pembrolizumab, approved in 2017, targets advanced MSI-H/dMMR EC. Dostarlimab received approval in 2021 for recurrent/advanced dMMR EC [26, 80]. Ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4), although approved in other MSI-H malignancies, does not hold an EC-specific approval and is under investigation in EC via PD-1/CTLA-4 dual blockade (e.g., NCT02982486, NCT05112601). Accordingly, combination therapies such as nivolumab plus ipilimumab (e.g., NCT02982486) and novel agents such as the PD-1/CTLA-4 bispecific antibody QL1706 (NCT06751277) should be regarded as investigational approaches in EC.

Emerging strategies in EC also include investigational dual checkpoint blockade and co-formulation approaches. Representative examples include nivolumab plus ipilimumab, botensilimab combined with balstilimab, and durvalumab with or without tremelimumab. Co-formulation approaches such as AI-061 further illustrate ongoing efforts to enhance therapeutic efficacy.

Other immunotherapy targets in EC

The immune checkpoint receptor LAG-3 suppresses T-cell activation. In EC, elevated PD-L1 and LAG-3 expression are strongly associated with MMRd and p53-mutant subtypes compared to MMR-proficient groups (P < 0.001), with LAG-3-positive tumours displaying higher histological grades (P < 0.001) [48]. Immunohistochemical markers such as PD-L1, VISTA, LAG-3, and GAL-3 may predict molecular features and clinical outcomes, aiding targeted therapy development.

LAG-3, TIGIT and VISTA are co-expressed in immune cells and are correlated with the density of CD8+ tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL). The high expression of LAG-3 and TIGIT is associated with an improvement in progression-free survival (PFS) (PFS: P = 0.03 for LAG-3; P = 0.01 for TIGIT), and is also associated with an improvement in OS (OS: P = 0.04 for LAG-3; P = 0.009 for TIGIT) [14]. Multivariate analysis indicated that TIGIT was a potential prognostic factor for OS [14]. In microsatellite stable (MSS) EC, PD-L1 positivity was associated with PD-1, CTLA-4, CD3, CD8, and LAG-3 (P < 0.001), among which LAG-3 was significantly correlated with PD-L1 expression [41].

The co-expression of PD-1 and TIGIT highlights the potential of dual immune checkpoint blockade as a promising therapeutic strategy [93]. Additionally, NY-ESO-1 demonstrates endogenous vaccine-like properties, eliciting robust humoral immunity that correlates with enhanced T-cell effector phenotypes and improved clinical outcomes [108].

PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 persist as primary immunotherapy targets in EC, whereas emerging biomarkers including LAG-3, TIGIT, and VISTA reflect immune microenvironment complexity and enable novel therapeutic strategies.

Epigenetic therapies and immunotherapies clinical trials in EC

Clinical trials exploring epigenetic therapies for gynecologic cancers, including EC-related studies, mainly focus on inhibitors targeting chromatin modifiers and rational combination strategies (Table 3). As shown in Table 3, most of these studies remain in early-phase development, reflecting that epigenetic therapy in EC is still at a translational rather than practice-changing stage. The investigated agents are concentrated in a relatively limited number of classes, particularly HDAC, LSD1, EZH2, and PRMT-directed compounds, either as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, or immune checkpoint blockade. Importantly, this pattern suggests that the future of epigenetic treatment in EC will likely depend less on single-agent activity and more on biomarker-guided combination approaches designed to overcome resistance and improve tumour immunogenicity. HDAC inhibitors include vorinostat (NCT03509207) for the treatment of metastatic uterine sarcoma, MS-275 (NCT00020579) for the treatment of refractory tumours, and EDO-S101 (NCT03345485), which is a HDAC inhibitor/alkylating agent mixture that can induce synergistic DNA damage. LSD1 inhibitors such as SP-2577 are used in combination with pembrolizumab (NCT04611139) to target rare ovarian cancer/EC with SWI/SNF mutations to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy. Other targets include EZH2 inhibitor CPI-0209 (NCT04104776) and PRMT5 inhibitor PF-06939999 used in combination with chemotherapy (NCT03854227). Current combination therapy strategies are gradually moving beyond single-agent epigenetic therapies. For instance, in the treatment of ovarian cancer characterized by HR features, olaparib is being used in combination with entinostat (NCT03924245); meanwhile, abemaciclib is being combined with palbociclib and fulvestrant (NCT04498520) to simultaneously target epigenetic regulation, cell cycle control, and estrogen signalling. Current experimental results indicate that the combination of epigenetic therapy and biomarker-driven combination therapy demonstrates promising efficacy against relapsed or refractory EC. The primary objective of early-stage trials was to assess the safety and feasibility of the treatment regimen. Conclusion and future perspectives

Current challenges in epigenetic drug development for EC

Current clinical trials investigating epigenetic drugs for endometrial carcinoma remain scarce and predominantly early-phase. Key challenges involve limited drug accessibility due to restricted investigational agent supply and recruitment barriers stemming from stringent molecular subtype requirements like dMMR or POLE-mutated status. While combination strategies pairing epigenetic inhibitors with chemotherapy or immune checkpoint inhibitors seek therapeutic synergy, non-specific targeting frequently causes toxicities including myelosuppression and gastrointestinal distress.

Future perspectives in EC

Novel delivery systems such as nanocarriers and antibody–drug conjugates represent promising approaches to enhance tumour specificity while minimizing systemic exposure. Future trials compellingly require biomarker-driven designs to refine patient selection and mitigate off-target risks.
