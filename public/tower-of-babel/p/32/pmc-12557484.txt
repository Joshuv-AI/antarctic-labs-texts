A bilingual Malay-English social media dataset for binary hate speech detection
Authors: Jun-Chen Tan, Lee-Yeng Ong, Meng-Chew Leow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, online hate speech has posed a growing threat to user safety, social harmony, and community cohesion especially on social media platforms. However, most existing hate speech datasets are monolingual and resource-rich, which leave Southeast Asia languages such as Malay underrepresented in natural language processing research. The aim of this dataset is to handle this gap by providing a balanced and quality-controlled resource that supports machine learning applications in multilingual settings. Binary classification is selected as a foundation task because it simplifies practical deployment in real-world and early-stage detection systems. It is beneficial in low-resource languages where detailed or multi-label annotations are always unavailable or inconsistent. This dataset presents 26,985 bilingual Malay-English social media texts curated from five public sources for binary hate speech detection. It combines human-annotated and filtered through controlled pseudo-labelling to retain only high-confidence, quality-controlled texts. The dataset is provided in UTF-8 encoded CSV format with 13,609 English and 13,376 Malay-language texts. Each entry includes the social media post, binary label (0 = non-hate, 1 = hate), language identifier (en or ms), and data source information. The dataset meets clear practical demands, including training multilingual transformer-based classifiers, benchmarking cross-lingual NLP models, and developing effective hate speech detection systems and educational NLP resources for English and Malay-speaking communities.Keywords: Hate speech detection, Malay-English bilingual data, Pseudo-labelling, Low-resource languages

Background

The dataset is collected in response to the growing need for high-quality, language-diverse resources for hate speech detection. Although English datasets such as HateXplain (∼20,000 texts annotated as hate, offensive, or normal) are abundant, Malay-language resources remain limited. Existing Malay-language datasets include (∼8000 texts) and Toxicity-Small (∼5000 texts), contain manually labelled hate and non-hate texts but suffer from class imbalance with non-hate underrepresented, and from noisy forum-derived texts. Since high-quality Malay-language non-hate texts are fewer than English coverage, additional unlabelled Malay-language sources include Snapshot and Supervised_sentiment, are used to enhance the non-hate class through controlled pseudo-labelling. Label scheme inconsistencies across datasets, such as multi-class annotations in HateXplain versus binary labels in HateM and Toxicity-Small, also affect direct integration and reinforce English bias. It has limited model transferability to Malay-language or code-switched contexts. Therefore, five publicly available datasets are consolidated, selected for complementary coverage across language, domain, and annotation style. All labels are standardised into a binary format (hate = 1, non-hate = 0). Language-specific preprocessing pipelines are applied (Ekphrasis for English; Malaya NLP, slang normalisation, and dictionary-based correction for Malay-language). The resulting balanced, language-tagged dataset supports bilingual benchmarking, cross-lingual transfer, and research on low-resource NLP, and semi-supervised learning [9].

Data Description

The dataset is provided as a single CSV file named bilingual_hatespeech_ms_en.csv, which contains 26,985 social media posts annotated for binary hate speech detection. In each row, a single cleaned and pre-processed text instance is provided in either English or Malay-language. The dataset combines annotated and pseudo-labelled content from five available sources: HateXplain [1], HateM [2], Toxicity-Small, Snapshot-Twitter-2022, and Supervised_sentiment. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The dataset is combined through a structured pipeline involving data acquisition, language-specific preprocessing, controlled pseudo-labelling, and dataset balancing. Five datasets are constructed to create the final dataset: HateXplain (English, labelled) [1], HateM (Malay, labelled) [2], Toxicity-Small (Malay, labelled), Snapshot-Twitter-2022 (Malay, unlabelled, and Supervised_sentiment (Malay, unlabelled).

1.Data Acquisition: All five source datasets are retrieved from public repositories in CSV or JSON format. The English dataset includes expert-labelled posts for hate/offensive/normal labels, which are binarised as hate (1) and non-hate (0). For Malay data, labelled datasets are included without modification, while unlabelled Malay-language datasets underwent controlled pseudo-labelling with language-specific models.

Data Acquisition: All five source datasets are retrieved from public repositories in CSV or JSON format. For Malay data, labelled datasets are included without modification, while unlabelled Malay-language datasets underwent controlled pseudo-labelling with language-specific models.

2.Pseudo-Labelling Technique: Pseudo-labels are applied for the unlabelled Malay-language sources with a controlled two-stage process that combined rule-based toxic-keyword safeguards with a model-based confidence thresholds technique. Mesolitica’s pre-trained NanoT5 sentiment and emotion models [3] are used to compute confidence scores for each sample. Since existing Malay datasets (HateM and Toxicity-Small) primarily supply hate-labelled texts, the non-hate class is underrepresented. Controlled pseudo-labelling therefore focused exclusively on expanding the Malay non-hate class, while avoiding the inclusion of high-risk pseudo-labelled hate texts.a.Malaya NLP Sentiment and Emotion Models: Each text is processed using Mesolitica’s pre-trained NanoT5 sentiment and emotion models [3]. Following previous practice in semi-supervised learning [10,11] and low-resource automatic labelling [3], strict cutoffs are applied.•Confidence score ≥ 0.85 for negative sentiment or anger emotion is labelled as hate (1) but not retained in the final dataset.•Confidence score ≤ 0.30 is labelled as non-hate (0).•0.30 < score < 0.85 is excluded to control uncertainty.b.Although both thresholds are calibrated, only the ≤ 0.30 non-hate labels are retained in the final dataset. The choice of 0.30 as the non-hate cutoff is motivated by the need to retain only extremely low-confidence negative/anger predictions, which empirical validation showed to align with human judgement, while excluding the majority of borderline and potentially noisy cases. The 0.85 hate cutoff is reported for completeness but deliberately excluded to avoid uncontrolled label noise.c.Keyword filtering and manual validation: A custom toxic-keyword lexicon of Malay-language (∼ 150 abusive terms) is used to prevent false non-hate assignments. Any non-hate text (≤ 0.30) containing lexicon terms is automatically excluded or flagged for manual review. After this filtering, a random sample of 200 retained texts is manually validated, with 0/200 errors observed. This corresponds to a conservative 95 % binomial confidence interval upper bound of ≤1.9 % potential mislabelling.Through this controlled pseudo-labelling technique by combining strict thresholding, toxic-lexicon filtering, and empirical validation, a total of 4540 non-hate pseudo-labelled texts are retained. These represent 16.8 % of the final dataset. The ≥ 0.85 hate boundary is reported for transparency but is not used for inclusion.

3.Language-Aware Preprocessing: Text preprocessing is applied separately for English and Malay-language. Deduplication is applied after cleaning to ensure only unique and high-quality texts are included.a.Malay-language: Malay-texts are processed using the Malaya NLP toolkit (version 5.1) for spelling correction, normalisation, and tokenisation. A custom dictionary which compiled from Reddit, blogs, and Malaysian Twitter is used to map informal tokens to canonical forms (e.g. dkt to dekat). A 200k English-Malay bilingual dictionary is used to translate embedded English terms to Malay base forms where appropriate. Probabilistic spelling correction from Malaya is applied conservatively to avoid over-normalisation. Regex rules remove non-ASCII characters, repeated punctuation, and placeholder spam. Quality filters remove samples with fewer than three words, more than 280 characters, only placeholders or emojis, or excessive placeholder tokens.b.English: English texts are cleaned using the Ekphrasis toolkit [7] with additional custom regular expressions. Ekphrasis’ SocialTokenizer is used to segment hashtags, contractions, and emoticons. Custom regex rules remove non-ASCII noise, compress repeated characters, and normalise common social-media abbreviations using the noslang dictionary (∼5400 texts). Placeholders are standardised to <user> and 〈url〉. Low-value texts (fewer than three words, emoji-only content, or excessive placeholders) are filtered.

5.To evaluate the effectiveness of the dataset, four multilingual transformer models including mBERT, XLM-R, InfoXLM, and DistilBERT are fine-tuned for binary hate speech detection. All models are implemented using the HuggingFace Transformers library with a PyTorch backend. Training is conducted on an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 3060 GPU, with random seeds fixed. For model evaluation, the dataset is divided into training, validation, and test sets using stratified sampling by label. Specifically, 20 % of the data is held out as a fixed test set, while the remaining 80 % is split into 71 % training and 9 % validation. This corresponds to an approximate 70/10/20 train/validation/test split. Early stopping with patience of 3 epochs is applied, and hyperparameters applied are summarised in Table 6.

Limitations

Several limitations are acknowledged in constructing this bilingual hate speech dataset. First, while the dataset includes annotated samples from HateXplain [1], HateM [2], and Toxicity-Small provide manually annotated texts, additional Malay-language texts are obtained through controlled pseudo-labelled of unlabelled sources like Snapshot and Supervised_sentiment using sentiment and emotion models. This process may still introduce bias or inaccuracies even under strict thresholds. To reduce label noise, only non-hate pseudo-labelled texts are retained. However, this design means that some hate expression from unlabelled sources may remain unrepresented. Second, although slang is compiled from online sources and prior research, some of the full diversity of context-specific slangs used in Malaysian social media may not be captured, which can affect coverage. Third, the dataset is limited to text-based social media posts and does not include multimedia content. This exclusion is a deliberate design choice to control scope and reduce potential confounding from multimodal signals for ensuring the pseudo-labelling framework remain consistent. Although efforts are made to balance language and label distributions, the dataset may still reflect domain-specific in the original sources, such as overrepresentation of certain topics, groups, or platforms.

Ethics Statement

In this work, data collected from available social media datasets and repositories are included. All datasets used are annotated and shared under open licenses or hosted on platforms like GitHub and Hugging Face. All social media texts have been fully anonymised during the preprocessing process with identifiable information such as usernames, URLs, and numerical identifiers replaced with placeholders. No attempt is made to re-identify users. The data collection and redistribution strictly comply with the terms of use and data redistribution policies of the respective platforms. No private or restricted-access data has been included in this dataset.
