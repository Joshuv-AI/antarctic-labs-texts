Evolution characteristics and driving factors of water use structure in typical urban agglomerations in China
Authors: Chenxu Guo, Xiaomeng Song, Jiyun Yao, Xiaojun Wang, Feng Chen, Hui Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the evolution of regional water use structures and their underlying drivers is essential for optimizing water resource management and promoting sustainable development at the regional level. This study investigates the three major urban agglomerations in China—Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH), the Yangtze River Delta (YRD), and the Pearl River Delta (PRD)—and analyzes data from 2014 to 2023 using information entropy, Lorenz curves, Gini coefficients, and grey relational analysis to examine the evolution of water use structure and its driving factors. The findings reveal that: (1) total water use remained stable in BTH, fluctuated upward in YRD, and slightly declined in PRD; (2) unconventional water resources (UWR) expanded substantially across all three regions, contributing to structural optimization, with agricultural and industrial water shares declining while domestic and ecological shares increased; (3) the grey relational analysis identified distinct dominant drivers across sectors and regions. In agriculture, the effective irrigated area and sown area for grain crops were the most influential; for industrial water use, wastewater discharge was predominant in BTH, the number of enterprises in YRD, and the proportion of the secondary industry in PRD; for domestic water, urbanization was the primary driver in BTH, per capita usage in YRD, and population growth in PRD; finally, for ecological water, UWR and reclaimed water were significant in BTH and PRD, but less so in YRD. These findings highlight the role of UWR in alleviating water scarcity and underscore the need for region–specific policies, as well as a more refined quantification of policy intensity in the future.

Introduction

Water use structure denotes the proportion of total water use (TWU) allocated to various sectors within a country or region during a specific period. It distinctly reflects patterns of water resource utilization and effectively captures the characteristics of water demand linked to socioeconomic development1,2. Analyzing the evolution of water use patterns powerfully reveals the dynamic relationship between regional socioeconomic development and water demand. This understanding is essential for developing robust strategies to tackle conflicts between water supply and demand3. Furthermore, assessing changes in water use structure enables an informed evaluation of water allocation efficiency, thereby supporting the formulation of management policies that effectively align socioeconomic growth with ecological protection4.

Previous studies have examined the evolution characteristics and driving mechanisms of regional water–use structures across various scales. Quantitative analyses have employed methods such as information entropy, the Gini coefficient, and Lorenz curves. For example, Sun et al.4 combined these with the Mann–Kendall test to reveal that the water–use structure in the Yellow River Basin became more balanced and diversified from 1998 to 2017, with a declining share of agricultural water use.; Wei et al.5 used these techniques to analyze the spatiotemporal evolution of water use structure and spatial matching of water resources, concluding that water resources matched well with population and precipitation but poorly with industrial output; Bao and Xu6 used information entropy and equilibrium degree to analyze evolutionary trends across 11 provinces (municipalities) in the Yangtze River Economic Belt; Chen et al.7 used these techniques to compare water–use structure across China’s seven regions, finding agricultural water evenly distributed, industrial and domestic water generally reasonable, but ecological water highly unequal with Gini coefficient greater than 0.4 in most areas. On the other hand, other research has concentrated on the factors that influence policy. Cazcarro et al.8 used input–output structural decomposition analysis (SDA) to quantify water consumption changes in Spain’s 1980–2007 economic transformation, finding that demand growth would have caused a large increase, but technology and intensity effects, mainly from shifts in agricultural crop structure, effectively offset it; Chen et al.9 used multi–regional Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI) decomposition model and information entropy to find economic development as the main positive driver of productive water use in Guangdong, water intensity and industrial structure as negative drivers, and population distribution positive in the Pearl River Delta (PRD) but negative elsewhere; Da Veiga et al.10 used multiple linear regression on 1746 Brazilian municipalities and found per capita water use is influenced by income, tariff, residents per household, temperature, washing machines, population, gender, piped water, and gross domestic product (GDP); Bao et al.11 applied information entropy, equilibrium degree, and principal component analysis to the Yangtze River Economic Belt from 2003 to 2020, finding that industrial output, GDP, water–saving irrigation area, and water use per 10,000 yuan GDP were the key drivers.

As China’s three most economically dynamic and densely populated core regions, the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH), the Yangtze River Delta (YRD), and the PRD play crucial roles in the national development strategy. However, spatial mismatches between their high–intensity economic activities and water resource endowments exacerbate regional water security challenges. The BTH region—containing merely 0.7% of China’s water resources—supports 5% of national arable land, 8% of the population, and 10% of GDP12. Severe resource–based water scarcity has resulted from ecological crises such as aquifer depletion and land subsidence brought on by chronic groundwater over–extraction and scarce and uneven precipitation. The YRD, despite its extensive river networks, experiences acute water scarcity due to pollution–induced water quality degradation. High economic development intensity and population density intensify pressure on water resources; per capita local water resources in the Taihu Lake Basin, for instance, are only one–fifth of the national average. Regional integration necessitates enhanced water allocation efficiency, making optimized water use structures imperative for supporting industrial upgrading and urbanization13. The PRD contends with both engineering–related and quality–induced water scarcity despite abundant precipitation, which exhibits spatiotemporal variability. Its concentration of manufacturing industries and export–oriented economy generates substantial water demand, requiring improved water use efficiency.

While existing research has enriched the theoretical foundation of water use structure, comparative analyses across multiple national–level urban agglomerations (UAs) remain limited, particularly in terms of systematically examining evolutionary patterns under varying development stages, resource endowments, and policy enforcements. Additionally, previous research has often emphasized single–dimensional socioeconomic or natural factors while overlooking the role of unconventional water resources (UWR) in structural optimization and spatial balance. Enhancing unconventional water utilization efficiency remains critical for addressing regional water constraints14. Therefore, this study focuses on the BTH, YRD, and PRD regions to (1) analyze the spatiotemporal evolution of their water use structures; (2) investigate how UWR optimizes these structures and mitigates spatial allocation imbalances; and (3) identify key drivers of water use structure changes and qualitatively describe their influence pathways based on policy analysis, quantitative data validation, and regional comparisons. This approach establishes a scientific basis for optimizing regional water allocation and promoting economic–ecological coordinated development.

Study area

As depicted in Fig. 1, the BTH, YRD, and PRD constitute China’s three principal UAs. The BTH region encompasses Beijing, Tianjin, and 11 prefecture–level cities in Hebei Province. It is the main economic center of northern China and covers 217,200 km2, or 2.26% of China’s land area, within the North China Plain. The YRD, known as China’s first world–class UA, is located in the lower Yangtze River plain and spans 358,000 km², or 3.73% of China’s total area, which serves as the country’s main economic hub. The PRD incorporates nine prefecture–level cities, including Guangzhou and Shenzhen, and contributes roughly 10% of China’s GDP, making it one of the most economically active areas in China. Although all are national–level UAs, the BTH, YRD, and PRD have distinct strategic goals and positioning due to their varying stages and patterns of socioeconomic development. The BTH region is in a late–industrialization transition phase, with de–industrialization in Beijing and Tianjin, industrial relocation to Hebei, and a shift toward a service–oriented, ecology–prioritized economy. Its urbanization rate of 61.3% in 2023 is the lowest among the three, but it has grown rapidly. The YRD is in a post–industrialization phase, with a highly developed manufacturing base, advanced services, and strong regional integration. Its urbanization rate reached 68.0% in 2023, and its economy is dominated by high–end manufacturing and modern services. The PRD is in an advanced industrialization phase, though some core cities like Guangzhou and Shenzhen have entered post–industrialization. Its urbanization rate of 75.4% in 2023 is the highest, driven by massive rural–urban migration and export–oriented manufacturing. These differences shape water demand: agricultural water share declines with economic transition, industrial water intensity decreases with technological upgrading, and domestic water demand rises with urbanization and income growth. Consequently, the effectiveness of water–related policies also varies across the three regions.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The water supply and use data in this study are derived from provincial (or municipal) administrative units within China’s three major UAs, primarily sourced from provincial Water Resources Bulletins, Statistical Yearbooks, and Water Resources Statistical Yearbooks. Water supply sources include surface water (SW), groundwater (GW), and UWR (e.g. reclaimed water, desalinated seawater, harvested rainwater, and brackish water). Water use sectors cover agricultural (AWU), industrial (IWU), domestic (DWU), and ecological water use (EWU). Here, total water supply (TWS) and total water use (TWU) are consistent in their statistical scope, encompassing both conventional and unconventional water sources. Generally, TWU represents water withdrawal, which is the water allocated to final users, including the water lost during delivery. DWU comprises residential and public water use (covering construction, commerce, catering, accommodation, and transportation). This statistical scope aligns with provincial water resources bulletins, ensuring data comparability and analytical consistency. Detailed data are presented in Table 1.

Methodology

Information entropy and equilibrium degree

Information entropy, proposed by Shannon15, is defined as the average information content after eliminating redundancy. It measures system stability, uncertainty, and information content while describing structural evolution in complex systems. This study applies it to analyze water use structures in the BTH, YRD, and PRD UAs, constructing an information entropy model to examine the evolution of these structures. A higher entropy value indicates a more balanced system when integrating agricultural, industrial, domestic, and ecological water use, thus reflecting the equilibrium level of various water uses.

Water use is categorized into four sectors: agricultural, industrial, domestic, and ecological. Within a specific period, if the proportion of each sub–item water use volume to the TWU is pi (i = 1, 2, 3, 4, ∑pi = 1, pi ≠ 0), then the information entropy H (nat) of the water use structure is:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H = - \sum\limits_{{i = 1}}^{4} {p_{i} } \ln p_{i}$$\end{document}

As society and the economy advance, water systems encompass diverse water use categories. To standardize entropy values and improve comparability in analyzing water use structures, the equilibrium degree J is introduced, where J∈(0, 1). A larger value of J indicates a more reasonable water use proportion for a specific water use type and a more stable water use system. When J is 1, the water use structure reaches an ideal state16. The calculation formula is:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J = H/H_{{{\mathrm{max}}}}$$\end{document}

where Hmax=ln n, which means that all calculation units have the same water use types, and the system structure reaches the most ordered state17.

Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient

The Lorenz curve was originally used to analyze wealth distribution inequality across periods or regions. Here, this method can be applied to evaluate the spatial equity of different water use sectors (agricultural, industrial, domestic, and ecological) among administrative units within a region. Taking AWU as an example, the Lorenz curve is constructed as follows. All administrative units are ranked in ascending order of their total AWU. The horizontal coordinate is the cumulative percentage of units, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{A}}_{\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{=}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{n}$$\end{document} (where i is the rank of the unit and n s the total number of units). The vertical coordinate is the cumulative percentage of AWU, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{B}}_{\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{=}{\sum }_{\mathrm{k}\mathrm{=1}}^{\mathrm{i}}{\mathrm{ω}}_{\mathrm{k}}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{W}$$\end{document} (where ωk is the water use of the k-th unit and W is the regional total). The resulting curve y=f(x) reflects spatial concentration. If the curve coincides with the line y=f(x), water use is perfectly evenly distributed among units; greater deviation indicates higher concentration and greater spatial imbalance.

To accurately quantify this unevenness, the Gini coefficient G (G∈[0,1]) is introduced. Originally a measure of income inequality, the Gini coefficient is fundamentally a measure of distributional evenness and has been widely applied in spatial equity analyses across water resources, energy, and environmental fields. In this study, the simplified formula based on Lorenz curve coordinates is adopted18.3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G = \sum\limits_{{i = 1}}^{{{\mathrm{n}} - 1}} {(A_{i} B_{{i + 1}} - A_{i+ 1} B_{i} )}$$\end{document}

where Ai and Bi stand for the cumulative percentage of units and the cumulative percentage of water use for the i–th unit, respectively, after ranking all provinces (or cities) by their total water use. Their meanings are consistent with those defined in the construction of the Lorenz curve.

This formula calculates the area between the Lorenz curve and the line of perfect equality, yielding a standardized index between 0 and 1. In water resources analysis, the Gini coefficient evaluates the allocation equity of a specific water use type among administrative units: a value closer to 0 indicates a more balanced distribution, while a value closer to 1 indicates greater concentration19. By calculating Gini coefficients for different water use types, the spatial allocation inequality can be quantified, revealing the effectiveness and optimization potential of regional water resource allocation policies. Grey relation analysis

The grey relational analysis method was proposed by Deng Julong20, which is a method for quantitative analysis of the dynamic development process of a system. This method describes the strength of relationships among factors through the order of grey relational degrees. Based on the data series of the factors, it employs mathematical approaches to investigate the geometric correspondence between indicators and measures the degree of correlation by analyzing and comparing the similarity of development trends among the indicators. This method overcomes the limitations of statistical techniques such as correlation analysis and PCA, which require large sample sizes or typical data distributions. It can work with a small number of samples without the need for specific distribution assumptions, thus offering stronger applicability.

To identify the main influencing factors of changes in water use structure, we apply a multivariate single‑series statistical analysis formula to calculate the grey relational coefficients and grey relational degrees between water consumption, the equilibrium degree of water use structure, and the influencing factors. In this study, following the above procedures, all driving factors were aggregated at the UA level. All sequences were normalized using the mean normalization method to eliminate dimensional effects and ensure comparability. For policy variables, dummy variables (0/1) were constructed to represent the implementation status of key policies, with the threshold set as the year of full implementation. For example, for the three major UAs, the “Water Ten Measures” policy was assigned a value of 1 from 2016 onward; tiered water pricing was assigned based on the first full year of implementation in each UA; the agricultural water pricing reform policy was assigned a value of 1 from 2016 onward; and the dummy variable for the ecological protection policy integrated the “Water Ten Measures” and the River Chief System, and was assigned a value of 1 from 2016 onward. This dummy variable approach follows established practices for assessing policy impacts in grey relational analysis.

Results

Changes of total water use

As shown in Fig. 2a, BTH’s TWU exhibited minimal growth over the past decade, increasing from 25.65 billion m³ in 2014 to 25.99 billion m³ in 2023 (0.15% average annual growth). Fluctuations remained within ± 1.5%, indicating a transition toward stock optimization. AWU—historically dominant—declined from 61.9 to 43.3% (3.4% average annual reduction), while IWU decreased from 13.62 to 9.09%, maintaining approximately 20% of agricultural use levels. Urbanization drove steady DWU growth (1.8% annually) from 4.64 to 5.46 billion m³. EWU surged by 22.0% annually, rising from 1.64 to 6.91 billion m³, with its share increasing from 6.41 to 26.6%. This shift reflects a transition from production–oriented to ecology–prioritized water allocation.

As shown in Fig. 2b, the YRD exhibited a distinct bimodal pattern in TWU: decreasing from 102.44 billion m³ in 2014 to 95.76 billion m³ in 2020 (1.16% annual reduction), then increasing to 108.80 billion m³ in 2023 (4.3% annual growth). AWU declined from 54.34 to 47.50 billion m³, reducing its share from 53.04% to 43.66% (1.47% annual reduction) while maintaining dominance. IWU maintained approximately a 30% share until 2020, then rebounded significantly to 37.26% in 2023. DWU’s share grew gradually from 18.07% to 20.99%. EWU increased by 10.4% annually (1.33–2.996 billion m³), largely driven by UWR adoption and implementation of the “Joint Protection Plan for the Ecological Environment in the YRD,” though its share remained below 3% throughout the period.

According to Fig. 2c, the PRD’s TWU gradually decreased from 17.63 billion m³ to 16.48 billion m³, or an annual reduction rate of 0.7%, with variations between ± 2.1%. Both AWU and IWU decreased as a result of the adoption of water-saving technologies and the use of UWR. In particular, the percentage of AWU decreased by 3.0% annually, from 26.5% in 2014 to 21.18% in 2023. Similarly, the IWU share decreased at a rate of 3.6% per year, from 43.52% to 33.01%. DWU, on the other hand, grew from 4.97 billion m³ to 6.52 billion m³, and its percentage increased from 28.19 to 39.56%, making it the sector that uses the most water. Despite having a relatively small base at the beginning, EWU expanded at a 13.8% annual rate, going from 0.32 billion m³ to 1.31 billion m³, and its share increased from 1.79 to 6.25%.

Utilization status of unconventional water resources

UWR differ from conventional surface and groundwater resources in their renewable nature and potential for reuse after appropriate treatment. These include harvested rainwater, reclaimed water (treated municipal and industrial wastewater), desalinated water, mine drainage water, and brackish groundwater21. As shown in Fig. 3, UWR utilization in the BTH grew by 138%, from 1.537 billion m3 in 2014 to 3.655 billion m3 in 2023, with its share of TWS increasing 5.99% to 14.06%. In the YRD, UWR utilization increased from 2.87 million m³ to 28.73 billion m³ over the same period, with its share rising from 0.27 to 2.64%. A notable surge occurred in 2016, when UWR utilization grew by 238.6%, and the growth rate of UWR share exceeded that of total water use. In the PRD, UWR utilization increased from 106 million m³ to 1.07 billion m³, with its share rising from 0.48% to 4.92%, and a sharp increase of 204% occurred in 2022. Across the three agglomerations, UWR types exhibit distinct spatial distributions. In the BTH, reclaimed water dominates in Beijing, while Tianjin supplements reclaimed water with desalinated seawater, and Hebei utilizes a mix of reclaimed water, brackish water, and rainwater harvesting22. In the YRD, Jiangsu Province contributes over half of the regional UWR, primarily from reclaimed water projects in the Taihu Lake Basin; Zhejiang and Anhui also show increasing UWR usage. In the PRD, reclaimed water is the main source in core cities, supplemented by desalinated seawater and rainwater harvesting. Despite the rapid growth, UWR remains a small fraction of the total water supply in the YRD and PRD, indicating substantial room for further expansion.

Spatial-temporal evolution analysis of water use structure

Spatial evolution of water use structure

We select 2014, 2017, 2020, and 2023 as representative years to analyze the spatial evolution of water use structures within three major UAs. These years were chosen for specific analytical reasons: 2014 serves as the baseline, while 2023 offers the most recent data. This temporal span captures long–term trends. 2017 reveals initial impacts of China’s “Water Ten Measures” policy. 2020 concludes the 13th Five–Year Plan and coincides with COVID–19 disruptions. This makes it useful for analyzing emergency–driven structural disturbances. The selection uses roughly three–year intervals. This balances data density and trend identifiability. Lorenz curves were plotted and Gini coefficients calculated for each sector (Figs. 4, 5 and 6).

In the BTH, Lorenz curves for distinct water use sectors exhibit divergent spatial patterns. With a consistent interannual curve overlap, AWU specifically shows the largest departure from the ideal equilibrium line, indicating deeply ingrained spatial disparities and substantial interprovincial or municipal inequality. IWU curves, while closer to equality than agricultural ones, maintain high temporal persistence, implying unresolved spatial heterogeneity and incomplete regional equilibrium. With steady trajectories and constant proximity to equality, DWU curves exhibit minimal inter–jurisdictional variation and long–term distributional balance. For EWU, the 2014 curve was closer to equality than that of 2023; despite substantial volumetric growth, increased spatial concentration led to wider allocation disparities by 2023.

AWU Lorenz curves in the YRD exhibit long–term significant spatial disparities as well as clear inter–provincial and municipal utilization gaps, despite being far from the optimal equilibrium line with high annual overlap. IWU curves stayed stably close in the first three years but deviated significantly from the line in 2023, indicating increased recent spatial concentration and widened inter–regional utilization differences. DWU curves, always closest to the line with stable form, reflect long–term balanced spatial distribution and minor inter–entity differences. EWU curves exhibit enduring long–term spatial disparities and unequal utilization, despite generally deviating significantly from the line with only minor annual fluctuations.

In the PRD, AWU annual curves show phased evolution: deviating significantly early but gradually approaching the optimal equilibrium line later, while IWU curves show the reverse—relatively balanced early but deviating significantly later, with high overlap between the two. IWU curves show a stronger agglomeration of high-use areas and widening inter-provincial and municipal utilization intensity gaps over time, while AWU curves show a shift from “concentrated high-intensity use” to “region-wide balance” due to a decline in high-use area concentration and an increase in low-use areas. DWU curves stay consistently close to the line with a stable annual shape, reflecting long-term balanced distribution and minor inter-entity differences. 2023’s EWU curve is notably closer to the line than 2014’s; though overall deviation remains large, it shifts from centralized supply to more balanced distribution.

Temporal changes of water use structure

Based on changes in AWU, IWU, DWU, and EWU in three major UAs from 2014 to 2023, their respective values and TWU were substituted into Eqs. (1) and (2) to analyze changes in information entropy and the equilibrium degree of each agglomeration. The results for the three UAs are shown in Fig. 7. In the BTH region, the information entropy of the water use structure exhibits an initial period of continuous growth followed by stabilization. Values increased from 1.054 (entropy) and 0.760 (equilibrium degree) in 2014 to 1.271 and 0.917 in 2021, representing a cumulative increase of 0.206 in entropy over this period. Subsequently, values fluctuated slightly to 1.260 and 0.909 by 2023. According to information entropy theory, this trend reflects a decreasing reliance on single water use categories. Increased EWU, coupled with reductions in AWU and IWU, enhanced the system’s balance and stability. YRD entropy grew gradually, rising from 1.038 to 1.124 and from 0.749 to 0.811 between 2014 and 2023, respectively (cumulative entropy growth: 0.084). The structure is still dominated by IWU and AWU, with a comparatively small amount going to EWU, despite the fact that this suggests improved systemic balance. PRD also showed increases in entropy and equilibrium degree, rising from 1.143 to 0.825 in 2014 to 1.235 and 0.891 in 2023 (cumulative entropy growth: 0.092). This indicates less inequality in sectoral proportions and more diversity and balance in the water use structure. Nevertheless, the proportion of water allocated for ecological uses remains low.

Overall, the three UAs exhibit increasing information entropy and equilibrium degree in their water use structures, reflecting enhanced systemic balance and stability. This positive trend is attributed to regional water resource management policies, economic development drivers, and notably, the application of UWR. Particularly through its utilization in ecological and industrial sectors, UWR has increased water source diversity while reducing dependence on single water sources.

The evolution of IWU is jointly determined by the scale expansion effect and the structural optimization effect, which exhibit a trade–off relationship. Due to differences in industrial development stages, industrial composition, and regulatory systems, the three major UAs have followed distinct dominant pathways.

In the BTH, the primary driving factor of IWU is the industrial wastewater discharge. The highly synchronized variation between this variable and IWU confirms the region’s policy orientation of “controlling water withdrawal through pollution discharge regulation.” The BTH region has long implemented a stringent linkage policy between industrial water conservation and pollution discharge permitting, restricting high water–consuming and high–emission industries, and imposing double resource taxes on groundwater extraction. Under this regulatory system, the volume of industrial wastewater discharge has become a comprehensive proxy for changes in IWU. The output values of light and heavy industries, as well as the number of industrial enterprises, also maintain high correlation degrees, indicating that comprehensive adjustments in industrial scale and structure play an important role.

The YRD, in contrast, exhibits a distinct scale–driven pattern. The grey correlation degree of the number of industrial enterprises ranks first, followed by the output values of heavy and light industries. After 2019, the number of industrial enterprises in this region increased significantly, accompanied by a corresponding rebound in total IWU during the same period. This phenomenon does not indicate a failure of water conservation policies; rather, it reveals that the YRD has a highly active industrial economy with strong water demand from emerging industries, and the scale effect partially offsets the water–saving achievements per unit of output value. Compared with the BTH, the correlation degree of industrial wastewater discharge is notably lower in the YRD, suggesting that the direct linkage between pollution control and water withdrawal is weaker than in the north.

The PRD region represents a structure–optimization–dominant type. The grey correlation degree of the share of secondary industry added value is the highest, followed by IWU per ten thousand yuan of value added and the output value of the light industry. This indicates that the primary drivers of the decline in IWU in the PRD are industrial structure upgrading and rapid improvements in water use efficiency. Guangdong Province has explicitly stipulated that the average annual growth rate of IWU shall not exceed 1%, and has promoted dual–pipe reclaimed water systems in some high–tech industrial parks. Furthermore, the correlation degree of the number of industrial enterprises in the PRD is relatively low, indicating that despite an increase in the number of enterprises, the decline in the share of high water–consuming industries and the reduction in water withdrawal intensity per unit have jointly achieved a decoupling of total IWU from enterprise growth.

Discussion

Analysis of unconventional water resources utilization patterns

The rapid increase in UWR utilization has become a notable feature of water supply transformation in China’s three major UAs. However, the role of UWR differs substantially across regions due to differences in water scarcity, policy enforcement, and economic feasibility. In the BTH region, UWR accounted for 14.06% of TWS by 2023, the highest among the three agglomerations. This reflects severe resource–based water scarcity and strong mandatory policies, such as Beijing’s regulation prohibiting tap water for landscaping and the Baiyangdian Lake replenishment program that supplies 200 million m³/year of reclaimed water. UWR has shifted from an emergency measure to a strategic supply component. In contrast, the YRD and the PRD exhibit much lower UWR shares (2.64% and 4.92% in 2023, respectively), despite a tenfold increase in absolute volume in the YRD. This suggests that in water–abundant but quality–constrained regions, UWR currently plays a supplementary role, mainly allocated to industrial cooling and ecological replenishment (e.g., Taihu Lake) rather than fundamentally altering the supply structure. The relatively high correlation between UWR and ecological water use in the PRD further supports this functional differentiation. These findings highlight that UWR does not contribute equally to all water use sectors. Its strongest impact is observed in EWU and IWU, whereas its direct substitution for AWU and DWU remains limited, a pattern that holds across all three UAs. Therefore, while UWR has alleviated local water scarcity and increased water supply diversity, its strategic role must be evaluated within the context of water resource endowment, policy framework, and economic structure for each region.

Innovations and comparisons

This study investigates the water use structures of China’s three major UAs (BTH, YRD, and PRD). By systematically analyzing their evolutionary characteristics, spatial equilibrium, and driving mechanisms from 2014 to 2023, this research offers several key innovations relative to the existing literature. In terms of research scope, existing studies have predominantly focused on individual provinces or river basins, such as Manas River Basin5, and the Yellow River Basin11, or provincial–level units like the Yangtze River Economic Belt6 and Guangdong Province9. Few studies have conducted horizontal comparisons across national-level UAs. This study incorporates the three major UAs, BTH, YRD, and PRD, into a unified analytical framework, revealing their similarities and differences in TWU, structural evolution, and spatial equilibrium. For instance, TWU in BTH remained relatively stable while shifting toward an ecology–prioritized pattern; the YRD exhibited a bimodal fluctuation; and the PRD showed a continuous, slight decline. These differences reflect divergent evolutionary pathways shaped by varying development stages, resource endowments, and policy orientations, a finding that is difficult to obtain from single–region studies.

This study also highlights the strategic role of UWR in structural optimization, an aspect that has received limited attention in previous research. In prior studies, UWR has often been treated merely as a supplement to conventional sources. By comparing the utilization scale, proportional structure, and substitution patterns of UWR across the three UAs, we show that UWR contributes substantially to alleviating water scarcity and improving system balance, particularly in water–scarce BTH. However, its impact is sector–specific and region–dependent, being strongest in EWU and IWU but limited in the agricultural and domestic sectors.

Furthermore, the application of grey relational analysis in this study quantitatively identifies the dominant drivers of sectoral water–use changes, overcoming the limitation of purely qualitative descriptions. The results reveal distinct regional driving patterns. AWU is most strongly correlated with effective irrigated area and grain crop sown area. IWU is driven by wastewater discharge in BTH, by enterprise numbers in YRD, and by structural optimization in PRD. DWU is driven by urbanization in BTH, per capita water use in YRD, and population inflow in PRD. EWU depends primarily on UWR and reclaimed water in BTH and PRD, while correlations are weaker in YRD. These cross–regional comparisons provide a more nuanced understanding of how region-specific contexts mediate the relationship between socioeconomic development and water demand, extending beyond the findings of previous single-region studies.

Limitations and future research

Several limitations merit consideration in this analysis. First, the temporal scope (2014–2023) encompasses only a decade, which is inadequate for capturing long–term evolutionary trends. Incorporating earlier datasets would provide clearer insights into distinguishing cyclical fluctuations from structural changes. Second, while utilizing prefecture-level city data highlights inter–city disparities within agglomerations, it falls short of revealing intra–city or sector–specific heterogeneities. Leveraging finer–scale data, such as county–level or industrial park–level information, would facilitate more precise policy recommendations. Third, due to data constraints, we only quantify total UWR use and its share, but cannot reveal its sectoral allocation or utilization efficiency. This data gap prevents direct application of LMDI decomposition; using aggregate data as a proxy would cause severe bias. Conducting case studies in representative cities, such as Beijing for reclaimed water and Tianjin for desalination, is necessary to evaluate cost–effectiveness and substitution potential. Most critically, quantifying policy variables through binary indicators (0 and 1) based solely on implementation timing presents a substantial methodological simplification. This metric fails to capture the nuances of policy intensity, duration of coverage, or cumulative effects. Moreover, decomposition methods such as LMDI require all driving factors to be continuous numerical variables; quantifying policy effects continuously (e.g., actual water pricing levels, subsidy intensity) would require specialized surveys and policy text mining, which are currently unavailable. Consequently, the lower grey relational degrees observed for policy dummies—such as the agricultural water pricing reform in the BTH region, r = 0.6167—should be interpreted with caution, as they may indicate measurement limitations rather than a lack of policy effectiveness. Future research must adopt more sophisticated policy quantification methods, such as text–based indices of policy strength (e.g., PMC index models) or cumulative exposure variables. Furthermore, it is essential to note that grey relational analysis identifies statistical correlations rather than causal relationships. Thus, the causal interpretations provided in Sect. 4.4 are qualitative in nature. Future studies should utilize decomposition methods, such as the SDA8 or LMDI9, to rigorously attribute changes in water–use structure to specific driving factors.

Conclusions

This study employs information entropy, equilibrium degree, Lorenz curves, and Gini coefficients to compare water–use structures in the BTH, YRD, and PRD, and to examine the role of UWR in sectoral water–use changes. Using grey relational analysis, this study quantitatively identifies the dominant drivers of agricultural, industrial, domestic, and ecological water use. The main conclusions are as follows:

TWU remained stable in BTH, fluctuated upward in YRD, and slightly declined in PRD. UWR expanded significantly in all three UAs, contributing to structural optimization—agricultural and industrial water shares declined while domestic and ecological shares increased, and information entropy improved. Grey relational analysis identified dominant drivers: for agricultural water, effective irrigated area and grain crop sown area were the most influential factors; for industrial water, wastewater discharge dominated in BTH, enterprise numbers in YRD, and secondary industry proportion in PRD; for domestic water, urbanization drove BTH, per capita use drove YRD, and population growth drove PRD; for ecological water, UWR and reclaimed water were the core drivers in BTH and PRD, while correlations were weaker in YRD.

Overall, UWR has played a positive role in alleviating water scarcity and optimizing water use structures, but significant spatial disparities persist, particularly in AWU and EWU. To further improve water use efficiency and spatial equity, future efforts should strengthen the strategic substitution role of UWR, promote cross–regional water rights trading and spatial coordination, and develop a water security system integrating technological substitution, policy regulation, and spatial coordination. More refined policy quantification methods are needed to better capture the causal effects of policies on water use structure evolution.
