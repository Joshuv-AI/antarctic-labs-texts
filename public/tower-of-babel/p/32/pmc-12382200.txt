A Multi-marker model based on serum IL-10 predicts response to conversion immunochemotherapy in gastric cancer patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xingzhou Wang, Hang Jiang, Mengjie Liang, Daming Cai, Shichao Ai, Qiongyuan Hu, Feng Wang, Feng Sun, Xuefeng Xia, Peng Song, Liang Tao, Song Liu, Meng Wang, Xiaofeng Lu, Wenxian Guan, Xiaofei Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConversion immunochemotherapy may enable curative surgery in patients with initially unresectable locally advanced gastric cancer, but its response rate remains low. Little evidence has shown how to easily predict therapeutic efficacy from hematological biomarkers, apart from tissue biomarkers. This study aimed to identify predictive factors for treatment response from hematological biomarkers and propose strategies for non-responsive patients.MethodsClinical and laboratory data from 153 PD-L1 negative and microsatellite stable advanced gastric cancer patients treated with conversion immunochemotherapy followed by radical gastrectomy were retrospectively analyzed. Correlations between key indicators and treatment outcomes were assessed, and a nomogram was developed to predict tumor regression grade (TRG).ResultsTRG was strongly associated with the prognosis of advanced gastric cancer treated with conversion immunochemotherapy. Factors significantly related to TRG included hypertension, pre-treatment serum albumin levels, tumor size, post-treatment serum IL-10, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and changes in tumor-associated carbohydrate antigens before and after treatment. Hypertension, pre-treatment serum albumin levels, post-treatment IL-10 levels, and restoration in tumor-associated carbohydrate antigens were identified as independent predictors of TRG.ConclusionDynamic monitoring of serum albumin, IL-10, and tumor-associated carbohydrate antigens can help assess treatment response and identify patients sensitive to conversion immunochemotherapy. For non-responsive patients, strategies such as the associations observed require validation in prospective interventional trials before specific interventions can be recommended.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Conversion immunochemotherapy, Prediction model, Hypertension, Serum albumin, IL-10, Carbohydrate antigen

Introduction

Gastric cancer is one of the major health threats in East Asia, especially in China [1, 2]. At diagnosis, some patients are already in locally advanced stages, which enabling these patients requiring preoperative conversion therapy to downstage the disease and create an opportunity for curative surgery [3]. In recent years, immunotherapy has become a frontline choice in conversion treatment for gastric cancer. For patients with advanced gastric cancer, particularly those with PD-L1 CPS ≥ 5, the recently published Checkmate-649 study demonstrated a significant improvement in the 5-year overall survival rate with nivolumab plus chemotherapy [4]. Additionally, various guidelines recommend the use of conversion immunotherapy in combination with chemotherapy as a first-line treatment option for HER2-negative metastatic gastric cancer with PD-L1 CPS ≥ 5 [3, 5]. Currently, the evaluation of conversion therapy effectiveness primarily relies on imaging assessments, using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) criteria to gauge disease progression and determine eligibility for curative surgery [6]. However, emerging evidences have shown that RECIST criteria do not predict tumor pathological regression and are also inadequate in evaluating patient prognosis [7, 8]. Additionally, due to radiation dose limitations, continuous imaging evaluation during treatment is challenging. Between imaging assessment points, patients often face the decision of continuing immunochemotherapy or undergoing surgery, with the former risking further disease progression and missing the optimal surgical window. At present, there is a lack of a specific, sensitive, and easy-to-use evaluation model to help clinicians continuously monitor disease changes following conversion immunochemotherapy.

Numerous studies have explored biomarkers related to the response in gastric cancer patients undergoing neoadjuvant or conversion therapy. For instance, elevated levels of carbohydrate antigen CEA and CA199 before neoadjuvant chemotherapy may be associated with poorer prognosis in gastric cancer patients [9]. Besides, increased serum IL-6 concentrations indicate a better response to conversion chemotherapy with the FLOT regimen [10]. Systemic inflammation markers, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), are also considered predictors of gastric cancer response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy [11]. However, these studies’ limitations lie in the absence of immunotherapy considerations. Anti-PD-1 therapy has shown higher pathological response rates in patients with PD-L1 CPS scores ≥ 5 [12–14], but this single indicator’s predictive value for gastric cancer response to conversion immunochemotherapy remains limited. Furthermore, for patients with microsatellite stability (MSS), there is currently no solid evidence from evidence-based medicine indicating whether they can benefit from conversion immunochemotherapy. Additionally, research identifying potential beneficiary subgroups among these patients is lacking. Thus, there is an urgent need to establish a straightforward clinical prediction model to better assess the benefits of immunochemotherapy.

Herein, we plan to conduct a retrospective study, comprehensively including baseline data and various examination results from gastric cancer patients before and after receiving conversion immunochemotherapy. This study aims to evaluate factors potentially related to the response to immunochemotherapy in gastric cancer, and further construct a predictive model to aid clinicians in assessing whether gastric cancer patients respond to immunotherapy, assisting in further clinical decision-making. In addition, to further explore why these hematological biomarkers could efficiently predict responses efficacy, we further conducted exploratory analyses flow cytometry and multiple immunohistochemistry staining to explore the association between tumor immune microenvironment and clinical efficacy of immunochemotherapy. By establishing the relationship between clinical and local tumor microenvironment factors and efficacy, this study describes the characteristics of gastric cancer patients who respond to immunochemotherapy, providing guidance for clinical stratification and further clinical trials.

Method

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

From 2019 to 2023, patients who met the following criteria were included in this retrospective cohort study: ① age between 18 and 80 years; ② diagnosed with locally advanced gastric cancer and received conversion immunochemotherapy (Paclitarel or Oxaliplatin/Cisplatin + 5-FU + Sintilimab/Tislelizumab, explicated in Table 1) due to tumor progression, with PD-L1 CPS scores < 1 and MSS; ③ evaluated as stable disease (SD) or partial response (PR) according to RECIST 1.1 criteria before surgery; ④ the interval between the last conversion immunochemotherapy and surgery was 3–4 weeks; ⑤ no contraindications to surgery. Patients with any of the following conditions were excluded from the study: ① failed to complete the conversion immunochemotherapy course due to treatment complications or tumor progression, or underwent emergency surgery due to gastrointestinal bleeding or pyloric obstruction; ② missing clinical or follow-up data; ③ distant metastasis discovered during surgery, precluding curative gastric cancer resection; ④ history of other tumors; ⑤ known history of autoimmune diseases; ⑥ preoperative use of anticoagulants or immunosuppressive drugs; ⑦ explicitly refused to participate in the study. Based on these inclusion/exclusion criteria, a total of 153 patients were included in the study (Fig. 1). Clinical samples were collected from 10 patients for experimental research. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, affiliated with Nanjing University Medical School (Approval number: 2025-0099-01). We called the patients for agreement for clinical data and FFPE sample collection in the retrospective study, and obtained written informed consent from all patients prior to sample collection in experimental research. In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the research has been registered on Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration number: ChiCTR2500098168. Detailed information can be viewed on: https://www.chictr.org.cn/bin/project/edit?pid=260856).

Patient Treatment and Procedure

Upon the patient’s initial hospital admission, the clinician employs upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and pathological examination to diagnose the tumor. Enhanced abdominal CT imaging is used to evaluate the tumor’s imaging stage. Concurrently, peripheral blood samples are collected to test complete blood cell count, comprehensive metabolic panel, carbohydrate antigens (CA125, CA199, CA724, CA242, AFP, and CEA), and serum cytokine levels. To date, we defined the “restoration” of carbohydrate antigens, which refers to normalization of carbohydrate antigen levels to within reference ranges after treatment. Patients with imaging stages of cT3-4, Nx, and M0, who are clinically evaluated as locally advanced and unresectable, are referred to the department of oncology for conversion therapy. 54 patients received chemotherapy with albumin-bound paclitaxel plus 5-FU, while 99 patients received chemotherapy with oxaliplatin or cisplatin plus 5-FU. Immunotherapy was administered using PD-1 monoclonal antibodies, either sintilimab, nivolumab or tislelizumab. Most of the patients received 3 cycles of treatment, while few of patients chose to receive 4–6 cycles of treatment. During treatment, the patient undergoes regular imaging and laboratory tests, and both the oncologist and surgeon jointly assess whether the patient should continue conversion immunochemotherapy or proceed to surgical treatment.

For patients undergoing surgical treatment, a professional surgeon is responsible for the surgical procedure and perioperative evaluation and treatment. Surgical treatment is performed only after surgical contraindications have been ruled out. Postoperatively, the patient follows routine recovery protocols, which include early mobilization, gradual reintroduction of diet, removal of drainage tubes, and wound sutures. The patient also receives regular follow-ups both in the hospital and after discharge. Further adjuvant treatment is decided and managed by professional oncologists. The patient is regularly followed up by professional personnel, with a median follow-up time of 15 months.

Tissue Preparation and Flow Cytometry

The specimens were immediately removed using sterile instruments and stored in tissue preservation solution (130-100-008, Miltenyi) and processed within 2 h. The specimens were placed on ice and washed with sterile PBS buffer until the washing buffer was clear. The adipose tissue was then carefully removed using sterile scissors, cut into 1–2 mm3 pieces and transferred into gentleMACS™ C Tubes. After mechanical dissociation using gentleMACS™ Dissociator (130-093-235, Miltenyi), enzymatic digestion was performed using Tumor Dissociation Kit (130-095-929, Miltenyi) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The single-cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm mesh and washed with RPMI-1640 culture medium. Cells were resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS and stimulated with PMA (50ng/ml, P8139, Sigma-Aldrich) and ionomycin (1 µg/ml, I3909, Sigma-Aldrich) In the presence of Brefeldin A (420601, Biolegend) at 37 °C in incubator for 4 h. The stimulation was ended with complete medium and cells were washed with the PBS containing 2% BSA for twice. Surface staining was performed with anti-CD45 (APC/Fire™ 750-Conjugated, 1:100, 982314, Biolegend), anti-CD3 (APC-Conjugated, 1:100, 317318, Biolegend), anti-CD4 (BV510-Conjugated, 1:100, 562970, BD Biosciences), anti-CD8 (BV605-Conjugated, 1:100, 301040, Biolegend), and anti-PD1 (BV421-Conjugated, 1:50, 564323, BD Biosciences) at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min. After washing with PBS, cells were fixed and permeabilized with BD Pharmingen™ Transcription Factor Buffer Set (562574, BD Biosciences) at 4 °C in the dark according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Anti-IFNγ (FITC-Conjugated, 1:50, 502506, Biolegend) and anti-FOXP3 (PE-Conjugated, 1:50, 320107, Biolegend) were used for intracellular staining at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. After washing three times with PBS buffer, data acquisition and detection were performed by flow cytometer (Aria II, BD Biosciences). The results were analyzed and images were exported using Flowjo (Version 10.8.1).

Multiplex Immunohistochemistry Staining.

The specimens were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin blocks. The paraffin blocks were prepared into 4 μm thick sections for staining. Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed in a microwave oven with sodium citrate buffer or EDTA buffer for 10 min. Co-staining of CD3, IFN-γ and IL-10 was performed using a Four color mIHC Fluorescence kit (Recordbio Biological Technology, Shanghai, China) based on the tyramide signal amplification (TSA) technology according to the manufacture’s instruction. DAPI was used to stain the cell nucleus. ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant (P36930, Invitrogen) was applied to the slices by the coverslip. After sealing with clear nail polish, images were acquired using laser scanning confocal microscopy (TCS-SP8, Leica). LAS X software (Leica) was adapted to analyze the results and export the images.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R Studio (version 4.2.1) and SPSS (version 22.0). The χ2 test was used to compare categorical variables and the t-test or analysis of variance was used to compare continuous variables. For continuous variables who do not satisfy the normal distribution, Wilcoxon paired t-test was used to determine whether these variables from different groups were statistically different. Receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC) were established to determine the cutoff values to discriminate patients with or without pathological response. Kaplan–Meier curves of overall survival was plotted using Graphpad Prism (Version 9.5). A nomogram for predicting the TRG response was built using the R library ‘rms’ package. The nomogram was further evaluated by conduction calibration curve using the R library ‘ResourceSelection’ package. DCA curve was performed using the R library ‘rmda’ package. All the generated results were visualized by R library ‘ggplot2’ package.

Results

Image features displayed limited efficacy in predicting conversion immunochemotherapy efficacy

To clarify whether imaging features or pathological tumor regression can accurately reflect the response of tumors to immunochemotherapy, we first evaluated the impact of both imaging and pathological features on patient prognosis. We found no significant difference in survival between patients assessed as partial response (PR) and stable disease (SD) according to RECIST criteria (Fig. 2A). However, patients with tumor regression grade (TRG) 0 and 1 (classified as responders) had significantly better prognosis compared to those with grades 2 and 3 (classified as non-responders) (Fig. 2B). Additionally, other pathological features, such as earlier AJCC stage, lower T grade, and negative lymph node metastasis, were significantly associated with better patient prognosis (Fig. 2C-E). Further analysis of the baseline characteristics revealed that patients evaluated as responders by TRG had significantly earlier AJCC and T grades, and lower rates of lymph node metastasis compared to the non-responders (Table 1). Therefore, tumor response evaluation based solely on imaging features is less accurate than evaluation based on pathological features.

Serum carbohydrate antigen levels serve as novel indicators of tumor response and progression to conversion immunochemotherapy

We divided the patients into two groups based on TRG grading: responders (TRG = 0/1) and non-responders (TRG = 2/3). We examined the differences in serum carbohydrate antigen concentrations between the two groups. In non-responding patients, the serum concentrations of AFP (p = 0.4003) and CEA (p = 0.1546) did not significantly decrease (Fig. 3A). In contrast, responding patients showed significant decreases in AFP (p = 0.0271) and CEA (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3B). Moreover, the serum concentrations of CA199, CA724, and CA242 also significantly decreased in the response group (Fig. 3B). Further analysis indicated that, in patients with elevated serum carbohydrate antigen levels before treatment, the serum concentrations of CEA (p = 0.0003), CA199 (p = 0.0157), and CA724 (p = 0.0424) significantly decreased in responders (Fig. 4A). Longitudinal tracking of carbohydrate antigen levels during treatment in patients with initially abnormal levels showed continuous decreases or stabilization in responders, whereas non-responders exhibited a trend of initial decrease followed by increase (Fig. 4B). This suggests that serum carbohydrate antigen levels may be crucial indicators of tumor response and progression.

Based on these findings, we propose identifying indicators related to TRG grading and constructing a predictive model for TRG. This model could assist clinicians in determining surgical eligibility based on evaluation results following preoperative conversion immunochemotherapy. We collected baseline characteristics, pathological results, imaging evaluation results (Table 1), laboratory test results before (Table 2) and after (Table 3) immunochemotherapy, and tumor marker test results before and after treatment (Table 4). We found that the absence of hypertension history, higher pre-treatment serum albumin levels, serum IL-10 concentration and restoration of at least two carbohydrate antigen concentrations were associated with a pathological response to treatment (Table 5). We summarized the predictors from our multivariable analysis in a hierarchical format, ranked by statistical significance (Table 6). The restoration of tumor markers and no history of hypertension showed the strongest associations with treatment response. Although serum albumin did not reach conventional statistical significance, its potential clinical relevance is still acknowledged.

Serum cytokine levels can also serve as hematological biomarkers for conversion immunochemotherapy

To further evaluate changes in systemic immunity and the local tumor immune microenvironment during immunochemotherapy in gastric cancer patients, we measured cytokine levels in peripheral blood. The results showed that IFN-γ, IL-10, and TNF-α were significantly higher in responders (p = 0.0003, p < 0.0001, p = 0.0106) (Fig. 5A). Although serum IL-2 and IL-4 levels increased in the response group, the differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.1732, p = 0.0909). There was no significant difference in IL-6 levels between the two groups (p = 0.3349). IFN-γ and TNF-α are critical effector molecules in immune responses by effector T cells, while IL-10 regulates immune microenvironment homeostasis. Multicolor immunohistochemistry revealed that IFN-γ and IL-10 were weakly stained in non-responders, with less T cell infiltration. In contrast, strong staining of IFN-γ, IL-10 and TNFα around T cells was observed in responders (Fig. 5B), indicating enrichment of these cytokines in the tumor immune microenvironment of responders, consistent with peripheral blood.

Association between cytokines and tumor response. A Comparison of post-treatment serum cytokine concentrations between the non-response group and the response group. B Multiplex immunohistochemical analysis of cytokine abundance in lesion tissues between the non-response and response groups

We further examined changes in the proportions of various cells within the tumor tissue. The proportion of IFN-γ+CD8+ T cells and IFN-γ+CD4+ Th1 cells was higher in responders (Fig. 6A-B), indicating that T cells are a significant source of elevated IFN-γ in tumor tissues. Additionally, the proportion of CD4+FOXP3+ regulatory T cells and PD-1+CD8+ T cells was higher in non-responders (Fig. 6C-D), suggesting a persistent suppressive tumor immune microenvironment despite the application of immune checkpoint inhibitors. This indicates other mechanisms allowing tumors to evade immunochemotherapy, warranting further basic research to understand the underlying reasons for the lack of response to immunochemotherapy.

Comparison of immune cell proportions in tumor tissues between the non-response and response groups. (A) Cytotoxic CD8 + T cells; (B) Th1 cells; (C) Regulatory CD4 + T cells; (D) PD-1 + exhausted CD8 + T cells

Nomogram constructed based on hematological biomarkers displayed superior efficacy in predicting conversion immunochemotherapy and patients’ prognosis

Consequently, we developed a nomogram for predicting the response of gastric cancer patients to immunochemotherapy (Fig. 7A). The model indicates that a lower multi-marker model score correlates with a higher likelihood of response to immunochemotherapy. The ROC curve for the model demonstrated that the area under the curve (AUC = 0.748) was significantly higher than that of the serum carbohydrate antigen (AUC = 0.626) and RECIST criteria (AUC = 0.569), indicating superior predictive ability (Fig. 7B). The calibration curve showed a good fit between the model predictions and actual outcomes (Fig. 7C). Additionally, the decision curve analysis (DCA) indicated that the multi-marker model provided higher predictive benefits compared to single factors in higher-risk thresholds (Fig. 7D). Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that patients with higher multi-marker scores and lower TRG had better prognoses (Fig. 7E).

Discussion

On the basis of traditional chemotherapy, conversion immunochemotherapy has created opportunities to benefits the prognosis of gastric cancer patients. However, due to the strong heterogeneity of gastric cancer and the fact that most gastric cancers are pMMR/MSS type, there are few tumor-associated antigens (TAA) that can be identified, and only a small number of patients can benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) treatment. It remains controversial whether conversion immunotherapy should be utilized in gastric cancer patients with PD-L1-negative (CPS < 1) and MSS. Although both fundamental theories and previous studies suggest that patients with higher PD-L1 expression and microsatellite instability are more likely to benefit from immunotherapy, there indeed exists a subset of PD-L1-negative and MSS patients who respond to conversion immunochemotherapy. However, there is a lack of research and discussion on identifying and characterizing these potential beneficiaries. Our study provides a new perspective for clinical practice, suggesting that patients with PD-L1-negative and MSS tumors who have no hypertension and high serum albumin levels could still be considered for conversion immunochemotherapy. Additionally, monitoring changes in serum tumor-associated carbohydrate antigen and cytokine levels could help evaluate therapeutic efficacy, allowing timely modification of treatment strategies or prompt surgical interventions if necessary.

The response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy is a substantial prognostic factor for disease-free survival and overall survival of gastric cancer [15, 16]. Besides, the ypStage provides reasonable survival prediction based on TNM grouping, whereas the clinical stage is not useful [17]. Our results show consistency with these previous studies in patients receiving conversion immunochemotherapy. Although RECIST criteria has been consistently modified to give more precise prediction for tumor response in recent years [18–20], consequently, there are currently few predictive factors that can accurately stratify responders to immunotherapy.

Current research looks to screen potential responders through pre-treatment data. To achieve this, machine learning methods have been developed to identify patients who can respond to immunotherapy [21–23]. Moreover, Chang et al. recently developed an online tool using prediction model called LORIS, which can predict the probability of a patient responding to ICI based on a six-feature model, including patient’s TMB, systemic treatment history, serum albumin, blood NLR, age, and cancer type [24]. However, these studies are limited as they predict the response based on baseline data and blood indicators before treatment, without accounting for dynamic changes during the patient’s treatment. This study proposes a new prediction model based on baseline data of patients receiving conversion immunochemotherapy and examination data during the treatment process. Unlike other studies, this model dynamically predicts patient response to ICI by comparing laboratory test results during and after treatment with those before treatment.

In recent years, hypertension has been recognized as a disease closely related to chronic inflammation and immune system disorders [25, 26]. This study first proposed that hypertension in gastric cancer patients before immunochemotherapy may be associated with a worse response rate. This may be because hypertension occurrence accompanies with body’s immune system homeostasis, manifested as abnormal activation of T cells, accumulation of memory effector T cells, chronic inflammatory damage, and functional destruction of terminal effector organs [27–30]. Additionally, changes in oxidative stress balance caused by chronic hypertension profoundly affect the immune system’s function [31, 32]. The use of calcium channel antagonists to maintain blood pressure in hypertensive patients is also associated with reduced local lymphocyte infiltration in tumors [33]. Recent studies have shown that different antihypertensive drugs affect reactive oxygen intermediates, blood cytokine concentrations, and immune cell functions differently [34, 35], suggesting that further clinical research and animal experiments should be carried out to provide a theoretical basis for developing comprehensive treatment plans for patients with tumors and hypertension.

Serum albumin, an important indicator of a patient’s nutritional status, is strongly correlated with tumor occurrence, prognosis, and response to immunotherapy. Suh et al. found that a low albumin/globulin ratio was associated with a higher incidence of cancer [36]. Zheng and Yoo et al. conducted a pan-cancer analysis of patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors and found that higher levels were associated with longer overall survival, progression-free survival, and higher objective response rates in tumor patients [37, 38]. Suzuki et al. found that serum albumin greater than 3.5 g/dL was an independent predictor of long-term prognosis in patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma after PD-1 monoclonal antibody treatment [39]. Albumin is also an important natural drug carrier in tumor treatment due to its biocompatibility, long half-life, good drug-binding ability, and natural targeting properties from the tumor’s EPR effect, highlighting its value in tumor treatment [40]. Our study found that pre-treatment serum albumin levels, rather than post-treatment levels, significantly influenced the tumor response to immunochemotherapy, suggesting that albumin supplementation before immunochemotherapy may have important clinical value.

Carbohydrate antigens are important indicators for the diagnosis and prognosis of gastric cancer [41, 42]. During tumor occurrence and progression, abnormal glycosylation in tumor cells [43], overexpression of carbohydrate antigens, and circulating tumor cells can lead to increase of carbohydrate antigen levels in peripheral blood. Thus, some carbohydrate antigens are considered tumor markers for gastric cancer, and their levels in peripheral blood may relate to the response to neoadjuvant therapy [44]. Our study shows that peripheral blood tumor markers, only before or after immunochemotherapy, are not significantly correlated with treatment response. However, we found that when more than two peripheral blood tumor markers returned to normal restored after treatment, the tumor were more likely to respond to immunochemotherapy. In particular, AFP decreased significantly in patients who responded to immunotherapy, while CEA, CA199, and CA724 decreased significantly regardless of pre-treatment abnormal levels. For non-responders, serum concentrations of CEA, CA199, CA724, and CA242 first decreased and then increased, suggesting that dynamic increases in these carbohydrate antigens may signal tumor tolerance to treatment, warranting further clinical trials.

The tumor response to immunochemotherapy is closely related to the remodeling of the local tumor immune microenvironment [45]. This is represented by reinvigorating exhausted T cells [46], exhibiting long-term memory phenotype of T cells [47], expansion of tumor-reactive T cells [48], and the transformation of myeloid cells to a pro-inflammatory phenotype [49, 50]. Our results preliminarily showed that in gastric cancer tissues responding to immunotherapy, the killing function of CD8+ T cells and the proportion of IFNγ+CD4+ Th1 cells increased, while the proportion of regulatory T cells decreased. Additionally, the proportion of PD-1+ T cells also decreased. While our findings align with previous reports, it is important to interpret these results with caution, given the limited sample size. As a result, further research is necessary to validate these findings.

Previous studies have shown that systemic immunity has similarity with local immunity in tumor, and changes in the local tumor immune microenvironment are influenced immune cell infiltration from peripheral blood and tumor-draining lymph nodes [51]. Besides, tumor-associated antigens released by local tumors may enter into peripheral blood, triggering a systemic immune response, which is manifested by increased serum cytokines and other indicators. Therefore, we explored whether systemic inflammatory markers were associated with tumor response. Our study showed no significant difference in the NLR, MLR and PLR between responders and non-responders. We consider this may be due to the use of G-CSF in some patients who suffered bone marrow suppression during treatment. At the same time, high heterogeneity of gastric cancer and individual patient conditions may also lead to different results from previous studies.

Our study reveals that IL-10, IFN-γ, and TNF-α are related to gastric cancer response to immunochemotherapy, consistent with previous study [52]. However, previous study did not detect PD-L1 expression as well as microsatellite stability. IFN-γ and TNF-α are cytokines released when T cells exert a killing effect on tumors and are important indicators of T cell effector function. We found that these two indicators not only increased in tumor tissue infiltrating lymphocytes but also in serum, suggesting that changes in the local immune microenvironment caused by immunochemotherapy may synchronize with changes in the immune microenvironment. Previous studies have shown that IL-10, a cytokine released by monocytes and macrophages, can promote tumors’ anti-apoptosis and rapid proliferation capabilities by activating the JAK-STAT3 pathway, and inhibit NF-κB signaling, which is generally considered an important signal for inhibiting the tumor immune response, in macrophages through metabolic reprogramming to limit inflammation. Recent studies have shown that IL-10-mediated STAT3 activation plays a crucial role in maintaining T cell stemness and anti-tumor immune response capabilities [53, 54], and designed fusion proteins can promote oxidative phosphorylation metabolism of terminally exhausted T cells, enhancing their proliferation and anti-tumor function [55, 56]. Furthermore, a recent publication demonstrated that IL-10 can upregulate its own receptor in tumor-infiltrating immune cells through a positive feedback loop, thereby maintaining persistently high IL-10R expression within the tumor microenvironment [57]. Notably, using engineered bacteria to locally induce IL-10 in the tumor further leverages this mechanism, which eventually reinvigorates tumor-resident memory T cells and enhance their anti-tumor cytotoxic function, resulting in a more robust and sustained anti-tumor response [57]. Therefore, IL-10 may have a dual role in tumors. Our findings indicate that elevated serum IL-10 levels are significantly associated with improved responses to immunochemotherapy in gastric cancer patients and serve as an independent predictor of treatment outcomes. Additionally, the increase in serum IL-10 may coincide with elevated IL-10 levels in tumor tissues and enhanced T-cell function. However, the precise relationship between these phenomena and the underlying antitumor mechanisms requires further investigation.

Our study has several limitations. Our results showed patients without hypertension and with higher serum albumin are more possible to respond to immunochemotherapy, but larger clinical studies are needed to verify this due to the small sample size. Besides, this was a single-center study without an independent external validation cohort, thereby limiting the robustness of our conclusions, despite the favorable calibration and DCA results in our study. Future prospective multicenter studies with larger sample sizes, in collaboration with multiple institutions, are warranted to further validate and strengthen our findings and to propose directions for the improvement of immunotherapy strategies. Further basic research is also needed on how hypertension and serum albumin can affect tumor response to treatment. Additionally, while our study suggests that T cell function, phenotype, and cytokine levels in peripheral blood may relate to tumor TRG, further research is needed to confirm these as predictors of treatment response. To date, although this study preliminarily identified serum IL-10 as a potential biomarker predicting the efficacy of conversion immunotherapy in patients with gastric cancer, and observed higher IL-10 concentrations within tumor tissues of patients exhibiting superior therapeutic responses, the synergy between serum IL-10 and intratumoral IL-10 abundance remains unclear and warrants validation through larger cohort studies. Our investigation into the underlying mechanisms was preliminary, hypothesizing that serum IL-10 could serve as a predictive marker due to its potential implication of a shift towards an anti-tumor immune microenvironment. However, the logical association supporting this hypothesis remains relatively weak, lacking deeper mechanistic exploration and validation, which will be addressed in future studies. Finally, unmeasured confounders including comorbidities, nutrition, and molecular data such as EBV status and TMB were not consistently available but will be included in future studies.

Overall, our study indicates that the absence of hypertension, higher pre-treatment serum albumin levels, increased post-treatment serum IL-10 levels, and a reduction in at least two tumor-associated carbohydrate antigens after treatment are significant predictors of response to immunochemotherapy in PD-L1 negative and MSS gastric cancer patients. Scoring these four indicators not only facilitates real-time assessment of treatment response for clinicians but may also serve as an important prognostic tool. Furthermore, our findings suggest that rational and precise blood pressure management, as well as albumin supplementation during immunochemotherapy, could have potential value in enhancing treatment responsiveness in gastric cancer patients, but this hypothesis requires further prospective interventional trials to assess causality and clinical benefit. Future efforts will focus on improving immunological and pathological examinations to build a more specific and sensitive prediction model for gastric cancer patients receiving immunochemotherapy before surgery to achieve accurate prediction and stratified treatment.
