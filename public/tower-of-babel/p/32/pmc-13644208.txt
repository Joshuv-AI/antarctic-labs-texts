Sporadic Creutzfeldt-jakob disease: a case report
Authors: Li Xie, Yanhong Dong, Guoen Yao, Junling Ge, Miao Li, Xiangdong Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCreutzfeldt-Jakob disease is a rare, transmissible, and fatal degenerative disorder of the central nervous system. As the most common human prion disease, it typically presents with progressive dementia, visual impairment, cerebellar ataxia, pyramidal or extrapyramidal signs, myoclonus, and akinetic mutism. Although dizziness is a prevalent clinical symptom, it is an uncommon initial manifestation of Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease.Case presentationWe report the case of a 71-year-old Chinese woman who presented to our hospital with dizziness. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed extensive cortical hyperintensity on diffusion-weighted imaging. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was positive for the 14-3-3 protein. Collectively, these findings, along with additional supportive investigations, led to the diagnosis of possible sporadic Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease.ConclusionsThis case illustrates that Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease can manifest with atypical symptoms like dizziness, highlighting the essential role of brain magnetic resonance imaging in securing the diagnosis. In light of the disease's rarity and clinical heterogeneity, detailed reporting of such cases provides valuable clinical insights that deepen physicians' understanding of this condition.

Introduction

Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD) is the most common human prion disease and is also known as subacute transmissible spongiform encephalopathy. It is a rapidly progressive, rare, and fatal neurodegenerative disease caused by the scrapie prion protein (PrPSc). CJD cases occur globally, with an annual incidence of approximately 1–2 cases per one million population [1]. To date, no effective therapy is available, and most patients die within one year of clinical onset. CJD is classified into four subtypes: sporadic, hereditary, iatrogenic, and variant. Among these, sporadic CJD (sCJD) accounts for 85–90% of cases [2]. The peak age of onset for sCJD is reported to be around 60–69 years, with no significant gender difference [3, 4]. The exact etiology of the disorder remains incompletely understood. Proposed mechanisms include the transformation of normal prion proteins into abnormal misfolded forms, post-translational modifications, or somatic mutations. Based on the polymorphism of methionine (M) and valine (V) at codon 129 of the prion protein gene (PRNP) and the glycotype (type 1 or 2) of PrPSc, sCJD is classified into six major subtypes: MM1, MV1, VV2, MV2, VV1, and MM2. The MM2 subtype is further divided into cortical (MM2C) and thalamic (MM2T) forms. Typical clinical manifestations of CJD include rapidly progressive dementia, myoclonus, cerebellar ataxia, pyramidal and extrapyramidal signs, and akinetic mutism. However, as previously reported, early-stage presentations are often atypical and may include symptoms such as stroke-like episodes [5] or sleep disturbances [6]. Consequently, early and accurate diagnosis remains challenging. Raising awareness of the diverse clinical symptoms and manifestations of this rare disease is therefore of great importance. This case describes a patient who initially presented with dizziness and was ultimately diagnosed with possible sCJD after further investigation. It highlights that CJD may initially present with atypical symptoms such as dizziness. Moreover, it underscores the critical value of brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in enabling early and timely diagnosis of the disease.

Case report

A 71-year-old Chinese female with an unremarkable medical history presented to our hospital with intermittent dizziness for over one year, which had worsened in the preceding two months. Her family reported concerns regarding intermittent memory loss over the past two months, noting that she occasionally forgot acquaintances’ names and exhibited slightly slowed responses. She had previously visited other hospitals for the same symptoms. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) on brain MRI performed at a local hospital revealed abnormal high signal intensities in the cortex of the bilateral temporal, occipital, parietal, frontal lobes and the left insular lobe. Electroencephalography (EEG) demonstrated diffuse 3–5 Hz mixed slow waves with low to high amplitude, predominantly in the anterior region. Given the characteristic imaging findings, further evaluation was recommended to rule out CJD, prompting her referral to our hospital. On examination, the patient was conscious and had no obvious positive neurological signs. Laboratory tests on admission were largely unremarkable, with the following exceptions: erythrocyte sedimentation rate 38 mm/h (reference range: 0–20), anticardiolipin antibodies 75.6 µg/mL (< 20), vitamin B12 86 pg/mL (180–914), homocysteine 16.4 µmol/L (0–15), and triglycerides 2.06 mmol/L (0–1.7). Routine serum tests—including complete blood count, C-reactive protein, liver and kidney function, electrolytes, coagulation profile, thyroid function, tumor markers, HIV, syphilis, hepatitis, antinuclear antibodies, and anti-β2 glycoprotein I antibodies—were within normal limits. Lumbar puncture revealed an opening pressure of 85 mmH2O. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed mildly elevated protein (0.53 g/L), with normal glucose and chloride levels. CSF cytology and microbiological studies were unremarkable. The 14-3-3 protein was positive in CSF, while real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) and next-generation sequencing were negative. Both serum and CSF were negative for paraneoplastic and autoimmune encephalitis antibodies. Genetic analysis of the PRNP gene revealed no pathogenic mutations; the patient was homozygous for methionine at codon 129 (M/M) and for glutamate at codon 219 (E/E). DWI on contrast-enhanced brain MRI showed extensive abnormal hyperintensity in the cerebral cortex, with no evidence of contrast enhancement (Fig. 1). Long-term EEG revealed recurrent asynchronous irregular slow waves in the bilateral frontal and anterior middle temporal regions. The patient declined brain biopsy. Based on these findings and the China Diagnostic Guidelines for CJD (2021 edition), the patient was diagnosed with possible sCJD. During follow-up more than 1 month after discharge, the patient’s dizziness worsened and her condition deteriorated rapidly. She experienced increased somnolence, reduced physical activity, and irritability, and ultimately died in an akinetic mute state three months after discharge.

Discussion

In this case, the patient did not present with the typical clinical symptoms of CJD, such as rapidly progressive cognitive impairment or myoclonus. The main manifestation was dizziness. Based on MRI findings and positive CSF 14-3-3 protein, the patient was diagnosed with possible sCJD. In the late stage, the patient developed behavioral disorders and an akinetic mute state, along with a rapidly progressive disease course, consistent with the diagnosis of possible sCJD.

Although dizziness has been described as an early symptom of sCJD in previous literature, it is considered an atypical manifestation. For instance, one report described a middle-aged woman who initially presented to the otolaryngology department with dizziness, which ultimately led to a diagnosis of sCJD [7]. Furthermore, an analysis of Chinese sCJD patients found that 8.67% initially complained solely of dizziness [3]. Despite this, dizziness as the predominant manifestation of CJD remains rarely reported.

Brain biopsy or autopsy remains the gold standard for the definitive diagnosis of CJD. However, the clinical diagnosis of CJD primarily relies on a combination of clinical symptoms, imaging, EEG, CSF analysis, and blood tests. MRI plays a crucial role in both the diagnosis and differential diagnosis of CJD. The characteristic imaging findings include abnormally high signal intensity in the cerebral cortex, basal ganglia, or thalamus on DWI or fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences. In this case, DWI revealed marked hyperintensity involving extensive areas of the cerebral cortex—a pattern often referred to as “cortical ribboning”—which is a typical imaging feature of CJD. Although similar cortical hyperintensities may be observed in conditions such as autoimmune encephalitis (AIE), metabolic disorders, or following seizures, there was no clinical evidence to support any of these alternative diagnoses in this patient. Brain MRI did not show the bilateral medial temporal lobe T2 hyperintensity characteristic of AIE, and clinically, there were no signs of metabolic disease or seizure activity. Considering the patient's clinical presentation and laboratory findings, these differential diagnoses were effectively excluded. While periodic sharp wave complexes (PSWCs) on EEG are a characteristic feature of CJD, they typically manifest in the mid-to-late stages and are less frequently observed in certain molecular subtypes. A retrospective study reported PSWCs in only 38.2% of sCJD cases [8]; therefore, the absence of this finding in the present case does not exclude the diagnosis. The 14-3-3 protein is a biomarker of neuronal cell death, and its detection in CSF is commonly employed as one of the clinical diagnostic criteria for sCJD [9]. The real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) assay enables the detection of PrPSc in both CSF and peripheral tissues, including skin, retina, and olfactory mucosa. A positive CSF RT-QuIC result is of great significance for the diagnosis of CJD. However, the negative result in this patient may be attributable to several factors, including an unusually early disease stage at the time of CSF sampling, potential technical limitations of the assay, or PrPSc concentrations falling below the detectable threshold [9, 10]. The codon 129 methionine homozygosity (MM) is considered the most susceptible genotype for CJD, and sCJD predominantly occurs in individuals with this genetic background [1]. In contrast, biomarkers such as neurofilament light chain (NfL) and tau protein, while indicative of disease progression, lack diagnostic specificity for CJD. Unfortunately, further subtyping analysis and testing for NfL and tau were not performed in this patient. The timely diagnosis in this case was primarily attributed to the characteristic neuroimaging findings. Brain MRI, a frequently employed examination in patients presenting with head discomfort, is often performed early in the diagnostic workup, thereby playing a potentially crucial role in raising initial suspicion for sCJD. Furthermore, elevated antiphospholipid antibody levels were noted in this patient. In the absence of documented vascular thrombotic events or adverse pregnancy history, this finding is considered consistent with asymptomatic antibody positivity.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations in this study that should be acknowledged. First, the patient did not undergo a brain biopsy or autopsy; consequently, no pathological diagnostic evidence was obtained. In addition, CSF RT-QuIC was not repeated, and RT-QuIC analysis of peripheral tissues was also not performed. Finally, further genetic characterization, including PRNP genotyping and biomarker testing for NfL and tau, was not conducted.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that CJD may manifest primarily with atypical symptoms like dizziness, and underscores the essential role of brain MRI in establishing the diagnosis. While the negative CSF RT-QuIC result increases diagnostic uncertainty, it does not definitively exclude the disease. Given the rarity of CJD and the atypical presentation of dizziness in this case, detailed reporting of such instances is essential. These reports contribute to a deeper understanding of CJD, serving as important clinical references for physicians encountering this rare condition.
