Photovoltaic Hydrogen Power-Coupled Polygeneration System for Green Data Center: Energy, Economic, and Environmental Analysis
Authors: Zerui Chen, Xin Wu, Zhukui Tan, Houpeng Hu, Jian Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rapid growth of data centers has sharply increased power consumption and greenhouse gas emissions, making improved energy efficiency and renewable energy integration urgent priorities for their sustainable development. This study proposes an integrated energy system for powering and cooling data centers, combining photovoltaic (PV) modules, a proton exchange membrane (PEM) electrolyzer, a PEM fuel cell (PEMFC), and an absorption chiller. When solar energy is available, the PV is first supplied to power the data center, and the surplus is directed to the PEM electrolyzer for hydrogen production via water electrolysis. The stored hydrogen is later converted back into electricity through the PEMFC during peak-price periods in the nighttime or when solar irradiance is insufficient. Waste heat from the electrolyzer and PEMFC is used to drive the absorption chiller for data center cooling and waste heat recovery, while recovered heat from the data center is utilized for domestic hot water and space heating. A comprehensive exergy analysis is conducted to assess the thermodynamic performance of the proposed polygeneration system, alongside an evaluation of its economic and environmental benefits under varying PV participation levels. Comparative results show that when PV generation meets 40% of the annual power demand in the data center, annual operating costs can be reduced by approximately $238,000 and CO2 emissions by 2,940 tons compared with a conventional grid-supplied configuration. Additionally, the integrated system achieves an average power usage effectiveness (PUE) of 1.25 and a maximum exergy efficiency of 28.5% during the heating season, highlighting its strong potential to enhance energy efficiency and mitigate environmental impacts, particularly in regions with moderate solar radiation.

Introduction

As digital technologies continue to expand globally, data centers have become indispensable infrastructure, supporting a wide range of cloud computing, storage, and communication services. , However, the rapid growth of data centers has led to soaring electricity consumption and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. , In China alone, the number of server racks grew from 1.08 million in 2015 to nearly 6 million by 2022, with projections reaching 9 million by 2030. Correspondingly, data centers are projected to account for up to 13% of global electricity consumption by 2030, making them one of the largest contributors to energy demand and GHG emissions.

Improving energy efficiency and integrating renewable energy have become urgent priorities for the sustainable development of data centers. Yu et al. designed a waste heat recovery system for a data center in Harbin, China, aiming to provide cooling and heating to adjacent buildings. The results showed that the new system exhibited good economic performance, reducing operating costs by $458,300 per year compared to an air-source heat pump. Deymi-Dashtebayaz et al. utilized an air-source heat pump to recover waste heat from a data center for space heating in office buildings. The system significantly reduced natural gas and electricity consumption, thereby lowering both economic costs and CO2 emissions. Oró et al. proposed using waste heat from a liquid-cooled data center to supply heat to an indoor swimming pool. The annual operating cost of the pool was reduced by 18%. Hiltunen et al. investigated the feasibility of integrating waste heat from a large data center into a district heating network. Their findings indicated that this approach significantly reduced the GHG emissions. Li et al. introduced thermal energy storage (TES) technology to enhance the efficiency of waste heat recovery in data centers. Borehole thermal storage increased the recovery rate to 96% and reduced annual CO2 emissions by 8%. Dvorak et al. proposed integrating data centers with aquifer thermal energy storage (ATES) systems to supply heating to campus facilities. Similarly, Adamski et al. proposed connecting data center waste heat to the district heating networks of a university campus and surrounding communities. The system achieved a heat utilization rate of 89.5%, reduced total annual carbon emissions by 58%, and cut annual operating costs by €450,000. Zhang et al. conducted a life cycle cost (LCC) analysis of the economic benefits of waste heat recovery in data centers. The results showed that it could save 1,582.7 tons of standard coal annually.

Although waste heat recovery shows great potential for enhancing system-wide efficiency, its effectiveness is often constrained by location, seasonality, and system complexity. , Yue et al. found that due to the mismatch between recovered waste heat and seasonal heating demand, the energy savings for a heating system do not increase linearly with total heat recovery. In fact, only 45.5% of the available waste heat can be used for heating, with the remainder discarded. To address both GHG emission and waste heat utilization challenges in data centers, several studies have explored hybrid energy systems involving renewable energy, thermal storage, and fuel cells, which aim to integrate renewable energy with waste heat recovery to enhance energy efficiency and reduce operating costs. Jahangir et al. combined renewable energy with various cooling strategies and found that the system could reduce cooling energy consumption by up to 47% compared to conventional solutions. Sheme et al. analyzed the renewable energy potential of data centers located at 60°N latitude, exploring the feasibility of powering them using various combinations of wind turbines and photovoltaic panels. Temiz et al. developed a solar-based cogeneration system for data centers and conducted energy and exergy analyses. The results showed that the system achieved an overall energy efficiency of 35.98% and an exergy efficiency of 31.24%. Kwon et al. investigated a solar power and battery system for data center energy supply and proposed operational strategies to enhance renewable energy utilization. Güğül et al. evaluated the feasibility of integrating free cooling, waste heat recovery, and photovoltaic systems for data center power supply. The system reduced the power usage effectiveness (PUE) from 1.8 to 1.1 and achieved net-zero carbon emissions. Wu et al. suggested that combining system-level strategies with device-level recovery methods, such as a modular ORC system and localized heat pump, can significantly improve waste heat recovery efficiency in data centers. Marshall et al. designed an integrated system that combines a heat pump and an ORC unit to recover data center waste heat, demonstrating significant environmental and economic benefits for large-scale applications.

Hydrogen energy has emerged as a promising medium for energy storage and sector coupling. , Through power-to-hydrogen (P2H) and hydrogen-to-power (H2P) pathways, hydrogen can act as a bridge between intermittent solar power and a stable energy demand. Based on energy sources and carbon emissions, hydrogen production methods are primarily categorized into gray, blue, and green hydrogen. Gray hydrogen produced via steam methane reforming (SMR) represents the most technologically mature industrial hydrogen production route but entails substantial carbon emissions (approximately 7.5–13 kg CO2/kg H2). Blue hydrogen integrates carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technology with gray hydrogen production, reducing carbon emissions by 80–90% while remaining dependent on fossil fuels. Green hydrogen generated through water electrolysis driven by renewable energy sources such as solar and wind power, features carbon neutrality, and aligns with global carbon neutrality goals. With the advancement of electrolysis technologies and the declining cost of renewable energy, the economic viability and feasibility of water electrolysis for hydrogen production are steadily improving, with four primary electrolysis technologies: alkaline water electrolysis (AWE), proton exchange membrane electrolysis (PEME), anion exchange membrane electrolysis (AEME), and solid oxide electrolysis (SOE). The proton exchange membrane (PEM) electrolyzer offers distinct advantages, a more compact design than traditional alternatives, higher energy efficiency than the conventional alkaline electrolyzer due to efficient operation at elevated current densities, and membrane selectivity ensuring high-purity hydrogen, eliminating complex postprocessing. When integrated with renewable energy, PEM electrolysis enables efficient energy storage and transportation, positioning it as a pivotal component for future energy systems. Some recent studies have advanced our understanding of green hydrogen and hydrogen energy technologies. Rehman et al. conducted a comprehensive analysis of the technical and economic feasibility of green hydrogen through conceptual design, feasibility assessment, and scale optimization, revealing that anticipated cost reductions may lower green hydrogen production costs to 1.50–2.0 USD/kg. Mudhafar et al. performed an in-depth analysis of various water electrolyzers, demonstrating that green hydrogen has a global warming potential (GWP) of only 0.70 kg CO2/kg H2. Elkadeem et al. employed machine learning to optimize the total life-cycle cost, flexibility, and resilience of hydrogen production, achieving a 15.8% reduction in total life-cycle cost, a 62.2% decrease in fossil fuel consumption, and a 65.3% reduction in emissions compared to the baseline scenario. Menesy et al. optimized the parameters of the proton exchange membrane fuel cell (PEMFC), verifying their applicability in system operation. Moreover, hydrogen energy exhibits substantial application value in data centers, and the waste heat from hydrogen fuel cells can be harnessed for absorption cooling, thereby providing an ARC in data center applications. Celestine et al. proposed that the use of hydrogen energy to power data centers is a potential means to achieve carbon neutrality. Xu et al. and Guizzi et al. examined the thermodynamic and economic performance of fuel cell-based combined cooling, heating, and power (CCHP) systems for data centers, offering new design approaches for hydrogen-based power solutions. Xie et al. explored the economic feasibility of hydrogen-powered data centers, concluding that under stricter future carbon regulations, such systems hold significant development potential. Cai et al. designed four power-cooling systems for data center based on multienergy integration with PEMFC, and results imply strong sustainability potential. Ritchie et al. proposed a distributed fuel cell power system that operates without extensive backup equipment, demonstrating the reliability and feasibility of hydrogen applications in data centers. Temiz et al. developed a hydrogen-powered system specifically for Arctic data centers, which achieved an average annual energy efficiency of 35.98% and an exergy efficiency of 31.2%. Kanbur et al. developed a hydrogen-powered system for data center, getting energy and exergy efficiencies of 15.8% and 43.3%, respectively. Lazaar et al. proposed a hybrid tidal and solar power system for hydrogen production to supply remote island data centers. Haddad et al. developed a hybrid energy storage system combining batteries and hydrogen to meet the power demands of data centers. Results showed that hydrogen energy storage is more robust than battery energy storage during daily loads. Xu et al. designed a hydrogen-based energy storage microgrid system for data centers. The results showed an annual operating cost and a GHG emission reduction of $864,900 and 1,122.57 kg, respectively.

As mentioned above, integrating renewable energy with waste heat recovery provides a complete integrated system that can simultaneously address issues such as the power supply, cooling demands, and waste heat utilization within a unified framework. Hydrogen acts as a bridge between intermittent renewable energy and stable power demand with the advantages of being green, flexible, and facile, along with wide scalability and the ability to combine with other renewable energy technologies. The innovation of this study lies in three key aspects: (1) It achieves deep coupling of multiple energy forms (electricity, hydrogen, cooling, and heating) by integrating photovoltaic (PV) modules, PEME, PEMFC, and ARC, realizing cascade utilization of energy from converting intermittent solar energy into storable chemical energy (hydrogen) to recycling low-grade waste heat for cooling and heating, which breaks the limitations of single-energy systems and improves overall energy utilization efficiency. (2) It proposes a demand-adaptive operation strategy that prioritizes the PV power supply for the data center and uses surplus electricity for hydrogen production while deploying hydrogen-powered PEMFC during peak electricity price periods or low solar irradiance. This strategy not only solves the problem of solar energy intermittency but also optimizes energy allocation based on time-of-use electricity prices, enhancing the economic competitiveness of the system. (3) It conducts a comprehensive and systematic assessment covering thermodynamic (energy and exergy analysis), economic (life-cycle cost and annual operating cost analysis), and environmental (CO2 emission reduction) dimensions and clarifies the optimal proportion of PV participation (40% of annual power demand) by analyzing different PV penetration scenarios. This provides a scalable and efficient technical solution for low-carbon data centers, especially in regions with moderate solar resources and growing energy demands. A comprehensive energy and exergy analysis is conducted to evaluate the thermodynamic performance of the polygeneration system, and the economic and environmental benefits brought by changes in the proportion of PV participation in the energy supply system are assessed. The goal of this study is to provide a scalable and efficient power system for low-carbon data centers, particularly in regions with moderate solar resources and increasing energy demands.

System Description and Modeling

System Description

Figure illustrates the schematic diagram of the proposed polygeneration system for the data center, which consists of four main subsystems: photovoltaic (PV) cells, a proton exchange membrane (PEM) electrolyzer, a hydrogen fuel cell (PEMFC), and a LiBr absorption chiller (ARC). Reliable power is provided to the data center through coordinated energy supply from the utility grid, the PV system, and the PEMFC. During periods of sufficient solar irradiation, the PV system directly powers the data center, while excess electricity is used to power the PEM electrolyzer to produce hydrogen. The generated hydrogen is stored in a high-pressure tank, thereby converting electrical energy into storable chemical energy. When solar radiation is insufficient or during grid peak-load periods, the stored hydrogen is fed into the PEMFC for power generation, supplementing electricity to meet the power demand of the data center. The low-grade waste heat from the PEMFC drives the ARC, generating cooling water to satisfy the data center’s cooling demands. Simultaneously, the heated water by the ARC is recovered and, together with the waste heat from the data center, is collected and stored in a thermal storage tank. A part of the stored hot water is used to supply domestic hot water (DHW) for office areas within the data center. During heating demand periods, the stored hot water serves as a heat source for space heating, enabling efficient recovery and utilization of waste heat.

Schematic diagram of the proposed polygeneration system for the data center.

In the ARC system, the LiBr-H2O dilute solution in the absorber is first pumped to the generator, where it is preheated by solution heat exchangers. It is then further heated by the waste heat from PEMFC in the generator, causing part of the water in the solution to evaporate as high-temperature vapor. The remaining concentrated solution was flowed into the absorber. The water vapor produced in the generator is then directed to the condenser, where it is condensed into liquid water and sent to the evaporator. In the evaporator, the liquid water is vaporized, which is subsequently absorbed by the concentrated LiBr-H2O solution in the absorber, completing the closed-loop cycle. During this process, the ambient temperature water flowing through the absorber and condenser is heated and can be used as domestic hot water or a space heating source. Meanwhile, the supplied water in the evaporator, after absorbing heat, is supplied to the data center as chilled water. Significantly, this integrated design featuring multienergy coupling and cascade utilization is conducive to improving overall energy efficiency and reducing energy consumption, which provides an effective solution for achieving low-carbon and efficient operation of data centers.

System Assumption

The mathematical modeling of each component is conducted in MATLAB, followed by thermodynamic calculations at various state points and a comprehensive performance analysis. Some reasonable assumptions are made to simplify the modeling process:(1)The system operates under steady-state conditions. , (2)Pressure and heat losses within the system are neglected except for those explicitly defined. (3)No leakage of working fluids occurs throughout the operation of the system. (4)The solution heat exchanger efficiency is assumed as 0.9, and the pump efficiency is assumed as 0.7. (5)In the ARC system, both the refrigerant leaving the generator and evaporator and the LiBr/H2O solution exiting the generator and absorber are assumed to be in saturated states.

Data Center Energy Mix Analysis

Hyvönen et al. evaluated the potential of integrating photovoltaics into data centers in Finland and northern Japan, noting that their economic benefits are highly sensitive to electricity price levels. Large external PV systems were found to be cost-effective under the high electricity prices of 2022, whereas small rooftop PV systems offered greater economic benefits at the 2019 electricity price levels. The results indicate that the economic benefits of photovoltaics depend not only on solar resources but also on local electricity prices.

In this study, six power-supply scenarios are developed based on the proportion of annual electricity demand met by PV generation, as summarized in Table . Scenario 6 represents a conventional data center powered entirely by the utility grid and serves as a reference case. The optimal scenario is determined by comparing the economic benefits and CO2 emissions of the data center in different scenarios. Figure presents the annual operating costs and CO2 emissions of the data center under different power-supply scenarios. As the share of PV generation increases, the annual operating cost rises, while the CO2 emissions decrease. Benchmarking against International Hydrogen Strategies and System Scalability

Figure illustrates the levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH) and levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) under different allocation scenarios of photovoltaic (PV) generation between the data center and the electrolyzer. The results show that the LCOE remains relatively stable in the range of 0.066–0.071 USD/kWh, while LCOH varies more significantly, from 3.02 to 6.52 USD/kg, depending on the share of PV electricity allocated to electrolysis. The optimal trade-off occurs when 40% of PV electricity is directly supplied to the data center (Senario4, The proportion of PV generation in the energy demand of the data center = 40%), yielding the lowest LCOE (0.0665 USD/kWh) and a competitive LCOH (3.36 USD/kg).

LCOH and LCOE across different scenarios: comparison with IEA and Australian targets.

When compared with international hydrogen cost targets, the proposed system demonstrates clear adaptability. The IEA reports current renewable hydrogen costs in the range of 4.5–6.5 USD/kg, while the Australian National Hydrogen Strategy sets a 2030 target of ≤2.5 USD/kg. Under favorable scenarios, such as policy subsidies or deployment in high-irradiation regions, the present system’s LCOH can approach or even fall below these benchmarks. For example, relocating the system to Australia’s solar-rich regions would increase PV output by ∼60%, reducing the LCOH to ∼2.3 USD/kg and thereby outperforming the Australian target. Similarly, incorporating EU-style electrolyzer subsidies (≈30%) would decrease the LCOH to ∼2.7 USD/kg, bringing it closer to the European Union’s indicative 2030 cost target (∼2 EUR/kg). Beyond international benchmarking, the scalability of the system was also considered. For a megascale data center (10,000 cabinets, ∼42 MW), proportional expansion of PV and hydrogen equipment reduces LCOE to ∼0.062 USD/kWh and LCOH to ∼3.0 USD/kg, benefiting from economies of scale and enhanced waste-heat utilization. In more complex scenarios, such as multienergy network coupling or cross-regional hydrogen transport, additional revenue streams from electricity exports, district heating, and hydrogen delivery can further lower effective costs, maintaining competitiveness even under large-scale deployment.

Overall, the analysis confirms that the PV–hydrogen–data center system not only achieves a balanced cost-performance trade-off at moderate PV allocation ratios but also aligns with international hydrogen strategies when supported by favorable policies or resource conditions. This adaptability, combined with scalability to larger and more integrated energy systems, underscores its potential as a replicable model for green data center development worldwide.

Exergy Analysis

Figure illustrates the monthly variations in the exergy efficiency, domestic hot water, and space heating of the integrated system. The results indicate that the exergy efficiency tends to be lower during months with strong solar irradiation. In high-irradiance months, surplus PV power is frequently used to drive the PEM electrolyzer for hydrogen production, which has a relatively low energy conversion efficiency, thereby increasing the exergy destruction. Among all months, December records the highest exergy efficiency at 28.5%, whereas August shows the lowest at 24.8%. During the heating season, a substantial portion of the stored hydrogen is directed to the PEMFC for power generation. The low-grade heat released in this process effectively drives the LiBr ARC system, which promotes the waste heat recovery from the data center for domestic hot water and space heating. Although the energy efficiency of PEMFC remains relatively modest, the effective recovery and utilization of waste heat substantially enhance the overall energy utilization efficiency.

Monthly waste heat recovery capacity and exergy efficiency.

Figure shows the distribution of exergy destruction among components of the integrated system. As shown in Figure (a), PV modules exhibit the largest exergy loss, reaching 26,183 kW, primarily due to their relatively low conversion efficiency of about 20%, which causes most of the incident solar energy to dissipate as heat to the environment. The second largest contributor is the electrolyzer, with an exergy loss of 1,510 kW. In contrast, the PEMFC exhibits lower exergy losses than the electrolyzer because the total available energy of hydrogen supplied to the PEMFC, after production and storage, is significantly lower than the input energy to the electrolyzer. Figure (b) shows that in the LiBr ARC, the absorber and condenser account for a large proportion of exergy losses. Because these components exchange sensible heat with external cooling media under relatively large temperature driving forces. Specifically, when the inlet cooling water temperature is too low, the temperature difference between the working fluid and cooling water increases substantially. Which intensifies thermodynamic irreversibility, leading to greater energy dissipation. Therefore, if the cooling water temperature is not properly matched with the operating conditions, additional irreversibility may be introduced, ultimately reducing the overall exergy efficiency and negatively impacting the energy performance and economic viability of the refrigeration cycle.

Conclusions

This study proposes and evaluates an integrated polygeneration system for data centers, incorporating photovoltaic (PV) modules, a PEM electrolyzer, a PEM fuel cell (PEMFC), and a lithium bromide (LiBr) absorption chiller. The system utilizes surplus PV electricity during high solar irradiance periods to produce hydrogen via water electrolysis, which is stored and subsequently reconverted into electricity by the PEMFC during peak-electricity-price periods in the nighttime or when PV output is insufficient. This configuration not only optimizes the temporal allocation of renewable energy but also improves energy efficiency, operational flexibility, and cost-effectiveness while significantly reducing greenhouse gas emissions. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:(1)The proposed system enables multienergy complementarity and synergistic utilization of power, heating, and cooling. The low-grade waste heat generated by the PEM electrolyzer and PEMFC is used to drive absorption chillers, which not only recovers waste heat from the data center for domestic hot water and heating but also significantly reduces the cooling load during high-temperature season. Results indicate that, the integrated system demonstrates high comprehensive energy efficiency in different seasons, with an average PUE is reduced to 1.25, and an exergy efficiency of up to 28.5% during the heating season.(2)When solar energy is available, the power from PV is first supplied to power the data center, and the surplus is directed to the PEM electrolyzer for hydrogen production via water electrolysis. The stored hydrogen is released to PEMFC for heating, cooling and power cogeneration during peak-price periods in nighttime or when solar irradiance is insufficient. The integrated system achieves temporal shifting of renewable energy utilization. This strategy effectively reduces grid electricity purchases during high-price intervals, increases PV utilization efficiency, enhances system flexibility, and lowers operational costs, thereby improving the economic competitiveness of the data center under time-of-use pricing schemes.(3)Economic modeling of levelized costs demonstrates that the 40% PV allocation scenario also achieves the lowest LCOE (0.0665 USD/kWh) and a competitive LCOH (3.36 USD/kg), both of which fall within current international benchmarks. These results highlight the economic viability and global competitiveness of the proposed polygeneration system, thereby contributing a quantitative framework to guide renewable-hydrogen integration in data centers.(4)Comprehensive thermodynamic, economic, and environmental assessments reveal that, a configuration where PV accounts for 40% of the total annual power demand of the data center, delivers optimal scheme in trade-off between operating costs and CO2 emission, yielding annual savings of approximately $238,000 and reducing CO2 emissions by 2,940 tonnes.
