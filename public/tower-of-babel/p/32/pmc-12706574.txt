Recent Advances in the Utilization of Imidazolium‐Based Ionic Liquids for Chemical‐Enhanced Oil Recovery: A Comprehensive Review
Authors: Noran Mousa, Basim Abu‐Jdayil, Abdulrazag Y Zekri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing global energy demand and the declining efficiency of conventional oil recovery methods underscore the urgency for advanced enhanced oil recovery (EOR) techniques. While chemical EOR enhances recovery, traditional surfactants face limitations such as high retention, thermal instability, and poor performance in high‐salinity environments. Imidazolium‐based ionic liquids (ILs) have emerged as a promising alternative due to their superior thermal stability, tunable interfacial properties, and potential for recyclability. This review evaluates their role in EOR, focusing on interfacial tension (IFT) reduction, wettability alteration, emulsification, viscosity control, and crude oil interactions. Long‐chain ILs like [C16mim][Br] achieve over 99.8% IFT reduction while improving rock wettability and dispersing asphaltenes—key for enhancing oil mobility and recovery. In addition to technical performance, the review addresses the economic feasibility and environmental sustainability of ILs. Despite higher initial costs, their advancement in synthesis, lower consumption rates, reusability, and reduced ecological impact offer long‐term advantages over conventional surfactants. Future directions include hybrid IL formulations, large‐scale applications, and AI‐assisted molecular design to optimize EOR efficiency across varied reservoir conditions.Keywords: chemical flooding, enhanced oil recovery, imidazolium ionic liquids, interfacial tension reduction, oil recovery efficiency, sustainable oil recovery, wettability alteration

Introduction

The global demand for oil and gas continues to rise, driven by increasing energy consumption across industries and households. However, supply from conventional sources struggles to keep pace due to declining production rates from existing fields. Primary recovery methods rely on natural pressure, while secondary recovery techniques, such as water and gas injection, together extract only about 20–30% of the original oil in place, leaving over 70% of reserves untapped.[ 1 ] As a result, the oil industry is experiencing supply shortages, which require advanced extraction methods. Despite efforts to increase production, the global oil supply remains limited, with conventional production declining and unconventional sources becoming crucial.[ 2 ] The global recovery rate from hydrocarbon reservoirs is estimated to reach only one‐third of the original oil.[ 3 ] Growing energy demands and the constraints of primary and secondary recovery methods highlight the urgent need for enhanced oil recovery (EOR) technologies to boost extraction efficiency and ensure a stable global energy supply.

EOR is vital in the oil industry as it enhances the oil recovery from mature and declining reservoirs. Traditional primary and secondary recovery techniques extract only about 20–40% of the original oil in place. EOR methods can enhance the recovery to 30–60% by modifying the reservoir properties and making them more suitable for extraction.[ 4 ] Currently, around 2% of global crude oil production relies on EOR, with certain countries like Turkey achieving 15% of their crude oil output using EOR techniques.[ 5 ] The effectiveness of EOR is evident in regions like North America, where investments in EOR have led to a 14% growth in proven oil reserves recorded between 2007 and 2017.[ 6 ]

There are three primary categories of EOR: thermal, chemical, and gas injection. Thermal EOR, including steam injection, is commonly used for heavy oil reservoirs, with steam flooding accounting for ≈10% of China's oil output.[ 7 ] Chemical EOR, including polymer flooding, is another standard method, especially in China, which constitutes nearly 86% of EOR applications.[ 8 ] Gas injection, particularly CO2 injection, has shown promise in extending reservoir life and improving oil displacement, especially when combined with other techniques.[ 9 ] However, EOR methods encounter substantial challenges, such as high operational costs, scalability issues, and environmental concerns. For instance, nanoparticle‐enhanced EOR methods experience cost and deployment barriers, even though they demonstrate up to a 30% increase in oil production recovery.[ 6 ] While nanotechnology and hybrid EOR methods continue to advance, addressing economic and environmental challenges is essential for their wider acceptance.[ 10 ]

One of the key techniques in chemical EOR is surfactant flooding, which involves injecting surface‐active agents to reduce the oil–water interfacial tension (IFT) and mobilize trapped oil. Surfactants are amphiphilic molecules composed of a hydrophobic hydrocarbon “tail” and a hydrophilic “head” group. By accumulating at the oil–water interface, they significantly lower IFT and can improve oil displacement. Surfactant flooding has been observed to yield up to ≈20% higher oil recovery compared to water flooding alone.[ 11 ] However, surfactant‐based EOR methods encounter challenges regarding high surfactant adsorption in porous media, low stability under elevated temperature and salinity conditions, and environmental pollution.[ 12 ] Despite these challenges, surfactant EOR remains an active research area, focusing on developing more efficient, environmentally friendly, and cost‐effective formulations capable of functioning in harsh reservoir conditions.[ 13 ] Driven by these challenges, researchers have turned to alternative chemical agents that could overcome the drawbacks of traditional surfactants. Ionic liquids (ILs) are a potentially greener alternative to conventional surfactants due to their low volatility, tunability, and recyclability. However, their overall environmental impact must be assessed on a case‐by‐case basis, considering their synthesis, toxicity, and environmental fate.

ILs are not conventional surfactants but rather organic salts (comprised entirely of cations and anions) that are liquid at or below 100 °C. A typical IL consists of a bulky organic cation (for example, an imidazolium or quaternary ammonium ion) paired with an inorganic or organic anion.[ 14 ] This fundamentally differs from a single surfactant molecule's structure. Unlike surfactants, which have a fixed hydrophilic–hydrophobic architecture, ILs are ionic compounds with highly tunable chemistry. This can be done by changing the cation/anion combination to tailor an IL's properties (polarity, solvation, thermal stability, etc.) to suit specific needs.[ 14 ] Many imidazolium‐based ILs and other ILs with long alkyl chains on the cation are surface‐active ILs (SAILs).[ 15 ] Recent studies indicate that ILs can demonstrate lower critical micelle concentration (CMC) values than conventional surfactants, resulting in higher efficiency at reduced concentrations.[ 16 , 17 ]

Owing to their unique properties, such as low volatility, high thermal stability, and tunable viscosity, ILs have attracted considerable interest in EOR. Their primary advantages in EOR include reducing IFT, altering rock wettability, stabilizing emulsions, and improving oil displacement efficiency.[ 18 ] Moreover, ILs are often considered environmentally friendly alternatives to traditional surfactants. Their nonvolatile nature means they do not emit harmful vapors, and certain ILs can be synthesized for biodegradability or lower toxicity, making them attractive from an environmental standpoint.[ 15 ]

ILs used in EOR come in various types, including imidazolium‐based, pyridinium‐based, ammonium‐based, phosphonium‐based, and poly‐ILs (PILs), demonstrating different effectiveness based on reservoir conditions. For example, PILs show excellent salt tolerance exceeding 10% and thermal stability up to 90 °C, making them particularly well‐suited for high‐salinity reservoirs.[ 19 ] Studies have demonstrated that ILs can improve oil recovery in carbonate reservoirs via spontaneous imbibition, with natural recovery rates between 27% and 34%, substantially exceeding the effectiveness of conventional surfactants, which usually boost recovery by just 11%.[ 20 ] These findings highlight the promise of ILs as viable options for EOR applications, offering a more efficient, sustainable, and cost‐effective alternative to traditional chemical flooding methods.

Imidazolium‐based ILs exhibit superior efficiency in EOR compared to other ILs, thanks to their capacity to significantly reduce IFT, modify wettability, enhance emulsification, offer excellent compatibility with reservoir conditions, and provide remarkable tunability. Recent research indicates that imidazolium ILs, such as [C16mim][Br], can decrease IFT values from 32.74 mN m−1 to as low as 0.051 mN m−1, achieving an impressive reduction of 99.8%, thereby boosting oil displacement efficiency.[ 16 ] In contrast, phosphonium‐based ILs generally show lower IFT reduction efficiency, with the highest reductions ranging from 74.42% to 92.99%.[ 21 ] Likewise, ammonium‐based ILs demonstrate moderate capabilities for reducing IFT, but they are typically less effective than imidazolium ILs at achieving ultralow IFT values.[ 22 ] Overall, imidazolium ILs surpass ammonium and phosphonium‐based ILs in reducing IFT, enhancing oil recovery efficiency, and altering wettability, thus establishing them as the most effective ILs for EOR applications.

This review aims to provide a comprehensive and up‐to‐date analysis of the advancements in using imidazolium‐based ILs for EOR. The main goal is to assess the physicochemical properties, interfacial dynamics, and efficiency of imidazolium ILs in lowering IFT, modifying wettability, stabilizing emulsions, changing viscosity, lowering adsorption, and enhancing oil displacement effectiveness under various reservoir conditions. This study thoroughly investigates the promise of imidazolium ILs as a sustainable and highly efficient replacement for traditional chemical‐EOR agents while also addressing operational issues, cost‐effectiveness, and environmental consequences. The scope of this review involves laboratory experiments, core flooding research, and cutting‐edge innovations in IL‐modified surfactants, emphasizing key performance indicators such as additional oil recovery (AOR%), IFT reduction, and adsorption minimization. Additionally, this review paper integrates the essential principles governing IL behavior in oil recovery and discusses new trends, such as hybrid IL formulations for optimizing EOR performance.

This study is the first comprehensive review to analyze the advancements in research related to imidazolium ILs for EOR up to 2025. While several existing reviews address the general impact of ILs in EOR, none deeply explore and discuss the complete characterization and evaluation testing of imidazolium‐based ILs in this regard, which has demonstrated exceptional effectiveness in modifying reservoir properties and improving oil recovery efficiency. The originality of this review stems from its thorough comparative evaluation of different alkyl chain lengths of imidazolium ILs with a variety of complex and straightforward anions, highlighting their unique benefits regarding IFT reduction, wettability changes, and reservoir compatibility. By systematically assessing the latest developments, this review serves as a crucial resource for researchers and industry experts looking for innovative, cost‐efficient, and environmentally sustainable solutions for the future of EOR.

Unique Properties of ILs Relevant to EOR

ILs are becoming increasingly recognized for their unique properties that make them ideal for EOR applications. One of the main characteristics of ILs is their minimal vapor pressure, which often approaches negligible levels and nearly eliminates the volatility issue at high temperatures typically faced in EOR operations. This attribute significantly decreases material loss due to evaporation, making ILs a more environmentally friendly option compared to more volatile organic solvents.[ 24 ] Compared to traditional chemicals, their low volatility and thermal stability reduce environmental hazards and emissions.[ 25 ] Additionally, ILs display various solubilities that can be customized for specific applications. This allows for the dissolution of different organic compounds, which is advantageous for mobilizing oil in reservoirs.[ 26 ] Another significant advantage of ILs in EOR is their impressive thermal stability. Imidazolium‐based ILs can withstand temperatures of up to 300 °C,[ 27 ] which is especially beneficial for thermal EOR methods that utilize high temperatures to lower the viscosity of heavy crude oils (HCOs).[ 28 ]

Additionally, ILs have lower CMCs than traditional surfactants, potentially reducing overall chemical costs.[ 29 ] ILs effectively reduce IFT and change wettability, resulting in higher recovery rates. Certain ILs can be separated and reused from produced water using ion‐exchange resins, achieving recovery rates of up to 95%.[ 30 ] ILs can be utilized with traditional injection systems, and their stability across different salinities supports deployment in various reservoirs.[ 31 ] Furthermore, ILs are effective corrosion inhibitors, minimizing material degradation in high‐salinity and high‐temperature environments.[ 32 ] Also, ILs with optimized chemical compositions demonstrate minimal corrosive effects, ensuring equipment longevity.

EOR is a vital area of research aimed at improving the efficiency of extracting residual oil from mature reservoirs. As global energy demand grows, EOR technologies are evolving to incorporate more innovative and effective methods. Among these, utilizing ILs as surfactants has earned significant attention. ILs, recognized for their unique chemical and physical properties, provide several advantages over traditional surfactants. These include enhanced thermal stability, reduced environmental impact, and the capacity to function across various salinity and temperature conditions. Pillai et al.[ 29 ] and Lago et al.[ 33 ] highlight the significance of ILs in lowering IFT to improve oil recovery. However, Pillai et al.[ 29 ] concentrate on imidazolium‐based ILs, whereas Lago et al.[ 33 ] examine phosphonium‐based ILs, with each study demonstrating that their respective types of ILs effectively reduce IFT under their specific conditions. Studies like those by Pillai et al.[ 29 ] and Pereira et al.[ 34 ] show that imidazolium and pyridinium‐based ILs can alter wettability, which is critical for displacing oil from reservoir rocks. The specific types and concentrations of ILs can differ, yet the main aim is to shift rock surfaces from being oil‐wet to more water‐wet. Both ILs enhance stability in tough conditions, but their environmental effects and potential for field reuse are crucial factors to consider.

Environmental, Economic, and Sustainability Aspects of ILs

Environmental Impacts of ILs

ILs are multifunctional compounds classified into various types, each characterized by unique structural properties, toxicity levels, biodegradability, and environmental effects. This section outlines these classifications and examines their ecological repercussions, as illustrated in Table 2 . The analysis emphasizes the importance of careful IL selection to balance effective performance and environmental sustainability. Specific adaptations, such as oxygen functionalization, can minimize the ecological footprint of ILs, making them more viable as environmentally friendly alternatives for industrial use. Continued research into their long‐term environmental consequences is essential for confirming their green credentials. 1) Protic ILs (PILs): PILs are ILs that facilitate the transfer of a proton from a Brønsted acid to a Brønsted base. They frequently display hydrogen‐bonding networks, influencing their chemical properties and improving their conductivity.[ 35 ] 2) Pyridinium‐based ILs: These ILs consist of pyridinium cation and are valued for their electrochemical stability and versatility in organic synthesis. Functionalizing with nitrile groups improves their solvent properties, though their toxicity varies with the alkyl chain length.[ 36 ] 3) Fluorinated ILs (FILs): FILs contain fluorinated cations or anions, providing thermal stability and hydrophobicity. They are advantageous in electrochemical applications, although concerns about persistence and aquatic toxicity persist.[ 37 ] 4) Aprotic ILs (AILs): These ILs lack hydrogen donors and generally exhibit more excellent stability than PILs. They find applications in energy storage systems and chemical synthesis but frequently demonstrate increased toxicity and reduced biodegradability.[ 38 ] 5) Choline‐based ILs: Derived from naturally occurring choline, these ILs are biodegradable and demonstrate low toxicity, making them environmentally preferable alternatives.[ 39 ] Chlorine ILs are water‐soluble and salt‐tolerant (due to their hygroscopic nature), but they may require pH buffering under very alkaline or high‐temperature conditions. 6) Caprolactam‐based ILs: They are an emerging class of ILs that offer significant cost and safety advantages. They can be synthesized easily by neutralizing caprolactam with various acids, yielding ILs that are generally low toxicity and inexpensive.[ 40 ] Caprolactam ILs generally stabilize in harsh environments (like other ILs). Still, they may require strong acid coanions (e.g., HSO4 −) to impart acidity, and their environmental fate has not been thoroughly studied. 7) Hydroxyl‐functionalized ILs (e.g., ILs with –OH, ether, or ester groups on the cation) combine the tunability of ILs with added hydrophilicity. Introducing oxygenated groups reduces their hydrophobicity and lipophilicity, leading to significantly lower toxicity and higher water solubility than their nonfunctional analogs. Hydroxyl or ester substituents on imidazolium or ammonium cations enhance biodegradability since ester bonds are hydrolyzable. These functionalized ILs remain highly thermally stable (often stable above 200–300°C) and generally maintain excellent salt tolerance. The main drawback lies in synthesis, as hydroxyl or ester groups raise production cost and complexity, with esters prone to hydrolysis in alkaline brines, forming by‐products.[ 41 ] 8) Imidazolium‐based ILs: These ILs are characterized by an imidazolium cation and are widely utilized due to their tunable properties. However, their long alkyl chains slightly increase toxicity and decrease biodegradability. By incorporating oxygen groups (e.g., esters or hydroxyls), these ILs exhibit enhanced biodegradability and reduced toxicity compared to standard imidazolium ILs. This functionalization helps lessen environmental persistence.[ 41 ]

ILs have often been labeled “green solvents” due to desirable properties like negligible vapor pressure and high thermal stability.[ 31 ] These traits mean ILs do not emit volatile organic compounds and are nonflammable, which is beneficial for air quality and safety, as illustrated in Figure 1 . However, being “green” in one aspect does not automatically translate to overall environmental benignity. Most standard imidazolium ILs are not readily biodegradable by microbes.[ 42 ] They can persist in water and soil, potentially becoming sources of pollutants. Thus, special conditions such as photochemical or advanced oxidation are required to break them down.[ 43 ] The toxicity of ILs generally increases with alkyl chain length on the cation, as the more hydrophobic, long‐chain ILs tend to bioaccumulate in lipid membranes.[ 36 ]

Environmental impact and sustainability assessment of ILs.

However, the tunable nature of IL chemistry allows for designing more ecobenign ILs. For instance, shorter‐chain or functionalized ILs are often less toxic, and researchers are actively exploring functional group substitutions to reduce IL toxicity.[ 36 ] Also, using biodegradable cations or anions can improve the profile. Choline‐based ILs (choline is a natural quaternary ammonium from biomass) paired with amino acid anions are one example: these so‐called amino acid ILs are reported to have good biodegradability, very low toxicity, and relatively low cost.[ 44 ] Some research has found that changing the anion can also influence toxicity and biodegradation. For example, ILs with easily hydrolyzable anions (like acetate or other organic anions) may break down faster than those with inert anions (PF6− and BF4−), which tend to persist.[ 36 ]

Considerations on the Cost‐Effectiveness of ILs in EOR

Another sustainability pillar is assessing the economic viability of ILs. ILs are increasingly recognized as innovative and effective alternatives to traditional chemicals in EOR. Their unique properties—such as customizable structures, thermal stability, and outstanding performance in high‐salinity reservoirs—make them ideal candidates for enhancing oil recovery. However, the widespread adoption of ILs depends on understanding their economic implications, including production and operational costs, scalability, and overall feasibility for large‐scale deployment. Various types of ILs—imidazolium‐based, phosphonium‐based, choline‐based, and oxygen‐functionalized—offer distinct advantages while facing specific economic challenges based on their chemistry and application. This review paper compares these ILs comprehensively, emphasizing their financial factors and underscoring the balance between costs and performance that influences their practical utility in EOR operations.

Imidazolium‐based ILs have moderate production costs due to the chemical complexity of synthesizing alkyl chain‐functionalized cations and compatible anions. Still, their production is increasingly justified by their superior performance in harsh reservoir conditions. Long‐chain imidazolium ILs, such as [C12mim][Cl] and [C16mim][Br], have demonstrated remarkable IFT reduction, surpassing traditional surfactants like cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) in laboratory experiments.[ 29 , 45 ] Imidazolium ILs’ chemical diversity and tunability allow for customization for specific EOR applications. They serve as surfactants and demulsifiers, thus increasing their cost‐effectiveness.[ 46 ]

Historically, ILs (especially imidazolium salts) were expensive specialty chemicals, sometimes costing $100–$1000 per kilogram in early 2000s literature. This would be prohibitive for large‐scale EOR applications requiring tons of chemicals. However, significant progress has been made in reducing IL costs through process innovations and bulk manufacturing. Some simple ILs are already cost‐competitive with traditional chemicals. For example, a technoeconomic analysis estimated that 1‐methylimidazolium hydrogen sulfate (an IL with a simple imidazolium cation and an inexpensive acid anion) could be produced for about $3–$6 per kg.[ 47 ] Even more impressively, triethylammonium hydrogen sulfate (a protic IL) was projected as low as $1.24 per kg, which is cheaper than some conventional organic solvents.[ 47 ] These estimates come from process models at the industrial scale and indicate that, for certain ILs, raw materials and production methods can be optimized to very low costs. Specifically for imidazolium ILs used in EOR, a recent advancement by Choi et al.[ 48 ] developed an improved synthesis/purification using a refrigerant as an antisolvent. This method achieved high‐purity [Bmim][BF4] at an estimated minimum selling price of about $12 000 per ton (≈$12 per kg).[ 48 ] This price, on the order of $10/kg, is within the same magnitude as specialty surfactants used in oil fields. In general, imidazolium ILs are becoming more economically feasible as manufacturing technology improves. These developments make imidazolium ILs increasingly competitive with other EOR chemicals on a cost basis.[ 49 ]

Furthermore, certain ILs are already produced commercially for other industries (e.g., Ammoeng series ILs). Bin‐Dahbag et al.[ 31 ] noted that the IL they tested (Ammoeng 102, a tetraalkylammonium IL) was commercially available and relatively low in cost.[ 31 ] In addition, choline‐based ILs come from natural, renewable sources, significantly lowering their production costs and making them economically attractive.[ 39 ] Similarly, caprolactam‐based ILs are an inexpensive and readily available industrial chemical, typically reacted with Brønsted acids to form low‐cost, thermally stable ILs. Their synthesis involves simple acid–base reactions, and recent studies have shown promising viscosity reduction effects on HCO at elevated temperatures, with lower toxicity compared to conventional ILs.[ 40 , 50 ]

However, phosphonium‐based ILs are among the most expensive to produce due to the complex synthesis of their large cation‐anion structures, limiting their availability.[ 33 ] Also, FILs, such as those containing the bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([NTf2]−) anion or other perfluorinated components, also incur substantially higher costs.[ 51 ] The fluorinated anions themselves are very expensive. As a result, these ILs can cost an order of magnitude more than simple imidazolium halides or acetates. Lastly, oxygen‐functionalized ILs provide reservoir‐specific advantages but require extra functionalization steps, resulting in moderately higher production costs.[ 41 ] The economic feasibility of ILs for EOR involves balancing costs with performance benefits.

One of the original motivations for calling ILs “green” was their reusability in closed‐loop processes. In EOR, if ILs can be recycled from produced water and reinjected, this aligns with the green chemistry goal of reducing waste. Conventional surfactants are typically not recovered; once they have performed their function, they remain in the reservoir or are produced with water and disposed of. They often degrade or precipitate in high‐salinity brines and temperatures above ≈60–80 °C. In contrast, imidazolium ILs have been shown to remain stable and functional in very harsh reservoir conditions of high temperature and salinity.[ 42 ]

IL Recovery and Recycling

The recyclability efficiency of ILs in EOR is vital for their cost‐effectiveness and environmental sustainability. Previous research has demonstrated that certain ILs can be recycled up to five or six times without losing efficiency.[ 52 ] Imidazolium‐based ILs, such as 1‐ethyl‐3‐methylimidazolium chloride ([EMIM][Cl]), demonstrate promising recovery potential with minimal degradation after multiple cycles.[ 53 ] Additionally, phosphonium‐based ILs, such as trihexyl(tetradecyl)phosphonium chloride ([THTDPh][Cl]), demonstrate high stability under extreme conditions and can be separated for reuse; however, their larger molecular structures present challenges for complete recovery.[ 54 ] Notably, choline‐based ILs are recognized for their biodegradability and exhibit excellent recyclability, attributed to their water solubility, facilitating easy separation and reuse via aqueous washing techniques.[ 18 ]

Furthermore, FILs, despite being highly stable, have poor biodegradability and need advanced separation methods, like ion‐exchange resins, to ensure efficient recovery.[ 30 ] Lastly, PILs are attracting attention for their adjustable properties and EOR performance, with membrane filtration and solvent extraction techniques demonstrating the potential for their recovery.[ 19 ] While IL recyclability varies based on its chemical composition and interaction with crude oil, advanced separation technologies such as pervaporation, pressure‐driven membrane technologies, ion exchange, and distillation are essential for enhancing their economic feasibility and environmental impact.[ 55 , 56 ]

ILs can be separated from water or other volatiles by vacuum distillation (rotary evaporation). Because most ILs have negligible vapor pressure, heating the recovered mixture evaporates the water, leaving the IL as a nonvolatile residue.[ 57 ] This simple method employed by Sakthivel et al. achieved high IL recovery in laboratory tests. For example, Parviainen et al. reported ≈95.6 wt% recovery of a dialkyl imidazolium acetate IL from aqueous solution using a rotary evaporator.[ 58 ] Thus, while vacuum distillation is effective for concentrating and purifying ILs, it is energy‐intensive.[ 57 ]

Ion‐exchange resins (particularly cation‐exchange types) offer a facile way to capture and later release ILs from dilute solutions. In the method demonstrated by Khoo et al.[ 57 ] a sulfonic acid resin was added to an aqueous solution containing imidazolium IL; the IL's cation was exchanged with H+ on the resin, binding the IL onto the solid phase. This process can remove the IL from water with high efficiency—up to 90–95%.[ 57 ] The IL can then be recovered by eluting the resin with a strong acid or salt solution, which releases the IL cation and regenerates the resin for reuse. Ion‐exchange adsorption is cost‐effective and scalable, but its performance may be affected by competing ions or impurities in the mixture.

In addition to the conventional recycling techniques, stimuli‐responsive recovery methods are gaining attention as innovative ways to simplify IL separation. These methods leverage external triggers (gas, temperature, pH, and light) to switch the phase behavior of IL‐containing systems reversibly. CO2‐switchable ILs/surfactants: Certain ILs or IL‐based surfactants are designed to change their solubility or aggregation state in the presence of CO2. For example, Pei et al.[ 59 ] reported a CO2‐responsive IL microemulsion system that could be reversibly switched from a stable water/oil single phase to a biphasic (completely separated oil and water) state by bubbling CO2 gas and switched back by removing CO2.[ 59 ] Indeed, among various stimuli (light, pH, redox, etc.), CO2 is highlighted as cost‐effective, nontoxic, and easily implementable in the field.[ 60 ]

Several ILs exhibit temperature‐dependent miscibility with water. These thermo‐responsive ILs have a critical solution temperature where the single‐phase IL–water mixture splits into two phases.[ 61 ] Typically, ILs with moderate hydrophilicity can display a lower critical solution temperature (LCST) behavior. They are completely miscible with water at ambient conditions but will phase‐separate into IL‐rich and water‐rich phases upon heating above the LCST. This thermal trigger avoids adding chemicals, and the phase transition is reversible. However, a practical limitation is the energy required to heat large volumes of fluid; thus, ongoing research is optimizing IL structures to lower these transition temperatures or use geothermal temperature gradients.

Adjusting the pH of an IL‐containing solution can also toggle its miscibility. Many protic ILs or polyionic mixtures will phase‐separate when the solution pH is shifted to protonate/deprotonate particular species. In one demonstration, a pH‐switchable aqueous biphasic system was created using an imidazolium IL and citrate salt; at high pH, the mixture was single‐phase, but upon lowering the pH, the salt converted to citric acid (less dissociated), causing the IL to separate into a distinct layer.[ 62 ] Applied to EOR, this concept means that by injecting an acidic or basic solution into the produced water (or leveraging the naturally acidic CO2 as a pH‐lowering agent), one might “flip a switch” and cause the IL to partition into a separable phase.

An emerging frontier is the design of ILs that respond to light, enabling remote, on‐demand separation. This is typically achieved by incorporating a photo‐switchable functional group into the IL (for instance, an azobenzene moiety on the IL cation). Under UV light, azobenzene groups convert from a trans‐ to a cis‐isomer, which is more polar and disrupts hydrophobic interactions; under visible light, they revert to the trans‐form Li et al.[ 63 ] recently showed that an azobenzene‐based IL surfactant (paired with an inorganic salt) could be switched between forming a single‐phase solution and an aqueous two‐phase system (ATPS) by alternating UV and visible light exposure.[ 63 ] The IL's polarity increased (cis‐isomer formation) upon UV irradiation, causing it to dissolve evenly in water; under visible light, it reverted to a hydrophobic (trans) state, yielding a separate IL‐rich phase.[ 63 ] This light‐triggered phase transition was fully reversible and rapid, offering a novel mechanism for IL recovery without chemical additives. Challenges include ensuring sufficient light penetration and the stability of photo‐switchable ILs under reservoir conditions. Still, the concept shows the breadth of stimuli‐responsive tools that could be harnessed in future EOR formulations.

ILs Affect Crude Oil Footprint, Interactions, and Corrosion

ILs have revolutionized crude oil processing by addressing significant challenges in EOR, refining, and infrastructure upkeep. Their highly adjustable chemical structures allow for targeted applications, such as lowering the environmental impact of oil extraction, enhancing crude oil flow properties through interactions with asphaltenes and other heavy components, and reducing corrosion in pipelines and equipment. Each category of ILs, including imidazolium‐, pyridinium‐, phosphonium‐, caprolactam‐, ammonium‐, and choline‐based ILs and oxygen‐functionalized ILs, provides distinct benefits tailored to specific operational requirements. These characteristics improve crude oil quality and recovery efficiency while promoting environmentally friendly and sustainable practices in the oil and gas sector. Influence of ILs on crude oil properties and pipeline integrity.

ILs present a promising opportunity to lessen the environmental impact of crude oil production by enhancing oil quality and recovery efficiency. Imidazolium‐based ILs are especially effective at decreasing crude oil viscosity and improving asphaltene dispersion, thereby minimizing energy‐intensive processes in downstream applications. These ILs also reduce IFT in oil–water systems, facilitating efficient oil recovery and lessening the overall environmental footprint of extraction processes.[ 64 , 65 ] Likewise, pyridinium‐based ILs help reduce the crude oil footprint by improving oil–water separation and enhancing the demulsification process, ensuring better waste emulsion management.[ 66 ] Pyridinium, caprolactam, and imidazolium‐based ILs interact with asphaltene molecules, preventing aggregation by enhancing their solubility, even in high‐salinity environments, leading to significant IFT reduction.[ 67 ] Moreover, phosphonium‐based ILs further lessen the environmental impact by effectively removing sulfur species such as thiophenes, resulting in cleaner crude oil with fewer harmful byproducts during processing.[ 68 ] In addition, choline‐based ILs are recognized for their environmentally friendly qualities, promoting sustainability while minimally affecting crude oil characteristics.[ 39 ]

ILs interact with crude oil components, which is crucial for their role in EOR and refining. First, imidazolium‐based ILs exhibit significant π–π* interactions with asphaltenes, which help prevent their aggregation and stabilize emulsions. This interaction enhances oil flow characteristics and reduces pipeline blockages caused by heavier components.[ 64 ] In contrast, pyridinium‐based ILs disrupt asphaltene layers, aiding emulsion dehydration and improving oil–water separation.[ 66 ] However, phosphonium‐based ILs exhibit unique reactivity with sulfur species, forming complexes that facilitate sulfur removal and significantly enhance oil quality.[ 68 ] In contrast, choline‐based ILs show minimal direct chemical reactions with crude oil, primarily acting as surface‐active agents to lower IFT and improve recovery.[ 39 ] Oxygen‐functionalized ILs interact with the polar components of crude oil, breaking down heavier fractions and enhancing viscosity, which is especially beneficial for recovering HCOs.[ 41 ]

The corrosion behavior of ILs varies with their chemical composition and reservoir conditions. Imidazolium‐based ILs are generally noncorrosive and, in some cases, act as corrosion inhibitors in acidic environments when paired with halogenated anions, protecting pipelines and equipment.[ 64 ] Also, pyridinium‐ and caprolactam‐based ILs exhibit low corrosivity, particularly when nonhalogenated anions are used, making them safer for long‐term applications.[ 66 ] However, phosphonium‐based ILs can show corrosivity in oxidative environments, but careful design of their molecular structure helps mitigate this risk.[ 68 ] Also, it is essential to note that the corrosion behavior of ammonium‐based ILs can vary depending on their chemical structure, particularly the nature of their cations and anions.[ 69 ] In contrast, choline‐based ILs are biodegradable and pose negligible corrosion risks to metallic infrastructure, enhancing their environmental compatibility.[ 39 ]

Imidazolium‐Based ILs for EOR

IFT Reduction

ILs, especially imidazolium‐based compounds, have become highly effective surface‐active agents in EOR. Surface activity refers to the ability of an amphiphilic molecule to adsorb at a fluid interface and form a monomolecular film, reducing the IFT between the phases.[ 70 ] ILs possess an amphiphilic character with charged head groups and hydrophobic tails, allowing them to behave surfactant‐like. However, their bulkier cationic head and fully ionic nature often will enable them to reduce oil–water IFT as effectively as, or even better than, traditional surfactants.[ 71 ] Notably, the imidazolium cation's bulky, heterocyclic head group (as opposed to a typical surfactant's smaller head) increases the IL's hydrophobicity and facilitates its migration to the interface. This structural difference allows IL molecules to pack efficiently at the interface, yielding a greater reduction in IFT for a given concentration. These ILs exhibit remarkable thermal stability and structural tunability, enabling ultralow IFT values even under difficult reservoir conditions marked by high‐salinity and elevated temperatures. Their effectiveness in reducing IFT without the need for cosurfactants simplifies the chemical formulation process while improving crude oil recovery efficiency. Various factors influence the reduction trend, including alkyl chain length, salinity, temperature, and counterion type. This section synthesizes findings from research conducted between 2018 and 2025, exploring key trends, comparative performance, and the mechanisms driving their adaptability and success across diverse reservoir conditions.

One of the primary determinants of IFT reduction is the alkyl chain length. Table 4 illustrates the effect of different alkyl‐chain imidazolium ILs on IFT reduction across different salinity levels and temperatures. The results show that [C8mim][Cl] achieves relatively lower IFT at lower concentrations than [C6mim][Cl], confirming that longer alkyl chains enhance surface activity by promoting micelle formation at reduced concentrations.[ 72 ] In contrast, shorter chain ILs like [C4mim][Cl] required significantly higher concentrations for notable IFT reduction,[ 73 ] making them less efficient in practical applications. Salinity also plays a crucial role in IL performance. While both [C6mim][Cl] and [C8mim][Cl] reduced IFT effectively in diluted seawater, their efficiency was lower in highly saline formation water. This reduction in performance is likely due to competitive interactions between shorter alkyl‐chain ILs and dissolved salt ions, which hinder IL adsorption at the oil–water interface.[ 72 ] Conversely, in high‐salinity conditions, [C4mim][I] performed better than [C4mim][Cl] and [C4mim][Br], suggesting that iodide anions enhance IFT reduction due to their higher polarizability.[ 73 ]

The IFT reduction achieved by long alkyl‐chain imidazolium ILs is presented in Table 4. The comparison of different studies reveals significant differences in performance. A clear trend observed in the data is that IL concentration is crucial in IFT reduction. For Indian crude oil, [C16mim][Br] reduced IFT from 11.5 to 0.12 mN m−1 at 6000 ppm in a high‐salinity brine of 350 000 ppm.[ 74 ] Similarly, for Malaysian crude oil, [C16mim][Br] at 300 ppm reduced IFT to 0.055 mN m−1 in a synthetic brine of 150 000 ppm. Combined with 3000 ppm ETA, the reduction was further enhanced to an extremely low 0.0076 mN m−1.[ 75 ] These results suggest that longer alkyl‐chain ILs, particularly C16‐based ILs, are highly effective in reducing IFT, and synergistic interactions with coadditives can amplify their efficiency.

Salinity and type of salt ions have a notable impact on IL efficiency, affecting the adsorption and aggregation behavior of ILs at the oil–water interface. Upon using Iranian oil, [C12mim][Cl] reduced IFT from 5.8 to 0.85 mN m−1 at 250 ppm in NaCl solution (40 880 ppm) and from 7.19 to 1.24 mN m−1 at 1000 ppm in Na2SO4 solution (33 086 ppm).[ 76 ] The higher initial IFT in sulfate solution indicates stronger interactions between sulfate ions and water molecules, which could hinder IL adsorption. Similarly, in the crude oil obtained from the North Sea, [C12mim][Br] at 10 000 ppm reduced IFT to 1.2 mN m−1 in Smart Water SW0Na (10 010 ppm), highlighting the influence of ionic strength in moderating IL performance.[ 17 ] In Southern Iran, where tests were conducted at 25 °C in distilled water, [C12mim][Cl] at ≈758 ppm reduced IFT from 29.1 to 1.6 mN m−1.[ 77 ] The significantly higher initial IFT in deionized water compared to brines suggests that ionic interactions minimize surface tension. This is further supported by Ramezani et al.[ 78 ] where Persian Gulf synthetic brine (39 711 ppm) at 2000 ppm [C12mim][Cl] reduced IFT to 0.67 mN m−1, reinforcing the role of salinity in stabilizing IL adsorption.[ 78 ]

CMC is a key parameter in evaluating the surface activity of ILs. The CMC is the minimum concentration at which surfactant molecules begin aggregating into micelles in the bulk solution, having already packed the interface to its capacity.[ 16 ] Lowering the CMC means that the IL can achieve maximum IFT reduction at a lower dosage, which is advantageous for chemical EOR. Below the CMC, adding surfactant has a strong effect on lowering IFT, but beyond this point, additional surfactant mostly goes into micelles, and the IFT reduction efficiency diminishes.[ 79 ] In addition, reducing the CMC often correlates with improved performance under reservoir conditions. Table 4 shows that ILs with longer hydrophobic chains tend to have lower CMC and greater surface activity, translating to more substantial IFT reduction and better oil‐mobilizing capability.

The data collected in Table 4 show that CMC values vary across studies depending on alkyl chain length, salinity, and solvent environment. The results indicate that longer alkyl chains lead to lower CMC values, consistent with the increased hydrophobicity of longer‐chain ILs. In Chinese crude oil and deionized water, [C16mim][Br] exhibited the lowest CMC at 128.4 ppm, while [C14mim][Br] and [C12mim][Br] had higher CMCs of 586 and 2385 ppm, respectively.[ 16 ] This trend suggests that increasing chain length reduces solubility in water, promoting micelle formation at lower concentrations. The dilution media salinity plays a crucial role in determining CMC values. In North Sea conditions, [C12mim][Br] had a CMC of 250 ppm in Smart Water SW0Na (10 010 ppm salinity),[ 17 ] which is comparable to its CMC in other saline conditions. The CMC value in deionized water is considerably higher than CMCs in brine solutions, reinforcing the role of salt ions in promoting micellization at lower concentrations by reducing electrostatic repulsion between IL molecules.

Longer alkyl‐chain ILs consistently show superior IFT reduction compared to shorter chain counterparts. In China, [C12mim][Br] reduced IFT from 32.74 to 3.17 mN m−1, while [C14mim][Br] achieved a much lower IFT of 0.54 mN m−1. The best reduction was obtained with [C16mim][Br], which lowered IFT to 0.051 mN m−1 at 128.4 ppm.[ 16 ] Figure 2 shows that increasing the alkyl chain length enhances hydrophobicity, improving IL adsorption and micelle formation, leading to more efficient IFT reduction.[ 80 ] Across all the studies, [C12mim][Cl] and [C16mim][Br] emerged as the most widely studied ILs, consistently achieving low IFT values across various reservoir conditions. The performance of ILs is influenced by concentration, salinity type, alkyl chain length, and temperature, with synergistic additives further enhancing effectiveness. Future research should focus on optimizing IL formulations for different reservoir conditions, particularly in high‐salinity environments, to maximize efficiency while minimizing environmental impact.

It can be concluded that imidazolium‐based ILs with longer alkyl chains (C12–C16) consistently achieve ultralow IFT values (<0.1 mN m−1) under high‐salinity and temperature conditions. This superior performance is attributed to enhanced micellization, strong surface activity, and effective interfacial adsorption. The use of coadditives further enhances IFT reduction, underscoring the importance of optimized formulation for practical EOR applications.

Emulsification and Phase Behavior

The emulsification and phase behavior of imidazolium‐based ILs are crucial in EOR. Two attractive studies examined the effectiveness of short‐chain gemini IL ([C8‐C4‐C8im]Br2) and long‐chain gemini IL ([C12im‐C4‐imC12]Br2), providing insights into microemulsion type with HCO (American Petroleum Institute (API) = 20.7°), phase behavior, solubility, and emulsification efficiency as illustrated in Figure 3 . Saien et al.[ 81 ] analyzed [C8‐C4‐C8im]Br2, which was tested at 298.2 K (25 °C) in brine solutions with NaCl and MgCl2. The IL facilitated the formation of Winsor III microemulsions at moderate IL concentrations, transitioning to bicontinuous phases at higher IL concentrations. The presence of salts enhanced microemulsion stability due to electrostatic screening effects.[ 81 ] In contrast, [C12im‐C4‐imC12]Br2 demonstrated that it promoted Winsor I microemulsions at lower concentrations and bicontinuous phases at higher IL dosages, with a strong transition to Winsor III at elevated temperatures (323.2 K).[ 82 ]

Solubility differences were observed between the two ILs. [C8‐C4‐C8im]Br2 exhibited high solubility in brine, forming stable aggregates with hydrodynamic sizes of 1–7 nm at low concentrations and 250–600 nm at high concentrations.[ 81 ] Conversely, [C12im‐C4‐imC12]Br2 had a lower CMC of 1.85 × 10−2 mol dm−3 at 328.2 K, indicating strong self‐aggregation tendencies. However, solubility decreased in high‐salinity environments due to enhanced micelle formation.[ 82 ] In addition, the emulsification efficiency of both ILs was evaluated using the emulsification index. [C8‐C4‐C8im]Br2 exhibited an emulsification index of 57.5% after 1 day, which slightly declined to 56% after 1 week, indicating moderate long‐term emulsion stability.[ 81 ] In contrast, [C12im‐C4‐imC12]Br2 achieved a higher emulsification index of 63.2% after 1 day, dropping to 52.3% after 1 week, highlighting superior initial emulsification but a slight stability reduction over time.[ 82 ] This drop in emulsification index was due to coalescence, IL desorption, salting‐out effects, and altered intermolecular interactions over time. Temperature‐driven micellization also shifted IL molecules from the interface, reducing stability. Despite this, the IL remained highly effective for EOR applications.[ 82 ]

The emulsification capability of gemini SAILs was systematically examined under varying salinity and pH conditions to assess their potential for EOR. Kharazi and Saien explored three homologous imidazolium‐based gemini SAILs—[C4im‐C4‐imC4][Br2], [C8im‐C4‐imC8][Br2], and [C12im‐C4‐imC12][Br2]—and their impact on emulsion formation and stability in HCO (API = 20°) ‐water systems.[ 83 ] Figure 4 shows that the medium (C8) and long‐chain (C12) SAILs formed more stable emulsions, as their strong amphiphilic nature allowed them to create tight interfacial films that prevented droplet coalescence. However, higher salinity caused a salting‐out effect, leading to weaker interfacial films and slight emulsion destabilization over time. Despite this, emulsification indices decreased by only 2–11% after 1 week, indicating good long‐term emulsion stability.[ 83 ]

Crude oil–water emulsions stabilized by 0.1 mol dm−3 gemini SAILs after 1 week, with 45× magnified microscopic images of the emulsions. Reprinted (adapted) with permission.[ 83 ] Copyright 2021, SSRN.

Kharazi and Saien[ 83 ] also discovered that the pH variations significantly influenced emulsion formation and stability, with higher pH values leading to enhanced emulsification. At pH 9.5, [C12im‐C4‐imC12][Br2] achieved an emulsification index of 92% after 1 day and 90% after 1 week, showing exceptional stability. This was attributed to increased adsorption of natural surfactants at the crude oil–water interface, which promoted emulsion formation. Conversely, acidic conditions (pH 2.5–4.0) weakened emulsification due to higher electrostatic repulsion among SAIL head groups, reducing their ability to form stable interfacial layers.[ 83 ]

The analysis of emulsification behavior indicates that gemini imidazolium‐based ILs can form stable Winsor‐type emulsions, especially under alkaline conditions and moderate salinity. C12‐based ILs exhibit superior emulsification indices (>90%) attributed to the formation of strong interfacial films and efficient micellization. However, emulsion stability is sensitive to salting‐out effects and elevated temperatures, emphasizing the need for careful IL design and dosage control to maintain long‐term efficiency.

Studies have shown that ILs like long alkyl‐chain imidazolium‐based effectively alter the wettability of carbonate reservoirs to water‐wet conditions, leading to increased oil recovery rates.[ 84 ] Such modifications improve oil recovery and mitigate formation damage, making ILs a valuable tool in EOR applications. Temperature influences IL efficiency by affecting molecular mobility and adsorption dynamics. The studies conducted by Sakthivel (2021) in Saudi Arabia at 25 and 100 °C using synthetic seawater (SSW: 67 500 ppm) showed that increasing the alkyl chain length of ILs enhanced water‐wetting characteristics. At 500 ppm, [C4mim][Cl] reduced the contact angle from 82.5° to 53.2° at 25 °C, while [C8mim][Cl] achieved a lower contact angle of 50.6°, indicating improved water‐wetness. At 100 °C, all ILs exhibited even greater wettability alteration, with [C4mim][Cl] reducing the contact angle from 73.6° to 42.1° and [C8mim][Cl] achieving a contact angle of 40.5°, highlighting the temperature dependency of IL efficiency in wettability alteration.[ 85 ] The effectiveness of higher temperatures suggests that IL adsorption at the oil–rock interface improves with increased molecular diffusion, leading to enhanced wettability alteration.

At 100 °C, different anions were tested using [C4mim]+ with PF6 − and BF4 −.[ 86 ] At 0.0025 M, [C4mim]+[PF6]− showed superior wettability alteration, reducing the contact angle from 147.2° to 39.9°, whereas [C4mim]+[BF4]− resulted in a contact angle reduction to only 67.3°.[ 86 ] This suggests that the PF6 − anion enhances water‐wetness more effectively than BF4 −due to its larger size, polarization, weaker electrostatic interactions, and lower hydration energy, which enable stronger IL−rock and IL−oil interactions. Sakthivel et al.[ 73 ] examined wettability alteration at 100 °C in high‐salinity formation brine (240 000 ppm) using 1000 mM dm− 3 IL concentration. The results indicated greater water‐wetness shifts at higher salinity, with [C4mim][Cl] reducing the contact angle from 158° to 107°, while [C4mim][I] achieved the lowest contact angle of 84.3°.[ 73 ] This suggests that halide anions play a role in wettability modification, with iodide (I−) performing better than chloride (Cl−) and bromide (Br−), likely due to synergistic interactions between IL cations and oil‐phase organic acids. Overall, larger, less charge‐dense anions, such as [PF6]− and [I]−, allow deeper penetration into the rock–oil–brine interface, enhancing wettability shift toward water‐wet states and facilitating improved oil mobilization.[ 73 , 86 ]

Imidazolium‐based ILs with long alkyl chains (C12–C18) and polarizable anions (e.g., PF6 − and I−) effectively shift surfaces from oil‐wet to water‐wet. These ILs reduce contact angles to below 40°, even at low concentrations. Larger anions improve surface interactions by enabling deeper penetration. The observed efficiency is attributed to enhanced molecular adsorption, increased hydrophobicity, and favorable electrostatic and steric interactions at the oil–rock interface. These findings suggest that longer‐chain ILs with polarizable anions are optimal for wettability alteration in carbonate reservoirs subjected to harsh conditions.

Adsorption Efficiency

Adsorption refers to the attachment of molecules from the injected fluid onto the surface of the reservoir rock.[ 88 ] In other words, when an imidazolium IL or surfactant is injected, some of its molecules can stick to the rock's surface (due to intermolecular forces like electrostatic attractions or hydrogen bonding). In contrast, retention is a broader concept describing the total amount of chemicals that stay behind in the reservoir and are not recovered. Retention includes molecules lost by adsorption onto rock surfaces and any that become trapped in tiny pore spaces or precipitated out of solution.[ 89 ] By minimizing adsorption and other retention mechanisms, ILs can reduce chemical loss in EOR operations, ensuring that more injected formulation participates in oil recovery.

The adsorption of surfactants onto reservoir rock surfaces is a critical challenge in EOR, leading to chemical loss and reduced efficiency. To mitigate this, ILs have been explored as sacrificial agents, reducing surfactant adsorption while maintaining interfacial activity. Hanamertani et al.[ 90 ] studied the adsorption behavior of internal olefin sulfonate (IOS) (anionic surfactant) and MFOMAX (in‐house anionic and amphoteric surfactants) before and after mixing with imidazolium ILs onto crushed Berea sandstone. Multilayer adsorption on a heterogeneous surface was observed upon using Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm models. At low concentrations (500–1000 ppm), IOS exhibited lower adsorption due to electrostatic repulsion with the negatively charged rock, while MFOMAX showed higher adsorption due to its amphoteric nature, allowing stronger interactions as shown in Figure 6a,b. The Langmuir model's maximum adsorption capacity (qm) was 500.888 mg g−1 for IOS and 675.828 mg g−1 for MFOMAX, further confirming MFOMAX's stronger adsorption.[ 90 ] These findings are critical for EOR, as they highlight the need for adsorption mitigation strategies to prevent excessive surfactant loss in porous media.

Figure 6c illustrates the impact of three ILs—IL‐1 ([C4mim][NTf2]), IL‐2 ([C4mim][PF6]), and IL‐3 (DES)—on IOS and MFOMAX surfactant adsorption onto crushed Berea sandstone, with IL concentrations ranging from 500 to 5000 ppm.[ 90 ] IL‐1 and IL‐3 were the most effective, reducing IOS (5000 ppm) adsorption from 8.14 mg g−1 to 2.4 mg g−1 at 2000 ppm (a 70% reduction). The strong electrostatic interactions of IL‐1 and the hydrogen bonding capability of IL‐3 contributed to their superior performance, demonstrating their potential as effective sacrificial agents for minimizing surfactant loss. While at a lower concentration of MFOMAX (2000 ppm), the adsorption decreased from 10.38 to 6.55 mg g−1 with IL‐1, 5.31 mg g−1 with IL‐2, and 1.60 mg g−1 with IL‐3, as presented in Figure 6d.[ 90 ] ILs exhibited higher solubility, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions, making them the best sacrificial agent for reducing surfactant loss through adsorption in EOR applications.

Pillai and Mandal[ 87 ] investigated the adsorption reduction using imidazolium‐based ILs on carbonate rock to enhance surfactant efficiency in EOR. Three ILs were tested, C8mimBF4, C10mimBF4, and C12mimBF4, with adsorption experiments conducted at varying IL concentrations (500–12 000 ppm). Adsorption results followed the Langmuir isotherm model, confirming monolayer adsorption on a homogeneous surface,[ 91 ] as shown in Figure 7a. The maximum adsorption capacities (q 0) were 5.39 mg g−1 for C8mimBF4, 3.25 mg g−1 for C10mimBF4, and 0.98 mg g−1 for C12mimBF4, indicating that longer alkyl‐chain ILs exhibited lower adsorption due to their higher hydrophobicity, which reduced electrostatic interactions with the rock surface. These findings confirm that C12mimBF4 demonstrated the lowest adsorption, making it the most effective IL for reducing surfactant loss in carbonate reservoirs.[ 87 ]

Moreover, Tafur et al.[ 92 ] improved EOR by incorporating 1‐dodecyl‐3‐methylimidazolium bromide ([C12mim]Br) into a surfactant blend of dioctyl sulfosuccinate sodium (AOT) and polyoxyethylene(8) octyl ether carboxylic acid (LF2). The initial 1 wt% binary blend of AOT (50 wt%) and LF2 (50 wt%) in SSW achieved adsorption of 0.42 mg g−1 rock and an AOR of 7.3% of original oil in place (OOIP). The optimized 0.8 wt% ternary blend (AOT 20.7 wt%, [C12mim]Br 25.3 wt%, and LF2 54 wt%) reduced adsorption by 50% (0.21 mg g−1 rock) and increased AOR to 11.5% OOIP. The cationic IL enhanced electrostatic interactions, reducing surfactant loss and improving interfacial activity.[ 92 ]

The reviewed studies reveal that imidazolium‐based ILs with longer alkyl chains, such as C12mimBF4, demonstrate lower adsorption on carbonate rocks due to increased hydrophobicity, which minimizes electrostatic interactions. When incorporated into surfactant formulations, these ILs significantly reduce surfactant adsorption, acting as effective sacrificial agents. Optimal IL selection based on chain length and anion chemistry is essential to minimize chemical loss and enhance EOR process efficiency.

IL Interactions with Crude Oil and Viscosity Modification

Molecular Interactions between ILs and Crude Oil

ILs, particularly imidazolium‐based types, play a crucial role in modifying the viscosity and stability of crude oil. Their unique interactions, including π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and micelle formation, help disrupt asphaltene aggregation, reduce IFT, and extract sulfur and polar compounds. These properties enhance oil mobility, separation efficiency, and flow assurance, making ILs valuable for EOR and refining. This section explores the key interactions of imidazolium ILs with crude oil components and their impact on viscosity modification and processability, which are illustrated in Figure 8 .

Molecular interactions between imidazolium‐based ILs and crude oil components.

π–π Interaction with Aromatic Hydrocarbons

Aromatic hydrocarbons contribute to emulsion stability by adsorbing onto asphaltene surfaces. Long alkyl‐chain imidazolium ILs can create π–π stacking interactions with these hydrocarbons, disrupting emulsion stability or preventing further asphaltene aggregation. Thus, it enhances crude oil processability and fluid transport. This interaction is especially valuable in chemical flooding scenarios, where controlling oil emulsions is crucial for optimizing separation and recovery.[ 93 ]

Disruption of Asphaltene Aggregation

Asphaltenes are high‐molecular‐weight polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons that exhibit strong intermolecular π–π stacking interactions, leading to aggregate formation. These aggregates can clog pipelines, diminish permeability in porous media, and increase the viscosity of crude oil. Imidazolium‐based ILs effectively disrupt asphaltene aggregation through various mechanisms, including hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, and steric stabilization. Experimental results indicate that even at low concentrations (1000 ppm), ILs significantly delay the onset of asphaltene precipitation, a finding supported by molecular modeling. This leads to asphaltene dispersants, reduced viscosity, and flow assurance issues in oil production and transportation.[ 64 ] Xiang et al.[ 94 ] found that [C12mim][Cl] plays a crucial role in disrupting asphaltene aggregation for EOR, as demonstrated in Figure 9 . This is achieved by raising the asphaltene deposition point from 63% to 68% while decreasing the asphaltene particle size from 79.35 to 48.54 μm. These changes extend the time asphaltenes remain dissolved, preventing early precipitation and minimizing flow assurance problems in reservoirs and pipelines. The imidazolium cation interacts with the aromatic rings of asphaltenes through π–π stacking.

Mechanism of asphaltene dispersion facilitated by imidazolium chloride IL. Adapted under the terms of the Creative Commons Attribution License (CC BY 4.0).[ 94 ] Copyright 2024, The Authors. Published by MDPI.

In contrast, the chloride anion engages in electrostatic and hydrogen bonding with polar heteroatoms, disrupting asphaltene clusters and promoting dispersion. Furthermore, the presence of the C12 alkyl chain introduces a steric hindrance, which hinders reaggregation and ensures the long‐term stability of the asphaltene dispersion. This results in enhanced oil mobility, lower viscosity, and improved efficiency of chemical flooding, establishing [C12mim][Cl] as a significant additive in EOR.[ 94 ]

Interaction with Resins

Resins stabilize asphaltenes in a “colloidal suspension,” contributing to higher oil viscosity. When long alkyl‐chain imidazolium ILs interact with resins, they destabilize these complexes by weakening electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions. Modifying interfacial properties also results in viscosity reduction. This process increases oil mobility, enhancing the effectiveness of water, gas, or chemical floods. Velusamy et al.[ 95 ] imply that ILs interact with resins through structural modifications in the oil matrix, reducing viscosity by disrupting resin–asphaltene complexes. The reduction in yield stress and viscosity at high temperatures and pressures demonstrates how ILs can influence the flow behavior of crude oil, likely involving the breakdown of stabilizing networks formed by resins and asphaltenes.[ 95 ]

Interaction with Sulfur Compounds

Sulfur compounds contribute to the corrosion and souring of crude oil. Imidazolium ILs help to lower sulfur levels and reduce corrosion risks by reacting with sulfur‐containing compounds like thiols.[ 96 ] ILs, particularly imidazolium‐based types, interact with sulfur compounds in crude oil through π–π interactions between their aromatic cation core and thiophenic sulfur compounds and hydrogen bonding with sulfur atoms. These interactions facilitate the selective extraction of sulfur compounds, such as dibenzothiophene, while preserving the surrounding hydrocarbon matrix. During EOR, this process mitigates sulfur‐related challenges, including corrosion and reservoir souring, enhancing oil flow and production efficiency. Additionally, ILs help maintain the chemical integrity of production infrastructure by reducing sulfur content in the extracted crude, making them valuable for extraction and refining operations.[ 97 , 98 ]

Hydrogen Bonding with Polar Compounds

Polar compounds in crude oil, such as phenols, can form hydrogen bonds with the imidazolium cation. This influences emulsion stability and interfacial properties, allowing for improved oil separation and mobility control. Imidazolium‐based ILs exhibit hydrogen bonding with the polar compounds present in crude oil through various mechanisms, depending on the nature of the compound. The interaction with ILs primarily involves CH‐π hydrogen bonding, in which the hydrogen atoms on the imidazolium ring interact with the π‐electrons of aromatic hydrocarbons. However, in the case of heteroaromatic compounds like pyridine (which contains nitrogen), a more potent and selective N—H hydrogen bond forms between the imidazolium's hydrogen and the nitrogen's lone pair. These interactions facilitate the selective extraction of polar heteroatoms and aromatic hydrocarbons from crude oil by disrupting their molecular associations and enhancing solubility within the IL phase.[ 48 , 99 ]

Rheological Evaluation of ILs for EOR

Imidazolium‐based ILs effectively reduce viscosity, stabilize asphaltenes, and enhance crude oil flowability, making them promising for EOR, as summarized in Figure 10 . Velusamy et al.[ 95 ] found that imidazolium‐based ILs, specifically C4mimCl and C8mimCl at 5000 ppm (0.5 wt%), significantly improved the rheological properties of HCO. At 25 °C and 0.1 MPa, C4mimCl reduced viscosity by 26.5%, while C8mimCl achieved a 31.5% reduction, with longer alkyl chains proving more effective. At 100 °C, the yield stress dropped by 25–30%, facilitating flow. Additionally, ILs disrupted asphaltene flocculation, reducing the solid‐like behavior of HCO as evidenced by lower storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″), with G′ decreasing from 11 to ≈7 Pa and G″ from 7 to ≈3 Pa. The crossover frequency was also reduced by 25–30%, confirming enhanced flowability, making ILs a promising solution for improving crude oil transport and processing.[ 95 ] Santos et al.[ 100 ] also found that C8–C12 imidazolium ILs (5–100 ppm) reduced viscosity enhancement at low asphaltene content, while their strong interactions with asphaltenes made them effective precipitation inhibitors. ILs like C8mim[NTf2] and C12mim[NTf2] stabilized asphaltenes, improving crude oil flowability and preventing aggregation‐related issues.[ 100 ]

Main rheological findings of imidazolium ILs for EOR in terms of viscosity modification and asphaltene dispersion.[ 94 , 95 , 100 , 101 , 102 ]

Moreover, Hou et al.[ 101 ] found that [C12mim][Br] and [C12mim][PF6] (200 ppm) reduced heavy oil viscosity, increased solid surface hydrophilicity, and improved oil detachment. Their high recovery rates (>95%) enabled recyclability up to 10 times, enhancing sustainability. In addition, [C12mim][Cl] (1500 ppm) achieved the highest viscosity reduction (49.87%) in HCO by disrupting asphaltene aggregation, reducing deposit sizes from 79.35 to 48.54 μm.[ 94 ] Meanwhile, Xiang et al.[ 94 ] confirmed that [C16mim][Cl] interacted with asphaltenes via electrostatic and π–π interactions, preventing large aggregate formation and enhancing dispersion. These findings confirm the effectiveness of imidazolium chloride ILs in viscosity reduction and asphaltene stabilization.[ 94 ]

The effects of adding longer alkyl chains C14–C16 imidazolium ILs at 15 g L−1 on the rheological and thermal properties of hydroxypropyl cellulose (HPC) solutions were also studied for EOR.[ 102 ] The findings highlight that C14–C16 ILs act as viscosity modifiers, increasing the viscosity of HPC‐injected fluids, thereby improving sweep efficiency. At high shear rates (≈50 s− 1), viscosity decreased, displaying pseudoplastic behavior, which is beneficial for fluid injectability and mobility control. Additionally, C14–C16 ILs delayed the phase separation of HPC solutions by increasing the phase transition temperature and reducing transition enthalpy, which suggests enhanced thermal stability. Finally, HPC/IL mixtures exhibited higher storage (G′) and loss moduli (G″), indicating improved elasticity compared to pure HPC, which is critical for maintaining structural integrity during injection.[ 102 ]

The findings confirm that imidazolium‐based ILs effectively reduce crude oil viscosity by disrupting asphaltene aggregation, weakening resin–asphaltene networks, and interacting with polar compounds. These interactions improve oil mobility and flow assurance. ILs such as [C12mim][Cl] and [C16mim][Cl] exhibit strong π–π and hydrogen bonding interactions that result in smaller deposit sizes and improved dispersion, making them excellent candidates for viscosity control in EOR operation.

AOR Assessment

Enhanced Light Crude Oil Recovery

Imidazolium‐based ILs have been investigated for their potential in EOR across diverse crude oil types, reservoir conditions, and salinity levels. For light crude oils, characterized by their higher API° gravity (typically above 31.1°), lower viscosity, and higher saturate content, EOR presents unique opportunities and challenges. Light crude oils are easier to displace due to their lower viscosity and lower asphaltene content, reducing resistance to flow. However, their lower density and weaker adhesion to reservoir rock surfaces can limit the efficacy of recovery methods that alter wettability or reduce IFT. These properties necessitate the optimization of IL formulations to ensure sufficient recovery efficiency under varying temperatures, salinity, and core conditions. Research from 2013 to 2022 highlights the adaptability of imidazolium‐based ILs to these conditions, demonstrating their effectiveness in improving oil recovery through IFT reduction and wettability alteration. Core and Reservoir Types: The type of core material and reservoir structure significantly impacts the efficiency of EOR using imidazolium‐based ILs. Carbonate cores have been extensively tested in[ 92 , 101 , 104 ] studies. These cores are characterized by lower porosity and permeability, presenting challenges for fluid flow but offering significant potential for wettability alteration. Hezave et al.[ 103 ] reported recovery improvements of 8–13% after using [C12mim][Cl] at 4000 ppm in carbonate cores with porosities of 13.5–15.3% and permeabilities between 5.9 and 7.3 mD.[ 103 ] However, sand‐packed systems were prominently used in the studies of refs. [45] and [104]. These systems typically exhibit higher porosity and permeability than carbonate cores, enabling more efficient fluid displacement. Nandwani et al.[ 45 ] reported additional recovery of 17.96–28.73% using [C16mimBr] in sand‐packed columns with porosities of ≈32–34%.[ 45 ] Quartz‐based sand packs were used by,[ 104 ] which reported porosities of 34–36% and permeabilities of 325–562 mD. These experiments demonstrated the adaptability of IL‐polymer flooding under both low‐ and high‐salinity conditions, achieving AOR of 13.68–27.94% of OOIP.[ 104 ] These diverse core and reservoir types highlight the adaptability of imidazolium‐based ILs across various geological settings. Carbonate cores benefit significantly from wettability alteration properties, while sand‐packed systems demonstrate high recovery potential due to their permeability advantages.

Light Oil Recovery Efficiency: The use of imidazolium‐based ILs in light oil recovery has demonstrated notable improvements in AOR across various studies. Indian reservoirs (Oil India Limited) with very light oil showed enhanced recovery with [C8mim][Cl] at 5000 ppm, yielding 13.68% AOR over 41–46% from water flooding.[ 104 ] When combined with polyacrylamide (PAM) polymer, the IL performance improved further, reaching an AOR of 25.13%. For paraffinic light oil in India, [C16mimBr] (3000 ppm) led to 17.96–28.73% AOR in sand‐packed columns, exceeding water flooding (60–75%), recovering 71.83% of trapped oil.[ 45 ] In Ankleshwar, India, artificial sand packs with [C16mimBr] at 6000 ppm exhibited a moderate AOR of 4.62–7.37%, supplementing 23.08–38.75% oil recovery from water flooding.[ 74 ] Meanwhile, in Spain, carbonate reservoirs with [C12mim]Cl at 500 ppm showed a 14.8% AOR over 76.28% water flooding.[ 105 ] Similarly, [C12mim]Br at 8000 ppm in Indiana Limestone formations achieved 11.5% AOR after 40.8% from water flooding.[ 92 ]

Salinity Tolerance: Salinity plays a crucial role in IL performance, as high‐salinity environments (>100 000 ppm) can impact ionic interactions and fluid miscibility. In Spain, carbonate formations with salinity levels of 49 730–50 000 ppm exhibited 11.5–14.8% AOR, confirming IL compatibility with moderately saline conditions.[ 92 , 105 ] Moreover, Sakthivel et al.[ 104 ] tested [C8mim][Cl] at 5000 ppm in 100 000 ppm saline conditions, finding a slight increase in AOR (14.29%) compared to zero salinity conditions (13.68%), confirming IL stability under high salinity. Nandwani et al.[ 74 ] examined [C16mimBr] (6000 ppm) at 350 000 ppm salinity, observing 4.62–7.37% AOR, indicating IL efficiency is reduced at extremely high salinity.

The recovery of heavy and medium crude oil with imidazolium‐based ILs demonstrates adaptability under challenging reservoir conditions, including high viscosity, salinity, and temperature. Compared to light crude oil, where IL performance has been widely validated, heavy and medium crude oil recovery shows similar trends but often requires ILs with stronger wettability alteration and IFT reduction capabilities. While light crude oil studies frequently benefit from higher permeability systems (e.g., sand packs), heavy and medium crude oils pose additional challenges, such as higher viscosity and asphaltene content. These challenges are overcome with advanced IL formulations, as demonstrated by research works presented in Table 8 . Imidazolium‐based ILs show promise for a broad spectrum of crude oil types and reservoir conditions, highlighting their versatility in EOR application. In medium crude oil fields, [C8mim][Br] at 5000 ppm in Bohai Suizhong Oilfield, China, resulted in 4.15% AOR over 38.91–41.92% water flooding efficiency in sand‐packed columns, whereas combining ILs with PAAD polymer (acrylamide, acrylic acid, and N,N‐diallyl‐2‐dodecylbenzenesulfonamide) significantly improved recovery to 21.65% AOR.[ 106 ] Similarly, in the Sarvestan oilfield, Iran, [C18mim][Cl] (170 ppm) in carbonate cores improved recovery by 13% AOR, supplementing 38% brine injection recovery.[ 84 ]

Synergetic effects of IL temperature and concentration significantly affect IL efficiency in EOR, with results demonstrating IL stability across a wide range of temperatures (22–100 °C). Heavy crude oil from Pelican Lake in Canada showed 8.18% AOR at 22 °C using [C12mim][Cl] (1000 ppm), supplementing 54.7% recovery from water flooding in quartz sand packs.[ 54 ] In Karamay Oilfield, China, a combination of higher concentrations of [C12mim][Cl] (5000 ppm) and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) improved recovery by 16.1–17.7% AOR at 50 °C, compared to 28.3–39.6% water flooding efficiency in sandstone cores.[ 107 ] In Saudi Arabia, tests on [C12mim][Cl] at 10–1000 ppm in Indiana Limestone (100 °C) resulted in 12.1–26.6% AOR.[ 108 ] However, upon using shorter alkyl‐chain IL with Saudi Arabia's Uthmaniyah crude oil, [C4mim]Cl, [C4mim]Br, and [C4mim]I at 10 000 ppm, they achieved 14%, 16%, and 20% AOR, respectively, supplementing 31–34% recovery from water flooding.[ 73 ]

Experimental data confirm that imidazolium‐based ILs significantly enhance AOR, particularly in high‐salinity carbonate and sand‐packed reservoirs. ILs like [C12mim][Cl] and [C16mim][Br] achieved AOR up to ≈28%, benefiting from synergistic effects of IFT reduction and wettability alteration. Tailoring IL structure to reservoir conditions—including salinity, temperature, and lithology—is critical for maximizing EOR performance and field applicability.

Oil Recovery by Spontaneous Imbibition

Spontaneous imbibition is a process driven by capillary forces. A wetting fluid (such as water or a surfactant solution) infiltrates a porous rock matrix and displaces oil without external pressure or induced flow. This phenomenon occurs due to an imbalance in capillary forces at the rock, fluid, and oil interfaces. Figure 11 presents recent spontaneous imbibition studies using C4 imidazolium ILs at different anions (BF4−, BF6−, Cl−, Br−, and I−) conducted by Sakthivel et al. in 2021, 2023, and 2025. They showed significant improvement in spontaneous imbibition and oil displacement under high‐temperature, high‐salinity conditions, making them promising candidates for cost‐effective EOR applications. The application of 500 ppm [C4mim][Cl] in Saudi carbonate cores demonstrated an AOR% of 26.55% compared to 31.25% with seawater alone, confirming its effectiveness in altering wettability and enhancing capillary‐driven oil displacement.[ 20 ] Higher permeability rocks (250 mD) exhibited greater recovery than lower permeability ones (50 mD), as increased pore connectivity allowed for better IL diffusion and interaction.[ 20 ] Additionally, ILs maintained chemical stability at 100 °C and high salinity (TDS = 67 500 ppm), confirming their suitability for harsh reservoir conditions.

Oil recovery by spontaneous imbibition studies using C4 imidazolium ILs at different anions (BF4 −, BF6 −, Cl−, Br−, and I−) at 100 °C.[ 20 , 73 , 86 ]

Sakthivel et al.[ 86 ] confirm that 500 ppm of [C4mim][PF6] achieved an AOR% of 45.0%, outperforming [C4mim][BF4] (33.4%) and seawater alone (24.3%).[ 86 ] The superior performance of [PF6]− ILs is attributed to their lower charge density, which allows for deeper penetration at the oil–rock interface, more substantial wettability alteration, and improved oil displacement. Molecular simulations confirm that [PF6]− interacts more effectively with the rock surface than [BF4]−, leading to greater oil recovery. Additionally, ILs exhibited excellent stability in high‐salinity environments (TDS = 67 500 ppm) and outperformed traditional surfactants (AOS and CTAB), which only improved recovery to 35.4% and 37.3%, respectively.[ 86 ] Overall, [C4mim][PF6] is a highly effective and cost‐efficient EOR agent, offering a promising alternative to conventional chemical flooding methods in carbonate reservoirs.

Imidazolium‐based ILs with halide anions ([Cl]−, [Br]−, and [I]−) significantly improved spontaneous imbibition oil recovery in Saudi carbonate cores (Uthmaniyah Oil Reservoir, 16–18% porosity, 105–120 mD permeability) at 100 °C and TDS = 67 500 ppm.[ 73 ] While seawater alone recovered 24% oil, ILs at 2000 ppm increased AOR% to 31% ([C4mim][Cl]), 36% ([C4mim][Br]), and 38% ([C4mim][I]), confirming that larger anions ([I]− > [Br]− > [Cl]−) enhance oil displacement.[ 73 ] The [I]− ILs demonstrated superior wettability alteration (contact angle shift from 158° to 79°) and better diffusion at the oil–rock interface, leading to more efficient oil mobilization. Molecular dynamic (MD) simulations further confirm that anion size and polarizability modulate IL orientation, with larger anions facilitating better surface packing and reducing IL loss via adsorption. For example, chloride anions can form protective layers on carbonate surfaces, preventing deep IL penetration, while iodide anions exhibit reduced surface screening, allowing more effective cation–oil interactions.[ 73 ] These insights highlight that anion selection is critical for thermodynamic stability and solubility, and optimizing EOR mechanisms such as wettability alteration, IFT reduction, and minimal adsorption losses.

Conclusion

This review presents a comprehensive analysis of imidazolium‐based ILs for EOR, highlighting their performance in key areas such as IFT reduction, wettability alteration, emulsification, viscosity control, and adsorption minimization. Compared to conventional surfactants and other IL families, imidazolium ILs consistently demonstrate superior efficiency across various reservoir conditions, particularly under high‐salinity and elevated temperatures.

Longer alkyl‐chain imidazolium ILs, such as [C12mim][Cl] and [C16mim][Br], achieve ultralow IFT values, enhance water‐wetness in carbonate rocks, and effectively disperse asphaltenes—improving crude oil mobility. Their low CMCs and surface‐active behavior support cost‐effective formulations. Additionally, these ILs reduce chemical loss by minimizing adsorption on reservoir rocks and offer good compatibility with coadditives, making them ideal candidates for hybrid EOR systems.

Beyond technical performance, imidazolium ILs are recyclable, chemically stable, and increasingly cost‐competitive due to advances in synthesis. Their environmental footprint can be managed through functional group modifications and recovery strategies, further supporting their viability as sustainable alternatives in EOR operations.

Future Directions

Future research on imidazolium‐based ILs for EOR should optimize their molecular structure, stability, and economic feasibility for large‐scale applications, as shown in Figure 12 . Modifying alkyl chain length and anion selection can enhance wettability alteration, reduce IFT, and improve salt tolerance, making ILs more effective under harsh reservoir conditions. Further studies should investigate hybrid formulations that combine ILs with nanoparticles, surfactants, or polymers to enhance their stability and performance. Addressing adsorption and retention issues within rock formations will also minimize IL loss and maximize recovery efficiency.

Future perspectives and research directions for imidazolium‐based ILs in EOR.

Additionally, MD simulations and AI‐driven predictive models can accelerate IL screening and customization for specific reservoir conditions. By leveraging machine learning (ML) algorithms, researchers can predict key physicochemical properties—such as IFT, viscosity, and adsorption—based on structural features and molecular descriptors of ILs.[ 109 ] These models help reduce experimental effort and cost while accelerating the discovery process. Furthermore, coupling AI‐driven molecular design with MD simulations and high‐throughput screening platforms provides a powerful approach for tailoring ILs for specific EOR challenges, such as high‐temperature, high‐salinity reservoirs. As the field evolves, AI‐integrated IL research is expected to be crucial in advancing the next generation of cost‐effective, sustainable, and high‐performance chemical flooding agents.

Beyond improving performance, reducing costs, minimizing environmental impact, and validating large‐scale field applications must be prioritized. Future efforts should focus on developing cost‐effective IL synthesis methods and evaluating IL reusability and biodegradability to ensure sustainability. Pilot‐scale field trials across various reservoir types will be crucial for validating laboratory findings and optimizing IL injection strategies. Synergistic approaches could further enhance oil displacement, including combining ILs with polymers or CO2 flooding. By incorporating advanced simulation techniques, ML, and sustainable chemistry, imidazolium‐based ILs have the potential to offer a commercially viable and environmentally friendly solution for EOR in complex reservoirs.
