Engineering Rhodotorula toruloides as a platform organism for de novo synthesis of fatty-acid esters
Authors: Junyang Wang, Yang Zhang, Baisong Tong, Qiongyu Meng, Kai Yuan, Zhihui Wang, Dengbin Li, Yufeng Guo, Zongjie Dai, Zihe Liu, Huimin Zhao, Shuobo Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fatty acid esters are building blocks in next-generation biofuels, eco-friendly detergents, crop-enhancing adjuvants, and high-value cosmetic emollients. However, their industrial production relies on two unsustainable approaches: petrochemical-derived chemical catalysis that poses sustainability challenges and botanical extraction processes that exacerbates land-use conflicts. Here we develop a de novo microbial biosynthesis platform using the non-conventional oleaginous yeast Rhodotorula toruloides as a chassis, and achieve high-level biosynthesis of structurally diversified esters without the addition of alcohol or lipid precursors. By screening specific pathway enzymes and conducting modular pathway engineering, we successfully reprogram the native lipogenic metabolism of R. toruloides for de novo synthesis of fatty acid ethyl esters (FAEEs) at 579 mg/L, fatty acid short-branched chain esters (FASBEs) at 169 mg/L, and wax esters (WEs) at 1.30 g/L in shake-flask fermentation. As a case study, we optimize the synthesis of WEs in a 5 L fermenter, and achieve a production of 13.04 g/L. These engineered strains potentially offer an efficient, economical and environmentally friendly platform for the industrial production of fatty acid esters and oleochemicals.

Introduction

Fatty acid esters, molecular hybrids of fatty acids esterified to diverse alcohols, serve as indispensable precursors for biofuels, biodegradable detergents, agricultural adjuvants, and premium cosmetics. Typical fatty acid esters include fatty acid ethyl esters (FAEEs), fatty acid short-branched chain esters (FASBEs), and wax esters (WEs)1. Fatty acid short-chain esters, such as FAEEs and FASBEs, produced by the reaction of long-chain fatty acids and short-chain alcohols, are more suitable for use as biofuels, with high calorific value, good fuel performance, and have the potential to replace fossil diesel2. WEs are long-chain fatty acid alkyl ester compounds composed of a long-chain acyl moiety and a long-chain alkyl moiety, which are widely used in lubricating oil, pharmaceutical, printing, food and other industries due to their excellent physical and chemical properties3. However, the availability of natural resources for fatty acid esters is constrained due to their origins from limited sources4,5, including plant and animal fats and oils, as well as fossil fuel reserves. At present, due to the increasing emphasis on ecological resources and the rapidly growing demand for green and sustainable development6, the use of microorganisms to produce a variety of renewable fuels and green chemicals, including fatty acid esters, is attracting more and more attention.I confirm that all accession codes and datasets mentioned in the paper have been scheduled for public release.

Typically, the synthetic pathway of fatty acid esters consists of an alcohol-producing module and a fatty acid synthesis module, which can be esterified by WE synthase (WS)7. A great deal of work has been recently carried out, including constructing pathways to produce specific esters and increasing titer of target ester molecules through various metabolic engineering strategies8–11. Although various fatty acid esters could be produced via microbial manufacturing, inadequate supply of fatty acid precursors in non-oleaginous strains, such as Escherichia coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae, may limit fatty acid ester production, suggesting that the use of host strains with strong lipid-producing capacity may be beneficial. Yarrowia lipolytica is the most studied oleaginous yeast12. An engineered Y. lipolytica expressing various fatty acid elongases along with WSs and FARs was successfully used to synthesize jojoba-like WEs (C38–C42), and the production level of WEs was increased to 2 g/L in a 5 L bioreactor by weakening the β-oxidation pathway and overexpressing genes associated with fatty acid precursor accumulation13. In a separate study, waste cooking oil was used as a carbon source for an engineered Y. lipolytica strain containing WS and FAR, and 7.6 g/L of WEs was produced in shake-flask fermentation4. Rhodotorula toruloides is another promising oleaginous yeast for fatty acid ester production14. Compared to Y. lipolytica, it has a high resistance to biomass hydrolysates containing inhibitors and has the ability to naturally utilize diverse carbon sources derived from plants, thereby presenting a broader range of industrial applications14,15. R. toruloides can not only use its own metabolic pathways to produce lipids but also produce various high-value oleochemicals by metabolizing them, which has huge potential for the production of fatty acid derivatives and is a good platform for the industrial production of oleochemicals. R. toruloides was recently engineered to produce fatty acid esters through metabolic engineering, but with the addition of exogenous ethanol, resulting in a FAEEs titer of 9.97 g/L16.

In this study, we explore the potential of R. toruloides as a sustainable oleaginous platform organism for producing oleochemicals from carbohydrate resources. We successfully de novo synthesize two value-added oleochemicals, namely FAEEs and FASBEs, along with one high-value oleochemical, WEs, in R. toruloides, thereby establishing a production platform for fatty-acid esters (Fig. 1). Leveraging the knowledge gained from previous research on the metabolic pathways of FAEEs, FASBEs, and WEs, as well as pathways for fatty alcohol, ethanol and short-branched chain alcohol synthesis, we construct and optimize fatty-acid ester biosynthesis pathways in R. toruloides by introducing relevant pathway genes. Additionally, we employ a microbial consortia strategy to facilitate FAEEs production. The engineered strains of R. toruloides achieve the highest reported titers to date for de novo synthesis of FASBEs and WEs in shake flasks. Scale-up fermentation achieves WEs at a concentration of 13.04 g/L. Results and discussion

De novo synthesis of FAEEs

FAEEs, promising bioenergy molecules for biodiesel, require robust lipid-accumulating chassis cells to establish mature synthetic biology-driven cell factories17,18. Several studies have reported the efficient production of FAEEs in oleaginous yeasts. However, they required the supplementation of ethanol in the medium16,19.

FAEEs can be produced by the esterification of ethanol and fatty acyl-CoA catalyzed by a WS16. To enable the strain R. toruloides to synthesize FAEEs de novo, codon-optimized AbWS* gene, five ethanol dehydrogenase genes (ZmADHB, KmADH1, KpMDR, BsADHII, and ScADH1) and an endogenous pyruvate decarboxylase gene (RtPDC) were overexpressed in the strain R. toruloides Δku70 (Fig. 2a). A titer of 29.0 mg/L of FAEEs was detected in the engineered strain Δku70-AbWS*-RtPDC-BsADHII (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). The proportion of each component of FAEEs detected was consistent with a previous report16, which was mainly composed of 3.3% ethyl myristate (C14:0–C2:0), 25.9% ethyl palmitate (C16:0–C2:0), 8.2% ethyl stearate (C18:0–C2:0), 50.9% ethyl oleate (C18:1–C2:0) and 11.7% ethyl linoleate (C18:2–C2:0). The predominance of ethyl oleate and palmitate aligns with endogenous fatty acid profiles (Supplementary Fig. 5)20, suggesting substrate-driven product distribution. As potential biodiesel components, FAEEs with different chain lengths and saturation levels confer distinct fuel properties21. Although the de novo synthesis pathway of FAEEs has been successfully constructed in R. toruloides, the titer of FAEEs in the engineered strain achieved only 29.0 mg/L, which was limited by insufficient ethanol supply, a bottleneck highlighted by prior studies where addition of exogenous ethanol boosted the titer to 1.02 g/L16. Testing alternative pyruvate decarboxylases (PDCs) from S. cerevisiae (ScPDC) and Z. mobilis (ZmPDC) in conjunction with alcohol dehydrogenase (ADHII) from B. spectabilis (BsADHII) yielded negligible improvement (29.9 mg/L FAEEs), suggesting low PDC activity and competition with mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase for precursors (Fig. 2c). To address this issue, the bifunctional acetaldehyde/alcohol dehydrogenases (AdhE) from Thermoanaerobacterium saccharolyticum, which can synthesize ethanol using acetyl-CoA22, were introduced into R. toruloide. A mutant AdhEG544D, which was reported to have higher ethanol synthesis capacity23, was introduced, enabling acetyl-CoA-to-ethanol conversion in R. toruloides. While the recombinant strain Δku70-AbWS*-adhEG544D produced 22 mg/L of FAEEs (Fig. 2c), titers remained suboptimal, likely due to intrinsic metabolic constraints limiting ethanol overproduction. To test whether ethanol supply still limits FAEE synthesis, two lines of investigation were performed. First, supplementing exogenous ethanol (1%, 3%, 5%) into engineered strains, including Δku70-AbWS*-RtPDC-BsADHII, Δku70-AbWS*-ScPDC-BsADHII and Δku70-AbWS*-adhEG544D, increased FAEE titers in a dose-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 6). Endogenous ethanol remained undetectable in these engineered strains, consistent with its rapid conversion to product. Second, we assessed the intracellular NADH/NAD⁺ ratio (Supplementary Fig. 7), given the dependence of ethanol synthesis on reducing power. Engineered strains showed an initial decrease in NADH, corresponding to pathway activity for ethanol production, followed by partial recovery, suggesting cellular adaptation. Together, these data confirm that insufficient ethanol supply is a major constraint on FAEE production and underscore the need to address regulatory mechanisms governing pyruvate flux and ethanol production in future engineering efforts. In addition, R. toruloides was shown to produce FAEEs from lignocellulose hydrolysate, albeit at lower yields than from glucose or xylose‑glucose mixtures due to growth‑inhibitory compounds (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). In subsequent work, growth optimization could be performed for hydrolysate use.

Co-culture strategy to increase the production of FAEEs

Although we have successfully engineered R. toruloides to de novo produce FAEEs, the current production levels remain low. Exogenous ethanol supplementation has been shown to significantly enhance FAEEs production in R. toruloides16, and a similar dose-dependent manner was also found in our engineered strains (Supplementary Fig. 6), suggesting ethanol availability as a key constraint. Co-cultures were established in shake flasks containing xylose:glucose (1:2) as the carbon source that mimics the industrial substrate composition of hydrolysate, and serves as a sustained carbon reservoir for lipid synthesis24–27. The inoculation ratio of R. toruloides to S. cerevisiae was varied (1:5, 1:1, 5:1, 10:1, 20:1, 50:1 and 100:1), with monocultures of each strain serving as controls. Fermentation at 30 °C with 200 rpm revealed optimal FAEEs production (89.6 mg/L) and ethanol accumulation (8.3 g/L) at a 50:1 ratio (Fig. 3b, c). Glucose was fully consumed across all conditions, but xylose utilization remained limited (≤38.7%) (Fig. 3d, e). Despite improved FAEEs titers, ethanol levels remained below the threshold required for maximal R. toruloides productivity16, highlighting unresolved metabolic bottlenecks.

To optimize FAEEs synthesis, we next developed an anaerobic-aerobic dual-chamber co-culture system using 0.22 μm filter membranes for compartmentalized fermentation (Fig. 3f). Dissolved oxygen (DO) levels were tightly regulated by magnetic stirring: aerobic chamber maintained ~2.0 mg/L DO, while the anaerobic chamber remained below 0.2 mg/L. Fermentation at 30 °C with 66 g/L glucose and 33 g/L xylose as carbon sources (10% inoculum) yielded robust growth: R. toruloides reached OD600 11.7 (8 g/L cell dry weight), while S. cerevisiae achieved OD600 11.2 (11.8 g/L cell dry weight) (Fig. 3g). Ethanol titers surged to 47.9 g/L, driving FAEEs production to 579 mg/L, comprising ethyl myristate (C14:0–C2:0, 11 mg/L), ethyl palmitate (C16:0–C2:0, 102 mg/L), ethyl stearate (C18:0–C2:0, 132 mg/L), ethyl oleate (C18:1–C2:0, 256 mg/L), and ethyl linoleate (C18:2–C2:0, 78 mg/L) (Fig. 3h, i). This dual-chamber co-culture system increased the ethanol production by 10-fold and FAEEs production by 6-fold compared to the shake-flask fermentation. In the dual-chamber system with mixed sugars (xylose:glucose), both strains exhibited preferential glucose consumption (Fig. 3h), consistent with known sequential utilization in R. toruloides28; the remaining xylose may play a critical role in maintaining metabolic homeostasis, thereby supporting lipid biosynthesis under a favorable C/N ratio26. The two chambers of the co-culture system are separated by a 0.22 µm filter membrane, permitting the free diffusion of small molecules (e.g., ethanol, glucose) while preventing physical cell contact. Control fermentations were performed where S. cerevisiae and R. toruloides were inoculated alone in the system. Ethanol production by S. cerevisiae in monoculture (Supplementary Fig. 9a) was consistent with the pattern observed in co-culture (Fig. 3h), indicating that its ethanol-producing ability was not significantly affected by the presence of R. toruloides. In contrast, R. toruloides alone produced only 48 mg/L FAEEs (Supplementary Fig. 9b), which is markedly lower than the 579 mg/L achieved in co-culture. This result directly confirms that efficient FAEE biosynthesis in our system is dependent on the supply of ethanol precursors from S. cerevisiae. Collectively, these single-strain controls validated the reliability of the dual-chamber system: the 0.22 μm membrane filter permitted diffusion of small molecules (ethanol, glucose) while completely blocking cell passage, and conclusively confirmed that high-efficiency FAEEs synthesis strictly depended on ethanol supplied by S. cerevisiae. Because the chambers are physically separated, stirring speeds can be adjusted independently, avoiding the oxygen demand conflict typical of single‑vessel co‑cultures. Understanding and optimizing the dose-response between stirring speed and growth/metabolism is essential for scale-up and industrial application in the future.

De novo synthesis of fatty acid short-branched chain esters

FASBEs, formed by the condensation of fatty acids and short-branched-chain alcohols, exhibit superior fuel properties compared to FAEEs due to methyl side chains that reduce intermolecular van der Waals forces and enhance low-temperature fluidity. While FASBEs have been synthesized in model microbes, their de novo production in oleaginous yeasts remains unexplored29,30. Building on advances in FAEE biosynthesis, we engineered R. toruloides to harness its lipid-rich metabolism for sustainable FASBE synthesis. To enable the de novo synthesis of FASBEs in R. toruloides, we introduced a heterologous pathway for short-branched-chain alcohol production. Endogenous amino acid-derived 2-ketoisovalerate and 2-keto-4-methylvalerate were converted to isobutanol and isoamyl alcohol via ketoacid decarboxylases (Aro10, Kivd, Pdc6) and alcohol dehydrogenase Adh2 (Fig. 4a). As shown in Fig. 4b, FASBEs were only detected in the engineered strain Δku70-AbWS*-ScADH2-Kivd, comprising isobutyl palmitate, isobutyl stearate, and isobutyl oleate and isobutyl linoleate (Supplementary Fig. 10). The total titer of FASBEs produced was 7.2 mg/L, consistent with the titer reported in E. coli10. Despite R. toruloides is rich in fatty acid precursors, FASBE levels remained low. This indicates that the limited availability of short-chain alcohols is the bottleneck, a situation analogous to what was observed during the production of FAEEs. To validate this hypothesis, we explored the use of recombinant strain ΔKu70-AbWS* to efficiently synthesize FASBEs under the condition of exogenous addition of isobutanol. The result showed that exogenous isobutanol supplementation (1%) boosted FASBEs titers to 698 mg/L (600 mg/L extracellular; 98 mg/L intracellular), with isobutyl oleate dominating (83%). Since the supply of isobutanol limited the production of FASBEs, we next tried to optimize the isobutanol biosynthetic pathway. The isobutanol biosynthetic pathway was constructed by subcellular compartmentalization: upstream mitochondrial enzymes (the endogenous Ilv2, Ilv5, Ilv3) converted pyruvate to α-ketoisovalerate (α-KIV), while cytoplasmic KDC and ADH completed synthesis31. To bypass intermediate transport inefficiencies and avoid the consumption of intermediates by competing pathways, we targeted pathway colocalization in mitochondria. Comparative analysis via sequence alignment revealed that certain mitochondrial localization sequences (MLSs) in S. cerevisiae were present in R. toruloides (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2, and Supplementary Data 1). Functional validation of endogenous localization sequence of R. toruloides and heterogeneous localization sequence of S. cerevisiae using MLS-GFP fusions revealed successful mitochondrial targeting, evidenced by punctate fluorescence (Fig. 4c). Three sequences (ScPDB1m, ScGRX2m and RtMAM33m) demonstrated robust localization as MLSs, enabling future engineering of compartmentalized pathways (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 12)32,33. Subsequently, the downstream heterogeneous enzymes KDC and ADH were targeted into the mitochondria using the N-terminal mitochondrial localization signal (MLS) ScGRX2m (Fig. 4d). The strain Δku70-AbWS*-ScADH2-Kivd-ScGRX2m, expressing the mitochondrially targeted pathway, achieved FASBE titers of 169 mg/L, significantly surpassing the cytoplasmic counterpart (Δku70-AbWS*-ScADH2-Kivd) (Fig. 4e). This result confirms mitochondrial activity of the enzymes and highlights the efficacy of the ScGRX2m MLS for organelle-specific targeting. Enhanced productivity likely stems from the higher effective enzyme concentration within mitochondria, owing to their reduced volume relative to the cytoplasm. In addition, endogenous isobutanol levels were quantified in the Δku70 background strain, the cytosolic variant, and the mitochondrially targeted strain (Fig. 4f). Notably, mitochondrial targeting significantly improved both isobutanol synthesis and FASBE production. The data reveal a positive correlation between isobutanol concentration and FASBE titer in these engineered strains.

De novo synthesis of wax esters

WEs are mainly obtained through plant extraction, chemical synthesis or catalytic production using immobilized lipases4. Chemical synthesis has drawbacks like the use of corrosive acids and high energy consumption, while plant extraction is often inefficient and challenging due to its naturally low abundance4. Moreover, both chemical synthesis and lipase-catalyzed WE synthesis require fatty alcohols as substrates4. Microbial de novo synthesis, combining fatty acyl-CoA reductase (FAR) and wax ester synthase (WS) to convert acyl-CoA to alcohols and esterify them, offers a sustainable alternative.

R. toruloides has been used for the synthesis and accumulation of fatty acid derivatives34, but it cannot naturally synthesize fatty alcohols. It has been reported that Marinobacter aquaeolei VT8 has two FAR, which can directly reduce fatty acyl-CoA to the corresponding fatty alcohols in a variety of chassis cells such as E. coli35, S. cerevisiae36 and Cyanobacteria37. Therefore, we introduced the M. aquaeolei FAR gene (Maqu), enabling direct acyl-CoA-to-alcohol conversion in R. toruloides (Fig. 5a). The engineered strain Δku70-Maqu produced 675 mg/L fatty alcohols (94% C18:0), contrasting S. cerevisiae38, where C16:0 dominates (Fig. 5b). To achieve de novo synthesis of WEs, both WS and FAR need to be expressed40. Codon-optimized Maqu was introduced into Δku70-AbWS* to obtain the strain Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu. As expected, co-expressing codon-optimized Maqu and AbWS* in R. toruloides Δku70 enabled de novo WE synthesis (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 13). The engineered strain achieved 525 mg/L WEs (Fig. 5d), primarily at the chain length of C36 (49.7%), with unsaturated variants (C36:1, C36:2) linked to the host’s high oleic content41. This titer surpasses prior yeast platforms (e.g., 36.5 mg/L in engineered S. cerevisiae)40, underscoring R. toruloides’ potential for lipid-engineered bioproduction.

The physicochemical properties of WEs hinge on the structural composition of their fatty acid and alcohol components. A key example is jojoba oil, a high-value natural WE composed of very long-chain monounsaturated fatty acids and alcohols (C38–C42), prized in cosmetics and pharmaceuticals for its unique functional traits42. Jojoba-like WEs (C38–C42) were shorter in the engineered strain Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu. The β-ketoacyl-CoA synthase (KCS), also known as fatty acid elongase, can catalyze further extension of fatty acids43. As R. toruloides natively produces limited fatty acids beyond C18, we introduced plant-derived elongases (CraKCS from Crambe abyssinica and CgKCS from Cardamine graeca for R. toruloides) into the WE-producing strain Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu to generate jojoba-like WEs. While CraKCS failed to express, the CgKCS-expressing strain Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu-CgKCS synthesized WEs with extended chain lengths (C32–C42), with a total amount of 565.03 mg/L (Fig. 5e, f and Supplementary Fig. 14). The WE titers in the KCS-expressing strain were comparable to those of its parental strain (Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu). This finding suggests that while overexpressing CgKCS significantly extends the fatty acid chain length, its effect on increasing the total WE titer is relatively modest. In Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu-CgKCS, C36 WEs dominated (203.33 mg/L), with unsaturated variants (C36:1/C36:2) comprising 54.13%, reflecting R. toruloides’ lipid profile dominated by C18 and C18:1 fatty acids (Supplementary Fig. 15). Notably, the engineered strains expressing CgKCS enable the synthesis of jojoba-like WEs (C38, C40, and C42), with a titer of 95.99 mg/L, accounting for 16.99% of the total WEs (Fig. 5e, f). Very long-chain fatty alcohols (VLCFAlcs) were observed in Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu-CgKCS (Supplementary Fig. 16), confirming the proposed biosynthetic pathway. As co-expressing heterologous KCS enzymes in Y. lipolytica boosted VLCFAs13, future work could optimize R. toruloides by genomic integration of multiple CgKCS copies, enhancing enzyme activity, or combinatorial KCS expression to increase jojoba-like WE yields.

We next characterized the substrate scope of AbWS* and MaWS by supplying a range of alcohols (decyl, lauryl, tridecyl and myristyl alcohol). Both enzymes synthesized the corresponding fatty acid esters from all tested alcohols, although titers varied, indicating differences in their catalytic efficiency (Fig. 6b–f; Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19). In addition, we evaluated their performance in de novo wax ester (WE) synthesis by co-expressing each WS with the long-chain alcohol pathway (FAR and KCS). Both enzymes produced WEs, but with distinct profiles. MaWS gave a higher ability in forming total WEs, and AbWS* showed higher activity in generating jojoba-like esters derived from very long-chain alcohols (Figs. 5f and 6g).

While AbWS* and MaWS differ in their chain-length preference and efficiency, the platform successfully accommodates multiple functional WSs. These results clearly demonstrated that the R. toruloides platform has the capacity to accommodate multiple WS enzymes. The observed differences in activity and product spectrum underscore the platform’s inherent modularity and expandability. The product spectrum can be tuned by selecting appropriate WSs and engineering the supply of their requisite precursors.

Optimization and fed-batch fermentation of WEs-producing strains

To enhance WE production, particularly that of high-value jojoba-like WEs, we engineered R. toruloides Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu-CgKCS to accumulate more fatty-acid precursors by overexpressing key genes in acetyl-CoA synthesis (ACL1 and ACS1) and malonyl-CoA synthesis (ACC1) under the strong promoter PGPD1. Enhancing their expression through metabolic engineering has been demonstrated to effectively increase the synthesis of fatty acids13,44. In the strain Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu-CgKCS, overexpression of ACL1 and ACS1 significantly enhanced WE titers, whereas ACC1 overexpression showed no significant improvement (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 20a). Specifically, the engineered strain Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu-CgKCS-ACL1 achieved a WE titer of 1.21 g/L, while the engineered strain Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu-CgKCS-ACS1 reached 1.08 g/L (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 20a). This result contradicts previous reports, such as in Y. lipolytica, where overexpressing ACC1 increased FAEEs production by 48%19. The discrepancy may be due to species-specific regulation of fatty acid synthesis, highlighting the complexity of lipid metabolism and indicating that amplifying a single “rate-limiting” enzyme is often insufficient without coordinating the broader metabolic network.

Next, we attempted to overexpress ACL1 and ACS1 in Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu-CgKCS at the same time, which resulted in the engineered strain Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu-CgKCS-ACS1-ACL1. The WEs titer of the strain Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu-CgKCS-ACS1-ACL1 reached 1.30 g/L, ~2.29-fold of the WEs produced in the parental strain Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu-CgKCS (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 20b). Notably, jojoba-like WEs (C38–C42) reached 278.29 mg/L (21.48% of total WEs), about 2.90-fold of the jojoba-like WEs produced in the parental strain Δku70-AbWS*-Maqu-CgKCS (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 20b). Similar ACL1 overexpression in Y. lipolytica increased C20 fatty acids13, suggesting a mechanism for enhanced long-chain WE synthesis. The production stability of this high-performing strain was confirmed over serial passages, with WE titers remaining consistent (Supplementary Fig. 21).

This study describes the metabolic engineering of R. toruloides for the de novo synthesis of value-added fatty acid esters (FAEEs, FASBEs, WEs) without exogenous precursors. FAEEs production reached 579 mg/L via an anaerobic–aerobic dual-chamber co-culture system with S. cerevisiae and R. toruloides. The key metabolic exchange is ethanol diffusion from the anaerobic (S. cerevisiae) to the aerobic chamber (R. toruloides). This exchange drove an approximately 12-fold increase in FAEE production (579 mg/L vs. 48 mg/L), confirming its critical role. For FASBEs, an engineered isobutanol pathway combined with AbWS* yielded 7.2 mg/L, but exogenous isobutanol supplementation (1%) boosted the titer to 698 mg/L, revealing alcohol supply as the bottleneck. Mitochondrial engineering of downstream isobutanol genes further improved the de novo production of FASBEs, reaching 169 mg/L. De novo WEs synthesis was achieved by introducing Maqu and AbWS*, yielding 525 mg/L. With chain extension via CgKCS and overexpression of ACL1 and ACS1 enabled the production of total WEs at 1.30 g/L, with jojoba-like WEs reaching 278.29 mg/L (21.48% of total). Fed-batch fermentation of the engineered strain achieved a record 13.04 g/L WEs (including 1.69 g/L jojoba-like WEs), the highest titer reported in microbial factories. Cytotoxicity assays confirmed that these esters were not inhibitory at production levels (Supplementary Fig. 22). Metabolically, fatty acyl-CoA serves as a central node, shared by the engineered pathways and native lipid biosynthesis. While competitive, native triacylglycerol synthesis and its associated lipid-body formation may also offer a route to stabilize ester products12. Therefore, comprehensive yield improvements will require future coordinated engineering of multiple nodes, focusing on: (i) balancing flux between alcohol and acyl-CoA branches, (ii) enhancing heterologous gene expression and mitigating potential bottleneck, and (iii) optimizing enzyme performance through homolog screening (Supplementary Figs. 23–28).

This study also evaluates R. toruloides as a synthetic biology platform for de novo synthesis of fatty acid esters, focusing on its modularity and expandability and its substrate versatility (Fig. 6). The platform reliably produced esters when AbWS* or MaWS was paired with various exogenous short-chain alcohols, and the detection of a spectrum of esters confirmed functional compatibility. More importantly, both wax synthases utilized de novo synthesized long-chain alcohols (derived from the KCS pathway) to produce wax esters, albeit with distinct specificities. These results demonstrate that the platform readily accommodates different enzyme-substrate combinations, allowing the product profile to be tuned by selecting specific WSs. Furthermore, the innate metabolic flexibility of R. toruloides is a key industrial advantage. We confirmed its capacity to utilize low-cost, complex feedstocks by achieving fatty acid titers comparable to those obtained with industrial molasses versus pure glucose (Supplementary Fig. 29). This inherent ability to process real-world substrates underscores its potential as a robust chassis for economically viable biomanufacturing.

In summary, fatty acid ester production with short-chain alcohols (e.g., ethanol, isobutanol) benefits from co-culturing S. cerevisiae (efficient alcohol producer) with lipid-rich R. toruloides, whereas long-chain alcohol-derived esters (e.g., WEs) capitalize on the native lipid-accumulating capacity of oleaginous yeast. These findings establish R. toruloides as a versatile, scalable platform for sustainable biosynthesis of high-value fatty acid derivatives, bridging synthetic biology with industrial bioprocessing.

Methods

Strains and culture conditions

The parent strain, R. toruloides Δku7016, is a derivative of R. toruloides IFO0880 with a deletion in the non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) gene, KU70. Agrobacterium tumefaciens AGL-1 strain was used for transformation experiments of R. toruloides45. E. coli DH5α was used for plasmid construction. All strains used in this work are listed in Supplementary Data 3.

R. toruloides strains were cultured in YPD (containing 10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L peptone, 20 g/L glucose) at 30 °C for genetic manipulation, or fermentation medium (with 48 g/L glucose, 15.7 g/L peptone, 15.7 g/L yeast extract) for fed-batch cultivation. E. coli and A. tumefaciens were grown in LB at 37 °C and 2YT (composed of 16 g/L tryptone, 10 g/L yeast extract, 5 g/L NaCl) at 28 °C, respectively. Antibiotics were supplemented as needed: kanamycin (50 μg/mL), ampicillin/hygromycin (100 μg/mL), cefotaxime (300 μg/mL), rifampicin (25 μg/mL). Detailed culture conditions can be found in the supplementary notes.

Plasmid construction and DNA manipulation

All plasmids used in this work are listed in Supplementary Data 4. The primers and optimized gene sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 5 and 2. The pKOCAR2 plasmid was used as the gene expression vector for yeast transformation46. This plasmid contains a hygromycin selection cassette composed of a GPD1 promoter from Rhodotorula graminis, a codon-optimized hygromycin gene and a SV40 terminator46. Downstream of the hygromycin selection cassette, a GPD1 promoter from R. toruloides and a 35S terminator were located, enabling the insertion of exogenous genes46. The details of the plasmid construction procedure can be found in the supplementary notes. All heterologous genes were codon-optimized, with their sequences provided in supplementary Data 2.

Analytical methods of biomass and extracellular metabolites

The growth curves of the R. toruloides strains were determined by measuring the cell density at 600 nm using a Genesys 20 spectrophotometer (Thermofisher Scientific Inc., USA). Analysis of extracellular metabolites was carried out using an HPLC system (Shimadzu LC-20AT, Japan). The culture samples were subjected to centrifugation. Subsequently, the supernatant was passed through a 0.22-μm membrane and stored at - 20 °C until analysis. To quantify the concentrations of glucose, xylose and ethanol, standard solutions of glucose, xylose and ethanol at various concentrations were prepared. Concentration gradients of 2 g/L, 1 g/L, 0.5 g/L, 0.2 g/L and 0.1 g/L were established to construct the standard curve. The chromatographic conditions were as follows: a chromatographic column (Xtiamte® Suger-H, 7.8 mm × 300 mm, 5.0 μm, Weich Co., LTD.), a column temperature chamber of 40 °C, a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min, an injection volume of 5 μL, and a mobile phase of 5 mM H2SO4.

Analysis of total lipids

To determine the fatty acid composition, total lipids were prepared and analyzed according to a previously reported method16. Briefly, cell cultures (200 μL) were mixed with 1 mL water, 10 μL of 40% tetrabutylammonium hydroxide, and 200 μL dichloromethane containing 200 mM methyl iodide and 100 mg/L myristelaidic acid (internal standard). The mixture was vortexed for 30 min, centrifuged (5000 × g) for 3 min, and 100 μL of the organic phase was dried and resuspended in 160 μL hexane for GC-MS analysis (QP2020, Shimadzu, Japan). The GC program was: initial temperature 40 °C held for 2 min, then ramped to 130 °C at 30 °C/min, then to 280 °C at 10 °C/min and held for 3 min. The temperatures of the inlet, transfer line, and ion source were 280, 300, and 230 °C, respectively.

Analysis of fatty alcohols

The fermentation solution was centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was then mixed with 30 mL ethyl acetate three times using a separatory funnel to collect the organic phase. The organic phase was concentrated using a vacuum concentrator for approximately 2 hours. Subsequently, 50 μL N-Methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA) (Aladdin reagent Co., China) was added and incubated at 80 °C for 30 min. Then, 50 μL n-hexane was added, and the mixture was centrifuged at 6000 × g for 3 min. The supernatant was detected by a SHIMADZU GC-MS-QP2010 PLUS mass spectrometer coupled with a SHIMADZU gas chromatograph (Shimadzu Inc., Japan). The GC-MS procedure involved an initial column temperature of 80 °C, held for 0.5 minutes, followed by a temperature increase to 250 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min, and held for 5 minutes. The carrier gas was helium at a flow rate of 1 mL/min, with a sample injection volume of 1 μL. The ion source temperature was 230 °C, the interface temperature was 280 °C, and the ion scan m/z was 50–650.

Analysis of FAEEs

FAEEs were present both extracellularly and intracellularly. The preparation and detection of intracellular FAEEs were performed according to a previous report16. Lyophilized cells were extracted for total lipids with ethyl heptadecanoate as internal standard. FAEEs were separated by TLC Silica gel 60 F254 plates (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), scraped from the plate, and extracted with hexane/methanol/ddH₂O (3:2:2, v/v/v) via vortexing for 1 h. After centrifugation (1676 × g, 5 min), the organic phase was dried under nitrogen and reconstituted in methanol/chloroform (95:5, v/v) for GC-MS analysis. For extracellular FAEEs, 100 µL of the dodecane overlay phase was directly mixed with 900 µL of ethyl acetate containing 0.5 mg/mL ethyl heptadecanoate as internal standard, vortexed briefly, and analyzed by GC-MS. GC-MS analysis was performed on a SHIMADZU GC-MS-QP2010 PLUS system equipped with a 5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The injection volume was 1 μL with a split ratio of 10:1. The carrier gas was helium at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The temperature program was: initial 80 °C held for 0.5 min, ramped to 250 °C at 5 °C/min, and held for 5 min. The ion source temperature was 230 °C, and the interface temperature was 280 °C. FAEEs were identified by comparison with authentic standards and quantified based on the internal standard calibration curve16. The ester concentrations measured from both intracellular and extracellular fractions were summed and normalized to the total volume of the original fermentation broth (mg/L).

Analysis of FASBEs

FASBEs were detected both extracellularly and intracellularly. The preparation and detection of intracellular FASBEs were performed according to the method for the detection of intracellular FAEEs16. Extracellular FASBEs, which are primarily dissolved in the dodecane phase of the fermentation solution, isobutyl heptadecanoate was used as the internal standard. The organic phase was diluted and then detected by GC-MS using the method described in the analysis of FAEEs16. The ester concentrations measured from both intracellular and extracellular fractions were summed and normalized to the total volume of the original fermentation broth (mg/L).

Analysis of WEs

The extraction of WEs was performed as follows: 1 mL of fermentation broth was mixed with ethyl docosanate (internal standard), frozen at −80 °C for 30 min, and then subjected to freeze-drying for 15 h. After freeze-drying, an appropriate amount of glass beads and 3 mL of extraction solvent (chloroform: methanol = 2:1) were added, vortexed for 1–2 hour, followed by the addition of 500 μL deionized water. The mixture was shaken for 3 minutes, and then centrifuged at 1500 × g for 5 minutes to separate the lower layer into a new centrifuge tube. The collected lipid samples were vacuum-concentrated for 2 hours until the solvent was completely evaporated. Samples (1–1.5 μL increments) were loaded onto thin-layer chromatography (TLC) plates, air-dried between layers, and developed in n-hexane:ether:acetic acid (80:20:1).

Bands visualized with 0.05% 2,7-dichlorofluorescein (dissolved in ethanol) were scraped, extracted with n-hexane/methanol/water (3:2:2), for 1 hour and then centrifuged at 2000 × g for 5 min. The upper layer was transferred to a clean centrifuge tube and vacuum concentrated for 2 hours until the solvent was completely evaporated. Residue was reconstituted in 200 μL n-hexane, vortexed for 2 min, and analyzed by GC-MS described in analysis of FAEEs16. The results are similarly expressed as milligrams per liter of fermentation broth (mg/L).

Co-culture system of S. cerevisiae and R. toruloides

R. toruloides and S. cerevisiae were activated on YPD solid medium, with single colonies transferred to 3 mL of YPD liquid medium and cultured at 30 °C in a 200 rpm shaker until OD600 reached 2. Seed cultures were inoculated into 100 mL of YPD liquid medium (10% v/v) with co-culture ratios of R. toruloides: S. cerevisiae ranging from 1:5 to 100:1 in 33 g/L xylose and 66 g/L glucose. Each ratio included two parallel groups: one supplemented with dodecane for FAEEs extraction, and another without dodecane for monitoring glucose, xylose, and ethanol levels. The culture was placed at 30 °C in a 200 rpm shaker, and the contents of glucose, xylose and ethanol were measured periodically during the fermentation process.

To address the inadequate anaerobic conditions for ethanol production in S. cerevisiae, a two-compartment co-culture system was developed, separating S. cerevisiae (anaerobic flask) and R. toruloides (aerobic flask) in YPD + X medium (66 g/L glucose and 33 g/L xylose). Dodecane (10% v/v) was added for FAEEs extraction, while pH and dissolved oxygen were monitored in real time at 30 °C. Samples collected every 2 h were analyzed for OD600, glucose, ethanol, and FAEEs (via GC-MS). Glucose was replenished to 40 g/L when residual levels dropped below 5 g/L, optimizing ethanol synthesis and FAEEs production.

Determination of MTSs

The complete sequences of mitochondrial proteins of S. cerevisiae were retrieved and confirmed through the S. cerevisiae database (https://www.yeastgenome.org/). To identify the corresponding protein sequences in R. toruloides, the protein BLAST tool on the website (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) was employed to search for homologs. The homologous proteins between the two yeast species were subsequently aligned and compared using the ESPript web server (https://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/cgi-bin/ESPript.cgi), and the alignment results are shown in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2. Thus, mitochondrial-localized proteins of S. cerevisiae were cross-referenced with the R. toruloides proteome database to select candidate MTSs (CAT2m, CYB2m, GRX2m, PDB1m from S. cerevisiae and ATP1m, CoxIVm, MAM33m, PDA1m, SOD2m from R. toruloides). These sequences were fused to the N-terminus of superfolder GFP (sfGFP) via plasmid constructs (pKC-ScCAT2m-sfGFP, pKC-ScCYB2m-sfGFP, pKC-ScGRX2m-sgGFP, pKC-ScPDB1m-sfGFP, pKC-RtATP1m-sfGFP, pKC-RtCoxIVm-sfGFP, pKC-RtMAM33m-sfGFP, pKC-RtPDA1m-sfGFP, and pKC-RtSOD2m-sfGFP), and transformed into R. toruloides (Supplementary Data 1 and 4). Transformants were cultivated in YPD medium, stained with 1 μM MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Beyotime, China) for 30 min, and resuspended in fresh YPD medium prior to imaging.
