Genetic labeling of the nucleus of tractus solitarius neurons associated with electrical stimulation of the cervical or auricular vagus nerve in mice
Authors: Md Sams Sazzad Ali, Ghazaal Parastooei, Swarnalakshmi Raman, Jalen Mack, Yu Shin Kim, Man-Kyo Chung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) is clinically useful for treating epilepsy, depression, and chronic pain. Currently, cervical VNS (cVNS) treatment is well-established, while auricular VNS (aVNS) is under development. Vagal stimulation regulates functions in diverse brain regions; therefore, it is critical to better understand how electrically-evoked vagal inputs following cVNS and aVNS engage with different brain regions.Objective:As vagus inputs are predominantly transmitted to the nucleus of tractus solitarius (NTS), we directly compared the activation of NTS neurons by cVNS or aVNS and the brain regions directly projected by the activated NTS neurons in mice.Methods:We adopted the targeted recombination in active populations method, which allows for the activity-dependent, tamoxifen-inducible expression of mCherry—a reporter protein—in neurons specifically associated with cVNS or aVNS.Results:cVNS and aVNS induced comparable bilateral mCherry expressions in neurons within the NTS, especially in its caudal section (cNTS). However, the numbers of mCherry-expressing neurons within different subdivisions of cNTS was distinctive. In both cVNS and aVNS, anterogradely labeled mCherry-expressing axonal terminals were similarly observed across different areas of the forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain. These terminals were enriched in the rostral ventromedial medulla, parabrachial nucleus, periaqueductal gray, thalamic nuclei, central amygdala, and the hypothalamus. Sex difference of cVNS- and aVNS-induced labeling of NTS neurons was modest.Conclusion:The central projections of mCherry-expressing cNTS terminals are comparable between aVNS and cVNS, suggesting that cVNS and aVNS activate distinct but largely overlapping projections into the brain through the cNTS.Keywords: Cervical vagus nerve stimulation, Auricular vagus nerve stimulation, Targeted recombination in active populations, Nucleus tract solitarius, Mice

Introduction

Afferent sensory nerves are crucial for transmitting information from the body’s peripheral tissues to the central nervous system (CNS). Somatosensory nerves carry various types of sensory information, including innocuous or noxious mechanical, thermal, and chemical stimuli within tissues, contributing to exteroception. Sensory nerves are heterogeneous in respect to their sensitivity to specific stimuli. This heterogeneity arises from several factors, including anatomical location, neuronal structure, and protein expression [1–5]. The vagal system detects sensory stimuli from internal organs, contributing to interoception and the homeostatic control of organs [1,2,6,7]. Interoception is essential for cognitive and emotional processes such as memory, decision-making, consciousness, and a sense of self [8–10]. Abnormal or disturbed interoception is associated with anxiety, obsessive-compulsive behavior, and chronic pain [11,12].

The vagus nerve (VN), the tenth cranial nerve and most widely projected in the body [13], is the primary pathway for conveying interoceptive information to the brain [14]. It provides an extensive afferent and efferent network of innervation for the viscera and acts as the interface between higher CNS circuits and the autonomic control circuitry of the brain.

Given its physiological significance, the VN has been targeted for therapeutic neuromodulation. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved cervical VN stimulation (cVNS) for the treatment of epilepsy, depression, stroke rehabilitation, and cluster headache [15–17]. Currently, stimulation of the auricular branch of the VN (aVNS) is also being explored as a treatment for various pathophysiological conditions, including atrial fibrillation [18], chronic pain in fibromyalgia, and migraine headaches [19,20]. However, the mechanisms underlying how neuromodulation of the VN produces therapeutic effects are not entirely understood.

The anatomy of the VN and its projections into the CNS has primarily been investigated through tract tracing in rodents [21–25]. Interoceptive signals conveyed via the VN are mainly transmitted to the nucleus of tractus solitarius (NTS) [3], a large nuclear complex in the dorsal medulla of the brainstem. The VN’s afferent projections are integrated at the NTS, specifically in its caudal section (cNTS) [3,26,27], where second-order neurons further project to upper brainstem and forebrain [23,28–31]. Prominent targets include the ventral medulla [32], parabrachial nucleus [28], ventrolateral periaqueductal gray (PAG) matter [31], the hypothalamus [23,32], and the central nucleus of amygdala (CeA) [30,33]. The NTS is rostrocaudally divided into three levels (rostral, intermediate, and caudal) and contains multiple subregions beneath the area postrema. These subregions contain several subnuclei that integrate and transmit different information [28,34]. Gustatory afferents innervate taste-relay neurons in the rostral NTS [34,35], while vagal inputs from the lungs, esophagus, gut, and heart target different subregions of the cNTS [3,36–40]. Although VNS activates c-Fos within cNTS neurons [27,41,42], it is unclear whether cVNS or aVNS distinctly activates the cNTS and which brain regions are subsequently projected by the cNTS neurons, which can advance our understanding on the NTS-mediated regulatory effects of VNS on brain functions.

In this study, we determined the extent of activation of the different subregions of the cNTS following aVNS and cVNS. We compared the brain regions directly projected by the aVNS- or cVNS-activated cNTS neurons via the targeted recombination in active populations (TRAP) method in mice [43].

Materials and methods

Animals

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were performed under a University of Maryland Baltimore-approved Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocol. For measuring the effects of VNS on heart rate and blood oxygen concentration, we used twelve C57BL/6J mice. For a preliminary experiment to assess the expression of c-fos, we used eleven C57BL/6J mice (cVNS, 2M and 2F; aVNS, 2M and 2F; sham, 2M and 1F). For activity-dependent, tamoxifen-inducible targeting of VNS-associated neurons via the TRAP approach [43], we used nineteen Fostm2.1(icre/ERT2)Luo>/J (FosCreER) mice (The Jackson Laboratory; #. 030323) (cVNS, 4M and 3F; aVNS, 4M and 4F; sham, 2M and 2F). The mice were between 14 and 16-week old.

Adeno-associated virus stereotaxic microinjection

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) serotype 9 encoding the Cre-dependent, mCherry reporter protein driven by a human synapsin promoter (AAV-hSyn-DIO-mCherry; Addgene, #50459-AAV9) was bilaterally injected in FosCreER mice into the cNTS (anteroposterior −7.4 mm; lateromedial −1.15 to −1.3 mm; dorsoventral 4.2–4.22 mm) taking stereotaxic coordinate direction from the Mouse Brain Atlas [44]. 0.1 μl per side was injected at a rate of 50 nl/min.

Targeted recombination in active populations (TRAP) method

In FosCreER mice [43], tamoxifen (75–150 mg/kg) was injected intraperitoneally three weeks after AAV microinjection. After 24 h, cVNS or aVNS was delivered. In the sham group, tamoxifen was injected, but no electrical stimulation was delivered.

cVNS

Under isoflurane anesthesia, the left side of the cervical VN trunk was exposed, and the thyroid tissue along this line was gently dissected and reflected rostrally. The left carotid sheath was isolated from the connective tissue using modified, curved glass hook electrodes and gentle opening-closing movements parallel to the vessels. After identifying the nerve located directly behind or adjacent to the carotid artery, it was dissected circumferentially. Care was taken not to damage the vessels or tug on the nerve by placing a 3-0 silk suture under the nerve to bypass the bipolar cuff electrode. Once the nerve trunk rested on the surface of the electrode, the silk suture was removed. After two to 3 min, electrical stimulation of the VN was performed by attaching the bipolar platinum cuff electrode (MicoProbes, ME 14806) to modified, toothless, micro-alligator clips connected to a GRASS SD-9 electrical stimulator (Natus Medical) (Fig. 1A). The electrode was sterilized before use. To establish an optimal parameter for cVNS, we adopted parameters from previous studies [45,46] and conducted a pilot study to qualitatively assess several modified parameters to induce Fos expression from NTS. A constant voltage of 5 V, 10 Hz, and 500 μs was applied to the nerve for 10 min (Fig. 1B). In sham-stimulated animals, the cervical vagal trunk was exposed, isolated from the surrounding tissue, and the electrode was carefully placed under the nerve but not stimulated. Once the stimulation was completed, the incision wound was closed using a 3-0 silk suture.

aVNS

While the mice were under 2–3% isoflurane anesthesia, bipolar electrodes were attached using toothless micro-alligator clips connected to an electrical stimulation module (GRASS SD-9 electrical stimulator) or a commercially available TENS EM49 nerve stimulator (Beurer GmbH). Alligator clips were lightly attached to the left cymba and cavum conchae—the territory of the auricular branch of the VN (Fig. 1A). Under isoflurane anesthesia, alligator clips were lightly attached to the cymba and cavum conchae of the left side ear (Fig. 1A). To establish an optimal parameter for aVNS, we adopted a parameter from a previous study [46] and conducted a pilot study to qualitatively assess several modified parameters to induce Fos expression from NTS. Based on the pilot study, we selected an aVNS parameter (Fig. 1B) that produced a comparable extent of Fos induction to that induced by the cVNS parameter described above. For aVNS, the left auricular branch of the VN received constant stimuli of 5 V, 10 Hz, and a 500 μs pulse width for 10 min with the electrical stimulation module or biphasic electrical impulses of 2 mA, 15 Hz, 0.2 msec and a 250 μs pulse width with the commercial nerve stimulator (Fig. 1B). In sham-stimulated animals, alligator clips remained attached to the conchae, but current was not delivered.

Monitoring of vital signs

During VNS, heart rate and oxygen concentration were continuously monitored by a Mouse Stat Jr. pulse oximeter (Kent Scientific).

Immunohistochemical staining

For assessing c-fos expression, mice were transcardially perfused using 4 % paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) 2 h after VNS. Brains were removed and stored in 4 % paraformaldehyde overnight for fixation. After cryoprotection using a 30 % sucrose solution, cryosection was performed to make brain slices through the cNTS (anteroposterior: −7.24 to −7.79 mm from Bregma) at a 20 μm thickness. Immunohistochemical labeling was performed using a primary antibody (rabbit c-fos IgG, 1:1,000, #2250T; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) to label c-Fos and a secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit IgG conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488, 1:1000 dilution, #115-475-166; Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc., West Grove, PA). In the TRAP experiments, mice were euthanized ten days after VNS to allow for the expression and accumulation of mCherry in the NTS neurons. Free-floating, rostrocaudal coronal sections (20 μm) of whole brains were collected. Immunohistochemistry was performed using a primary antibody against red fluorescent protein (1:1000; rabbit IgG, # 600-401-379; Rockland Immunochemicals, Pottstown, PA) and a secondary antibody (1:1000; goat anti-rabbit Cy3 IgG, Jackson ImmunoReaearch #111-165-003). The sections were counterstained with Nissl.

Image acquisition and quantification

Samples were imaged on a ZEISS Axiovert 200M (ZEISS Group) or a confocal microscope (Nikon W1). All z-sections for each sample were projected into a single image for quantification. The brain regions of interest and subnuclei within NTS were identified based on Nissl staining and registered manually by M.A. based on visual similarity between the observed regions and the map at the corresponding rostrocaudal Bregma level in the Mouse Brain Atlas [44]. We investigated the cNTS from the caudal to bregma −7.24 to −7.79 mm. Ten brain sections best representing the serial rostrocaudal levels of cNTS in approximately 50 μm interval were selected in each brain for the quantification of mCherry-positive (mCherry+) neurons. The Obex (−7.3 mm caudal to bregma) landmark was used to distinguish between the rostral and caudal parts of the NTS, which helped us estimate the rostrocaudal distances of the cNTS. We drew the cytoarchitectural boundary for the cNTS subdivisions in serial rostrocaudal levels in Nissl-stained sections using the Mouse Brain Atlas [44] as a guide.

The mCherry+ cNTS neurons were manually counted from serial coronal sections using a counter in the ImageJ software (Windows 64; NIH, Bethesda, MD) to avoid repeating the same cell count. For quantification of mCherry+ nerve terminals, we used Z-stacked images, including the brain regions of interest. Using ImageJ, the images were converted to 8 bits by applying thresholds suitable to identify maximum nerve terminals, eliminating subthreshold background fluorescence. The supra-threshold area was then quantified. The area of brain region of interest was also measured, and the mCherry+ terminal/region of interest ratio was calculated in each image. Throughout the quantification, the experimenters were blinded to the VNS groups.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed in both sex-aggregated and disaggregated manners to determine overall characteristics in both sexes and to identify sex dependence. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism version 10 (Graphpad). Statistical comparisons were performed using Student’s t-tests or an analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a post hoc test as indicated in the figure legends. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

cVNS and aVNS similarly activate c-fos within the cNTS

To qualitatively investigate whether cVNS or aVNS induces c-fos expression within cNTS subdivisions, we delivered electrical stimulation to the VN in C57BL/6J mice under isoflurane anesthesia (Fig. 1A). For optimal labeling, we selected parameters modified from electrical stimulation parameters previously used in mice (Fig. 1B) [40,41]. Heart rate and blood oxygen concentration were monitored during the 10-min period of stimulation. Both cVNS and aVNS induced bradycardia (Fig. 1C). Heart rate reduction was more rapid and robust in cVNS. There were no changes in blood oxygen concentration (Fig. 1D).

Two hours after electrical stimulation, the mice were transcardially perfused, and the caudal hindbrain tissues were collected and prepared for the detection of c-fos expression within the cNTS. Both cVNS and aVNS induced intense expressions of c-fos within the cNTS (Fig. 2A–D). In the sham group, cNTS neurons expressing c-fos were rare (Fig. 2E and F). cVNS caused a small amount of c-fos expression within the trigeminal spinal interpolar and caudal nucleus (Vc) transition zone and in the Vc (data not shown). In contrast, aVNS did not cause c-fos expression in any trigeminal nucleus complex, including the Vc (Fig. 2G). These data suggest the possible application of the TRAP approach to genetically label VNS-associated neurons in the NTS.

cVNS and aVNS TRAP neurons in distinct subdivisions of the cNTS

To confine the Fos-TRAP approach within the NTS but not other areas of the brain, the AAV9-hSyn-DIO-mCherry was injected into the cNTS in FosCreER mice (Fig. 3A). After tamoxifen injection, aVNS or cVNS was performed. The cNTS with both cVNS and aVNS showed robust mCherry expression, and the mCherry signals overlapped with Nissl (Fig. 3B–D). mCherry expression was observed bilaterally even though the VNS was applied unilaterally to the left side (Fig. 3B and C, left panel). In contrast, the sham group did not express mCherry in the cNTS (Fig. 3B and C, right panel).

We counted the mCherry+ neurons within the cNTS rostrocaudally from −7.24 mm to −7.79 mm to Bregma (Fig. 4A and D). The total number of mCherry+ neurons throughout the cNTS rostrocaudal regions was comparable between cVNS and aVNS in sex-aggregated analysis. However, female mice showed a slight but significantly lower number of labeling in aVNS, whereas male mice showed no significant difference (Fig. 4B). Despite unilateral VNS, labeling was observed bilateral NTS. In both cVNS and aVNS, the ipsilateral side showed greater labeling than the contralateral side (Fig. 4C). In sex-aggregated analysis of the rostrocaudal distributions, the greatest number of mCherry+ neurons were observed from −7.4 mm to Bregma from both VNS approaches, which decreased in the rostral and caudal directions. However, this occurred more abruptly toward the caudal direction (Fig. 4D). The rostrocaudal distribution of the mCherry+ neurons within the cNTS was comparable between cVNS and aVNS mice, except at −7.48, −7.55, −7.6, and −7.66 mm to Bregma, where mCherry+ neurons were significantly higher in cVNS than aVNS mice (Fig. 4D). In males, cVNS labeled a significantly greater number of neurons only at −7.48 mm, whereas in females, these were greater than aVNS in −7.48, −7.6, and −7.72 mm to Bregma (Fig. 4E and F).

We also quantified the mCherry+ neurons within different subdivisions of the cNTS (Fig. 5A). mCherry+ neurons in different subdivisions and different rostrocaudal layers were counted (Supplemental Fig. 1). Then, all the labeled neurons in the given subnucleus along the different rostrocaudal layers were added in each mouse. Despite comparable total numbers of mCherry+ neurons within the entire cNTS for cVNS and aVNS mice, the labeling within the subdivisions was significantly different (Fig. 5B). The dorsolateral part of the solitary nucleus (SolDL) showed the greatest labeling with cVNS, whereas the dorsomedial part (SolDM) showed the most labeling with aVNS. Moreover, labeling in the SolDM and SolDL showed significant differences between the two VNS groups, which were not due to the different areas between the groups. For example, the anatomical areas of SolDM were not significantly different between cVNS and aVNS groups, while the densities of mCherry+ neurons in the unit area of SolDM were significantly greater in aVNS than in cVNS mice (Supplemental Fig. 2). Labeling in the parasolitary nucleus (PSol) and solitary nucleus ventrolateral part (SolVL) was significantly higher in cVNS than aVNS mice, whereas the solitary nucleus medial part (SolM) and the central part (SolCe) showed no significant differences. Labeling in the solitary nucleus commissural part (SolC) was also greater in cVNS than in aVNS (Fig. 5B). In a sex-disaggregated analysis, the pattern was identical to the aggregated analysis except for the lack of significant differences in SolC in males and females (Fig. 5C and D).

VNS-associated mCherry+ neurons were highly diverse in size and shape (Fig. 6A and B). Neurons were either bipolar or multipolar; some were as small as 25 μm2, whereas others were greater than 100 μm2. The size distribution of mCherry+ neurons was distinct between the medial and lateral parts of the cNTS such that the lateral subnuclei (such as Psol or SolVL) contained significantly larger neurons than the medial subnuclei (Fig. 6C). No difference was found between cVNS and aVNS. In the sex-disaggregated analysis (Fig. 6D and E), mCherry+ neurons in the lateral subnuclei were greater than the medial subnuclei in both male and female cVNS and aVNS mice.

Anterograde projections of the axonal terminals of TRAPped cNTS neurons were comparable

Within the hindbrain of cVNS mice (Fig. 7A–E), mCherry-labeled axonal terminals were observed in different regions, such as the rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM), parvicellular reticular formation (PCRt), Vc, medullary reticular formation (MRt), Kölliker-Fuse nucleus (KF), medial parabrachial nucleus (MPB), the lateral parabrachial nucleus (LPB), and locus coeruleus (LC). LPB was labeled the most densely, whereas the other regions were labeled relatively more weakly (Fig. 7A–D). In the aVNS mice (Fig. 7A’–E′), the mCherry+ axonal terminals were comparable with cVNS mice. Labeling of these regions was bilateral. The density of mCherry+ axons in RVM and LPB was not significantly different between cVNS and aVNS mice (Fig. 7F and G).

A substantial number of mCherry+ axonal terminals were observed within different subdivisions of the PAG matter and midbrain reticular formation in cVNS and aVNS mice (Fig. 8A–C). Terminals were abundant in the lateral and ventral subdivisions of the PAG rather than its dorsal part. In both cVNS and aVNS mice, a considerable number of mCherry+ terminals were also observed in the dorsal and lateral subdivisions of the dorsal raphe nucleus (DR). The mCherry+ terminals in the dorsolateral PAG were greater in cVNS than in aVNS mice (Fig. 8D).

In the forebrains of mice with aVNS, we did not observe mCherry+ terminals in the insular cortex or other cortical areas. As with cVNS, labeling was observed in the dorsal thalamus of the VPM, VPL, VM, CM, PC, MD, PV, and the parvicellular part of the VPM (VPPC) (Fig. 9A1–B1). Compared to cVNS mice, condensed labeling was observed in the VPM, VPPC, PC, MD, and PV areas rather than the VPL (Fig. 9A1–B1). Overall, the labeling in the thalamic nuclei appeared comparable between cVNS and aVNS, as exemplified in PV (Fig. 9E). mCherry-labeled axonal terminals were also observed in the CeA and hypothalamic area (Fig. 9C1–D1), mirroring what was found in cVNS mice. Within the CeA, mCherry+ terminals were enriched in the CeM, while relatively weaker labeling was found in the CeL. We did not observe any labeled terminals in the CeC and BLA (Fig. 9C1). In the hypothalamus, mCherry+ terminals were observed in the LH, VMH, and MTu, while less labeling was seen in the DMH (Fig. 9D1). Terminal densities within the CeA or LH were not significantly different between cVNS and aVNS (Fig. 9F and G). There was no significant difference among groups when we performed a sex-disaggregated analysis of the mCherry+ axonal density in RVM, LPB, PAG, PV, CeA, and LH (Supplemental Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, we compared central connections via the cNTS associated with two different methods of VNS—cVNS and aVNS. As TRAP was limited within the cNTS through viral injection, mCherry-expressing nerve terminals in different brain regions indicated a direct anterograde projection of cNTS neurons associated with cVNS and aVNS. Our data suggest that aVNS produces activation of cNTS neurons and their projections into the brain that are comparable to those produced by cVNS. Male and female mice also showed comparable neuronal labeling, except that aVNS produced modestly lower numbers of neuronal labeling in the caudal part of cNTS. This is surprising since aVNS stimulates only a subset of auricular branch of vagal afferents, whereas cVNS stimulates much greater vagal afferents inputs. Given the comparable variations of data and the qualitative differences in labelings of NTS subregions between cVNS and aVNS, we suppose that the protocols of aVNS and cVNS produced comparable extent of neuronal activations in plateau and that the overall comparable extent of labeling might not be due to the suboptimal parameter of cVNS.

Despite the comparable extent of NTS activation, distinct subregions of the cNTS were associated with either cVNS or aVNS. The roles these subregions play in interoception and homeostatic controls are not clearly understood; therefore, it is difficult to presume the therapeutic implications of differential activation of cNTS subregions by aVNS or cVNS. Recent studies have suggested that these subregions contribute to processing interoceptive inputs from different organs. For example, stomach stretch-responsive neurons are clustered in the cNTS region bordered ventrolaterally. In contrast, neurons responsive to duodenum stretch are situated more ventromedially—near the border of the cNTS and the area postrema [47]. Different subregions of the cNTS are also neurochemically distinct and enriched with various neuropeptides, neurotransmitters, and receptors [48]. cNTS neurons in different subregions exhibit distinct firing patterns such that the dorsomedial part of the cNTS predominantly displays phasic firings compared to other subregions [49]. Therefore, aVNS and cVNS may produce different physiological and therapeutic outcomes through the activation of different subregions of the cNTS, which needs further detailed studies to elucidate.

cNTS neurons are functionally diverse. Some receive synaptic input directly from the VN and are involved in controlling autonomic reflexes by projecting to the ventral medulla, a critical region for autonomic control [32]. NTS neurons integrate vagal inputs with descending information from the forebrain [50]. Medial and lateral cNTS subdivisions receive input from a similar distribution of retrogradely labeled central neurons. However, they receive diverse regulatory inputs from different regions, such that afferent neurons in the hypothalamus are excitatory (glutamatergic), while those in the amygdala are inhibitory (GABAergic) [26]. Other NTS neurons receive most of their neural inputs from different brain regions, with little to no direct input from the periphery [32,51–53]. The NTS also contains a diverse array of interneurons that provide highly complex regulations [51,54]. Some large-sized cNTS neurons often receive inputs primarily from interneurons with little or no direct vagal input [32]. However, despite this, these neurons process intricate information and send their axons to the rostral brain regions, including the PVH [32]. We found that mCherry+ neurons associated with the VNS were highly varied in neuronal size and morphology and likely included all types of cNTS neurons. Interestingly, larger-sized neurons were enriched in the lateral subdivision of the cNTS, which was activated more by cVNS than aVNS. Further investigations of the physiological and therapeutic consequences following the activation of distinct cNTS neurons by cVNS and aVNS are warranted.

Axonal tracing studies by intraganglionic injections of viral tracers to the nodose or jugular ganglia [22,25] or injecting retrograde or antegrade tracers within different levels of the NTS have resulted in projections from the periphery to the NTS or from the NTS to the upper brainstem and forebrain, such as the ventral medulla [32], parabrachial nucleus [28], ventrolateral PAG matter [31], the hypothalamus [23,32], and the CeA [30,33]. Our Fos-TRAP approach demonstrated that regions projected by cNTS neurons associated with the VNS are consistent with these reports. There is evidence of overlap between brain regions influenced by vagal stimulation and those implicated in pain transmission and emotional regulation [28,55–58]. Brain imaging studies in humans and rodents have indicated that VNS induces widespread changes in multiple cortical and subcortical brain regions. Preclinical studies have shown that NTS neurons are directly and indirectly connected to the LC and DR (centers for noradrenaline and serotonin release, respectively), which modulate corticolimbic regions involved in mood regulation, such as the amygdala and hippocampus [59]. These neural connections might contribute to the beneficial effects of VNS on mood and cognitive disorders.

Interestingly, these regions are also implicated in nociception [60–64]. Considering the high comorbidity of chronic pain with anxiety or depression [65], this overlap is unsurprising. The LPB plays a critical role in affective pain pathways by relaying nociceptive inputs to the thalamus and limbic regions [66]. The amygdala plays a role in emotional-affective behaviors and pain modulation [67]. The PAG and RVM, including the raphe magnus and LC, are well-known regulators of nociception in the spinal cord [68,69]. In patients with different types of chronic pain, such as temporomandibular disorders, fibromyalgia, or irritable bowel syndrome, maladaptive changes in descending pain modulation and affective pain pathways occur [70]. Antidepressants such as serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors are widely used to manage chronic pain in these patients [71]. Therefore, it is possible that aVNS and cVNS induce attenuation of chronic pain and antidepressant effects through common neural mechanisms, and that interoceptive inputs intersect with affective pain transmission pathways and modulation to produce analgesia.

Our study has some limitations. In our approach, cVNS directly activated vagal afferents and efferents, whereas aVNS only activates vagal afferents. Therefore, we were unable to control the potential indirect effects of efferent activities on NTS neuronal labeling. However, we did not consider surgical denervation of the vagal nerve as nerve resection activates vagal afferents to induce Fos expression in NTS, which leads to the genetic labeling of NTS neurons without VNS. This would have directly confounded the interpretation of the results. Furthermore, nerve injury may produce plastic changes along the peripheral and central vagal pathways [72], which is well established in the somatosensory system [73]. Nevertheless, simultaneous activation of vagal afferents and efferents could have affected NTS neuronal labeling. Despite the similarity of NTS projections between rodents and humans [74], direct translation of our data from mice to humans is challenging. For example, aVNS modestly reduced heart rate in mice, consistent with previous studies [75,76]. This effect may be mediated by the integration of NTS with autonomic efferent centers, such as dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus, which drives parasympathetic tone [77,78]. In contrast, aVNS does not appear to change heart rate in humans [79], suggesting that the mechanisms or extent of brain integration of NTS inputs and its efferent regulations might be different between mice and humans. The physiological significance of our anatomical data in vagal modulation therapy also needs to be further elucidated.

In conclusion, our results suggest that cVNS and aVNS activate distinct but largely overlapping projections onto the brain through the cNTS and that aVNS may be an effective method to modulate broad central circuits comparable to the cVNS.
