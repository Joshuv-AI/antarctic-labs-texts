Bioenergetics of the combat sports brain: Between risk and resilience
Authors: Damian M Bailey, Benjamin S Stacey, Kevin Cox, Lewis Fall, Josip Butkovic, Zvonimir Vrselja, Yaniv Assaf, David C Poole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Combat sports provide a unique human model in which cerebral ischaemia-reperfusion stress, adaptive neuroprotection, and impact-induced neurodegenerative risk coexist. Across striking and grappling disciplines, exercise-induced metabolic and redox stress, repetitive head impacts and transient cerebral ischaemia–reperfusion during vascular neck restraints expose the brain to competing adaptive and injurious stimuli. Cerebral ischaemic preconditioning (cIPC) research demonstrates that brief, sublethal reductions in cerebral blood flow (CBF) activate a conserved hormetic programme involving modulation of oxidative–inflammatory–nitrosative stress (OXINOS), reduced glutamate excitotoxicity, mitochondrial stabilisation, anti-apoptotic and autophagic pathways, and metabolic reprogramming. These responses preserve glucose-and lactate-dependent bioenergetics, neurovascular unit integrity, and cognitive function. Repetitive sportive strangulations may engage cIPC-like mechanisms, potentially explaining elevated basal CBF reported in elite Brazilian jiu-jitsu athletes (~500 pre-syncopal exposures per year). In contrast, repetitive impacts and rotational shear in boxing and mixed martial arts initiate neurometabolic cascades marked by axonal injury, exaggerated OXINOS, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neurovascular disruption, promoting tau pathology associated with chronic traumatic encephalopathy. By integrating cerebral bioenergetics, hormesis, ischaemic tolerance and traumatic brain injury, this review positions combat sports as a translational model for defining cerebral resilience and the balance between adaptive neuroprotection and cumulative neurological risk.

Background and conceptual framework

Cerebral bioenergetic vulnerability and evolutionary constraint

The human brain is among the most oxygen (O2)-sensitive organs in the body, reflecting both its extraordinary computational capacity and narrow bioenergetic margin.1,2 Although accounting for only ~2% of body mass, it consumes ~25% of the body’s basal oxygen (O2) budget, 3 ten times that predicted by its mass alone 4 (Figure 1). Neural tissue is exceptionally costly to maintain, up to ~16-fold more metabolically demanding than resting skeletal muscle and other somatic tissues, 5 because continuous ATP turnover (~8.3 kg per day equivalent to six times the brain’s mass), 6 is required to preserve ionic gradients, recycle neurotransmitters, and sustain high-frequency synaptic signalling.7–9 This extraordinary bioenergetic burden is evident at the cellular level: at rest, each cortical neuron consumes an estimated ~4.7 billion ATP molecules per second, while cortical grey and white matter together turn over the energetic equivalent of ~56 g glucose per day. 10

These demands are met almost exclusively through oxidative phosphorylation. The brain stores negligible glucose and only limited glycogen, although lactate provides an important supplementary fuel during increased metabolic demand.14,15 This dependence, together with a cerebral microvascular architecture optimised for O2 supply rather than reserve, renders the brain exquisitely vulnerable to anoxia and ischaemia.1,11,16 Consequently, even brief interruptions in cerebral blood flow (CBF) arising from mechanical trauma or vascular neck compression can rapidly precipitate bioenergetic failure and neuronal dysfunction 17 (Figure 2). This vulnerability contributes to the pathophysiology of ischaemic stroke, global hypoperfusion and traumatic brain injury (TBI), underscoring the importance of endogenous neuroprotective mechanisms.

Combat sports as a physiological continuum of stress: Balancing resilience and susceptibility

This review examines how fundamental cerebral bioenergetic constraints intersect with the distinctive physiological stresses imposed by combat sports, which are unified by physical confrontation but differ in the type, magnitude and cumulative burden of exposure. We consider four interrelated stress domains: exercise-induced metabolic and redox stress, which may promote hormetic adaptation; transient cerebrovascular and ischaemia–reperfusion (IR) stress during vascular neck restraints, which may engage cerebral ischaemic preconditioning (cIPC)-like pathways; biomechanical stress arising from repetitive head impacts and cervical loading, which may cause acute and cumulative injury; and systemic stressors, including rapid weight loss, hypohydration, musculoskeletal injury and inflammation, which may further shape neurological outcome. These stressors are distributed differently across striking disciplines (e.g. boxing, kickboxing, karate and taekwondo), grappling disciplines incorporating vascular neck restraints (e.g. Brazilian jiu-jitsu (BJJ), judo and wrestling) and mixed martial arts (MMA), which combines striking and grappling.

Across this spectrum, the brain is exposed to differing combinations of metabolic, cerebrovascular, biomechanical and systemic stress, which may favour either adaptive neuroprotection or cumulative injury. Striking-based sports exemplify repetitive mechanical trauma associated with neurovascular unit (NVU) disruption, pathological tau accumulation and progressive cognitive decline, 18 whereas grappling disciplines largely minimise head impact but impose intermittent, sublethal cerebral IR during controlled vascular compression, which may engage cIPC-like neuroprotective pathways.19,20 The following section clarifies the terminology surrounding vascular neck restraints and introduces the concepts underpinning this physiological continuum.

Definitions and conceptual clarity

Historically, these techniques have been referred to in the literature as chokes; however, this terminology is anatomically imprecise and can be misleading. 21 As highlighted by Stellpflug 21 in correspondence addressing terminology used in the combat-sports literature, 22 the term choke is widely used by athletes and practitioners despite the underlying physiological mechanism being predominantly vascular rather than respiratory. Whereas asphyxia generally denotes O₂ deprivation from airway obstruction, these techniques primarily restrict carotid and vertebral arterial inflow and venous outflow and are therefore more accurately termed vascular compression or strangulation. Airway compression may occasionally occur and lead to submission, but is mechanistically distinct from the vascular phenomena considered herein.

Central to this review are the concepts of hormesis, ischaemic tolerance, resilience and susceptibility. Hormesis describes a biphasic adaptive response whereby low-level or sublethal stress promotes compensatory biological adaptation, whereas excessive stress becomes maladaptive. 23 Ischaemic tolerance represents an inducible form of endogenous neuroprotection in which prior exposure to a brief, non-injurious ischaemic stimulus increases resistance to a subsequent injurious insult. 24 Resilience, in the context of combat sports, refers to the capacity of the brain to preserve, recover or adapt neurovascular, metabolic and cognitive function following repeated biomechanical and/or metabolic challenges. 25 Susceptibility denotes the converse state, in which the magnitude, frequency or cumulative burden of stress exceeds adaptive capacity and injurious processes predominate. Together, these concepts explain how the dose and pattern of repeated stress may drive either adaptive neuroprotection or progressive neurodegeneration.

Accordingly, this review integrates cerebral bioenergetics, exercise physiology and neurotrauma to examine how the combat-sport brain responds to exercise-induced redox and metabolic stress, transient cerebrovascular IR during vascular neck restraints and injurious biomechanical loading. In doing so, it positions combat-sport athletes as a unique human model for defining the physiological boundary between adaptation and cumulative neurological injury.

Exercise-induced hormesis and adaptive neuroprotection

Exercise as a prototypical hormetic stimulus

Regular moderate-to-intense physical activity remains a powerful non-pharmacological intervention for preserving brain health across the lifespan, attenuating cognitive decline and reducing the risk of stroke and dementia.26–29 Higher cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) is consistently associated with superior cognitive performance across multiple domains. 30 Longitudinal cohorts report up to a 40% reduction in dementia incidence among individuals who maintain or improve CRF across adulthood. 31

These clinical benefits reflect the deeply conserved biological principle of hormesis whereby transient, sublethal stress induces durable adaptive neuroprotection. 32 Exercise represents a prototypical hormetic stimulus, with tightly regulated redox signalling central to the adaptive process. 33 Each acute bout elicits a controlled elevation in reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS) derived from mitochondrial respiration, redox-active enzymes and immune-related sources 34 (Figure 3). Concomitant increases in CBF and metabolic substrate delivery help preserve neuronal ATP homeostasis, while repeated exposure enhances cerebrovascular function, metabolic flexibility and tolerance to subsequent physiological stressors.

Redox signalling as a driver of neuroprotection

At physiological, albeit presently undefined levels, these redox transients function not solely as toxic, indiscriminate agents of damage but as neuroprotective intra- and extracellular signals that activate cytoprotective pathways supporting cellular O2 homeostasis.39,40 These pathways encompass redox-sensitive stress-response networks, metabolic and mitochondrial regulators, transcriptional and epigenetic programmes, neurotrophic and synaptic mediators, endothelial and vascular signalling pathways and immune–inflammatory modulators33,35 (Figure 3). They also enhance glucose and lactate utilisation and, with repeated exposure, drive haemodynamic, structural and functional adaptations that preserve cerebral bioenergetics, slow cognitive ageing and reduce neurological vulnerability 27 (Figure 3).

Shared mechanisms between grappling sports and cIPC

The physiological responses elicited by grappling-based vascular neck restraints share several features with experimentally induced cIPC, including transient reductions in CBF, reversible cerebral IR, redox-sensitive signalling and neurovascular adaptation. These parallels provide a framework for exploring potential adaptation without implying physiological equivalence. First described in the myocardium 41 and subsequently extended to the brain,42,43 both direct cerebral and remote ischaemic preconditioning confer robust neuroprotection.19,44 Brief, reversible reductions in CBF, characterised by intermittent sinusoidal cerebral IR, generate more pronounced yet physiologically adaptive ROS/RNS transients 45 that intensify many of the same neuroprotective signalling cascades engaged by exercise 46 (Figure 4). These include preservation of mitochondrial function, improved metabolic flexibility, enhanced utilisation of glucose and lactate, and reinforcement of NVU integrity, all of which have been implicated in increased tolerance to subsequent metabolic stress following experimental cIPC. Emerging evidence suggests that this metabolic adaptation extends beyond glucose utilisation alone. Experimental cIPC upregulates astrocytic CD147 via P2X7 receptor/HIF-1α-dependent signalling, promoting membrane translocation of the monocarboxylate transporters MCT1 and MCT4 and increasing extracellular lactate availability during subsequent ischaemic stress. 47 This enhanced astrocyte-to-neuron lactate shuttling may provide an alternative oxidative substrate for ATP generation, supporting mitochondrial function and cerebral ischaemic tolerance.

Strangulation associated with BJJ, judo, wrestling and MMA transiently restricts carotid and vertebral arterial inflow, 53 resulting in short-lived reductions in global CBF with concomitant decreases in O2 and glucose delivery, followed by rapid reperfusion that may exceed basal levels 48 (Figure 4). Given the growing recognition that lactate serves as both a metabolic substrate and signalling molecule within the brain,14,15,47 repeated transient reductions in cerebral perfusion may also challenge and potentially condition pathways involved in glucose–lactate metabolic coupling. In practice, athletes typically submit by ‘tapping out’ before loss of consciousness occurs, although sustained near-complete vascular compression for ~6–10 s can induce brief, fully reversible syncope. 17 Full syncope reflects a more severe acute event, characterised by loss of consciousness and more marked interruption of cerebral O2 and substrate delivery, and may therefore more closely resemble experimental cIPC paradigms involving near-complete interruption of perfusion. By contrast, the more common pre-syncopal exposures may provide a lower intensity, frequently repeated cerebrovascular and bioenergetic stimulus. The cerebral bioenergetic effects, dose–response relationships and long-term cerebrovascular consequences of these exposures remain poorly defined.

Even when terminated before syncope, the associated bioenergetic challenge may engage some of the conserved redox-sensitive and hormetic signalling pathways that have been identified in experimental cIPC models. 46 These pathways span humoral, neural and systemic mechanisms,49,50 collectively increasing tolerance to subsequent cerebral IR through preservation of NVU integrity and cerebral bioenergetic function (Figure 4). Although these findings do not provide direct evidence of cIPC, recent clinical observations in grappling athletes indicate that repeated sportive vascular neck restraints are not associated with increased carotid intima-media thickness or elevations in circulating biomarkers of NVU injury, suggesting no detectable chronic carotid structural damage or acute biochemical evidence of brain injury in trained practitioners. 54

The prevalence of controlled strangulation in BJJ is high, with elite athletes reporting 3593 ± 5244 pre-syncopal strangulations over an eight-year competitive career, equating to ~500 strangulations per year. 20 These manoeuvres occur routinely during training under both minimal and maximal resistance, involving active contraction of the cervical muscles, including the sternocleidomastoid, trapezius and scalenes. Over years of training, these recurrent, intermittent ischaemic exposures may share certain temporal characteristics with experimental cIPC paradigms and offer a biologically plausible explanation for the elevated basal global CBF and intact cognitive function recently reported in BJJ athletes relative to age-, sex- and CRF-matched non-grappling controls 20 (Figure 4). However, direct evidence that repeated sportive strangulation induces cIPC-like neuroprotection in humans remains lacking, and the dose–response relationships and long-term cerebrovascular consequences of repeated pre-syncopal and syncopal exposures remain to be established.

Collectively, controlled vascular neck restraints may be compatible with adaptive cerebrovascular responses, but whether these represent true cIPC or a related physiological phenomenon remains unknown. Exercise and cIPC may nevertheless converge on regulated redox signalling, mitochondrial stability, NVU remodelling and glucose–lactate flexibility, illustrating how repeated bioenergetic stress could promote neuroprotection.

Mechanical injury and sports-related mild traumatic brain injury

Cervical spine and vascular injury: Rare but clinically consequential

While exercise and intermittent ischaemic stress may promote neuroprotective adaptation, the combat-sport environment also exposes the brain and cervical vasculature to mechanical forces capable of precipitating both acute and cumulative injury to the cervical spine 55 and arterial vasculature. Although rare, cervical arterial dissections have been reported across multiple combat sports, most commonly involving the vertebral artery in disciplines characterised by high-velocity head rotation or cervical loading, including MMA,56,57 karate, 58 wrestling 59 and judo, 60 with isolated cases described in BJJ. 61 Carotid artery dissections have also been documented, predominantly in striking-based disciplines such as taekwondo, 62 karate 63 and MMA, 64 where direct blows to the neck or rapid hyperextension-rotation may impose blunt trauma and/or focal tensile stress on the extracranial carotid artery. Transient carotid compression may also create a point of vascular fixation, such that subsequent neck hyperextension with contralateral rotation generates sufficient traction to disrupt the intimal layer distal to skull-base entry. Although uncommon, these potentially life-threatening events underscore the importance of considering cervical arterial dissection in symptomatic athletes presenting with headache, neck pain, cranial nerve palsy, Horner’s syndrome, vertigo, ataxia or focal neurological deficits following cervical trauma or rotational loading and highlight the need for heightened clinical vigilance.65,66

Neurometabolic cascade of repetitive head impacts

More commonly, repetitive head impacts characteristic of boxing, kickboxing, Muay Thai, karate, taekwondo and MMA perturb ionic homeostasis causing pathological elevations in intra-axonal calcium that precede calpain-mediated proteolysis of mechanosensitive cytoskeletal elements and may culminate in irreversible axonal pathology.67,68 Recent advances in instrumented mouthguards, among the most accurate wearable technologies for quantifying head acceleration events (HAEs), have provided objective evidence of the substantial biomechanical loads experienced during combat sports. 69 Jansen et al. 70 recorded 896 video-verified impacts in amateur boxing and MMA, reporting peak linear (PLA) and angular (PAA) accelerations of up to ~65 g and 6912 rad/s, respectively, with MMA associated with greater angular loading and distinct impact-location profiles compared with boxing. Similarly, Tiernan et al. 71 reported PLA and PAA of ~87 g and ~7560 rad/s during concussive MMA impacts. Doan et al. 72 reported mean PLA and PAA of ~23 g and ~1761 rad/s, respectively, across only two rounds of intercollegiate boxing sparring, highlighting the cumulative nature of head-impact exposure even during short, controlled bouts. These accelerations overlap with those documented in rugby 73 and American football, 74 indicating that combat sports expose athletes to both substantial single-impact magnitudes and a high cumulative burden of HAEs. Importantly, a study combining mouthguard-derived kinematics reporting PLA and PAA of 65 g and 6545 rad/s, with blood-borne biomarkers, including neurofilament light and glial fibrillary acidic protein, suggests that biomechanical loading may differentially influence neuroaxonal and astroglial responses. 75 Together, these findings strengthen the link between repetitive combat-sport head loading, downstream neurometabolic stress and cumulative neurological risk.

These mechanical insults initiate a characteristic neurometabolic cascade marked by spreading depolarisation, an early and self-propagating wave of near-complete neuronal depolarisation associated with collapse of transmembrane ion gradients, elevated mitochondrial ROS and markedly increased metabolic demand, excessive glutamate release, sustained calcium influx, mitochondrial dysfunction and acute bioenergetic failure76,77 (Figure 5). Amplified OXINOS promotes structural damage to cell membranes, cytoskeletal scaffolds, mitochondria and nucleic acids, while concurrent disruption of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) facilitates peripheral immune infiltration and sustained microglial and astrocytic activation (Figure 5). Spreading depolarisation is increasingly recognised as a key mediator linking traumatic injury to neurovascular dysfunction, BBB disruption and adverse neurological outcome, particularly when recurrent or superimposed upon metabolically compromised tissue.18,78 Progressive neuroinflammation may subsequently contribute to demyelination, synaptic dysfunction and chronic neurodegenerative processes that extend well beyond the initial injury event. 79

Neurological outcome is not determined solely by injury burden but also by the capacity of endogenous protective mechanisms to preserve cerebral homeostasis. Preservation of mitochondrial function, efficient redox buffering, maintenance of the neurovasculome and composite NVU integrity, and sustained cerebral substrate delivery may help limit secondary injury propagation and promote recovery.77,88 Failure of these defences shifts the balance towards exaggerated and sustained OXINOS, bioenergetic failure, BBB dysfunction, and progressive neurodegeneration.

Biomarkers and clinical stratification of sports-related mild TBI

Collectively, these primary and secondary injury processes converge on exaggerated OXINOS, a unifying downstream phenotype increasingly implicated in long-term neurodegenerative risk following sports-related mild TBI89,90 (Figure 5). Accordingly, current research seeks increasingly sensitive and specific fluid biomarkers to better stratify TBI, detect secondary injury, predict outcome and inform return-to-play decisions.81,91–93 Particular attention has focused on biomarkers reflecting axonal injury, astroglial activation, BBB disruption and neurodegeneration, with the ultimate goal of improving individualised risk assessment and clinical management following repetitive head-impact exposure. 91 Although the mechanistic link between acute TBI and chronic traumatic encephalopathy remains unresolved, the focal accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau within perivascular and subcortical neurofibrillary tangles, often adjacent to axonal injury and microhaemorrhage, suggests that axonal disruption, microvascular injury, BBB breach and sustained neuroinflammation create a permissive milieu for tauopathy and progressive neurodegeneration.78,94

Complementary neuroimaging studies provide evidence linking repetitive head-impact exposure to structural, functional and cerebrovascular alterations. Although generally limited to small combat-sport cohorts, particularly boxers,95–99 these studies have identified several recurring structural and functional alterations. Structural MRI and diffusion tensor imaging in boxers and MMA fighters demonstrate that greater cumulative head-impact exposure is associated with reduced regional brain volumes and compromised white-matter integrity, particularly within subcortical regions including the thalamus and caudate, alongside slower cognitive processing.96,100 Reduced fractional anisotropy and increased diffusivity may be evident even when conventional imaging appears normal, consistent with diffuse axonal injury.98,99 Resting-state functional MRI has also identified altered large-scale network connectivity that correlates with impairments in processing speed and executive function. 80 Perfusion imaging findings are more heterogeneous, although arterial spin labelling studies have reported regionally reduced CBF, particularly within temporal regions. 101 Collectively, these findings indicate that repetitive head impacts may progressively erode neurovascular and cognitive function, linking acute injury with long-term neurological vulnerability.

MMA at the intersection of injury and adaptation

MMA presents a uniquely complex physiological landscape in which athletes are simultaneously exposed to mechanical trauma and intermittent strangulation-induced cerebral IR. This places MMA at the intersection of injury and adaptation, with potentially neuroprotective cIPC-like stimuli coexisting alongside cumulative mechanical trauma. This balance is further complicated by the widespread practice of rapid weight loss (‘weight cutting’), which acutely reduces plasma volume and disrupts electrolyte homeostasis, leading to reductions in global CBF.102,103 Hypovolaemia may therefore further impair cerebral O2, glucose and lactate delivery, heightening susceptibility to traumatic forces and potentially blunting any cIPC-like neuroprotection.

Ultimately, long-term neurological outcomes in MMA likely reflect the dynamic interplay between cumulative mechanical injury and the capacity for adaptive, redox-mediated resilience, modulated by systemic factors including hydration status, genetic susceptibility and CRF. MMA therefore provides a unique human model for defining the inflection point at which beneficial adaptation gives way to pathological injury. Defining this threshold may help inform training practices, athlete monitoring, return-to-combat decisions and future neuroprotective interventions.

Clinical translation

Combat sports provide a distinctive translational framework for examining how the human brain navigates the boundary between adaptation and irreversible injury. Repeated transient cerebral IR during controlled vascular neck restraint may engage endogenous cIPC-like mechanisms, providing a biologically plausible parallel to emerging clinical conditioning strategies. Remote ischaemic preconditioning, typically delivered via intermittent limb occlusion owing to its relative safety and ease of application, has shown promise in experimental models of stroke and neurodegeneration,104–108 although further clinical validation is required. Insights from grappling athletes may therefore help refine the dose and timing of conditioning stimuli for therapeutic translation.

Combat sports also expose the brain to systemic signals that may modify neurological outcome. 109 The liver-derived exercise factor glycosylphosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipase D1 can improve BBB integrity and cognitive function through effects on cerebrovascular tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase, illustrating how peripheral exercise adaptations may support neuroprotection. 110 Conversely, musculoskeletal and myofibrillar injury associated with strikes, throws and blunt trauma may trigger local and systemic OXINOS, inflammatory cytokine release and endothelial activation that, when excessive or unresolved, could promote neuroinflammatory signalling and compromise NVU function.111,112

These competing exercise- and injury-derived signals may collectively shape cerebrovascular health and cognition. However, combat sports also reveal the limits of endogenous neuroprotection. Repetitive head impacts in boxing, kickboxing, Muay Thai, karate, taekwondo and MMA can overwhelm adaptive capacity, promoting axonal injury, exaggerated OXINOS, NVU dysfunction and progressive cognitive decline. Integrating biomarkers of exercise adaptation, tissue injury, systemic OXINOS and NVU integrity may help identify when physiological adaptation is giving way to cumulative injury, thereby improving risk stratification, athlete monitoring and identification of those most likely to benefit from targeted interventions. Defining this inflection point is central to athlete safety, return-to-combat decisions and regulatory oversight, with broader implications for concussion, stroke, neurodegeneration and healthy brain ageing.

Conclusions

The human brain operates within an exceptionally narrow bioenergetic margin that confers both profound vulnerability and remarkable adaptive potential. Combat sports provide a unique human model in which repetitive mechanical trauma and transient metabolic and cerebrovascular stress coexist, engaging competing pathways of injury and adaptation. Repetitive head impacts promote axonal injury, exaggerated OXINOS, neurometabolic failure and tau pathology, whereas controlled pre-syncopal vascular neck restraints may engage cIPC-like redox, metabolic and neurovascular adaptations. Whether these responses constitute true cIPC remains unresolved. Long-term neurological outcome likely depends on the nature, magnitude and frequency of exposure, and on individual adaptive capacity. The key message for athletes, coaches and clinicians is that combat-sport exposure is neither uniformly harmful nor protective: controlled metabolic and cerebrovascular challenges may promote adaptation, whereas repeated or severe biomechanical loading can overwhelm endogenous protection and drive cumulative neurological injury. Defining the threshold at which adaptation gives way to cumulative injury will inform athlete safety, monitoring and return-to-combat decision-making, while potentially revealing neuroprotective strategies relevant to concussion, stroke, neurodegeneration and brain ageing.
