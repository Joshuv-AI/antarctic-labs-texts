The ASC-c isoform as a modulator of inflammasome activation: Insights into molecular mechanisms and therapeutic applications
Authors: Meenakshi Sharma, Anthony Waterston, Christopher J Randolph, Lauren E Stark, David M Gravano, Michael E Colvin, Eva de Alba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCanonical inflammasome assembly is driven by interactions between sensors and effector procaspase-1, primarily mediated by the adaptor ASC. Homotypic interactions between Death Domains pyrin (PYD) and caspase recruitment and activation domain (CARD) lead to sensor and ASC oligomerization, along with the recruitment of procaspase-1. ASC self-association is essential for inflammasome activation, which initiates inflammatory responses. Therefore, uncontrolled inflammasome activation contributes to chronic inflammatory diseases. ASC-c, an isoform of ASC, acts as a negative regulator of the inflammasome. To better understand ASC-c’s regulatory role, we examined its structural properties and interactions with ASC. Our nuclear magnetic resonance data show that ASC-c’s CARD is properly folded, whereas the PYD consists of two α-helices instead of the six-helix bundle typical of Death Domains. In addition, a chemical shift perturbation analysis indicates that ASC-c interacts with ASC. We obtained transmission electron micrographs revealing that ASC-c polymerizes into filaments and filament bundles, which display greater heterogeneity than those of ASC based on dynamic light scattering. Overall, our results suggest that ASC-c binding to ASC can have an impact on ASC self-association, thereby affecting inflammasome assembly. Based on these findings, we designed a peptide encompassing the two helices of the ASC-c PYD to target ASC for therapeutic purposes. We demonstrate the interaction between the peptide and ASC by fluorescence anisotropy and show the stability of the complex by molecular dynamics simulations. Finally, cell-based assays measuring inflammasome activation indicate an inhibitory effect of the ASC-c peptide, pointing to its potential use in drug design.Keywords: ASC isoforms, inflammasome, inflammasome inhibition, NMR, peptide design, protein-peptide interactions, protein-protein interaction, protein structure

Innate immunity is the body's first line of defense against various pathogens and cellular stress, playing a pivotal role in maintaining homeostasis and providing protection from infections. One of the central mechanisms underlying innate immunity is the activation of inflammasomes. These supramolecular complexes serve as key mediators in initiating the inflammatory response by acting as a signaling platform within several cell types (1). Among the diverse inflammasomes, the apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain (ASC) has emerged as a critical adaptor molecule, facilitating inflammasome assembly by connecting effector and sensor proteins (2, 3).

The danger signal recognition by sensor proteins from the AIM2-like receptor (ALR) (4, 5) and NOD-like receptor (NLR) (6) families prompts sensor oligomerization and the recruitment of the adaptor ASC through homotypic interactions mediated by their pyrin domains (PYD). In this process, ASC self-associates, forming perinuclear specks or pyroptosomes (2, 7, 8). The ASC specks interact with the effector protein procaspase-1 via CARD-CARD interactions, inducing proximity-mediated activation of procaspase-1 (9, 10, 11), which leads to the maturation of the proinflammatory cytokines interleukin 1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18) and the cleavage of the protein gasdermin D (12, 13). The cleaved N-terminal domain of gasdermin D self-assembles to create pores in the plasma membrane, thereby inducing a form of cell death known as pyroptosis (14, 15). The release of IL-1β and IL-18 promotes the migration and recruitment of neutrophils and monocytes to the injury or infected site, containing the infection and maintaining tissue homeostasis (16, 17). Pyroptosis leads to the release of large intracellular components like ASC specks into the extracellular matrix (18, 19, 20).

Extracellular ASC specks (eASC) are active and thus capable of recruiting and activating procaspase-1 (19, 20). Additionally, the specks can be phagocytized by surrounding cells and macrophages, leading to the perpetuation of the inflammatory response by promoting ASC polymerization in the cytosol without inflammasome activation by a sensor (18). Due to this characteristic, eASC can also act as a death signal and propagate inflammasome responses to bystander cells (21). The dysregulation of inflammasome sensor, adaptor, and effector, along with the release of eASC, has been linked to various diseases such as cardiovascular, neurodegenerative, and autoimmune disorders, cancer, as well as the cytokine storm observed in COVID-19 patients (22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29). Hence, precise fine-tuning and regulation of the inflammasome are essential for controlled activation in response to danger signals and preventing unwanted initiation. The human body employs several mechanisms to ensure proper regulation, including cell-extrinsic and cell-intrinsic processes (30). Cell-extrinsic regulation controls the transcription of inflammasome components and early priming events. Cell-intrinsic regulation eliminates cells that compromise function or pose a danger to the whole organism by controlling ion fluxes, oxidative states, autophagy, and interactions with host proteins.

Specific regulatory proteins have evolved to regulate the inflammasome at the sensor, adaptor, and effector levels through interactions involving Death Domains (31, 32). For example, pyrin domain-only proteins (POPs) like POP1 (also called PYDC1 or ASC2), POP2 (PYDC2), and POP3 inhibit the interaction between sensors and ASC (33, 34, 35). Likewise, caspase activity can be controlled by CARD-only proteins (COPs) like CARD16 (also known as pseudo-ICE or COP1), CARD17 (INCA), and CARD18 (ICEBERG) (36, 37, 38, 39). Furthermore, alternative splicing of ASC leading to various ASC isoforms with different inflammasome-activating functions also regulates inflammasome assembly (40, 41).

ASC isoforms are known as canonical ASC, ASC-b, ASC-c, and ASC-d (40, 41). At the primary structure level, ASC-b carries a much shorter linker due to an in-frame deletion of 93 to 111 amino acids and retains the full PYD and CARD. ASC-c lacks residues 26 to 85 corresponding to helices three to six of the ASC PYD; therefore, it contains a partial PYD, the full linker, and the CARD (Fig. 1). ASC-d is a novel 69-amino-acid-long peptide replacing residues 36 to 195 due to a frameshift. As a result, it only retains the first 35 residues corresponding to helices 1 2 of the PYD. These isoforms exhibit distinct localization patterns, efficacies, and functions in regulating inflammasome activity, thereby influencing various stages of inflammatory responses (40, 41).

The relevance of ASC isoforms has been demonstrated in different diseases, such as palindromic rheumatism, which is a type of inflammatory arthritis, and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47). ASC-b is dominantly expressed in patients with palindromic rheumatism. This expression is induced by a single-nucleotide polymorphism and suppressed via IL-1β regulation of alternative splicing. IL-1β, in turn, promotes canonical ASC expression (42, 43). Based on these results, an alternation in the expression of ASC and ASC-b is proposed to be connected to the periodic symptoms characteristic of palindromic rheumatism (43). In addition, ASC expression is a marker of AD (44, 45, 46, 47). Antibodies raised against different ASC epitopes differentially identify central nervous system cells in AD. These results have been connected to antibody recognition of different ASC isoforms (46, 47). ASC specks from microglia, the primary immune cells of the central nervous system, bind to amyloid-β, enhancing its aggregation and thus spreading this pathology in AD (47). It is possible to speculate whether ASC isoforms contribute differently to the oligomerization of amyloid-β.

The role of ASC isoforms in inflammatory diseases could be related to their different functions in inflammasome formation and activation. For instance, ASC-b is commonly co-expressed with NLRP3, ASC, and procaspase-1, and acts as an inflammasome adaptor (41). However, it exhibits different efficacy compared to ASC, manifested by the production of lower levels of IL-1β. On the other hand, ASC-c colocalizes with ASC and procaspase-1 and inhibits IL-1β secretion. ASC-b disperses in the cytoplasm and forms irregularly shaped aggregates when co-expressed with ASC. ASC-c displays long filamentous structures in the cytoplasm and forms circular, less compact perinuclear aggregates when co-expressed with ASC (41). ASC-d is diffusely present in the cytosol and does not colocalize with any inflammasome components (41). Its specific role remains unknown. Our recent nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) studies reveal that ASC-b shares identical structures with ASC PYD and CARD at the molecular level (48). However, ASC-b exhibits slower self-association rates and a reduced tendency to form densely packed filaments compared to ASC (48, 49). These findings indicate that the shorter linker accounts for the observed differences in ASC-b and ASC self-association properties (48, 49). The specific structural details and mechanistic insights into how the expression of ASC-c regulates ASC assembly at the molecular level to inhibit IL-1β secretion remain unexplored.

To bridge this knowledge gap, we studied ASC-c and its interaction with ASC at the structural level using various biophysical techniques and computational methods. We determined the 15N and 13C chemical shifts of the ASC-c polypeptide backbone using solution-state NMR and compared them with our previously reported ASC data (50) to elucidate ASC-c secondary structure. This analysis reveals that ASC-c's partial PYD adopts two helices, while its CARD comprises a six-helix bundle identical to that of ASC. We comprehensively analyzed ASC and ASC-c interactions, identifying the specific binding interfaces. Our results reveal that both ASC-c PYD and CARD interact with ASC via interfaces that are also involved in ASC self-association. An analysis using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) unveils unique macrostructural characteristics and dynamic behaviors of ASC-c, distinct from ASC and ASC-b.

Finally, we designed a peptide derived from the ASC-c structure and examined its interaction with ASC using a combination of NMR, molecular dynamics simulations, and fluorescence anisotropy. Importantly, we studied the effect of the ASC-c peptide on the release of IL-1β in activated macrophages. Overall, our findings offer a pioneering molecular-level insight into ASC-c's impact on the inflammasome, indicating that ASC-c binding to ASC might disrupt ASC self-association, potentially leading to inflammasome inhibition. In addition, our results show that the ASC-c peptide can be used as a scaffold to develop small-molecule inhibitors for disrupting immune cell function in ASC-mediated inflammatory pathways. Our work is highly significant as it establishes a foundation for developing therapeutics to regulate chronic inflammation.

Results

Structural characterization of ASC-c and comparison with canonical ASC

The structural characterization of ASC-c was achieved by acquiring two-dimensional (2D) [1H-15N]-HSQC (51) NMR spectra at a protein concentration of 200 μM, 298 K, and pH of 3.8 (Fig. 2). It is well established from previous studies that ASC exhibits poor solubility at neutral pH but remains soluble under acidic conditions (50). NMR studies have confirmed that ASC adopts a properly folded structure at pH ∼ 4 (50). Therefore, we have performed our NMR experiments under acidic conditions to maintain ASC-c in a soluble state.

The NMR spectrum of ASC-c reveals a folded protein. Two-dimensional [1H,15N]-HSQC spectrum of 15N-labeled ASC-c at pH 3.8 and 25 °C. Assigned peaks are labeled with the one-letter amino acid code and their corresponding residue number in the polypeptide sequence. Some assignments are not shown for clarity. The chemical shifts of ASC-c have been deposited in the Biological Magnetic Resonance Data Bank with accession number 52441.

Signals in the 2D [1H-15N]-HSQC spectra correspond to the correlation between the chemical shift of 15N and its directly attached 1H. The position of each peak is influenced by the chemical environment of the individual amides within the protein. This results in a distinct and unique fingerprint spectrum, offering an overall representation of the protein's structural characteristics. The [1H-15N]-HSQC spectrum of ASC-c displays good chemical shift dispersion along both 15N and 1H chemical shift axes, indicating a well-folded protein structure (Fig. 2).

To elucidate the structural information of ASC-c at the residue level, we assigned the NH cross-peaks in the HSQC spectrum using conventional triple-resonance experiments (CBCACONH and HNCACB) (51, 52). ASC-c is comprised of 135 amino acids, including seven prolines that do not contribute to the signals observed in the [1H-15N]-HSQC spectrum. Backbone amide chemical shift assignments were successfully obtained for all ASC-c amino acid residues except for proline (Fig. 2). The chemical shifts of ASC-c have been deposited in the BMRB (ID: 52441).

Detailed NMR studies on the structure of ASC have been reported (50, 53), which we can compare with information on ASC-c. The schematic representation of domain organization and polypeptide sequence alignment of ASC and ASC-c is shown in Figure 1. The structure of ASC consists of two domains, an N-terminal PYD (residues 1–89) and a C-terminal CARD (residues 113–195), connected by a 23-residue-long flexible linker (residues 90–112). NMR studies reported that PYD and CARD of ASC adopt a six-helical bundle motif, and the linker displays residual secondary structure (50). The comparison between the polypeptide sequences of ASC and ASC-c reveals that the latter retains identical CARD and linker sequences (Fig. 1B). The amide 1H and 15N chemical shifts of the ASC and ASC-c CARD domains are similar, with a small combined average difference of 0.01 ± 0.02 ppm, whereas this difference is 0.6 ± 0.3 ppm for the PYD domains (Experimental Procedures section). This analysis indicates that the structures of the CARD of ASC-c and ASC are identical and that the differences observed in the ASC-c PYD region do not produce any significant impact on the CARD structure.

Due to the well-dispersed chemical shifts observed in the partial PYD region of ASC-c, we expected this domain to be folded and display structural characteristics distinct from ASC PYD. To explore this further, we conducted a secondary chemical shift analysis (Fig. 3). The deviations of observed 13Cα chemical shifts from their random coil values (54, 55, 56), known as secondary chemical shifts, were used to study the polypeptide’s secondary structure. In Figure 3, we show an overlay of ASC and ASC-c secondary 13Cα chemical shifts (Experimental Procedures section). The ASC chemical shifts are available from a previous study (BMRB ID: 15313, (50)). The presence of positive 13Cα values is indicative of the α-helical structure, and successive values around 3 ± 1.0 ppm (56) are commonly construed to be fully developed secondary structures. In contrast, lower values often suggest that the involved amino acids populate the secondary structure to a lesser extent (55, 56). The secondary chemical shifts of the ASC-c CARD closely resemble those of ASC (Fig. 3), strongly indicating an identical six-helix bundle motif. This result is consistent with the combined average amide chemical shift difference. Furthermore, the linker region of ASC-c exhibits similarities to that of ASC, with only minor deviations likely due to the slightly more flexible nature of this fragment in ASC-c (Fig. 3).

Secondary chemical shifts reveal that the ASC-c CARD and partial PYD are folded. Overlay of secondary 13Cα chemical shifts of ASC and ASC-c versus residue number (residue numbering follows the ASC sequence). ASC-c (blue bars) and ASC (magenta bars) secondary shifts are nearly identical in the CARD region, consisting of a six-helix bundle. The PYD region of ASC-c displays two distinct α-helical populations, indicated by positive deviations from random coil reference values (54, 55, 56). Boxes at the top denote the sequence of α-helices within the PYD and CARD in ASC.

The sequence of positive secondary chemical shifts in the partial ASC-c PYD region indicates the formation of an α-helical structure. We observed two well-separated groups of positive changes in chemical shifts, showing the presence of two helices with different helical populations. The first helix spans from residue A4 to N14 and has a helical content of 32.48 ± 9.71%, while the second helix comprises residues from T16 to A28 and has a helical content of 65.58 ± 16.95%. The helical population was determined by comparing the average 13Cα secondary chemical shifts of ASC-c residues in the helices to the reference value of 3 ± 1.0 ppm, which represents the average experimental 13Cα secondary chemical shift for α-helices (56) (Experimental Procedures section).

Overall, our NMR findings highlight significant structural differences between canonical ASC and the ASC-c isoform, which could govern their opposite biological functions as activator and inhibitor of the inflammasome, respectively.

Structure-based ASC-c peptide design and secondary structure analysis

Previous studies have established that ASC-c functions as a natural inflammasome inhibitor by decreasing the expression of IL-1β in the cells (41). Further cell studies have shown that ASC-c co-localizes with ASC and procaspase-1, but not with the PYD-containing inflammasome sensor NLRP3 (41). These findings suggest that ASC-c can inhibit the inflammasome by interacting with ASC via its PYD and/or CARD domains, and with procaspase-1 via CARD.

Several small synthetic and endogenous molecules have displayed the ability to inhibit the NLRP3 inflammasome (57, 58, 59). Nonetheless, the use of small-molecule drugs can occasionally present drawbacks, such as irreversible binding, requirement for higher doses that lead to toxicity, low bioavailability, or unwanted results in clinical trials (60). Consequently, in recent years, researchers have focused on developing inflammasome inhibitors based on peptides. Peptides present several advantages, including target specificity, potency, reduced toxicity, and a lower impact on normal physiological processes (61, 62). Designing peptides derived from protein-protein interactions or modifying natural peptides provides a promising approach for developing novel anti-inflammatory therapeutics. The large size of full-length ASC-c limits its practical use as an inhibitor, but smaller regions of the protein could be promising leads. Based on the structural information obtained from our NMR studies, we designed a peptide comprising helices α1 and α2 of the ASC-c PYD to specifically target the adaptor ASC, which interacts with different types of sensors and effectors, leading to inflammasome assembly. We have named this polypeptide fragment, comprising residues M1-T29, ASC-c peptide (Fig. 1B).

To investigate the secondary structure of the peptide, far-UV circular dichroism (CD) spectra were recorded at various concentrations ranging from 6 to 60 μM at pH 3.8 (Fig. 4). The spectral profile displayed two minima at 222 and 208 nm, which are indicative of a correctly folded α-helical structure (Fig. 4A) (Experimental Procedures section). The minimum at 208 nm is lower than that at 222 nm, and it is similar to previously reported CD data of monomeric ASC PYD recorded at 10 μM concentration and pH 4.5 (62) and full-length ASC and ASC-c at pH 3.8 (63). The magnitude of the signal at 208 nm and 222 nm increases slightly with increasing concentration. However, the 222/208 ratio remains nearly identical, ranging from 0.57 to 0.58 at 6 μM and 60 μM, respectively. The molar ellipticity values at 222 nm were used to determine the percentage of the α-helical population adopted by the peptide (Fig. 4B) (Experimental Procedures). The estimated helical percentage ranges from 15.3% at 6 μM to 18.3% at 60 μM. These results suggest that a tenfold increase in the peptide concentration does not substantially impact the secondary conformation of the peptide. Overall, our CD analysis reveals an average α-helical population of ∼ 17 ± 1% for the peptide at the given concentrations.

The ASC-c peptide exhibits the characteristic minima for α-helices at 208 nm and 222 nm, with a small variation in the spectral profile observed at different concentrations. B, percentage of the α-helical population calculated from the molar ellipticity values at 222 nm for each concentration. The red dashed line indicates an average α-helical content of ∼ 17 ± 1% adopted by the peptide.

Taken together, the CD data on the peptide and the NMR results on full-length ASC-c indicate that the PYD fragment can adopt up to 66% population of helical conformation when integrated within the entire protein. However, the helical population of the isolated peptide decreases to ∼ 17%. Therefore, the context of the full-length protein likely enhances the stability of the PYD, leading to increased helicity. Based on these results, it is possible to consider a heterotypic interaction between the CARD and the partial PYD of the ASC-c isoform.

ASC-c filament bundle formation and comparison with ASC and isoform ASC-b

Previous negative staining Transmission Electron Microscopy (ns-TEM) studies have established that ASC forms filaments with an average width of approximately 6.4 ± 0.8 nm and lengths ranging from 600 to 800 nm (64). Similar measurements were reported for filaments formed by the ASC-b isoform, which are approximately 7.0 ± 0.2 nm wide and 200 to 400 nm long (48). Both proteins function as inflammasome activators, undergoing polymerization into filament bundles. However, their intracellular distribution patterns differ significantly (41). Cell-based studies have revealed that the inflammasome inhibitory protein ASC-c also generates filamentous macrostructures within the cytoplasm (41). To investigate the oligomerization of ASC-c in greater detail, we conducted ns-TEM studies and compared the results with previously reported data for ASC (64) and ASC-b (48).

To promote ASC-c filament formation, we followed the same procedure previously described for ASC and ASC-b (48, 64), which involves unfolding and slowly refolding the protein at neutral pH. The ns-TEM analysis revealed that ASC-c polymerizes, forming filaments and filament bundles (Fig. 5). Examination of 60 individual ASC-c filaments indicates an average width of 7.5 ± 0.8 nm and several 100 nanometers in length, similar to the filament dimensions of ASC and ASC-b.

ASC-c self-assembles into bundles of filaments.A–D, negative staining TEM micrographs displaying ASC-c bundles at different magnifications. Scale bars are indicated. Panel A shows the width (69.5 nm) of a 9-filament bundle. The bundle width divided by the number of filaments reports the width of a single filament, 7.7 nm, which falls within the average and standard deviation measured for 60 individual filaments (7.5 ± 0.8 nm).

Further, the ns-TEM images reveal that ASC-c polymerizes into filaments that self-assemble to create an interlocking network of bundles (Fig. 5). The width of these filament bundles ranges from 22 to 106 nm, with a slight dominant occurrence in the 22 to 41 nm range, potentially accommodating about 3 to 5 filaments per bundle. ASC forms bundles consisting of 2 to 7 filaments, with a predominant occurrence of 3 to 4 filament bundles (64). On the other hand, ASC-b typically polymerizes into 1 to 3 filament bundles, with the majority being 2 filament bundles (48). These results demonstrate that ASC-c forms wider bundles compared to ASC-b, while maintaining similar widths to ASC.

Upon closer examination, we observed a direct correlation between the overall width of the ASC-c bundle and the total number of filaments multiplied by the width of a single filament within it. This indicates a lateral stacking pattern of individual filaments within the bundles, as previously observed for ASC and ASC-b (48, 64). Additionally, similar to ASC-b, ASC-c filaments do not show the presence of rings attached to preexisting filaments, as observed in ASC (64).

Consolidating insights from prior research on ASC and ASC-b, along with our ASC-c results, it is evident that all three variants form individual filaments of similar width. The width of the ASC-c filament bundle is comparable to ASC, while ASC-b forms slightly narrower bundles. Overall, these characteristics might hold significance in ASC-c's interaction with ASC and mediating distinct functionalities in regulating inflammasomes.

Kinetics of ASC-c self-association and comparison with canonical ASC and ASC-b isoform

Our preliminary analysis via ns-TEM revealed that ASC-c tends to polymerize into filamentous structures and multiple filamentous bundles. To further investigate the kinetics of ASC-c self-association, we performed an analysis using dynamic light scattering (DLS). Despite the challenges in accurately analyzing filamentous (non-spherical) particles, this technique remains instrumental in elucidating the fundamental aspects of the polymerization process, including self-association kinetics, the number of distinct oligomerization species, and polydispersity. Moreover, it can detect low-populated species within heterogeneous populations of aggregates because the intensity of scattered light is proportional to the sixth power of the particle diameter.

In our DLS experiments, we measured the intensity of scattered light as a function of particle size at ASC-c concentrations of 700 μM and 350 μM, at times ranging from 30 min (after protein dissolution) to 46 h (Figs. 6, A–C and S1). The ASC-c concentrations used in the DLS experiments are significantly larger than the ASC level found in cells, which is ∼ 10 μM (7). The reason for this difference is that ASC oligomers are prominent under acidic conditions only at concentrations higher than 200 μM (48, 49, 50). Oligomerization is hindered by the shielding of electrostatic interactions at acid pH (48, 49, 50).

A, initial measurement taken 30 min after sample preparation shows high polydispersity. B and C, After 16 h (B) and 46 h (C), the solution maintains a consistent bimodal size distribution. The intensity of scattered light is proportional to the population and size of the different species in the solution. At 30 min, the solution shows high polydispersity, characterized by a multimodal particle size distribution. Four distinct peaks were observed, representing different-sized populations at ∼ 5 nm, 24 nm, 126 nm, and 769 nm (Fig. 6A). The four oligomeric species observed in the solution at the starting time of the experiment consolidate into two detectable species over time. After 16 h, the ASC-c solution displays a bimodal size distribution, which persists even after 46 h. This suggests that the polymerization process has reached a thermodynamically stable state. Overall, our DLS results indicate a propensity to form oligomers with a more consistent and uniform size distribution. The kinetics of self-association were previously characterized for ASC and ASC-b under analogous conditions (48). ASC exhibits a single species of monodisperse polymers. Similar to ASC-c, ASC-b also displays two prominent signals.

The average polydispersity values for the ASC-c polymerization are 0.49 ± 0.04 and 0.54 ± 0.03 at 700 μM and 350 μM, respectively. These values indicate that polydispersity is not significantly affected by doubling the ASC-c concentration (Fig. S1). In addition, ASC-c polydispersity is higher than ASC (0.35 ± 0.04) but lower than ASC-b (0.79 ± 0.06) (Fig. 6D). This result indicates that ASC forms more compact and uniform oligomers, while ASC-c exhibits moderate heterogeneity. On the other hand, ASC-b shows higher heterogeneity in particle sizes.

These findings are significant in explaining how protein oligomers are distributed within the cells. Studies showed that the three proteins form filaments inside the cell but exhibit differential heterogeneity and form different macrostructures (41). Our DLS data provide overall structural characteristics on ASC-c polymerization and corroborate observations in the cell-based studies, indicating that ASC forms compact perinuclear aggregates of uniform size, whereas ASC-b and ASC-c display expanded aggregates (41). The lower polydispersity of ASC-c oligomers compared to ASC-b agrees with results from the cell-based studies showing that ASC-b forms assemblies that are more diffused within the cytosol compared to ASC-c (41).

ASC-c interaction with ASC at the residue level

Previous studies have demonstrated that ASC-c co-localizes with ASC when both are expressed together (41). Contrary to the long filamentous structures formed by ASC-c alone, the presence of ASC prompts the recruitment of ASC-c towards perinuclear ASC aggregates. These findings suggest that ASC-c interacts with ASC, potentially influencing its self-association, thus explaining previous observations of ASC-c-mediated inhibition of the inflammasome, demonstrated by the lower levels of IL-1β secretion in macrophages (41). We used NMR spectroscopy to gain a better understanding of the interaction between ASC and ASC-c.

NMR chemical shifts serve as highly sensitive indicators of the nuclei's local electronic environment, often perturbed by binding events. Hence, changes in chemical shifts upon protein-protein interactions yield crucial insights into the interaction site and binding affinity. NMR spectra of the protein are acquired at increasing ligand concentrations to determine chemical shift perturbations (CSP) upon ligand binding. The resulting perturbations in chemical shifts due to complex formation can be attributed to the proximity between the protein and the ligand and/or conformational changes induced by binding.

To investigate ASC-ASC-c interactions, we conducted two different titration experiments. The first experiment involved a forward titration in which a reference [1H-15N]-SOFAST-HMQC spectrum of 15N-labeled ASC at 30 μM was acquired in the absence of unlabeled ASC-c, followed by a series of analogous spectra obtained at increasing concentrations of unlabeled ASC-c up to 330 μM (Fig. 7A). In the second experiment, a reverse titration was performed by acquiring a reference spectrum of 15N-labeled ASC-c at 30 μM in the absence and presence of increasing concentrations of unlabeled ASC, reaching up to 540 μM (Fig. 7B).

Forward titration (15N-labeled ASC and unlabeled ASC-c)

We monitored changes in the chemical shifts of cross-peaks corresponding to each amide group upon the addition of the unlabeled protein. We did not observe any significant line broadening during the titration. This result indicates that the protein-protein interaction occurs on a time scale faster than the chemical shift; thus, the fast-exchange regime (65, 66). Therefore, the observed chemical shifts represent a population-weighted average of chemical shifts in the free and bound states.

The CSP data of ASC were calculated as described in the Experiment Procedures Section and plotted against the residue number (Fig. 7A). The dashed horizontal line represents 1.5 times the standard deviation (0.044 ppm) calculated for 195 residues. Both the PYD and CARD of ASC participate in the interaction, with PYD exhibiting overall higher chemical shift perturbations. Residues showing chemical shift changes equal to or greater than 1.5 times the standard deviation in the PYD region include: D10 located on the α1-helix, T16 on the turn between helices α1 and α2, E19, F23, K24, L25, L27 on the α2-helix, V30, I39 on the turn between helices α2 and α3, and R41, G42, A43, L44, L45, S46, M47 on the α3-helix. The most significant changes in chemical shifts are observed for residues T16 and L44, with shifts of 0.28 ppm and 0.16 ppm, respectively.

These results indicate that helices α2 and α3 in ASC PYD mediate the interaction with ASC-c. NMR studies on ASC PYD self-association revealed the involvement of two distinct, oppositely charged surfaces: one comprising helices α1, α4, and the N-terminus of the α5-helix, and the other involving helices α2, α3, and the C-terminus of the α5-helix (67). Together, these results highlight the predominant role of helices α2 and α3 in the interactions mediated by the PYD domain. Additionally, eight out of the 16 residues of ASC PYD displaying some of the largest chemical shift perturbations upon binding to ASC-c were also identified in ASC PYD self-association (67). These findings suggest a correlation between ASC-c binding and the reduced ASC self-association observed in cells in the presence of ASC-c (41), which could contribute to inflammasome inhibition.

We also observed perturbations in the ASC CARD region. Residues showing chemical shift changes equal to or larger than 1.5 times the standard deviation in the CARD region include: D134 on α2-helix, K174 and S184 on α5-helix, D191 on α6-helix, and S195 at the C-terminus. NMR studies on ASC CARD self-association revealed the involvement of residues located in the turn preceding α1-helix, as well as helices α2, α3, α5, and α6, forming three distinct contact regions (64). Cryo-EM studies on ASC CARD filament formation show that type 1 interactions involve the α2 interface (E130 and D134) of one molecule interacting with the helices α1 and α4 interfaces of another (68). Type 2 interactions include W169 and Y187, while type 3 interactions involve the interaction between the α4-helix (R160) and the α3-helix (D143 and E144) (68). Overall, out of five residues that showed higher CSP in the interaction between ASC-c and ASC, four residues (D134, S184, D191, and S195) are directly involved in ASC CARD self-association, as demonstrated by cryo-EM, NMR, and mutational studies (64, 68).

To better define the interacting surfaces, we mapped the chemical shift perturbations resulting from the ASC-ASC-c interaction onto the structure of ASC (Fig. 8A). These regions indicate changes in the local chemical environment of ASC when binding to ASC-c. Additionally, we highlighted the interfaces involved in ASC PYD-PYD interactions and ASC CARD-CARD interactions (Fig. 8, C and D), using data derived from NMR and cryo-EM studies for comparison with the ASC-ASC-c interaction interfaces.

Reverse titration (15N-labeled ASC-c and unlabeled ASC)

We also determine the CSPs in ASC-c upon titration with ASC (Fig. 7B). For clarity, ASC-c and ASC residue numbers are aligned as the former lacks residues 26 to 85 of the ASC amino acid sequence. The dashed horizontal line in Figure 7B, corresponds to 1.5 times the standard deviation (0.029 ppm) calculated from 135 residues. Both PYD and CARD of ASC-c are shown to be involved in the interaction with ASC. Residues exhibiting chemical shift changes ≥1.5 times the standard deviation in the PYD region include R5, D6, D10, and E13 from α1-helix, T16 on the turn between helices α1 and α2, and A17, E19, L20, and A27 (A87 in ASC numbering) on α2-helix. Residues T89 and H90 (amino acids 29 and 30 in the ASC-c sequence, respectively) of the linker region also show chemical shift perturbations. These results indicate that both helices of the partial PYD participate in the interaction with ASC. Among these residues, the chemical shifts of D10, T16, and E19 are perturbed in both forward and reverse titrations.

We have also observed CSPs in the CARD region. Residues showing the largest perturbations include, following ASC numbering, are: H113, F114, D116, Q117, H118, R119, A121, I123, and R125 on α1-helix, N128 on the turn between α1-helix and α2-helix, D134 on α2-helix, Y146 on α3-helix, K158 and M159 on α4-helix, D175 and S184 on α5-helix, S186, Y187, L188, V189, D191 and L192 on α6-helix and S195 at C-terminus. Among these residues, D134, S184, D191, and S195 undergo CSPs in both forward and reverse titrations. We mapped the chemical shift perturbations into a model structure of ASC-c generated using I-TASSER (69) to show the regions involved in the ASC-ASC-c interaction (Fig. 8B).

Heterotypic interactions between the Death Domains CARD and PYD are rare (70, 71). However, the PYD domain of ASC-c does not adopt the Death Domain fold, which consists of a six-helix bundle. Thus, we do not rule out the possibility of heterotypic interaction between the partial PYD and the CARD in ASC-c and ASC. The models generated using I-TASSER illustrate potential orientations of the two domains, showing the PYD in proximity to the CARD (Fig. S2). The incomplete Death Domain fold in ASC-c PYD could prompt PYD-CARD interactions for stabilization.

Assuming only homotypic interactions take place, our results indicate that the interacting surfaces between the ASC PYD and ASC-c partial PYD predominantly involve helices α2 and α3 of ASC PYD and helices α1 and α2 of the ASC-c partial PYD. Analogously, the interaction between the ASC CARD and the ASC-c CARD is dominated by helices α2, α5, and α6 of the ASC CARD and helices α1, α4, α5, and α6 of the ASC-c CARD. Our NMR titration results confirm the hypothesis that ASC-c has the potential to interact directly with ASC in solution.

Equilibrium dissociation constant of ASC–ASC-c interaction

Changes in chemical shifts are a valuable tool for analyzing binding affinity at the individual amino acid level. We observed that the dependence of chemical shift perturbations on the ligand's concentration adopts the shape of a binding isotherm. Thus, we derived apparent equilibrium dissociation constants (KDapp) for the protein-protein complex by fitting the experimental data to a model representing a binding isotherm (Experimental Procedures section).

The changes in chemical shifts of several ASC residues (F23, L25, I39, A43, S46, and M47) demonstrate a dependence on the concentration of unlabeled ASC-c that follows a typical saturation binding curve (Fig. 9A). The apparent KD values obtained from fitting range from 120 μM to 190 μM, with an average value of 170 ± 30 μM for the forward titration (Table 1). Similarly, by fitting the binding curves of several ASC-c residues (T16, E19, A27, Q57, R65, Y86, and the side chain of Q119) (Fig. 9B), we obtain KDapp values ranging from 250 μM to 460 μM, with an average value of 340 ± 90 μM for the reverse titration (Table 1). The titration curves of several residues involved in the ASC-ASC-c interaction displayed behaviors that do not follow a regular binding isotherm (Fig. S3). ASC and ASC-c undergo multifaceted oligomerization processes required for the formation of intricate networks of interacting filaments. These processes may account for the different trends in chemical shift changes observed as a function of concentration among various groups of residues.

The observed CSPs can only result from the interaction between the 15N-labeled and the unlabeled protein, not from self-association of the labeled protein. We previously showed by NMR that ASC and ASC-b behave as monomers at pH ∼ 3.8 and concentrations below 200 μM (48, 50). Additionally, our previous analytical ultracentrifugation data on ASC-PYD indicate that the protein remains monomeric from 50 μM to 200 μM (67). Our 15N NMR relaxation (T2) data on full-length ASC-b and ASC also suggest that the proteins act as monomers at 200 μM (48, 50). Therefore, 15N ASC-15N ASC interactions are absent at the low concentrations used in the titration experiments (30 μM) at pH 3.8. The CSPs are only observed when the concentration of the unlabeled ligand is increased.

ASC and ASC-c have a strong tendency to self-associate at concentrations higher than 200 μM under acidic conditions, posing challenges in performing NMR titrations and potentially affecting the apparent binding affinity values. We observed that ASC-c exhibits lower solubility and a higher propensity to form filamentous protein networks compared to ASC. The different self-association behaviors between ASC and ASC-c may explain the observed variations in the dissociation constants, notably in the forward titration, where ASC-c reaches a high concentration of 330 μM. Furthermore, the elevated KDapp values indicate weak binding, leading to fast exchange on the NMR time scale. We believe that the affinity for ASC and ASC-c is likely higher at neutral pH. The NMR-derived equilibrium dissociation constants are indicative of an interaction and can be useful for comparative studies at acid pH.

ASC-c self-association at the residue level

We performed similar titration experiments on 15N-labeled ASC-c with unlabeled ASC-c to study the protein’s self-association behavior at the residue level. A 30 μM concentration of 15N-labeled ASC-c was titrated with unlabeled ASC-c at concentration ratios of 1:1, 1:2, 1:4, 1:6, 1:10, 1:14, and 1:18. A reference [1H-15N]-SOFAST-HMQC spectrum of 15N-labeled ASC-c was acquired in the absence of the unlabeled protein. Changes in 1H and 15N amide chemical shifts were monitored in a series of fast relaxation spectra acquired at increasing concentrations of unlabeled ASC-c. Chemical shift perturbations larger than 1.5 times the standard deviation were observed for 15 amino acids in the PYD and 22 amino acids in the CARD (Fig. 7C). Specifically, amino acids D6, D10, A11, E13 from α-helix 1 and T16, A17, E18, E19, L20, K24, L25, Q86, A87, A88, H90 (the latter four are Q26, A27, A28, and H30 in ASC-c numbering) from α-helix 2 in the PYD are likely involved in ASC-c self-association. Additionally, residues I100, Q101, S106, and K109 in the linker region also show CSPs larger than the average. Residues in the CARD participating in ASC-c self-association are: H113, F114, Q117, R119, in α1-helix, N128 and V129 in the turn between α1-helix and α2-helix, E130, D134, A135 in α2-helix, S157, K158 in α4-helix, F165 in the loop connecting helices four and 5, T172, D175 in α5-helix, S186, L188, V189, E190, D191, L192, R194 in α6-helix and S195 at C-terminus.

Importantly, these NMR data provide evidence of ASC-c self-association in line with the TEM results on its polymerization into filaments. Not surprisingly, most of the amino acids in ASC-c PYD and CARD with CSPs above average are also involved in the interactions with ASC. These results suggest that ASC-c polymerization is driven by interactions of an analogous nature to those with ASC, which suggests that ASC-c can oligomerize with ASC, likely interfering with its polymerization and therefore with inflammasome assembly.

Interaction between ASC and the ASC-c peptide and structural models of the complex

Our results indicate the presence of direct interactions between ASC and ASC-c and ASC-c self-assembly in a cell-free context. These interactions could hinder inflammasome assembly by interfering with ASC self-association. Thus, we have further investigated the interaction between ASC and the ASC-c peptide to test whether this peptide could be used as a template for the design of drugs aimed at inhibiting ASC oligomerization.

Chemical shift titration experiments were conducted using 15N-labeled ASC and unlabeled ASC-c peptide, and the corresponding chemical shift perturbations are shown in Figure 7D. The dashed horizontal line represents 1.5 times the standard deviation (0.018 ppm) calculated from 195 residues. The chemical shift changes observed in the ASC-ASC-c peptide titration are less pronounced compared to those seen with ASC-ASC-c (Fig. 7A). However, several ASC residues from both the PYD and CARD regions exhibit chemical shift changes exceeding 1.5 times the standard deviation, indicating an interaction between ASC and the ASC-c peptide. In the ASC PYD region, the residues with significant chemical shift changes include D10 on α1-helix, T16 on the turn between helices α1-α2, A17, E18, E19, K24, L25, K26 on α2-helix, V30, G35, I39 on the turn connecting helices α2-α3, L44, and S46 on α3-helix, D48 on the turn between helices α3-α4, A49, L52, T53 on α4-helix, and Y60, A70, M76 on α5-helix. In the CARD region, the residues with significant chemical shift changes include N128 on α1-helix, D134 on α2-helix, N170, K174, A180 on α5-helix, Y187, V189, D191, L192, E193, R194 on α6-helix, and S195 on C-terminus.

It is noteworthy that the ASC-c peptide, which exhibits a partial PYD structure, showed heterotypic interaction with the ASC CARD in addition to homotypic interaction with the PYD. Our titration results suggest that the interaction between ASC and the ASC-c peptide is dominated by residues located in helices α2 and α3 of the ASC PYD and helices α5 and α6 of the ASC CARD. These results are consistent with our findings obtained from titration experiments using 15N-labeled ASC and unlabeled ASC-c.

We further elucidated the interaction between ASC and the ASC-c peptide by guiding protein-protein docking protocols with HADDOCK (72) using the detailed structural information from our NMR studies. With this computational strategy, we built 3D structural models of the protein-peptide complex for additional studies by molecular dynamics simulations.

Molecular dynamics studies of the structure and stability of the ASC-ASC-c peptide complex

To gain additional knowledge on the interaction between the ASC-c peptide and ASC, we performed 1 microsecond (μs) all-atom classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations on the HADDOCK structures of 6 complexes between the ASC-c peptide and the PYD and CARD of ASC. These 6 complexes displayed the highest buried surface area from the top 10 clusters derived from HADDOCK. We named these complexes ASC-c peptide:CARD_1, 2, and 3, and ASC-c peptide:PYD_1, 2, and 3. The final structures of the complexes after 1μs of simulation indicate that the ASC-c peptide nicely fits into the surface of the ASC domains by adopting different conformations (Fig. 10). For instance, the peptide in the ASC-c peptide:CARD_1 structure appears wrapped around the CARD at the expense of losing secondary structure, whereas it retains α-helical conformation, identified by NMR, in the ASC-c peptide:CARD_2, and 3 complexes at the expense of a reduction in the peptide-protein interface area (Table 2). Analogously, the peptide in the complexes with the PYD also adopts various conformations, with ASC-c peptide:PYD_3 showing the two α-helices (Fig. 10).

To obtain additional insight into the stability of the complexes, we calculated the final buried surface areas upon complex formation using the PDBePISA online protein-interaction program (73) (Table 2). The complexes ASC-c peptide:CARD_1, and 3 display the largest interfaces. For the ASC PYD dimers, ASC-c peptide:PYD_1 shows the largest buried surface area, whereas 2 and 3 have similar values (Table 2). These values agree with studies on protein-protein interactions reporting that stable complexes are characterized by minimum interface areas of 500 Å2 (74). Contact areas of this and larger dimensions lead to enough interactions to compensate for entropy loss upon complex formation (74). Plots of the fraction of amino acids that adopt α-helical structure versus simulation time reveal that the net fraction of ASC-c peptide residues in α-helices falls approximately between 15% and 40%. Equilibrium dissociation constant of the ASC-ASC-c peptide interaction by fluorescence polarization

The chemical shift perturbations observed in the NMR titration experiments of ASC with the ASC-c peptide, while indicating an intermolecular interaction, are small compared to the data obtained in the NMR titration of the full-length proteins (Fig. 7, A and D). Thus, to provide additional evidence of the interaction and determine the equilibrium dissociation constant of the complex, we have used fluorescence polarization. This technique is ideally suited to study interactions between molecules with large size differences. We labeled the ASC-c peptide with a fluorophore via maleimide reaction and monitored the change in fluorescence polarization upon the addition of increasing concentrations of full-length ASC.

We performed the experiments at two pH values to test the effect of pH on the ASC-ASC-c peptide interaction. The titrations were conducted at pH 3.8, where ASC is soluble, and at pH 5.0, where sufficient ASC remains in solution for the experiments, and there is less shielding of the electrostatic interactions relative to pH 3.8. Under both conditions, we observed an increase in the fluorescence polarization of the labeled ASC-c peptide upon addition of ASC, following the shape of a binding isotherm (Fig. 12). The fluorescence anisotropy of the fluorophore attached to the peptide, which is initially low because of fast molecular tumbling, increases due to the longer rotational correlation time resulting from the increase in size upon complex formation. These results clearly indicate that the ASC-c peptide forms a stable interaction with ASC. It is worth noting that the difference in fluorescence polarization in the plateau regions of both binding isotherms (Fig. 12) could be due to changes with pH in the fluorophore excitation and emission behavior.

Red lines indicate the fit of the data to a simple binding isotherm used to determine the KD values (0.050 μM at pH 5.0, and 0.434 μM at pH 3.8). The equilibrium dissociation constants obtained by fitting the binding isotherms are 0.050 μM at pH 5.0 and 0.434 μM at pH 3.8 (Fig. 12). These data provide evidence of high affinity of the ASC-c peptide for ASC at pH 5.0 and indicate that the affinity is pH dependent. The pH dependence can be explained by the presence of electrostatic interactions between the peptide and the protein upon complex formation. In fact, based on our NMR data, we have identified amino acids involved in the ASC-ASC-c interaction that can change their protonation state in the 3.8 to 5.0 pH range, e.g., E18, E19, D48, D134, D191, and E193. The population of the negatively charged form of the amino acid increases at more basic pH values that are close to the amino acid’s pKa, thus facilitating electrostatic interactions. Overall, the fluorescence polarization results indicate that the ASC-c peptide is a reliable starting point for designing molecules capable of interacting with ASC for potential therapeutic purposes. The next step is to test whether the ASC-c peptide has an inhibitory effect on inflammasome activation.

Inhibitory effect of the ASC-c peptide on inflammasome activity

Inflammasome activity can be monitored by the levels of IL-1β released by immune cells in response to a danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) or a pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) (1, 7, 8, 40, 41). To test a potential effect of the ASC-c peptide on inflammasome activation, we derived macrophages from THP-1 monocytes and triggered the inflammasome response with the PAMP lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and the ionophore nigericin. This treatment induces K+ efflux from the cell, which activates the NLRP3 inflammasome sensor (75, 76). NLRP3 oligomerization leads to ASC recruitment and self-assembly, followed by procaspase-1 activation. The active procaspase-1 cleaves IL-1β, which is released to the extracellular environment upon cell death by pyroptosis.

Firstly, we treated the THP-1-derived macrophages with 50 μM and 100 μM ASC-c peptide solutions in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for 24 h and studied the potential cytotoxicity effects using live/dead assays. We found close to 100% live cells across treatment conditions in the flow cytometer data, which indicates that the ASC-c peptide is not cytotoxic at the concentrations tested (Fig. S8). After peptide treatment, we primed and activated the NLRP3 inflammasome with LPS and nigericin, respectively, and determined the levels of IL-1β in the supernatants by ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay). We observed a statistically significant decrease in IL-1β (p = 0.04) in the presence of the ASC-c peptide at 50 μM compared to the control (Fig. 13). However, further increasing the peptide concentration to 100 μM does not lead to a statistically significant reduction of IL-1β compared to the control under the predefined threshold (p < 0.05). This result may reflect biological variability and/or a non-linear concentration-response relationship rather than a strictly monotonic dose effect.

At higher cell passage numbers, a statistically significant decrease in the overall IL-1β levels was not observed in any treatment type (Fig. S9). Initial IL-1β concentrations of several 1000 pg/mL, specifically 2,200 pg/mL, drop to 400 pg/mL as cell passage number increases. This is likely caused by cell age and stress.

Overall, these data demonstrate that the ASC-c peptide is not cytotoxic and exerts an inhibitory effect on inflammasome activity. Our results suggest that the ASC-c peptide could be used as a lead in drug design strategies.

Physiological relevance of the results

Most of the experiments have been performed under acidic conditions, which might raise concerns about the physiological relevance of the results.

We have demonstrated by NMR that ASC, ASC-b, and ASC-c are properly folded at acidic conditions (48, 49, 50). Their role in inflammasome formation is to engage in Death Domain-Death Domain binding and polymerization. Structural studies have shown that electrostatic interactions between positively and negatively charged regions of the Death Domains, together with the hydrophobic effect, govern binding (31, 67, 68, 71). Working at pH 3.8 decreases the probability of electrostatic interactions with the positively charged side chains. Thus, acidic conditions only diminish the interacting capabilities of the Death Domains and shift the equilibrium of complex formation toward the monomeric species. However, complex formation is not prevented and can be favored by increasing the protein concentration and/or the pH.

We have reported the formation of early ASC oligomers caused by a slow pH increase from 3.0 to 4.1 and demonstrated more robust polymerization at pH 6.5 and 7.5 by TEM (64). Additionally, NMR studies showed that increasing the concentration of ASC and ASC-b at pH 3.8 promotes oligomerization (48, 49). Furthermore, our fluorescence anisotropy studies show that the ASC-c peptide interacts with ASC at pH 3.8 and pH 5.0, with a larger binding affinity at the higher pH value. Consequently, our findings on the structure of ASC-c and the identification of amino acids involved in the interaction with ASC at acidic pH, as well as its ability to polymerize at physiological pH, as observed by TEM, can be extrapolated to physiological conditions. Importantly, we have demonstrated the inhibitory action of the ASC-c peptide in cells under physiological conditions.

Discussion

Inflammation is a vital defense mechanism that promotes survival by targeting infections and injuries, but it becomes damaging when chronically activated. Sustained inflammation disrupts normal cellular physiology, damaging tissues and organs, and therefore significantly impacts health and lifespan across age groups. It is a leading global health concern, accounting for more than 50% of deaths worldwide, as reported by the World Health Organization, and continues to drive disease progression globally (77).

Given the significant impact of inflammatory diseases on global public health, a detailed understanding of the precise mechanisms of inflammasome assembly, activation, and regulation is crucial. Within this context, ASC serves as a central adaptor protein in inflammasome activation and is increasingly recognized as a therapeutic target for chronic inflammatory diseases. Despite the critical role of ASC isoforms in modulating inflammasome activation and innate immune responses, the molecular mechanisms and structural characteristics of ASC isoforms remain largely unexplored. While our previous work provided a comprehensive structural characterization of the ASC-b isoform (48), knowledge of the structure, functional interactions, and mechanistic roles of ASC-c is scarce. In this study, we investigated the structural characteristics of ASC-c and its interaction with ASC at the molecular level to understand the mechanism underlying its inhibitory function. Our findings also highlight the potential of ASC-c as a novel target for designing small, peptide-based drugs aimed at modulating inflammasome activation.

Our NMR studies reveal that ASC-c retains structural similarities to ASC in its CARD and linker region but shows a distinct arrangement of the PYD. Unlike ASC’s PYD, which follows the canonical six-helix bundle typical of the Death Domain superfamily (70, 71), ASC-c’s PYD comprises only two helices. This structural divergence could underlie ASC-c’s role as an inflammasome inhibitor, in comparison to canonical ASC and the ASC-b isoform, which are inflammasome activators. The structurally conserved CARD of ASC-c suggests that it may still participate in homotypic CARD-CARD interactions with ASC, procaspase-1, or other CARD-containing proteins. However, ASC-c’s partial PYD may hinder efficient binding to sensors carrying a PYD. This hypothesis aligns with a previous study demonstrating colocalization of ASC-c with inflammasome components (41). This structural variation implies that while ASC-c may participate in similar signaling pathways, it likely has a modulatory, rather than an initiating, role in inflammasome assembly.

In addition to its distinct structural characteristics, ASC-c macrostructural organization differs from ASC and ASC-b (48, 64). Our ns-TEM analysis reveals that ASC-c polymerizes into filaments that assemble into an interlocking network of bundled structures. These bundles are wider than those formed by ASC-b while maintaining filament widths comparable to ASC. The average polydispersity index of ASC-c oligomers suggests an intermediate level of homogeneity, indicating they are more uniform than those of ASC-b, though slightly less so than ASC. These findings explain the differential oligomeric distributions and assembly of ASC-c in the cellular environment, where it forms more cohesive filamentous bundles within the cytosol (41).

Our CSP analysis provides residue-level insights into ASC and ASC-c interactions, highlighting specific key binding sites. NMR titration experiments using unlabeled ASC-c with labeled ASC revealed significant CSPs in both the PYD and CARD regions of ASC, with particularly pronounced effects observed in the PYD domain. PYD-PYD and CARD-CARD homotypic interactions mediate ASC self-association, suggesting that ASC-c binding potentially influences ASC filament formation. Further analysis of the CSP data from titrations of unlabeled ASC with ASC-c reveals perturbations in both domains of ASC-c. The binding interfaces involve helices α2 and α3 of ASC PYD, helices α2, α5, and α6 of the ASC CARD, and helices α1 and α2 of the ASC-c partial PYD, and helices α1, α4, α5, and α6 of the ASC-c CARD. Overall, these results indicate a multifaceted interaction between ASC and ASC-c involving various PYD and CARD regions of each protein. This aligns with the PYD-PYD and CARD-CARD associations characteristic of inflammasome assembly, supporting robust signal transduction in response to inflammatory stimuli (71).

Previous NMR studies have shown that the PYDs of ASC and NLRP3 interact through helices α2 and α3, which are also involved in ASC PYD self-association, but with a higher affinity for the former interaction (KD ∼ 22 μM) (67). This suggests that while NLRP3 binds effectively to ASC PYD, it does not disrupt its self-association. Instead, the presence of NLRP3 may enhance the stability of ASC oligomers, facilitating the formation of larger complexes required for active inflammasome assembly (76, 78). In contrast, the interaction of ASC with ASC-c appears to reduce ASC self-association, leading to inflammasome inhibition (41). Overall, our data suggest that ASC-c binding to ASC interferes with ASC filament formation, likely through competitive binding at the PYD and CARD self-association interfaces. This disruption could impair ASC's ability to recruit essential inflammasome components, sensor and effector molecules, thereby modulating inflammasome activity.

Furthermore, the dissociation constant (KD) values offer helpful insights into the binding affinities among inflammasome components, highlighting distinct interaction profiles that impact functional outcomes. For example, the average KD values for ASC PYD-PYD and CARD-CARD interactions are approximately 65 μM and 50 μM, respectively (64, 67). The ASC PYD and NLRP3 PYD interaction shows an average KD of 22 μM, reflecting a stronger affinity that aligns with NLRP3’s role in efficiently recruiting ASC to facilitate inflammasome assembly (67). The RIP2 CARD and caspase 1 CARD complex has been reported to have a KD ∼ 1.4 μM (79), and the Apaf-1 CARD and procaspase 9 CARD interaction shows a KD of ∼ 0.7 μM (80). These values suggest moderate to high binding affinities during inflammasome assembly. In comparison, we found by NMR an apparent KD in the range of 170 to 340 μM for the ASC-ASC-c interaction. These values are higher than those reported at physiological pH because our NMR experiments were conducted under acidic conditions, thereby reducing the contribution of electrostatic interactions to protein–protein binding and polymerization.

In contrast, the KD value for the complex between the ASC-c peptide and ASC determined here by fluorescence polarization is 0.050 μM at pH 5.0, indicating a strong interaction, and 0.434 μM at pH 3.8. The latter KD is significantly smaller than the range of 170 to 340 μM for the ASC-ASC-c interaction determined by NMR at the same pH, indicating that the peptide binds with higher affinity. The discrepancy between the KD values of the ASC-c/ASC and ASCc-peptide/ASC complexes can be explained by the absence of self-association processes competing with heteromolecular binding in the latter, as the peptide does not oligomerize.

Notably, POP1 (also known as ASC2), an inhibitor of ASC, exhibits a strong interaction with ASC, with a KD of 4 μM for POP1-ASC PYD interaction (62). The difference in binding affinities between ASC-c and POP1 reflects their distinct roles in inflammasome regulation. POP1's high-affinity inhibition suggests a direct inhibitory function in inflammasome formation under certain conditions, while ASC-c’s lower affinity may enable transient modulation, facilitating reversible regulation of inflammasome assembly dynamics.

In addition to the ASC-c isoform, POPs and NLRC3 also act as endogenous inhibitors of ASC. As discussed above, POPs, particularly POP1 and POP2, inhibit ASC inflammasome formation by competing for the PYD interface essential for ASC self-association and NLRP3 interaction (33, 34). NLRC3, a member of the NLR family, acts as a negative regulator of inflammasomes (81). It interacts with ASC and procaspase-1 and competes with ASC for binding to procaspase-1, thereby disrupting speck formation and reducing IL-1β secretion (82).

Several exogenous ASC inhibitors have also been developed. For example, the alpaca-derived single-domain antibody VHHASC selectively targets the ASC CARD domain through a type II interface, effectively inhibiting inflammasome activation (83). Humanized monoclonal antibody IC100, binds to ASC-PYD filaments, disrupting their structure and thereby limiting inflammasome formation (84). The small molecule MM01 disrupts ASC oligomerization and speck formation in vitro and in vivo by likely interacting with the ASC CARD domain (85). Lonidamine serves as another inflammasome inhibitor, directly targeting the ASC CARD to block ASC oligomerization and prevent ASC recruitment of pro-caspase-1 (86). A spirodalesol analog binds to ASC in the nucleus, attenuating its activity and preventing recruitment by NLRP3 (87). Clinical studies are still needed to confirm the safe use of these small molecules.

Developing small and short-lived inflammasome inhibitors is beneficial because their effects wear off quickly, allowing for easy reversibility in case of an infection. Building on our finding that the ASC-c partial PYD is capable of binding with ASC, we designed the ASC-c peptide as a potential therapeutic to target ASC-mediated disorders. Previous research has shown that a truncated form of NLRP2P, known as POP4, which is comprised of 45 amino acids, forms helices α1 and α2 of the PYD, similar to the ASC-c PYD (88). POP4 does not interact with NLRP3 or ASC but is capable of inhibiting NF-κB activity in response to TNFα (89). Analogous to POP1, POP2 contains six α-helices forming a DD fold that binds with ASC and prevents its recruitment to upstream PYD-containing sensors (34, 89). These studies showed that only the helices α1 and α2 are necessary for the full functionality of POP2 and POP4. We have demonstrated in cell-based assays that the ASC-c peptide, comprising helices α1, α2, and the first turn of helix α6 of the ASC-c PYD, inhibits inflammasome activity in macrophages. These results point to the ASC-c peptide as a promising lead in the design of anti-inflammatory drugs.

Similar to our peptide design, in another in vivo study, two peptides were engineered based on helices α2 and α3 of the ASC PYD and CARD (90). Both peptides show concentration-dependent inhibition of IL-1β release following stimulation with various NLRP3 inflammasome activators. Additionally, this research demonstrated that the ASC PYD-α2-α3 peptide, when conjugated with either Antennapedia or Angiopep - 2 tags, crosses the blood–brain barrier, effectively inhibiting IL-1β release in mouse microglial cells. This highlights a potential pathway for delivering ASC-targeting treatments directly to the central nervous system to modulate neuroinflammation.

Our CSP data from titrations of the unlabeled peptide with ASC show perturbations across both ASC domains. The interaction predominantly affects the PYD but also suggests a heterotypic interaction with ASC CARD, involving the same key interfaces observed in the ASC:ASC-c interaction. Heterotypic interactions within the Death Domain family underscore the complexity and regulatory mechanisms governing inflammasome signaling pathways. Zhou et al. identified several novel heterotypic interactions among human Death Domain superfamily proteins (70). Utilizing amplified luminescence proximity homogeneous assay and co-immunoprecipitation assays, they identified and validated heterotypic interactions between inflammasome-related proteins, such as NLRC3 CARD–NLRP4 PYD and NLRC5 CARD–NLRP9 PYD. Our recent studies also demonstrated hydrogel formation through heterotypic interaction between an engineered CARD-only protein and the same ASC-c-derived peptide used in this study (63). Altogether, this evidence emphasizes the versatile nature of inflammasome signaling, where heterotypic interactions play a vital role in maintaining flexibility and specificity in immune responses.

Experimental procedures

Expression and purification of ASC and ASC-c

DNA sequences encoding full-length human ASC and ASC-c DNA sequences with an N-terminal 6X His-tag were cloned into pET-15b vectors and transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells for protein expression. The transformed cells were plated on an agar plate supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin and incubated overnight at 37 °C. A single, well-isolated colony was then selected and inoculated into 250 mL of LB medium containing ampicillin. The culture was incubated overnight with agitation at 220 rpm, at 37 °C for ASC and 30 °C for ASC-c. Subsequently, this overnight seed culture was transferred to 2 L of LB medium for large-scale cultivation and further incubated until reaching an OD600 in the range of 0.6 to 0.8. For NMR experiments, cells were cultured in M9 minimal media containing 48 mM Na2HPO4, 22 mM KH2PO4 (pH 7.2), 9 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgSO4, 1 mM CaCl2, 100 μg/mL ampicillin, 1X MEM vitamin solution, 19 mM 15NH4Cl and unlabeled or 13C-labeled 22 mM glucose. Protein expression was induced by adding 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), followed by incubation for 2 to 3 h at 37 °C and 220 rpm. The overexpression of ASC and ASC-c was confirmed by SDS-PAGE. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8,000 rpm and 4 °C for 30 min.

For protein purification, the pellet was resuspended in a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole and 6 M guanidine-HCl at pH 8.0, with the addition of 500 μM tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP-HCl) and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Cells were lysed by sonication followed by centrifugation at 35,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The post-lysis supernatant was filtered through a 0.2 μm filter and loaded onto a pre-packed HisTrap 5 mL affinity column. The protein was eluted with a buffer containing 6 M guanidine-HCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole, and 500 μM TCEP-HCl at pH 8, at a flow rate of 1 mL/min using an HPLC system. Protein fractions were collected and dialyzed against water supplemented with TCEP-HCl at pH 3.8 to reduce the denaturant concentration. The protein solutions were further purified via reverse-phase chromatography using a C4 column pre-equilibrated with 5% acetonitrile, 94.9% H2O, and 0.1% TFA. Proteins were then eluted from the column using a gradient of 5% water, 94.9% acetonitrile, and 0.1% TFA, at a flow rate of 5 mL/min using HPLC. Eluted protein fractions were collected and lyophilized.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

A 50 μM ASC-c sample was prepared in a buffer containing 6 M guanidine-HCl, 20 mM Tris (pH 7), and 100 mM NaCl. The sample was dialyzed three times against a buffer containing 20 mM Tris (pH 7) and 100 mM NaCl, with each dialysis step lasting 3 h for equilibration. To prepare the TEM sample, 4 μL of precipitated protein solution was deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to sit for 10 min. The grid was then washed three times with 40 μL droplets of HPLC water, each for 10 s. Subsequently, the grid was stained three times with 40 μL droplets of 2% uranyl acetate for 10 s each, followed by an incubation period of 5 min. The excess staining solution was carefully wiped off. Images of ASC-c filaments were captured using a Talos F200C G2 Transmission Electron Microscope equipped with a Field Emission Gun (X-FEG) operating at 200 kV. Image acquisition was performed using a Ceta 16M Camera with a resolution of 4k × 4 pixels. Analysis of ASC-c filament and bundle dimensions was carried out using Fiji (99).

Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

ASC-c was prepared at concentrations of 700 μM and 350 μM in HPLC water at pH 3.8. Experiments were conducted using a Malvern analytical Zetasizer Pro instrument equipped with a 633 nm laser. The solvent had a refractive index of 1.33, and the absorbance at 633 nm was measured to be 0.01. A 200 μL protein solution was placed in a ZEN0040 cuvette, and data were collected in triplicate at specific time intervals after sample preparation, including 30 min, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 10 h, 16 h, 31 h, 44 h and 46 h. At each time point, the sample equilibrated for 2 min in the instrument chamber before data acquisition. The particle size distribution and polydispersity were estimated from autocorrelation analysis of scattered light intensity at different time points using the built-in ZS Xplorer software. The data were then plotted using Origin software.

Molecular docking

The molecular docking of the ASC-c peptide with the ASC PYD and CARD domains was conducted using the HADDOCK web server (72). The full-length ASC structure (PDB 2KN6) was truncated from residues 1 to 91 for the PYD domain and residues 113 to 195 for the CARD domain and generated corresponding PDB files using PyMOL (100). The 3D structure model of the ASC-c peptide was predicted from primary sequences using the I-TASSER program (69).

Experimental restraints for the ASC docking to the ASC-c peptide included residues with chemical shift values greater than 1.5 times the standard deviation in the ASC-peptide NMR titration. These active residues were clustered at helices α2 and α3 (T16, A17, E18, E19, L25, K26, V30, G35, S46 and D48) of the PYD and helices α5 and α6 (N170, K174, Y187, V189, D191, E193, R194 and S195) of the CARD, with a solvent exposure index >20% calculated with GETAREA (101). For the peptide, previously reported restraints based on studies on ASC self-association with >30% solvent exposure were used, including α1-helix (G2, R3, D6, D10, and T16) and α2-helix (E19, K22, K24, and L25) (53).

The docking calculations generated and scored 1000 docked structures in the rigid body docking iteration (it0), refined 200 structures in the semi-flexible refinement iteration (it1), and evaluated 200 structures in the final analysis. Solvated docking was performed using water as the solvent for all iterations. HADDOCK identified the top 10 clusters from which the top 3 clusters with the maximum buried surface area were further examined in PyMOL (100). The structure with the highest buried surface area was selected in each cluster (a total of three structures for the ASC-c peptide:ASC PYD and ASC-c peptide:ASC CARD complexes, respectively) as the starting structure to perform molecular dynamics simulation studies.

Molecular dynamics

We performed all-atom classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations on the ASC-ASC-c complexes determined by HADDOCK. Specifically, we started with the three best structures from HADDOCK (see Molecular Docking) for ASC-c peptide bound to the PYD domain and to the CARD domain, for a total of 6 MD simulations. The starting structures for ASC obtained from the HADDOCK calculations were truncated from residues 1 to 91 for the PYD and 113 to 195 for the CARD to reduce the overall size of the system for the MD simulations.

After a series of energy minimization and equilibration steps, we ran 1 μs of dynamics. The net charge on the complexes was neutralized by adding Na+ or Cl- ions, and additional Na+/Cl- pairs were added to yield an ion concentration of approximately 70 mM in the system. All simulations were run in the NPT ensemble using Gromacs 2016.3 (102) with the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field and the TIP3P water model (103, 104). The integration was performed using the Verlet cutoff script and a 2-fs integration time step. We used the LINCS algorithm (105) to constrain all bonds to hydrogen to their equilibrium length, the Bussi et al. (106) thermostat was used to maintain a temperature of 300 K, and the Parrinello-Rahman barostat (107) was used to maintain an average pressure of 1 bar.

The secondary structure analysis of both the ASC-c peptide and ASC domains was done on structures sampled from the MD trajectories using the Define Secondary Structure of Proteins (DSSP) algorithm, which analyzes the hydrogen bond patterns along the protein backbone and categorizes each residue into one of eight types of secondary structure, including α-helices and β-sheets. These assignments are typically presented as graphs with the simulation time on the X-axis, the residue number on the Y-axis, and distinct colors for each secondary structure assignment in the field of the plot. Persistent secondary structure assignments are therefore presented as solid-colored stripes running across the plots.

Inter-protein “contact maps” were generated by sampling the MD structures every 2 ns for the final 500 ns of the full 1 μs trajectory. A total of 250 structures from each of the six structures simulated were analyzed using the web application PDBePISA (73) (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/pisa/), which identifies the hydrogen bonds and salt bridges (hydrogen bonds between oppositely charged amino acids). We automated this process using the Selenium web-interface library in a Python script. The same structures were analyzed using a home-written Python script to identify all close contacts (within 2.8 Å) between amino acids on the ASC-c and ASC domains. The results are plotted as “contact” maps shown in Figures 11 and S5. The red lines indicate hydrogen bonds (including salt bridges), and the dashed black lines indicate close contacts between the ASC-c and ASC domains. Lines are plotted only for contacts that are present in at least half of the sampled frames.

Fluorescence polarization

Labeling of the ASC-c peptide with the fluorophore Alexa Fluor 488

The lyophilized ASC-c peptide (1 mg) with an extra cysteine amino acid at the C-terminus for maleimide reaction was dissolved in 500 μL of 50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.2. Alexa Fluor 488 maleimide (0.4 mg) was dissolved in 50 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide, then diluted into 450 μL of Tris-HCl buffer at pH 7.2. Both solutions were combined for final concentrations of 301 μM peptide and 602 μM Alexa Fluor 488 maleimide. The reaction mixture was gently rotated on a Fisher brand rotator at 40 rpm for 4 h at room temperature. The reaction was halted and purified via reverse-phase chromatography using an Agilent Zorbax 300 SB-C18 column. The column was equilibrated in HPLC-grade water with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. The labeled peptide was eluted using a 1 h gradient from 0% to 100% of a 49.9/50 mixture of HPLC-grade water and acetonitrile containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. Peptide labeling was confirmed by mass spectrometry. Fractions containing the labeled peptide were pooled and lyophilized with a Labconco FreeZone lyophilizer.

Preparation of ASC and ASC-c-Alexa Fluor 488 peptide mixtures for fluorescence polarization

The lyophilized, fluorescently labeled ASC-c peptide was resuspended in 1 mL of 50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.2, and a peptide concentration of 4.52 μM was determined by absorbance spectroscopy at 493 nm. Stock solutions of the peptide were prepared at a concentration of 20 nM by a series of dilutions into a buffer containing 500 μM TCEP-HCl at pH 3.8 and 5.0. Lyophilized ASC was dissolved in a buffer containing 500 μM TCEP at pH 3.8. The solution pH was gradually raised to either pH 3.8 or 5.0 using 0.05 mM NaOH, then stored at 25 °C for 72 h. After this incubation period, the sample was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 2 min in an Eppendorf Centrifuge model 5418 to remove particles and precipitated material. The final ASC concentration was determined by absorbance spectroscopy at 280 nm. A stock of double the maximum desired concentration was then prepared in 500 μM TCEP-HCl at the final experimental pH: 20.4 μM at pH 5.0 and 240 μM at pH 3.8. A volume of 200 μL of an ASC stock was added to the first well of each row in a Thermo Scientific black 96-well plate. A serial dilution was then done in the plate wells using 500 μM TCEP-HCl at the target pH, decreasing the concentration by 50% until the final concentration, for which 100 μL of the diluted ASC solution was set aside, thus leaving all wells with a volume of 100 μL. All ASC solutions in the respective wells were thoroughly mixed by pipetting before subsequent transfers to ensure homogeneity. The plate was transferred to a dark room, where 100 μL of the fluorescent ASC-c peptide stock was added to each well except for the negative controls. Inflammasome activation experiments

THP-1 monocytes (ATCC TIB - 202, of authenticity validated by short tandem repeat profiling, as indicated in the specifications) were cultured in ATCC-formulated Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (ATCC) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Life Technologies) and 100 units/ml Penicillin-Streptomycin (Life Technologies). Cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator and then plated in 6-well plates at a cell concentration of 106 cells/mL. Differentiation into macrophages was induced by overnight treatment with 50 nM PMA (Phorbol 12-Myristate 13-Acetate) in DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide). Cells were washed with PBS (Phosphate Buffered Saline) to remove PMA, the media was replaced, and cells were incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, adherent macrophages were washed with PBS and detached from the plates by adding 1 mL of 0.25 mM trypsin∖EDTA for 20 min. Trypsin∖EDTA was then neutralized by adding 2 mL of culture media. Detached cells were harvested and centrifuged at 400g for 5 min to form a pellet, which was resuspended in media, followed by cell counting.

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a concentration of 105 cells/well in 100 μL and incubated for 24 h with 50 μM and 100 μM ASC-c peptide solutions in DMSO, resulting in a final volume of 200 μL in the wells. After incubation, cells were harvested and washed with 100 μL PBS to remove the treatment conditions. Macrophages were subjected to 4 h treatment with 100 ng/mL LPS (lipopolysaccharide) for inflammasome priming, followed by 1 h treatment with 50 μL of 5 μM nigericin for NLRP3 inflammasome activation. The cell suspensions were removed from the wells and placed in new 96-well plates with conical bottoms for centrifugation to pellet any cells and cell debris. DMSO treatments were included as negative controls to compare with the peptide treatments.

The supernatants were removed from the plates after centrifugation and stored at 4 °C overnight. Released IL-1β present in the supernatants was detected by ELISA using a Biotek Epoch microplate reader. We used Human IL-1β uncoated ELISA kit with plates (Invitrogen). To perform the ELISA, the plate was coated with 100 μL/well of capture antibody in diluted coating buffer, sealed, and incubated overnight at 4 °C. All wells were aspirated and washed three times with more than 250 μL/well of wash buffer, allowing approximately 1 min of soaking in each wash step. The plate was blotted on absorbent paper. Wells were blocked with 200 μL of 1X ELISA diluent, incubated at room temperature for 1 h, and covered. We added 100 μL/well of the supernatant samples to the appropriate wells. The plate was sealed and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After five washing and aspiration cycles, the antibody solution (100 μL/well) was added to the plate, sealed, and incubated for 1 h. The plate wells were washed and aspirated five times, followed by the addition of 100 μL/well of diluted Avidin-HRP (horseradish peroxidase). The plate was sealed and incubated at room temperature for 30 min, followed by seven washing and aspiration cycles. Subsequently, we added 100 μL/well of 1X TMB (3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine) solution and incubated at room temperature for 15 min, followed by the addition of 100 μL/well of stop solution. Cells were incubated in the staining solution for 15 min at room temperature before being analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD LSR II flow cytometer with 488 nm laser excitation and 530/30 (green) and 610/20 (red) bandpass emission filters. All analysis was performed using FlowJo v10 (BD Biosciences) and Prism v6 (GraphPad) software.

Statistical comparisons between treatment groups and vehicle control were performed using two-tailed unpaired t-tests assuming unequal variances (Welch’s t test). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Data shown are from one representative experiment with technical replicates.
