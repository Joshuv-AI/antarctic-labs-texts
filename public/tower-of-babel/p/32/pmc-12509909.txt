Protocol for quantifying PARP inhibitor-induced changes in PARP1 dynamics and activity through live-cell imaging
Authors: Petar-Bogomil Kanev, Sylvia Varhoshkova, Aleksander Aleksandrov, Stoyno Stoynov, Radoslav Aleksandrov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryPoly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) is a crucial DNA repair protein and a target of PARP inhibitors (PARPi), which suppress its catalytic activity while also altering its association with damaged chromatin. Here, we present a live-cell imaging protocol for obtaining high-quality kinetics of fluorescently labeled PARP1 and poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR)-binding proteins at micro-irradiation-induced DNA damage sites in untreated and PARPi-treated cells. This approach can be easily adapted to other DNA repair proteins to gain insight into the mechanism of action of diverse DNA repair-targeting drugs.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Kanev et al.1Subject areas: Biophysics, Cell Biology, Cell culture, Cell-based Assays, Cancer, Microscopy, Molecular Biology

Before you begin

Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) acts as a sensor for single- and double-strand DNA breaks.2 Binding to broken DNA ends triggers PARP1 catalytic activity, resulting in the synthesis of long, negatively-charged poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR) chains that are attached onto various target proteins, including PARP1 itself.3 Chromatin PARylation signals the presence of DNA breaks and serves as a recruitment platform for a broad set of PAR-binding proteins.4 PARP1 is targeted by small-molecule inhibitors (PARPi), which suppress its catalytic activity.2 Several PARPi are clinically approved, most prominently for the treatment of BRCA1/2-mutated breast, ovarian, and prostate cancer.5 It is now well-established that, independent of BRCA status, PARPi cytotoxicity most strongly correlates with their ability to enhance PARP1 binding to damaged DNA, an effect referred to as “PARP trapping”.1,6 Various methods have been employed to evaluate PARP trapping,7,8,9,10 leading to multiple mechanistic models that warranted reconciliation.

Recently, we employed a combination of high-speed live-cell imaging, precise UV laser micro-irradiation (IR), fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP), and mathematical modeling to provide a unified mechanism that explains the prolonged presence of PARP1 molecules at DNA damage sites by taking into consideration both PARPi-induced catalytic inhibition and trapping.1 Specifically, we demonstrated that both catalytic inhibition and slower exchange at lesions contribute to the overall PARPi-induced PARP1 retention at damaged chromatin. In turn, greater retention tightly corresponds to a delay in downstream DNA repair events and PARPi cytotoxicity. The protocol described herein yields three straightforward and easy-to-obtain measures of PARP1 behavior, which faithfully quantify changes in PARP1 catalytic activity and dynamics upon PARPi treatment.1 Several key features distinguish our protocol from commonly employed micro-irradiation procedures used in the field. The most important of these are: (1) the analysis of live cells at high (sub-second) temporal resolution using spinning-disk confocal imaging with no detectable photobleaching and/or phototoxicity, (2) the use of stable transgenic cell lines, expressing the respective proteins of interest (POIs) at near-physiological levels, (3) precise and gentle UV laser micro-irradiation in a small and well-defined nuclear region, (4) no pre-treatment with DNA damage-sensitizing compounds except for the drugs under study, (5) robust and well-automated image analysis sequence, yielding high-quality kinetic data, (6) the use of biologically meaningful mathematical models to describe experimental data and extract important parameters, granting a robust quantification of DDR protein dynamics beyond the frequently-used “inspection by eye”. We present a detailed dissection of our approach, which can be promptly repurposed for the study of other DNA repair proteins and/or DNA repair-targeting compounds, offering a unique view into their mechanisms of action. While the implementation of this protocol describes the use of HeLa Kyoto cells, we have applied this protocol in various other cell lines, including PC3, DLD1, and Cal51,1,11 obtaining highly reproducible results provided that the experimental conditions are kept uniform.

Generation of stable cell lines using BAC transgenes

Timing: 2–3 weeks

Stable HeLa Kyoto cell lines expressing fluorescently-tagged POIs from bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) transgenes are used for most experiments reported herein.1,12 BAC vectors can accommodate large fragments of DNA, including full-length eukaryotic genes with their promoters and intronic sequences.13,14 Consequently, POIs are expressed at near-physiological levels in all of their splice isoforms and under native cellular control13,15 (Figure S1A). The stable cell lines expressing fluorescently labeled POIs from BAC transgenes retain their non-labeled endogenous counterparts. However, we confirmed that the presence of endogenous, non-labeled PARP1 does not affect in any way the kinetics of PARP1-EGFP (Figure S1B). Notably, CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing has recently become significantly more accessible for endogenous protein tagging, including the tagging of DNA repair proteins.8,16,17

Note: HeLa Kyoto cells, besides being undemanding and inexpensive to culture, are particularly suitable for extended time-lapse imaging involving laser IR as they are “slow movers” with relatively large and unflattened nuclei, which greatly facilitates downstream image analysis.

Generation of transiently transfected cells using plasmid DNA

Timing: 3–4 days

While widely used, transient transfection with plasmids expressing fluorescently-tagged DNA repair POIs under the control of strong promoters with viral origin suffers from a number of disadvantages.19,20 First, POI overexpression may compromise the process(es), which the tagged protein participates in (Figures 1I–1K). Consider that overexpression is often used to force an effect or phenotype. Second, high levels of POI expression are frequently deleterious to cells, adversely affecting cellular physiology (Figure 1I, white arrows point to dead/dying cells). Third, the expression levels of POIs from transiently-transfected vectors may change drastically in the course of time-lapse imaging experiments, which distorts downstream image analysis steps. These effects need to be mitigated as much as possible (see problem 1, troubleshooting).

Step-by-step method details

Quantitative analysis of PARP1 kinetics at IR-induced DNA damage sites following PARPi treatment (PRC estimation)

Timing: 3–5 days are required prior to the first IR experiment. Thereafter, multiple experiments can be performed in a single day, depending on the PARPi treatment conditions.

PARP inhibitors (PARPi) prolong gross PARP1 residence at IR-induced DNA damage sites. This step, involving estimation of the PARP1 Retention Coefficient (PRC) following PARPi treatment, enables precise quantification of alterations in PARP1 retention at IR foci in PARPi-treated cells compared to untreated controls. From an imaging point of view PRC is calculated as the ratio between the halftime of PARP1 foci dissolution in PARPi-treated cells versus the halftime of PARP1 foci dissolution in untreated cells (Figure 2F). The higher the PRC values the longer PARP1 IR foci persist in PARPi-treated cells compared to untreated cells.1

Note: BAC-tagged cell lines may not express the respective fluorescently-labeled POIs at homogenous levels, presumably due to intrinsic differences in transcriptional control on a cell-to-cell basis. When this is the case, we recommend selecting cells with intermediate expression levels of the POI, which would facilitate downstream image analysis, particularly the segmentation and tracking of the IR foci.

2.Live-cell imaging of PARP1 recruitment and removal at IR-induced DNA damage sites.Note: Spinning-disk confocal microscopy (SDCM) is particularly suitable for the study of dynamic molecular processes in living cells, since it delivers rapid acquisition over extended periods of time with minimal photobleaching and phototoxicity.24,25 Our imaging setup includes an Andor Revolution spinning-disk confocal system, equipped with a Nikon ECLIPSE Ti microscope, an Andor Borealis module, improving excitation uniformity, a Yokogawa CSU-X1 dual spinning disk, an Andor FRAPPA (photobleaching and photoactivation) module, and an Andor iXon Ultra 897 EMCCD (Electron Multiplying Charge-Coupled Device) camera (512x512 pixels resolution, EM gain – 300, no binning) (Figure 1A). The recruitment is followed by a period of time which the protein spends at DNA damage sites (we denote this period as detachment delay, τ) (Figure 2D). The detachment delay precedes the final dissociation reaction, which causes the removal of the protein from damaged chromatin sites. CRC equations blend two separate exponential equations: the first one fits the recruitment of the protein, and the second one fits the removal of the protein. Importantly, these two exponential equations are mathematically linked by the detachment delay phase (τ) (see Table 1). Since PARP1 and PAR-binding proteins exhibit very fast kinetics, we use one of the simplest CRC equations to model their behavior, which includes only a single recruitment and a single removal reaction (Figure 2D). Quantitative analysis of PARP1 turnover dynamics at IR-induced DNA damage sites following PARPi treatment (PTC estimation)

Timing: 3–5 days are required prior to the first IR experiment. Thereafter, multiple experiments can be performed in a single day, depending on the PARPi treatment conditions.

PARPi can alter the exchange rate (turnover) of PARP1 molecules at IR-induced DNA damage sites through reverse allostery.1,9 This step, involving estimation of the PARP1 Trapping Coefficient (PTC) following PARPi treatment, enables precise quantification of alterations in PARP1 turnover at IR foci in PARPi-treated cells compared to untreated controls. PTC values are calculated by comparing the halftime of PARP1 IR foci fluorescence recovery after photobleaching in PARPi-treated cells to that in untreated cells (Figure 3F). Hence, the higher the PTC value, the longer PARP1 molecules spend in a DNA lesion-bound state, and vice versa.1

Avoid placing IR ROIs close to nucleoli and the nuclear periphery (Figure 3A).CRITICAL: PARP1 IR foci are short-lived, particularly in untreated cells. Dissolution of PARP1 IR foci begins during the recovery of PARP1-EGFP fluorescence in the bleached IR focus (Figure 3B). To account for this, a second IR focus can be induced within the same nucleus. Comparing the fluorescence intensity of PARP1-EGFP between the bleached and non-bleached IR foci allows correction for PARP1 dissociation during recovery, resulting in more accurate estimates of both the halftimes of fluorescence recovery and the extent of the mobile/immobile fraction (Figures 3A–3D).Note: PARP1 IR foci induced within the same cell exhibit essentially uniform kinetics (Figure S1C). However, PARP1 foci dynamics can vary slightly between individual cells. Therefore, for the most accurate results, we do not recommend using a PARP1 IR focus from one cell to normalize results obtained from another.c.Follow the recruitment of PARP1 at both IR-induced DNA damage sites until the kinetics reaches a plateau (maximum level of recruitment of PARP1-EGFP) (Figures 3A and 3B).d.Photobleach one of the two PARP1 IR foci precisely after they reach maximum fluorescence intensity.CRITICAL: The plateau of maximum PARP1-EGFP intensity at IR sites is short-lived (Figure 3B). Note: As PARP1 generates more than 90% of PAR chains at DNA lesions,35 nuclear PARylation levels are largely proportional to PARP1 activity. As a read-out of PARylation we use the amount of ARH3-EGFP (also known as ADPRHL2) recruited to IR-induced DNA damage sites.1 ARH3 is a PAR-degrading enzyme, which is recruited to DNA lesions in an exclusively PAR-dependent manner36,37(Figures 4A and 4E). The PARP1 Inhibition Coefficient (PIC) is calculated by comparing the amount of ARH3-EGFP recruited to IR foci in PARPi-treated cells versus that in untreated cells. Higher PIC values indicate reduced ARH3-EGFP recruitment to DNA damage sites, hence, lower PARylation levels.1 Similarly, other fluorescently-tagged PAR-binding proteins, such as XRCC1, may also be used as PAR sensors in living cells10 as well as various “artificial” sensors, representing PAR-binding motifs (WWE, PBZ, macrodomains) derived from PAR-binding proteins fused to a fluorescent reporter.38,39,40,41,42 Crucially, as all stable cell lines we use express the respective POIs, including ARH3-EGFP, at near-physiological levels, we do not encounter protein overexpression artifacts (see problems 1 and 4, troubleshooting).

Expected outcomes

Laser micro-irradiation combined with live-cell imaging is widely employed in the study of DNA repair protein dynamics.43 However, faithful evaluation requires a number of aspects to be carefully considered. The protocol presented here offers a straightforward approach to elucidating PARP1 dynamics and activity in living cells, which rests on a solid quantitative basis. If the major features of this protocol are implemented, it should yield precise quantification of PARP1 kinetics (gross recruitment and removal), exchange rate (turnover) and catalytic activity (PARylation levels) at IR-induced DNA damage sites in vivo, thus delivering the most significant features of PARP1 behavior at broken DNA ends.1 This can be achieved in untreated cells or under various treatment conditions (i.e. PARPi) as well as in diverse genetic backgrounds. The PRC, PTC, and PIC coefficients provide exact and simple means to evaluate the above-described changes in PARP1 properties. The protocol may be readily applied to PARP1 mutants to study PARP1 binding to DNA breaks,11 as well as to tackle the mechanistic basis of PARPi resistance.40 Given that PAR-binding proteins are recruited at IR-induced DNA damage sites with kinetics largely similar to that of PARP1, we contemplate that our protocol may be successfully employed to uncover the exact effects of proteins proposed to shape PARP1 behavior, as well as the PARPi response, such as HPF1,44,45,46 TRIP12,47 ALC1,27,48 and XRCC1.49 Finally, with minor adjustments, this protocol may be repurposed for the study of other DNA repair proteins and/or DNA repair-targeting compounds, offering a valuable avenue for novel mechanistic discoveries in the DDR network.

Limitations

The PRC, PTC, and PIC coefficients provide a robust quantitative framework for the study of PARP1 biology and how various stimuli—such as PARP inhibitors (PARPi)—modulate PARP1 behavior. However, these three parameters cannot be obtained simultaneously in the same cell, as they require distinct experimental workflows and are derived from different read-outs. All three coefficients are based on modeling the dynamics of either PARP1 or ARH3 at the single-cell level. While each value is calculated from a relatively small number of cells—typically 15 to 30 per coefficient per condition—they rely on high-quality kinetic data. Thus, our approach enables highly precise quantification of key properties of PARP1 molecules at damaged chromatin sites, albeit at the expense of experimental throughput. Additionally, while we have observed remarkably consistent PRC and PTC values under conditions of complete catalytic inhibition across multiple cell models beyond HeLa Kyoto - including PC3, DLD1, and Cal51 cells1,11,40 - it remains plausible that other experimental systems may yield different values. We believe that minor and straightforward modifications to the experimental procedure described herein - such as adjustments to basic imaging settings or time-lapse parameters - should be sufficient to accommodate these differences.

Problem 3

Significant photobleaching and/or phototoxicity in the course of time-lapse imaging experiments.

Potential solution

•The current experimental procedure is implemented using a spinning-disk confocal microscope, which offers minimal to no photobleaching and phototoxicity, while delivering fast acquisitions. These advantages may not be shared with other commonly used systems such as confocal laser scanning microscopes (CLSM). As a general recommendation, if photobleaching and/or phototoxicity becomes a concern, users can implement several straightforward adjustments to the protocol described herein. These include: (1) increasing frame intervals to reduce the total number of time points, (2) decreasing excitation time and/or laser power, and (3) imaging only a portion of the available field of view to accelerate the acquisition speed, while still maintaining kinetic data of good quality. In addition, PARPi treatment prolongs PARP1 residence at damaged chromatin sites (Figure 2E), and for some inhibitors acquisitions of up to 1 h are necessary.1 If appropriate, you may ascribe different frame intervals (longer intervals for longer acquisition experiments) to separate parts of the time-lapse movie in a bid to reduce photobleaching.

These include: (1) increasing frame intervals to reduce the total number of time points, (2) decreasing excitation time and/or laser power, and (3) imaging only a portion of the available field of view to accelerate the acquisition speed, while still maintaining kinetic data of good quality. In addition, PARPi treatment prolongs PARP1 residence at damaged chromatin sites (Figure 2E), and for some inhibitors acquisitions of up to 1 h are necessary.1 If appropriate, you may ascribe different frame intervals (longer intervals for longer acquisition experiments) to separate parts of the time-lapse movie in a bid to reduce photobleaching.

Problem 4

Engineered PAR sensors exhibit significant deviations in their behavior compared to ARH3-EGFP expressed at near-physiological levels.

Potential solution

•Great caution must be exercised in using “artificial” PARylation sensors, which most often are fluorescently-tagged PAR-binding domains (WWE, PBZ, macrodomains, etc.), derived from full-length PAR-binding proteins.38,39,40,41,51 We show that high PBZ-GFP expression levels significantly abrogate natural PARylation/dePARylation dynamics in living cells, presumably by outcompeting cellular PAR-binding/PAR-degrading enzymes.1 Hence, the main issue with using fluorescently-labeled PARylation sensors in vivo would be their overexpression, often encountered in transiently-transfected cells, which obstructs physiological PARylation dynamics and turnover. Therefore, we recommend using transiently-transfected cells, which express the respective engineered PAR sensors at very low levels, comparable to those of natural PAR-binding proteins/degraders, such as ARH3 (Figures 1I–1K).
