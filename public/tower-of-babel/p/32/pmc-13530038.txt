Investigation of Minimal Clinically Important Difference of the Extended Barthel Index in Inpatients With Acute to Subacute Stroke in China
Authors: Yue Sun, Ying Sun, Jiajia Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo determine the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of the Extended Barthel Index (EBI) in patients with stroke.MethodsIn total, 209 patients with stroke were enrolled from a rehabilitation hospital. The EBI was administered at baseline, day 1, and week 4 after enrolment. At week 4, occupational therapists, patients, and expense payers independently rated the perceived ability change in activities of daily living. The MCID was estimated using distribution-, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve-, anchor-, and regression-based methods.ResultsDistribution-based estimates produced small to moderate effect sizes. ROC curve analysis yielded optimal cutoffs around 2.5–3.5 points depending on the roles; sensitivity was high, whereas specificity was low. Anchor-based and regression methods produced separate thresholds for deterioration and improvement across stakeholders. Based on the anchor-based method, the recommended MCID for deterioration ranged from −2.0 to 0 points and for improvement from 4.0 to 6.0 points, varying by rater perspective.ConclusionAnchor-based MCID values are recommended for clinical use to distinguish improvement from deterioration, directly reflecting the perspectives from patients, therapists, and payers. The established thresholds can guide therapists' goal setting, progress monitoring, and rehabilitation policy decisions.Keywords: stroke, activities of daily living, Extended Barthel Index, minimal clinically important difference, distribution-based, anchor-based, simple linear regression

Introduction

Activities of daily living (ADL) evaluation is important in the functional assessment of patients with central nervous system injury. ADL evaluation scales provide a simple and effective assessment of an individual’s independence in self-care (Fallahpour et al., 2022). In clinical practice, occupational therapists are essential for evaluating and rehabilitating patients’ independence in ADL following stroke. Commonly used methods for ADL evaluation in patients with stroke include the Barthel Index (BI) (Duffy et al., 2013), modified BI (Ohura et al., 2017), and Functional Independence Measure (Lee et al., 2022), which have demonstrated reliability and validity (Leung et al., 2007; Quinn et al., 2011). The scale results can be used as a basis for patients’ “illness recovery”, “disability identification”, and “insurance compensation”, among other aspects (Clark et al., 2015).

In the 1990s, the Extended BI (EBI) was developed (Jörger et al., 2001). The EBI is a practical tool for objectively assessing functional independence, informing intervention plans, and tracking rehabilitation progress. Its value in occupational therapy lies in the inclusion of domains beyond basic self-care and mobility (Jansa et al., 2004; Jörger et al., 2001). In addition to the BI’s self-care (seven items) and mobility (three items) categories, the EBI additionally evaluates communication (Comprehension, Expression), social cognition (Social Interaction, Problem solving, Memory/Learning/Orientation), and vision (Vision). The EBI additionally includes three categories (six items), and the evaluation level of each item was adjusted from 2–4 levels to 3–5 levels relative to the BI (Jörger et al., 2001). This broader scope helps clinicians better address complex functional recovery and support both basic and instrumental ADL performance, thereby contributing to more individualised and comprehensive therapy outcomes. The EBI has demonstrated reliability and validity for the functional evaluation of patients with stroke (Jansa et al., 2004) and has been used to evaluate clinical treatment efficacy in multiple sclerosis, stroke, Parkinson’s disease, and other diseases.

In most clinical studies related to the functional rehabilitation of patients with central nervous system diseases, clinical treatment efficacy is usually inferred through hypothesis testing (p-values) of differences in scale scores between or within groups (Clark et al., 2015; Duffy et al., 2013; Fallahpour et al., 2022; Jansa et al., 2004; Jörger et al., 2001; Lee et al., 2022; Leung et al., 2007; Ohura et al., 2017; Quinn et al., 2011). In some cases, a significant outcome (<0.05 or <0.001) can be observed when the between-group difference in scale score is small, despite a lack of clinically significant change in the patient’s function (Abdallah & Brull, 2021; Embry & Piccirillo, 2020). Consequently, it is statistically proposed that scale score changes retain clinical significance (Harris et al., 2023; Kalkman, 2020); hence, the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was conceived. Salas Apaza et al. (2021) updated and explained the meaning and application of the MCID to better conduct related clinical research on functional rehabilitation after stroke. The MCID can be calculated through various methods (Embry & Piccirillo, 2020; Gatchel et al., 2010; Hsieh et al., 2007; Malec & Ketchum, 2020): in the calibration method, different calibration standards (“anchors”) are used to obtain different MCID values; in the distribution method, different intermediary indicators are used to obtain different MCID values. A meta-analysis revealed the MCID may differ across studies and depending on the measurement method applied. Engel et al. (2018) investigated the difference in consideration indicators included in the MCID calculation process.

However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have investigated the MCID of the EBI on stroke (McGlothlin & Lewis, 2014); therefore, this study aimed to investigate the MCID of the EBI in a cohort of patients with stroke.

Methods

Trial Design

This clinical, prospective, longitudinal study evaluated the MCID of the EBI assessed in a cohort of patients with stroke. Guzik et al. (2019) discussed the Wisconsin gait scale’s MCID calculation method in gait analysis; accordingly, we employed four established methods—receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve-, distribution-, anchor-based, and simple linear regression—to calculate the EBI MCID. Each method provides a different perspective: the distribution-based method relies on statistical criteria, the ROC method utilises the cutoff value that maximises the Youden index to determine the MCID, the anchor-based method incorporates patient/clinician perspectives, and anchor-based simple linear regression provides more sensitive results that can be used to verify the reliability of the anchor-based method results.

This clinical study was approved by the ethics committee of Kunshan Rehabilitation Hospital (Approval No.: KSKF2020003). All patients or their guardians provided written informed consent (trial purpose, research content, research process, inclusion and exclusion criteria, patient benefits and risks, obligations to be undertaken, privacy protection, etc.). Patients were made aware of their right to withdraw from the trial at any time.

Patients

Stroke diagnosis was based on the stroke diagnosis criteria revised by the fourth China Cerebrovascular Academic Conference in 1995 and confirmed by imaging techniques, such as computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. The patients were aged 18–80 years, and the disease course was 15–180 days. The inclusion criteria were a first case of stroke, stable condition, symptoms of paralysis in one limb, high-school education level or higher, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score of ≥18, and the ability to complete the EBI scale evaluation even with the assistance of a guardian. The exclusion criteria were a stroke diagnosis of reversible ischaemic neurological deficit or transient ischaemic attack, significantly reduced ADL due to other diseases besides stroke, post-stroke complications (cerebral oedema, intracerebral haemorrhage, epilepsy, etc.) during the study period, and obvious negative emotional difficulties or severe mental illness.

In total, 253 eligible patients with stroke were recruited from the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at Kunshan Rehabilitation Hospital in China between January 2021 and August 2021. Among them, 32 patients were excluded based on the exclusion criteria, 4 declined to participate, and 8 were lost to follow-up; ultimately, 209 patients were included. A CONSORT diagram of enrolment is shown in Figure 1. All patients underwent the clinical pathway of post-stroke rehabilitation. The patients comprised 150 men and 59 women with a mean age of 59.25 ± 13.24 years, mean time from diagnosis of 34.49 ± 11.37 days, and mean MoCA score of 25.97 ± 2.32. In total, 114 patients had left hemiplegia, and 95 patients had right hemiplegia.

Assessment Tools

At enrolment and at 1 day and 4 weeks after enrolment, doctors used the simplified EBI (https://www.cjpmr.cn/ch/reader/create_pdf.aspx?file_no=2021070602&flag=1&journal_id=zhwlyxykf&year_id=2021) to assess the patients’ ADL. At 4 weeks after enrolment, subjective evaluations of treatment efficacy based on the patients’ ADL were made considering the roles of patients, occupational therapists, and expense payers. The first scale used was the EBI (Bartolome et al., 2021), which comprises five categories (16 items), including self-care activities (Eating and Drinking, Grooming, Dressing/Undressing, Bathing, Toilet, Controlling Bowels, and Controlling Bladder), mobility activities (Moving from Wheelchair to Bed and Returning, Locomotion, and Ascending and Descending Stairs), communication (Comprehension and Expression), social cognition (Social Interaction, Problem Solving, and Memory/Learning/Orientation), and vision (Vision). The scores for each item range 0–4 points, resulting in a total scale score of 0–64 points, with higher scores indicating a closer to normal degree of independence in the patient’s ADL. The second scale used was the Clinical Global Impressions-Severity (CGI-S) to assess the curative effect on the patient’s ADL (Busner et al., 2009). As per CGI-S guidelines, based on the change in the patients’ ADL before and after enrolment, scores of 1–7 were assigned, wherein 1 = significant improvement, 2 = obvious improvement, 3 = slight improvement, 4 = no change, 5 = slight deterioration, 6 = obvious deterioration, and 7 = significant deterioration. The scoring data of the above scales were sorted and checked.

Statistical Analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 21; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for data analysis. Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation ( x¯ ±s), and count data are expressed as frequency. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to describe the test–retest reliability of the EBI, with |r| ≥ 0.75 indicating good reliability. For the distribution-based method (Malec & Ketchum, 2020), intermediate indicators (standard deviation [SD], effect size [ES], standardised response mean [SRM], and reliable change index [RCI]) were used to estimate the MCID value of the EBI. The recommended values for the ES and SRM are 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 for low, medium, and high effects, respectively. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the data distribution. ROC curve analysis was conducted for three anchors (occupational therapists, patients, and expense payers), and the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity −1) was calculated (Hassanzad & Hajian-Tilaki, 2024). For each anchor, CGI-S scores 1–3 defined “improved” and 4–7 “not improved”. Using ΔEBI score (difference at enrolment vs 4 weeks after enrolment) as the test variable, the optimal MCID cutoff value was defined as that which maximised the Youden index. For the anchor-based simple linear regression method (Bahreini et al., 2020; Pouchot et al., 2008), ΔEBI was the dependent variable, and the ADL curative effect grade was the independent variable. In the simple linear regression model, when R2 was ≥0.6, slight deterioration (5) and slight improvement (3) were observed in the regression equation, and slight deterioration and improvement in the MCID were obtained for different roles. For the anchor-based method (Hsieh et al., 2007), ΔEBI was used to calculate the calibration validity of the curative effect grade of ADL, which was expressed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient, with coefficients ≥0.35 (p < .05) indicating validity. The MCID of the EBI was estimated using the mean (or median) ΔEBI (absolute value) of patients with ADL curative effect grades of 3 or 5.

MCID Calculated Using ROC Curve Analysis

ROC curve analysis was performed using the classifications provided by occupational therapists, patients, and expense payers. The area under the curve (AUC) values for all three roles exceeded 0.99, indicating a strong discriminative ability of ΔEBI in distinguishing between improved and not improved status. However, specificity was consistently low (0.0% for occupational therapists, 3.8% for patients, and 4.5% for expense payers), suggesting that most patients deemed “not improved” had mean EBI score increases exceeding the optimal cutoffs identified for each role (3.50 points for occupational therapists, 2.50 points for patients, and 3.50 points for expense payers). The ΔEBI score of patients with an ADL efficacy grade of 3 or 5 was calculated, and the mean value was used as the MCID value. Discussion

Applying the distribution-based method resulted in EBI MCID values ranging 3.53–6.51 points. Despite showing near-perfect AUC values, the ROC curve-derived cutoffs (2.5–3.5 points) had poor specificity, suggesting that the EBI is more sensitive to subtle functional improvements than dichotomous clinical judgments (e.g., “improved” vs. “not improved”). In contrast, the anchor-based and linear regression analyses provided separate MCID thresholds for improvement (4.0–6.0 points) and deterioration (−2.0–0 points), offering greater clinical utility by distinguishing between positive and negative changes. The MCID range of the EBI is slightly broader than that reported for the original BI in patients with stroke (approximately 1.85–5.00 points) (Hsieh et al., 2007). The wider range may reflect the additional items included in the EBI—communication, social cognition, and vision—which may introduce greater score variability while enhancing sensitivity to clinically meaningful functional gains beyond basic self-care.

Previous studies have highlighted that inferences based solely on hypothesis testing of differences in clinical treatment efficacy are unscientific (Chan, 2013; Jaeschke et al., 1989) and that using the smallest clinically significant change value/MCID is necessary to further infer difference (Embry & Piccirillo, 2020; Engel et al., 2018; Gatchel et al., 2010; Hsieh et al., 2007; Malec & Ketchum, 2020).

Several factors must be considered when applying and interpreting the MCID distribution method analysis (Woaye-Hune et al., 2020). First, the MCID calculated with 0.5 SD reflects only the SD of the individual’s EBI at a certain time point, and its size is closely related to EBI variability. A relatively conservative MCID with a large value can be obtained by expanding the sample size and broadening the inclusion criteria (Dekker, 2019). Second, the MCID calculated using the RCI is essentially the test–retest reliability (internal consistency) coefficient of the evaluated individual at a certain time point multiplied by the SD of the indicator. It has been improved based on the 0.5 SD method to calculate the MCID, considering the influence of reliability; however, it still does not reflect the changes before and after the EBI evaluation. Thus, the RCI is the same as the 0.5 SD calculation method of the MCID and lacks clinical significance (Bloom et al., 2023). Thirds, the MCID calculated using the ES considers the patients’ ΔEBI score from before to after treatment. Compared with the MCID calculated using the 0.5 SD or RCI methods, it has some clinical significance; the ES of the patients’ EBI at baseline indicated a near-moderate effect. At present, most clinical studies on stroke using the EBI as the evaluation index have calculated the MCID using the ES method; nonetheless, due to heterogeneity in the observation period and intervention measures across studies, differences in the calculation results remain (Gu et al., 2022). Fourth, the MCID calculated using the SRM not only considers the ΔEBI from before to after treatment but also accounts for the SD of the difference before and after EBI evaluation. Compared with the ES method, the MCID calculated using the SRM method affects patients’ EBI; pre–post changes (the reciprocal of the variability of the EBI difference) are more sensitive (Angst, 2016). Thus, in the present study, the MCID distribution method resulted in 6.19 points (0.5 SD method), 6.51 points (RCI method, high effect), 6.19 points (ES method, medium effect), and 3.53 points (SRM method, high effect).

ROC curve analysis demonstrated AUC values >0.99 for all three anchors, indicating a strong discriminative ability of ΔEBI. However, specificity was consistently low (0.0%–4.5%), suggesting that patients judged as “not improved” had mean EBI increases exceeding the ROC-derived cutoffs. This implies that the EBI may be more sensitive to change than clinical judgment, capturing functional improvements that are not obviously apparent (Hassanzad & Hajian-Tilaki, 2024). Of note, neither the ROC nor distribution-based methods can distinguish between improvement and deterioration, providing only a single MCID estimate. In contrast, the anchor-based and linear regression methods yield separate MCID values for improvement and deterioration, offering superior clinical interpretability.

The EBI MCID determined using the simple linear regression method concurrently corroborated the findings of the anchor-based method. The ΔEBI from before to after treatment was used as the dependent variable in the simple linear regression equation (Khow et al., 2021; Tenan et al., 2021). When the independent variable (subjective evaluation of ADL efficacy) increases or decreases by one level, the coefficient is equal to the change range of the MCID difference; based on a statistically conservative strategy, we used the upper limit of the 95% confidence interval of the independent variable coefficient as the EBI calibration standard of the MCID results (Lee et al., 2017). Analysis of the three regression models revealed notable differences in how each stakeholder group perceived changes in functional status. The slope inverse was the largest for patients (1/−2.579), whereas those for occupational therapists (1/−3.095) and payers (1/−3.104) were relatively similar and noticeably lower than that of patients. This suggests that, within the same range of EBI score variation, patients were the most likely to detect treatment-related changes in ADL, whereas occupational therapists and payers generally required larger shifts to perceive meaningful changes. This pattern likely reflects each group’s distinct priorities: patients tend to be more attentive to subtle personal experiences in daily function, clinicians often focus on clinically observable improvements, and payers emphasise functional gains that are both demonstrable and cost-effective. When the EBI MCID was calculated using simple linear regression analysis, the deteriorating MCID was −1.94, −1.31, and −0.32 points for the occupational therapists’, patients’, and expense payers’ roles, respectively, and the improved MCID was 4.25, 3.85, and 5.88 points, respectively. The close agreement between the anchor-based simple linear regression and anchor-based method results further confirms the robustness of these findings.

The EBI MCID calculated using the calibration method offers superior clinical interpretation and is easier to understand by occupational therapists and patients (Sedaghat, 2019). The subjective evaluation of the curative effect on ADL was used as the calibration standard (“anchor”), and the results were calculated using a numeric rating scale to evaluate the difference between the patients’ ADL ability before and after treatment (Embry & Piccirillo, 2020). In patients with a slightly worsened ADL ability in the subjective evaluation (Kalkman, 2020), the occupational therapists’ MCID was −2 points, the patients’ MCID was −1 point, and the payers’ MCID was 0 points. The payer is the most sensitive to ADL changes, which may be judged as a slight regression in the patients’ self-care ability when the patients’ ADL remain unchanged. Occupational therapists were the most tolerant of patients’ ADL deterioration; an EBI decrease of 2 points was considered slight regression. Among patients with slightly improved ADL ability in the subjective evaluation, the occupational therapists’, patients’, and expense payers’ MCID values were 4.5, 4, and 6 points, respectively. Expense payers are the least sensitive to patients’ ADL improvement, which is judged as a slight improvement when the patient’s EBI increases by 6 points. Conversely, patients are the most sensitive to their own ADL improvement, which is judged as slightly improved when the EBI increases by 4 points.

Collectively, the convergence of distribution-, ROC curve-, and anchor-based estimates around 3.5–6.5 points supports the EBI’s responsiveness. More importantly, the anchor-based values are recommended for clinical application because they directly reflect stakeholder perspectives. For instance, a 4-point EBI improvement roughly corresponds to regaining independent bowel management or ascending one flight of stairs with supervision—functional milestones that patients, therapists, and payers can readily interpret. A 6-point improvement, as required by expense payers, may represent a more substantial functional gain, such as achieving independent locomotion or problem-solving in ADL. This discrepancy suggests that patients are the most sensitive to small functional gains, whereas payers require larger changes to justify continued rehabilitation funding. These thresholds can guide treatment planning (e.g., setting realistic 4-week goals), progress monitoring (e.g., identifying clinically meaningful deterioration), and policy decisions (e.g., evidence-based insurance coverage criteria).

The established MCID values for the EBI offer significant practical implications for clinicians (Abdallah & Brull, 2021; Kalkman, 2020; Weimar et al., 2002), patients, and expense payers by supporting evidence-based rehabilitation practice. For occupational therapists, the MCID aids in evaluating treatment effectiveness, guiding clinical decisions, and improving the standardisation and objectivity of care plans. For patients, it provides a clear understanding of functional progress in ADL, which may enhance treatment adherence and engagement. Including expense payers as an anchor is justified by their substantial role in rehabilitation decisions for patients with stroke. In the present study, payers were primarily family members who bear the financial burden and base treatment continuation on perceived gains, reflecting real-world clinical and economic trade-offs. Excluding this viewpoint would limit the practical applicability of EBI-based MCID values in discharge planning and resource allocation, as the MCID helps payers optimise rehabilitation benefits by facilitating evidence-based funding decisions and avoiding unnecessary costs.

This study had certain limitations. First, its single-centre design may limit the generalisability of the findings. Furthermore, the study primarily included patients in the acute to subacute phase (approximately 1 month post-stroke); consequently, the EBI MCID values established herein may not be directly applicable to patients in the chronic stage (e.g., ≥6 months post-stroke). Given that the current clinical pathway for post-stroke rehabilitation mandates a rehabilitation cycle of every 4 weeks, our study duration was restricted to 4 weeks. Thus, the impact of longer rehabilitation interventions on the calculations of the EBI MCID remains unclear. Future research should validate the proposed EBI-based MCID thresholds in multicentre cohorts, across different stroke stages (e.g., chronic phase), and over longer follow-up periods to confirm their generalisability and utility in diverse clinical and policy-making contexts.

Conclusion

This study identified the MCID of the EBI using four methods in patients with stroke. We recommend using the anchoring method results as criteria for the EBI MCID: the deteriorating MCID was −2.0, −1.0, and 0 points for the occupational therapists’, patients’, and expense payers’ roles, respectively, while the improved MCID was 4.5, 4.0, and 6.0 points, respectively. These findings offer practical value for clinicians, patients, and healthcare payers. For occupational therapists, the EBI MCID aids in evaluating rehabilitation efficacy, guiding treatment adjustments, and enhancing clinical decision-making. For patients, it helps clarify functional progress, potentially increasing treatment engagement and adherence. For payers, it supports optimised resource allocation, improves the overall benefit of rehabilitation interventions, and may help reduce unnecessary costs. Nonetheless, validation of the proposed EBI-based MCID thresholds is required.
