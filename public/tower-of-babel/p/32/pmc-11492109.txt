Cognitive Impairment, Associated Clinical Factors, and MR Volumetric Measures in Myelin Oligodendrocyte Glycoprotein-IgG–Associated Disease
Authors: Ann-Kathrin Kogel, Theodoros Ladopoulos, Carolin Schwake, Ingo Kleiter, Bianca Teegen, Nadine Siems, Christian Prehn, Solveig Lichtenberg, Marius Ringelstein, Orhan Aktas, Refik Pul, Britta Krieger, Carsten Lukas, Iris-Katharina Penner, Ralf Gold, Ruth Schneider, Ilya Ayzenberg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesCognitive impairment is a common and challenging symptom in multiple sclerosis and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disease; however, data in myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein-IgG–associated disease (MOGAD) remain scarce. In this cross-sectional study, we investigated the frequency of cognitive impairment, associated clinical factors, and MRI volumetric measures in MOGAD.MethodsParticipants were investigated in a single center by certified psychologists and underwent a standardized 3-T-MRI protocol. MRI data were processed with FreeSurfer for gray and white matter volume estimation, presented as a fraction of total intracranial volume. Sera screening for antineural antibodies has been conducted using cell-based assays. The following clinical factors were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis: sex, age, overall number of previous relapses, and specifically the history of acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM)/ADEM-like episodes and other brain relapses.ResultsThirty-two patients with MOGAD (19 female, median age 29.4 years) after a median of 2 relapses with a median EDSS of 1.0 were recruited. Seven patients (21.9%) demonstrated cognitive impairment with the most prevalent deficits in mental flexibility (16.7%), attention (11.1%–14.8%), and verbal working memory (10.3%). 72.4% suffered from fatigue and 42.9% from signs of depression, moderate to severe in 28.6%. The overall number of previous relapses (odds ratio [OR] 1.789, 95% CI 1.041–3.074) and specifically ADEM/ADEM-like episodes (OR 16.929, 95% CI 1.228–233.427) were the only clinical factors associated with cognitive impairment in a multivariate logistic regression model. Screening for antineuronal antibodies remained negative. Cerebral white matter (WM) (0.300 vs 0.317, p = 0.003) and deep gray matter (DGM) (0.036 vs 0.038, p = 0.002) volumes were reduced in patients with MOGAD compared with healthy controls (n = 32). Both cognitive impairment (0.031 vs 0.036, p = 0.003) and history of ADEM/ADEM-like episodes (0.032 vs 0.036, p = 0.006) were associated with reduced DGM volume compared with unaffected patients with MOGAD.DiscussionDespite a low overall disability, every 5th patient with MOGAD experiences cognitive impairment. Cognitive impairment is associated with a higher number of relapses and particularly ADEM/ADEM-like attacks. Although both WM and DGM atrophies are apparent in MOGAD, the latter only seems to have an association with cognitive impairment.

Introduction

Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) antibody-associated disease (MOGAD) is an autoimmune disease of the CNS distinct from multiple sclerosis (MS) and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD). Prevalent symptoms are optic neuritis, myelitis, and brainstem symptoms. Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) episodes and cortical encephalitis are further well-recognized phenotypes of MOGAD that occur more frequently in pediatric and, to a lesser extent, in adult patients.1-4 ADEM attacks in MOGAD do not always consistently meet all classical diagnostic criteria, and the term “ADEM-like episodes” has been frequently used in the literature to describe encephalopathy with or without other multifocal neurologic deficits and usually large (>1 cm) brain lesions characteristic for ADEM.2 Of interest, apart from characteristic perioptic enhancement, spinal cord, and brainstem lesions, supratentorial lesions occur in at least one-third patients with MOGAD (35.4% at onset, 46.8% at least once).1 Particularly, tumefactive lesions (TL) are characteristic for MOGAD, but, compared with MS and NMOSD, they usually tend to resolve completely and are not linked to a worse prognosis.5

Despite being highly prevalent and clinically relevant in both NMOSD (depending on the study 29%–67%,6 19%–40%,7 54%,8 and 30%–70%9) and MS (40%–65%10 and 40%–70%11), cognitive impairment has not been sufficiently investigated in adult MOGAD yet. In the EU pediatric MOG consortium consensus memory impairment, attention deficits, poor concentration, and/or learning or academic difficulties ranging from 10 to 50% of pediatric patients with MOGAD were reported. Cognitive residual deficits were associated with a young age (<10 years old), ADEM or ADEM-like phenotype, and abnormal intracranial MRI findings, including deep gray matter (thalamic and putaminal) lesions.12 Several case reports described cognitive impairment in patients with MOGAD after acute or subacute encephalitis.13,14 Of interest, several case reports and series reported coexisting anti-N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor (NMDAR)-IgG and MOG-IgG in patients with predominantly encephalitic manifestation, however also demyelinating attacks in some patients.15

In this study, we aim to identify the frequency of cognitive impairment, and associated clinical factors as well as volumetric changes in brain MRI in adult patients with MOGAD.

Methods

Participants

Patients were prospectively recruited in the treating centers during regular routine office visits and examined exclusively in the Department of Neurology, St. Josef Hospital Bochum, between June 2018 and July 2022 while in relapse-free remission. Inclusion criteria for the study were demyelinating disease, MOG-IgG seropositivity, and the ability to undergo neuropsychological examination. The diagnostic criteria proposed by the international MOGAD panel3 were applied retrospectively. Comorbid neurologic and psychiatric disorders were assessed during the initial neurologic and following neuropsychological examination. Patients with clinically manifest or prediagnosed attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), major depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, or other psychotic disorders as well as additional neurologic diseases of the CNS were excluded. For the detection of MOG-IgG, serum samples were analyzed during the initial workup at least once by established cell-based assays (MOG IFT, EUROIMMUN, Laboratory Stöcker, Germany, cutoff 1:10 and MOG Live Cell Assay, Laboratory Krone, Germany, cutoff 1:160). Sera of 22 of 32 participants were further tested for NMDAR-IgG, contactin-associated protein (CASPR2)-IgG, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) a/b-IgG, leucine-rich glioma-inactivated protein (LGI1)-IgG, dipeptidyl aminopeptidase-like protein 6 (DPPX)-IgG, and metabotropic glutamate receptor (mGluR)-IgG. The following clinical data were collected: sex, age, level of education, disease duration, number and type of previous relapses (optic neuritis, myelitis, ADEM/ADEM-like episodes, cortical encephalitis, attacks with brainstem or cerebellar involvement), and Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS).

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients participating in the study. The local ethics committees approved the study protocol in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) in its currently applicable version (registration number Ruhr-University Bochum 16-5937).

Neuropsychological Examination

Participants were examined by 2 certified neuropsychologists using a standardized test battery, including the examination of attention (subtest “Alertness” of the “Testbatterie zur Aufmerksamkeitsprüfung,” TAP16), mental flexibility as an executive function (subtest “phonematic category change” of the “Regensburger Wortflüssigkeitstest,” RWT17), visual memory (subtest “visual reproduction” of the revised “Wechsler Memory Scale,” WMS-R18), and mental rotation (subtest 7 of the “Leistungsprüfsystem,” LPS 719), verbal working and short-term memory (subtest “digit span” of the WMS-R18) as well as verbal learning and memory (“Verbaler Lern- und Merkfähigkeitstest,” VLMT recall 5 and recall 720). Cognitive impairment was defined as performance <7th percentile21 of demographically adjusted scores in ≥2 tests representing different cognitive functions.6,10,22 Patients were only examined if neuropsychologists assessed that they were visually and physically able to perform the examination adequately. Participants further completed the self-reported Fatigue Scale for Motor and Cognitive Functions (FSMC)23 and the Beck Depression Inventory II (BDI-II).24

MRI Analysis

Three-Tesla MRI investigation was performed with a minimum interval of 3 months after the last relapse in 25 patients with MOGAD as well as in 32 age-matched and sex-matched healthy control participants (HC). Imaging was performed with a single 3 T scanner (Achieva Philips, Best, The Netherlands) using a standardized imaging protocol according to the 2021 MAGNIMS-CMSC-NAIMS consensus recommendations,25 including isotropic 3D fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequence for lesion quantification and structural isotropic T1-weighted 3D sequence. Detailed sequence parameters are provided in the eMethods section in the supplement. MRI data sets were processed with FreeSurfer 6.026 using the recon-all pipeline with default settings (including lesion filling) without manual interventions. Mean gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) volumes were extracted from the morphometry statistics (aseg.stats) for regions of interest as defined by the Desikan-Killiany (DK) atlas.27 All extracted brain volumes were normalized for total intracranial volume (TIV) for further statistical analyses and reported as a fraction of TIV. GM volumes were estimated for total GM, cortical GM, and global deep gray matter (DGM) including thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, hippocampus, amygdala, nucleus accumbens, ventral diencephalon, and substantia nigra volumes. In addition, volumes of these DGM substructures were calculated separately. As a clinical feature of particular interest, for patients with a history of ADEM/ADEM-like episode, baseline MRI scans from the time of relapse (performed outside our center) were retrospectively reviewed to characterize morphology and dynamics of the lesions. MRI scans were available in 4 and detailed radiologic reports in 3 of 7 patients with ADEM/ADEM-like episodes.

Statistical Analysis

Means and SD or medians and interquartile range (IQR) were used to report averages as appropriate. A multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to analyze associations of clinical factors with cognitive impairment. The following parameters were included into the model: sex, age, number of relapses, ADEM/ADEM-like episodes, and cortical encephalitis/other cerebral attacks. The odds ratios (OR) presented in the manuscript are unadjusted. Group comparisons have been performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted, and graphs were generated using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 28 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY).

Data Availability

Anonymized data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request compatible with data protection regulations.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Cohort Characteristics

A total of 32 patients with MOGAD (19 female, median age 29.4 years, IQR 21.3–40.6) were enrolled in the study. Most of the patients (31 of 32) met the diagnostic criteria proposed by the international MOGAD panel.3 The median disease duration was 29.4 months with a median of 2 relapses. Fifteen patients had monophasic and 17 relapsing disease courses. Specifically, 17 patients experienced optic neuritis, 15 myelitis, 7 ADEM/ADEM-like episodes, 2 cortical encephalitis, 3 relapses with brainstem or cerebellar involvement, and 4 other brain relapses at least once in their history. 22 patients (69%) were on disease-modifying therapies. Cognitive Impairment, Depression, and Fatigue

Results of the neuropsychological testing are summarized in Table 2. Median time between the last attack and neuropsychological examination was 17 [3–49] months. Seven of 32 patients (21.9%) exhibited cognitive impairment. Deficits in mental flexibility (16.7%), attention (11.1–14.8%), and verbal working memory (10.3%) prevailed. Most patients (72.4%) experienced fatigue, severe fatigue (FSMC total score ≥63) in 34.5%. Cognitive fatigue was present in 69.0%, while motor fatigue affected 58.6% of participants. Signs of depression (BDI-II score >14) were noted in 42.9%, with 28.5% experiencing moderate to severe depressive symptoms (BDI-II score ≥20). There was no significant difference in fatigue and depression scores between MOGAD patients with and without cognitive impairment.

Clinical Factors and Association With Cognitive Impairment

Sera screening for all antineuronal antibodies was negative. The number of previous relapses (OR 1.789, 95% CI 1.041–3.074) and a history of ADEM/ADEM-like episodes (OR 16.929, 95% CI 1.228–233.472) were identified as 2 potential influencing factors on cognitive abilities in a multivariate logistic regression analysis. Detailed results are listed in eTable 2 in the supplement. Four of 7 patients with a history of ADEM/ADEM-like episodes suffered from cognitive impairment. During the ADEM/ADEM-like episodes, all 7 patients had TL that ranged in size from 2.70 cm*2.40 cm to 5.02 cm*4.17 cm. In the follow-up investigations, TL were almost completely resolved in 6 of 7 and no longer detectable in 1 patient.

MR Volumetric Changes in Patients With MOGAD With and Without Cognitive Impairment

Median time between the last attack and MRI examination was 14 [6–32] months. Patients with MOGAD (n = 25) had reduced cerebral WM (0.300 vs 0.317, p = 0.003) and DGM (0.036 vs 0.038, p = 0.002) volume compared with HC (n = 32) (Figure, A). Patients with MOGAD with monophasic and relapsing course demonstrated no significant volumetric differences in the studied structures. MOGAD patients with cognitive impairment (MRI performed in 6/7 patients) demonstrated a reduction of the total GM and DGM volume compared with both HC (GM volume 0.404 vs 0.428, p = 0.018, DGM volume 0.031 vs 0.038, p < 0.001) and patients without cognitive impairment (n = 19) (GM volume 0.404 vs 0.423, p = 0.043, DGM volume 0.031 vs 0.036, p = 0.003) (Figure, B). Focusing on DGM structures, MOGAD patients with cognitive impairment showed reduced thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, amygdala, nucleus accumbens, and ventral diencephalon volumes compared with cognitively preserved patients. We also observed a trend toward reduced hippocampal volume, although it did not reach the level of significance. Group comparisons of DGM structures and exact DGM volumes are shown in eFigure 1 in the supplement. Since ADEM/ADEM-like episodes were associated with cognitive impairment, we conducted an additional MR volumetric analysis in this group. We could confirm a reduced DGM volume in patients with ADEM/ADEM-like episodes in the history (n = 7, 4 with and 3 without cognitive impairment) compared with those without (n = 18) (0.032 vs 0.036, p = 0.006) as well (Figure, C).

Discussion

In this single-center study, we performed a thorough assessment of cognitive impairment, associated clinical factors, and MRI-volumetric changes in adult patients with MOGAD. Despite a low overall disability, as measured by a median EDSS of 1, one in 5 patients in our cohort experienced notable cognitive impairment. The most prevalent deficits were observed in mental flexibility, attention, and verbal working memory. These findings are reasonably comparable with previous studies in pediatric MOG-IgG–positive patients, where memory impairment, attentional problems, poor concentration, and/or learning or academic difficulties have been reported in 10%–50%.12,28 Prevalence of cognitive impairment in adult NMOSD and MS seems to be higher (ranging between 19%–70% and 40%–70%, accordingly), with a substantial variation across different studies and cohorts.6-11 In NMOSD, most significant deficits were reported in executive function, attention, verbal fluency, and memory performance which closely aligns with the findings in our MOGAD cohort.6,8,9 Cognitive dysfunction in MS seems to affect a wider range of domains, including verbal and visual short-term memory, as well as processing speed and fluidity.10,11,29 Of interest, some of the affected cognitive functions in patients with MOGAD (working memory and attention) seem to be similar to those in ADHD.30 However, no data on the association of ADHD and MOGAD are available so far. More than two-thirds (72.4%) of patients with MOGAD suffered from fatigue and more than 40% from depressive symptoms, moderate to severe in one-third of patients. These results are consistent with previously published data reporting a frequency of fatigue in 76% of patients with MOGAD,31 compared with 64%32 in NMOSD. The neuromyelitis optica study group (NEMOS) found depression in 42% of adult patients with MOGAD, at least moderate in 23%33 and in around 40%34 of patients with NMOSD.

The central finding of our study is the association of cognitive impairment with the overall number of relapses and specifically with a history of ADEM/ADEM-like episodes. These findings are novel because no systematic studies of cognitive impairment risk factors in MOG-IgG–seropositive patients have been conducted so far. Some previous studies have reported residual cognitive impairment after pediatric ADEM, however irrespective of MOG-IgG antibody status.35,36 Other studies reported a link between ADEM and specifically, multiphasic disseminated encephalomyelitis, and cognitive impairment in MOG-IgG–positive children.12,37,38 Significant residual cognitive impairment following ADEM has been reported in a 35-year-old woman. This patient exhibited severe deficits in multiple functions, particularly in executive and attention tasks, notably resembling findings in our cohort.39 Data on the influence of fatigue and depression on cognitive performance in MS are ambiguous. Some studies reported an influence of fatigue and/or depression on performance in certain cognitive domains (attention and processing speed),40,41 others found no correlation between fatigue and general cognitive performance, especially in a comparably extensive test battery.42 Both fatigue and depression scores were similar in MOGAD patients with and without cognitive impairment in our cohort. An influence of depression on cognitive impairment in MOGAD can certainly not be excluded and should be investigated in further studies. Despite the previously reported increased prevalence of antineuronal antibodies in patients with MOGAD,15 we could not identify any positive sera in our cohort.

MR volumetric analysis revealed a reduction in both white and deep gray matter volume in the MOGAD cohort compared with healthy controls. These findings are partly consistent with studies of Rechtman et al. who reported significantly decreased total, WM, GM, and DGM volume43 and Messina et al.44 who observed reduced DGM in patients with MOGAD compared with HC. Partly contradictory results of the MR volumetric studies can probably be attributed to the limited sample sizes, different volumetric analysis techniques, and heterogeneous clinical presentations in various MOGAD cohorts. Total GM volume was reduced in patients with MOGAD with cognitive impairment compared with cognitively preserved patients, which was mainly driven by the reduction of the DGM. Association of the ADEM/ADEM-like episodes with both cognitive impairment and decreased DGM volume seems to be the most plausible explanation of this finding. Compared with the brain lesions in MS and other demyelinating diseases, large TL, characteristic for MOGAD ADEM, typically disappear on the follow-up MRI.5 Despite the almost complete resolution and previously reported favorable prognosis in MOGAD ADEM, the potential long-term consequences, including brain atrophy and cognitive deficits, have not yet been systematically studied. Future studies are certainly needed to re-evaluate our findings and investigate potential effect of early and aggressive relapse therapy on the long-term cognitive deficits in MOGAD ADEM.

In the awareness of the relatively small patient number and the naturally low volume of DGM nuclei, our volumetric analysis confirmed a consistent relevant volume reduction of specific structures such as thalamus, nucleus caudate, globus pallidum, amygdala, nucleus accumbens, and ventral diencephalon in patients with cognitive impairment. An association between DGM lesions, particularly in the thalamic and putaminal regions, and academic difficulties has been previously reported in pediatric MOGAD.38 Similar atrophy of DGM structures, including hippocampus, thalamus, putamen, caudate, and amygdala, has also been reported in adult MOGAD.43-45 However, only 1 of these studies explored selected cognitive functions and found a correlation between hippocampus/parahippocampal volume and verbal memory.45 It is worth noting that a correlation between specific neuropsychological domains and distinct brain structures may require longitudinal investigation of large patient cohorts and should be interpreted with caution. Precise mechanisms of DGM atrophy in MOGAD remain unclear. Histopathologic studies confirmed DGM involvement in MOGAD with often a massive CD4 T-cell and granulocytic infiltration, however a substantially less complement activation compared with NMOSD. The relatively well-preserved oligodendrocytes may explain the high level of remyelination in MOGAD.46 Despite that, an acute relapse-associated axonal damage seems to occur (e.g., massive retinal nerve fiber and ganglion cell layer atrophy after optic neuritis) and to be the primary driver of neurodegeneration in MOGAD. Supportive, prolonged neuroaxonal retinal damage for up to 12 months after optic neuritis, but no signs of subclinical, relapse-independent neurodegeneration, has been observed in optical coherence tomography.47 Several additional mechanisms for nonlesional DGM atrophy, such as retrograde and anterograde degeneration resulting from a focal axonal transection in distant white matter lesions, transsynaptic degeneration, and neuron loss due to deafferentation, are well-known in MS48 and may also be relevant in MOGAD.

Our study has several limitations. Owing to the low prevalence of MOGAD and limited sample size, the subgroups were small, resulting in wide confidence intervals in the multivariate logistic regression model. Selection bias cannot be excluded because part of patients have been referred to our clinic for the second opinion. However, we were able to scan and investigate all patients in a single center avoiding interrater differences. Results of the MR volumetric analyses were conclusive in the investigated subgroups; however, due to the multiple comparisons between patients with and without CI, as well as ADEM/ADEM-like episodes in the history, an increased risk of type I error should be considered. Larger hypothesis-driven studies are needed to validate the generalizability of our results. The used test battery partly includes neuropsychological tests exclusively used in German-speaking countries, which makes international comparison difficult. As ADHD is often underrecognized, a potential influence of previously undiagnosed ADHD on cognitive abilities cannot be excluded in our cohort.49 However, we see a relevant advantage in the examination by experienced psychologists familiar with the center's standardized test battery. MRI was not available in all patients with MOGAD due to several reasons (braces, claustrophobia, walking disability, and personal restraint due to the COVID-19 pandemic). The applied MR volumetric method does not allow conclusions regarding regional changes in cortical structures or microstructural WM pathology which may also be relevant to cognition. Chronic steroid therapy may have an effect on MRI volumetry. Considering the partly contradictory findings50 and the fact that only 4 of 32 patients received low-dose prednisone (median 10 mg), we believe that these effects can be neglected at the cohort level.

Despite a relatively low overall disability, every fifth adult patient with MOGAD experiences cognitive impairment, with the most prevalent deficits in mental flexibility, attention, and verbal working memory. A history of ADEM/ADEM-like episodes and the number of previous relapses seem to be the main clinical factors associated with cognitive impairment. Although both WM and DGM atrophies are apparent in MOGAD, only the latter seems to have an association with cognitive impairment. Further studies in larger cohorts are necessary to validate our findings.
