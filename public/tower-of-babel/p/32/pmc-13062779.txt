Acinetobacter baumannii encodes multiple mutagenically and biochemically active DNA polymerase V variants
Authors: Debika Ojha, Malgorzata Jaszczur, Phuong Pham, John P McDonald, Jaan C Selod, Roger Woodgate, Myron F Goodman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Acinetobacter baumannii natural isolates encode multiple copies of E. coli DNA polymerase V (pol VEc) umuDC homologs, some with as many as four nonidentical umuC and three nonidentical umuD genes encoding twelve possible pol VAb variants. Here, we show that six of the twelve pol VAb drive spontaneous and methyl methanesulfonate-induced mutations when expressed in E. coli. Mutagenesis depends on co-expression with RecAAb. Five mutagenically active pol VAb combinations assemble in vitro as stable mutasome complexes that synthesize DNA, pol VAb Mut = UmuD′2CAb-RecAAb-ATP/ATPγS. One of the mutasomes requires an A. baumannii-encoded β/τ processivity clamp for activity in vitro. Translesion DNA synthesis (TLS) occurs at T^T cyclobutane dimers, with different variants exhibiting different nucleotide misincorporation specificities. As observed for E. coli pol V and R391 ICE-encoded Rum pol, a single amino acid substitution in RecA, RecAAb M196D, abolishes A. baumannii pol V-induced mutagenesis.

Introduction

A potential incipient pandemic is the proliferation of multidrug-resistant bacterial pathogens [1]. Horizontal gene transfer and selective mutations play a major role in microbial evolution, allowing bacteria to acquire new genes and phenotypes that enable resistance to antibiotic stress (reviewed in [2, 3]). Although significant progress has been made towards the identification of mutations that cause antibiotic resistance, e.g. mutations in genes associated with drug uptake, drug targeting, and drug metabolism (reviewed in [3, 4]), there are limited data regarding specific proteins responsible for making mutations that lead to drug resistance [5–10].

A. baumannii is an opportunistic pathogen in humans, typically affecting people with weakened immune systems, causing pernicious hospital-derived infections [11, 12]. A. baumannii has been classified among the ESCAPE organisms, a group of clinically important, health care-associated pathogens that have the potential to develop substantial antimicrobial resistance [13, 14]. Carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii are one of the most critical-priority pathogens on the World Health Organization priority list of antibiotic-resistant bacteria for effective drug development.

We have recently shown that the integrative conjugative element (ICE) SXT/R391-encoded Rum DNA polymerase (RumA′2B), a homolog of E. coli pol V (UmuD′2C) [15, 16], plays a central role in the rapid acquisition of multidrug resistance in E. coli in response to SOS-inducing antibiotics and nonantibiotic external stressors [17]. A. baumannii carries multiple diverse copies of umuC and umuD gene homologs that are simultaneously induced by SOS [18]. A. baumannii ATCC 17978 strain contains 3 umuD and 4 umuC genes that can potentially code for 12 pol VAb (UmuD′2CAb) variants.

It is currently unknown if individual UmuDAb and UmuCAb proteins are expressed and if any pol VAb (UmuD′2CAb) polymerase complexes are formed. Here, we determine the ability of each of the 12 pol VAb variants to generate spontaneous and drug-induced mutations when expressed in E. coli. Pol VEc and R391-encoded Rum pol are active biochemically only when assembled as mutasomes by binding to a RecA monomer and to a molecule of ATP/ATPγS, pol V Mut = UmuD′2C-RecA-ATP/ATPγS. We have investigated mutasome assembly for each A. baumannii pol V variant and have determined biochemical properties for the variants with respect to translesion DNA synthesis (TLS). By determining the specificity of deoxynucleotide insertion during TLS, we have identified properties that can be used to distinguish between mutasomes. Antibiotic options for the treatment of A. baumannii infections are narrowly circumscribed because of high rates of resistance. We view our analysis of A. baumannii pol V as providing an initial step in determining the extent to which diverse pol VAb generate mutational landscapes that are likely to play a central role in the rapid acquisition of multidrug resistance.

Materials and methods

A. baumannii ATCC 17978 umuDC, recA, and other genes

The gene and protein sequences of three UmuDs and four UmuCs encoded in the A. baumannii ATCC 17978 strain were obtained from the NCBI database. The corresponding GenBank accession numbers are as follows: umuD0636 (AIS_0636) GenBank No.: ABO11085.1; umuD1174 (AIS_1174) GenBank No.: ABO11604.1; umuD1389 (AIS_1389) GenBank No.: ABO11817.2; umuC0637 (AIS_0637) GenBank No.: ABO11086.2; umuC1173 (AIS_1173) GenBank No.: ABO11603.2, umuC2015 (AIS_2015) GenBank: ABO12442.2; umuC2008 (AIS_2008) GenBank No.: ABO12435.2.

All A. baumannii gene sequences used in this study were codon-optimized for expression in E. coli (Genscript) and the plasmids encoding various combinations of umuC, umuD, or umuD′ were constructed by scientists at Genscript (Table 1). The sequences of other genes and proteins used in this study, such as RecAAb and βAb-clamp (encoded by dnaNAb gene; GenBank No.: CP000521.1), and τAb-complex (encoded by dnaXAb, holAAb, and holBAb; GenBank No.: CP000521.1) were derived from NCBI databases, and respective plasmids were also constructed by scientists at Genscript (Table 1). UmuCAb, UmuD′Ab, RecAAb, and RecAAb M196D were expressed from plasmids under the control of the E. coli recA wild-type promoter.

Spontaneous and methyl methanesulfonate-induced mutagenesis

The A. baumannii pol V-dependent spontaneous and damage-induced mutagenesis was assessed using the E. coli strain RW616 [lexA51 (Def) ΔumuDC ΔrecA] (Table 1). E. coli strain RW616 carries chromosomal deletions of the endogenous E. coli umuDC operon and E. coli recA. The presence of the lexA51(Def) allele results in constitutive induction of the SOS response in E. coli, which enhances the detection level of pol VAb-dependent mutagenic activity for both spontaneous and DNA damage-induced mutagenesis. The in vivo mutagenic activity of pol VAb variants was measured in three experimental setups: (i) with A. baumannii RecA (RecAAb) encoded on the plasmid pGS106; (ii) without RecA; (iii) with RecAAb M196D mutated variant encoded on plasmid pGS205 (Table 1).

E. coli strain RW616 was transformed with two low-copy plasmids, one encoding a codon-optimized “ready-to-go” post-translationally activated UmuD′2CAb variant and a second plasmid encoding RecAAb or RecAAb M196D (Table 1). Three independent chloramphenicol and spectinomycin-resistant colonies (CmR and SpecR) from each transformation were inoculated into 5 ml Lysogeny broth (LB) medium supplemented with appropriate antibiotics (Cm, 12.5 μg ml−1; Spec, 50 μg ml−1) and grown overnight at 37°C. Overnight cultures were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in an equal volume of SM buffer [19]. Aliquots of 100 μl from each resuspension were plated in triplicate on Davis and Mingioli minimal plates [20] supplemented with 1 μg ml−1 histidine and incubated at 37°C for 4 days and subsequently imaged.

Damage-induced pol VAb-dependent mutagenesis was measured in the presence of methyl methanesulfonate (MMS). The MMS-induced mutagenesis protocol was identical to the spontaneous mutagenesis measurements except that a sterile filter disk was placed at the center of each plate, and 5 μl of a one to five dilution of MMS dissolved in dimethylsulfoxide was spotted onto the filter disk. Plates were incubated for 4 days at 37°C and subsequently imaged.

To determine whether A. baumannii-encoded UmuDs can undergo RecAAb -dependent autocleavage to UmuD′Ab [which is required for pol VAb (UmuD′2CAb) complex formation], the mutagenic activity was assessed in E. coli RW616 transformed with plasmids encoding umuDCAb variants instead of postranslationally activated umuD′CAb (Table 1), and with a plasmid expressing RecAAb.

The mutagenesis experiments were performed with three replicates for each pol VAb ± RecAAb strain combination. There was <15% variation in the number of mutants per plate between replicates.

Proteins

Pol VAb variants

Twelve His-tagged A. baumannii-encoded pol V variants were purified from E. coli RW644 (Table 1), co-transformed with plasmids encoding untagged UmuD′Ab proteins (pGS97–pGS99) and plasmids encoding His6-tagged UmuCAb proteins (pGS90–pGS93) (Table 1). The purification of 12 pol VAb (UmuD′2CAb) variants was performed using an established purification protocol for E. coli pol V using Ni-NTA agarose and gel filtration chromatography [21]. The His6-tag was not removed from purified pol V variants. We have previously shown that the presence of a His6-tag at the N-terminus of E. coli UmuC does not interfere with pol V in vivo or in vitro activities [21].

RecAAb: A. baumannii ATCC 17978-encoded recA was cloned into the expression vector pET22b(+), to generate pGS211 and transformed into E. coli RW1290 strain (Table 1). RecAAb was purified utilizing a protocol established for the purification of E. coli RecA [22].

βAb-clamp

In the A. baumannii ATCC 17978 strain, the βAb-clamp is encoded by the dnaNAb gene, which shares ∼45% homology with E. coli β. pGS176 plasmid carrying the E. coli codon-optimized dnaNAb gene was transformed into the E. coli BL21 (λDE3) expression strain, and the protein was purified using the protocol previously established for E. coli β [23]. Briefly, a single colony was inoculated into LB containing ampicillin (Amp; 100 μg ml−1) and grown overnight at 37°C with shaking. One liter of LB medium supplemented with Amp was inoculated with an overnight-grown culture at a 1:100 dilution, and cells were grown to OD600 = 0.6, at which point the protein expression was induced with 1 mM Isopropyl-β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for 2 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 0.5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 50 mM NaCl, 5 mM Dithiothreitol (DTT), and 10% glycerol. Cells were lysed by sonication, and the lysate was cleared by centrifugation. The proteins were precipitated with ammonium sulfate at 70% saturation. The protein pellet was resolubilized in the buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 0.5 mM EDTA, 5 mM DTT, 50 mM NaCl, and 10% glycerol, and dialyzed overnight in the same buffer. Proteins were loaded onto two connected HiTrap Q (5 ml each) columns and separated using a 50 mM–1 M NaCl salt gradient in buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5 mM EDTA, 5 mM DTT. Fractions containing βAb-clamp were pooled and dialyzed against 10 mM sodium acetate (pH 7.5), 0.5 mM EDTA, and 10% glycerol. The sample was then loaded onto two connected HiTrap Heparin (5 ml each) columns equilibrated with 10 mM sodium acetate-containing buffer at pH 7.5. The βAb-clamp does not attach to HiTrap Heparin at pH 7.5 and was collected as flow-through. The pH was adjusted to 6.0, at which βAb-clamp binds to HiTrap Heparin and is purified by running a salt 0–1 M NaCl gradient in buffer containing 10 mM sodium acetate (pH 6.0), 0.5 mM EDTA, and 10% glycerol. Fractions containing purified βAb-clamp were pooled and dialyzed into storage buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 0.5 mM EDTA, 5 mM DTT, and 10% glycerol. Protein aliquots were stored at −80°C until further use. The sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) gel image of purified βAb-clamp is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1A.

Pol VAb mutasome assembly

To measure the in vitro DNA synthesis, each pol VAb variant was assembled as a mutasome complex with RecAAb following the protocol previously established for pol VEc Mut [25]. Briefly, RecAAb nucleoprotein filament (RecAAb*) was formed on 45 nt- poly(dT) single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) that is covalently attached to Cyanogen Bromide Sepharose 4B beads (Sigma) through the amino linker present on the 5′-end of ssDNA (3′-end is free) (Table 2). RecAAb* is formed in a small spin column by incubating ssDNA-beads with RecAAb and with ATPγS for 20 min at 37°C in the buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 8 mM MgCl2, 5 mM DTT, 0.1 mM EDTA, 25 mM sodium glutamate, and 4% (v/v) glycerol. Unbound RecAAb and excess ATPγS are removed by extensive washes with reaction buffer. To form pol VAb Mut complexes, the pol VAb variants were incubated with beads-RecA* for 15 min at 37°C. The pol VAb Mut complexes were separated from beads-RecA* by centrifugation of the spin column; the pol VAb Mut is present in the flow-through, while beads-RecA* are retained in the spin column. The concentration of the pol VAb Mut was estimated by SDS–PAGE using known concentrations of UmuCAb, UmuD′Ab, and RecAAb as internal standards.

DNA synthesis measurements in vitro

To verify that pol VAb Mut variants (pol VAb -RecAAb-ATP) are active as stand-alone polymerases that can synthesize DNA in the absence of a RecAAb*, the polymerase activity was measured on a 5′-32P-labeled primer/template (p/t) hairpin (HP) with a 3-nt single-stranded overhang (oh) (50 nM 3 nt oh p/t HP, Table 2). RecA* cannot be formed on 3 nt oh p/t HP. The activity of pol VAb Mut variants (300 nM) was measured in the presence and absence of ATP/ATPγS (500 μM) and with all four dNTPs (mix of dATP, dTTP, dGTP, dCTP, 500 μM each) in 1 × reaction buffer [20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 8 mM MgCl2, 5 mM DTT, 0.1 mM EDTA, 25 mM sodium glutamate, and 4% (v/v) glycerol]. The synthesis reactions were carried out for 60 min at 37°C, and they were terminated by the addition of stop solution containing 20 mM EDTA in 95% formamide. The DNA synthesis products were resolved on 20% denaturing polyacrylamide gels containing 8M urea, allowing the detection of single-nucleotide incorporation. Gel band intensities were measured on the Typhoon FLA 9500 phosphorimager and quantified using Image Quant software (USC Nanobiophysics Core). The fraction of extended primer (%PE) was calculated by integrating the band intensities of extended primer DNA divided by the total integrated DNA band intensity.

Measurement of pol VAb mut-dependent translesion DNA synthesis (TLS)

The TLS activity of pol VAb Mut variants was assessed on linear 32P-labeled 50 nt p/t DNAs (32P-labeled 21-nt primer/50-nt template) containing a cis-syn CPD (T^T dimer), or abasic lesion (Table 2). The T^T dimer and abasic lesions are located 7 and 8 nt from the 3′-OH of the primer template DNA (Table 2). The TLS efficiencies of pol V Mut variants (300 nM) were measured on 25 nM lesion-containing p/t DNA in the presence and absence of ATP/ATPγS (500 μM) and with all four dNTPs (dATP, dTTP, dGTP, dCTP; 500 μM each).

To assess pol VAb Mut specificities while copying across a T^T dimer, we performed a series of DNA synthesis assays with 25 nM 32P-labeled 48 nt p/t DNAs (5′-32P-labeled 18-nt primer/48-nt template) containing a T^T dimer 1 nucleotide from the 3′-OH of the primer. In this experimental design, dTTP is incorporated 1 nt downstream and upstream of the T^T dimer. The activity of each pol V Mut variant (300 nM) was measured in the presence of ATPγS (500 μM) and with various dNTP combinations: (i) dTTP (500 μM), (ii) dTTP, dATP (500 μM each), (iii) dTTP, dCTP (500 μM each only), (iv) dTTP, dGTP (500 μM each), (v) all four dNTPs (500 μM each). Results

A. baumannii encodes damage-inducible SOS genes recA, and pol V (umuDC) [18, 26]. Hospital isolates of A. baumannii strains contain large variations in the number of chromosomally- and mega-plasmid-encoded umuD and umuC genes, each gene family member having a different DNA sequence [18]. Strain A. baumannii ATCC 17978 contains the largest number of pol V-like gene copies, with three umuD and four umuC variants [18], which, if combined as pol V = UmuD′2C complexes [27–29], would possibly result in twelve different DNA polymerases. Previously, RT-qPCR data for A. baumannii ATCC 17978 showed the induction and relative transcript levels of all A. baumannii umuD and umuC genes in response to UV, MMS, and Ciprofloxicin exposure [18]. There are no previous data that address whether A. baumannii umuD and umuC genes encode mutagenically active DNA V polymerase complexes, nor which UmuD′2C combinations are formed in response to DNA damage.

Here, we have investigated the mutagenic and biochemical properties of each of the twelve umuDC combinations encoded in A. baumannii ATCC 17978. Four questions are addressed: (i) How do spontaneous and drug-induced (MMS) mutations depend on the expression of each of the twelve UmuD′2C combinations in E. coli in the presence, or absence, of RecAAb?; (ii) Which of the UmuD′C combinations are biochemically active?; (iii) Do the active pols assemble as mutasomal complexes, pol V MutAb = UmuD′2CAb-RecAAb-ATP/ATPγS [25, 30]; (iv) Can the pols copy damaged DNA templates, i.e. catalyze TLS in vitro?

Six UmuD′2CAb are mutagenically active in the presence of RecAAb

A. baumannii ATCC 17978 strain carries three umuD (umuD0636, umuD1174, and umuD1389) and four different umuC gene variants (umuC0637, umuC1173, umuC2008, and umuC2015) (Fig. 1A). Similar to E. coli, the chromosomally encoded umuD0636-umuC0637 and umuD1174-umuC1173 form two umuDCAb operons (Fig. 1A). However, in contrast to E. coli umuDC, in which the gene sequences overlap by one nucleotide, there is a 20 nt overlap between the umuD0636-umuC0637 genes, and a 1 nt gap between the umuD1174-umuC1173 genes (Fig. 1A). Also, in contrast to E. coli, umuD1389, umuC2008, and umuC2015 are encoded individually on the A. baumannii chromosome (Fig. 1A). The UmuDAb and UmuCAb protein variants share ∼40%–55% homology with E. coli UmuD and UmuC (umuDCEc) (Fig. 1B). In E. coli, the 139 amino acid UmuD protein is converted to UmuD′ by a RecA-mediated self-cleavage reaction at C24-G25 in the N-terminus of UmuD [31, 32]. Post-translational conversion of UmuD to UmuD′2 is required for pol V formation and DNA damage-induced mutagenesis [31, 32]. The three A. baumannii-encoded UmuD proteins are considerably larger than E. coli-encoded UmuD, and range in size from 164 amino acids to 203 amino acids, with ∼60%–78% sequence homology between the variants (Supplementary Fig. S2A). As a consequence, putative cleavage sites are located much further away from the amino terminus of UmuDAb (UmuD0636, A48-G49; UmuD1173, A47-G48; UmuD1389, A83-G84). The UmuD cleavage site, along with homologous Ser and Lys residues required for the conversion of UmuD to UmuD′ is highly conserved in all UmuD homologs (Supplementary Fig. S2B), and the damage-induced conversion of UmuD to UmuD′ has been documented for UmuD1389 [32–34]. Notably, the largest UmuD1389 variant (also called UmuDAb) plays a dual role unique to A. baumannii; the N-terminal part forms a complex with DdrR, which acts as a unique LexA-like transcriptional repressor of ∼159 A. baumannii SOS-regulated genes [35–37], while the post-translationally converted UmuD1389 to UmuD1389′2 may associate with one of the four UmuCAb variants to form an error-prone pol VAb variant, analogous to E. coli pol V. The four A. baumannii UmuC proteins vary in length from 430–442 amino acids, so all are slightly larger than the 422 amino acids of E. coli UmuC, and there is ∼56%–78% amino acid homology between the four UmuCAb variants (Supplementary Fig. S3). Posttranslational cleavage of the three UmuDAb proteins to UmuD′2Ab can potentially lead to the formation of 12 different heterotrimeric complexes (UmuD′2CAb) with each of the four UmuCAb catalytic pol subunits, which we designate as pol V1–12 (Fig. 1C). Although we cannot rule out the possibility that three UmuD′Ab variants may also form heterodimers that can associate with UmuCAb variants, greatly expanding the number of pol VAb variants potentially formed in A. baumannii. It is unknown whether any combinations of A. baumannii UmuD′2Ab and UmuCAb variants can form UmuD′2CAb (i.e. pol VAb variant) complexes that are active in vivo and in vitro. Exposure of A. baumannii to DNA-damaging agents has been shown to induce transcription of all umuD and umuC genes [18], potentially leading to the formation of multiple UmuD′2C variants, whose activity would be difficult to distinguish in A. baumannii since each UmuC can form at least three different pol V variants. Thus, we used E. coli to identify which of the 12 pol V variants (Fig. 1C) form mutagenically active pol V variants.

The expression of RecAAb is required to observe spontaneous and MMS-induced mutagenesis for the six mutation-proficient combinations (Fig. 2B). We have previously shown that a single site in RecA, corresponding to M197 in E. coli RecA serves as a “master regulator” for mutagenesis, with mutations abolished for E. coli pol V and for Rum pol in the presence a variety of bacterial RecA protein homologs [17, 38]. Consistent with the E. coli and Rum pol data, spontaneous and MMS-induced mutations are abolished for the six mutagenically active pol VAb variants expressed in the presence of RecAAb M196D (Fig. 2C).

UmuDCAb mutagenesis in the presence of RecAAb

The in vivo studies above were performed in the presence of recombinant UmuD′Ab. We repeated the mutagenesis assays in the presence of full-length UmuDAb in combination with six UmuCAb variants that promoted mutagenesis in the presence of recombinant UmuD′Ab (corresponding to pol V1, V2, V4, V6, V8, V12) (Fig. 3). MMS-induced mutagenesis was observed with all three full-length UmuDAb. UmuD0636 was converted to UmuD′0636 to generate mutagenically active polV1 and polV4. UmuD1174 was converted to UmuD′1174 to generate mutagenically active polV8. The highest levels of MMS-induced mutagenesis were observed with LexA-like UmuD1389 [33, 35–37] and with UmuC2015 (precursor to pol V12) (Fig. 3). Our studies therefore indicate that full-length UmuD0636, UmuD1174, and UmuD1389 can all undergo damage-induced cleavage to generate mutagenically active UmuD′Ab in vivo. Despite this, UmuD0636 and UmuD1174 did not promote damage-induced mutagenesis when co-expressed with UmuC1173 (precursors to pol V2 and polV6). Such phenotypes may be related to the spatial regulation of full-length UmuD and recombinant UmuD′. Intact UmuD is converted to UmuD′ on a remote RecA nucleoprotein filament, then must locate UmuC1173 in the cell to generate UmuD′2C. In contrast, recombinant UmuD′ is co-transcribed with UmuC1173 and likely forms a UmuD′2C complex as soon as the two proteins are translated.

Four pol VAb form catalytically active mutasomes by binding to A. baumannii-encoded RecA

We have previously shown that the regulation of pol V homologs is a multistep process that is initiated by the formation of stable heterotrimeric UmuD′2C complexes. In E. coli, the UmuC subunit alone is insoluble, and disruption of its interaction with UmuD′2 eliminates damage-induced mutagenesis [21, 27, 39, 40]. The E. coli pol V (UmuD′2C) polymerase complex is catalytically inactive [28, 30]. To synthesize DNA, pol VEc must assemble as a mutasomal complex with a RecA monomer and a molecule of ATP or ATPγS, pol VEc Mut = UmuD′2CEc-RecAEc-ATP/ATPγS [25, 30, 38]. Mutations generated by A. baumannii-encoded UmuC and UmuD′ subunits expressed in E. coli require the expression of RecAAb for the six combinations of mutagenically active pol VAb (Fig. 2A and B). By analogy with pol VEc, the dependence of in vivo mutagenesis on RecAAb suggests that DNA synthesis in vitro requires assembly of pol VAb mutasomes, pol VAb Mut = UmuD′2CAb-RecAAb -ATP/ATPγS (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S7A).

The first step in mutasome formation in vitro is the expression of stable UmuD′2C complexes. We co-expressed (in E. coli) each of the three UmuD′2Ab pol subunits with each of the four His6-tagged UmuCAb pol subunits and identified which of the 12 possible variants of pol VAb formed stable UmuD′2C heterotrimeric polymerase complexes. We were able to purify six out of twelve A. baumannii pol Vs as stable UmuD′2Ab-UmuCAb complexes (pol V1, 2, 4, 6, 11, 12) (Supplementary Figs S4A and S5A). As observed for other pol V homologs [25, 30, 38], the six stable pol VAb variants are catalytically inactive (Supplementary Fig. S5B) and require activation by binding to a RecA monomer transferred from the 3′-end of a RecA* nucleoprotein filament and to ATP/ATPγS, as described below (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs S5 and S7). No stable complexes were formed in vitro for pol V3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10 (Supplementary Figs S4B and C, and S6A). For these latter six combinations, we determined that the individual UmuC and UmuD′2 pol subunits eluted in separate fractions during size exclusion chromatography (Supplementary Figs S4B and C, and S6A). The UmuCAb subunits acting individually are catalytically inactive (Supplementary Fig. S6B).

The in vitro assembly of pol V MutAb is carried out in three steps (Fig. 4A, the ‘Materials and methods’ section, and Supplementary Fig. S7) as described previously for pol VEc Mut [25, 30] and Rum Mut [38]. In step 1, purified pol VAb (UmuD′2CAb) is incubated with a RecAAb* nucleoprotein filament formed with ATPγS on ssDNA-agarose beads. During incubation, a monomer of RecAAb is transferred from the 3′-proximal tip of RecAAb* to form an inactive pol VAb Mut (UmuD′2CAb-RecAAb) complex [25, 30]. Following incubation, in step 2, pol VAb Mut is purified free of RecAAb* using a spin column that separates RecAAb*-beads from pol VAb Mut [25]. In step 3, pol VAb Mut is activated by binding to a molecule of ATP/ATPγS, which triggers a conformation change in which RecA is reoriented relative to UmuC and UmuD′2 [41, 42], allowing the mutasome to bind to and synthesize primer-template (p/t) DNA [25, 41].

Six pol VAb variants (pol V1, 2, 4, 6, 11, 12) assembled in vitro into mutasomal complexes with a monomer of RecAAb (Fig. 4B). Four of the six pol V mutasome variants are catalytically active (pol Mut V1, 2, 4, 6) (Fig. 4C). The four active mutasomes required activation with slowly hydrolysable ATPγS to incorporate nucleotides on short p/t hairpin DNA with a 3 nt single-stranded template overhang (Fig. 4C). A RecA* nucleoprotein filament cannot form on a 3-nt ssDNA region, which is the footprint of a RecA monomer. We have used this short p/t DNA configuration to ensure that DNA synthesis is catalyzed by a “stand-alone” pol V Mut and not by RecA* transactivation of pol V [30].

Pol V6 Mut is the most active in vitro (100% primer extension), followed by pol V2 Mut (68% PE), with similar activities observed with pol V1 Mut (22% PE) and pol V4 Mut (15% PE) (Fig. 4C). These data show that despite having different lengths and homologies (Fig. 1A, B), UmuD′2Ab and UmuCAb variants can “mix-and-match” subunits to form active mutasomal complexes upon association with RecAAb and ATPγS. The sizable differences in pol activities suggest the possibility that each mutasome has distinctive biochemical properties. Pol V11 Mut and pol V12 Mut were inactive in vitro (Fig. 4C), despite being able to assemble as mutasomes with RecAAb (Fig. 4B).

Pol MutAb variant-distinctive biochemical properties

E. coli pol V Mut and Rum Mut exhibit robust TLS at T^T pyrimidine dimers and abasic (apurinic/apyrimidinic) sites, which act as strong replication blocks to high fidelity replication pols [43–46]. We have used DNA templates containing site-specific DNA damage to determine if there are differences in biochemical properties that can distinguish between the multiple mutasomes. We will address two questions: (i) Are any of the biochemically active pol VAb mutasomes proficient in TLS? (ii) Are there mutasome-specific differences in deoxynucleotide misincorporation specificities at a template damage site?

We have measured TLS for the four catalytically active pol VAb mutasomes (Fig. 5). Each mutasome catalyzes robust TLS at a T^T dimer and at an abasic site (Fig. 5), with stalling occurring directly at the lesion and synthesis continuing past the lesion, reaching the end of the template. As observed for the hairpin DNA with a 3 nt template overhang (Fig. 4C), pol V6 Mut is the most active polymerase. The % PE reported in Fig. 5 refers to total primer extension on p/t DNA containing a T^T dimer, an abasic site, and undamaged p/t DNA. The p/t DNA extension activity for each enzyme is essentially the same on the three p/t DNAs, as one would expect, since the polymerase copies DNA without “knowing” that a lesion is present. However, TLS efficiencies do not scale with the enzyme’s p/t DNA extension activity (Fig. 5). When we compared the % extension past the lesion with the % of extension before and across the lesion, the ratio revealed that pol V1 Mut is the most efficient in performing TLS despite showing less p/t DNA extension activity, while the most active pol V6 Mut is the least efficient at TLS and seems to stall opposite the lesion considerably more than the less active mutasomes (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. S8). There are also mutasome-specific differences in deoxynucleotide misincorporation specificities opposite a T^T dimer (Fig. 6). The template strand contains a site-specific T^T dimer with a flanking A at both sides of the lesion (Fig. 6A). A single T is incorporated to reach the T^T dimer site, but T cannot be incorporated opposite the lesion for any of the mutasomes (Fig. 6B, lane 3). TLS is observed for all the mutasomes when four dNTPs are present in the reaction (Fig. 6B, lane 7), as observed in Fig. 5A. Pol V6 Mut performs TLS with either dATP or dCTP present in the reaction (along with dTTP) (Fig. 6B, lanes 4 and 5). Pol V4 performs TLS with dATP (Fig. 6B, lane 4), and a small amount of dGTP incorporation was detected opposite T^T dimer, although bypass was not observed. Pol V1 and Pol V2 perform TLS with dATP (Fig. 6B, lane 4).

Since we have shown that ATP or ATPγS is required for E. coli pol V and Rum pol mutasomes to bind to p/t DNA at the 3′-OH and that ATP hydrolysis, catalyzed by an intrinsic mutasome-encoded DNA-dependent ATPase, results in p/t DNA-pol V Mut dissociation [25], we speculate that in the presence of ATP, incubation with βτAb stabilizes the pol VAb Mut-p/t DNA complex, allowing sufficient time for the addition of ∼3–10 nt prior to ATP hydrolysis-catalyzed enzyme-DNA dissociation (Fig. 7B, lanes 4). Discussion

In E. coli, the UmuD′2C subunits of pol V are co-transcribed from a single umuDC operon during the SOS response [48]. In A. baumannii ATCC 17978, the 3 UmuDAb and 4 UmuCAb subunits are distributed between two umuDCAb operons and one umuDAb gene and two individual umuCAb genes (Fig. 1A). The simultaneous expression of A. baumannii ATCC 17978-encoded umuD and umuC genes allows, in principle, for the formation of as many as twelve variants of error-prone pol VAb in vivo. We have found that six of the twelve are mutagenically active in vivo. These include the two operon-encoded umuDC genes and the individual umuD1389 and umuC2015 genes. At least one combination for all three UmuD′2 and three out of four UmuC subunits forms stable UmuD′2C (i.e. pol V) complexes that subsequently assemble as biochemically active mutasomes by binding to a RecAAb monomer and a molecule of ATP/ATPγS (Fig. 4). The only nonparticipant is UmuC2008, which is inactive in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table S1), although it can nevertheless be expressed in E. coli and purified (Supplemental Fig. S6 and Supplementary Table S1).

Here are eight “take-home” conclusions obtained from the in vivo and in vitro data:

Pol VAb-dependent mutagenesis requires the expression of RecAAb (Fig. 2A and B).Mutagenesis is abolished in the absence of RecAAb (Fig. 2B). A foundation to determine the roles of pol V variants in A. baumannii host bacteria.

Our mutagenic and biochemical characterizations of the twelve pol V variant combinations encoded in A. baumannii ATCC 17978 provide a clear rationale and framework for future studies in A. baumannii. Previously, it was unknown which, if any, of the pol VAb variants were DNA polymerases. The uncertainty in putative polymerase functions stems in part from the fact that all A. baumannii UmuC and UmuD′ vary significantly. There is ∼56%–78% and 82%–86% amino acid sequence homology between UmuC and UmuD′ variants, respectively (Supplementary Figs S2 and S3). The formation of E. coli pol V and ICE-encoded Rum pol requires precise interaction between the homologous UmuC catalytic subunit and post-translationally activated UmuD′2. The last 26 amino acids of the C-terminus of E. coli UmuC are critical for its interaction with UmuD′2 [49–51] and deletion of two [40] or more amino acids eliminates pol V-dependent mutagenesis [49–51]. For A. baumannii, UmuC variants, the last ∼30–50 residues (positions ∼380–422) are not conserved and show considerable variability across homologs (Supplementary Fig. S3). The amino acid variability for each UmuC C-terminus, especially differences in charged amino acids, may determine the ability of individual UmuC variants to form heterotrimeric complexes with the well-conserved UmuD′2 variants (82%–86% homology between UmuD′ variants; Supplementary Fig. S2A). Our results show that UmuC2008, whose C-terminus is 9–10 amino acids longer than the other homologs (Supplementary Fig. S3B), is unable to form any stable and active UmuD′2C complexes (Figs 2, 4, 8, and Supplementary Fig. S6), further reinforcing the likelihood that the C-terminus of UmuC determines the probability of pol VAb variant formation. The other three variants, UmuC0637, UmuC1174, and UmuC2015, form at least one pol V that purifies as a stable UmuD′2C heterotrimeric complex that subsequently assembles into an active mutasome with RecAAb and ATP.

The demonstration that multiple A. baumannii-encoded umuDC can generate mutations in E. coli and form biochemically active pol V mutasomes provides compelling justification to address the roles for each pol V variant in A. baumannii. For example, what might be the contribution made by each umuCAb, catalytic subunit to stress-induced mutagenesis and acquisition of drug resistance? Are different pol V variants induced in response to different stressors? Are any of the variants involved in repairing ssDNA gaps formed during replication fork stalling and restart? Perhaps assembly of the variants may be determined by differential levels of umuC and umuD expression and UmuD′2C and UmuD′2C-RecA-ATP stability. Perhaps the availability of multiple variants provides a fitness advantage.

A. baumannii is a target pathogen central to the goal of eliminating multidrug resistance. Therefore, the key biological question to ask is: “how might the presence of multiple pol V variants accelerate evolution and the acquisition of A. baumannii multidrug resistance? Bacterial pathogens generally exhibit high genome plasticity, enabling rapid adaptation to environmental hazards, host immune evasion, and antimicrobial resistance. Antibiotic options for the treatment of A. baumannii are narrowly circumscribed because of high rates of resistance. Numerous paths can lead to the acquisition of drug resistance in pathogenic bacteria; a potentially important one is the rapid generation of a broad mutational landscape by the induction of SOS error-prone DNA synthesis, which increases the probability of mutations in antibiotic-resistance targets. The presence of multiple homologs of potential pol Vs is a common feature of bacterial pathogens, in which the umuDC-like genes are often identified on mobile genetic elements such as ICE and mega-plasmids [15, 52, 53] and with variants encoded on the chromosome.

Notably, genetic and biochemical regulation of the A. baumannii pol V variants occurs “identically” to E. coli pol V and Rum pol [16, 25, 30, 38, 43]. We have shown that pathogen-encoded Rum pol accelerates the development of multidrug resistance to CIP, Rif, and Amp in E. coli in response to SOS-inducing antibiotic and nonantibiotic stressors, bleomycin, ciprofloxacin, and UV radiation [17]. We have further shown that the impact of Rum pol on the rate of acquisition of drug resistance appeared to exceed potential contributions from the other cellular processes [17]. To the extent that this close analogy carries over to A. baumannii, there is the potential for the A. baumannii pols to drive the rapid acquisition of drug resistance. Each A. baumannii pol V mutasome performs robust TLS at template damage (T^T dimer and abasic moiety, Fig. 5) known to stall moving replication forks [28, 54, 55]. The mutasomes exhibit varying nucleotide misincorporation specificity when copying a T^T dimer (Fig. 6B). Might the A. baumannii pol V variants undergo selective SOS induction in response to different drugs? Might they be specific DNA lesions caused by treatment with antibiotics or exposure to environmental pollutants? We speculate that multiple pol V variants encoded by A. baumannii effectively expand cellular adaptability to exogenous stress, most prominently antibiotics, rapidly accelerating the generation of resistant clones. If so, then possible strategies to delay the acquisition of drug resistance could involve the use of small-molecule inhibitors that target A. baumannii pol V variants individually or collectively.
