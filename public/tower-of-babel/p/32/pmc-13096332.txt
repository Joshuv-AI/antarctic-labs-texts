The role of reactivations during consolidation in the structure and accessibility of episodic autobiographical memories
Authors: Diane Lenormand, Alexandre Gaston-Bellegarde, Eric Orriols, Pascale Piolino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpisodic autobiographical memory (EAM) relies on vivid encoding contexts, with the self as a central reference point. However, EAM models that account for both encoding and consolidation processes in naturalistic settings remain relatively sparse. This study investigated the impact of memory encoding and subsequent reactivations during the retention interval on EAM. Sixty participants incidentally encoded thirty events varying in emotional valence (positive, neutral, negative) and interaction type (action or observation), while navigating through a naturalistic virtual city. At the end of the encoding session, all participants rated each event on several subjective memory-related dimensions, including emotional valence, self-relevance, and anticipated rehearsal. One month later, they completed free recall and recognition tests. Additionally, a subgroup of thirty participants completed intermediate reactivations through free recall tests, administered immediately and one week after encoding. Between-groups analyses showed that one-month free recall performance was enhanced in the group with intermediate reactivations, whereas recognition performance did not differ, except for a significant increase in false recognitions in the reactivation group. Mixed-effects models based on the subjective encoding ratings identified two core predictors of long-lasting EAM, regardless of reactivation: event novelty and the richness of mental imagery. With intermediate reactivations, additional predictors emerged, such as emotional valence and prospective relevance of the event. Overall, this study contributes to predictive models of EAM by demonstrating how reactivations modulate memory consolidation in a naturalistic context, supporting a dynamic view of consolidation aligned with the Multiple-Trace and Trace-Transformation theories. Introduction

What makes an event memorable? Is it its inherent features and subjective evaluation during encoding, or does reactivation through retrieval play a more predominant role? How well can real-life ‘episodes’ be remembered, if they are never rehearsed, retrieved, or reactivated? Episodic memory (EM), as defined by Tulving1, is the cognitive function that directly addresses this issue. EM refers to the long-term memory of everyday, personally experienced events with their rich encoding context. This context includes the characteristics of the events, but also associated spatiotemporal details—what, where, when and how. These elements are bound together to form the memory trace of events, often accompanied by various other features of the personal experience such as phenomenological, sensory-motor and idiosyncratic information features. When such events inherently characterise the self, are integrated into one’s life story, and are thus characterised by a much longer retention time, they are a part of autobiographical memory, more specifically episodic autobiographical memory (EAM). The Self-Memory System, described by Conway and Pleydell-Pearce2, makes the self a central part of EAM. In this model, the working self continuously evaluates ongoing experiences against current personal goals, prioritising the encoding of goal-relevant events. Both EM and EAM are characterised by mental time travel, or autonoetic consciousness, which is the capacity to mentally re-experience past events with a vivid sense of reliving3.

After encoding, the memory trace does not remain static. Instead, it undergoes a number of transformations, often regrouped under the term consolidation4,5. Consolidation is not an instantaneous process; rather, it can span weeks, months or years, beginning with the recruiting of synaptic activity at the level of neurons, occurring over minutes to hours after encoding (Cellular or Synaptic Consolidation, see for instance Lee6, Dudai, et al.7 and Gisquet-Verrier and Riccio8). One predominant model of consolidation, the Multiple-Trace Theory (MTT)9, along with its complementary Trace-Transformation Theory (TTT)10, challenges the standard view of consolidation based on Systems Consolidation11,12.

The Systems Consolidation model posits that memories are initially recorded in the neocortex, with the hippocampus (HP) binding the various pieces of information together13,14. Over time, and with subsequent reactivations, memories are thought to progressively disengage from the HP for retrieval, relying instead solely on the neocortex. In contrast, the MTT argues that the HP encodes and binds information with the neocortex and continues to play an active role in memory retrieval over time. The memory trace spreads across these different structures, and each time a memory is retrieved, a new trace is created, mediated by the HP. Thus, older episodic memories that have been retrieved several times have a strengthened trace due to their multiple representations. Their retrieval always engages the HP, while only semantic memories, by integrating information from multiple sources in the neocortex over time, become independent from it.

The transformation hypothesis states that these changes in neural representations reflect shifts in the nature of the memory itself. Hence, retrieval, or the attempts thereof, contributes to the ongoing evolution and strengthening of the memory trace over time.

However, not all retrieval attempts reinforce EM and EAM (e.g., for autobiographical memories, see Schwabe and Wolf15), and the influence of retrieval, a form of memory reactivation, on subsequent EAM, is not always straightforward (for a recent review, see Roediger and Abel16). Under certain conditions, attempts to remember prior information can increase the chance of accurate recall later on. This is known as the testing effect or retrieval practice effect16–18.

Reactivation, which functions as an updating mechanism19, is influenced by several factors20. While some elements do not have a significant influence on this process—such as using a direct or indirect reactivation, the type of memory test, or the strength of reactivation—, the type of stimulus has a reactivation-induced effect, more pronounced for narratives than for lists of words in the instance of the meta-analysis by Scully, et al.20. Furthermore, the temporal order in which reactivation cues are presented between encoding and subsequent retrieval, as well as the perspective of the picture cues (e.g., angle or height), also influences reactivation-induced changes21.

Contrary to the lingering consolidation hypothesis22, even older memories are susceptible to change. According to the reconsolidation hypothesis19,23, a reactivation-dependent destabilisation process precedes a protein synthesis-dependent re-stabilisation phase. This means that memories become ‘labile’ again and require consolidation-like processes to regain stability, like newly acquired memories. This perspective challenges the classical view that memory consolidates once and is then permanently strengthened and stabilised.

Reconsolidation has been extensively studied in the context of fear memory24,25, though findings have sometimes been difficult to replicate or have been inconsistent19. However, this process also increases susceptibility to memory distortions and false memories. Reactivation can lead to the incorporation of incorrect details or external misinformation, especially when new information is introduced during the labile phase26,27. Studies have shown that reactivation, followed by misleading suggestions, can indeed modify the original memory trace, leading individuals to recall events that never occurred or misattribute details21,28,29.

Interestingly, reconsolidation has also been integrated into the consolidation process itself30, or explained through a context-dependent view of consolidation8. Overall, reactivation seems to support both the MTT and TTT, in line with an integrative view of reconsolidation and consolidation: upon reactivation, memories become labile again, whether they are recent or old. Notably, mere reactivation after a month has been proved to enhance subsequent recall, whereas the absence of such reactivation leads to a decline in memory performance31. Moreover, when reactivation cues match the encoding context, correct recognition improves21. As previously noted, this same reactivation process, if influenced by external factors, can also increase the risk of memory distortions21,26.

Several factors influence EAM from the moment of encoding. Emotion, defined along the orthogonal axes of emotional valence and intensity32,33, is a well-established factor influencing both the formation and subsequent retrieval of EAM34–36. Low levels of emotional activation do not seem sufficient to enhance EM consolidation, while excessively high levels of emotional activation tend to impair retrieval37. The effect of emotions on EAM is modulated by age, with a negativity bias observed in younger populations and a positivity bias emerging with age38. Additionally, trait anxiety may further influence this modulation39. Emotional intensity entails a focal enhancement of central information38,40, while negative valence promotes long-term binding38,41.

Some physiological measures seem to be sensible to changes in emotional content. For instance, the mean heart rate (HR) decelerates when viewing negative items compared to positive or neutral ones42–45. Moreover, an elevated mean HR, when paired with high emotional intensity, predicts better memory performance46,47. Electrodermal activity (EDA) is another physiological marker of emotional processing. Emotional pictures typically elicit larger EDA amplitudes than neutral ones48, and changes in the EDA also correlate with the emotional intensity49,50. Physiological measures can also be used to study the self. For example, both heart rate and skin conductance have been proposed as objective indices of self-related processing51. For instance, heart-rate variability can be used to examine bodily self-awareness and interoceptive processes, showing differential responses depending on whether biofeedback is self-congruent or other-congruent52. Relatedly, meditation focusing on the heart rhythm leads to a qualitative expansion of the sense of self (e.g., increased self-esteem)53. An example of the echo of the self in skin conductance is provided by the rubber-hand illusion, which illustrates the notion of body ownership (see for instance Alimardani, et al.54, Armel and Ramachandran55 or Braun, et al.56)memories. Finally, in real-life-like contexts (evaluation of real or fictional emotional pictures), Makowski, et al.57 showed that the EDA amplitude is linked to autobiographical relevance, and to a lesser extent, conceptual relevance, further supporting its role as physiological marker of meaningful or self-relevant experiences.

In the case of flashbulb memories, the personal importance attached to the event and the intensity of emotional response influence how well the event is retained in memory58, with the extreme novelty of the event also playing a role59. Talarico and Rubin60 cite emotional affect, particularly emotional intensity, and rehearsal as key factors in the formation of flashbulb (or vivid, see Rubin and Kozin61) memories. They also describe subjective, self-related processes such as significance and surprise as crucial in determining the formation of such memories. Flashbulb memories often concern real-life events that are common to many, such as the death of a celebrity or a terrorist attack. Studying real, daily-life events is more challenging due to the lack of control over memory retrieval. To circumvent this issue, researchers have used wearable cameras in staged events, allowing participants to carry out predefined tasks while wearing cameras that automatically capture images from their perspective. This approach provides an ecologically valid task with strong experimental control and the necessary information to check retrieval accuracy (see for instance St Jacques and Schacter21; Mair, et al.62; Jeunehomme, et al.63).

Other studies have employed daily recordings, such as diaries64, videos65 or audio recordings66, to document real-life events. Based on these considerations, we previously investigated EAM under real life conditions, aligning with its multi-faceted and rich definition. We examined the evolution of personal events in daily life, recorded from a first-person perspective using a wearable camera, and rated in the evening of their encoding67. Memory was subsequently tested five days and one month after the end of the ten-day encoding period. Using machine learning (ML) algorithms, we found a strong role of mental imagery, self-reference, and prospection (anticipated details) during encoding and first recall in predicting whether events would be remembered or forgotten after one month. Other studies have also attempted to predict EAM based on various factors. For instance, using a multilevel multinominal mixed-effect model, Wolf and Zimprich68 found that long-term memories from the reminiscence bump in autobiographical memory are predicted by increased personal significance compared to pre-bump events, greater novelty compared to post-bump ones, and a more positive emotional valence compared to memories from other time periods. ML approaches have also been used to distinguish autobiographical from non-autobiographical cued memories based on brain activation patterns observed with fMRI. Erez, et al.69 successfully classified autobiographical memories, which corresponded to passively viewed videos recorded from a real-life environment one week before scanning. Similarly, a week after a virtual encoding of war-related military patrols in a virtual environment, Malta, et al.47 found a strong predictive role of physiological data in voluntary memory retrieval in participants with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), based on their regression analyses. Tang, et al.70 predicted memory for realistic episodes of a TV show up to one year post-encoding and found that several properties played an important role in this prediction, including auditory inputs, actions, the depiction and elicitation of emotions, and the presence of specific characters.

Following these findings, we aimed to further investigate the formation of long-term EAM and the impact of intermediate reactivations using an ecological and controlled encoding setting. Virtual reality (VR) allows to design controlled laboratory experiments while maintaining high ecological validity, thus enabling the exploration of cognitive functions that closely resemble real-life experiences. The virtual environments (VE) can simulate various real-world settings, such as museums, parks, apartments, or cities. In a recent series of VR experiments71, we asked participants to explore an immersive virtual city, where they encountered 30 events varying in emotional valence (valence factor: negative, neutral, or positive) and interaction level (interaction factor: active interaction or passive observation). During encoding, we additionally recorded physiological measures, including HR, respiration rate, and EDA, to collect objective data related to each event. After this incidental encoding session, participants provided subjective ratings of the events across multiple memory-relevant dimensions, using scales similar to those applied in prior studies involving wearable cameras67.

In the present study, using the same experimental paradigm, participants completed surprise memory tests (free recall of events) at different intervals: some were tested immediately, one week later, and one month after encoding (three-recall or Reactivation Group), while others were tested only once at one month (one-recall or No-Reactivation Group). Our primary goal was to compare one-month EAM performance between the two groups, and to identify, for each group, the predictors of one-month EAM performance. Predictors were drawn primarily from subjective ratings and, exploratorily, from objective measures (i.e., physiological data) collected during the encoding phase of each event.

Specifically, we sought to address the following questions: Are intermediate memory tests necessary to sustain good memory retention of the events encountered during the virtual walk after one month? Do the mechanisms that support memory consolidation operate similarly in both groups? In other words, would the one-month free recall be predicted by the same encoding variables, even in the absence of intermediate reactivations and without any additional experimental manipulation?

If long-term memory performance is similar in both groups (with and without reactivation), it would be reasonable to assume that similar consolidation processes operated in both cases, in line with the classical systems consolidation model11,12. Conversely, if memory performance differs between groups, with better performance in the Reactivation Group, this would align more closely with the MTT, which posits that each retrieval creates a new trace that strengthens the existing memory network. In this case, different processes may underlie long-term memory performance at one month depending on the presence or absence of intermediate reactivations.

We hypothesised that the Reactivation Group would show better EAM free recall performance after one month16,31. We also expected different processes to support memory maintenance across the two groups. More precisely, we predicted that in the No-Reactivation Group, one-month memory retention would be primarily driven by the frequency of real-life occurrence58,60 and by emotional factors at encoding38,72. In contrast, for the Reactivation Group, intermediate reactivations were expected to modulate this prediction, with additional influences from self-related processes67. Finally, we expected physiological signals at encoding to play a role in predicting one-month memory outcomes in both groups, with greater heart and respiration rates and a higher EDA response amplitude, associated with successful recalled events.

Methods

Participants

The population was comprised of a total of 60 healthy participants, recruited specifically through the academic network platform of the RISC-UAR 3332 CNRS (Relay of information on the sciences of cognition), and among students from the Bachelor’s Degree of Psychology and students from the Medicine Faculty of the Université Paris Cité.

Exclusion criteria were a high depression score73, high travel sickness74 and anxiety75. The participants were instructed not to take alcohol, drugs, or medications prior to their visits to the laboratory. The inclusion of the participants in the two groups was serial: the Reactivation Group was included from late April to early June 2022 (with three participants included in October and November), and the No-Reactivation Group from October 2022 to January 2023. Exclusion criteria were signs of depression (score > 15 on the Beck Depression Inventory73), high travel sickness (score > 7 on the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire74), and anxiety (score > 65 on the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory-Form Y75). Further exclusion criteria included a psychiatric, neurological, or cardiac condition, head trauma, drug addiction, recent neurosurgery, pacemaker, the need for a respiratory help device, paralysis and amputation, pregnancy, frequent dizziness, vertigo, or nausea, being a free diver and being susceptible to develop severe COVID-19 symptoms. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and audition and were French speakers.

For the Reactivation Group, the participants were aged 18 to 40 years old (M = 26.27, SD 7.08; Female ratio: 66.67%) and mostly were students (63.33%). Four of them were left-handed (13.33%). Among the 34 participants who came for the encoding session, three experienced and reported cybersickness and, therefore, could not complete the first session (8.82% total cybersickness); one participant did not complete the second and third sessions and was excluded from the analyses. For the No-Reactivation Group, the participants were aged 18 to 35 years old (M = 22.47, SD = 5.41; Female ratio: 73.33%) and were mostly students (73.33%), with two of them being left-handed (6.67%). Again, 34 participants came to the laboratory for the encoding session. Among them, four participants were excluded from the analyses: three experienced and reported cybersickness, and were therefore unable to complete the first session (8.82% total cybersickness), and one participant was excluded because they did not meet the age criteria for inclusion.

At the 5% significance level, using the Grubbs test (outliers package76), our results could not reject the hypothesis that both the lowest and the highest mean scores at the one-month free recall, averaged on the participants, are not outliers for each reactivation group. Therefore, no participant was excluded from the analyses.

All participants gave written informed consent and were compensated for their participation (10€ per hour). The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University Paris Cité (CER U-Paris Cité) (N° IRB: 00012021-60), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. It was also authorized by the Data Protection Officer (DPO) and registered in the processing activities of Université Paris Cité (FR-UPC-20220401-MC²Lab_DLD_P), following the RGPD guidelines.

Procedure

The number of recall sessions was the main between-group manipulation. The Reactivation Group completed three surprise memory tests: immediately, one week and one month after encoding. In contrast, the No-Reactivation Group completed only a single surprise memory test, one month after encoding. Figure 1 describes the general procedure for the two groups.

Pre-encoding cognitive tests

Both groups first underwent a pre-VR evaluation with a series of cognitive tests to control their matching: standard visual EM (Family Pictures test from the Wechsler Memory Scale III77), EAM (TEMPau78), for memories from a week and from a month before the encoding session), short-term memory (forward digit span79), executive functions (trail-making test80), verbal fluency tests for a letter, animal names and self-characteristics81, and their descriptive image ability (through the description of a picture from a VE resembling the one they would experience in the VR session); but also their self-images (Tennessee self-concept scale, TSCS82), and their emotional state (Befindlichkeits-Skala, BfS83). The latter was evaluated before and after VR to estimate the potential emotional impact of the VR task. The complete list of tests and scales used in this study can be found in the Supplementary Materials (Sup. Mat. 1: Family Pictures test, TEMPau, digit span, trail-making test, verbal fluency tests, description ability, Tennessee self-concept scale, Emotional state, embodiment, presence, cybersickness).

VR training session

A VR training session followed this initial evaluation. In this, the participants could familiarise themselves with the virtual navigation. They followed a predefined path, similar to the one that would be presented in the main encoding session, but in a different part of the virtual town. The training session did not have a time limit and the participants learned how to interact with different types of events: an observation, an interaction with a smartphone, and an interaction with objects to move (apples in a basket).

VR encoding protocol

The two groups did the same VR encoding session in the same VE, and in the same experimental conditions. The virtual walk, in a virtual city resembling Paris, France, comprised 30 unique events (each lasting approximately 20 s), which were encountered in three paths shown in a counterbalanced order across the participants in each group (see Sup. Mat. 2 for further details about VR hardware, VE and its characteristics and specificities). The participants had to follow a predetermined path, marked by a white, luminous line on the pavement, and could either observe or interact with the events. The objects that should be interacted with were highlighted in yellow (see a path video in Sup. Mat. 2). During the interactive events, the participants thus had to press a real button on the controller, which increased their physical involvement in the VE compared to mere navigation and observation. These interactions involved moving toward an interactive object and ‘grabbing’ it to accomplish an action. Of the 30 events, 15 were observed and 15 were interactive. The events also varied in emotional valence and could be positive (12 events), neutral (6 events), or negative (12 events) (Fig. 2; complete list in Sup. Mat. 3).

Examples of events in the virtual environment with their main different characteristics. Positive observation: “A couple kisses on the street.” Neutral observation: “A jogger runs past”. Negative observation: “In a line in front of the post office, a woman loses patience. She decided to cut the line while complaining about the situation”. Positive interaction: “A man is playing the piano beautifully; the participant puts a few coins in his hat”. Neutral interaction: “A man asks what time it is; the participant shows him the locked screen of their phone to answer”. Negative interaction: “The participant walks near a trash can that catches fire. They try to put it out with an extinguisher, but it does not work (though the fire is still contained).”.

The encoding was incidental, as the participants were only instructed to assess their enjoyment of the environment for daily-life tasks. Furthermore, the encoding was unique, as each event was viewed only once by each participant, with no intentional repetitions or systematic patterns introduced in the VE.

Additionally, both groups of participants were instructed to take a photograph of anything they felt was personally meaningful (e.g., personal preferences, beliefs, goals or memories). These photographs could depict either the VE in general or one of the specific events of interest.

Physiological measures and eye-tracking

During the VR encoding session, physiological data was recorded, including HR mean, respiration rate, and EDA (Sup. Mat. 4 for physiological and eye-tracking hardware, data and pre-processing). Of note, no physiological data were recorded during the recall phase. Eye-tracking was also implemented during encoding for the No-Reactivation Group as a control measure. Its purpose was to assess how much time each participant spent looking at zones containing the events of interest (with missing data for one participant). Since the No-Reactivation Group did not complete the immediate free recall, this measure allowed us to check whether any potential retrieval deficits at one month could be due to insufficient encoding.

Post-encoding questionnaires and subjective ratings

Immediately after the VR encoding session, the participants rated their levels of embodiment84, presence85, and cybersickness74 during the VR experience.

Furthermore, at the end of the encoding session (and after the free recall for the Reactivation Group), the participants rated each event on several subjective scales described in Table 1.

The participants subjectively assessed the variables on scales from 0 to 5 (emotional intensity: very weak to very strong; emotional valence: very negative to very positive; self-relevance: no relevance to very high relevance; remembering: ‘only knowing of it’ to ‘the event is remembered exactly as it happened’; perspective: third-person perspective to first-person perspective; self-concept: does not reflect versus completely reflects goals etc.; thinking: never to very often; conversation: never to very often; anticipated details: very few to a lot; reliving: not at all to completely; mental images: none to a lot; frequency of real-life encounter: never to very often).

Memory retrieval protocol

After the encoding session and the post-VR evaluation of embodiment, presence, and cybersickness, the Reactivation Group did a surprise free recall (the immediate memory test) around ten minutes after the end of the encoding session. A week after encoding, the Reactivation Group performed a second free recall, followed by a final free recall a month after encoding (delay: M = 29.53, SD = 3.18), which was also completed by the No-Reactivation Group (delay: M = 29.1, SD = 2.72) (detailed instruction in Sup. Mat. 5). The free recalls lasted a maximum of 10 min to have enough time to try to recall as many events with as many details as possible. The one-month free recall was followed by a recognition test of the events in the VE for both groups. With 25% of lures (similar events as those encoded), the encoded events were shown as static images from the event, and participants had to assess whether they recognised this static image as being one of the encoded events. They also had to provide a Remember/Know/Guess (RKG) judgment86. They were instructed to provide a ‘Remember’ judgement for events they could relive and retrieve many details about, a ‘Know’ judgement for events they knew they had encountered but could not retrieve specific details about, and a ‘Guess’ judgement for events they were ensure about.

For the Reactivation Group, each retrieval session ended with the participants’ subjective evaluation of all the events (immediate recall and one-month recall) or only the events recalled (one-week recall), using subjective scales described in Table 1. For this rating, the events were presented as short excerpt videos (GIFs) of the events without context, that is, with a white and grey background. The No-Reactivation Group subjectively rated all the events at the end of the encoding session and after the free recall and recognition test at a month. More precisely, the participants were asked to ‘rate the different events that they encountered during the virtual walk’ (translated from French).

The free recalls were rated by the experimenters on an EAM scale evaluating the main objective components of EM—what, where, when –, as well as subjective components—emotion, internal thoughts, self-relevance (Fig. 3). More precisely, the presence of any correct, objective information of such elements in the recall provided the attribution of 1 point (What/Where/When binary scores). Additional details lead to the attribution of up to 2 supplementary points in each component (What/Where/When details). ‘What’ elements concerned the contents of the event: multimodal elements such as visual, auditory or sensorimotor details. ‘Where’ elements concerned both egocentric and allocentric spatial details. ‘When’ elements concerned the temporal location of the event in the path, its temporal contiguity and temporal source. Based on the what/where/when binary scores, 1 point was also attributed to each binding type (what-where, what-when, and what-where-when binding scores). The subjective elements result in the attribution of 1 point for each mention of the emotional experience, self-relatedness and internal thoughts about the event. This scoring method allowed to differentiate between an objective episodic score, the EM score (sum of what-where-when EM components), and an episodic autobiographical one, the EAM score (sum of EM components and subjective scores) (see detail of the instructions, scoring procedure and an example of a scored event in Sup. Mat. 5).

Quotation of the memories. The episodic score (EM) is the sum of What, Where, When and their details (maximum of 9), the episodic autobiographical score (EAM) is the sum of the EM score and the subjective reports, which are the reported emotion, self-relevance, and internal thoughts (maximum of 12).

Both at the end of the encoding session and at the end of the one-month retrieval session, each participant was asked some questions about their enjoyment of the experiment (e.g., Did you enjoy using the VR setup?/Were the instructions of this last session clear?). The main goal of these questions was to provide contextual information for the virtual and memory tasks, serving as a control for interindividual differences in overall enjoyment and satisfaction. Consolidation-related questions (e.g., How frequently have you been thinking about the VR session that you did when you last came to the lab?/Generally speaking, how would you rate your tiredness over the period of time since you last came to the lab?) were asked at the end of the one-month retrieval session for both groups, as well as at the end of the one-week retrieval session for the Reactivation Group.

Data analyses

The data were analysed using R87 (version 4.4). Missing physiological data in both groups were replaced with the mean value for the corresponding event. In the Reactivation Group, 32 values were missing for HR mean (3.56% of the dataset), 33 for respiration mean (3.67%), and 451 out of 900 events did not elicit an EDA response. For the No-Reactivation Group, 32 values were missing for HR mean (3.56%), 23 for respiration mean (2.56%), and 481 out of 900 events did not elicit an EDA response. The Psych package88 was also used for the dataset description.

Preliminary control checks

First, the equivalence between the two groups was evaluated using non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests (rstatix and coin R packages) on the descriptive and cognitive variables including age, retention delay until one-month recall, depression, anxiety, transport sickness and cognitive assessments. Further comparisons included the BfS, embodiment, presence, VR cybersickness, satisfaction, enjoyment (at the end of both the encoding and one-month recall sessions), and consolidation-related questions at the end of the one-month session. A Bonferroni correction was applied at the group level. Any variable showing a significant difference between the two groups would be included as covariates in subsequent analyses.

Second, some specific control checks were carried out for each group, including an analysis of the evolution of the participants’ emotional state and the relationship between the one-month recall and consolidation-related questions. For the No-Reactivation Group only, the time spent looking at the events was also examined.

The equivalence at encoding of the physiological data between the two groups was controlled.

Between-group comparison

For the between-group comparisons of rating and performance for each probed item (see for instance Rubin, et al.89), non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests were carried out to compare subjective assessments (encoding and one-month delay) and retrieval memory scores (free recall) at one month. A Benjamini & Hochberg correction90 was applied to control for the false discovery rate at the group level. The number of events retrieved and recognition performance (number of hits, false alarms and RKG rates) were also compared.

Then the influence of emotion and interaction factors during VR on memory performance at one month was investigated using a three-way robust ANOVA between Reactivation and No-Reactivation groups, implemented with the WRS2 package (t3way function)91. Scores were averaged on the modalities of the emotion valence factor of the events for each participant.

For each group, we finally examined whether memory performance was better for events that participants chose to photograph. For the Reactivation Group, data from 4 out of 30 participants were excluded from this analysis due to missing data.

Predicting one-month memory performance from encoding

To predict one-month memory performance, we used mixed-effects models for each group. First, subjective assessments at the end of encoding served as predictors for one-month EAM scores. Linear mixed models were fitted using residual maximum likelihood via the lmer function from the lmerTest package92. Participants and events were modelled with random intercepts, and t-tests for significance were computed using Satterthwaite’s method. Conditional and marginal R2 values were computed with the multilevelTools package93, where conditional R2 estimates the variance explained by the entire model, and marginal R2 estimates variance explained by fixed effects alone. Second, physiological measures collected during encoding were used as predictors of one-month EAM scores. For each group, separate mixed-effects models were run for each physiological variable (heart rate, respiration rate, and EDA).

Models featuring the recognition were fitted using logistic models (generalized linear mixed models fit by maximum likelihood, Laplace Approximation), where hit rates for old trials were coded as 1 and misses coded as 0. An estimation of R² was performed using an equivalent mixed model.

Discussion

The main purpose of this study was to examine the influence of reactivation during the consolidation phase on long-term EAM encoded in a naturalistic setting. Going further than most previous studies on consolidation, this novel study proposed an ecological experiment as close to real-life conditions as possible while maintaining laboratory control, both in the manipulated factors and in retrieval accuracy checks. Based on existing consolidation models, specifically the MTT9,94 and TTT10, we assumed that the encoding mechanisms underlying one-month memory performance would differ depending on the presence of previous reactivations. Specifically, we expected better performance and additional encoding-related processes when retrieval was preceded by reactivations. Consistent with these predictions, the results revealed significant differences between the Reactivation and No-Reactivation Group, with superior free recall in the Reactivation Group, whereas recognition performance did not differ between groups (with the exception of more ‘Remember’ responses in false alarms for the Reactivation Group). On top of core predictors present in both groups (the frequency of real-life encounter, with rarer events being better recalled, and mental images, with more visual images being associated with better memory), additional predictors emerged in the Reactivation Group (such as prospective aspects).

The discussion is structured around two main objectives: (a) comparing memory performance under ecological encoding conditions between groups with and without reactivations; and (b) identifying the predictors of the one-month memory traces, based primarily on subjective ratings and, exploratorily, on physiological measures.

Which conditions lead to better memory performances in a naturalistic setting?

With or without reactivation?

With equivalent participant groups on the encoding measures, overall better delayed memory scores were observed in the Reactivation Group compared to the No-Reactivation Group. In other words, both EM and EAM scores were higher for the Reactivation Group, but the difference in the EAM scores between the two groups was entirely driven by the objective episodic component, i.e., what, where, when elements, rather than by the subjective one, i.e., internal thoughts, emotions, self-reference. Without experimentally controlled reactivation, there was thus a loss of details and of the ability to recall the different contextual components of the episode. This result is in line with EAM retention curves reported in prior research (e.g., see Rubin95 or Piolino, et al.96), and with the semanticisation process of EM and EAM over time2,66,78,97. However, the potential gains in semantic information which would confirm a generalisation were not probed in this study. Here, it is only possible to conclude about the impoverishment of the EAM trace over time without reactivation. Although the No-Reactivation Group freely recalled fewer events than the Reactivation Group, consistent with previous works such as Wichert, et al.31, their recognition performance remained equivalent to that of the Reactivation Group (notably, hit rates with ‘R’ answers). This suggests a weakening of strategic access during EAM free recall rather than a complete loss of core memory content, specifically reflecting a decline in generative retrieval compared to direct retrieval98. Interestingly, the Reactivation Group produced more false recognitions based on a sense of recollection, thus indicating an increase in false memories with high subjective confidence and illusory re-experiences21. The finding underscores how the reactivation of memories can enrich memory content but also increase susceptibility to distortions and false memories26,27,99, in a view akin to the reconsolidation theory19,26,27 (see Introduction). In the present experiment, successive reactivations likely strengthened the memory trace (for a review, see Roediger and Abel16). At the same time, successive retrievals of the virtual walk may also have introduced distortions and interferences between the memory traces of encountered events, thereby creating confusion when participants identified new events (lures)26. With reactivations, participants were more likely to identify lures as being remembered. This result is particularly interesting, as no new external information was introduced by the experimenters during the reactivations.

Moreover, at the one-month assessment, the No-Reactivation Group reported higher ratings of remembering, thinking, and mental images than the Reactivation Group. This could reflect their first memory reactivation occurring at that moment, triggered by viewing the pictures of the events (albeit without the audio, full content of the event and spatiotemporal context). In contrast, the Reactivation Group (who underwent two prior reactivations) maintained events retrieval without a significant performance decline over a month. Of note, contrary to previous literature, there was no clear testing effect as we found no significant increase in memory scores with the number of reactivations in the Reactivation Group16. However, although no cues were presented before the free recalls, it is possible that each subjective assessment acted as a weak, delayed cue for subsequent recall which aligns with previous studies (e.g., St Jacques and Schacter21). This is particularly relevant for the where component of EAM (both ego- and allocentric information), that augmented in the Reactivation Group at the one-week recall71.

On the role of valence and self in memory consolidation

Apart from the significant effect of reactivation, there was also an effect of the valence factor, with both negative and positive events being better recalled than neutral ones, across both groups36–38. Interestingly, the two-way interaction between valence and interaction (i.e., being actor or observer of the event) factors underlined the dual role of emotion and self in reinforcing the formation of the EAM trace (although this result should be carefully considered, see limits below). Gutchess and Kensinger72 described a model in which self-relevance and emotions share encoding processes, with common electrophysiological signatures and brain activation patterns. The self-referential effect in memory can occur independently of emotional valence at encoding, as shown in prior studies (e.g., Pereira, et al.100). However, the interaction between self-reference and emotional content can enhance memory encoding under some conditions. For instance, research shows that deciding whether an emotional event is self-related significantly impacts memory retention101. This interaction also affects source memory, as negative self-relevant information can interfere more with source recollection compared to neutral or positive information100. Therefore, while self-referential processing can boost memory performance regardless of emotional valence, combining self-reference and emotional content can lead to more robust memory encoding in some contexts.

The influence of the self could indeed be observed in several ways: the subjective assessments allowed the participants to rate how the events related to them on several scales linked to the self, with notably the self-relevance, perspective and self-concept. Moreover, in addition to the active navigation in immersive VR, one of the manipulated factors was the interaction: in half of the events, the participants could interact with an object, while in the other half they simply observed. Additionally, the participants could take photos of events and of their surroundings in the VE that they found self-relevant. Having taken a photograph of subjectively assessed self-relevant events in the VE led to an improved recall at a month, with reactivations during the consolidation phase. We found that neutral, interactive events were less well recalled than neutral, observed ones, while the opposite was true for positive events (but not significant for negative ones).

Triberti and Riva102 described a model linking intentions, actions, and the sense of presence in which agency and control allow individuals to enact intentional actions by using environmental items, and then monitor the consequences of these actions. This aligns with the framework of the Self as described by Damasio103, which operated at three levels: the first level at the basic, motor-based self-awareness distinguishing between what is the Self and non-Self, the second level regarding feelings and finer perception (emotional and perceptual self-awareness), and the third in the involvement of AM and self-concept knowledge. The subject perceives, moves and acts in a defined environment which reflects knowledge of the consequences of their actions. In the present study, the Self was most likely particularly involved, even in the observation modality, as the participants moved by themselves in the VE, had to follow a path and evaluate their environment to take pictures of any self-related element. The self-choice effect introduced by the photographs taken by the subjects during encoding showed that, over the sixty participants, having photographed an event led to a better memory of it a month later, in line with previous studies (for instance, Baldwin, et al.104). Interestingly, this result contradicts the typical photo-taking impairment effect (see for instance Soares and Storm105), in which photographed items are remembered less well than non-photographed ones. In the present study, however, the photo-taking activity was embedded within a focused task, i.e., the assessment of the relevance of elements in the virtual environment to the self, which may have circumvented this effect106. The active versus observation interaction in neutral events would also suggest that self-performed action might not always enhance memorisation, as it did for positive events (see enactment effect, Tuena, et al.107). In a previous VR study, Plancher, et al.108, despite an enhancement of the spatial component of EM, found that motor control in the VE impaired recognition of factual information. The authors hypothesised a detrimental effect of the attentional cost of motor control, which could lead to a dual-task-like situation. Jebara, et al.109 showed that low navigation control (reducing cognitive load within the VE) and itinerary choice, or volitional control, both enhance EM and binding memory. However, high navigation control similar to real-life driving and passively viewing the VE led to lesser EM performances. Here, exploratory analyses (Sup. Mat. 7) revealed that executive functions correlated with the action modality for the Reactivation Group, hinting at the fact that this modality could indeed be more demanding in terms of attentional cost. Moreover, these exploratory analyses also revealed that the predictors of long-term EAM were more numerous and various for the action modality than for the observation modality for both groups; it is likely that an active interaction with the VE, on top of an active navigation, thus engages more executive functions and more predictive processes to successfully encode and consolidate information compared to its observation counterpart.

Overall, these first findings show that reactivation plays a crucial role in maintaining EAM over time, preserving objective episodic components (what, where, when, binding) while also increasing false memories. Additionally, self-referential processing and emotional valence influence memory consolidation. These findings contribute to our understanding of how memory is structured and transformed over time in naturalistic, yet experimentally controlled settings (see integrative theoretical discussion below).

Prediction of long-lasting EAM relies on several processes, including reactivation

Predictive role of subjective assessment at encoding

The different free recall predictions highlighted, for both groups, the role of the frequency of real-life encounters and mental images. This first finding aligns with previous studies on real-life, long-term memories, such as vivid61 or flashbulb58 memories, in which the novelty or surprise of an event contributes to subsequent memory performance (see for instance Talarico and Rubin60). In the same vein, mental images, or visual mental imagery, are highly characteristic of both EM and EAM, contributing to the feeling of recollection3,110. Thus, these two variables predict memory performance overall, independently of reactivation, and define the core encoding processes underlying long-lasting memory consolidation: novelty and the evocation of visual mental images.

Without reactivation, no additional factor significantly predicted the one-month free recall. However, memory performance without reactivation could rely on other processes not probed in the present study. Interestingly, when comparing the subjective assessments of the two groups, the only variable at encoding that differed was the sense of remembering. This may explain why this variable stood out in the Reactivation Group model for one-month free recall. However, no other variables accounted for differences between the models.

For the Reactivation Group, additional encoding processes were recruited, including emotional valence, remembering, thinking, and conversation. Emotional valence is a well-known factor influencing long-lasting memory38. More precisely, we found that negative valence predicted good memory performance, whereas emotional intensity did not, which is in line with previous studies such as Mickley and Kensinger111. Memory consolidation may rely on the relevance of emotions for future endeavours (see also working self, Conway110), making consolidation an adaptive process that is influenced by emotional arousal, valence, and relevance to goals, either immediately during encoding or retroactively112. At one month, the recollection was predicted by mental images for the Reactivation Group and by emotional valence for the No-Reactivation Group. This further highlights the importance of mental imagery reinforced by reactivations, and shows that events benefit from a negative emotion in recollection even without reactivation, reinforcing the role of emotions in memory consolidation. Likewise, rehearsal is a well-established influence on EAM, with a higher number of rehearsals leading to higher ratings of vividness during subsequent recognition113. Thus, with reactivation, retrospective and prospective aspects (i.e., remembering, thinking, conversation) and emotional valence (absent without reactivation), emerged as significant predictors of the one-month EAM free recall. Long-lasting EAM was better when the participants estimated, at encoding, that they would often think about the event, and worse when they estimated they would not think about it much, suggesting an important metacognitive component of long-term EAM67. This reflects the processes at play during encoding in Conway’s self-memory system model110, in which the working self selects information coherent with current goals and aspirations. The self is also involved in consolidation, as information can be integrated into the conceptual self. Thus, the prospective estimation that internal rehearsal will be important may reflect the selection by the working self of encoded information, leading to more successful integration into long-term information databases114,115.

All in all, building memory engram is not a one-time, one-shot process but a gradually constructed one. When the two groups were considered together, with the reactivation condition added as a covariate, emotional valence, sense of remembering, mental imagery, and frequency of real-life encounters were confirmed as relevant predictive variables, highlighting their roles in understanding long-term EAM formation.

Predictive role of physiological data at encoding

Physiological data, such as skin conductance and heart rate at encoding, played distinct and unexpected roles in memory performance across the two groups. In the Reactivation Group, EDA response while navigating the VE enriched with specific events predicted their one-month recall, whereas no such effect was found in the No-Reactivation Group. The moderate levels of cybersickness reported by the participants could have prevented the emergence of reliable predictive effects116, but they were similar in the two groups. Interestingly, in the Reactivation Group, exploratory analyses revealed significant correlations between the EDA response and the objective memory scores (what, where, when), while no such correlation was found with the total subjective memory scores (emotions, internal thoughts, self-relevance) at one month. The influence of the EDA response was thus exclusively sustained by its correlation with the objective episodic component. In contrast, no correlation between EDA and any memory sub-score in the No-Reactivation Group was found. It is therefore possible that the EDA response is an indicator, during encoding, of the sustained trace over time of episodic information. If this episodic information is lost, for instance due to a lack of reactivations during consolidation, then the EDA response will not be a predictor of long-term recall. This link between EDA and memory performance can be explained by the role of autonomic arousal in enhancing encoding and consolidation processes. EDA reflects the activation of the autonomic nervous system, which is closely tied to emotional salience and attentional engagement117. When individuals experience heightened physiological arousal during encoding, particularly in response to meaningful or emotionally significant events, the amygdala and hippocampus interact more effectively to strengthen memory traces118. This association between EDA and memory recall restricted to the reactivation group, suggests that physiological engagement at encoding, combined with later memory reactivation, facilitates trace stabilisation and access to stored experiences. In contrast, the absence of this effect in the No-Reactivation Group implies that without reinforcement through reactivations during consolidation, autonomic arousal alone may not be sufficient to predict long-term retrieval benefits119.

Theoretical contributions: a dynamic view of the prediction of EAM through time

This study on naturalistic long-term EAM provides new evidence regarding the formation of long-lasting memories depending on the presence or absence of intermediate reactivations during the consolidation phase, and highlights the mechanisms underlying consolidation. This experimental manipulation of reactivation may not have prevented the participants to rehearse or refresh the events by themselves, but no link was found between the one-month EAM score and consolidation-related questions about the rehearsal of the VR session. The findings prove that, regardless of reactivation status, common variables, namely the frequency of real-life encounter and vivid mental imagery, predict long-term EAM retention.

We suggest that during encoding, an emphasis on the novelty of an event relative to daily life, coupled with the presence of detailed mental images about this event, play a key role in successful retention over time, irrespective of what happens during the consolidation phase. However, it is noteworthy that these factors alone are insufficient to sustain a detailed memory trace over time, as memory performance in free recall was poorer in the absence of reactivations. Additionally, with the presence of intermediate reactivations, emotional valence and prospective aspects (anticipated internal and external rehearsals) seemed to support the maintenance of a rich, detailed memory trace over time (Fig. 5).

Overall, these findings offer important new insights into the mechanisms underlying memory consolidation. Referring back to our initial hypotheses, the results of this study align with the MTT9 and TTT10. Indeed, both models state that consolidation is a dynamic process, in which successive retrievals can integrate new information, thereby strengthening and stabilising the existing memory trace as it evolves. Within this dynamic framework of memory consolidation, our results suggest that additional processes operate to preserve the memory trace over time. Specifically, the predictors of long-lasting EAM are not static but evolve as individuals engage in more retrievals to reinforce the memory trace22,120. Without the introduction of any additional information21,28,29, encoded events thus seem to be strengthened by successive retrievals through time and sustained by various factors. This study substantiates a theoretical and empirical foundation for the dynamic nature of memory consolidation, highlighting the crucial role of encoding, reactivations and retrievals in sustaining episodic autobiographical memories over time. Strengths, limits and perspectives

Our experimental paradigm, taking place in immersive VR, considered an array of subjective and objective variables recorded during encoding. With intermediate reactivations, memory performances in free recalls are better, though there is no loss regarding recognition without reactivations. At the core of the mechanisms underlying the recall lie the frequency of real-life encounter and visual mental imagery, with additional processes at play in the case of reactivations: emotional valence, prospective aspects and the sense of remembering. This study thus sheds new light on the processes involved in ecological consolidation, hinting at a potential deficit in retrieval during free recalls without any experimental reactivation, rather than a complete loss of information. The additional processes present with reactivation likely sustain the rich retrieval of EAM. These processes are present as early as encoding but are only potentiated or activated when reactivation plays an active role during the consolidation phase, that is, when the memory is actively retrieved, likely during the construction phase of generative retrieval114,122. This interpretation aligns with the MTT model of consolidation, in which additional processes are progressively incorporated as successive retrievals occur. To our knowledge, this study presents the first predictions of EAM based on conditions during encoding while differentiating between reactivation and no reactivation conditions.

However, some limits were identified and should be addressed in future studies. There are presumably other variables or processes that sustain memory, at least in the case of the absence of reactivation, that were not probed in the present study. In a previous study, Tang, et al.70 based their prediction on the visual properties of the events, while Erez, et al.69 based theirs on activations in different brain areas. Of note, different aspects of the self could be explored in a similar setting. For instance, the minimal self (i.e., bodily self) might play a role in predicting the free recall in the No-Reactivation Group (see Penaud, et al.123). Likewise, though we did not find a conclusive role for objective physiological measures, other indices, such as heart-rate variability124, could be considered. Due to the minimum time duration needed for each epoch, our experimental paradigm was not suited for an implementation of heart-rate variability measures. Future studies might consider this measure when investigating long-lasting EAM. Other measures linked to eye-tracking could also be considered during encoding, such as pupillary dilatation, or head position and rotation, though there is no consensus on their role in predicting memory performance (see for instance Bergt, et al.125 or Cadet, et al.126). As a result, they were not included in the present study, but these measures should be considered as future research advances. Besides, eye-tracking during retrieval may also provide helpful insight to distinguish familiarity and recollection127. Furthermore, future studies would benefit from a larger sample size and from the collection of more precise recognition scores to get more information on the predictors of this memory performance. Finally, they should consider a way to circumvent the limit posed by the maximum time duration possible in VR128. Naturalistic events tend to be longer than the simple viewing of a picture; moreover, participants ‘walked’ from on a virtual path from one event to the next, increasing ecological validity but reducing the number of events. As a result, the number of events in each of the categories for the interaction * valence encoding factors was limited, and any results pertaining to this interaction should be considered with caution.

In the context of VR and clinical applications, the role of presence and embodiment should be further investigated by varying the modalities of these two factors85,129,130. Moreover, considering the loss of information and accuracy in the free recall for the No-Reactivation Group, but not in performance for the recognition test, as of note, this result should be interpreted with caution due to the relatively small number of lures (N = 10). It would also be valuable to test older adults using this paradigm. As memory performance declines with age, this experiment would allow us to determine whether such a decline holds for both recognition and free recall, and whether the same underlying mechanisms persist across aging78,131. Notably, a positivity, rather than a negativity bias, would be expected111. Interestingly, future research could also investigate emotional and self-biased at encoding examining the production of false memories or erroneous information during long-term free recall following reactivation. Our recognition data suggest that false information, accompanied by a strong sense of recollection, could be higher in this case21.

From a therapeutical perspective, VR-based episodic memory training, derived from the present study, could strengthen encoding and consolidation and enhance recall by periodically reactivating key emotional and self-related events within immersive environments, particularly for aging individuals, older adults with depression, mild cognitive impairment and early-stage neurodegenerative diseases132,133. More generally, given the importance of encoding factors and reactivation in long-term memory retrieval, our paradigm could be adapted for cognitive rehabilitation to restore EAM function. This could be achieved through a VR environment that systematically reactivates personal and meaningful events via repeated and/or interactive exposures, benefiting individuals with various disorders affecting EM and EAM134. Finally, our VE paradigm could be adapted to assist participants in processing and integrating traumatic experiences through immersion and interaction and subsequent reactivation, which aligns with the principles of Virtual Reality Exposure Therapy135.

In summary, this study provides a first comprehensive account of the effects of reactivations on long-lasting EAM encoded in a naturalistic context. This novel experimental paradigm paves the way for real-life EAM prediction by leveraging encoding and consolidation processes, using an ecological VR setting featuring naturalistic events. Moreover, it holds promising clinical applications to enhance EAM in real-life settings.
