Paradoxical psoriasis after dupilumab treatment of prurigo nodularis treated with adalimumab
Authors: Jennifer Astrup Sørensen, Cæcilie Bachdal Johansen, Alexander Egeberg, Jacob P. Thyssen, Simon Francis Thomsen
Published: 2024

Introduction

Prurigo nodularis (PN) is a chronic, inflammatory skin condition characterized by intensely pruritic, hyperkeratotic nodules most commonly showing a symmetric distribution on arms, legs, and torso. PN is divided into an early-onset atopic and a late-onset nonatopic form.1 The pathogenic mechanisms behind PN remain unclear, but patients’ skin exhibits alterations in nerve density and inflammation including activation of various proinflammatory pathways such as interleukin (IL) 4, IL-13, IL-31, and neuropeptides.2

Dupilumab is a fully human monoclonal antibody blocking the IL-4 receptor α thereby inhibiting IL-4/IL-13 signaling and is used in the treatment of moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis (AD), moderate-to-severe asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, eosinophilic esophagitis, and PN. IL-4, IL-13, and IL-31 are all part of type 2 inflammation driven by the T helper (Th)2 cells. It has been suggested that inhibition of IL-4 and IL-13 can lead to increased IL-17 activity, which has been proposed as the mechanism behind paradoxical development of both psoriasis3 and arthritis4 after dupilumab treatment.

Paradoxical development of psoriasis after dupilumab treatment has been described previously,5,6 particularly in patients with AD, but to our knowledge only one case of scalp psoriasis has been reported after dupilumab treatment of PN.7 Here, we describe a patient with PN, who experienced widespread psoriasis after dupilumab treatment, but obtained complete resolution of all symptoms after subsequent treatment with adalimumab.

Case report

A 53-year-old man who had been treated for several years with thalidomide for PN initiated dupilumab 300 mg every second week after a loading dose of 600 mg while discontinuing thalidomide in June 2020 because of increasing disease activity (Fig 1). He was then treated successfully and without any side effects for 13 months until July 2021 (Fig 2). The treatment was then discontinued because of sustained remission of PN. The patient experienced a flare of PN after about 3 months and dupilumab 300 mg every second week was reinitiated in December 2021. In the beginning of February 2022, the patient was seen in the outpatient clinic because of persistent symptoms and a change in the skin lesions clinically consistent with psoriasis. The patient scored his current itch as less intense (maximum visual analog scale, 4.5) as compared with his itch during PN flares (maximum visual analog scale, 9.5). Dupilumab was discontinued and the patient began daily treatment with potent to very potent topical corticosteroids. After 1.5 months with insufficient effect of treatment (Psoriasis Area and Severity Index, 12.8), adalimumab was initiated in late March 2022. Three weeks later the patient had a telephone consultation and reported almost complete clearance of psoriasis symptoms including the chronic PN lesions. The patient was seen in June 2022 with complete clearance of all skin symptoms (Psoriasis Area and Severity Index, 0; visual analog scale itch, 0; and Dermatology life Quality Index, 0) as was the case in June 2023 only reporting a small flare of itch during a COVID-19 in January 2023. The patient reported no side effects of the treatment with adalimumab. The patient had no personal or family history of psoriasis, he did, however, have a history of asthma, rhinitis, nasal polyps, juvenile plantar dermatosis as a child, and acetylsalicylic acid induced anaphylaxis. Two previous skin biopsies were performed in 2015. The first showed spongiotic reaction with superficial perivascular lymphocyte inflammation with some histiocytes and scattered eosinophils, ie, dermatitis. The second showed superficial inflammation, no granulocytes in the keratin layer, subtle spongiosis, superficial lymphocyte inflammation among some eosinophils, characteristic of a chronic eczematous reaction. At time of first psoriasis presentation, blood samples for chronic infections and a strep A throat swab were normal. A new biopsy from lesional skin showed psoriasiform hyperplasia with lamellar parakeratosis with neutrophil granulocytes, minimal spongiosis, no eosinophils, and slightly dilated vessels in the papillae, morphologically consistent with psoriasis.

Discussion

A recent review of 26 studies including 47 adult patients on dupilumab, mainly for AD, found a mean duration of treatment before onset of psoriasis of 3.7 months.5 Whether dupilumab was continued or discontinued was reported in 70% of patients. Among these about half discontinued dupilumab. Of the patients who discontinued dupilumab, 12 of 16 had outcome data reported; 2 achieved complete resolution of psoriasis symptoms, 8 had some sort of improvement, and 2 had no improvement. Among all 47 patients, 25 had outcome data, and of these, 10 patients achieved complete resolution after discontinuation, 12 had varying degrees of improvement, and 3 had recurrent or stable disease.

An immunologic explanation for the development of psoriasis after treatment with dupilumab possibly alludes to inhibition of IL-4. Th1 and Th17 cells, known to be increased in patients with psoriasis are negatively regulated by IL-4. It is also known that IL-4 is overexpressed in AD.8 Therefore, it has been hypothesized that blocking of the IL-4/IL-13 pathway could lead to a shift toward the Th1/Th17 phenotype, which in turn could result in psoriatic skin plaques due to the inflammatory cytokine cascade including IL-17.3 A recent case report found that a patient receiving tralokinumab, a selective IL-13 inhibitor, for AD, also developed a paradoxical psoriasis reaction, suggesting that not only dual blockage of IL-4 and IL-13 signaling can lead to the push toward the IL-17 pathway, and subsequently a psoriasis reaction.9

Paradoxical immune reactions to biologic treatments are well known and on the rise with the rapid increase in both usage and number of different treatments available.10 Treatment with dupilumab has also been linked to the development of spondylarthritis, which is another IL-17 driven disease.11

This is, to our knowledge, the first reported case of paradoxical psoriasis after treatment with dupilumab for PN, which has been treated successfully with adalimumab. This showcases important knowledge on a safe and well tolerated treatment for dupilumab-induced psoriasis when discontinuation of dupilumab and topical corticosteroids are not sufficient.
