Obesity and shorter QT intervals: sex- and adiposity distribution-specific patterns of ventricular repolarization in healthy Asian youth
Authors: Jun Wang, Dongdong Zhu, Xuechun Ding, Juxia Chen, Juan Wu, Juan Wang, Zhixiang Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveObesity is linked to prolonged ventricular repolarization (QT interval) in older populations, but its electrophysiological impact in healthy Asian youth remains unclear. This study aimed to assess the association between adiposity and ventricular repolarization in this population.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 3,156 healthy Asian youth (mean age 19.6 years). Adiposity was quantified using body mass index (BMI), body fat percentage (BFP), waist circumference (WC), and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR). The primary outcome was the Fridericia-corrected QT interval (QTcF). Sex-stratified robust regression was employed, and mediation by heart rate variability (HRV) was explored.ResultsGreater adiposity was associated with shorter QTcF, contrary to the conventional paradigm. This association exhibited marked sexual dimorphism. In males, all four adiposity indices were significantly linked to QTcF shortening (e.g., β = -2.97 ms per SD increase in BMI). In females, significant associations were confined to BMI and BFP. Furthermore, in females, vagal tone (rMSSD) and sympathovagal balance (LF/HF) statistically mediated part of the BMI/BFP-QTcF relationship, accounting for 6.3% to 9.8% of the effect. Sensitivity analyses confirmed robustness.ConclusionIn healthy Asian youth, obesity is associated with shorter ventricular repolarization, modulated by sex and adiposity type. These findings suggest unique early-life electrophysiological adaptations and underscore the importance of considering sex and body composition in cardiovascular risk assessment for young populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06285-8.Keywords: Obesity, QTc, Sex differences, Autonomic nervous system, Asian youth

Background

The rising global incidence of cardiovascular diseases presents a particularly concerning situation in younger populations, with obesity serving as a major driver [1]. Obesity is significantly associated with various adverse cardiovascular outcomes, notably arrhythmias [2, 3]. The QT interval, a key index of ventricular repolarization, can trigger fatal arrhythmias when abnormally altered, whether prolonged or shortened [4, 5]. Systematic reviews and large-scale cohort studies indicate that QT prolongation is common in individuals with obesity, metabolic syndrome, and type 2 diabetes, correlating with delayed myocardial repolarization, increased sympathetic tone, and ion channel remodeling [6]. For instance, compared to normal-weight individuals, overweight/obesity populations show significantly longer corrected QT intervals (mean difference 21.74 ms) and greater QT/QTc dispersion (mean difference 15.17 ms) [7]. Although less common, QT shortening, as seen in short QT syndrome (SQTS), is closely linked to calcium/potassium channel gene mutations and also significantly increases ventricular fibrillation risk [8, 9]. However, existing research has notable limitations:

Population bias

Most conclusions are based on Western middle-aged/older adults or patients with comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, hypertension). The QT-obesity association in healthy youth (18–25 years) lacks systematic evaluation.

Ethnic gap

The body fat distribution in Asian populations, characterized by higher visceral adiposity [10], may influence repolarization patterns [11], yet related studies are scarce.

Sex specificity

Women typically have longer QT intervals than men [12, 13], but the sex-specific impact of obesity on QT remains unclear.

Furthermore, QT correction formulas (e.g., Bazett’s) may lead to over-correction in different populations due to variations in cardiac electrophysiology and body size [14–16]. Consequently, the core scientific questions in current QT research are: In healthy Asian youth, does obesity affect the QT interval? Is the direction of association (prolongation or shortening) consistent with conventional understanding? Are there specific patterns related to sex and obesity type (e.g., central vs. general)? Additionally, besides obesity, could other electrophysiological factors, such as autonomic function, be involved in regulation? Addressing these questions is crucial for identifying early warning indicators of obesity-related cardiovascular risk in young populations.

Therefore, this study aims to fill this critical knowledge gap. Without making prior assumptions about the direction of association, we conducted a large-scale cross-sectional study to assess the association characteristics between obesity and ventricular repolarization in healthy Asian youth. We focused on: (1) the direction and strength of the association between obesity and QTc in this population; (2) the presence of significant sexual dimorphism; (3) whether obesity types reflecting different physiological/pathological implications (e.g., general vs. central) exhibit distinct association patterns. By addressing these questions, this study provides key evidence from the youth window for understanding the life-course evolution and population heterogeneity of obesity-related cardiovascular risk.

Methods

Study population and sampling

This was a cross-sectional observational study conducted on the campus of Anqing Medical College, China, from February 20 to October 20, 2025. A hybrid sampling strategy was used: cluster sampling enrolled students from mandatory health science course classes (n = 2,298), combined with campus voluntary recruitment (n = 1,157), resulting in the preliminary recruitment of 3,455 students. Exclusion criteria included: (1) self-reported physician-diagnosed cardiovascular disease, metabolic disease (e.g., diabetes, thyroid dysfunction), severe respiratory disease, neurological or psychiatric disorders; (2) current pregnancy; (3) acute infection within the past 4 weeks; (4) long-term use of medications known to affect heart rate (HR), weight, or autonomic function (e.g., beta-blockers, psychotropic drugs); (5) inability to complete all measurement procedures. After exclusion, 3,180 participants (784 males, 2,396 females, mean age 19.56 ± 1.02 years) formed the initial analysis cohort. Exclusions included: 124 individuals who did not meet inclusion criteria (11 with self-reported diseases, 35 taking medications affecting cardiovascular or autonomic function, and 78 with missing adiposity data), 36 with missing heart rate variability (HRV) data, and 115 with missing exercise data. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anqing Medical College (2025-02-004). All participants provided written informed consent. Data were de-identified to protect privacy.

Variable definitions, data collection, and quality control

Weight (Wt), height (Ht), and body fat percentage (BFP, via bioelectrical impedance analysis) were measured using a smart ultrasonic height-weight scale (Yun Kang Bao CP30B, Shenzhen, China). Waist and hip circumferences (WC, HC) were measured using a standard tape measure. Body mass index (BMI) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) were calculated from these measurements. Obesity and its types were defined according to the 2024 Chinese Guidelines for the Clinical Management of Obesity [17]: WC (male ≥ 90 cm, female ≥ 85 cm); BMI ≥ 28; BFP (male ≥ 25%, female ≥ 30%); WHR (male ≥ 0.9, female ≥ 0.85). Meeting any one criterion defined obesity of the corresponding type.

Electrocardiographic indices were obtained using a conventional 12-lead ECG device (CONTE 8000G, sampling rate 1000 Hz, Qinhuangdao, China). Five minutes of resting supine ECG data were collected (25 mm/s, 10 mm/mV) between 08:00 and 22:00. Data were extracted using the device's software (version V3.4.7, Wuhan, China): R-R interval (RR), all normal-to-normal intervals (SDNN), low-frequency power/high-frequency power ratio（LF/HF）, median root mean square of successive differences (rMSSD), and average QT interval (AVQT) [18, 19]. The following parameters were uniformly calculated using AVQT and RR intervals: HR = 60 / (RR / 1000), Bazett-corrected QTc (QTcB) = QT / √(RR / 1000), Fridericia-corrected QTc (QTcF) = QT / ∛(RR / 1000), Framingham-corrected QTc （QTcL）= QT + 0.154 × (1000 − RR), and Hodges-corrected QTc （QTcH） = QT + 1.75 × (HR − 60) [20].

Before measurement, the room temperature was maintained at 22–24 °C. Participants rested seated for at least 10 min (no vigorous exercise, food, or stimulant consumption within 4 h). The device performed self-initialization. Electrodes were placed according to standard 12-lead ECG positions, and skin was cleaned with alcohol swabs to ensure contact impedance below 5 kΩ. A baseline calibration procedure was run to ensure a stable ECG signal with minimal noise (amplitude > 0.5 mV, noise level < -60 dB, ≥ 95% analyzable normal-to-normal intervals, and ΔHR < 10 bpm). The device was recalibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions every 50 measurements. To account for potential diurnal variation, ECG measurement times were recorded and categorized as morning (8:00–11:59), afternoon (12:00–17:59), or evening (18:00–22:00); this time variable was included as a covariate in regression analyses. To ensure the reliability of QT interval measurement, a random 10% sample of ECGs (n = 320) underwent independent manual review. For each ECG, five consecutive sinus rhythm beats were selected (preferably lead II, alternatively V5, V6, or I, based on waveform clarity). Two cardiologists independently measured the QT interval on separate days. Inter-rater reliability was quantified using ICC [21].

Exercise data (total data accumulated over 16 weeks of the semester) were collected via the smartphone application “Budaolepao” (version 4.1.3, Lepao Sports, Wuhan, China). The app uses the phone’s built-in Global Positioning System (GPS) and accelerometer to record users’ walking/running activities; data were automatically uploaded to the cloud and exported. Total valid exercise distance (TVED) was defined by the app’s default algorithm: a single session must cover at least 1.50 km, with a pace between 5’0” and 12’0” per km, and pass through two checkpoints. Sessions ≤ 3 km must be completed within 1 h, and sessions > 3 km but < 10 km within 2 h (including pause time); sessions exceeding these time limits were not counted as valid achievements but recorded as ordinary exercise data. Based on sex-specific 25th (P25) and 75th (P75) percentiles of TVED, participants were divided into three groups [22]: low (TVED < P25), medium (P25 ≤ TVED ≤ P75), and high (TVED > P75) activity levels. Individuals with zero values were included in the low activity group. This activity grouping was used as a covariate in regression analyses.

Statistical methods

A cross-sectional design was employed. All analyses were performed using Python 3.12.8 and core libraries (statsmodels 0.14.0, scipy 1.13.0, matplotlib 3.8.0) [23], with a significance level of α = 0.05. For data quality control, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, using a two-way mixed-effects model ICC(3,1) for absolute agreement) quantified inter-rater and human-automated measurement agreement [21], supplemented by Pearson correlation and Bland-Altman analysis (95% limits of agreement). Clinical acceptability was defined as a difference ≤ 20 ms [24]. The primary analysis comprised four stages: Stage 1 assessed normality of continuous variables using sex-stratified Shapiro-Wilk tests. As all variables deviated from normality (p < 0.05), data are presented as median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. Sex differences were tested using the Mann-Whitney U test, and Cliff’s delta effect sizes (δ) [25] with their bootstrap 95% CI (1000 repetitions) [26] were calculated. The QTc correction formula least dependent on HR was selected by evaluating the absolute value of Spearman’s correlation (r) between each formula and HR, combined with the mean absolute deviation from the raw QT interval. Prespecified exclusion criteria (QTc > 480 ms or < 320 ms) [16] were applied to form the final analysis cohort. Stage 2: In the final cohort, the primary regression model used sex-stratified Huber robust regression (ε = 1.35) as the base method to handle potential outliers [27, 28]. Heteroscedasticity was diagnosed using the Breusch-Pagan test (α = 0.05); if significant, HC3 robust standard errors (Heteroskedasticity-Consistent covariance matrix estimator, type 3) were used for inference. Models tested the association between four standardized continuous obesity indices (WC, WHR, BFP, BMI) and the selected QTc dependent variable, adjusting for HR, age, physical activity level, and measurement time segment. Model diagnostics included residual normality (Shapiro-Wilk test), heteroscedasticity (Breusch-Pagan test), and multicollinearity (variance inflation factor, VIF). All regression coefficients were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated via bootstrap (1000 repetitions). For significant associations, restricted cubic splines (RCS) explored nonlinear trends [29], with knots set at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the obesity index distribution. Stage 3: First, obesity prevalence and sex differences were reported based on Chinese guideline standards using chi-square tests. Three levels of categorical comparisons followed: Level 1 compared physiological indicators between groups with obesity and without obesity within each sex; Level 2 compared the distribution of these indicators between males with obesity and females with obesity under the same obesity definition; Level 3 transformed continuous obesity indices into binary variables. Unadjusted mean differences in the selected QTc variable were calculated, and standardized regression coefficients (β) were estimated using Huber robust regression models adjusted for HR, age, physical activity level, and measurement time. Stage 4: Bootstrap method (1000 repetitions) tested the mediating role of HRV indicators in the obesity-QTc association path [30]. All analysis variables were Z-score standardized. Path coefficients, indirect effects, direct effects, total effects, and mediation proportions were calculated. Finally, comprehensive sensitivity analyses were performed, including exclusion of individuals with extreme HR, use of quantile regression [31] to test association heterogeneity at different percentiles of the QTc distribution, substitution of the dependent variable, and repetition of core analyses using the initial cohort and a sex-balanced subsample analysis. All effect sizes and regression coefficients are reported with bootstrap 95% CI. All p-values from hypothesis tests involving multiple comparisons underwent sex-stratified false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure [32].

Results

Data quality, baseline characteristics, and QTc consistency assessment

Manual review of a randomly selected 10% sample (n = 320) demonstrated excellent inter-rater agreement, with an ICC(3,1) of 0.943 (95% CI: 0.930–0.954). Even better agreement was observed between manual and automated measurements, as indicated by an ICC (3,1) of 0.987 (95% CI: 0.984–0.990). Comparing the correlation of different QTc correction formulas with HR, QTcB showed the strongest positive correlation (ρ = 0.460, p < 0.001, Additional file 1), while QTcF had no significant correlation with HR (ρ = 0.013, p = 0.449). QTcF was therefore selected as the primary outcome for all subsequent analyses. After excluding 6 individuals with QTcF > 480 ms (3 males, 3 females) and 18 with QTcF < 320 ms (4 males, 14 females), the primary analysis cohort comprised 3,156 individuals (Additional file 2).

Table 1 shows that, except for the RR interval and calculated HR, all other indices showed statistically significant sex differences (FDR p < 0.05). Males had higher median values than females for Ht, Wt, BMI, WC, HC, WHR, and TVED. The Cliff’s delta effect sizes (|δ|) for Ht, WHR, and BFP were 0.87, 0.64, and − 0.48, respectively. Females had higher median values for AVQT and all QTc corrections (QTcB, QTcF, QTcL, QTcH), with effect sizes ranging from − 0.14 to -0.29. For HRV, males had higher median standard deviation of SDNN and LF/HF ratio, while females had higher rMSSD. The distribution of measurement times also differed by sex (p < 0.001).

Continuous association between obesity indices and QTcF

Sex-stratified robust regression analysis (Table 2) revealed significant sexual dimorphism in the obesity-QTcF association after adjusting for HR, age, physical activity level, and measurement time. In males, increases in all four continuous obesity indices were independently associated with significant QTcF shortening (all FDR p ≤ 0.028). For each standard deviation (SD) increase in BMI, QTcF shortened by 2.97 ms (95% CI: -4.47 to -1.41; FDR p < 0.001). In females, although the associations were overall weaker, both BMI and BFP remained significantly associated with QTcF shortening (β = -1.09 ms/SD, FDR p = 0.008 and β = -1.36 ms/SD, FDR p = 0.002). The association with WC did not reach statistical significance (β = -0.29 ms/SD, FDR p = 0.438). WHR showed borderline significance in females (β = -0.78 ms/SD, FDR p = 0.051). Model diagnostics indicated very low variance inflation factors for all models, suggesting negligible multicollinearity. Most models exhibited heteroscedasticity, and all model residuals deviated from normality. However, the Huber robust regression method employed is robust to such violations. Restricted cubic spline analysis confirmed that the associations did not deviate from linear trends across all sex and obesity index combinations (all nonlinear p > 0.05), supporting a pattern of progressive linear change in obesity’s effect on QTcF in the youth population.

Validation of physiological phenotypes and sex differences based on clinical obesity classification

Based on the Chinese guidelines for obesity diagnosis, this study first revealed significant sex differences in obesity prevalence and the distribution of BMI categories (Additional file 4). Males had significantly higher obesity prevalence than females based on WC (25.6% vs. 8.3%), BMI (18.0% vs. 9.7%), and WHR (19.3% vs. 7.4%) (all p < 0.001), while BFP-defined obesity showed no significant sex difference (29.7% vs. 27.9%, p = 0.340). Building on this, we further validated the sexual dimorphism in the obesity-QTcF association. Figure 2 compares the median distributions of HRV indices and QTcF between obesity and non-obesity individuals within each sex. In males, the median QTcF was significantly lower in groups with obesity (defined by WC, BMI, BFP, and WHR) compared to those without obesity (WC: -4.28 ms, FDR p = 0.001; BMI: -5.21 ms, FDR p < 0.001; BFP: -4.10 ms, FDR p = 0.005; WHR: -4.33 ms, FDR p = 0.029). Consistently, these males with obesity exhibited significant autonomic dysfunction, including significantly lower median SDNN and rMSSD and higher median LF/HF ratio (all relevant comparisons FDR p < 0.001). In females, a significant reduction in QTcF median was only observed in the BFP-defined group with obesity (-2.88 ms, FDR p = 0.003), which also showed decreased rMSSD and increased LF/HF. However, under other obesity definitions, females showed only autonomic function changes without accompanying significant differences in QTcF.

Mediation analysis based on standardized variables for autonomic function

To further explore potential mechanisms, we conducted mediation analyses based on the obesity-HRV-QTcF pathway. The results revealed a sex-specific role of autonomic function in the obesity-QTcF association (Fig. 4, Additional file 5). In females, HRV showed significant mediating effects: for BMI, both rMSSD (β = -0.005, 95%CI: -0.008 to -0.002, FDR p < 0.001) and LF/HF (β = -0.006, 95%CI: -0.009 to -0.002, FDR p < 0.001) exhibited significant indirect effects, with mediation proportions of 7.91% and 9.79%, respectively. For BFP, rMSSD (β = -0.004, 95%CI: -0.008 to -0.002, FDR p < 0.001) and LF/HF (β = -0.005, 95%CI: -0.008 to -0.002, FDR p = 0.002) were also significant mediating paths, with mediation proportions of 6.32% and 6.81%, respectively. SDNN showed no significant mediating effect in any female model. In males, although total effects of all obesity indices on QTcF were significant, no HRV indicator showed a significant indirect effect after FDR correction.

Model robustness validation

Sensitivity analysis results were as follows (Additional file 6): After excluding individuals with extreme HRs (< 50 or > 100 bpm), the sex-stratified association patterns were consistent with the main analysis (male BMI: β = -3.34 ms/SD; female BFP: β = -1.37 ms/SD). Repeating the analysis using the Bazett correction formula (QTcB) as the dependent variable, the negative associations between all obesity indices and QTcB remained significant (male BMI: β = -3.09 ms/SD; female BFP: β = -1.42 ms/SD). Quantile regression analysis indicated that the association patterns persisted at the median (50th) and upper quartile (75th) of the QTcF distribution (male BMI at the 75th percentile: β = -4.51 ms/SD). Repeating the analysis on the initial cohort without excluding QTc extreme values (n = 3,180) yielded results consistent with the primary analysis cohort (male BMI: β = -2.88 ms/SD). A sex-balanced subsample analysis (777 females randomly selected to match the male sample size) confirmed the robustness of the main findings: the effect sizes for female BMI and BFP remained directionally consistent, and detailed results are provided in Additional file 7.

Discussion

In this large sample of healthy Asian youth, we observed a phenomenon that contrasts with the conventional understanding: obesity was associated with relatively shorter ventricular repolarization (QTcF). This finding emerged from a systematic, unbiased assessment of this specific population. While its direction differs from mainstream evidence derived from older or metabolically impaired groups, our methodological analyses support its robustness. This indicates that the cardiac electrophysiological impact of obesity may not be uniform and could be modulated by life stage (possibly reflecting metabolic status) and population characteristics. The observed “QT shortening” in these presumably healthy youth might thus represent a distinct early electrophysiological adaptation, adding to our understanding of the varied cardiac effects of obesity.

Our study provides evidence for considering how obesity-related cardiac phenotypes may evolve across the life course. The association displayed clear sexual dimorphism. In males, all four continuous adiposity indices (BMI, BFP, WC, WHR) were independently associated with QTcF shortening. In females, significant associations were largely confined to indices of general adiposity (BMI, BFP). Furthermore, in females, part of the association between BMI/BFP and QTcF was statistically mediated by measures of autonomic function (vagal tone [rMSSD] and sympathovagal balance [LF/HF]). In contrast, associations involving central adiposity indices (WC, WHR) were weaker in females, and no significant autonomic mediation was detected for these paths. These observed differences may be underpinned by known sex disparities in sex hormone milieu, adiposity biology, and autonomic regulation.

Collectively, our findings suggest that for young populations, particularly Asian youth, the assessment of obesity-related cardiac risk should consider factors beyond a singular paradigm. A more nuanced perspective that integrates developmental stage, sex, and patterns of body fat distribution appears warranted.

The obesity-associated QTcF shortening observed here provides key evidence for understanding the life-course spectrum and population heterogeneity of obesity’s cardiac electrophysiological effects. The vast majority of studies based on middle-aged and older populations consistently indicate that obesity is associated with QT interval prolongation [7, 33]. However, evidence in youth populations presents a more complex picture. For instance, Gondoni et al. [34] found that lifestyle intervention did not significantly change QT intervals in patients with coronary heart disease and obesity, suggesting more complex drivers of QT changes in disease states. Dykiert et al. [35] further showed that obesity primarily increases QT dispersion rather than mean QT interval, suggesting obesity might affect arrhythmia risk by increasing myocardial electrophysiological heterogeneity. Guo et al. [36] also emphasized that metabolic syndrome (rather than obesity alone) is associated with QTc prolongation. Given that all participants were screened as healthy individuals and had no diagnosed metabolic diseases, our cohort likely represents a state of preserved metabolic health. This may explain the manifestation of QT shortening rather than prolongation, as opposed to older or metabolically impaired populations where QT prolongation is typically observed, echoing the compensatory QT shortening phenomenon observed in animal models with obesity by Durak et al. [37]. Moreover, this QT shortening phenotype is not unprecedented in youth populations. Dhutia et al. [38] and Kobza et al. [39] both reported the existence of short QT intervals in large youth screening cohorts, and it was associated with male sex. Our study further systematically linked this phenotype with both continuous and categorical obesity indices and revealed its particular sex-dependence in Asian youth, thereby enriching the epidemiological understanding of this phenomenon. However, the pro-arrhythmic mechanisms of QT shortening remain unclear [19], warranting cautious interpretation of its clinical significance.Clinically, the observed QTc shortening (2–3 ms per SD increase in adiposity) is far smaller than the diagnostic threshold for short QT syndrome (< 320 ms). Thus, it should not be equated with pathological QT shortening. Instead, this finding may represent an early physiological correlate of increased adiposity in healthy young individuals. Whether this phenotype carries any long-term prognostic significance—whether protective or adverse—remains unknown and requires prospective follow-up.

From a mechanistic perspective, obesity may influence the QT interval through multiple pathways. (1) Direct Electrophysiological Effects: Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), as an active endocrine organ, secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines (interleukin-1 β [IL-1 β], tumor necrosis factor-α [TNF-α]) and lipid mediators that can directly regulate myocardial ion channel function [40, 41]. Patel et al. [41] demonstrated that the EAT secretome inhibits the inward rectifying potassium current (IK1) and enhances the L-type calcium current (ICaL), while Jhuo et al. [42] found in a metabolic syndrome model that adipokines simultaneously reduce delayed IK1 and increase ICaL. Although IK1 reduction should theoretically prolong repolarization, the predominant enhancement of ICaL may drive the net effect, leading to action potential duration shortening (manifesting as QT shortening). This hypothesis aligns with Ernault et al.‘s [43] finding that atrial EAT reduces IK1 by 35% (with 26% decreased Kcnj2 expression), causing resting membrane potential depolarization, potentially further exacerbating the pro-repolarizing effect of calcium currents. Recent animal experimental evidence [44] indicates that obesity-induced oxidative stress (e.g., Nox2 activation) can specifically inhibit functions like the transient outward potassium current (Ito), and this effect is more pronounced in male mice, providing a novel molecular-level hypothesis for understanding the stronger QTc shortening association in males. (2) Upstream Regulatory Factors: Baseline regulation of cardiac ion channels by sex hormones is central to electrophysiological sex differences [13, 45–47]. Testosterone is known to upregulate the rapid delayed rectifier potassium current (IKr) and inhibit ICaL, thereby shortening action potential duration [48, 49], whereas estrogen has opposite effects [13, 46, 49]. This sex difference may further amplify differences in baseline electrophysiological properties: e.g., female cardiomyocytes have higher ICaL density and lower IKr [50], while male Ito current dominance might make them more sensitive to obesity-related repolarization changes. Panicker et al. [51] found that in healthy young males, early repolarization patterns are often accompanied by slower HRs and slightly shorter QTc intervals, similar to the phenotype of males with obesity in our study, suggesting that the male heart may possess specific repolarization reserve characteristics. Furthermore, sex hormones rapidly modulate channel activity via non-genomic pathways (e.g., NO signaling) [52], potentially explaining greater male sensitivity to obesity-related QT shortening. (3) Involvement of Autonomic Nervous System: Our exploratory mediation analysis found that vagal tone (rMSSD) and sympathovagal balance (LF/HF) were significant mediating paths linking obesity and QTcF in females, but no significant autonomic mediation effect was found in males. This aligns with Vasheghani et al.‘s [53] observation in diabetic neuropathy—autonomic function primarily increases repolarization heterogeneity (QTd) rather than directly affecting mean QT interval. Smetana and Malik [54] further noted that sympathovagal imbalance (not merely a single neural component) plays an important role in repolarization regulation, with sympathetic activation tending to prolong QT and parasympathetic dominance potentially shortening QT, though compensatory capacity in healthy youth may mask such effects. (4) Ethnic and Metabolic Specificity: The Unique Phenotype of Asian Youth. The visceral fat distribution in Asian populations [10] might exacerbate electrical remodeling through lipotoxicity or inflammatory pathways, but the exact dominant mechanism requires further validation. Our findings suggest that physiological pathways through which obesity affects ventricular repolarization may differ between sexes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations to consider when interpreting the results. (1) The cross-sectional observational design cannot establish causality between obesity and QTcF changes, nor can it fully exclude the influence of unmeasured confounding factors (e.g., genetic background, dietary habits, serum electrolyte levels, menstrual cycle in females). (2) The study population comprised healthy Asian youth students from a single center, with an imbalanced sex ratio (approximately 75% female), which may affect statistical power and representativeness in sex comparisons. While homogeneity is an advantage, it may limit the generalizability of findings to other ethnicities, age groups, or populations with metabolic diseases. (3) Although known diseases were strictly excluded via self-report, the study could not rule out all metabolic subclinical states via blood biochemical indicators; these unmeasured factors may constitute residual confounding. (4) Although multimodal analyses revealed significant sex heterogeneity and explored the potential role of the autonomic nervous system, the study lacks direct mechanistic evidence, such as precise measurements of myocardial ion channel function, epicardial adipose tissue activity, or sex hormone levels. Thus, the interpretation of the intrinsic physiological pathways underlying the observed associations remains preliminary. (5) Despite employing a rigorous manual review process, residual error in QT interval measurement may persist. (6) While smartphone app-recorded exercise data are superior to self-report, they may not fully capture all physical activities beyond regular running. (7) The mediation analyses in this study are exploratory and correlative in nature; they cannot establish a causal mediating role of autonomic function in the process of obesity affecting QTc. The observed associations may reflect shared upstream regulatory mechanisms. (8) Although the observed QTc shortening effect sizes are on the order of milliseconds, their consistency across sexes and obesity indices in the youth population, along with their correlation with the established arrhythmia risk indicator QTc, suggest this is likely a biologically consistent early signal rather than measurement noise. However, its translation into absolute risk for future clinical events requires confirmation through long-term follow-up.

The findings of this study open several valuable avenues for future research. (1) Establish large prospective cohorts comprising multi-ethnic youth populations to verify the trajectory of QTc shortening through repeated measurements and clarify its association with long-term arrhythmic events. (2) Integrate multi-omics data (e.g., genomic, metabolomic) and advanced imaging techniques (e.g., cardiac magnetic resonance for assessing myocardial characteristics and epicardial fat) to deeply dissect the molecular and structural basis of obesity’s effect on cardiac electrophysiology. (3) Particularly, there is a need to focus on exploring the fundamental mechanisms underlying the differential responses of males and females to obesity. (4) Develop and apply QTc correction formulas better suited for Asian youth populations. (5) Obesity-related intervention strategies may need to target specific mechanisms, such as modulating the adipose secretome rather than merely improving autonomic function, and should consider metabolic profiles in clinical decision-making. Ultimately, these basic research efforts hold promise for translation into clinical practice, providing a scientific basis for developing targeted, sex-specific early identification and intervention strategies for cardiovascular risk in youth populations.

Conclusions

This study indicates that in healthy Asian youth, obesity is associated with QTc shortening, and this association exhibits sex- and adiposity distribution-specificity. This finding suggests the existence of unique early electrophysiological adaptation mechanisms. The precise clinical and biological significance awaits final validation through prospective studies incorporating multimodal data.
