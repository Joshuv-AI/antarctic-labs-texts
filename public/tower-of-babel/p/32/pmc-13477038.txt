Oncologic outcomes of segmentectomy in c-stage I non–small cell lung cancer with visceral pleural invasion: A multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Norifumi Tsubokawa, Takahiro Mimae, Yoshihiro Miyata, Yujin Kudo, Takuya Nagashima, Hiroyuki Ito, Norihiko Ikeda, Morihito Okada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesGiven the increased recurrence risk associated with visceral pleural invasion (VPI) and its unique subpleural lymphatic pathways, this study aimed to compare survival and recurrence patterns between segmentectomy and lobectomy in patients with small-sized (≤3 cm), radiologically solid-dominant clinical stage I non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with pathologic VPI.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the data of 2247 patients who underwent segmentectomy or lobectomy for small-sized (≤3 cm), radiologically solid-dominant clinical stage I NSCLC across multiple institutions between 2010 and 2022. Baseline characteristics were balanced using propensity score matching.ResultsOverall, 418 patients had pathologic VPI. Among them, 81 (19%) patients underwent segmentectomy, and 337 (81%) underwent lobectomy. Pathologic lymph node metastasis occurred in 99 (23%) patients, with no significant difference between the 2 groups (P = .135). After propensity score matching (n = 79 each), overall survival and recurrence-free survival did not significantly differ between the 2 groups (P = .645 and P = .629, respectively). In the propensity score–matched cohort, multivariable Cox analysis showed that segmentectomy was not an independent prognostic factor for overall survival (hazard ratio, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.26-1.48; P = .287) and recurrence-free survival (hazard ratio, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.45-1.56; P = .594). The locoregional recurrence rates were similar between the segmentectomy and lobectomy groups (10% vs 9%, respectively).ConclusionsSegmentectomy was not associated with worse survival or local recurrence compared with lobectomy in this selected multicenter retrospective cohort of patients with small-sized, radiologically solid-dominant clinical stage I NSCLC with pathologic VPI.

Central Message

Segmentectomy resulted in outcomes comparable with those of lobectomy in patients with small-sized and radiologically solid-dominant c-stage I non–small cell lung cancer with visceral pleural invasion.

Perspective

Segmentectomy for small-sized, radiologically solid-dominant c-stage I non–small cell lung cancer with visceral pleural invasion remains controversial. This study reveals that segmentectomy may provide outcomes comparable to those of lobectomy when adequate lymph node dissection is achieved. Prospective validation is necessary to clarify the efficacy of segmentectomy in high-risk patients.

In recent randomized clinical trials, including Japanese Oncology Group 0802/West Japan Oncology Group 4607L1 and Cancer and Leukemia Group B 140503,2 segmentectomy has been established as a viable alternative to lobectomy in selected patients with small-sized, early-stage non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). However, indications for biologically aggressive tumors remain controversial. Visceral pleural invasion (VPI) is a well-recognized adverse prognostic factor associated with an increased risk of lymph node metastasis.3,4

Segmentectomy is considered the most appropriate sublobar resection method because it involves anatomical dissection along the segmental bronchus and vessels, enabling systematic lymph node dissection and securing a sufficient surgical margin, whereas wedge resection is limited to lymph node sampling. However, in VPI-positive NSCLC, unique subpleural lymphatic drainage patterns crossing the intersegmental boundaries5 raise concerns that segmentectomy may result in incomplete lymph node dissection and increase the risk of residual disease. In addition, definitive conclusions regarding the recurrence patterns and lymph node spread have not been previously provided.6,7 Therefore, this study aimed to compare survival and recurrence patterns between segmentectomy and lobectomy in patients with a whole tumor size of ≤3 cm, radiologically solid-dominant, clinical stage I NSCLC with VPI.

Methods

Ethics Statement

The institutional review board of Hiroshima University Hospital (approval number E2018-1216-02) approved this study on November 30, 2022, and waived the requirement for informed consent owing to its retrospective nature.

Study Cohort

This retrospective study included 2247 patients at Kanagawa Cancer Center, Tokyo Medical University Hospital, and Hiroshima University between 2010 and 2022 who underwent segmentectomy or lobectomy for clinical stage I NSCLC characterized by solid-dominant tumors (0.5 < consolidation-to-tumor ratio [CTR] ≤1) and a whole tumor size ≤3 cm. Each institution maintained a prospectively curated database. All patients underwent preoperative evaluation using high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT), 18-fluoro-2-deoxyglucose positron emission tomography combined with computed tomography (FDG-PET/CT), and pulmonary function tests. Lymph nodes were considered clinically negative for metastasis when their short-axis diameter on HRCT was <10 mm and showed no abnormal FDG uptake on PET/CT. Invasive nodal staging procedures, such as endobronchial ultrasound or mediastinoscopy, were not routinely performed and were reserved for cases in which mediastinal involvement was suspected on imaging. Patients who received neoadjuvant treatment were excluded. Clinical staging was determined on the basis of the eighth edition of the Tumor, Node, and Metastasis Classification for Lung and Pleural Tumors.8

Surgical Procedure

The surgical approach and extent of resection were determined by a multidisciplinary cancer board comprising thoracic surgeons, pulmonologists, pathologists, and radiologists, who considered the tumor characteristics and patient status. Segmentectomy was performed when the preoperative computed tomography (CT) images indicated an adequate resection margin that was clearly larger than the tumor diameter. Surgical margins were evaluated on preoperative CT imaging via the use of axial, sagittal, and coronal HRCT scans or 3-dimensional reconstructed CT imaging. All patients provided informed consent after receiving thorough counseling regarding the potential risks and expected benefits of each surgical method.

Anatomical dissections of the pulmonary artery, vein, and bronchus were routinely performed during segmentectomy. The intersegmental plane was identified intraoperatively using either the inflation-deflation technique or indocyanine green fluorescence imaging. The lung parenchyma was divided using staplers or electrocautery to ensure sufficient surgical margins. Lymph node status and surgical margins were macroscopically evaluated during surgery, and suspected lymph node metastases were confirmed using intraoperative frozen section analysis. Conversion to lobectomy was considered when surgical margins were positive or close, or when lymph node metastasis was identified. Postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy was considered for patients with pathologic stage IA ≥20 mm or more in advanced disease stages.

Follow-up Examination

Patients underwent systematic follow-up every 3 to 6 months for the first 5 years and subsequently annually up to 10 years, including clinical examination, tumor markers, and imaging. Further investigations, such as FDG-PET/CT scans or magnetic resonance imaging of the brain, were promptly conducted if recurrence was clinically or radiologically suspected. In clear cases of recurrence confirmed using FDG-PET/CT, histologic confirmation was not mandatory. However, for uncertain or ambiguous lesions, biopsy was performed to establish a definitive diagnosis. Recurrences were classified as locoregional if they occurred within the ipsilateral thoracic cavity, including the surgical margins, ipsilateral bronchial stumps, hilar or mediastinal lymph nodes, and pleural space. All other recurrence locations of recurrence were classified as distant recurrence.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Fisher exact test. Median follow-up was calculated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was defined as the interval from surgery to first recurrence, death from any cause, or last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from surgery to death from any cause or last follow-up. Survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. The main outcomes of interest were OS and RFS, and subgroup analyses were performed on the basis of tumor size (≤2 cm vs 2-3 cm), VPI status, and nodal status.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to adjust for confounding factors. Propensity scores were calculated via logistic regression on the basis of age (continuous), sex (male vs female), CTR (<1 vs 1), tumor location, and maximum standardized uptake value (continuous). Patients were matched 1:1 using greedy matching with a caliper width of 0.20 times the standard deviation of the logit-transformed propensity score. Balance was assessed by calculating the standardized mean differences. Multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed in the matched cohort to adjust for residual confounding, including clinically relevant variables not fully balanced after matching. No missing data were observed for variables included in the multivariable analysis.

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP (version 17.0; SAS Institute) and EZR software (version 1.51; Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University), which is a graphical interface for R statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Patient Characteristics

Overall, 418 patients underwent either segmentectomy or lobectomy for small-sized (≤3 cm), radiologically solid-dominant (0.5 < CTR ≤1) clinical stage I NSCLC with pathologic VPI (Figure 1). Among them, segmentectomy and lobectomy were performed for 81 and 337 patients, respectively. Among segmentectomy, single- and multisegmentectomies were performed in 38 (47%) and 43 (53%) patients, respectively. The clinicopathologic characteristics before and after PSM are summarized in Table 1. Before PSM, patients in the segmentectomy group were significantly older, had smaller tumors, lower maximum standardized uptake value, more frequent left upper lobe location, and a greater incidence of lymphatic invasion than those in the lobectomy group did. Pathologic lymph node and skip N2 metastases were identified in 95 (23%) and 19 (5%) patients, respectively; however, no statistically significant differences were observed between the 2 groups. Survival Outcomes

The median follow-up duration after surgery was 3.8 years (IQR, 3.2-4.5 years). The median follow-up was shorter in the segmentectomy group than in the lobectomy group (2.0 years [IQR, 1.4-2.6 years] vs 4.5 years [IQR, 3.8-4.9 years]). In the entire cohort, OS and RFS did not significantly differ between groups. The 5-year OS was 86.2% (95% CI, 70.7%-94.2%) in the segmentectomy group and 75.8% (95% CI, 69.7%-80.9%) in the lobectomy group (P = .540), whereas the 5-year RFS was 66.0% (95% CI, 48.7%-79.8%) and 59.3% (95% CI, 53.1%-65.2%), respectively (P = .681) (Figure 2). Discussion

In this study, OS and RFS did not significantly differ between segmentectomy and lobectomy, and recurrence patters were also comparable (Figure 5). Indeed, several previous studies9,10 have also indicated the feasibility and oncologic adequacy of segmentectomy even for tumors with aggressive biological features. Similar trends were also observed when stratified by pleural status and tumor size. One possible explanation is the adequacy of lymph node dissection during segmentectomy. In our cohort, the proportion of nodal upstaging and the incidence of hilar or mediastinal lymph node recurrence in the segmentectomy group were comparable with those in the lobectomy group, indicating that appropriate nodal clearance can be achieved even with segmentectomy.Figure 5Graphic abstract including methods, results, and implications of this study.

Tumors with VPI can spread via subpleural lymphatic pathways that traverse the intersegmental boundaries5; nevertheless, the actual risk of residual disease appears to be low when adequate lymph node dissection is performed. Notably, these findings are based on dye injection, not on the tracking of tumor cells, and thus may not directly reflect the behavior of malignant spread. Consistent with this, in the supplementary analysis of JCOG0802/WJOG4607L, Maniwa and colleagues11 reported that nodal metastasis to nonadjacent segments was rare. Collectively, these findings suggest that segmentectomy can provide oncologic outcomes comparable to those of lobectomy when adequate lymph node dissection is achieved.

Of note, recurrence was observed in 114 patients (27%), indicating a potential need for postoperative adjuvant therapy. Current National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines version. 7, 2025,12 recommend adjuvant therapy in stage IB with high-risk features including poorly differentiated tumors, vascular invasion, VPI, wedge resection, and unknown lymph node status. However, the latest pivotal adjuvant trials13, 14, 15 have uniformly excluded patients with small-sized (≤3cm) pathologic stage I NSCLC, even with VPI, leaving a gap in evidence for small, node-negative NSCLC with VPI. Beyond current guideline-defined factors, circulating tumor DNA16 and lymphatic invasion17 represent promising tools to refine patient selection and guide individualized adjuvant strategies in early-stage NSCLC; however, their clinical utility warrants further validation in prospective studies.

Furthermore, the comparison between ≤2 cm and 2- to 3-cm tumors is an important point when considering the intensive indication for segmentectomy, particularly in relation to surgical margin adequacy and tumor aggressiveness. In recent randomized clinical trials,1,2 the efficacy of segmentectomy for tumors up to 2 cm in size was demonstrated, leaving the role of segmentectomy for solid-dominant tumors measuring between 2-3 cm unclear. Currently, in 2 ongoing clinical trials18 in Japan investigators are aiming to clarify this issue, and their results are of great interest. In our subgroup analyses stratified by tumor size (≤2 cm and 2-3 cm), both groups showed comparable oncologic outcomes between segmentectomy and lobectomy. Thus, our findings support segmentectomy as a feasible surgical option even in VPI-positive cases.

Specifically, our results suggest that completion lobectomy may not be necessary for patients who are found to have VPI after surgery. Because it is often difficult to diagnose VPI preoperatively or intraoperatively unless there is clear evidence of chest wall invasion or adjacent organs, segmentectomy may be an acceptable option in such cases.

Study Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, because of its retrospective nature, preoperative variables such as performance status, pulmonary function, comorbidities, and the reasons for selecting segmentectomy were unavailable, possibly introducing selection bias. To minimize confounding, multivariable adjustment and propensity score matching were applied. Second, data regarding surgical margins, conversion from segmentectomy to lobectomy, and spread through air spaces were also unavailable, limiting a comprehensive assessment of resection adequacy. Because inadequate surgical margin and the presence of spread through air spaces are known to increase the risk of locoregional recurrence after sublobar resection, this remains a major limitation of our study. In addition, the presence of VPI could not be determined intraoperatively, and conversion to lobectomy was performed only when the margin was deemed insufficient or lymph node metastasis was detected. Therefore, surgical selection bias related to unrecognized VPI cannot be completely excluded. Nonetheless, the comparable outcomes observed between the groups suggest that the impact of such conversions may have been limited. Third, although the study period spanned more than 12 years, the median follow-up duration was relatively short and differed between surgical groups, potentially underestimating late recurrence and long-term survival differences. Longer follow-up will be necessary to confirm the robustness of these findings. Fourth, the impact of adjuvant therapy on prognosis was not analyzed because of potential selection. Finally, postoperative pulmonary function was not specifically evaluated due to a lack of data, even though it is correlated with prognosis.19 Therefore, caution should be taken when interpreting our findings, acknowledging the potential biases and data constraints inherent in retrospective analyses. Further prospective validation in future trials and longer follow-up are warranted to confirm these findings.

Conclusions

Segmentectomy was not associated with worse survival or local recurrence than lobectomy in this multicenter retrospective cohort of carefully selected patients with small-sized (≤3 cm), radiologically solid-dominant clinical stage I NSCLC with pathologic VPI. Given the retrospective design and potential residual confounding, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and further prospective studies are warranted to better define the optimal extent of resection in this setting.
