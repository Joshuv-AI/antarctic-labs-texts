Impact of bed roughness and submergence on velocity profiles and flow structures in hydraulic jumps
Authors: Nishank Agrawal, Ellora Padhi, Frédérique Larrarte, Gopal Das Singhal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydraulic jumps downstream of spillways exhibit high near-bed velocities that can induce cavitation and scour. While bed roughness is known to modify velocity distribution and roller characteristics, the underlying flow physics governing velocity transition, turbulence dissipation, and vortex evolution under varying submergence conditions remain insufficiently understood. This study presents an integrated experimental and numerical investigation of free and submerged hydraulic jumps over smooth and corrugated beds to elucidate these mechanisms. Experiments were conducted at discharges of 11, 13, and 15 l s⁻1 for submergence ratios ranging from 0 to 0.4, and complemented by three-dimensional CFD simulations using a RANS-based RNG k–ε model in FLOW-3D. Detailed analyses of velocity similarity profiles, boundary layer development, turbulent kinetic energy (TKE), dissipation rate, total energy loss, and Q-criterion based vortex structures were performed. The results reveal that bed corrugation accelerates boundary layer development, enhances turbulence breakdown, and increases energy dissipation compared to smooth beds. Submergence shifts the turbulence generation zone downstream and increases roller length; however, corrugated beds consistently confine coherent vortices and reduce near-bed velocity under all tested conditions. The study provides new physical insight into turbulence–roughness–submergence interactions in spillway-induced hydraulic jumps and offers design guidance for improving stilling basin performance and reducing downstream scour risk.Keywords: Boundary layer development, Corrugated bed, Hydraulic jump, Submergence, Vortex dynamics, Velocity distribution, TKESubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Hydraulic jumps play a crucial role in open channel hydraulics, where turbulence and eddy formation dissipate the excess energy released from hydraulic structures such as spillways, sluice gates, and weirs, thereby protecting stilling basins and downstream riverbeds. However, if not properly controlled, hydraulic jumps can also generate adverse effects. The velocity distribution within a hydraulic jump is highly non-uniform, resembling that of a wall jet transitioning toward the semilogarithmic profile of open channel flow, with maximum velocity typically occurring near the bed and gradually decreasing toward the water surface1,2. High velocity close to the bed can lead to localized cavitation damage and excessive shear stresses, which often initiate severe scour downstream3. To mitigate the adverse impacts of near-bed velocities, several researchers have investigated the influence of bed roughness on hydraulic jumps. Rough surfaces such as corrugated beds, blocks, or baffles alter the flow structure, enhance turbulence, and redistribute momentum vertically4–6. Notably, the introduction of bed roughness has been shown to vertically shift the peak velocity upward, thereby reducing near-bed stresses and enhancing energy dissipation efficiency7,8. To quantify this vertical shift, the velocity is normalized by maximum velocity um, at each section, while the flow depth (z) is normalized by a length scale b. The parameter b is defined as the vertical distance from the channel bed where the local velocity u = 0.5um and the velocity gradient is negative as shown in Fig. 1a9. Using this scaling, longitudinal velocity profiles in the forward flow region at different sections tend to collapse into a common band, often following a fourth-degree polynomial curve. In addition, Long et al.10 introduced another characteristic length scale, L, is used to quantify the decay of the maximum velocity along the streamwise direction. This scale represents the longitudinal distance from the jump toe at which um = 0.5u1 described in Fig. 1a, where u1 denotes the initial supercritical velocity. When the longitudinal distance x is normalized by L, the maximum velocity decay profiles (um/u1) from different sections also collapse into a consistent band.

Definition sketch of (a) free jump condition, (b) submerged jump condition downstream of spillway. The blue area represents the negative velocity region, known as the roller zone.

In Fig. 1a and b, the parameters described include z1 and z2, representing the sequent depths at spillway toe and after the free jump, respectively, while z3 and z4, represent the sequent depths at spillway toe and after the submerged jump, respectively. The tailwater depth is denoted by zt. The variables u1 and u2 are the maximum velocities at spillway toe and after the jump, respectively and um is the maximum velocity at any given section. Additionally, Lr and Lj denote the roller length and jump length, respectively. The variable δ measures the distance from the bed to the point of maximum velocity at any section, commonly called the boundary layer thickness.

A second factor influencing hydraulic jumps is submergence, which occurs when the downstream tailwater depth is higher than the sequent depth corresponding to a free jump as shown in Fig. 1b. Submergence is characterized by the submergence factor, S = (z4 – z2)/z2, defined as the ratio of the excess tailwater depth in a submerged jump to the tailwater depth in a free jump9. Submerged jumps typically dissipate less energy than free jumps, as the excess tailwater depth restricts air entrainment, suppresses surface roller formation, and modifies turbulence dynamics10–12. Although submerged jumps are common in real-world situations, especially downstream of gated structures, they are less understood compared to free jumps. Studies1,8,13 reported that free jumps exhibit greater energy dissipations on a smooth bed compared to submerged jumps, primarily due to the faster decay of maximum velocity in the forward flow region of a free jump. Moreover, submerged jumps typically exhibit longer roller and jump lengths than free jumps, necessitating an increase in stilling basin length to accommodate these characteristics. Previous studies by various researchers as Wu and Rajaratnam2,14, Long et al.10, Zobeyer et al.15, Wang and Chanson11, Takahashi and Ohtsu16, Bai et al.17, and Bagheri et al.18 extensively explored the dynamics of free and submerged jumps on smooth beds, contributing valuable insights into their behaviour and performance. When examining the development of hydraulic jumps on a rough bed, it has been observed that the introduction of roughness increases shear stress, reducing jump length and stabilizing the flow5. Besides, there are several studies exist which showed that presence of roughness element (gravels, cobbles, corrugation, etc.) causes reduction in velocity, with enhanced Reynolds shear stress and turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) dissipation19–24. Additionally, incorporating roughness has proven to be cost-effective as it reduces the required length of the stilling basin downstream of outlet structures. While numerous studies have explored the influence of rough beds on free jumps25–31, Samadi-Boroujeni32 examined free jumps on various triangulated roughness downstream of a spillway. Habibzadeh33 investigated submerged jumps with baffle blocks. Most recently, Agrawal and Padhi34 comprehensively reviewed experimental and analytical studies on smooth and rough beds, concluding that hydraulic jumps on rough beds result in greater energy dissipation, smaller roller length, and reduced sequent depth. Research on submerged jumps over rough beds is still limited1,4,7,35–37. These studies found that roughness increases boundary layer thickness in submerged jumps than those formed over smooth beds. The decay of Reynolds shear stress and turbulence intensities are quicker on fully rough walls compared to smooth walls, but slower than on transitional rough walls.

Recent advances in computational fluid dynamics (CFD) have opened new opportunities for studying the complex turbulence structures within hydraulic jumps. Traditional laboratory studies, while valuable, are limited in their ability to capture three-dimensional flow fields and the dynamics of coherent vortical structures. RANS-based turbulence modeling, particularly with the RNG k–ε or realizable k–ε closures, has been successfully applied to simulate hydraulic jumps and provide insights into mean velocity, pressure distributions, and turbulence intensities38. Beyond mean flow analysis, modern vortex identification techniques such as the Q-criterion have been used to detect coherent vortices, quantify their evolution, and link their dynamics to turbulence energy dissipation39,40. Such methods allow for a more detailed understanding of the breakdown of large eddies into smaller turbulent structures, which governs mixing and energy dissipation processes in both free and submerged jumps. Numerous studies have focused specifically on submerged hydraulic jumps over smooth beds using CFD approaches3,32,41–43. Additionally, several researchers have utilized CFD in combination with soft computing techniques to analyze submerged jumps over different macroroughness configurations22,35,44,45. Viti et al.46 provided a comprehensive review of numerical modeling strategies for hydraulic jumps.

Nevertheless, significant research gaps remain. Previous studies on hydraulic jumps over smooth and corrugated beds, under both free and submerged conditions, have primarily emphasized global flow characteristics such as sequent depth ratio, roller length, and boundary layer thickness. These investigations generally reported that increasing bed roughness leads to a reduction in roller length and sequent depth while enhancing boundary layer thickness, whereas increasing submergence results in longer rollers and higher sequent depths but suppresses boundary layer growth. Despite these findings, most existing studies remain largely result-oriented and provide limited insight into the underlying flow physics governing these observed behaviors. In particular, the evolution of near-bed velocity structures, turbulence dissipation mechanisms, vortex dynamics, and the transition of wall-jet velocity profiles toward the equilibrium logarithmic distribution typical of open-channel flows have not been systematically examined for both free and submerged hydraulic jumps.

The present study addresses these gaps by adopting an integrated experimental and numerical approach to investigate free and submerged hydraulic jumps over smooth and corrugated beds. Detailed experimental velocity measurements are conducted for discharges of 11, 13, and 15 l s⁻1 with submergence ratios ranging from 0 to 0.4. Similarity velocity profiles and boundary layer development plots are employed to quantify the vertical and horizontal shifts in the location of maximum velocity and to examine the evolution of near-bed flow structures under varying submergence conditions. Complementary numerical simulations are performed using FLOW-3D with the RNG k–ε turbulence model for a discharge of 15 l s⁻1 under different submergence levels. Post-processing includes analyses of velocity and water surface profiles, turbulent kinetic energy (TKE), TKE dissipation rate, total energy line (TEL), and vortex structures identified using the Q-criterion to elucidate turbulence characteristics and vortex breakdown mechanisms within the hydraulic jump.

While the authors’ recent study39 examined the influence of bed roughness combined with geometric modifications such as negative steps and end sills on hydraulic jump behavior, that work primarily focused on free hydraulic jumps and employed numerical simulations supported by limited experimental measurements for validation purposes. In contrast, the present study emphasizes detailed and systematic experimental investigations, providing comprehensive velocity measurements under both free and submerged conditions. Although the numerical framework adopted in this study, including analyses of velocity distributions, turbulent kinetic energy, and Q-criterion based vortex identification, is similar to that used in the earlier work but the underlying physical focus is fundamentally different. The present study specifically isolates the effect of submergence, allowing a clear assessment of how varying submergence levels alter hydraulic jump dynamics over smooth and corrugated beds. We believe that, taken together, the authors’ earlier study and the present investigation provide comprehensive insights into hydraulic jump behavior under both free and submerged conditions, particularly clarifying the role of submergence in modifying flow structures and dynamics over smooth and corrugated beds.

Experimental setup and procedure

In this section, we present a detailed overview of the experimental flume setup utilized in our research, along with descriptions of the models employed for the investigations.

Experiments were conducted within a rectangular open channel flume of 0.3 m wide, 0.5 m deep, and 5.5 m long, with transparent glass sidewalls for optical observation, as shown in Fig. 2. The portion where the negative velocity prevails is called a recirculatory zone, characterized by significant energy dissipation. The length up to which the unstable zone persists is called roller length Lr. Beyond this length, the flow tends towards stabilization, resembling a steady state with no negative velocity. For a better understanding of the experimental setup, a schematic diagram is depicted in Fig. 2. It included an inlet and outlet tank for water supply and collection, respectively, with recirculation facilitated by a circular pipe. A 5 Horsepower centrifugal pump transferred water from the inlet tank to the channel, passing through a settling tank upstream to stabilize the flow.

Schemetic diagram of experimental setup for corrugated bed downstream of spillway.

Additionally, a honeycomb structure was provided to reduce turbulence before the water entered the channel. A movable sharp-crested rectangular weir, adjustable from 0 to 90°, was employed to regulate flow depth. This setup offered precise control over the flow parameters, enabling researchers to conduct detailed experiments on water flow behaviour in open channels under various flow conditions.

Experiments were conducted on ogee spillways with two types of beds: a smooth bed and a corrugated rough bed, each 0.1 m thick, positioned 0.8 m from the channel inlet. The smooth bed, referred to as Model 1, is shown in Fig. 3a. The rough bed, referred to as Model 2, features a sinusoidal corrugation with an amplitude (roughness height) of 22 mm and a wavelength of 68 mm, as depicted in Fig. 3b. This type of sinusoidal roughness has been used in some studies5,25. The crest of this roughness was strategically aligned with the spillway toe to mitigate cavitation effects5 and depicted comprehensively in the accompanying Fig. 3. The spillway was designed in accordance with the channel’s dimensions and discharge carrying capacity, following standard design procedures to ensure optimal performance and reliability47.

Detailed schematic diagram of (a) Model 1 and (b) Model 2.

Experiments were conducted under varying discharge conditions: Q = 11, 13, and 15 l s−1 and corresponding upstream Froude number 5.55, 5.09 and 4.83. Discharge rates were quantified using flowmeter positioned within the supply line. Additionally, Water depth was monitored using both a pointer gauge and electronic water level sensors, each having an accuracy of ± 1 mm. In regions of relatively stable free surface, the pointer gauge was used for direct measurements. However, within the recirculating and highly fluctuating roller zone, water depth was recorded using the electronic sensor at a sampling frequency of 10 Hz for a duration of 2 min at each location (approximately 1200 samples per point). The reported free-surface elevation corresponds to the time-averaged value of the recorded signal, thereby minimizing the influence of instantaneous fluctuations and air–water mixing effects. For the rough corrugated bed, water depth measurements were initiated from the crest of the corrugation. The corrugated bed begins 0.02 m downstream of the spillway toe; hence toe-depth-based hydraulic parameters are evaluated on the smooth bed, while any crest-referenced depth definition is applied only within the corrugated reach. Velocity measurements were taken along the channel centerline, at 5 cm intervals from the spillway toe to the end of the smooth and corrugated bed and vertically at 5 mm intervals. These measurements were facilitated by a pitot static tube with an external diameter of 6 mm, connected to an inverted manometer, to capture longitudinal velocity (u). To assess the sensitivity of the Pitot tube, particularly near the bed and within the mixing zone, velocity measurements were recorded five times over 2-min intervals at each location and then time-averaged. The average deviation was only 0.04%, confirming measurement reliability. For proper alignment, the Pitot tube was mounted at the center of the flume trolley and aligned precisely with the flume’s centerline. Due to shallow flow depths (less than 5 cm) near the spillway toe and along parts of the channel, instruments such as Acoustic Doppler Velocimetry (ADV) or flow trackers were unsuitable, making the Pitot tube the most practical option. Notably, no corrections were applied to the velocity data to accommodate turbulence or the presence of air bubbles. Both free and submerged jumps were observed based on tailwater conditions, providing valuable insights into flow dynamics. Numerical setup

To visualize the flow and understand its underlying behavior and physics, numerical simulations were performed using the CFD software FLOW-3D.

Numerical model

FLOW-3D employs the Finite Volume Method (FVM) to numerically solve the Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) equations on a Cartesian, staggered grid framework. Solid boundaries and flow obstructions are represented using the Fractional Area/Volume Obstacle Representation (FAVOR) technique, originally introduced by Hirt and Sicilian48. The three-dimensional incompressible flow is governed by the continuity and momentum equations, expressed as eqs. (1) and (2) in terms of time-averaged velocity and pressure fields.

where, U denotes the mean velocity vector, P the mean pressure, ρ the fluid density, and ν the kinematic viscosity. The Reynolds stress tensor , which arises from the averaging process, requires appropriate turbulence modeling to achieve closure. In this study, the Renormalization Group (RNG) k-ε turbulence model developed by Yakhot et al.14 was adopted due to its proven capability in simulating complex free-surface flows such as hydraulic jumps (e.g.,35,38). Although RANS-based turbulence models are limited in resolving instantaneous coherent structures and turbulence anisotropy, they provide reliable predictions of time-averaged flow quantities, which are the primary focus of the present investigation. The present study aims to systematically compare free and submerged hydraulic jumps over smooth and corrugated beds across different submergence levels. Previous studies have employed higher-fidelity turbulence models to investigate hydraulic jumps. For example, Jesudhas et al.49 used a three-dimensional Detached Eddy Simulation (DES) framework to capture complex turbulence structures, shear-layer dynamics, and interactions between the wall jet and roller region in a classical hydraulic jump. Similarly, Zhang et al.50 applied Large Eddy Simulation (LES) to resolve coherent vortical structures, turbulence evolution, and free-surface fluctuations in hydraulic jumps, highlighting the ability of LES to represent instantaneous three-dimensional flow physics.

Despite these advantages, the strong emphasis of LES and DES on transient and highly unsteady turbulence makes systematic comparison of submergence-induced variations in mean flow characteristics computationally demanding and less straightforward. Since the present study focuses on time-averaged velocity fields, turbulent kinetic energy, dissipation rates, and averaged vortex structures, a RANS-based approach is more appropriate. Accordingly, the RNG k–ε model implemented in FLOW-3D was adopted, with wall effects implicitly accounted for through no-slip boundary conditions. The application of LES or hybrid RANS–LES (DES) approaches is beyond the scope of the present work and is identified as a potential direction for future research.

Boundary conditions

While experiments were conducted for Q = 11, 13, and 15 l s−1 under submergence conditions ranging from 0 to 0.4, numerical simulations were carried out only for Q = 15 l s−1 with submergence levels of 0, 0.25, and 0.4. The simulation boundary conditions were configured to closely replicate the experimental setup. The spillway geometry and associated bed were modeled identically to those used in the physical experiments, as shown in Fig. 4. At the inlet, a constant discharge of 15 l s−1 and an upstream head of 0.385 m (as observed in the experiments) were applied. Hydrostatic pressure was applied at the outlet to match the experimental tailwater depth for jump formation as shown in Fig. 4. While tailgate adjustments easily produced free and submerged jumps in experiments, the numerical model was highly sensitive to downstream conditions. Exact experimental depths did not always produce a free jump in simulations, requiring multiple iterations with minor adjustments (± 1–2 cm) to achieve the desired flow condition. Once a free jump was established, submerged conditions were generated by increasing the tailwater depth accordingly. No-slip conditions were applied to the bed and sidewalls to reflect the physical boundaries used in the experiments. The free surface was modeled using atmospheric pressure boundary conditions. The Volume of Fluid (VOF) technique, introduced by Hirt and Nichols51, was employed to track the water surface. This method utilizes a volume fraction (F), which ranges from 0 (indicating air) to 1 (indicating water) represent a fluid–air mixture region, while F = 0.5 represents the fluid-air interface, thereby identifying the free surface location.

Boundary conditions representation for Model 1: Red ‘Q’ indicates constant discharge, red ‘P’ represents hydrostatic pressure, blue ‘P’ denotes atmospheric pressure, and green ‘W’ signifies no-slip boundary conditions (wall boundary condition).

Meshing information and sensitivity analysis

The computational domain was defined to closely replicate the experimental setup, extending 38 cm upstream of the spillway geometry and continuing 2 m in the x-direction, 0 to 0.3 m in the y-direction, and 0 to 0.4 m in the z-direction. Three uniformly structured meshes with characteristic grid sizes of 6 mm (coarse), 5 mm (medium), and 4 mm (fine) were generated to assess grid sensitivity. The computational grid in FLOW-3D is based on a structured Cartesian mesh, where geometric features are represented using the built-in FAVOR™ (Fractional Area/Volume Obstacle Representation) method. In this approach, solid boundaries such as the spillway slope and sinusoidal corrugation are embedded within the Cartesian cells through fractional area and volume calculations. The total number of cells was 1,179,800 for the 6 mm mesh, 2,046,720 for the 5 mm mesh, and 4,017,000 for the 4 mm configuration. Based on the grid refinement assessment and consideration of computational efficiency, the 5 mm mesh was selected for subsequent simulations, as it provides adequate geometric resolution while avoiding the substantially higher computational cost of the 4 mm grid. In Fig. 5, the positive x-axis originates at the spillway toe, marking the start of the stilling basin, while locations upstream of the toe are shown as negative values. This axis convention is maintained in the subsequent figures as well.

As shown in Fig. 5a, the water surface profile, in Fig. 5b, the total energy line, and in Fig. 5c depth average velocity (V) were compared for mesh sizes of 5 mm and 4 mm along the flow direction. The results exhibit nearly overlapping distributions across the computational domain. Numerical model validation

To validate the numerical model, water surface profiles along the flow direction (Fig. 6) and longitudinal velocity profiles at x = 0.60 and 0.85 m (Fig. 7) were plotted for Q = 15 l s−1. These velocity measurement locations were selected downstream of the roller region, as the presence of intense recirculation and backward flow within the roller zone can significantly affect the reliability of Pitot tube measurements. The results were analyzed under free jump conditions in Model 1 and a submergence ratio of S = 0.25 in Model 2. In both cases, the numerical predictions closely matched the experimental observations. While noticeable deviations are observed near the spillway toe in the water surface profile (Fig. 6), the numerical predictions agree well with the experimental measurements in the fully developed hydraulic jump region downstream. The deviations at the toe are attributed to the inherently unsteady and highly sensitive nature of jump initiation in spillway flows. Unlike hydraulic jumps downstream of sluice gates, where the upstream depth and jump location can be controlled. The spillway toe is free to migrate and is strongly influenced by local turbulence and free-surface instabilities, making exact matching at that location inherently difficult.

Results and discussions

This section is organized into two parts: experimental findings and numerical results. In the experimental section, we analyze velocity profiles over both smooth and corrugated beds under free and submerged flow conditions. The analysis focuses on how hydraulic jump behavior is influenced by bed roughness and submergence levels. Additionally, the results are compared with previous studies to provide broader context and validate the observations. The numerical section presents velocity vector fields, turbulence kinetic energy (TKE) distributions, TKE dissipation rates, and Q-criterion plots to illustrate vortex structures and further understand the flow dynamics. It is important to note that experimentally, velocity measurements inside the corrugation grooves were not possible due to probe length limitations; therefore, near-bed velocities were measured above the corrugation crests. Since the analysis focuses on the main flow region above the corrugation elements, this limitation does not affect the reported near-bed velocity trends. Moreover, numerical simulations capture the flow within the corrugation cavities, including local recirculation effects.

Analysis of experimental observations

Free jump on smooth and corrugated bed

To explore the behavior of free jumps on both smooth and corrugated bed surfaces, velocity profiles were analysed under discharge rates of 11, 13, and 15 l s−1 and corresponding Froude number 5.55, 5.09 and 4.83. The analysis is focused on velocity data obtained at every 5 cm interval along the centerline in a longitudinal direction (x) and at 5 mm increments vertically along the flow depth (z). For all the velocity plots, the upper and lower range of velocity are kept 2.5 m s−1 and − 0.5 m s−1, respectively, to maintain uniformity among the plots and that helps in comparison study.

Free jump on smooth bed (SF jump)

Figure 8 illustrates the formation of free jumps on a smooth bed for different discharges. In the figure, the continuous, dashed, and dash-dot lines represent the water surface, roller zone, and boundary layer development, respectively. The lines and corresponding symbols are coloured dark green, red, and blue to represent discharges of Q = 15 l s−1, Q = 13 l s−1, and Q = 11 l s−1, respectively. It is important to note that, throughout this article, a free jump on a smooth bed is referred to as an SF jump. In Fig. 8, it is observed that along the vertical direction, velocity is maximum close to the bed, and then gradually decreases with an increase in flow depth, becoming zero. Thereafter, it becomes negative. Such pattern of velocity profile is observed at the beginning of the jump and up to some extent in the longitudinal direction. The portion where such a pattern is evident is termed an unstable flow zone. In this flow zone, the flow is highly turbulent and chaotic known as recirculatory zone or roller zone, where significant energy dissipation occurs. The roller length (Lr) was determined as the horizontal distance from the spillway toe to the downstream point where reverse flow disappears. Quantitatively, Lr was identified by tracing the zero longitudinal velocity contour in the velocity profiles; the region above this zero-velocity line (dash line) represents negative (recirculating) flow within the roller. The hydraulic jump length (Lj) was measured from the toe to the location where the free surface becomes nearly horizontal and the flow reaches stable subcritical conditions.

Spatial variation of the velocity profiles of SF jumps under discharges (a) Q = 11 l s−1, (b) Q = 13 l s−1, and (c) Q = 15 l s−1.

The distance from the jump initiation (x = 0) to the point (x = 0.5, for Q = 11 l s−1, x = 0.55, for Q = 13 l s−1, and x = 0.6, for Q = 15 l s−1) where water surface profile reached the z2 depth, ensuring a constant flow depth, is termed the jump length (Lj). Additionally, a dash dotted line illustrates boundary layer development, marked by the joining of maximum velocities along the flow direction. Here, the maximum velocity occurs near the bed gradually decreases along the flow direction as the boundary layer develops. Furthermore, Fig. 8 shows that an increase in discharge corresponds to an augmentation in roller length, boundary layer thickness, and sequent depth. Another observation from Fig. 8 is a slight reduction in the near-surface velocity downstream of the hydraulic jump. With the channel aspect ratio (width to depth ratio) w/z ≈ 2, the flow is moderately confined, and lateral boundary effects may influence the cross-sectional momentum distribution. Such confinement can give rise to weak secondary currents, which may contribute to the observed redistribution of velocity near the free surface.

Free jump on the corrugated bed (CF jump)

To evaluate the impact of bed roughness on the free jump, the spatial variation of velocity profiles on a corrugated rough bed is investigated and presented in Fig. 9. It may be noted that here, free jump on the corrugated bed is referred to as the CF jump. From Fig. 9, it is found that in case of the CF jump, the velocity profiles during the CF jump follow same pattern to those observed during the SF jump. However, upon closer comparison, it is found that the CF jump leads to a reduced roller length and sequent depth compared to those seen in the SF jump. For Q = 15 l s−1, the roller length in the CF jump is approximately 0.30 m, whereas it is about 0.40 m in the SF jump. Similarly, the post-jump sequent depth is 0.10 m for the CF jump and 0.11 m for the SF jump. One notable finding relates to the rate of development of the boundary layer thickness within the roller zone, which appears to be faster when the bed is rough compared to when it’s smooth. This accelerated development can be attributed to the quicker transformation of larger eddies, generated from the inlet velocity, into smaller eddies upon encountering the rough bed surface. In contrast, while this phenomenon also occurs during the SF jump, the development length of the boundary layer extends over a longer distance. The boundary layer becomes nearly fully developed at m in the CF jump, while in the SF jump it develops around m. This indicates that the boundary layer’s development length varies depending on the bed conditions, even under identical flow conditions. Moreover, the corrugated bed aids in stabilizing the flow over a shorter length, compared to a smooth bed. These findings highlight the subtle differences in flow behavior and boundary layer development influenced by bed roughness, which can significantly impact hydraulic processes. For instance, in the design of stilling basins, using a rough bed can enhance energy dissipation and reduce the risk of downstream scour.

Submerged jump on smooth and corrugated bed

Further, to examine the influence of submergence on velocity profiles over smooth and corrugated beds, profiles were generated for various submergence factor S for a discharge of 15 l s−1.

Submerged jump on smooth bed (SS jump)

Figure 10 depicts the spatial variation of velocity profiles for a submerged jump on a smooth bed for varying submergence factor S = 0.084, 0.25, and 0.4. Here, the submerged jump on a smooth bed is referred as an SS jump. From Fig. 10, it is seen that in case of SS jump, the jump initiates prior to the toe of the spillway, eventually submerging the toe. For all three S values, the boundary layer thickness δ is smaller and as it moves along the downstream, it gradually increases, becoming stable after certain distances. Further, it is evident that after x = 0.6 m, δ equals 5 cm, 2 cm and 1.5 cm for S = 0.084, 0.25 and 0.4 respectively. Thus, it suggests that with increasing submergence, both the roller length and sequent depth increase, while the growth of the boundary layer thickness within the jump region decreases. However, this reduction in boundary layer growth is expected to be limited to the jump region, beyond which the boundary layer thickness generally continues to increase8,12. However, a similar pattern is not followed in the case of SS jump and the reason is attributed to the underdeveloped flow condition within the smooth bed length. To be specific, in the present study, the expected increase in boundary layer thickness beyond the jump region does not occur, since the flow takes longer distance to stabilize, preventing it to achieve the development length within the stilling basin. Notably, the roller length and sequent depth are greater in the SS jump than in the SF jump for the same discharge. Despite the velocity after the jump being lower in the SS jump than the SF jump, the increased roller length leads to a longer basin length. Additionally, the rate of development of the boundary layer in the SS jump is observed to be slower than in the SF jump. This decelerated development is due to the fact that larger eddies take longer distances to convert into smaller ones because of submergence. In the SS jump, larger eddies travel below the water surface, which delays their collapse, leading to an increased roller length and slower boundary layer development. In contrast, the SF jump promotes faster eddy transformation due to air entrainment and stronger mixing at the free surface33. As a result, the SS jump proves to be less effective than the SF jump for designing a stilling basin, needs longer basin length to accommodate jump.

Spatial variation of the velocity profiles of SS jumps under Q = 15 l s−1 for different submergence S = 0.084, S = 0.25, and S = 0.4

Submerged jump on corrugated bed (CS jump)

To account the impact of submergence and bed roughness together on the flow, spatial variation of velocity profiles of a submerged jump occurring on a corrugated bed is presented in Fig. 11, for S = 0.095, 0.25, and 0.4. For all three values of S, the discharge condition is kept constant. Note that the submerged jump with corrugated bed is denoted as CS jump. Like the SS jump, in case of CS jump, increasing submergence results in enhanced roller length, sequent depth, and diminished velocity after the jump. Particularly, the CS jump exhibits a roller length that is lesser than the SS jump, but greater than the CF jump. Further, the rate of boundary layer development in the CS jump is more progressive than that of the SS jump. This faster development can be attributed to the faster conversion of large eddies into smaller ones near the bed due to surface roughness, leading to quicker boundary layer growth and subsequent flow stabilization beyond the roller zone. Despite having a greater roller length than the CF jump, the CS jump maintains lower velocity due to its higher sequent depth. This characteristic emphasizes the advantage of submerged jumps on rough beds, as they effectively dissipate energy while maintaining stability in the flow.

Water surface profile

Water surface profile analysis shown in Fig. 16 indicates that as submergence increases, the water surface rises. Although bed roughness generally increases flow depth in uniform open-channel flow due to increased resistance, hydraulic jumps behave differently. In corrugated-bed jumps, enhanced bed shear and turbulence generation increase energy dissipation within the roller, leading to a more rapid reduction of downstream momentum. Consequently, under identical inflow conditions, the sequent depth over corrugated beds is observed to be slightly smaller than that over smooth beds.

As flow exits the spillway, the high kinetic energy is transformed through a hydraulic jump, accompanied by a rise in flow depth and significant turbulence generation. The peak turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) occurs near the jump toe, indicating intense energy dissipation in this region. Downstream persistence of high TKE may increase erosion potential, whereas insufficient TKE results in weaker energy dissipation and higher residual velocities. Therefore, the spatial distribution of TKE is critical in assessing jump efficiency and downstream stability.

Figure 17 presents the contours of Turbulent Kinetic Energy (TKE) for both smooth and corrugated beds under free and submerged jump conditions. For SF jump, TKE is generated at high intensity near the toe of the spillway and gradually dissipates toward the end of the stilling basin, as shown in Fig. 17a. With increasing submergence, the overall TKE magnitude decreases; however, the zone of higher TKE shifts downstream, away from the spillway toe. Although the SS jump at S = 0.4 exhibits the lowest flow velocity due to the increased post-jump depth (Fig. 16) and maintains the longest roller zone extending to the end of the 1 m basin (Fig. 15e), the sustained turbulence suggests a potential risk of downstream scour. A similar pattern is observed for the corrugated bed cases. As submergence increases, the TKE region also shifts downstream, but the shift is comparatively smaller than that seen for the smooth bed. Importantly, for the corrugated bed at S = 0.4 (CS jump), shown in Fig. 17f, the TKE dissipates before reaching the end of the 1 m stilling basin. This indicates that, in contrast to the smooth bed, the corrugated surface enhances turbulence dissipation within the basin. Additionally, the reduced velocity resulting from increased flow depth (Fig. 16) and a confined roller zone (Fig. 15f) further minimizes the risk of downstream erosion. Additional cases include the CF jump (Fig. 17b) and CS jump at S = 0.25 (Fig. 17d), which further support this trend.

Turbulent dissipation

Turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) dissipation represents the rate at which turbulence is converted into thermal energy, directly contributing to the overall energy loss within the flow. A higher rate of TKE dissipation implies greater energy dissipation, as the incoming kinetic energy is largely transformed into potential energy and turbulence, which is eventually lost as heat. Figure 18 illustrates TKE dissipation for both smooth and corrugated bed conditions. In the smooth bed cases (Fig. 18a, c, and e), a decrease in TKE dissipation is observed with increasing submergence, indicating reduced turbulence intensity and energy loss.

A similar trend is noted for corrugated beds; however, the magnitude of TKE dissipation is consistently higher than in the smooth bed cases. This increase is attributed to the enhanced roughness from corrugations, which promotes greater turbulence breakdown and dissipation, as shown in Fig. 18b, d, and f. Notably, corrugated beds exhibit an additional region of TKE dissipation over the corrugation element. These results emphasize the important role of corrugations in promoting greater overall energy dissipation within the hydraulic jump region. These findings are consistent with the TKE distributions shown in Fig. 17, where regions of high TKE correspond to areas of elevated dissipation, highlighting a strong correlation between turbulence intensity and energy loss across both bed configurations.

Total energy profile (TEL)

Figure 19 presents the variation of total energy along the flow direction. Consistent with the TKE dissipation patterns observed in Fig. 18, cases exhibiting higher TKE dissipation correspond to lower total energy levels. Among all configurations, CF jump shows the greatest energy loss, followed by the SF jump, the CS jump at S = 0.25, and then at S = 0.4. The SS jump at S = 0.25 exhibits a total energy trend nearly identical to the CS jump at S = 0.4, while the SS jump at S = 0.4 shows the least energy dissipation. The magnitude of energy loss depends on how effectively each configuration enhances turbulence and dissipation within the jump. Corrugated beds promote greater energy reduction due to increased near-bed shear, flow separation, and vortex generation, which intensify turbulence breakdown. In contrast, higher submergence limits roller development and suppresses free-surface mixing, resulting in comparatively lower energy dissipation. Consequently, the CF case exhibits the highest energy loss, whereas the highly submerged smooth-bed case shows the least.

Although a slight downstream increase in the computed total energy line is observed after the collapse of the roller, this does not represent physical energy recovery. The hydraulic jump region is characterized by strong non-hydrostatic pressure fluctuations and significant vertical velocity components, which are not fully captured in the simplified depth-averaged energy expression. Downstream of the roller, the pressure field approaches hydrostatic conditions, and the velocity distribution becomes more uniform, leading to an apparent increase in the calculated total energy. Physically, the net energy across the stilling basin decreases due to turbulence-induced dissipation and bed friction.

Based on an initial total energy of 0.4 m, the energy loss across the stilling basin was quantified for both smooth and corrugated beds under free and submerged conditions. For the smooth bed (M1), the final energies of 0.24 m (free jump), 0.274 m (), and 0.294 m () correspond to energy losses of 0.16 m (40%), 0.126 m (31.5%), and 0.106 m (26.5%), respectively. For the corrugated bed (M2), the corresponding final energies of 0.225 m, 0.253 m, and 0.27 m yield larger energy losses of 0.175 m (43.75%), 0.147 m (36.75%), and 0.13 m (32.5%). These results indicate that energy dissipation decreases with increasing submergence for both bed configurations; however, the corrugated bed consistently produces greater energy loss compared to the smooth bed under all conditions. Moreover, the relative effectiveness of corrugation in enhancing energy dissipation becomes more pronounced as the submergence ratio increases.

Conclusions

This study addressed key research gaps in understanding the flow physics of hydraulic jumps over smooth and corrugated beds under varying submergence conditions. Unlike earlier investigations that primarily reported global parameters such as roller length, sequent depth, and boundary layer thickness, the present work systematically examined the evolution of near-bed velocity structures, turbulence dissipation mechanisms, vortex dynamics, and the transition of wall-jet velocity profiles toward equilibrium distributions typical of open-channel flows.

The combined experimental and numerical approach enabled detailed characterization of velocity redistribution, boundary layer growth, turbulence production, vortex breakdown, and total energy dissipation for both free and submerged hydraulic jumps. The results demonstrate that bed corrugation significantly modifies near-bed flow structures by accelerating the breakdown of large coherent vortices into smaller turbulent structures, thereby enhancing turbulent kinetic energy dissipation and reducing downstream transport of organized vortices. Submergence, on the other hand, shifts the turbulence generation region downstream, increases roller length, and delays energy dissipation; however, the corrugated bed consistently maintains higher dissipation efficiency compared to the smooth bed under identical submergence levels.

A major contribution of this work lies in isolating and quantifying the role of submergence in modifying hydraulic jump dynamics over rough beds, which has not been systematically addressed in previous studies. By integrating similarity velocity analysis, turbulence diagnostics (TKE and dissipation), total energy evaluation, and Q-criterion based vortex identification, the study provides a physics-based explanation for observed changes in roller development, boundary layer evolution, and energy loss mechanisms.

From an engineering perspective, the findings indicate that corrugated stilling basins can enhance turbulence dissipation, confine coherent vortex structures, reduce near-bed velocities, and potentially mitigate downstream scour, particularly under moderate to high submergence conditions. These insights provide guidance for optimizing stilling basin design where submergence effects cannot be neglected.

Overall, this investigation advances the fundamental understanding of turbulence–roughness–submergence interactions in spillway-induced hydraulic jumps and complements previous work by offering a comprehensive assessment of both free and submerged jump behavior over smooth and corrugated beds.
