Neuron-specific deficiency of autophagy increases neuronal loss in traumatic brain injury
Authors: Chunying Pei, Bo Li, Sijia Wen, Kai Zhao, Shuxian Yu, Tao Li, Yu Li, Yanran Fu, Linlu Tian, Hongwei Xu, Huakun Cheng, Rui Li, Ivet Bahar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe involvement of neuronal autophagy in traumatic brain injury (TBI) remains elusive. Previous investigations, as far as our knowledge extends, have modulated autophagy either through systemic administration of autophagy inhibitors/inducers or by eliminating key regulators of autophagy across all somatic cells, lacking specificity for neurons. Consequently, drawing conclusions from such studies may be muddled by inhibiting autophagy in other cell types, including astrocytes, microglia, and immune cells. To discern the precise role of neuronal autophagy in TBI, we generated tamoxifen-induced, neuron-specific, autophagy-deficient mice by crossing atg5flox/flox mice with map2-CreERT2 mice. We then induced TBI in either control mice or mice with neuron-specific autophagy deficiency. Our findings revealed that the absence of autophagy, specifically in adult neurons, led to exaggerated neurologic-deficit syndromes and more pronounced neuronal loss. Additionally, we demonstrated that neuronal autophagy is orchestrated by immunity-related GTPase family M member 1 (Irgm1) in neural injury, as evidenced by neuron-specific irgm1 knockout mice displaying a significant reduction in neuronal autophagy and heightened neuronal loss compared with control mice. Collectively, our data provide more conclusive evidence that fortifies the neuroprotective role of autophagy in TBI.Keywords: traumatic brain injury, autophagy, neuron, Atg5, immune-related GTPase family M member 1

Significance Statement

Immunity-related GTPase M/immunity-related GTPase family M member 1-mediated neuronal autophagy protects against neuronal loss during traumatic brain injury.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) refers to brain injury due to an external force, varying in severity (1). TBI can manifest in diverse symptoms and have enduring effects. Common causes of TBI include car accidents, falls, sports injuries, and physical assaults (1, 2). Mild TBIs, often referred to as concussions, typically result in a brief loss of consciousness or a period of confusion and usually resolve within a few weeks (1, 3). In contrast, moderate-to-severe TBIs may lead to prolonged cognitive, behavioral, and physical impairments, necessitating ongoing medical care and rehabilitation (1). Salvaging neural cells from injury-induced cell death becomes imperative in treating patients with TBI.

Autophagy, a fundamental cellular process that degrades and recycles cellular components, plays a crucial role in various physiological and pathological processes (4). Neuronal autophagy is pivotal for maintaining cellular homeostasis in neurons and is involved in neural development, synaptic plasticity, and aging (5). In response to TBI, autophagy is up-regulated in various cell types in the brain, including neurons and glial cells. It aids in eliminating damaged organelles, such as mitochondria, and protein aggregates, which can be detrimental to cells (6). Impaired autophagic flux is involved in cell death and inflammation following TBI and significantly contributes to the pathology of experimental TBI (7). Research suggests that enhancing autophagy following neural injury may be a promising therapeutic strategy for promoting recovery and mitigating long-term cognitive and neurological deficits (8, 9). Animal studies demonstrate that activating autophagy with pharmacological agents, such as rapamycin, can improve cognitive function and reduce brain damage following neural injury (10). However, previous studies predominantly manipulated autophagy at a systemic level, lacking specificity for neuron. Consequently, their conclusion may be confounded by inhibiting autophagy in other cell types (e.g. astrocytes, microglia, and immune cells).

This study aims to address this knowledge gap. Autophagy-related 5 (Atg5) is a key protein in phagocytosis of cell membrane elongation in autophagy vesicles and plays an important role in autophagy. We first generated tamoxifen-induced neuron conditional autophagy-deficient mice by crossing atg5flox/flox mice with map2-CreERT2 mice. Additionally, we explored another autophagy-related protein—immunity-related GTPase M (IRGM)/immunity-related GTPase family M member 1 (Irgm1)—to investigate its role in specifically modulating neuronal autophagy post-TBI. IRGM (human)/Irgm1 (mouse) is a key molecule involved in the immune response and cellular defense against intracellular pathogens (11–22). Previous studies have highlighted IRGM/Irgm1's ability to facilitate autophagy in various cell types, such as SY5Y neuronal cell line (11, 13, 16, 23–25). Unlike other autophagy-related genes, irgm1-deficient mice are viable and exhibit normal neural development, likely attributed to its low expression under physiological conditions. Series of studies from our lab have shown that Irgm1 is up-regulated in neurons during ischemic stroke, suggesting a potential protective role in neuronal survival through enhanced autophagy (26). However, whether Irgm1 regulates neuronal autophagy in TBI remained unknown.

In the present study, we induced a mouse model of TBI in either neuron-specific atg5-deficient mice or Irgm1-deficient mice and compared their clinical and pathological outcomes. Our findings suggest that neuronal autophagy does indeed have a neuroprotective function, and IRGM/Irgm1 may represent a potential therapeutic target specifically modulating neuronal autophagy post-TBI.

Materials and methods

Animals

C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old, 22–25 g) were purchased from HFK Bioscience (Beijing, China). C57BL/6 wild type, C57BL/6 background atg5flox/+, irgm1flox/+ mice, map2-CreERT2 mice (cat. no. NM-KI-00037), and mCherry-EGFP-LC3 mice (cat. no. NM-KI-00124) were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. (Shanghai, China). Neuron atg5 conditional knockout (atg5ΔNeuron), irgm1 conditional knockout (Irgm1ΔNeuron) mice and mCherry-EGFP-LC3ΔNeuron knockin were generated via atg5flox/flox mice, irgm1flox/flox mice, or mCherry-EGFP-LC3 mice with map2-CreERT2 mice. The littermates atg5flox/flox (atg5Control), irgm1flox/flox (Irgm1Control), and mCherry-EGFP-LC3Control mice were used as control mice. To induce conditional knockout of Atg5, Irgm1 and knockin of mCherry-EGFP-LC3 in the neurons, tamoxifen (75 mg/kg; 10540, Sigma) was intraperitoneally administered to 8-week-old mice daily for 5 days. Seven days later, the mice were used for subsequent experiments. Mice were housed on a 12 h light/dark cycle at 23 ± 1 °C with free access to food and water. The animal experimental plan was approved by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University. Animal studies were in accordance with the National Experimental Animals Guidelines. Transgenic mice were identified by polymerase chain reaction analysis of genomic DNA in the tail using the following primers: for Atg5: P1:5′-TTCAGTGTACCCTGTGTATTGG-3′, P2:5′-TTCAGTGTACCCTGTGTATTGG-3′; for Irgm1: P1:5′-CATGCCCTACTACCACGAATGTT-3′, P2:5′-CCCCGTGTCAATCTAAATGAGC-3′; for mCherry-EGFP-LC3: P1:5′-TCAGATTCTTTTATAGGGGACACA-3′, P2:5′-TAAAGGCCACTCAATGCTCACTAA-3′, P3:5′-GGTGTTGTCGGGGAAATCATCGTC-3′, P4:5′-AGGAGCCTGCCAAGTAAC-3′; for map2-Cre: P1:5′-GCATCACCCCGACGACTCAG-3′, P2:5′-GTATTGGATAGCCTTCAGGCAC-3′, P3:5′-GGTGTTATAAGCAATCCCCAGAA-3′. PCR results were visualized on 1.2% agarose gel.

Mouse model of TBI

The mouse TBI model was performed by a standardized weight-drop device to induce a mild closed head injury (CHI) (27, 28). Mice (10–12 weeks, >28 g) were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of 4% choral hydrate. We used a 2.0- to 2.5-cm midline longitudinal incision of the scalp to expose the skull. The mice were placed onto the platform of the CHI apparatus. The area to be impacted lies on the left parietal bone between bregma and lambda, 1 mm lateral to the midline. A contusion injury was performed perpendicular to skull by using a CHI apparatus with a rounded steel impactor probe (3 mm diameter) and a steel rod (10 mm diameter, 40 g weight). The falling height of the metal rod was 6 cm which will cause a mild CHI avoiding a result of trauma-induced respiratory depression and death. The rod is being released and free falls onto the mouse skull. The rod was retracted immediately after the impact to avoid unwarranted rebound injury. After that, the wound was closed using sutures and sterilized with iodine. Then, the mice were returned to cages to recover for 24 h with free access to a standard diet. The sham-injured mice underwent identical surgical procedures except for the actual weight drop.

Patient brain samples

Five brain tissue sections of brain trauma patients and four healthy control tissues of patients with cerebral hemorrhage were obtained from Heilongjiang Provincial Hospital (Table 1). All enrolled patients signed informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (ⅰ) adults (18–70 years old) and (ⅱ) patients with brain trauma with clear indications for craniotomy surgery. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (ⅰ) patients with open craniocerebral injuries, (ⅱ) patients with previous central nervous system diseases, and (ⅲ) secondary surgeries. After removing necrotic cores during surgery, the pericontusional tissues (PCTs), not exceeding 3 mm3, are obtained using tissue forceps from the PCT. In the control group, normal cortex tissue was obtained in the patients who received hematoma removal surgery. For these patients, the surgeon had to remove healthy brain tissue in order to get to the hematoma foci.

Neurological severity score

The neurological impairment was evaluated using the neurologic severity score (NSS) at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 days after TBI. Briefly, the mice were assigned 10 tasks to assess motor function, alertness, and physiological behavior (28). One point is awarded for the inability to perform the tasks and no point for succeeding. The points of each task were summed to obtain the NSS. A higher score represents a more severe brain injury (Table 2). All tests were conducted and analyzed by an investigator who was blinded to the experimental design.

Nissl staining

Nissl staining was carried out using paraffin-embedded brain slices prepared from samples harvested at 1 day after Sham or TBI operation. In brief, 5-μm thick brain slices were dewaxed, rehydrated, and stained with the Nissl staining solution (C0117, Beyotime, China) for 10 min. After rinsing two times with distilled water, brain slices were dehydrated in 95% alcohol for 2–3 min and cleared in xylene for 5 min before being sealed with neutral balsam. The pictures were captured under a light microscope. Normal neurons are characterized by a large cell body, with abundant cytoplasm, and with substantially significant levels of Nissl body. However, damaged neurons are characterized by a shrunken cell body, condensed nuclei, reduced or disappearance Nissl body. In each section, two fields (400×) at the impact site were randomly selected. The mean value of two fields was recorded as the section's data. Six sections from three mice were randomly selected to analyze the number of damaged neurons with ImageJ.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA from brain cortex around the injury site and primary neuron samples from each group were extracted using the RNAiso Plus (9109, TaKaRa), as instructed by the manufacturer. Then, cDNA was synthesized from total RNA for each sample using PrimeScript FAST RT Reagent Kit (RR092A, TaKaRa). The Irgm1 primers were 5′-CGCGATCAGACCTCCTCTTG-3′ and 5′-CGCGATCAGACCTCCTCTTG-3′. The GAPDH primers were 5′-CAGAAGACTGTGGATGGCCC-3′ and 5′-ATCCACGACGGACACATTGG-3′). Real-time qPCR was performed in a 25-μL reaction system containing cDNA, forward/reverse primers, and TB Green PreMix (CN830A, TaKaRa). Relative expressions of RNAs were computed by the 2−▵▵Ct method.

TUNEL staining

To better assess neuronal apoptosis in the cortex, immunofluorescent double staining of terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) and neuronal nuclei (NeuN) was conducted to determine colocalization of apoptotic cells and neurons. In brief, frozen sections were immunostained with mouse anti-NeuN antibody (1:100, ab104224, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight, Dnk pAb to Ms IgG (DyLignt488) secondary antibodies (1:200, ab150105, Abcam) for 2 h at room temperature and subsequently subjected to TUNEL staining using One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay kit (C1090, Beyotime), according to the manufacturer's suggested protocol. Finally, the sections were stained the nuclei with DAPI and imaged under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). In each section, two microscopic fields (200×) at the impact site were randomly selected for quantitation of the total count of neurons and the numbers of TUNEL-positive neurons using ImageJ. The mean value of two fields was recorded as the section's data. The percentage of apoptotic neurons was calculated as (TUNEL-positive neurons/total neurons) × 100%. Two blinded investigators performed all procedures.

Immunofluorescence staining

After cardiac perfusion and isolation, the brain tissues were fixed overnight in 4% PFA and dehydrated in 30% sucrose for 48 h. Subsequently, 5 μm serial frozen sections around the injury site were collected and blocked with blocking solution (P0102; Beyotime) for 2 h. Afterward, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against IRGM (1:100, IG9, AbMart), Irgm1 (1:100, IC11; 1:100, p618, AbMart), Atg5 (1:200, ab108327, Abcam), NeuN (1:100, ab104224; 1:100, ab177487, Abcam), GFAP(1:300, sc-6170, Santa cruz), Iba-1(1:500, ab178846, Abcam), cleaved caspase-3 (1:100, 9664S, CST), and mCherry (1:200, M11217, Invitrogen) at 4 ℃ overnight. After that, the slides were thoroughly washed three times with PBS, and then incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After washing three times with PBS, the slides were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. The images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (F1000, Olympus Corporation), and fluorescence data were processed using ImageJ. All tests were conducted and analyzed by an investigator who was blinded to the experimental design. The staining controls are provided in Figs. S1 and S2.

Transmission electron microscope

The cerebral cortex tissues near the lesion site were removed and cut into blocks of ∼3 mm × 3 mm. The samples are immersed in 2% glutaraldehyde for 24 h, and then were postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h, dehydrated in graded ethanol and embedded in embedding medium. After staining with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, ultrathin sections were observed with the transmission electron microscope (H-7700, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Western blotting

The cerebral cortex tissues around the injury site were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktail and then homogenized using ultrasound. Protein concentration was measured by BCA protein assay (P0012, Beyotime). Equal protein amounts were separated by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) or 4–20% SDS–PAGE (M00657, GenScript, USA) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore). The membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk in tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Triton X-100 (TBST) for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against IRGM (1:1,000, IG9, AbMart), Irgm1 (1:1,000, IC11, AbMart), LC3 (1:1,000, 2775S, CST), β-actin (1:25,000, A3854, Sigma) and GAPDH (1:40,000, 10494-1-AP, Proteintech). After washing 3 times for 15 min with TBST, the membrane was incubated in the HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. After further washes with TBST (15 min, 3 times), the blotted protein bands were visualized using the Tanon 5200 Imaging System after incubating with enhanced chemiluminescence and quantified by ImageJ software. The raw gels are provided in Figs. S3–S5.

RNA-seq data analysis

The dataset GSE79441 was obtained from the official Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) website to acquire RPKM data for mRNA transcripts of cerebral injury mice and a normal control group. Duplicated genes and genes with a total expression sum of zero were removed, and differentially expressed genes were identified following voom data transformation, linear model fitting, and empirical Bayesian analysis using the limma package in R (v4.4.1). The differentially expressed genes were identified as differentially expressed (|log2(foldchange)|>1, adj.p.val < 0.05). The ggplot package was utilized to generate volcano plots and box plots for visualizing the results.

Statistics

The statistical significance of the data was analyzed with GraphPad Prism version 9.5. Shapiro–Wilk test and F test (or Brown–Forsythe test) were used to examine the normality of the date and equality of variance, respectively. Data were analyzed by unpaired t test for two independent groups and one-way ANOVA with Dunnett's test for multiple independent groups that were normally distributed and had the same variance. Data were analyzed by Welch's t test for two independent groups that were normally distributed and different variances. Assuming sphericity depending on the situation, two-way ANOVA with Šídák's test was used for comparisons between multiple groups and regions. Data in figures were represented as mean ± SEM. P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001).

Results

The deficiency of autophagy in neurons increases the severity of TBI and contributes to increased neuronal loss

To precisely delineate the role of neuronal autophagy in neural injury, we generated neuron-specific autophagy-deficient mice (atg5ΔNeuron) by crossing atg5flox/flox mice with map2-CreERT2 mice. Since microtubule associate protein 2 (Map2) is almost exclusively expressed by mature neurons, this approach allowed us to selectively deplete atg5, a key factor of autophagy, from adult mature neurons following tamoxifen administration (Fig. 1A). Mice were validated through immunofluorescence staining of Atg5 with neural cell markers (Fig. 1B and C). We also monitored autophagic activity in neurons post-TBI using TEM (Fig. 1D). We showed that atg5 is depleted only in adult neurons following tamoxifen administration (Fig. 1B), and neuronal autophagic activity is substantially reduced in atg5ΔNeuron mice post-TBI (Fig. 1D). Subsequent comparison of neurological symptoms post-TBI, assessed through NSS, revealed that atg5ΔNeuron mice exhibited more severe neurological symptoms and pathological damage compared with control mice (Fig. 2A). In addition, we observed that atg5ΔNeuron mice also suffer from greater neuronal damage as measured by Nissl staining, and neuronal apoptosis as indicated by TUNEL and Caspase-3 staining (Fig. 2B–D). These data collectively underscore the significance of neuronal autophagy in promoting neuron survival in the context of TBI.

The expression of IRGM/Irgm1 in neuron is up-regulated post-TBI

To further investigate the role of autophagy-related genes in neuroprotection in TBI, we acquired transcriptome sequencing data from the GEO database for the TBI mouse model sample and conducted an in-depth analysis. We used the limma package to perform differential gene expression analysis on the data and identified 574 up-regulated genes and 52 down-regulated genes (Tables S1 and S2). Among the differentially expressed genes, we found that Irgm1 expression was up-regulated in the cortical injury tissue of the TBI mice, compared with the control group (Fig. 3A and B). In a mouse model of TBI, we evaluated the expression of Irgm1 using RT-qPCR and western blotting. Our finding demonstrated that Irgm1 was significantly up-regulated in the PCT, reaching peak levels at 24 h post-TBI (Fig. 3C and D). Neurons were identified as the primary cell type expressing Irgm1 (Fig. 3E and F). Meanwhile, the protein levels of LC3-II, indicative of autophagosome numbers, were obviously increased after TBI (Fig. 3D). They exhibited a similar expression tendency with the Irgm1 after TBI. Furthermore, we observed that human IRGM and LC3-II protein levels were also up-regulated in the injured brain tissue compared with those from the control (Fig. 4A–D). Immunofluorescence assays revealed that IRGM mainly expressed in neurons post-TBI (Fig. 4B and C).

Knocking out Irgm1 specifically from adult neuron reduces neuronal autophagy and exaggerates TBI

To investigate the role of neuron Irgm1 in TBI, we generated neuron-specific irgm1 knockout mice (irgm1ΔNeuron) using similar approach mentioned above (Fig. 5A). We validated irgm1ΔNeuron mice using immunofluorescence staining post-TBI. Following tamoxifen administration, Irgm1 expression is significantly decreased in neurons of irgm1ΔNeuron mice (Fig. 5B). Monitoring neuronal autophagic activities through transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and western blotting revealed a substantial reduction of autophagosome in irgm1ΔNeuron mice as compared with the control mice after TBI (Fig. 5C and D). Additionally, we employed neuron-specific autophagy reporter mice—LC3EGFP-mCherry, where LC3 expressed by neuron was specifically tagged with EGFP and mCherry. In lysosome–autophagosome with lower pH, EGFP was quenched, but mCherry remained stable. Hence, neurons with higher autophagic activities may display an increased mCherry signal. We found that neurons of irgm1ΔNeuron mice show decreased mCherry signal as measured by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5E), further suggesting that neuronal autophagic activities is reduced in irgm1ΔNeuron mice. In line with our observations in atg5ΔNeuron mice, the decrease of neuronal autophagy correlated with more severe neurological symptoms (Fig. 6A) and increased neuronal damage and neuronal apoptosis (Fig. 6B–D). These data suggest that Irgm1-mediated neuronal autophagy plays a crucial role in promoting neuron survive post-TBI.

Discussion

In this investigation, our primary objective was to delve into the previously understudied realm of neuronal autophagy in TBI, a facet often hindered by the absence of specialized tools. Employing tamoxifen-induced neuron-specific autophagy-deficient mice, we successfully unveiled the pivotal role of neuronal autophagy in safeguarding neuron survival post-TBI. Additionally, we identified Irgm1 as a mediator of neuronal autophagy, providing robust evidence that solidifies the neuroprotective stance of autophagy in neural injury. This sheds light on the intricate molecular mechanisms governing neuronal autophagy.

Autophagy, a crucial cellular process ensuring cellular homeostasis, is implicated in a myriad of physiological and pathological conditions. Governed by a complex network of proteins and signaling pathways, it responds to diverse stimuli such as nutrient deprivation and oxidative stress. Dysregulation of autophagy has been linked to various diseases, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and metabolic disorders (29–32). Mice deficient in key regulators of autophagy, such as atg5 and atg7, are born developmentally normal but die within 1 day of birth due to suckling failure. Interestingly, neuronal-specific expression of ATG5 can rescue atg5-null mice from death (33). Previous studies showed that autophagy can either be protective or detrimental to neuron in TBI. On the one hand, the administration of autophagy inducer, rapamycin, improved the neurobehavioral function and neuronal survival (9, 10), while impaired autophagy flux could be involved in increased neuronal loss (7). In addition, many neuroprotective drugs attenuate TBI-induced injury in accompany with enhancement of neuronal autophagy (34–38). On the other hand, dysregulated autophagy has also been implicated in the pathophysiology of TBI. For example, excessive autophagy has been observed in animal models of TBI and may contribute to neuronal death and inflammation (39–44). Despite the dual role of autophagy in TBI—being both protective and detrimental—our study aimed to address the uncertainty surrounding the precise role of neuronal autophagy. Notably, our innovative approach using atg5flox/flox and map2-CreERT2 mice, where tamoxifen administration selectively depleted atg5 in adult mature neurons (45), provided definitive evidence supporting the neuroprotective function of neuronal autophagy in TBI. This approach may pave the way for elucidating the roles of neuronal autophagy in other diseases where its involvement is implicated.

Irgm1, a member of the IRG family, plays a crucial role in host defense against intracellular pathogens (16, 20–22). Irgm1 is expressed in various tissues, including the spleen, liver, and lung, and is up-regulated during various diseases, including sepsis, multiple sclerosis, melanoma, stroke, and atherosclerosis (26, 46–48). Studies have shown that Irgm1 is involved in multiple cellular processes, including autophagy and mitochondrial homeostasis. In neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease, Irgm1 has been shown to play a role in mitochondrial homeostasis and autophagy, which are processes that are critical for neuronal survival and function (23). Our previous research indicated its up-regulation in neurons during ischemic stroke, suggesting a potential protective role in neuronal survival through enhanced autophagy (26). Expanding on this, our current study shows that the neuronal expression of IRGM/Irgm1 significantly increased during the early stages after TBI in human and mice. Simultaneously, the protein levels of LC3-II and the number of autophagosomes in neurons increased in the injured brain tissue after TBI. We conclude that there may be a functional correlation between the concomitant increases, which may be that the significantly increase of Irgm1, while gradually decreased, contributes to activation of autophagy at the early stages in TBI.

To find a link between increased Irgm1 in neurons and activation of autophagy in TBI, we knocked out irgm1 specifically from adult mature neurons. That results in a significant reduction of neuronal autophagy, heightened neuron apoptosis and loss, implying the neuroprotective nature of Irgm1-mediated neuronal autophagy in neural injury. Further exploration is warranted to unveil the molecular intricacies of Irgm1 in regulating autophagy during TBI.

Atg5 and Irgm1, as two key molecules in the process of autophagy, have synergistic in the formation and maturation of autophagosomes. Atg5 is involved in the membrane extension of autophagosomes through the formation of complexes, while Irgm1 may accelerate the degradation process of autophagosomes by promoting the fusion of lysosomes and the connection between autophagosomes and lysosomes. This synergistic effect helps cells to quickly and efficiently remove damaged or excess organelles and pathogens and maintain cell homeostasis.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive reassessment of the role of neuronal autophagy in TBI. The findings underscore the importance of Irgm1-mediated autophagy in neurons, indicating a potential avenue for reducing TBI-induced neuronal loss. Future investigations will delve into whether these insights extend to other forms of neural injury, such as ischemia and neurodegeneration.
