Hospital‐Wide Quality Assessment Using All‐Condition Versus Summed‐Condition Risk Adjustment
Authors: Valérie Gopinath, Kelsey Chalmers, Vikas Saini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Public reporting of hospital performance has been used for decades to provide quality information to patients, governments, and industry. However, variations of patient mix, individual patient severity, and varying hospital case mix represent challenges to reliable and valid results [1]. This study compares two approaches, CMS Care Compare and the Lown Institute Hospitals Index of Social Responsibility, on reporting a hospital‐wide mortality measure as a signal of overall hospital quality.

In recent years, patient level risk adjustment and hierarchical modeling with hospital level random intercept have become standard when CMS and others report hospital performance. To circumvent needing to adjust for hospital case‐mix, CMS reports on separate condition cohorts (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart attack, heart failure, stroke, pneumonia) and two surgical cohorts (coronary artery bypass grafting, complications after surgery) [2]. However this means all patients are not accounted for in a hospital's publicly reported performance.

In order to allow policymakers to monitor a hospital‐wide endpoint [3], CMS has recently begun reporting a 30‐day mortality measure that includes all admitted patients. This method splits admissions into 15 cohorts of specific conditions and procedures and estimates risk adjustment models on each. The results of the separate models are then combined using a weighted sum into a hospital‐wide 30‐day risk standardized mortality rate (RSMR). CMS's rationale for this approach is the clinical distinctness of these cohorts requires risk‐adjustment models to be estimated separately. The approach thus seeks to adjust for patient mix as well as differences in the case mix of services and procedures in hospitals [4].

The Lown Institute also publishes a hospital‐wide metric of 30‐day mortality. In contrast to the CMS approach, the RSMR is based on a single hierarchical risk adjustment model using nearly all admissions at the hospital [5]. Lown's rationale for using a single risk adjustment model has been, like CMS, to try to provide greater insight into hospital‐wide treatment effects [5].

Our goal in this study was to compare hospitals' estimated performance, RSMR, from these two hospital‐wide approaches. We compared the two approaches using RSMR correlation and decile rank reclassification rate (i.e., what percentage of hospitals grouped by RSMR decile using one approach are assigned to a different decile using the other approach). We also explored whether certain hospital characteristics are associated with larger shifts in RSMR between the two approaches, which might indicate systematic differences between methods driven by characteristics not otherwise discernible.

The Lown Institute and CMS also use different patient covariate risk adjustment methods. The Lown Institute's approach selects from a greater number of candidate patient‐level covariates than CMS, using the Risk Stratification Index (RSI), which has been shown to outperform CMS's Hierarchical Condition Categories (HCC) in single and multilevel risk adjustment models on multiple cohorts [6, 7]. RSI was first introduced in 2010 at the patient level using ICD‐9 diagnosis and procedure codes. Adapted to a multi‐level application at the hospital and patient levels in 2021 and to using ICD‐10 in 2022, RSI derives patient‐level risk scores from individual ICD‐10 diagnosis and procedure codes [8, 9, 10, 11]. RSI has approximately 4400 covariates reduced from the ICD‐10 diagnosis codes for inclusion in modeling [10]. HCC, developed in 2004, originally mapped ICD‐9 codes to 189 potential covariates for inclusion in modeling and is used by CMS and insurance companies to assign risk scores to patients in order to estimate health costs [12, 13].

In order to fairly compare the two cohort approaches (the Lown Institute's ‘all‐condition’ approach and CMS's ‘summed‐condition’ approach), we used both RSI and HCC for patient covariate risk adjustment. Therefore, we compare four hospital RSMR estimates: all‐condition using RSI, all‐condition using HCC, summed‐condition using RSI and summed‐condition using HCC.

Methods

Data and Study Sample

We used a 100% sample of Medicare inpatient Fee‐For‐Service (FFS) claims from calendar years 2019 to 2021. Index admissions were selected based on CMS's methodology [4]. Patients were between 65 to 94 years of age, enrolled in Medicare FFS part A for 12 months prior to the admission date, not transferred from another acute care facility, not admitted for psychiatric conditions or rehabilitation, and not having a diagnosis of metastatic cancer or other terminal conditions (Content S1). We included a 1‐year lookback for covariates including all inpatient, outpatient, and carrier claims from the index admission. Our sample included 3638 hospitals classified in CMS Care Compare as Acute Care or Critical Access Hospitals in 50 US states and DC. We excluded specialty hospitals since their case‐mix is very different from general hospitals. We labeled hospitals as specialty by examining claim volumes of admission type codes, DRGs and Major Diagnostic Codes, and identifying those with more than 45% admissions for orthopedic or cardiac, more than 80% surgical procedures, or more than 80% of surgeries being elective if they had at least 45% surgical procedures.

Our primary analysis included hospitals with at least 150 admissions. Since hospitals with small volumes may have less consistent risk‐adjusted results than larger hospitals [14], we conducted a secondary analysis using only large hospitals (those with at least 4000 admissions during the analysis timeframe; N = 1355). We used the secondary analysis for reference and to understand sensitivity of results to hospital volume. We used 2019 data for model development and applied those risk models to 2020 for validation. In addition, we used 2020 data to develop a second set of models applied to 2021 for validation. The all‐hospital data had 6,159,499 admissions in 2019, 4,888,612 admissions in 2020, and 4,681,540 admissions in 2021.

Statistical procedures were performed using SAS, version 9.04, R, version 4.2.2, and Python, version 3.10.12. The WCG Institutional Review Board deemed this study exempt from IRB review as claims data was de‐identified (Sponsor Protocol No.: WO6484<include protocol ID in non‐anonymized submission>).

Patient Condition/Procedure Cohorts

We followed CMS methodology to classify admissions into 15 cohorts by procedure and condition using AHRQ clinical classification software (CCS) categorizations of ICD‐10 diagnosis and procedure codes [4]. Six of the cohorts included patients undergoing surgeries in the hospital (“surgical” cohorts) and the remaining nine were for medical care only admissions (“non‐surgical” cohorts) (Content lists the cohorts).

Some hospitals had zero or very few admissions within some of the 15 cohorts. We applied a minimum volume threshold based on the observed death rate within each cohort to ensure sufficient sample size in the summed‐condition risk adjustment models. In brief, cohorts with low observed event rates required a larger hospital volume threshold than cohorts with high observed event rates (Content S3). Application of this volume threshold excluded approximately < 0.2% of claims, resulting in 15.7 million index admissions in the all‐hospital cohort and 12.8 million index admissions in the large‐hospital cohort. We used the resulting cohorts to measure hospital performance using both all‐ and summed‐condition methods.

Approaches to Measure Hospital Performance

We estimated hospital‐wide 30‐day RSMR using four approaches: all‐condition using RSI patient covariates, all‐condition using HCC patient covariates, summed‐condition using RSI, and summed‐condition using HCC. The mortality outcome was death within 30 days from the hospital admission date. In all cases, the outcome was risk‐adjusted using a hierarchical logistic regression model with a hospital random intercept.

All‐Condition Approach

In the all‐condition approach, we estimated a single multilevel model across all index admissions. This provided outcome probability estimates for each admission at two levels: (1) expected mortality based on patient risk alone and (2) predicted mortality based on patient risk including a random intercept for the hospital effect. We calibrated models so estimated mortality was more closely aligned with observed mortality. This was done across binned risk predictions using piecewise regression. Calibrated predictions more closely match observed mortality in each bin. This primarily adjusted predictions at the highest level of risk to more closely match observed mortality (Content S4).

For each hospital, we summed predicted and expected probabilities after calibration across patients to estimate both the total predicted (P) and expected (E) patient deaths. We constructed the P/E ratio representing mortality risk attributable to the hospital, where 1 is the average across all hospitals.

Summed‐Condition Approach

In the summed‐condition approach, we estimated separate multilevel models for each of the 15 patient condition cohorts. For each hospital, we calculated a P/E ratio for each of its included patient cohorts as in the step described above for the all‐condition approach. We calculated a hospital's combined P/E ratio by summing the log of the P/E ratios from each cohort, weighted by the proportion of the hospital's total admission volume from that cohort. This was according to CMS methodology and referred to as the hospital‐wide standardized mortality ratio [4]. If the hospital had no admissions in a cohort (either because they had no admissions of this type or because the volume sufficiency criterion was not met), then the hospital's weight for this cohort was zero and did not contribute to the combined P/E ratio.

For both all‐ and summed‐condition approaches, we calculated a hospital's 30‐day RSMR as the hospital's P/E ratio multiplied by the observed mean event rate across all admissions.

Patient Covariates Used in Risk Adjustment

Patient covariates included in all risk adjustment models were age, gender, Medicaid dual eligibility status and end stage renal disease (ESRD) indicator. We also included indicators for principal diagnosis CCS category (mapped from the ICD‐10 diagnosis code) in each model. For summed‐condition cohorts, we included the principal diagnosis for that admission. We implemented a grouping of low frequency and low mortality CCS categories within each patient cohort according to CMS methodology [4, 15]. For all‐condition models, we included principal diagnosis CCS categories using variable selection (Content S5).

In addition to principal diagnosis, we included patient covariates using either RSI or HCC. Both approaches derived patient covariates from ICD‐10 diagnosis codes present on admission, as well as from a one‐year lookback of inpatient, outpatient, and carrier claims.

For HCC, we created patient indicators by mapping ICD‐10 diagnosis codes to HCC. We performed this step across all index admissions for the all‐condition approach and among index admissions within each cohort in the summed‐condition approach. We included the HCC indicators CMS specified in their documentation for summed‐condition models and used variable selection among all HCC indicators for the all‐condition model [15].

For RSI, we mapped patients' ICD‐10 diagnosis codes to one of 4426 diagnosis groups using a method provided by the Health Data Analytics Institute (the algorithm is described previously) [10, 11] and then created indicators for each group. These indicators formed the full set of candidate covariates for inclusion in risk adjustment. We used variable selection among the candidates for both all‐ and summed‐condition risk adjustment models. Similar to the HCC approach above, we performed this step across all index admissions for the all‐condition approach and among index admissions within each cohort in the summed‐condition approach.

Approach Comparisons

We compared hospitals' performance between methods using RMSR correlations and rank reclassification. To measure rank reclassification rate, we calculated the percentage of hospitals whose RSMR rank changed decile between methods (any decile, and more than one decile). We compared model fit under the different approaches (all‐ vs. summed‐condition, and RSI versus HCC) using both the receiver operating characteristic curve C‐statistic to assess discrimination as well as Akaike Information Criteria and Bayesian Information Criteria (AIC and BIC) [16, 17]. We also assessed overfitting of the RSI models using precision recall area under the curve (PR‐AUC) and the 10‐fold cross validation receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC‐AUC).

To explore associations of hospital characteristics with differences in estimated performance between all‐ and summed‐condition approaches, we calculated the difference in RSMRs between the two approaches using RSI. We explored the impact of hospital characteristics on this difference using random forest regression and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values [18]. The outcome variable in the random forest regression was RSMR (all‐condition) minus RSMR (summed‐condition). The hospital characteristics included in the random forest regression were rural area status; proportion of hospital patient days that were Medicaid dual‐eligible; proportion of admissions in selected non‐surgical cohorts: (1) pulmonary; (2) cardiac; (3) gastrointestinal; (4) infectious disease; and (5) orthopedics; proportion of admissions that were non‐surgical; and proportion of admissions that were elective surgery. We also included total admissions volume and breadth. We defined breadth as the number of cohorts in which the hospital had index admissions (Content S6). We selected these features for inclusion in the random forest regression from a larger set of hospital characteristics after reviewing feature pairwise correlations and feature SHAP values having higher importance. Feature correlations from the final selected characteristics are in Content S7.

Results

There were 3638 hospitals in our principal analysis cohort. The 2019 development dataset used to estimate risk adjustment models included 6,159,499 admissions (77.9 median age; 54.4% female) with a 7.6% observed 30‐day mortality rate (Tables S1 and S2). The 30‐day observed mortality rate in the patient condition cohorts ranged from 1.8% for surgical orthopedics to 15.1% for non‐surgical cancer.

Table 1 shows that the correlation between all‐ and summed‐condition hospital RSMR using either RSI or HCC was high (0.89 using RSI and 0.87 using HCC). Using RSI produced a lower RSMR decile rank reclassification rate between all‐ and summed‐condition methods (54.3% hospitals shifted deciles; 14.1% shifted > 1 decile) compared to HCC (any decile 58.4%; > 1 decile 17.9%). RSMRs produced with RSI versus HCC using an all‐condition approach were more correlated (0.87) and had slightly lower reclassification rates (29.7% shifted > 1 decile) than RSMRs using summed‐condition (0.85 correlation and 30.3% > 1 decile reclassification). Of all method comparisons, hospitals were most highly correlated and least likely to move decile rankings between the all‐ and summed‐condition approaches when using RSI (14.1% of hospitals shifted > 1 decile). Figure 1 shows hospital RSMR rank comparisons between all‐ and summed‐condition methods and between patient covariate methods, with the highest correlation, the closest comparison being the comparison of all‐ to summed‐condition using RSI. We found a similar pattern in method comparisons when using 2020 data for model development (Table S2, Figure S1).

We examined the SHAP values of the hospital characteristics used as predictors in a random forest regression where the outcome was the difference between all‐ and summed‐condition RSMRs using RSI (Figure 2). Low share of non‐surgical pulmonary admissions had a negative impact on the predicted difference, meaning these hospitals performed better using all‐condition compared to summed‐condition. Model Performance

Model discrimination was slightly higher using RSI compared to HCC for the all‐condition approach (0.88 C‐statistic using RSI versus 0.87 for HCC; Table 2). For the summed‐condition approach, RSI had the same or better discrimination than HCC on all 15 cohorts except for surgical cancer (0.88 C‐statistic for RSI vs. 0.90 for HCC). Using RSI, C‐statistics for the 15 condition cohorts ranged from 0.84 (non‐surgical cancer and renal) to 0.95 (neurosurgery), while using HCC the cohort models' performances ranged from 0.80 (non‐surgical cancer and renal) to 0.93 (neurosurgery).

In addition to assessing model performance on 2019 development data, we applied the estimated models prospectively to 2020. For the all‐condition model, C‐statistics for RSI were 0.87 and for HCC were 0.87 (Content S8). For the 15 cohorts, prospective C‐statistics using RSI ranged from 0.82 to 0.92; using HCC, they ranged from 0.79 to 0.93. Validation results for the 2020 models applied prospectively to 2021 data were similar (Content S8). Risk decile plots for the 15 cohorts are in Content S9.

We assessed potential overfitting using RSI. The mean ROC‐AUC for all‐condition using RSI developed using 2019 data remained at 0.88 under 10‐fold cross validation and decreased minimally to 0.87 when validated prospectively with 2020 data. Similarly, the mean ROC‐AUC for the all‐condition RSI model developed using 2020 data remained at 0.87 under 10‐fold cross validation and decreased minimally to 0.86 when validated prospectively with 2021. In addition, the precision‐recall curves for both of these model developments and validations showed almost no degradation of the PR‐AUC (Contents S10 and S11). RSI had a better fit (lower AIC and BIC values) than HCC for the summed‐condition approach in non‐surgical cohorts (Tables S3 and S4).

Large Volume Hospital Results

In our secondary analysis of the study cohort restricted to hospitals with higher volumes (hospitals with at least 4000 admissions, N = 1355), the correlation between all‐ and summed‐condition RSMR using either RSI or HCC was higher and the rank reclassification rate was lower compared to the primary analysis cohort (Table 1). For large hospitals, the correlation between all‐ and summed‐condition RSMR using either RSI or HCC was very high (0.97 correlation using RSI and 0.95 using HCC), while the correlation between RSI and HCC RSMR using either all‐ or summed‐condition was lower in comparison, but nearly identical (0.79 for all‐condition and 0.81 for summed‐condition). The RSMR rank reclassification rate also followed this pattern. All‐ versus summed‐condition comparisons had a lower rank reclassification rate (38.8% reclassification to any decile using RSI vs. 49.7% using HCC) than comparisons between RSI and HCC (72.2% reclassification with all‐ vs. 73.2% with summed‐condition). Of all method comparisons, large volume hospitals were least likely to move decile between all‐ and summed‐condition when using RSI (only 1.9% of hospitals shifted more than one decile). Figure S2 shows hospital RSMR rank comparisons between all‐ and summed‐condition methods and between patient covariate methods, with the highest correlation, the closest comparison result being the comparison of all‐ to summed‐condition using RSI. We found the pattern of model C‐statistic results (all‐ vs. summed‐condition and RSI versus HCC) in the large hospital cohort to be similar to that in the all‐hospitals cohort with higher C‐statistics in some cases (Table S5). We found consistent results using the models developed separately on 2020 data (Table S2, Figure S3).

In both study hospital cohorts, rank reclassification rates were lower when comparing all‐ versus summed‐condition using either RSI or HCC than when comparing RSI versus HCC using either the all‐ or summed‐condition approach. Additional results on risk adjustment model sensitivity to cohort (large versus all hospitals) are in Table S6.

Discussion

Fair public hospital performance assessments require robust adjustment methods for both patient and hospital factors that potentially impact mortality‐risk but are outside the control of a hospital. Such reports can be consequential: public hospital reporting has been linked to both investments in clinical quality improvements and the public reputation of hospitals [19, 20]. We investigated two approaches for grouping patient cohorts in risk adjustment models to report hospital‐wide performance and found that results were generally similar. At the same time, we compared RSI and HCC patient covariate methods resulting in four different methods of calculating RSMR: all‐condition RSI, all‐condition HCC, summed‐condition RSI, and summed‐condition HCC. The correlation between all‐ and summed‐condition hospital RSMRs was high, and rank reclassification low, using either RSI or HCC. The differences were lower still when confined to a large hospital cohort where case‐mix is presumably less variable. Further, the difference between hospitals' RSMR performance between all‐ versus summed condition methods was generally smaller when using RSI than when using HCC.

The rationale for separately estimating patient risk within specific conditions and procedures is compelling, as adjusting for hospital variation in case‐mix would seem warranted. Since one might expect inherently less heterogeneity of patient comorbidities within a cohort defined by a single condition or procedure than in the total population of all patients, confining analysis to narrowly defined cohorts and then using weighted sums to combine them, as CMS does, would seem a promising way to achieve that objective. Our results to date, however, fail to demonstrate that such an approach produces significantly different results than including all admissions in one model.

An all‐condition approach achieved results similar to CMS's summed‐condition approach whether using HCC or RSI, although the results were closer when using RSI. RSI thus continues to be a reasonable, well‐tested covariate selection method. This view is strengthened by the fact that, when looking at both model discrimination and model fit, the RSI method of risk adjustment outperforms HCC in the all‐condition approach and within nearly every condition and procedure‐specific model for the summed‐condition approach. This was true for both the principal analysis using the cohort of all hospitals as well as the secondary cohort restricted to large volume hospitals. These findings are consistent with previous reports [6, 7]. For the all‐hospital cohort, as neither method was significantly superior, we cannot advocate that one method replace the other in such settings. When small hospitals were removed from the analysis, the superiority of RSI compared to HCC results was clearer: the all‐ versus summed‐condition comparisons had higher correlation values and lower rank reclassification rates. This is perhaps unsurprising since the inclusion of smaller hospitals likely injects more noise and greater variability of case‐mix, while their removal might be expected to reduce case‐mix variability at the hospital level, and increase sensitivity to variability at the patient level, where RSI might be expected to improve model discrimination and stability. Thus, hospital‐wide performance evaluations using RSI may be preferable when examining large volume hospital cohorts.

Concern for confounding by case‐mix is the likely reason that most hospital ranking systems such as CMS Compare and US News currently report their results on a specialty‐by‐specialty basis. The argument for CMS to use the summed condition tactic is based on existing literature suggesting hospital‐wide metrics are susceptible to varying case mix. Shahian et al. [1] proposed limiting measurement to selected conditions as an alternative to the methodological challenges posed by a hospital‐wide measure. Mackenzie et al. [21] proposed disaggregation of mortality measurement by condition or service line to avoid the problem of imprecision from a hospital‐wide metric. However, as noted in CMS's methodology brief for a hospital‐wide metric, there would be value in having a hospital‐wide measure that is not cohort specific because those existing measures do not allow for measurement of a hospital's broader performance [4].

The current results do not show the summed condition approach being distinctly superior to the all‐conditions approach, implying that summing conditions is not solving a hospital level case mix problem that the all‐conditions approach leaves unsolved, particularly in large hospitals.

Our study adds to the evidence that using RSI to select covariates yields improved results, at least for larger volume hospitals. We have previously shown that including social determinants of health in the risk adjustment models using RSI did not improve model performance, suggesting that the higher risk of these determinants on mortality may already be reflected in the comorbidities coded in claims data and which are allowed into the model as covariates [22]. Speculatively, our findings in this study further support the notion that RSI's greater granularity (i.e., the number of potential covariates available for inclusion in the model, in this case over 4000) may function as a form of case‐mix adjustment pari passu at the patient level. Understandable concerns about overfitting when using the larger number of covariates with RSI are not supported by our analysis.

We did find that some of the result differences between summed‐ and all‐condition approaches using RSI were predicted by certain hospital characteristics. Hospitals with a high proportion of admissions in non‐surgical pulmonary cases, low volume, or rural area status were associated with better performance using the summed‐condition approach. The greater difference in results when small hospitals are in the cohort, coupled with the fact that lower volume, rural, and a higher proportion of nonsurgical pulmonary cases were associated with those differences is further evidence that smaller, often rural, hospitals represent a challenge for assessing quality fairly. For these hospitals in particular, novel methods and metrics of quality may be needed.

Our results support the view that using RSI for patient level risk adjustment and an all‐conditions approach to hospital‐wide performance assessment is, at the least, not inferior to other current approaches. Our findings promote the value of further research to ensure these publicly reported measures are refined as needed to be as fair as possible when it comes to ranking hospitals, recognizing that there may always be trade‐offs and perhaps differing perspectives on what is fair in hospital quality comparisons.

Limitations

The use of claims data to define condition and procedure cohorts may prevent adequate case mix adjustment at the hospital level. In theory, better patient classification into condition cohorts and less unmeasured confounding might be mitigated with access to clinical records. This hypothesis may soon be testable since CMS is introducing a model for hospital‐wide mortality with derived variables from electronic health records [3].

CMS developed the 15 patient cohorts used in their summed‐condition approach with input from clinicians and stakeholders. Future work could address whether these groupings are the most optimal for case‐mix adjustment, and whether hospitals could be classified into peer groups for more fair comparisons. At the same time, the all‐condition approach considers all admissions in a single cohort relying fully on risk adjustment to address clinical distinctness among its patients. An alternative all‐condition approach which allows interaction effects between the 15 cohorts and patient covariates could be considered but remains unexplored given the compute time required in such models.

Conclusion

This study shows a single all‐condition risk‐adjustment model for 30‐day mortality using RSI is a valid method, achieving similar hospital‐wide performance assessment results compared to the summed‐condition approach used by CMS. The better model discrimination and fit, particularly for large‐volume hospitals, suggest that performance results with RSI are likely to be more stable and reproducible. Further investigation into those hospital characteristics most sensitive to choice of method could lead to future refinements of either the all‐ or summed‐condition method to ensure fairness in hospital performance reporting.
