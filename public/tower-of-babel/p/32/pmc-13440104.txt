Imaging endpoints in inflammatory bowel disease: current evidence and future directions for ultrasound and MRI
Authors: Ilaria Faggiani, Silvio Danese, Laurent Peyrin Biroulet, Vipul Jairath, David T. Rubin, Mariangela Allocca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImaging has become a central and rapidly evolving domain in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) because it captures disease manifestations that extend beyond the mucosa and, therefore, beyond what can be visualized by endoscopy, including transmural inflammation, extraintestinal complications and small-bowel involvement that are not adequately assessed by conventional approaches. Main bodyIn this narrative review, we synthesize the latest evidence on non-invasive cross-sectional imaging—intestinal ultrasound (IUS) and magnetic resonance imaging/enterography (MRI/MRE)—and outline a pragmatic framework for integrating these modalities into clinical trials. The timing of this review reflects the recent publication of two major consensus statements that specifically address how IUS and MRI can be standardized and operationalized as trial endpoints. We first summarize recommended technical standards for acquisition and reporting. We then outline trial-ready definitions of response and remission for clinical trials using validated scores and discuss the emerging but still variably defined construct of transmural healing, understood as the resolution of inflammation across the full thickness of the bowel wall and increasingly associated with improved long-term outcomes. Building on these components, we propose a practical pathway to incorporate imaging from screening, through on-treatment monitoring at predefined windows to adjudicate response/remission, and, where appropriate, to evaluate transmural healing. We emphasize central reading, harmonized timing, and standardized data repositories to minimize bias.ConclusionEven though meaningful steps have been taken to integrate cross-sectional imaging, it remains essential to evaluate its use pragmatically in both trials and routine care. Background

Imaging plays a central role in the diagnosis and longitudinal assessment of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) [1, 2]. These are chronic, immune-mediated, relapsing–remitting disorders of the gastrointestinal tract characterized by progressive structural damage and a variable clinical course. In CD, inflammation may involve any segment from mouth to perianal region and typically extends transmurally, affecting the full thickness of the bowel wall and surrounding tissues [1]. UC is classically characterized by continuous inflammation starting in the rectum and limited to the colon. Although traditionally considered a mucosa-restricted disease, emerging evidence suggests that inflammatory activity may extend to deeper bowel wall layers, supporting the concept that UC may, at least in part, behave as a transmural disease rather than a purely mucosal condition [2, 3].

Historically, disease assessment and therapeutic targets in IBD have relied on a combination of clinical indices, biomarkers, and endoscopy. Colonoscopy has long been regarded as the reference standard for mucosal assessment, and mucosal healing remains a key long-term therapeutic target [4]. However, these conventional approaches have important limitations. Clinical symptoms correlate only modestly with objective inflammation, serum biomarkers lack specificity, and endoscopy is restricted to the mucosal surface and to segments accessible to the scope, thus failing to capture transmural disease activity, small-bowel involvement, and extraintestinal complications. Moreover, colonoscopy is invasive, burdensome for patients, and not well suited for repeated monitoring. These limitations have become increasingly relevant in the context of modern treatment strategies, which aim for deeper and more sustained disease control [5].

In this evolving landscape, cross-sectional imaging has emerged as a central and rapidly expanding domain in IBD. Techniques such as intestinal ultrasound (IUS) and magnetic resonance imaging/enterography (MRI/MRE) provide direct visualization of the entire bowel wall and surrounding structures, allowing comprehensive assessment of disease extent and severity. These modalities enable detection of transmural inflammation, evaluation of small-bowel segments beyond the reach of endoscopy, and identification of complications such as strictures, fistulas, and abscesses [6, 7]. Importantly, they also allow dynamic and repeated assessment of disease activity over time, making them particularly suitable for tight monitoring strategies and treat-to-target approaches, while reducing the burden associated with repeated colonoscopy.

IUS is a non-invasive, radiation-free technique that does not require bowel preparation or contrast administration, is easily repeatable for longitudinal monitoring, and is generally cost-effective; for these reasons, it is widely valued by clinicians and well accepted by patients. Its bedside availability and immediate results further enhance its role as a practical tool for real-time clinical decision-making and frequent monitoring. MRI is also non-invasive and typically performed with oral and intravenous contrast, providing a comprehensive assessment of deep bowel loops and proximal small-bowel involvement, and is particularly relevant in CD. Compared with IUS, MRI is especially useful when bowel segments are not easily visualized by IUS and for a more complete evaluation of disease extent and complications beyond the reach of endoscopy.

Over the past two decades, the therapeutic landscape of IBD has expanded considerably with the introduction of biologics and small molecules, leading to improved outcomes and quality of life. In parallel, treatment targets have evolved from symptom control toward objective measures of inflammation and, increasingly, toward deeper endpoints such as endoscopic healing [4]. More recently, attention has shifted toward transmural healing (TH) as an emerging treatment goal. TH refers to the resolution of inflammatory activity across the full thickness of the bowel wall, ideally accompanied by normalization of perienteric inflammatory changes. This concept is particularly relevant in CD, where disease is inherently transmural, but is also increasingly being explored in UC, where deeper bowel wall involvement may contribute to disease persistence and relapse [3]. Achieving transmural healing is associated with improved long-term outcomes, including reduced risk of complications, hospitalizations, and surgery, and may represent a more comprehensive therapeutic target compared with mucosal healing alone [8, 9]. Importantly, TH cannot be adequately assessed by endoscopy alone and requires cross-sectional imaging for evaluation.

While randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have historically focused on clinical, endoscopic, and in UC, histological endpoints, imaging endpoints (IUS and MRI) have only recently begun to be incorporated into both clinical trials and real-world studies [6, 7, 10]. This shift reflects both the limitations of traditional endpoints and the growing recognition of the added value of imaging in capturing disease biology more comprehensively, as well as their potential role as repeatable, non-invasive tools for longitudinal monitoring and treatment optimization.

In this narrative review, we outline the rationale for incorporating IUS and MRI as efficacy endpoints in both RCTs and real-world research, summarize current consensus on technique, scoring systems, and timing of assessment, and highlight existing evidence gaps along with practical steps to standardize methodology and facilitate regulatory and clinical implementation.

Methods

We conducted a targeted literature search in Pubmed, Embase, and Scopus to identify relevant studies on imaging endpoints in Inflammatory Bowel Disease. Search terms included combinations of ‘IBD trials,’ ' trials endpoint,’ ' transmural healing,’ ' trans-mural response,’ and ' transmural remission,’ together with ‘Crohn’s disease, ‘ulcerative colitis ‘, ‘inflammatory bowel disease’, ‘CD’, ' UC ‘, ‘IBD’, ‘intestinal ultra-sound’, ‘magnetic resonance imaging’, ‘IUS’, ‘MRI’. The search was limited to articles published in the English language.

Given the narrative nature of this review, studies were selected based on their relevance to the scope and objectives of the manuscript, rather than through a formal systematic selection process. The selection of articles was guided by the authors’ expertise in the field and supplemented by key references identified through the screening of bibliographies.

Results

Why imaging endpoints matter in IBD trials

Prognostic implications

A growing body of evidence suggests that imaging-derived TH is associated with superior long-term outcomes compared with endoscopic mucosal healing (MH), even though TH is not yet a formal treatment target. Notably, approximately 30% of patients who achieve MH at colonoscopy still show residual inflammatory activity on cross-sectional imaging, underscoring a potential discordance between mucosal and transmural disease control [11]. In cohort analyses, patients achieving TH had a markedly lower risk of bowel damage progression than those with MH (HR 0.05; 95% CI 0.00–0.40; P = 0.002). TH was also associated with fewer major adverse outcomes, comprising surgery, bowel damage progression, or hospitalization (HR 0.16; 95% CI 0.04–0.60; P = 0.006) [12]. Trial cohorts are typically composed of advanced therapy naïve patients as well as advanced therapy exposed (typically up to three class failures) and are selected to minimize confounders such as active complications. For this reason, it is crucial to set more stringent targets in trials, recognizing that efficacy rates observed under tightly controlled conditions typically underestimate real-world effectiveness in broader, less selected populations.

Assess small bowel & proximal segments

Colonoscopy—typically mandated at baseline and as an endpoint in IBD trials—provides mucosal views of the colon and, when feasible, the terminal ileum. However, ileal intubation is not always successful and the jejunum and proximal ileum are inherently out of reach. Cross-sectional imaging fills this gap. IUS and MRE visualize the terminal ileum even when intubation is incomplete, and can also assess the proximal small bowel, where skip lesions and subclinical activity are common in CD [13].

Detect complications

Cross-sectional imaging (IUS and MRI) detects and tracks complications, such as strictures, fistulas, abscesses, and penetrating disease, that endoscopy misses, strengthening safety oversight and endpoint validity in IBD trials. It can help characterize strictures by identifying features suggestive of predominant inflammation versus fibrosis, although a definitive discrimination remains challenging and often requires integration with clinical, endoscopic, and surgical findings. Imaging can also map fistula trajectories and activity, and refines deep or pelvic collections for timely intervention. Pelvic MRI and TPUS together optimize perianal assessment and serial monitoring [13].

Intestinal ultrasound (IUS) as a trial endpoint

Technical & methodological standards for UC and CD trials

Based on the 2025 international consensus by Allocca et al., patient preparation and execution should be standardized across visits, employing light fasting, consistent positioning with graded compression, and fixed machine presets so that baseline and follow-up acquisitions are directly comparable. Sites should use ultrasound systems equipped with both a low-frequency convex probe (approximately 1–6 MHz) for panoramic mapping and a high-frequency linear probe (approximately 2–12 MHz) for detailed bowel-wall interrogation, with color Doppler capable of setting and displaying intramural flow velocity at 5–7 cm/s. The scan proceeds transabdominally in all cases with a systematic, segment-by-segment evaluation that covers the terminal ileum, any visualized small bowel, and all colonic segments [6, 14].

To standardize IUS acquisition and reporting, for each intestinal segment record bowel wall thickness (BWT), color Doppler signal (CDS), and bowel wall stratification (BWS); then assess extraintestinal findings as indirect markers of inflammation—such as inflammatory fat (i-fat), mesenteric hypertrophy, and mesenteric lymphadenopathy—and, finally, screen for complications including strictures, fistulas, and abscesses. All pathological segments should be recorded in both longitudinal and transverse planes, using cine-loop sweeps that span the entire lesion from normal-to-normal margins. Vascularity should be captured with color Doppler cine loops over the same region. In clinical trials, the most severely affected (index) segment identified at baseline is typically reassessed at follow-up and used to determine imaging-defined response [15].

Definition of remission and response in UC and CD trials

A recent systematic review of 51 studies on IUS response and remission underscored substantial heterogeneity in definitions [16]. The latest consensus sought to harmonize criteria but still permits a narrow set of alternatives. The most endorsed definitions for response were: a ≥ 25% reduction in BWT from baseline when improvement is limited to a single parameter (94.1%, 16/17), and a ≥ 25% reduction in BWT plus a one-grade decrease in CDS (93.7%, 15/16). For remission, the top choices were BWT ≤ 3 mm (88.2%, 15/17) and BWT ≤ 3 mm with normalization of all IUS parameters (94.1%, 16/17). IUS activity scores

For multicenter clinical trials, standardized IUS scores are essential to ensure uniform outcomes across sites. This is because two core sonographic features, specifically BWT and CDS, act as practical surrogates of inflammatory activity: in most settings, inflammation is suggested when BWT exceeds 3 mm, and when CDS is increased. For this reason, validated IUS indices largely rely on BWT and CDS as the most reproducible markers of active disease and change over time. Two validated indices—correlated with endoscopy as the reference standard—are the Bowel Ultrasound Score (BUSS) for CD and the Milan Ultrasound Criteria (MUC) for UC [26, 27]. Both are composite measures that prioritize BWT and CDS, with predefined thresholds that map to endoscopic activity states. In CD, ultrasound response may be defined as a ≥ 1.2-point decrease in BUSS, a threshold that predicts endoscopic response (> 50% reduction in SES-CD) with AUC 0.79 (95% CI 0.64–0.89), 74% sensitivity, and 83% specificity [26]. Ultrasound remission may be defined as BUSS ≤ 3.52, which predicts endoscopic remission (SES-CD ≤ 2) with an AUROC of 0.86 [28]. A post hoc analysis from the METRIC trial showed that, in adult terminal ileal CD, BUSS had 68% sensitivity (95% CI 61–74) and 85% specificity (95% CI 76–91) for active disease when benchmarked against an MRE reference standard (sMaRIA), and 66% sensitivity (95% CI 56–75) with 68% specificity (95% CI 47–84) against a histological reference standard [29]. In UC, ultrasound response can be defined as a ≥ 2-point decrease in MUC from baseline. With this threshold, MUC predicted patients achieving MES ≤ 1 with 89% sensitivity (95% CI 65–98) and 77% specificity (95% CI 59–90; AUROC 0.81, 95% CI 0.67–0.90), and predicted MES = 0 with 100% sensitivity (95% CI 59–100) and 62% specificity (95% CI 46–76; AUROC 0.82, 95% CI 0.68–0.91) [27]. Ultrasound remission can be defined as MUC ≤ 6.2, as a threshold of MUC > 6.2 predicts MES > 1 with 85% sensitivity (95% CI 66–96) and 94% specificity (95% CI 70–99) [30]. For CD, the International Bowel Ultrasound Segmental Activity Score (IBUS-SAS) is another widely used composite index that extends beyond BWT and CDS by also incorporating BWS and i-fat (4 × BWT + 15 × i-fat + 7 × CDS + 4 × BWS) [31]. Its main advantage is broader phenotyping of disease activity, as it captures both mural and extraintestinal inflammatory features. However, BWS and i-fat may be more prone to interobserver variability than BWT or CDS, and trial-validated thresholds for response and remission are not yet established [31].

Magnetic resonance enterography (MRE/MRI) as a trial endpoint

Technical & methodological standards for CD trials

MRE should be acquired on 1.5T or 3T scanners using motion-robust small-bowel protocols. Experts recommend rapid single-shot fast spin-echo sequences with short TE or SS-TSE Short TE, obtained without fat suppression in axial and coronal planes, followed by SS-FSE/HASTE with fat suppression (axial and coronal) and a steady-state free-precession coronal series with fat suppression to depict luminal caliber, mural signal, and perienteric changes. Intravenous gadolinium is administered to evaluate relative contrast enhancement and delineate ulcers and hyperemia [7]. Images are interpreted by two blinded central readers, with a study manual defining standardized grading of image quality, prespecified feature ratings (e.g., wall thickening, edema, ulcers, perienteric fat stranding), and uniform measurement conventions (e.g., perpendicular mural thickness, segmental scoring, and documentation of artifacts) [7, 32].

Definition of remission and response in CD and UC trials

As with IUS, a recent systematic review has examined how response and remission are defined on MRI in CD, including 18 studies and highlighting substantial heterogeneity across definitions [33]. A subsequent Delphi consensus addressed key ambiguities and, while noting that MRI is not formally validated as an endpoint for IBD trials, endorsed the Magnetic Resonance Index of Activity (MaRIA) and simplified MaRIA (sMaRIA) as the preferred activity scores for both practice and research. From the Delphi process, trial-ready thresholds emerged: response may be defined by a total MaRIA < 11 or an improvement ≥ 25%, or by sMaRIA decrease ≥ 1 point; remission may be defined by a total MaRIA < 7 or sMaRIA < 1. Importantly, the authors also state that evidence is currently insufficient to endorse a unified definition of transmural healing, owing to heterogeneous expert definitions and limited prospective validation [7].

Addressing this gap, the DEVISE-CD initiative developed a validated MRI-based score specifically designed to quantify transmural disease activity and healing—the C-score [34]. Applied to actively inflamed segments (i.e., with mural edema), the C-score integrates three parameters: BWT (continuous), ulcers (presence/absence), and enlarged lymph nodes (presence/absence). To date, the C-score is the only validated imaging metric explicitly tailored to transmural healing in CD [34].

MRE activity scores

In a similar way to IUS, MRE-based activity indices are used to standardize assessment, improve reproducibility across sites, and provide surrogates of endoscopic inflammation. Most scores were developed against endoscopic and/or histopathological reference standards and combine a small set of imaging features that capture complementary components of active disease. BWT is a central element, reflecting mural inflammation and remodeling, and it is typically interpreted together with markers of activity such as mural edema, ulceration, and relative contrast enhancement (RCE) [44, 45]. These features underpin the two most widely used luminal CD indices, MaRIA and sMaRIA. In particular, sMaRIA has shown high diagnostic performance for segmental disease activity, with segmental sMaRIA ≥ 1 identifying active CD (sensitivity 90%, specificity 81%) and segmental sMaRIA ≥ 2 identifying severe CD (sensitivity 85%, specificity 92%) [44, 45]. Consistent with recent consensus efforts, MaRIA/sMaRIA therefore represent practical, trial-ready anchors for defining MRI response and remission in CD. In contrast, no MRI scoring system has yet been validated for UC. Interestingly, a recent multicenter study suggested that baseline MRE-derived indices add limited incremental prognostic value for predicting a disabling disease course, while MRE remains highly valuable for diagnosis, defining disease extent, and longitudinal monitoring [46].

The importance of cross-sectional imaging

Cross-sectional imaging in IBD offers two complementary, non-invasive options. MRI/MRE typically requires preparation (fasting, oral contrast, almost always antiperistaltics), takes longer, is more expensive, and is less convenient for patients with claustrophobia; however, it yields durable image datasets that can be centrally reviewed and re-read over time. IUS, by contrast, needs no preparation, can be performed at the point of care by trained gastroenterologists, can be repeated frequently to guide therapy and directly visualizes peristalsis, often clinically informative. Its dynamic, real-time nature is not a limitation per se; rather, in trials it underscores the need to record and archive each examination (e.g., cine loops and stills) so that a core lab can perform blinded central reading. In the METRIC multicentre study of small-bowel CD, MRE outperformed IUS for detecting disease presence (sensitivity 97% vs. 92%) and for defining disease extent (sensitivity 80% vs. 70%; specificity 95% vs. 81%). Specificity for presence was 96% with MRE and 84% with IUS indicating a borderline difference. Overall, these results confirm MRE’s edge for mapping disease while showing that both modalities perform strongly [35].

Overall, cost-effectiveness must be carefully evaluated when integrating cross-sectional imaging into trials and routine care. IUS is likely to be advantageous: in a recent tertiary-center experience, routine IUS avoided downstream endoscopy or MRE in approximately 63–65% of patients and generated direct cost savings of ~ US$92,069 for 60 IUS assessments, while preserving decision-making quality [47]. Given the patient-centric direction of care, emerging evidence suggests that tailoring the imaging strategy can meaningfully influence patient preferences and engagement. In a cohort of 326 participants, 147 (45.1%) received IUS-driven care: among all respondents, IUS was the most preferred modality (60.7%), while preferences were lower for blood work (51.1%), colonoscopy (22.4%), stool tests (22.4%), and other cross-sectional imaging such as MRE (46/219; 21.0%) [48]. Adherence, however, is influenced by broader behavioral determinants: poor disease understanding, low perceived treatment control, lack of motivation or self-management skills, and suboptimal patient–provider communication are recurrent barriers [49]. Incorporating IUS within routine clinic visits appears to counter several of these obstacles: patients undergoing IUS reported better understanding of their disease and symptoms, greater confidence in making informed decisions, and transient improvements in medication adherence [50].

Timing strategy: aligning imaging with drug mechanisms

Evidence indicates that early on-treatment IUS changes can predict subsequent endoscopic response/remission and longer-term outcomes. In a prospective UC cohort (de Voogd et al., n = 51), BWT ≤ 3.0 mm at week 6 predicted endoscopic remission (MES = 0) at weeks 8–26 with 78% sensitivity, 90% specificity, and AUROC 0.82 (P = 0.007) [53]. Early assessment can also be pivotal for hard outcomes such as surgery. In fact, a BWT > 6 mm was considered an optimal cutoff for predicting the risk of colectomy in the first 3 months after diagnosis (OR 38, 95% CI, 8–270, p < 0.001) [54].

At the same time, pharmacodynamics varies across drug classes, and both drug type and drug exposure should inform the imaging schedule. In UC, significant reductions in BWT have been observed as early as week 2 with infliximab and tofacitinib, whereas vedolizumab typically shows a significant BWT decrease only by week 6 [53]. In CD, meaningful BWT reductions have been documented by week 4 with ustekinumab [17].

Consensus timelines converge on an early IUS check in the colon (4–8–12 weeks) and a slightly later IUS window for the terminal ileum (12–16 weeks), with follow-ups at 24 and 48–52 weeks [6]. IUS remission is typically assessed at 12, 24, and 48–52 weeks for both sites [6]. For MRI, week 24 is the anchor for response. Transmural healing on MRI is positioned as a longer-term goal, commonly assessed at 1 or 2 years [7].

Overall, timing should be individualized, distinguishing the more stringent, protocol-driven timepoints of clinical trials from real-world practice, where early and frequent imaging is valuable but not always feasible.

How to handle special populations

Perianal disease

Patients with perianal disease have often been excluded from IBD trials, but this is gradually changing, as illustrated by ongoing phase 3 trials such as FUNZION CD (NCT05347095) and DIRECT-CD (NCT06059989), which evaluate the perianal efficacy of guselkumab and subcutaneous infliximab, respectively [55, 56]. For assessment, pelvic MRI remains the cornerstone, while transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) is a widely used alternative and is often employed to assess perianal disease. At the same time, transperineal ultrasound (TPUS) is emerging as a non-invasive, patient-centered tool that enables serial monitoring at the point of care [57]. Historically, study endpoints relied on clinical examination alone, defining response as “>50% closure of draining fistulas at two or more consecutive visits” and remission as “absence of any draining fistulas at two consecutive visits” [58]. More recent work has shifted endpoints toward imaging. In fact, the TOpClass group’s Delphi consensus defines radiological healing on pelvic MRI as the absence of T2-weighted hyperintense signal within the fistula tract [59]. The same consensus provides practical guidance on timing, recommending assessment within a flexible window of 6 to 12 months after definitive surgery or initiation of advanced therapies [59]. Definitive efficacy endpoints for TRUS and TPUS have not yet been established. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of TRUS and TPUS. TRUS is often poorly tolerated, and TPUS may miss deep pelvic fistula extensions or abscesses beyond the field of view. For these reasons, both techniques are generally considered second-line compared with pelvic MRI, particularly when deep disease is suspected.

Elderly and pregnancy

Dedicated clinical trials that enroll only elderly adults (≥ 60–65 years) or pregnant patients with IBD are lacking. Most evidence derives from post-hoc analyses and real-world observational cohorts. Nevertheless, many advanced therapies now in use for IBD are considered acceptable in these populations, and are increasingly prescribed. Because colonoscopy may be unsafe or impractical for monitoring in some older or pregnant patients, non-invasive cross-sectional imaging offers a pragmatic alternative. MRI and IUS can support objective disease assessment with favorable tolerability, though image acquisition can be technically challenging in the third trimester because uterine enlargement and bowel displacement limit visualization [60]. Endpoint selection in these populations is more complex, and targets and assessment schedules should be individualized based on patient characteristics and risk–benefit considerations. How to integrate AI.

Emerging AI tools are beginning to standardize cross-sectional imaging in IBD by automating key measurements, reducing inter-reader variability, and enabling scalable central review. For IUS, a 2025 study automated BWT measurement and abnormal/normal classification across 4,565 pediatric images, achieving sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 94% at a 2-mm cutoff, with excellent agreement versus manual measures (ICC 0.94) [61]. For MRE, a 2024 systematic review identified deep-learning applications that enhance image quality, perform bowel/lesion segmentation for burden quantification, and even measure motility, all of which could reduce variability and facilitate objective scoring in multicenter settings [62]. Building on this, there is increasing interest in moving beyond linear, segment-based metrics toward AI-assisted quantification of total inflammatory burden using volumetric measurements on MRE. Recent feasibility studies in luminal CD and emerging work in perianal disease suggest that volume-based metrics (e.g., ileal inflammation volume or fistula volume) may provide sensitive, standardized readouts of treatment response, and semi-automated approaches that integrate bowel lesions with extraintestinal features such as creeping fat further support this direction [63–66]. Nevertheless, most studies remain preliminary and need external validation.

AI algorithms may perform standardized quality checks, pre-segmentation, and preliminary extraction of key metrics, with expert readers retaining final authority and adjudicating discrepancies as needed. Models should be validated on multicenter data, and monitored for performance drift. At present, limitations such as domain shift, dataset bias, limited explainability, and uncertain regulatory acceptance mean that AI should play a supportive, rather than replacement, role within imaging workflows for clinical trials [67].

Evidence gaps & future directions

Despite growing adoption, particularly in CD, there remains a shortage of randomized and robust real-world studies that pre-specify IUS and MRI as efficacy endpoints. In UC, the evidence base is even thinner, despite the potential of IUS to complement endoscopy by capturing functional impairment and markers of chronic damage. A further gap concerns definitions: harmonized criteria for eligibility and for on-treatment response and remission, including cut-offs and timepoints, are still evolving. Progress will depend on tighter collaboration between radiologists and gastroenterologists to align acquisition protocols, scoring, and clinical interpretation.

Trials should integrate IUS and MRI from screening through longitudinal monitoring, using validated scores, predefined timing windows, and standardized acquisition with blinded central readers to minimize bias. Modality selection ought to be tailored to the patient and the center: patient factors (e.g., claustrophobia, likelihood of adherence to repeated visits), disease phenotype and location (colonic disease favoring IUS; proximal small-bowel or high CD suspicion favoring MRI), and center resources (availability of high-quality equipment and experienced operators) should guide the pathway. Feasible methods such as IUS enable earlier, tailored reassessment in the face of discordant data, reducing unnecessary reliance on invasive procedures like endoscopy while maintaining tight disease control. In many scenarios, the two modalities should be integrated rather than viewed as alternatives, and imaging response/remission endpoints should be combined with clinical, biochemical, and endoscopic readouts to provide a global assessment of the disease.

In support of this concept, interim ECCO data from the ongoing VECTORS treat-to-target trial indicate that IUS-based outcomes are feasible within a protocolized escalation strategy. In the imaging arm, targets include corticosteroid-free IUS response (≥ 25% BWT reduction) and TH (BWT ≤ 3.0 mm with CDS = 0 in all evaluable segments), and early follow-up showed measurable improvements in segmental ultrasound activity and BWT, supporting the practicality of serial IUS endpoints in a treat-to-target design [68].

Conclusion

Even with recent progress in integrating cross-sectional imaging, its implementation should be assessed pragmatically across both trials and routine care, including formal cost-effectiveness analyses and evaluation of whether IUS/MRI can improve adherence and follow-up compared with traditional assessment pathways.
