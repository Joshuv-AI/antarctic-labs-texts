Characterization of Enterococcus species in surface drinking water from Akoko Edo Nigeria reveals contamination levels and risks to public health
Authors: Nnenna A. Isokpehi, Abeni Beshiru, Ezekiel Green, Isoken H. Igbinosa, Abraham G. Ogofure, Etinosa O. Igbinosa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study focused on the assessment of drinking surface water for the presence and characteristics of Enterococcus species, which are indicative of water contamination and pose potential health risks to consumers. Our year-long investigation into several water bodies included using chromogenic medium and membrane filtering to isolate Enterococcus. The antimicrobial susceptibility of these bacteria was assessed through micro broth dilution, while virulence factors and biofilm formation were determined phenotypically. Resistance and virulence traits were detected using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) techniques. The study revealed varying bacterial densities measured in log10 CFU/100mL, with fecal coliforms, total coliforms, and Enterococcus species all present in the water, highlighting potential contamination issues. Enterococcus distribution showed a variety of species, with E. faecium being the most prevalent. Alarmingly, 63.9% of the isolates displayed multidrug resistance (MDR), and efflux pump genes associated with antimicrobial resistance were detected. The presence of virulence genes and genes associated with biofilm formation indicates the potential of these Enterococcus species to cause diseases and contribute to water quality problems. Given that this surface water serves as a drinking water source for local communities, the findings indicate a potential public health threat. The study provides crucial data for health professionals to conduct risk assessments, reducing the risk of health issues and enhancing consumer safety in relation to drinking water.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-13068-2.

Introduction

Water has a substantial impact on each person’s health and is crucial for plant life. The structure, synthesis, cell constituents, and nutrient transport into the cells depend on the water and body metabolism1. The human body contains over 70% water, with approximately 75% of the earth’s crust covered in water2. The availability of high-quality water is essential for reducing water-borne illnesses and enhancing the quality of life. Surface water is a significant natural supply for home and industrial operations3. The surface water used for this study is located in rural environments where all forms of human activities depend on it, such as drinking water, irrigation, fishery, laundry, waste disposal, and, most notably, as a source of ancestral heritage. However, water pollution has many causes, many of which are substantial human activities. Since 2017, there has been no change in the percentage of people worldwide who have access to portable water, which has risen from 61% to 71% within the range of twenty-two years (2000–2022)4. The remaining percentage to achieve portable water is in progress with Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 6), which seeks to guarantee that by 2030, everyone will have equitable access to safe, affordable drinking water. Access to safe drinking water is a fundamental human right and essential for public health5. However, surface water sources, including rivers, streams, and lakes, are susceptible to contamination from various sources, posing risks to human health. Enterococcus species are commonly found in the gastrointestinal tract of humans and animals and are widely used as indicators of fecal pollution in water environments6. Their presence in drinking water can indicate contamination by fecal matter and potential pathogenic organisms, raising concerns for public health.

Enterococci are ubiquitous and vital indicators of fecal pollution in water bodies. They are part of the normal intestine microflora in humans and animals7. Nevertheless, many infections acquired in healthcare facilities are caused by Enterococcus species, including Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium8. Due to their resistance to antimicrobials and capacity to acquire and spread, enterococci have emerged as a significant nosocomial pathogen with antimicrobial resistance (AMR) determinants3. More so, they have developed resistance to nearly all clinically used new and old antimicrobial medications, making antimicrobial treatments more difficult. AMR is continuously rising worldwide, and a significant factor in this rise is the frequent and unchecked use of antibiotics in human and veterinary treatment9. Increasing evidence points to the environment as a significant reservoir and source of ARGs for bacteria that cause human and animal infections. Agroecosystems may release antimicrobials, bacteria with a resistance gene, and resistant bacteria into the water damaged by manure10. In Nigeria, particularly in regions like Akoko Edo, where surface water is a primary source of drinking water for local communities, ensuring water quality is crucial. However, limited studies have been conducted to comprehensively assess the presence and characteristics of Enterococcus species in these water sources and their implications for public health.

Efflux-mediated resistance makes antibacterial treatment complicated, thereby providing initial resistance, which can lead to urgent combined resistance mechanisms, like drug target modification or drug inactivation11. Drug targets may change, medications’ structures may alter or degrade, and outer membrane proteins’ permeability may decrease, preventing drugs from entering cells and improving efflux transporters, which lowers intracellular concentrations of drugs that are the foundations of resistance mechanisms to antimicrobials12. Isolating virulence genes from surface water sources in Enterococcus species is a severe concern. Virulence genes cause colonization, invasion, and translocation via epithelial cells of a host’s tissue13 to evade the host’s immunologic response. However, the existence of these virulence genes in species of Enterococcus obtained from surface water sources used in agriculture, drinking, and recreational purposes can yield risk, particularly for the immunocompromised populace3. Understanding the distribution, antimicrobial susceptibility, virulence factors, and potential for biofilm formation of Enterococcus species in drinking surface water is essential for assessing the risk of waterborne diseases and guiding effective public health interventions. Additionally, investigating the prevalence of multidrug-resistant strains and the genetic determinants of AMR can provide insights into the mechanisms driving resistance development in environmental bacteria. Therefore, this study aims to fill this gap by characterizing Enterococcus species in drinking surface water in Akoko Edo, Nigeria. By elucidating the microbial composition and assessing AMR and virulence traits, the study seeks to identify potential sources of contamination and evaluate the associated public health risks. The findings will inform policymakers, water authorities, and health professionals to implement targeted interventions for improving water quality and safeguarding public health in the region.

While the occurrence of Enterococcus species in surface water has been reported in various global and national contexts14–16 to our knowledge, this is the first systematic investigation focused on the surface drinking water sources (Ovokoto Spring and Ojerame Dam) in Akoko-Edo, Nigeria. Previous studies in Nigeria have concentrated on other regions or on broader surveys, often overlooking localized, rural, and under-resourced communities17–21. Our study fills this geographical and data gap, providing baseline information critical for local water management and public health planning. Unlike many earlier studies that have reported only the presence or total counts of Enterococcus spp. as fecal indicators14,22 our work employs both molecular and biochemical techniques for species-level identification. This allows for a more nuanced risk assessment, as certain species such as E. faecalis and E. faecium are more frequently associated with human disease15,23. Furthermore, our study provides detailed AMR profiles of the isolates, an aspect rarely addressed in regional water quality research in Nigeria, despite the global concern over environmental reservoirs of AMR24,25. Our research uniquely offers a holistic perspective on the factors influencing microbial contamination in these specific water bodies. Such integrative analyses are limited in the current literature, particularly for rural African contexts16. The findings are interpreted in the context of local water usage patterns, sanitation infrastructure, and public health risks, providing actionable recommendations for regional stakeholders. This localized focus is essential for the development of targeted interventions and aligns with calls for context-specific water safety research in low- and middle-income countries24,26.

Results

Population density of fecal coliforms, total coliforms, and Enterococcus species from surface water

The mean bacterial population density in surface water samples, illustrated in Fig. 1, exhibits the following ranges: fecal coliforms (0.60 ± 0.84–3.18 ± 0.25 log10 CFU/100mL), total coliforms (1.59 ± 0.25–3.51 ± 0.43 log10 CFU/100mL), and Enterococcus species (0.52 ± 0.60–1.92 ± 0.39 log10 CFU/100mL). Seasonal variation reveals distinct patterns during the wet season [fecal coliforms (1.20 ± 0.20–2.33 ± 0.11 log10 CFU/100mL), total coliforms (1.88 ± 0.35–2.47 ± 0.41 log10 CFU/100mL), Enterococcus species (1.28 ± 0.92–1.92 ± 0.39 log10 CFU/100mL)] and dry season [fecal coliforms (0.60 ± 0.84–3.18 ± 0.25 log10 CFU/100mL), total coliforms (1.59 ± 0.25–3.51 ± 0.43 log10 CFU/100mL), Enterococcus species (0.52 ± 0.60–1.23 ± 1.43 log10 CFU/100mL)]. Significant differences exist in population density among different stations (p < 0.05). Ninety-seven Enterococcus species were identified at species level using API 20E and PCR, distributed as follows: Enterococcus faecalis 21(21.7%), Enterococcus faecium 29(29.9%), Enterococcus casseliflavus 15(15.5%), Enterococcus gallinarum 7(7.2%), Enterococcus durans 4(4.1%), Enterococcus hirae 2(2.1%), and other Enterococcus species 19(19.6%).

Antimicrobial susceptibility profile of the Enterococcus species from surface water

Figure 2 illustrates the AMR profile of Enterococcus species, indicating resistance rates for penicillin (74.2%), ampicillin (71.1%), vancomycin (38.1%), teicoplanin (13.4%), erythromycin (59.8%), tetracycline (38.1%), ciprofloxacin (45.4%), rifampin (53.6%), chloramphenicol (48.5%), and HLR-gentamicin (14.4%). The antimicrobial susceptibility profile of representative Enterococcus species is shown in Table 1. Among the isolates, 11.3% showed no resistance to tested antibiotics, while 6.2% displayed resistance to 11 antibiotics. About 63.9% of isolates demonstrated multidrug resistance potential, resisting one drug in three antimicrobial classes, while 72.2% were resistant to three different antibiotics. MAR indices ranged from 0 to 0.69, with 62.9% having a MAR index greater than 2. There is a significant correlation between the production of biofilms and the enterococcal MAR index (r = 0.550, p < 0.01). The antibiotic resistance profile of Enterococcus species did not significantly differ based on the isolate’s location (p > 0.05).

Biofilm formation profile of Enterococcus species from surface water systems

Table 2 depicts the biofilm formation profile, categorizing it into strong [E. faecalis 33.3%, E. faecium 6.9%], moderate [E. faecalis 47.6%, E. faecium 27.6%, E. casseliflavus 53.3%, other Enterococcus sp. 10.5%], weak E. faecium 31.0%, E. casseliflavus 40.0%, E. gallinarum 28.6%, other Enterococcus sp. 21.1%], and no biofilm formation E. faecalis 19.1%, E. faecium 34.5%, E. casseliflavus 6.7%, E. gallinarum 71.4%, E. durans 100%, E. hirae 100%, other Enterococcus sp. 68.4%]. The optical density of Enterococcus species with biofilm formation significantly correlates with antibiotic minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values, including penicillin, ampicillin, vancomycin, teicoplanin, erythromycin, tetracycline, minocycline, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, gatifloxacin, nitrofurantoin, rifampin, chloramphenicol, and quinupristin-dalfopristin (p < 0.01 or p < 0.05). No correlation was found with HLR-gentamicin and linezolid (p > 0.05). Biofilm formation also significantly correlates with virulence factors such as gelatinase activity, hemolytic activity, hyaluronidase activity, hyl, gelE, cylA, esp, asa, and gcpA (p < 0.01 or p < 0.05). The biofilm formation profile of Enterococcus species did not significantly differ based on the isolate’s location (p > 0.05).

Distribution of efflux pump genes in surface water-borne Enterococcus species

Figure 3 illustrates the distribution of efflux pump genes among Enterococcus species isolated from surface water. The mefA gene was detected in several species, with its presence observed in 9.5% of E. faecalis isolates, 13.8% of E. faecium, 6.7% of E. casseliflavus, and notably, in 50% of E. durans isolates. The msrC gene exhibited a broader distribution, being found in 14.3% of E. faecalis, 55.2% of E. faecium, 33.3% of E. casseliflavus, and 14.3% of E. gallinarum isolates, as well as in 5.3% of isolates classified as other Enterococcus species. The tetK gene was present in 9.5% of E. faecalis, 13.8% of E. faecium, and 13.3% of E. casseliflavus isolates. In contrast, the tetL gene showed the widest distribution across species, being detected in 38.1% of E. faecalis, 34.5% of E. faecium, 20% of E. casseliflavus, 14.3% of E. gallinarum, 25% of E. durans, 50% of E. hirae, and 5.3% of other Enterococcus species. When considering all isolates collectively, the overall prevalence of these efflux pump genes among Enterococcus species was 9.3% for mefA, 26.8% for msrC, 8.2% for tetK, and 25.8% for tetL. Statistical analysis revealed several significant correlations. The presence of mefA was significantly associated with resistance to erythromycin and rifampin, as well as with the occurrence of the tetK and tetL genes (p < 0.05). The msrC gene showed significant correlations with adrA, csgD, and gcpA, as well as with resistance to vancomycin, erythromycin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, rifampin, and chloramphenicol, in addition to the MAR index (p < 0.05). The tetK gene was significantly linked to biofilm formation, resistance to erythromycin, tetracycline, minocycline, and rifampin, the MAR index, and the presence of both mefA and tetL (p < 0.05). Similarly, the tetL gene was significantly associated with biofilm formation, resistance to erythromycin, tetracycline, minocycline, levofloxacin, rifampin, and gentamicin, as well as with the MAR index, mefA, and tetK (p < 0.05). Notably, there were no significant differences in the efflux pump gene profiles of Enterococcus species with respect to the location of the isolates (p > 0.05).

Detection of virulence and biofilm-associated genes in Enterococcus species from surface waters

Figure 4 presents the distribution of virulence-associated genes among Enterococcus species isolated from surface water. The hyl gene was detected in several species, with its prevalence observed in 23.8% of E. faecalis isolates, 31.0% of E. faecium, 40% of E. casseliflavus, and 42.9% of E. gallinarum. The gelE gene exhibited a notably high frequency, being present in 66.7% of E. faecalis, 41.4% of E. faecium, and 73.3% of E. casseliflavus isolates, as well as in 26.3% of isolates classified as other Enterococcus species. Lastly, the asa gene was detected in 38.1% of E. faecalis, 24.1% of E. faecium, and 20% of E. casseliflavus isolates.

Discussion

This study examined the ubiquity of Enterococcus species in surface water bodies and its genetic makeup, pathogenicity, and antibiotic resistance characteristics. There were significant differences in the population density from the different stations, which can be attributed to the difference in the activities carried out in each station, such as the difference in anthropogenic activities and the deposition of wastes into the water27. In addition, seasonal variation may have accounted for the difference in the population density as bacteria counts showed higher counts in the wet season compared to the dry season28 which can be due to the increased land runoff during the wet season that results in higher fecal input from different sources, thereby increasing the infectious dose. However, there is reduced intensity of sunlight during the wet season, which may encourage the survival of fecal bacteria in water29.

The increase in population density could also result from a high concentration of organic materials being introduced into the surface water through waste disposal, according to a study at Ado Ekiti, Nigeria, in which spring water contained an astonishingly high number of Enterococcus species30. Furthermore, research conducted on drinking water samples collected from specific dairy cattle farms in Portugal revealed a significant population density of Enterococcus species in the drinking water, suggesting potential fecal contamination from warm-blooded animals and other domesticated animals31. The high count of enterococci, as it exceeds the WHO standards, suggests that the surface water is fecally polluted and unfit for drinking.

Antibiotic resistance is a factor that contributes to the pathogenesis of enterococci that can be acquired or found in them32. Overall, 72.2% of the Enterococcus isolates from the surface water in the study showed resistance to one or more of the tested antimicrobial agents. High resistance to penicillin, ampicillin, and erythromycin was observed, and this might be because these antibiotics are widely used in both humans and cattle and can be transmitted to surface water33. Penicillin is one of the antibiotics that can be easily accessed over the counter in most developing countries before everyday use. Consequently, this high antibiotic resistance found in surface water might be attributed to the constant release of resistant bacterial isolates, primarily in the form of excreta from people and animals, as well as possession of residues of the antibiotics that sustain bacterial species through selective pressure34.

Moreover, no resistance was found among the Enterococcus species against linezolid and quinupristin-dalfopristin, as similarly observed in a previous study on water samples33. This could be because the antibiotics are new in the market and have no constant use in the area. The low resistance to minocycline, levofloxacin, and gatifloxacin might be due to the low use of the antibiotics in the area, as ciprofloxacin is the referenced antibiotic used in the fluoroquinolone subclass, while the resistance to vancomycin was in line with the study conducted on Enterococcus sp isolated from recreational waters35. Antibiotic resistance is of concern as it may create severe health challenges for people since it makes contamination treatment difficult. The detection of ciprofloxacin-resistant Enterococcus sp in surface water has been reported36. Elevated resistance to these therapeutic agents can be associated with frequent antibiotic use, as previously recorded35.

The MAR index of the Enterococcus species in this study indicates inputs from livestock farming waste, wastewater, and other anthropogenic activities. It has been previously emphasized that values of MAR index above 0.25 are most likely associated with health risk environment37. Multiple antibiotic resistance can result in the emergence of newly developing resistant bacteria, which may then extend to users and cause diseases that may be challenging to cure. The multiple antibiotic resistance index obtained from this work correlated with the strong biofilm formation by the isolates. The isolated Enterococcus species were sensitive to some of the antibiotics used in this study. This might be because of limited antibiotic exposure, as the study area is rural. The observed MAR in this study concerns the possible treatment failure in using antibiotics from several antimicrobial classes.

The three Enterococcus species, E. casseliflavus, E. faecalis, and E. faecium, expressed virulence activity. The presence of β-hemolytic activity, hyaluronidase activity, and gelatinase activity suggest an increase in drug resistance; it also enhances the organism to be pathogenic over a species’ inherent environment. In agreement with this study, the detection of gelatinase activities was also observed in an investigation involving surface water38. The production of gelatinase has been notably linked to the hydrolysis of peptides like gelatin, collagen, casein, and hemoglobin39. Hyaluronidase activity has been observed in various Enterococcus species, with E. faecalis having the highest activity, followed by E. faecium40. These virulence genes in the surface water can spread Enterococcus species virulence genes in the surroundings to the exposed populace. The prevalence of these virulence genes indicates that this gene is connected to biofilm production. However, other studies have reported no association between these virulence genes and biofilm production41,42.

Biofilm formation in enterococci depends on several factors, and the role of various genes in the process is yet to be confirmed43. From this research, E. faecium and E. faecalis showed strong biofilm production, and this was in concert with another research works reported. In contrast, moderate biofilm was seen in E. faecalis, E. casseliflavus, E. faecium, and other Enterococcus species. There was a strong link between the development of biofilms and gelE, hyl, cylA, esp, asa, gcpA, and β hemolytic activities. It shows that these genes form a biofilm, creating barriers and promoting infections. Previous studies have reported the correlation of esp with biofilm formation and its potential to enhance biofilm formation44. The detection of the cylA and asa gene from Enterococcus aligns with previous investigation on surface water that reported the presence of these genes45. However, this contrasts with some investigations where few or no cylA genes were discovered46. These genes have been suggested to be a notable marker for microbial source tracking for fecal contamination45 an indication that the isolates in this study could be associated with fecal matter. Genes involved in enterococcal biofilm formation play a critical role in spreading antibiotic resistance globally47. Biofilms facilitate gene transfer, making these bacteria adaptable to various environments, from water sources to healthcare environments48. The strong link between biofilm formation and resistance genes presents significant public health challenges, especially in persistent infections on medical devices49. An understanding of these genetic elements is essential for developing targeted strategies to control infections and curb antibiotic resistance worldwide. The identification of multiple efflux pump genes among Enterococcus species isolated from surface water underscores the remarkable adaptability and resilience of these bacteria in diverse environmental contexts. Efflux pumps, such as those encoded by mefA, msrC, tetK, and tetL, play a pivotal role in mediating multidrug resistance by actively extruding a wide variety of antimicrobial agents and toxic compounds, thereby reducing intracellular drug concentrations and enhancing bacterial survival under selective pressure50. The detection of these genes across several Enterococcus species in this study is consistent with previous reports that have demonstrated the widespread distribution of efflux-mediated resistance mechanisms in both clinical and environmental isolates51,52. The detection of these four efflux pump genes contradicts another study that screened for the four efflux pump genes but did not detect mefA and tetK51. Enterococcus species have been reported to possess mefA53. In agreement with this study, the tetL gene was the most common among the Enterococcus species in an earlier report54. Tetracycline and erythromycin efflux pump genes were detected among the Enterococci isolates. Most of the isolates possessed more than one tetracycline resistance gene. Some research works have associated tetracycline resistance gene detection in enterococci with the exposure of animals to tetracycline to promote growth55. Detecting tetL efflux pump genes on the Enterococcus species was significant in this study, as other studies have monitored the ubiquity of the tetracycline resistance gene in water56. Genetic elements related to efflux pumps and antibiotic resistance in Enterococcus species play a critical role in the global spread of AMR, posing a public health threat57. The detection of multiple tetracycline resistance genes, especially in environmental water samples, highlights these bacteria’s adaptability and the risk of transferring resistance genes to other pathogens58. This association underscores concerns over antibiotic misuse in agriculture, which can impact human health by promoting resistance59. An understanding of these genes is essential for controlling antibiotic resistance, managing water quality, and guiding antibiotic policy in medicine and agriculture.

The presence of the msrC gene was identified in Enterococcus species obtained from screening, and these genes are responsible for encoding an efflux pump that confers slight resistance to macrolides and types B streptogramins60. These genes were detected more in E. faecium isolates, and this is not far from another report, in which msrC genes are largely harboured by E. faecium, and it was hinted that the gene might be indigenous in the chromosomes of the isolates61. They suggested that this gene is specific to species. However, in this investigation, msrC was isolated from sixteen E. casseliflavus, one E. gallinarum, and three isolates of E. faecalis. Notably, horizontal gene transfer has been severally linked to msrC genes in many Enterococcus species62–64. Efflux-mediated resistance causes the ineffectiveness of antibiotic medication. They offer a foundation for the evolution of other resistance mechanisms, such as drug target change or drug inactivation65. The significant associations observed between efflux pump genes and resistance to multiple antibiotics, as well as their correlation with biofilm-associated genes and the MAR index, suggest a complex interplay between resistance determinants and adaptive traits. Such co-selection and potential co-localization of resistance and biofilm genes on mobile genetic elements, including plasmids and transposons, have been documented as key drivers of the persistence and dissemination of AMR in bacterial populations66. The ability of efflux pumps to contribute to both antibiotic resistance and biofilm formation further enhances the survival prospects of Enterococcus in challenging environments, as biofilms provide a protective niche that facilitates horizontal gene transfer and shields bacteria from hostile agents67.

The absence of significant spatial variation in efflux pump gene profiles across sampling locations indicates that these resistance determinants are widely disseminated in the aquatic environment, likely due to the mixing of bacteria from various sources such as wastewater, agricultural runoff, and wildlife51,68. This widespread distribution is a cause for concern, as surface waters can serve as reservoirs and conduits for the transmission of AMR genes, potentially impacting both environmental and public health69. Overall, these findings reinforce the growing recognition of the environment’s role in the evolution and spread of AMR. The prevalence and interconnectedness of efflux pump genes with other resistance and adaptive traits highlight the potential for surface water environments to act as hot spots for the emergence and dissemination of multidrug-resistant Enterococcus populations. This underscores the urgent need for integrated surveillance and management strategies to mitigate the environmental spread of AMR, particularly in regions where surface waters are used for drinking, recreation, or agriculture.

The detection of a broad array of virulence-associated genes among Enterococcus species from surface water underscores the potential health risks posed by environmental reservoirs of this genus. The presence of genes such as hyl, gelE, cylA, esp, and asa in environmental isolates is noteworthy, as these determinants have been linked to increased pathogenicity, persistence, and adaptability in both clinical and non-clinical environments70–72. For instance, gelE, encoding gelatinase, is known to facilitate tissue invasion and immune evasion, and its strong correlation with other virulence factors and biofilm-associated genes in this study suggests a synergistic effect that may enhance the overall virulence potential of these isolates73. The co-occurrence of gelE with genes involved in hemolysis, hyaluronidase activity, and biofilm formation points to a coordinated regulatory mechanism that enables Enterococcus to thrive under environmental stresses and to persist in aquatic habitats.

Biofilm formation is a critical survival strategy for Enterococcus in fluctuating environments, providing protection from antimicrobial agents, predation, and environmental fluctuations74. The high prevalence of biofilm-associated genes such as adrA, csgD, and gcpA among the isolates highlights the capacity of these bacteria to form robust, multicellular communities on various surfaces. This trait not only facilitates environmental persistence but also increases the likelihood of horizontal gene transfer, including the dissemination of virulence and resistance determinants75. The observed genetic interconnectedness between virulence and biofilm genes suggests that these traits may be co-selected in response to environmental pressures, further compounding the challenge of controlling the spread of pathogenic and resistant Enterococcus in natural waters. Importantly, the lack of significant spatial variation in the profiles of virulence and biofilm-associated genes across different sampling locations indicates widespread dissemination of these traits, likely driven by the mixing of bacteria from multiple sources such as wastewater, agricultural runoff, and wildlife68. This finding aligns with global concerns regarding the role of environmental waters as reservoirs and conduits for the transmission of opportunistic pathogens and their associated genes76. The potential for these environmental Enterococcus populations to contribute to human and animal infections, particularly in regions where surface water is used for drinking or recreation, underscores the need for comprehensive monitoring and risk mitigation strategies. Overall, the observed genetic profiles highlight the adaptive and pathogenic potential of environmental Enterococcus populations. The interplay between virulence and biofilm formation, supported by the co-occurrence and significant correlations among key genes, emphasizes the importance of considering both traits in the context of environmental surveillance and public health risk assessment42. These findings reinforce the concept that surface waters are not merely passive recipients of microbial contamination but can actively shape the evolution and dissemination of clinically relevant bacterial traits.

The widespread detection of antibiotic resistance genes among Enterococcus species from surface water environments reflects the growing global concern over the environmental dissemination of AMR. The presence of multiple tetracycline resistance determinants, such as tetA, tetC, and particularly tetM, aligns with previous reports indicating that tetracycline resistance is one of the most commonly encountered resistance phenotypes in environmental enterococci, likely due to the extensive use of tetracyclines in both human medicine and agriculture77,78. The high prevalence of tetM is especially notable, as this gene is frequently associated with mobile genetic elements, facilitating its horizontal transfer among diverse bacterial populations79. Chloramphenicol resistance genes, including cat1, cat2, and cat3, were also detected across several species, highlighting the persistence of legacy resistance determinants even for antibiotics that are now less commonly used. This persistence may be attributed to co-selection with other resistance genes or continued environmental exposure to related compounds80. The detection of macrolide resistance genes, particularly ermB, is consistent with its recognized role as a dominant macrolide resistance determinant in both clinical and environmental enterococci81. The co-occurrence of ermB with other resistance genes may further enhance the resilience of these populations to antimicrobial pressure.

The identification of glycopeptide resistance genes, notably vanA and vanC, is of particular public health concern, as these genes confer resistance to vancomycin, a last-resort antibiotic for treating multidrug-resistant enterococcal infections82. The presence of these genes in environmental isolates suggests that surface waters can serve as reservoirs and potential sources of vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE), posing a risk for the dissemination of these traits into human and animal populations14. Similarly, the detection of blaTEM, a gene encoding a beta-lactamase that confers resistance to penicillins and some cephalosporins, and various qnr genes, which mediate reduced susceptibility to quinolones, underscores the broad spectrum of resistance mechanisms circulating in these environments83. The diversity and prevalence of these genes reflect the cumulative impact of antibiotic use and environmental contamination, as well as the ability of Enterococcus to acquire and disseminate resistance determinants through horizontal gene transfer84. Notably, the lack of significant spatial variation in resistance gene profiles across sampling locations suggests a widespread distribution of these determinants, likely facilitated by the mixing of bacteria from multiple anthropogenic and natural sources85. This finding is consistent with the concept of the environment as an integrative reservoir for AMR, where resistance genes can persist, accumulate, and be transferred among different bacterial hosts86. The absence of certain resistance genes, such as tetB, tetD, and blaOXA, may reflect local patterns of antibiotic usage, selective pressures, or the competitive dynamics within microbial communities. Overall, the detection of a wide array of antibiotic resistance genes in environmental Enterococcus populations underscores the urgent need for integrated surveillance and management strategies to mitigate the spread of AMR. These findings reinforce the importance of considering environmental reservoirs in the broader context of One Health, recognizing the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health in the fight against AMR.

We acknowledge that the data presented in this study were collected from December 2018 to November 2019, and given the passage of time, there is potential for significant temporal changes in resistance patterns. As microbial resistance can evolve rapidly, we recommend follow-up studies to track any shifts in resistance patterns since the time of sampling. Such investigations will be crucial for understanding current trends and adapting strategies for effective management of resistance.

Conclusion

Findings from this study have shown that potable water is a great challenge within rural communities as most surface waters serve as reservoirs to fecal bacteria due to indiscriminate pollution of these bodies through anthropogenic activities and disposal of effluents. The virulence attributes and antibiotic resistance of the screened Enterococcus species suggest a severe environmental and public health threat. The detection of Enterococcus species with MDR and efflux pump determinants represents a potential threat to consumer safety consequent of difficult-to-treat infection culminating in prolonged hospital stays. Data from this study could aid health professionals in evaluating risk assessment to minimize health casualties while improving consumer safety.

Materials and methods

Description of the study site

The study area comprised two surface water bodies, Ovokoto Spring and Ojerame Dam, located in Akoko-Edo, Nigeria. The coordinates for the sampling stations at Ovokoto Spring are as follows: Station 1 at 07.29461°N, 006.11497°E; Station 2 at 07.29482°N, 006.11338°E; Station 3 at 07.29631°N, 006.11150°E; and Station 4 at 07.29948°N, 006.10270°E. For Ojerame Dam, the coordinates are: Station 1 at 07.29790°N, 006.15305°E; Station 2 at 07.29647°N, 006.15216°E; Station 3 at 07.27930°N, 006.13980°E; and Station 4 at 07.26403°N, 006.12711°E. Ovokoto spring water is beneficial to the residents of the Akoko-Edo community as a source of drinking water. Some residents in the community worship the Ovokoto god at the source of the Ovokoto spring water (termed station 1). Non-residents of the Ovokoto community are only allowed access to the source water if a resident in the community accompanies them. The locals believe the Ovokoto water contains some spiritual-medicinal characteristics with healing potential. Some food animals, such as chickens and goats, offered to serve the Ovokoto god were found roaming the water source. Taking photos of the source water is also highly prohibited as it is considered an aberration of the customs and traditions of the locals. The other three stations (stations 2, 3, and 4), which are extensions from station 1, were different points where the locals usually fetch water from as well as do some other domestics such as bathing, agricultural activities and washing of clothes (stations 3 and 4). During the dry season (November to February), the other three stations from the Ovokoto spring water (stations 2, 3, and 4) are usually dry. However, during the wet season (March to October), all stations (stations 1–4) typically contain water. The Ojerame Dam is used by residents of the Akoko-Edo community as a source of drinking water for humans and their livestock and for other domestic purposes such as washing clothes and bathing. Station 1 of the Dam, represents the Dam point itself with activities such as clothes washing, swimming, and bathing similar to the activities of station 2. However, in station 3 of the Ojerame Dam, the Akoko-Edo residents carry out some palm oil milling activities and indigenous food fermentation processing (garri and fufu processes) and discharge the liquid waste directly into the water body. In addition, a huge solid waste site is beside the route where the water flows from station 3 to station 4.

Sample collection

Samples were obtained monthly from December 2018 - November 2019 and categorized based on seasons. The wet season was classified as the months of rainfall (March to October), while the dry season was classified as the period where rainfall ceases (November to February). Samples were obtained from Ovokoto Spring water and Ojerame Dam from 4 stations each. The source for each was classified as station 1, while stations 2, 3, and 4 were classified as points as the water flows down. Due to closeness, water samples were collected in sterile 1-litre plastic containers from each location monthly. The samples were then transported in less than 6–8 h of sampling to the Applied Microbial Processes and Environmental Health Research laboratory for microbiological analyses.

Enumeration of fecal coliforms, total coliforms, Enterococcus species, and isolation of Enterococcus species

For the isolation of Enterococcus and coliforms, the membrane filtering technique was employed. In summary, 100 mL of sampled water was passed through sterilized filter membranes with a pore size of 0.45 μm. The filtered sample was then transferred onto m-Endo agar LES and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Similarly, a 100 mL water sample was passed through sterilized filter membranes with a pore size of 0.45 μm. The filtered sample was then transferred onto m-Fc agar and incubated at 44.5 °C for 24 h. Colonies from m-Endo agar LES that showed dark red from pink alongside a green metallic sheen top were enumerated as total coliforms and expressed in CFU/100 mL. Colonies from m-Fc agar that were blue were enumerated as fecal coliforms and expressed in CFU/100 mL.

In contrast, dark hollow colonies from bile esculin azide agar were enumerated as Enterococcus species and described in CFU/100 mL. An average of 5 to 10 sheen colonies on the filter of m-Endo agar LES, as well as an average of 5 to 10 blue colonies on the filter of m-Fc agar, are inoculated into tubes of lauryl sulphate tryptose (LST) broth, and incubation at 37 °C for 48 h was done. Positive gas for 48 h on LST tubes was incubated at 37 °C while subculturing on brilliant green lactose bile broth (BGLB). The 48-h gas production in BGLB was utilized to validate the coliform test. Where dark hollow colonies from bile esculin azide agar were obtained, a minimum of 1 and maximum of 3 well-isolated dark hollow appearances were aseptically purified using nutrient agar using the plate streaking method, incubation for 24 h at 37 °C was done. Purified Enterococcus species were confirmed using the Analytical Profile Index (API) 20E. Analysis was carried out following the producer’s instructions. Data was interpreted via API database (V4.1) using apiwebTM identification software. Pure and verified Enterococcus species were stored at 4 °C in slants of nutrient agar until needed for future investigations.

DNA extraction anhead2d polymerase chain reaction amplification of Enterococcus species

The broth of tryptone soy was used to revive DNA extracted from purified isolates, and incubation was at 37 °C for 18 h. Deoxyribonucleic acid extraction was performed utilizing the Bacterial DNA kit of PeqGold according to their producers’ instructions. The quality and amount of the extracted total genomic DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop TM 1000 Spectrophotometer. PCR amplification for detecting Enterococcus species followed the procedures and conditions previously adopted6. Supplementary Table S1 lists the primers utilized to identify Enterococcus species. The identification process included the utilization of positive controls, such as E. faecium (ATCC 19434), E. faecalis (ATCC 19433), E. casseliflavus (ATCC 25788), E. hirae (ATCC 8043), E. gallinarum (ATCC 700425), and E. durans (ATCC 19432). Deionized water was used as a substitute for the amplified product during each test. The amplification procedure used a Peltier-Based Thermal Cycler (MG96/Y, Zhejiang, China). PCR amplicons were electrophoresed at 100 V for 55 min on agarose gel of 1.5% and buffer of 0.5 TAE (CLS-AG100, Warwickshire, United Kingdom). The UV transilluminator (EBOX VX5, Vilber Lourmat, France) was used to view the gels.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Following the recommendations of the Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute (CLSI)87 the Enterococcus species antibiotic susceptibility profile was carried out using broth dilution assay. When the cells are comparable to 106 CFU/mL, the optical density (OD) of the turbidity of the broth was found to agree with an optical density of the McFarland standard. Microplates (96 wells) containing 50µL of serially diluted antibiotics were prepared. An aliquot of 50µL of the standardized bacterial was aseptically dispensed into each well, mixed gently, and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The most negligible concentration of antibiotic, inhibiting visible growth after incubation, was regarded as the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). Positive controls of E. faecalis (ATCC 19433) and E. faecium (ATCC 19434) were used. The antibiotics used in this study obtained from mast diagnostics in the United Kingdom include penicillins [penicillin (10 units), ampicillin (10 µg)], glycopeptides [vancomycin (30 µg), teicoplanin (30 µg)], macrolide [erythromycin (15 µg)], tetracyclines [tetracycline (30 µg), minocycline (30 µg)], fluoroquinolones [ciprofloxacin (5 µg), levofloxacin (5 µg), gatifloxacin (5 µg)], nitrofurantoin [nitrofurantoin (300 µg)], ansamycin [rifampin (5 µg)], phenicol [chloramphenicol (30 µg)], streptogramin [quinupristin-dalfopristin (15 µg)], oxazolidinone [linezolid (30 µg)] and aminoglycoside [high-level of resistance (HLR) to gentamicin (120 µg)].

Virulence factor formation with biofilm formation

The gelatin liquefaction technique determined how much gelatinase would be produced as earlier described88. Cultures that did not solidify were thought to have successfully hydrolyzed gelatin. On 5% sheep blood agar plates, enterococci isolates were deemed to have α-hemolysis by observing a clear zone of hydrolysis surrounding the colonies where inoculation took place, which allowed for the identification of complete cell lysis89. Brain heart infusion broth (Lab M, Lancashire UK) supplemented with 1.0 g of agar-agar (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) per 100 ml was used to analyze hyaluronidase produced through spot inoculations. In addition, an aqueous solution containing 2 mg/ml of hyaluronic acid and a 5% (w/v) bovine albumin fraction was filtered through 0.20 μm filters and inoculated into the media. Zones of clearance all over the bacteria colonies were said to have positive results90. The microtitre plate method was used to evaluate biofilm formation91,92.

Virulence, antibiotic resistance, and efflux pump genes screening

Virulence genes (Vgs) screened via PCR for the PCR-identified Enterococcus species include gelatinase (gelE), aggregation substance (asa), cytolysin A (cylA), hyaluronidase (hyl), and extracellular surface protein (esp). These Vgs were screened using amplification conditions and procedures previously used6,7,33. The primers and annealing conditions for the Vgs are listed in Table S1. Biofilm-related genes, which include adrA (cellulose synthesized protein), csgD (production of curli fimbriae proteins), and sdiA (involved in quorum sensing), were screened for biofilm-forming Enterococcus species using PCR. The annealing conditions and procedures used to detect the biofilm-related genes are listed Table S1. Only Enterococcus species resistant to selected antibiotics were screened for antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs). ARGs screened include tetracycline (tetA, tetB, tetC, tetD, tetG, tetM), chloramphenicol (cat1, cat2, cat3), erythromycin (ermA, ermB, ermC), vancomycin (vanA, vanB, vanC−1, vanC−2/3), beta-lactam (blaTEM, blaPSE, blaOXA), aminoglycoside (aadB, aacC), quinolones (qnrA, qnrB, qnrC, qnrD, and qnrS). All isolates were tested for possession of efflux pump genes (EPGs) via polymerase chain reaction using amplification conditions and procedures described previously51. EPGs screened include mefA, tetK, tetL, and msrC. The primers for the identification of EPGs are in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel 2013 and the analytic program IBM SPSS version 21.0 were used for data analysis. The Duncan Multiple Range Test was performed to identify the reason for the significant difference after the One-Way Analysis of Variance was used to screen for it. Additionally, correlation analysis was done to demonstrate how one variable affects another. Statistics were judged significant for probability values under 0.05.
