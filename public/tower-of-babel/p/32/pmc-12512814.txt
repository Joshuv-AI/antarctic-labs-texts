Enhancement of shared decision-making competencies in diabetes care using the BOPPPS model: a training program design for general practitioners
Authors: Hongyan Song, Panpan Feng, Kai Lin, Xiaoning Han, Xiting Wang, Mi Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes mellitus is one of the most prevalent chronic diseases worldwide. China has the highest number of diabetic cases worldwide. However, diabetes mellitus is associated with poor awareness, treatment, and control rates. Thus, strategies such as shared decision-making (SDM) are needed to improve diabetes management. In China, SDM training tailored for general practitioners (GPs) is limited, hindering its clinical application. This study aimed to develop and evaluate a diabetes-specific SDM training program for Chinese GPs to increase their communication and decision-making skills in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) care.MethodsTen GPs from Xicheng District, Beijing, participated in the program. The curriculum, which focused on reflective learning theory, was structured using the BOPPPS model (Bridge-in, Objective, Pre-assessment, Participatory Learning, Post-assessment, Summary) in a 4-session (160-min) training program and incorporated group discussions, role-playing, case analyses, and decision aids. Training effectiveness was assessed using quantitative methods (writing tests, role-playing evaluations, and surveys) and qualitative focus group interviews. Statistical and thematic analyses were performed using SPSS and NVivo, respectively.ResultsThe test scores after training were significantly higher than those before training (p = 0.028). Role-playing assessments demonstrated that most participants mastered SDM core elements. Moreover, survey assessments indicated high levels of satisfaction and perceived practicality of the training. Qualitative feedback revealed enhanced attitudes, knowledge, and skills with positive impacts on clinical practice. The participants recommended increasing case complexity, optimizing scheduling, and establishing feedback mechanisms for further refinement.ConclusionThis SDM training program effectively improved the competencies of GPs in diabetes care, offering a novel educational framework for T2DM management. Although this study has several limitations (small sample size and single-city focus), it provides a foundation for scaling up training, evaluating long-term outcomes, and refining curricula to enhance diabetes care quality and patient health outcomes as a pilot study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-02995-8.Keywords: Shared decision-making, BOPPPS model, Diabetes care, General practitioners, Training program

Introduction

The prevalence of diabetes mellitus, a major global health challenge, is increasing disproportionately relative to population growth. Surveillance data revealed that the number of diabetes cases in 2021 was 529 million, which is projected to reach 1.31 billion by 2050 [1]. Uncontrolled diabetes promotes severe microvascular (such as retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy) and macrovascular complications (including cardiovascular disease and stroke). These complications contribute to 31.8 million disability-adjusted life-years worldwide [2, 3]. Thus, diabetes is the eighth leading cause of mortality and disability [1, 4]. China has the highest number of diabetes cases (140 million cases), accounting for 25% of the global prevalence [5]. Additionally, diabetes is the sixth leading cause of mortality in China [6], and its incidence continues to grow [7]. This growth occurs despite significant systemic gaps, with only 36.7%, 32.9%, and 50.1% of cases being diagnosed, receiving evidence-based treatment, and achieving glycemic control, respectively [8]. These limitations highlight the need for innovative strategies to enhance diabetes management and patient engagement in care.

Shared decision-making (SDM) is a critical strategy for addressing the escalating burden of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [9]. Chinese clinical guidelines and international consensus from the American Diabetes Association and European Association for the Study of Diabetes (from 2012 onward) mandate patient-centered care, which is defined as clinical decision-making guided by individual preferences, needs, and values [10, 11]. SDM, which is the gold standard for operationalizing patient-centered care [12, 13], is a collaborative process in which clinicians and patients evaluate evidence-based options through structured risk-benefit discussions, integrating clinical guidelines with personal values and preferences [14, 15]. Previous studies have demonstrated the multidimensional benefits of SDM, including improved treatment adherence [16], enhanced quality of life [17], reduced decision conflict [18], optimized chronic disease self-management, and decreased healthcare costs [19]. Thus, SDM can address the escalating burden of T2DM through patient empowerment and resource-efficient care. However, the implementation of SDM in China’s primary care system faces multifaceted challenges spanning operational, cultural, and competency gaps. Structural barriers include severe time constraints (2–3 min consultations [20] versus the 8–23 min required for effective SDM with decision aids [21]), low utilization rates of decision aids, and a critical shortage of culturally adapted tools [22]. These systemic deficits are compounded by cultural incongruence—family-centric decision norms conflict with SDM’s collaborative ethos—while hierarchical traditions predispose 72% of patients to defer to relatives or physicians for major health choices [20, 23]. Furthermore, training fragmentation persists, with no standardized national protocols to equip general practitioners (GPs) with SDM competencies [23, 24]. This is particularly detrimental in low-resource, high-diabetes-incidence settings where GPs often lack both confidence and skills to implement SDM effectively [25, 26].

To address these gaps, a culturally adapted SDM training program for GPs has been proposed, which is based on the BOPPPS model. The BOPPPS instructional model [27, 28] has distinct advantages over traditional frameworks such as analysis → design → development → implementation → evaluation (ADDIE) for cultivating SDM competencies, as evidenced by three key contrasts: (1) traditional lecture-based sessions, which lack real-time feedback and deliberate practice, and (2) the ADDIE model, whose front-loaded design delays iterative skill rehearsal. By embedding formative assessment and participatory activities in every session, the BOPPPS is uniquely suited for SDM training, which depends on immediate feedback and iterative refinement. Although the BOPPPS model is widely used in undergraduate medical education, its application for GP training in diabetes care has not been previously explored. The curriculum integrates evidence-based risk communication, contextualized SDM simulations, and locally validated tools to bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical application. This culturally tailored approach seeks to enhance the implementation of SDM in primary care, improving T2DM management and patient outcomes.

This study designed and evaluated a BOPPPS model-based training program to enhance general practitioners’ SDM competence in diabetes care, with the pilot phase specifically assessing feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary signals of behavioral change. The training program was implemented to increase the ability of GPs to communicate effectively with patients, identify the preferences and values of patients, and involve patients in the decision-making process for diabetes management. The findings of this study will contribute to improvements in diabetes care and patient outcomes in primary healthcare settings. The SDM approach empowers patients to take an active role in managing their chronic condition. This study provides evidence for the subsequent intervention design of clinical research.

Methods

Study participants and ethical considerations

This study enrolled 10 GPs who participated in the sixth iteration of the Xicheng District General Practitioner Teacher Training Program in Beijing, China, during the program’s active period from March to September 2024. The sample size was determined by three factors: (1) cohort completeness (all 10 GPs in the 2024 program cycle) and (2) alignment with pilot SDM intervention standards. For pilot studies of complex interventions such as SDM training, samples of 10–30 participants are well established in the literature, allowing for meaningful feasibility assessment while maintaining methodological rigor [29, 30]. Since its inception in 2016, this program—supported by the Xicheng District Health Commission—has trained 148 GPs [31]. Xicheng District, a central hub for education, culture, and technology in Beijing, has a population of about 1.1 million (2022 data) and 99 community health centers (general practice clinics) with about 400 GPs [32]. Informed consent was obtained from all GPs to participate in this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals with formal registration as GPs who have completed standardized residency training or transitional training in general practice; (2) individuals with > 1 year of clinical experience in community practice; (3) individuals with an active family physician contract with registered patients diagnosed with diabetes mellitus; and (4) individuals who can commit to completing all components of the training curriculum. This study was performed according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University First Hospital Health Service Center (Ethics Approval No. 2024-Research 529–002). The Xicheng District Health Commission provided institutional support for this research.

Research design

This study involved the design of a training curriculum and its subsequent implementation with outcome evaluation. Reflective learning theory [33] was used to guide the development of the theoretical framework for the SDM training program targeting GPs in diabetes care. This theory, which is also termed transformative learning theory, uses the “deconstruct-reflect-reconstruct” approach to help physicians shift from authoritarian practices to patient-centered collaboration. The three stages of this approach (triggering reflection, critical reflection, and action) drive knowledge-to-behavior transformation. Role-playing, experiential reflection, and simulated practice are used to increase cognitive flexibility, address systemic barriers (e.g., time constraints), and foster a culture of SDM in diabetes care.

The BOPPPS model [29] was adopted to structure course delivery and evaluation. SDM skill development was implemented using the following six steps: bridge-in, enhancing engagement using real SDM videos; objective, focusing on SDM competency; pre-assessment, identifying communication gaps; participatory learning, simulation of clinical scenarios and case studies; post-assessment, conducting multidimensional evaluations; and summary, providing clinical decision trees for ongoing learning. This model aligns with patient-centered chronic care, effectively bridging SDM theory to practice through structured skill transfer.

Bridge-in

In the bridge-in phase, the following three strategies were used to engage learners: Video cases (Excerpt from American College of Cardiology’s SDM video): Relevant clinical videos demonstrating physician-patient SDM were embedded into the course slides at key content points; Question-based prompts: Discussions were initiated with targeted queries (e.g., “Based on the video, how would you define SDM?”); Conceptual explanation: A brief didactic session clarified the significance of SDM and its implementation challenges in clinical practice.

Objective

This study defined learning objectives and expected outcomes. The learning objectives were to enhance the knowledge of SDM principles, refine SDM communication skills, and improve diabetes-specific collaborative decision-making competencies. The expected outcomes were (a) equipping GPs with SDM knowledge and actionable communication strategies to optimize patient-centered care and (b) gathering feedback to refine the training curriculum.

Pre-assessment

A dual-method baseline evaluation was performed in this study. A standardized survey (Appendix 1) was undertaken to assess the pre-existing SDM knowledge, self-reported skills, and clinical experiences of the participants using a questionnaire. A written examination involving multiple-choice and multiple-response questions (Appendix 2) was conducted to test the understanding of core SDM theories and diabetes management concepts.

Participatory learning

The participatory learning phase comprises four interactive components. During small-group discussions, participants (2–4 per group) analyzed clinical SDM cases to identify patient preferences, values, and care priorities. During role-playing simulations, clinicians practiced SDM communication using a structured four-step SDM protocol integrated with digital/paper-based decision aids. The four steps were as follows: Step 1: Invite patient participation (e.g., “Your perspective is critical to this decision”); Step 2: Explain all options, including benefits, risks, and alternatives to treatment; Step 3: Elicit patient preferences and collaboratively evaluate trade-offs; Step 4: align decisions with patient values and establish follow-up plans. Decision aids (such as the Type 2 Diabetes Glycemic Target Tool [34], Diabetes Medication Choice [35], and Statin Choice [36]) and pocket cards serve as compact visual guides (Appendix 3) and concise SDM prompts for clinical workflows. During case-based analysis, real-world clinical scenarios were discussed to contextualize SDM applications. During instructor-led demonstrations, instructors combined case simulations with standardized SDM workflows to model best practices.

Post-assessment

Written examination: The pre-assessment test (Appendix 2) was repeated to measure knowledge retention. Simulated practice assessment: Ten participants were randomized into five dyads for role-playing exercises. Each dyad selected one of three type 2 diabetes clinical cases to simulate SDM consultations, with one GP acting as the clinician and the other as the patient. Peer evaluations (Shared Decision-Making Questionnaire-Patient Version, SDM-Q-9) [37, 38] and clinician self-assessments (SDM-Q-Doc) [39, 40] were collected post-simulation (Appendices 4 and 5). The Chinese versions of both the SDM-Q-9 (patient-reported) [38]and SDM-Q-Doc (physician-reported) [40] instruments were formally developed with authorization from their original developers (Kriston and Scholl, respectively) and published in December 2018 following rigorous cross-cultural adaptation. Psychometric evaluation revealed strong reliability metrics: the SDM-Q-9-Chinese demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.945) and acceptable test-retest reliability (r = 0.319, p < 0.05), whereas the SDM-Q-Doc-Chinese showed similarly robust internal consistency (α = 0.867) and superior test-retest reliability (r = 0.810). These validated Chinese adaptations now serve as psychometrically sound tools for evaluating shared decision-making processes in clinical settings across China. Both tools use a 6-point Likert scale to quantify SDM process quality from dual perspectives. Instructors provided structured feedback, highlighting strengths and addressing gaps in SDM implementation.

Summary

The concluding phase reinforced learning through the following methods: content synthesis: a systematic review of SDM principles and diabetes care applications; experience sharing: participant reflections on clinical practice integration challenges; and resource dissemination: customized SDM tools (e.g., decision aids, clinical algorithms) were recommended for sustained skill development.

Evaluation

After the course, a post-class questionnaire was administered to assess the reactions of the participants. This evaluation covered aspects such as content quality, teaching methods, textbook quality, instructor performance, course organization, and overall satisfaction. The survey instrument is detailed in Appendix 6. A 3-point Likert scale (agree, neutral, or disagree) was used for this assessment.

After the completion of the course, a focus group interview was conducted with 10 GPs who had finished the training. The interviews aimed to collect the experiences, feelings, and suggestions of the GPs regarding the training course. Two researchers assisted in the interview process, with one leading the discussion and the other taking notes and posing supplementary questions. The interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim to ensure the integrity and accuracy of the data. The researchers reviewed and cross-checked the transcriptions. After the participants were informed about the recording process and their privacy was assured, the data were anonymized. All participants provided informed consent.

Data collection and analysis

Data collection

The baseline data of the GPs were collected. Theoretical, practical assessment, and questionnaire data were organized. Video and audio recordings were collected to ensure the accuracy of the data.

Quantitative data analysis

The quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 software. Continuous data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (x ± s). The homogeneity of variance was tested. Means were compared via paired t tests if the data exhibited homogeneity of variance. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Qualitative data analysis

The study employed an inductive analytical approach, allowing themes to emerge directly from the data. The total interview duration was 45 min, and the transcripts were completed within 24 h of the interviews. The recordings were transcribed verbatim and reviewed synchronously by two researchers (SHY and FPP) to correct any errors. The transcripts were then returned to the participants for feedback to verify accuracy. All three team members—SHY, FPP (holding a master’s degree in general practice), and MY (holding a PhD in general practice)—possessed extensive experience in qualitative research. The transcribed texts were imported into NVivo12, where both researchers independently conducted open coding.

Before performing line-by-line coding analysis, we independently reviewed the interview transcripts multiple times to understand their content. Using “semantic paragraphs” as the smallest unit, we identified keywords and phrases as analytical units to generate initial codes.

The coding process followed the six-step thematic analysis framework proposed by Braun and Clarke [41], encompassing data familiarization, initial code generation, theme construction, potential theme review, theme definition and naming, and report writing. Discrepancies in data analysis were resolved through structured procedures. When disagreements arose, a third researcher (MY) was introduced as an arbitrator. The three researchers (SHY, FPP, and MY) engaged in detailed discussions to collectively review the transcribed texts and understand the rationale behind each coding decision. They subsequently reexamined relevant sections of the interview transcripts and reassessed coding decisions based on new insights gained during consultations. This iterative process ensured comprehensive consideration of all the perspectives, ultimately resulting in coding decisions backed by robust data support. A total of 109 initial codes were generated.

Subtheme refinement: Sub-themes were identified through code review and data integration. The initial coding was clustered into provisional sub-themes, resulting in 10 identified sub-themes.

Theme development: Three core themes were synthesized from these sub-themes, with relevant citations selected from the text to highlight them.

The final analysis, including themes, sub-themes, and citations, was reviewed by two interviewees for feedback. As no significant objections were raised, the credibility and authenticity of the research conclusions were confirmed.

Written test results

The pre-training and post-training written test scores of all GPs were analyzed. All 10 primary care GPs completed the test assessments. A paired samples t test revealed a statistically significant improvement in diabetes-related SDM competency scores following training, with posttest scores (M = 80.50, SD = 6.43) significantly higher than pre-test scores (M = 72.00, SD = 12.07), t(9) = 2.61, p = 0.028 (two-tailed), demonstrating a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.83, 95% CI [0.09, 1.54]), with complete statistical outputs verified in Appendix 7.

Role-Playing

After the role-playing exercise, the GPs conducted peer evaluations. The “patients” used the SDM-Q-9 to evaluate performance, assigning numerical values (0 = strongly disagree, 1 = somewhat disagree, 2 = slightly disagree, 3 = slightly agree, 4 = somewhat agree, 5 = strongly agree). GPs also conducted self-evaluations using the SDM-Q-Doc (scoring system as above). The scores are shown in Tables 3 and 4. The “Patient” rating assessment revealed that 80% of the physicians scored in the moderate or higher range (≥ 50 points), indicating generally positive evaluations of SDM implementation. The “Doctor” rating assessment revealed that 100% of the physicians self-rated their performance in the moderate or higher range (≥ 50 points), demonstrating their confidence in effective SDM implementation. These results suggest that the SDM process was successfully adopted by GPs and that they mastered the essential elements of SDM after training.

Questionnaire

After training, a questionnaire was administered to assess the perceptions and satisfaction of the GPs with the training. Feedback was collected on various aspects, including the training content, practicality, ease of understanding, communication and interaction, training atmosphere, case selection, participation, and training effectiveness. All 10 GPs who received the training completed the questionnaire. Among the GPs, 80% selected positive comments for each question. None of the GPs selected negative comments. One GP remained neutral regarding the following three aspects: “The training content can guide clinical SDM practice,” “The case selection for this training is reasonable,” and “This training met my expectations.” Another GP remained neutral regarding “The training atmosphere was lively and the program was interactive” and “The participants were highly engaged in the training process.” These findings indicate that the training provided a good learning experience and achieved satisfactory outcomes.

Themes

Semi-structured focus group interviews were conducted to obtain the experiences and suggestions of GPs for improving training. The following three main themes were identified from the interview data (Appendix 8): training experience, impact of training, and feedback on training.

Training experience

Most doctors deemed the course design to be meticulous and practical, with easily acceptable implementation and innovative teaching methods.

Impact of training

Most doctors recognized the importance of SDM in clinical practice and reported improvements in their attitudes, knowledge, and skills.

Feedback on training

The suggestions by GPs included increasing the number and complexity of cases in the course design, introducing detailed background information in case studies, optimizing the schedule, and advance notification of course timing and content, adding future feedback mechanisms, and considering challenges encountered during implementation.

Discussion

This study developed a training framework for GPs in China using the BOPPPS model to improve their SDM capabilities in managing T2DM. This training offers innovative strategies and practical guidance for diabetes management.

Participation, acceptance, and learning experiences were the critical factors influencing the effectiveness of this training program. The program significantly improved the SDM knowledge and skills of GPs. Comparative analysis of pre-training and post-training test scores revealed that the training enhanced the understanding of SDM principles and practices. Role-playing exercises and subsequent evaluations using the SDM-Q-9 and SDM-Q-Doc further confirmed the ability of GPs to implement SDM in simulated interactions, with most GPs achieving high scores. These findings indicate the strong educational impact and feasibility of the program. The questionnaire responses revealed high overall satisfaction with GPs reporting that the content was practical and accessible.

Qualitative feedback identified the following three main themes: training experience, impact on clinical practice, and suggestions for improvement. The GPs praised the course design, noting improved communication skills and the clinical value of SDM. GPs also offered valuable suggestions, including increasing case complexity, optimizing the training process, and introducing feedback mechanisms to align the program with clinical needs.

This study implemented diverse teaching strategies within the BOPPPS model framework under reflective learning theory, integrating case-based teaching, group discussions, role-playing, standardized role-playing demonstrations, and video presentations to achieve complementary advantages and teaching objectives. In particular, role-playing, standardized role-playing demonstrations, and video presentations were deemed effective by the participants.

Role-playing, which served as a pivotal nexus between the BOPPPS model and reflective learning, was the central component of the participatory learning phase. Additionally, role-playing simulated diabetes care dilemmas, compelling clinicians to integrate medical evidence with patient preferences under time constraints. In the reflective learning cycle, role-playing exposes skill gaps during debriefing sessions, triggering critical self-assessment and behavioral refinement. The participants resolved practical challenges that are often overlooked in didactic teaching, with peer and instructor feedback being vital for skill enhancement [30, 42, 43]. The discrepancy between the “patient” evaluation of the physician self-assessment score and the physician self-assessment score in Group 2 reflects potential contributing factors: (1) participant-specific challenges in Group 2 (GP6 as patient/GP2 as physician), where qualitative feedback revealed comprehension gaps in clinical explanations, rubric understanding, and role-play recall; (2) fundamental clinician-patient assessment divergences; and (3) systematic physician overestimation of SDM implementation in self-evaluations. This multifactorial variance underscores the critical need for multisource assessment methodologies in competency evaluation.

The study further integrated case-based learning with instructor-led standardized role-playing demonstrations, guiding GPs to reflect on clinical challenges and adopt evidence-based SDM workflows. This “case-analysis-simulation-feedback-demonstration” approach improved SDM knowledge retention, practical application, and theory-practice integration of GPs. The findings of this study are consistent with those of previous studies, which reported that role-playing enhances teaching quality [30, 44], fosters collaborative learning environments [42, 44, 45], reduces performance anxiety through error-safe practice [46], and cultivates empathy and patient trust [47]. In this study, role-playing strengthened patient-centered communication, directly supporting SDM goals. In particular, role-playing serves dual functions of instruction and assessment [47, 48]. Role-playing immerses clinicians in simulated diabetes consultations to internalize SDM techniques and identify competency gaps (e.g., overlooked nonverbal cues) that cannot be determined using written exams. The contextual, practice-oriented design met learner needs, enhancing the efficacy of SDM training.

Videos are useful visual aids. In the bridge-in phase, contrasting SDM/non-SDM consultation videos induced cognitive dissonance, activating learning motivation. The participants praised videos for clarity, repeatability, and accessibility. Video-based learning is reported to enhance knowledge retention, confidence, and procedural mastery [49–53]. The videos used in this study explicitly demonstrated SDM steps, comparative outcomes, and patient-centered communication nuances, providing actionable guidance for clinical practice.

The integration of decision aids and pocket cards represented a novel synergy of the structured outputs and reflective practice of the BOPPPS. Pocket cards used in the post-assessment phase codify SDM workflows for sustained knowledge reinforcement. Decision aids, which are designed to present treatment options, risks/benefits, and patient values [54, 55], streamlined SDM discussions and improved patient engagement and satisfaction [54, 56, 57]. In contrast to previously reported tools with singular focuses, the aids used in this study integrated multifactorial considerations (e.g., comorbidities, lifestyle) into visual, algorithm-driven guides. Clinicians reported high usability and seamless integration into workflows, with many adopting pocket cards as quality assurance tools for SDM implementation.

Suggestions for improvement

Focus group interviews with GPs revealed actionable recommendations for course refinement. First, the participants advocated enhancing complexity and case volume. The current role-playing scenarios feature limited case diversity and simplistic content. GPs proposed the incorporation of challenging clinical simulations, such as patients withholding medical histories or questioning treatment plans, to increase interactive difficulty and deepen discussions. These adaptations aim to mirror real-world clinical complexities, strengthening the application of SDM. Second, GPs emphasized optimizing training schedules and providing advanced notification of course content. Pre-course communication of timelines and content outlines was recommended to facilitate participant preparation and role immersion. Third, GPs highlighted the integration of practical needs and the establishment of feedback mechanisms. The high workloads and time constraints in community practice can potentially hinder SDM implementation. Thus, future iterations could prioritize targeted decision protocols aligned with clinical realities. Embedding structured feedback channels enables iterative content optimization based on practitioner experience. Future studies should investigate the long-term impacts of the program and evaluate the feasibility of periodic refresher courses to sustain SDM competencies. These iterative enhancements can ensure that the evolution of the dynamic curriculum is aligned with evolving healthcare demands. Training efficacy can be further optimized via the following strategies: (1) increasing case complexity and volume; (2) instituting pre-course task notifications; and (3) modernizing decision aids through artificial intelligence-powered app tools to increase usability and visual communication. Thus, there is a need for continuous pedagogical adaptation to meet shifting clinical priorities and patient expectations.

Significance of the study

This study advances novel perspectives and methodologies in diabetes SDM training. Previous studies have focused predominantly on fragmented approaches to SDM skill development for GPs. In contrast, this study established a systematic training framework through the structured closed-loop design of the BOPPPS model along with multimodal teaching strategies. To optimize the educational paradigm, the integration of diverse pedagogical strategies effectively facilitates the transition of the decision-making mindset of GPs from experience-dependent to patient-centered models. We also contrasted our findings with those of Légaré et al. [58] and Körner et al. [59] by highlighting two key differences: our single 160-min BOPPPS workshop achieved post-training SDM-Q gains comparable to their multi-session, 3–6-month programs, demonstrating superior time efficiency, whereas both prior studies targeted hospital-based specialists, we worked with community GPs who began with lower baseline SDM exposure, illustrating broader applicability and underscoring the unique contribution of our pilot approach. In primary care practice, enhanced SDM competencies improve the self-management efficacy of patients, offering actionable solutions to diabetes management challenges. Finally, the modular design of this training system aligns with continuing medical education requirements for GPs, shifting educational focus from knowledge dissemination to competency cultivation. This transformation directly addresses the National Health Commission’s “Healthy China 2030” mandate for core GP competencies. The modular architecture further provides a scalable template, potentially extending to other chronic disease management training programs to strengthen holistic care delivery.

Limitations and future directions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to develop and implement an SDM training course for diabetes care in China. This study has several limitations. The study cohort was from only one city in China, which may not reflect the perspectives and training needs of doctors in other regions. The small sample size (10 GPs) limits the generalizability and accuracy of the findings. Future validation through multicenter studies employing larger, more heterogeneous cohorts is warranted to confirm these preliminary observations. Our pilot study did not include a control group. Therefore, the observed improvements could be partly attributable to time effects or Hawthorne effects. Future studies should adopt a cluster randomized controlled trial (RCT) design to address potential confounding factors systematically. Additionally, the long-term effects of the course and its applicability in different healthcare settings must be validated. Future studies should test the program in broader clinical contexts and collect long-term follow-up data. The findings of this study may not completely reflect real-world clinical practice, as the study relied on self-reported data and simulated patient interactions. Direct observations of patient consultations must be incorporated in the future to assess the actual implementation of SDM skills by GPs in clinical settings, which will provide a comprehensive evaluation of the long-term impact of training on patient health outcomes.

Conclusions

This study developed and evaluated an SDM training program for GPs by integrating multimodal teaching strategies within the BOPPPS model framework. The intervention enhanced the SDM competencies of GPs in terms of cognitive understanding and practical application, establishing a novel educational paradigm for diabetes management. To advance clinical communication and patient engagement, we recommend scaling up this pedagogical approach in medical education. Furthermore, the modular architecture of this program provides a transferable template for future chronic disease management training initiatives, promoting holistic care delivery.
