A systematic analysis of the gender disparities in oncology journal editorial boards
Authors: Taha Koray Sahin, Ilgın Koc Kus, Bahadir Koylu, Firat Sirvan, Neyran Kertmen, Seyda Gunduz, Deniz Can Guven
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite growing emphasis on equity and diversity, women remain underrepresented in academic leadership roles, especially on the editorial boards of scientific journals. The composition of editorial boards plays a major role in shaping the peer-review process, research dissemination, and academic advancement. While gender disparities have been documented in many specialties, the data from oncology journals remain scarce.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 310 oncology journals indexed in the InCites Journal Citation Reports. Data were manually extracted from journal websites between April and May 2025.Variables included the number and gender of editorial board members and editors-in-chief, Journal Impact Factor (JIF) quartile, SCIE/ESCI indexing, journal and publisher continent, professional society affiliation.ResultsAmong 21,065 editorial board members, 5,592 (26.5%) were women, with representation among editors-in-chief even lower at 89 out of 378 (23.5%). Female representation was highest in Q1 journals (30.5%) and lowest in Q4 journals (17.9%) (p < 0.001). Geographical disparities were prominent: women represented 49.5% of editorial board members in U.S.-based journals, compared to 10.1% in Asia and 1.5% in Australia (p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, absence of a female editor-in-chief (OR:2.77, 95%CI:1.61–4.75; p < 0.001) and lack of professional association affiliation (OR:1.78, 95%CI: 1.11–2.85; p = 0.017) were independently associated with gender disparity.ConclusionGender disparities remain prominent across oncology journal editorial boards, particularly in leadership roles and journals affiliated with Asia. Effective strategies are needed to increase efforts to promote equity in academic leadership, as well as improve transparency in the editorial selection process so inclusivity and diverse perspectives can be integrated into oncology research.Keywords: Gender, Disparity, Editorial board, Oncology, Journal

Background

In recent decades, gender equity and diversity in academic medicine have received increasing attention [1]. The proportion of women entering the medical workforce has increased considerably, but this representation has not translated to comparable gains in academic leadership roles [2]. Gender disparities in academic medicine reflect structural and institutional inequalities in representation, evaluation, and advancement rather than differences in individual merit [3, 4]. These disparities are shaped by gender role expectations that influence perceptions of competence and leadership suitability.

Role congruity theory posits that prejudice toward female leaders arises from a perceived mismatch between communal traits stereotypically associated with women and the agentic traits associated with leadership [5]. This perceived incongruity results in both lower perceived suitability for leadership and more negative evaluations of women’s leadership behaviors. The “glass ceiling” metaphor further describes invisible barriers limiting women’s advancement into top academic positions despite formal equality in promotion systems [6]. In parallel, the “leaky pipeline” phenomenon illustrates how women disproportionately exit or stagnate at successive career stages due to cumulative structural disadvantages [7]. Together, these frameworks indicate that increased female representation at entry levels alone is insufficient to achieve equity without institutional transformation.

Across biomedical fields, there is still a discrepancy in representation of women in positions of power and influence such as department chairs, society leaders, grant review panels, and especially on the editorial boards of scientific journals [8, 9]. Editorial positions provide not only an academic prestige but also influence over research priorities, peer review, and publication decisions, making representation in these positions a critical component of equity in academia [10, 11]. Editorial boards consist of editors-in-chief, associate or section editors, and board members selected based on scholarly productivity, prior reviewing experience, and disciplinary reputation, who oversee peer review, adjudicate decisions, and ensure ethical and reporting standards [12, 13]. Through these roles, they function as gatekeepers of scientific knowledge, shaping research visibility and prestige; thus, underrepresentation in editorial positions may perpetuate cumulative disadvantages in academic recognition, promotion, and funding opportunities [14].

Within this broader context, disparities in editorial board composition have been increasingly reported in recent years [15, 16]. Studies across multiple medical specialties have consistently shown that women hold a smaller proportion of editor-in-chief, associate editor, and general editorial board positions compared with their male counterparts [17–20]. This lack of diversity may perpetuate systemic inequities through multiple interacting mechanisms. Editorial underrepresentation can constrain women’s influence over the definition of research priorities and methodological standards, while homophily in editorial assignment and peer review processes may inadvertently reproduce existing gender networks [14, 21]. In addition, leadership visibility shapes aspirational pathways; persistent male dominance in editorial leadership may reinforce stereotypes regarding authority and expertise. Importantly, these mechanisms are dynamic rather than deterministic [6]. While the presence of women editors does not automatically eliminate bias, empirical evidence suggests that greater gender representation among editors correlates with increased representation among peer reviewers, highlighting the interconnected nature of publishing systems [21].

Oncology is among the most research-intensive disciplines in academic medicine, characterized by a high volume of scientific publications, substantial global research funding, and a central role in shaping clinical guidelines and translational research priorities [22, 23]. Given its inherently broad and multidisciplinary structure, oncology encompasses medical, surgical, and radiation oncology as well as related interdisciplinary domains such as psycho-oncology, oncology nursing, and rehabilitation. Integrating multiple subspecialties across the continuum from basic to clinical research, oncology represents a highly representative framework for evaluating equity in academic leadership [24–26]. Consequently, inequities in editorial leadership within oncology journals have implications that extend beyond a single specialty, influencing research visibility, priority setting, and the broader dissemination of cancer science worldwide.

In oncology, similar gender disparities have been identified. Specifically, Dai et al. found that among the leading oncology journals, women made up just 24% of editorial board members, with 20% serving as editors-in-chief [27]. Likewise, Patel et al. demonstrated that women represented only 27.4% of editorial board members and 15.9% of editor-in-chief positions in hematology and oncology journals, with women essentially absent from editorial leadership positions [28]. These findings are consistent with patterns observed in other medical fields, indicating that structural and cultural barriers still play a role in women's lack of progression into the senior editorial role [29]. Structural and cultural barriers underlying these disparities vary across sociocultural contexts. Ecological systems approaches suggest that leadership emergence is shaped by interactions across individual, organizational, and societal levels [30]. Gender stereotypes, implicit bias, differential sponsorship, and institutional norms may operate differently in Western versus Asian or other regional academic systems. Cross-national analyses emphasize that gender inequities are embedded within broader social hierarchies and cultural expectations, necessitating contextualized interpretation of findings [30, 31]. Despite growing awareness of these issues, comprehensive evaluations of gender representation across the full spectrum of oncology journals remain limited. Previous studies have focused on high-impact or subspecialty journals, often with a limited scope [27]. Given the central role of oncology journals in shaping clinical guidelines, research dissemination, and global cancer policy, inequities in editorial leadership may influence research visibility, agenda-setting processes, and the epistemic direction of cancer science worldwide [32]. Accordingly, we aimed to systematically examine the gender composition of editorial boards and editors-in-chief across all oncology journals.

Methods

Study design and journal selection

This cross-sectional, descriptive-analytical study evaluated the gender composition of editorial boards and editors-in-chief of oncology journals. The list of journals was obtained from the InCites Journal Citation Reports (available via ClarivateTM, London, UK) under the “Oncology” category. Large-volume open access publishers that appoint transitional editors for each special issue or topic were excluded to avoid potential confounding due to temporary or rotating editorial structures. Journals that had ceased publication or did not provide publicly accessible information on their editorial boards were also excluded.

Data collection

Data were collected between April and May 2025 through a systematic review of the official websites of each journal. All journal websites were accessed manually by the study investigators; no automated web-scraping techniques or algorithm-based extraction tools were employed. Three independent reviewers (TKS, IKK and BK) extracted information on editorial board composition, focusing on sections such as “Editorial Board,” “Editorial Committee,” “Editors,” or “Associate Editors.” For the purposes of analysis, associate editors and similarly titled roles (including section editors, handling editors, and editorial committee members) were categorized as editorial board members. For each journal, journal characteristics were retrieved from the Clarivate Analytics Web of Science JCR 2023. Information was recorded on the number of editors-in-chief, the number of editorial board members, and the gender of each editor. Professional association affiliation was determined based on explicit statements on journal websites indicating official sponsorship, ownership, or governance by a recognized professional society or association (e.g., “official journal of” or “published on behalf of”). Journal impact factor (JIF) was obtained from the 2023 JCR, and journals were classified as having a high impact factor if the JIF was ≥ 2.8, corresponding to the median impact factor of the included oncology journals.

Gender classification

Gender classification was achieved in a systematic manner and followed the process adopted in prior bibliometric studies [20, 33]. Due to reliance on publicly available data, gender was classified into binary categories (male or female), and the study did not assess sexual orientation, gender identity, or LGBTQ + status. Initially, the gender of each editorial board member was determined based on publicly available information on journal websites, including first names, photographs, institutional profiles, and gender-specific pronouns. When this information was insufficient, internet searches were conducted to clarify gender identity. If the gender could not be determined, a web–based online service (https://genderize.io/) was used to determine the gender. Any discrepancies were resolved by majority consensus among the three reviewers (TKS,IKK, and BK). Individuals whose gender could not be established according to these two methods were excluded from the analysis. Overall, inter-reviewer reliability was high, with a intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.984 (95% CI 0.981–0.987), indicating strong agreement among the three independent reviewers (TKS,IKK and BK).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the overall gender distribution across journals. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact or Chi-square tests, and continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated to examine associations between female representation and journal characteristics such as impact factor and age. Gender disparity was determined based on the overall mean proportion of female editorial board members. Multivariable logistic regression models were then used to identify independent predictors of higher female representation on editorial boards, including quartile ranking, impact factor, publisher country, and year of establishment. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 24, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

General characteristics of oncology journals and editorial boards

A total of 322 oncology journals indexed in the Clarivate Analytics Web of Science were initially identified. After excluding large-volume open access publishers with transitional editors, journals that did not provide any information regarding their editorial board members on their websites, and journals that had ceased publication, 310 journals were included in the final analysis. Across these journals, there were 378 editors-in-chief and 21,065 editorial board members (Table 1). The proportion of women among editors-in-chief was 23.5% (n = 89), which was even lower than the representation observed among editorial board members (26.5%; n = 5,592) (Fig. 1). Editorial board members represented 43 different countries (Fig. 2). Sixty-three journals (20.3%) had more than one editor-in-chief. Only 85 journals (27.4%) had at least one female editor-in-chief. Only 28 journals (9%) had editorial boards with female representation exceeding 50%. These journals were predominantly based in America (64.3%) and Europe (35.7%), with none originating from Asia or Australia. A total of 101 publishers were represented.

Gender distribution across journal characteristics

The representation of women varied significantly by journal characteristics (Table 2). The proportion of female editorial board members was highest in journals ranked in the highest impact quartile (Q1: 30.5%) and lowest in journals ranked in Q4 (17.9%) (p < 0.001). SCIE-indexed journals showed slightly higher female representation than ESCI-indexed journals (26.8% vs. 25.6%), but this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.103). Geographic differences were notable: female editorial board members were more prevalent in journals based in the United States (49.5%) and Europe (37.8%) than in journals based in Asia (10.1%) or Australia (1.5%) (p < 0.001). Similar trends were observed by publisher continent (p < 0.001), with female representation highest in journals published in the Americas (51.4%) and lowest in Asian publishers (4.7%). Journals affiliated with professional associations or societies had a significantly higher proportion of female editorial board members compared to unaffiliated journals (48.8% vs. 51.2%, p < 0.001). Journals with higher impact factors (JIF ≥ 2.8) also had greater female representation (58% vs. 42%, p < 0.001). Journals published for less than 15 years had slightly higher female representation than journals published for ≥ 15 years (52.4% vs. 47.6%, p < 0.001).

Factors associated with gender disparity

The proportion of female editorial board members demonstrated a weak but statistically significant positive correlation with both the 2023 impact factor (r = 0.124, p = 0.029) and 5-year impact factor (r = 0.161, p = 0.005). No significant correlation was observed between female representation and journal publication duration (r = −0.004, p = 0.948). The total number of editorial board members, stratified by gender, was not significantly correlated with impact factor (p = 0.257, 0.266, and 0.364 for total, men, and women, respectively), 5-year impact factor (p = 0.208, 0.204, and 0.376, respectively), or journal age (p = 0.829, 0.818, and 0.906, respectively) (Table 3). Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses identified several factors associated with lower female representation on editorial boards (Table 4). The absence of a female editor-in-chief was strongly associated with gender disparity (OR:2.77, 95%CI:1.61–4.75, p < 0.001). Journals with lower impact factors (< 2.8) were more likely to have gender disparity on univariable analysis (OR = 1.72, 95%CI: 1.10–2.70, p = 0.017), though this association was attenuated in the multivariable model (p = 0.078). Lack of affiliation with a professional association was independently associated with gender disparity (OR:1.78, 95%CI:1.11–2.85, p = 0.017).

Discussion

We observed persistent gender disparities across the editorial boards of oncology journals, characterized by the marked underrepresentation of women in editorial roles. Higher female representation was significantly associated with both the presence of female editors-in-chief and the journal’s affiliation with a professional association. This study represents the most comprehensive assessment of gender representation across oncology journal editorial boards to date, expanding upon prior analyses that focused solely on high-impact or subspecialty journals.

The underrepresentation of women on editorial boards has been identified and demonstrated across journals in multiple medical disciplines and other scientific disciplines [34–37]. While female authorship in oncology journals has increased substantially over the past decade [38], progress toward gender equity in editorial boards has lagged with slow advancement and enduring structural barriers. We found that women accounted for only 26.5% of editorial board members and 23.5% of editors-in-chief, with fewer than one in ten journals achieving female-majority representation on their boards. Our results are consistent with those of Dai et al., who evaluated 60leading oncology journals and found that women comprised 24% of editorial board members and 20% of editors-in-chief [27]. Similarly, Patel et al. examined hematology and oncology journals and reported that women represented 27.4% of editorial board members and only 15.9% of editors-in-chief [28]. Even within the subspecialty of gynecologic oncology, male dominance of editorial boards remains evident [39, 40]. Together, these data show that gender disparities in editorial board composition is sitll evident across specialties and journal types, warranting continued efforts to understand and address the underlying structural causes.

The underrepresentation of women on editorial boards, particularly in leadership positions within medical oncology journals, stems from multiple interconnected factors. Editors and editorial board members are often chosen from candidates who have demonstrated an established scientific profile, solid authorship record as a senior or lead author, and experience with peer reviewing [27]. Nevertheless, cultural, structural, organizational, and societal barriers remain to recruiting, promoting, and retaining women members within editorial roles [41]. To interpret these structural barriers more deeply, established sociological and gender theories provide useful explanatory frameworks. Role congruity theory suggests that leadership roles are historically associated with agentic, stereotypically masculine traits, creating implicit bias when women pursue or occupy such positions [5]. Similarly, homosocial reproduction theory posits that individuals in positions of power tend to select successors who resemble themselves in gender and background, thereby perpetuating male-dominated leadership structures [42].

The “glass ceiling” phenomenon further limits women’s advancement to senior academic leadership, referring to invisible yet persistent institutional barriers that prevent women from reaching top positions despite equivalent qualifications and scholarly productivity [43]. In academic medicine, this may manifest as women attaining senior faculty status and substantial publication records while remaining underrepresented among department chairs or editors-in-chief of major journals [44]. The “glass cliff” concept indicates that women may be appointed to leadership roles under precarious or high-risk conditions, meaning that leadership opportunities may arise during periods of organizational instability, crisis, or declining performance [45]. For example, women may be selected to lead journals undergoing restructuring, reputational challenges, or stagnating impact metrics, thereby assuming leadership in comparatively vulnerable contexts [46]. These leadership dynamics may also interact with the “leaky pipeline”, in which women disproportionately exit or stall at successive academic transitions, resulting in fewer women reaching the senior ranks that typically feed into editorial leadership roles [47].Together, these mechanisms help explain why disparities persist despite substantial increases in female authorship and workforce participation in oncology.

One of the most striking findings of this study is the strong association between the presence of female editors-in-chief and greater representation of women among editorial board members. This leadership signal has been identified in other fields and likely represents several mechanisms [8, 27]. First, female leaders might consciously attempt to form greater inclusive editorial teams, through professional networks which tend to include more women [29]. Second, their presence at the top may help to normalize female leadership in the journal, and may diminish unconscious biases during nominations or appointments [2]. Third, female editor-in-chiefs may serves as role models for other qualified women who might accept invitations to the editorial board and remain involved in editorial work [48]. This finding has practical implications, scuh as diversifying leadership at the highest level may produce downstream effects on board composition. Society affiliation was another strong predictor of gender representation. In comparison to independent journals, there were more female editors in societies' journals. This could be attributed to either clearer processes around appointing editors, mission-related diversity initiatives, and/or more formal kinds of accountability in their societies [49]. In contrast, independent journals may rely more on informal selection processes; it could be said that informal processes beget an ongoing gender imbalance. Publisher-level policies may also significantly influence editorial diversity. In recent years, major publishing agencies such as Elsevier, Springer Nature, and Wiley have introduced formal commitments to inclusion and diversity, including public reporting of editorial board composition, diversity dashboards, and structured nomination processes. For example, some publishers display gender distribution of editors and editorial board members, increasing transparency and institutional accountability [50]. However, because journal groups in our dataset were highly heterogeneous in terms of publisher affiliation, governance structure, and reporting transparency, we were unable to perform a stratified analysis to provide direct empirical support for this interpretation.

Geographic disparities also emerged as a significant dimension of gender representation. Editorial boards with ≥ 50% female representation were predominantly based in North America and Europe, with none originating from Asia or Australia. Overall, women were represented on 49.5% of U.S.-based journal boards but only 10.1% of Asia-based journals and 1.5% of those based in Australia. These differences are likely to reflect larger sociocultural and structural differences in gender norms, academic hierarchies, and leadership pathways. Kalaitzi et al. point out that societal culture profoundly shapes women’s ability to access leadership roles in healthcare, with traditional gender norms acting as formidable barriers in some regions. [29] Additionally, regional differences in the maturity of gender equity initiatives within academia and publishing likely contribute to this gradient. Many institutions in North America and Europe have established systematic gender equity approaches in the past two decades, which included leadership development initiatives, and in publishing formal policies related to diversity [51–53].

Regional variation in editorial representation can also be interpreted through sociopolitical and cultural frameworks. In North America and Europe, gender equity policies have been institutionalized through legislation, research funding mandates, and publisher-level commitments. For example, the European Commission’s Gender Equality Plans under Horizon Europe funding require institutional strategies for gender balance, and organizations such as the National Institutes of Health (NIH) have emphasized diversity in leadership and peer review panels [54, 55]. Major publishers, including Elsevier and Springer Nature, have introduced Inclusion and Diversity dashboards and formalized commitments such as the Joint Commitment for Action on Inclusion and Diversity in Publishing. These structural initiatives may partially explain the relatively higher representation observed in Western-based journals. In contrast, some Asia–Pacific contexts operate within sociocultural traditions influenced by Confucian hierarchical norms, where seniority-based leadership structures and traditional gender role expectations may shape career trajectories differently [56, 57]. Confucian hierarchical norms refers specifically to role-graded expectations that prioritize deference to seniority and authority, alongside reciprocal obligations in which superiors are expected to guide with benevolence and moral exemplarship while juniors reciprocate with loyalty and respect [58]. In organizational settings, these norms may be reflected in more seniority-weighted and centralized decision-making, where advancement depends strongly on endorsement by senior figures within established hierarchies [59]. A second relevant strand is the historically influential gender-role differentiation between inner (domestic) and outer (public) spheres, including didactic traditions such as threefold dependency (sancong) across the life course, which can shape expectations about women’s leadership trajectories [59]. While substantial progress toward gender equity is occurring across Asia, leadership pathways may remain more centralized and less transparent, potentially affecting editorial appointment processes. These differences highlight the importance of contextualized strategies rather than uniform global solutions.

Structured mentorship programs and journal-initiated pipeline programs have the potential to enhance gender diversity within editorial boards [60, 61]. The Advocates and Allies programs might serve as models of active practice in terms of gender equity [62]. Superb women scientists may not seek out academic leadership due to a lack of mentors and female role models [63]. Providing explicit encouragement, constructive directions, as well as, a supportive academic climate can help reduce hesitance and alleviate concerns that prevent women from pursuing leadership opportunities. Editorial boards should prioritize equity, diversity, and inclusion, while ensuring that all members possess the necessary expertise and core competencies relevant to the journal’s scope, regardless of gender [64]. To operationalize these principles, several concrete and implementable strategies may be considered. Journals could introduce transparent and publicly advertised nomination procedures, establish term limits for editorial appointments to prevent structural stagnation, and publish annual demographic summaries of editorial board composition to enhance transparency and accountability [65]. Developing formal editorial training tracks, such as associate editor development programs or editorial internships for early- and mid-career scholars, may broaden the leadership pipeline [66]. Professional societies and publishers may also introduce diversity benchmarks, unconscious bias training for selection committees, and periodic diversity audits to monitor progress. Such coordinated, multi-level interventions-spanning journals, societies, publishers, and funding bodies-are more likely to produce durable improvements than isolated initiatives. Encouragingly, some editors-in-chief have initiated research awards and recognition programs aimed at highlighting outstanding women researchers and fostering more inclusive academic environments, which may contribute to long-term cultural change.

The study has several limitations. First, gender classification was based on publicly available information and name-based inference, which may have introduced misclassification in cases where gender could not be clearly determined. In particular, the use of name-based gender inference tools such as Genderize.io is known to be associated with false-positive classifications, especially for culturally ambiguous names, although a recent study reported over 95% accuracy for in a multi-national dataset of 32,968 gender-labeled clinical trial authors [67]. Second, gender was categorized in a binary manner, which does not capture non-binary or gender-diverse identities. Third, the analysis relied on information available on journal websites at a single time point, and editorial board compositions may change over time. This study did not benchmark editorial board gender composition against the underlying gender distribution of the oncology workforce, as such data are not uniformly available across countries, subspecialties, and academic ranks; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as a descriptive assessment of representation patterns rather than a normative comparison. Moreover, our analysis did not integrate country-level demographic indicators or validated sociocultural measures, thereby limiting our ability to formally contextualize observed editorial disparities within broader structural and cultural frameworks. However, the data from ESMO annual report 2023 (51%) and ASCO 2022 snapshot (35.8%) firmly suggests a gender disparity in journal leadership compared to workforce in oncology [68]. Finally, we have only analyzed journals indexing in the InCites Journal Citation Reports. The results did not reflect the current status of all oncology-related journals. Journals indexing other indexes should also be evaluated in novel studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we revealed that women continue to be substantially underrepresented in oncology journal editorial leadership across the globe. A female editor-in-chief and society affiliation were important factors in a more equitable gender balance. Nonetheless, regional and intersectional underrepresentation provide areas for consideration of the context but also methods in developing strategies. By utilizing transparent policies, diversifying leadership, and building inclusive networks, oncology journals can make significant strides toward achieving gender equity.
