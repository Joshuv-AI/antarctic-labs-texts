Mucopolysaccharidosis type I: founder effect of the p.P533R mutation in North Africa
Authors: Latifa Chkioua, Houda El Fissi, Yessine Amri, Chayma Sahli, Fadoua Bouzid, Hela Boudabous, Neji Tbib, Salima Ferchichi, Taieb Massoud, Najat Alif, Sandrine Laradi, Hassen Ben Abdennebi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMucopolysaccharidosis type I is a lysosomal storage disease resulting from a deficiency in alpha-L-iduronidase (IDUA), which causes the accumulation of partially degraded dermatan sulfate and heparan sulfate. This retrospective study, spanning eight years, analyzed data from 45 MPSI patients. The report aimed to explore the potential origin of the p.P533R mutation in the Maghrebin population by constructing a single-nucleotide polymorphism haplotype around the IDUA gene, in order to propose a molecular proof of a founder effect of the MPSI/p.P533R allele.Patients and methodsAll of the studied patients were from Libya (2), Mauritania (1) Morocco (21) and Tunisia (21) with first cousins being the most frequent union. The diagnosis of MPSI patients often involves the combination of urinary screening, leukocyte IDUA activity determination, and DNA molecular analysis. In our study, to identify the common p.P533R mutation, we performed both DNA sequencing and tetra-primer ARMS PCR assay. Additionally, Haploview was used to determine the specific haplotype that cosegregates with the p.P533R mutation. Controls were genotyped to ensure that all the SNPs were in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.ResultsIn the present report we confirmed the very strong impact of consanguinity on the incidence of MPSI disease. Furthermore, studied families of mixed ancestry shared common and specific haplotype, which was observed in studied populations, suggesting the presence of a founder effect in the North Africa.ConclusionThe p.P533R missense mutation was identified in each patient originated from Libya, Mauritania, Morocco and Tunisia. Furthermore, these MPSI patients exhibited the same IDUA haplotype. The occurrence of a shared AAGGGTG haplotype, among North African populations may be attributed to substantial historical gene exchange between these groups, likely stemming from migration, inter-ethnic marriage, or other forms of interaction throughout history.Keywords: Mucopolysaccharidosis type I, P.P533R mutation, Consanguinity, Common ancestor, Polymorphisms, Haplotypes, Maghrebin populations

Background

Mucopolysaccharidosis type I (MPSI) is an autosomal recessive disorder resulting from the deficiency of α-L-iduronidase (iduronohydrolase, EC 3.2.1.76; IDUA). The lack of the IDUA activity leads to the accumulation of partially degraded dermatan sulfate and heparan sulfate [1, 2].

The IDUA gene is located on the short arm of chromosome 4 at region p16.3 [3]. It consists of14 exons spanning about 19 kb producing a transcript of 2.3 kb that encodes a precursor protein of 653 amino acids [4]. To date, more than 300 different disease-causing mutations in the IDUA gene have already been reported [5].

The primary treatment for affected individuals involved bone marrow or umbilical cord blood transplantation, which are successful if applied early, albeit some associated risks still persist [6, 7]. Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) offered another viable treatment option, shown to be both safe and effective for patients diagnosed with MPSI [8]. In addition, gene therapy offers a potential cure and has demonstrated promising outcomes in animal models of MPSI, illustrating reductions in GAGs levels and enhancements in neurological symptoms [2, 9].

Several studies have indicated a shared genetic background between the Tunisian and Moroccan populations for different genetic diseases [10]. For the knowledge, the Maghreb, distinct from the Mashreq, is situated in the northwest of Africa and encompasses three countries: Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia. Politically, Libya and Mauritania are also included to form the Greater Maghreb. The populations of the Greater Maghreb, comprising both Berbers and Arabs, share common languages, customs, and religion [11].

Moreover, consanguinity was observed as a global phenomenon with diverse prevalence across different countries. Influenced by various factors such as religion, society, and economy, it is particularly prevalent in many populations, especially in the Middle East and developing countries. Several studies examined the impact of consanguinity in recessive disorders, emphasizing the significant role of intermarriage in the recurrence of homozygous mutations [12].

For instance, in Tunisia, specific mutations like p.G1483D and p.R1763X in the COL7A1 gene present a limited geographical distribution, being firstly observed in the region of Sidi Bouzid and Kasserine, respectively. These mutations likely originated in the Middle East and were introduced in Tunisia following Banu Hilal invasions that settled in the Maghreb in the eleventh century [12]. The g.4339delG variant in the CYP1B1 gene is predominant in Moroccan patients with primary congenital glaucoma disease [13, 14]. Another example is the (AAAS c.1331 + 1G > A) mutation which was found, not only in Moroccan but also in other North African patients, such as Libyan, Tunisian and Algerian, [15–17] affected by the Triple A syndrome, [18]. This finding is consistent with the rate of consanguinity (30%) observed in Morocco [19].

We were interested in the p.P533R mutation, considered to be the most frequent in North African populations. In Tunisia and Morocco, the occurrence of the p.P533R mutation appears to be associated with the high frequency of consanguineous marriages [20, 21].The p.P533R mutation in exon 11 (CCG → CGG) results in the substitution of a neutral amino acid (proline) with a basic amino acid (arginine) at position 533 of the IDUA protein. This mutant protein, IDUA-p.P533R, has been shown to retain some residual catalytic activity when expressed in CHO-K1 cells [22]. The introduction of a larger amino acid like arginine (Arg) requires more space in the contact zone between the beta sandwich and the helix involved in substrate binding. As a result, this mutation affects the stability and activity of the IDUA protein, which may be associated with different MPSI phenotypes [23].

Of note, in a previous study, we reported a correlation between parental consanguinity and the frequency of MPSI [6]. The specific molecular lesion p.P533R was found to be common among all Maghrebin populations, including those of Maghrebin immigrants in France. This suggests that p.P533R could be considered a founder mutation in Maghrebin populations [21, 24, 25]. However, the frequency of carriers of the p.P533R mutation in the Maghreb population has not yet been determined; data from the gnomAD database (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/variant/4-1003418-C-G?dataset=gnomad_r4) are only available for African and African American populations, which is 0.00002509.

The present study aimed to analyze the genetic profiles of MPSI patients and draw haplotype structures in North African populations.

Patients and methods

This study was conducted on four mixed Ancestry Maghrebin families with the MPSI: thirteen Moroccan families, fourteen Tunisian families, one Mauritanian family, and one Libyan family (Fig. 1). These families were recruited in pediatrics departments of different geographic areas of Morocco and Tunisia (Central, Northern, and South areas). In statistical analysis, when a nuclear family presents multiple patients, only one was considered. Similarly, when patients had related families, only one family was considered. The Moroccan and Tunisian controls were randomly selected and genotyped. We included in this study all patients homozygous for the p.P533R mutation, while patients compound heterozygous for this allele were excluded.

Tetra primer-ARMS-PCR of p.P533R mutation in the IDUA gene

The p.P533R mutation was identified either by Tetra primer-ARMS-PCR which was performed in laboratory of Human Genome and Multifactorial Diseases, Monastir, Tunisia as previously described [26] or by DNA sequencing of IDUA gene.

Haplotype analysis

To estimate the origin of the common p.P533R mutation, haplotypes were determined using IDUA Sanger sequencing of four amplified genomic fragments, each one including one, two and/or three polymorphisms. The mutant and wild alleles were tested for seven polymorphisms. These included the following known polymorphisms in: (1) exon 1 (A8A,CGCGCCGCG [allele C] or CGCGCAGCG [allele A]), (A20A; GCCGCGCCC [allele G] or GCCGCACCC [allele A]), (Q33H;GTGCAGGTG [allele G] or GTGCATGTG [allele T]), (2) exon 3 (R105Q; GGACGGGGC [allele G] or GGACAGGGC [allele A]), (3) exon 7 (A314A; AGGGCGGAC [allele G] or AGGGCCGAC[allele C]), (4) intron 12 (IVS12 + 72; ccatgcggtc [allele T] orccaggcggtc [allele G]) and (IVS12 + 75; ccatgcggtc [allele G) or ccatgctgtc [allele T]).The details of the SNPs are presented in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The linkage disequilibrium (LD) between the seven SNPs and the haplotypes reconstruction were determined using Haploview version 4.2 software (https://www.broadinstitute.org/haploview/haploview) for the Moroccan and Tunisian populations.

The Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) for each polymorphism was examined using the chi-square (χ2) test. Haplotype frequencies were quantified using the Expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm, and the LD block structure was determined using the Solid Spine of the LD algorithm. The degree of genetic linkage between the seven SNPs in the studied populations was estimated as Lewontin's coefficient D’ and Pearson's correlation coefficient r2.A D’ < 1 with shades of pink/red or light blue indicates weak or nonexistent LD, respectively, while a dark red color {D’ = 1} indicates strong pairwise LD between the analyzed SNPs [27]. The distributions of allele and haplotype frequencies between the case and control individuals in both Moroccan and Tunisian populations were estimated using the test χ2. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all statistical tests were two-sided.

Results

The GAGs values in the studied patients are high compared with those of normal controls of the same age. The patient population was subdivided into three groups according to their average age. Patients aged between 9 months and 3 years had an average GAGs level of 40 mg glucuronic acid per gram of creatinine, twice that of normal controls of the same age. Patients aged 3 to 7 years had a mean GAGs level of 104 mg glucuronic acid per gram of creatinine, 3.5 times higher than normal controls of the same age. Patients aged 8 to 12 years had a mean GAGs level of 109 mg glucuronic acid per gram of creatinine, four times higher than normal controls of the same age.

Rate of consanguinity in Maghrebin families with MPSI

Consanguinity was observed in all the families studied: 67% (9 out of 13) in Moroccan families, 100% (14 out of 14) in Tunisian families, and both families from Mauritania and Libya. Among these families, six Moroccan families and fourteen Tunisian families presented first-cousin unions (Fig. 1).

p.P533R detection in Maghrebin families

All 45 patients from the four studied countries were homoallelic for the p.P533R mutation. Their parents were heteroallelic, while their healthy relatives could either be heteroallelic or homoallelic for the wild-type allele.

Haplotypes associated with MPS I/p.P533R in Moroccan and Tunisian population

It is worth noting that the AAGGGTG haplotype was present in 100% of the Moroccan and Tunisian patients, while in the control subjects, the frequencies of this haplotype were 32.2% and 23.3%, respectively. The associated p-value was highly significant (< 0.001). Furthermore, the AAGGGTG haplotype was also observed in patients from Libya and Mauritania. LD determination was not possible due to the lack of control DNA samples from the two countries.

Moreover, the CGTGGTG, CGGGGTG, and CGGGCTG haplotypes were never found in MPSI patients from either Moroccan or Tunisian populations. In contrast, Moroccan controls showed frequencies of 21.7%, 16.6%, and 13.5% for these haplotypes, respectively, while Tunisian controls presented frequencies of 26.7%, 18.3%, and 16.7%, respectively. As a result, these haplotypes are considered protective against MPSI/p.P533R. The remaining five haplotypes do not confer protection nor present a risk for the disease.

Discussion

Several previous studies have documented the prevalence of consanguineous unions in various communities across North Africa, the Middle East, and West Asia. In these regions, intra-familial unions account for a significant proportion of marriages, ranging from 20 to 50% [19, 28, 29]. Tunisia, in particular, has a relatively high rate of inbreeding, with consanguinity reported in 49% of the population and first cousin marriages accounting for 32% of all marriages [30]. According to data from the National Office for Family and Population Affairs, consanguinity remained notably widespread in Tunisia from 1991 to 2001, with close and unknown consanguineous unions comprising 21% and 19% of all marriages in the country, respectively [31].

Consanguinity in Maghrebin MPSI patients

Tunisian families, like other North African families, tend to be large and extended. In this study involving five married couples from Family 8 in southern Tunisia, we observed a notably high consanguinity rate of 71%, with first cousin unions being the most common. In the 14 Tunisian families included in this study, consanguinity was present in every case, accounting for a 100% rate. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have also highlighted the prevalence of first cousin inbreeding as the most common form of consanguineous unions in Tunisia [32].

In this study, we expect the degree of consanguinity within Moroccan families to be more than 80%. For instance, in families 4 and 6 whose parents claim not to be consanguineous couples originated from the same village. Out of thirteen families included in this study, consisting of both Berber and Arab-origins, nine of them exhibit consanguineous relationships. This finding aligns with a previous study indicating that about two-thirds of all consanguineous marriages are between first cousins [33]. Additionally, another study reported a similar global prevalence of consanguinity and first cousin unions [21]. Furthermore, several reports have highlighted a correlation between consanguinity and various genetic conditions [34, 35], including Hurler disease [12, 20], congenital hearing loss [36, 37], and degenerative spinocerebellar diseases [38, 39]. This present study confirms these findings, providing further evidence of the significant impact of consanguinity on the incidence of MPSI disease.

In addition, in this report, we have identified two other MPSI families from North Africa: two sibling patients from Libya and one patient from Mauritania. We have found these two individual families in departments of Tunisian hospitals. In reality, the small number of Libyan and Mauritanian families is due to these families moving to Tunisia to consult genetic testing and receive treatment. It is worth mentioning that the first cousin unions also occurred in these two families, suggesting a high prevalence of consanguinity within these populations.

p.P533R a common allele in North Africa

It has been reported that the p.P533R allele was found to be frequent in Algerian, Moroccan, and Tunisian patients with a rate of 80%, 92%, and 62%, respectively [21, 24, 25]. In contrast, this mutation does not exceed 3% in European patients, but 10% and 11% in Iberian and Sicilian patients, respectively [40, 41] who shared a historical connection with the North African population [42, 43].

The patients from Libya and Mauritania were found homoallelic for the same common p.P533R mutation. It is likely that other MPSI patients from these countries carry the same genotype. The homozygosity for the same common p.P533R allele in Algeria [25], Morocco [21, 44], Tunisia [24], Libya, and Mauritania (present report) would indicate the spread of this mutation in North Africa. Haplotype analysis in control groups from Libya and Mauritania would be important for further investigation.

Founder effect of p.P533R mutation

Block 1, which covers a span of 16 kb, encompassed the first exon containing three SNPs (rs11248061, rs10902762, and rs10794537), but also the exon 3 with the rs3755955, the exon 7 with rs6830825 and the intron 12 with two SNPs (rs2305488 and rs2305489). Haplotype analysis, considering different combinations of the seven SNPs examined, revealed the presence of nine haplotypes in the Tunisiana and Moroccan cohorts (Table 3).

In this report, we noticed that among three SNPs within the exon 1 of the IDUA gene, only two: rs11248061 and rs10902762 are strongly associated with the p.P533R allele in Moroccan and Tunisian patients. This association has previously been described in Tunisian patients [24, 45, 46], Italian patients [47], and other populations as well [22, 48]. Consequently, the rs11248061 and rs10902762 likely play an active role in the phenotypic expression of MPSI disease.

The four mixed Ancestry families share a common founder AAGGGTG haplotype which was found to be the most frequent and the specific in the four populations. The prevalence of the second most frequent CGTGGTG haplotype was found to be similar in Moroccan and Tunisian populations (18.2%). The AAGGGTG haplotype was predominant (100%) in patients, suggesting a strong association with a high-risk factor. This molecular finding supports the hypothesis that the IDUA-p.P533R allele was transmitted into the mixed Ancestry population from North Africa, given the established genetic influence of emigrants throughout the region. Furthermore, the presence of a common ancestor, combined with consanguinity, may have facilitated the widespread dissemination of a particular variant and its associated haplotype across the four populations. Nevertheless, further studies with a larger cohort of patients from these populations are required to determine the frequency of this haplotype and provide evidence for this hypothesis.

Conclusion

Consanguinity increases the risk of developing MPSI disease. Our results indicate that the presence of a specific haplotype, which is closely associated with disease-causing mutations, suggests that the p.P533R mutation likely originates from North Africa. This finding is significant in understanding the genetic foundation of MPSI disease and may help to provide valuable insights for genetic testing and genetic counseling for affected individuals in the Maghrebin populations.
