Determinants of Tetanus Toxoid Vaccination Among Women of Reproductive Age in Somaliland: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Mustafe Maxamed Cabdi, Kalid Hassan Mohamed, Abdirahmaan Mohamed Yasin, Abdi Ahmed Nour, Hodan Mukhtar Farah, Ladan Abdi Barkhad, Kheyriya Ibrahim Hassan, Mohamed Said Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsUnderstanding the predictors of Tetanus Toxoid (TT) protection among women of reproductive age is essential for designing effective interventions to eliminate Maternal and Neonatal Tetanus (MNT) in Somaliland. This study aims to identify the sociodemographic, economic, and geographic determinants of TT protection.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted using the 2020 Somaliland Demographic and Health Survey (SLDHS). Binary and multivariate logistic regression models, accounting for the complex survey design, were applied to identify determinants of TT protection (receipt of ≥ 2 doses) among 2630 women.ResultsSignificant predictors included Antenatal Care (ANC) attendance, education, wealth, and region. The adjusted odds of protection were over 8 times higher for women with 4 + ANC visits (AOR = 8.52; 95% CI: 6.31–11.51), nearly 3 times higher for the wealthiest women (AOR = 2.83; 95% CI: 1.98–4.05), and over 3 times higher for women with secondary or higher education (AOR = 3.48; 95% CI: 2.11–5.74).ConclusionFindings highlight the importance of strengthening ANC services as the primary platform for TT delivery. Tailored interventions are recommended to target the identified risk factors, including uneducated, poor, and rural women, particularly in underserved regions.Keywords: logistic regression, maternal health, multivariate analysis, predictors, public health, Tetanus Toxoid

Introduction

Maternal and Neonatal Tetanus (MNT) is a life‐threatening but preventable disease that disproportionately affects populations in low‐income countries with limited access to healthcare [1]. It is often caused by unhygienic childbirth practices where Clostridium tetani spores contaminate the umbilical stump [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends Tetanus Toxoid (TT) vaccination for all women of reproductive age, particularly during pregnancy, as a cornerstone of its MNT elimination strategy [3]. Maternal antibodies are transferred to the fetus, providing crucial passive immunity for the newborn during the first few vulnerable months of life [4]. In fragile and post‐conflict settings like Somaliland, high maternal and infant mortality rates underscore the urgent need for effective preventive health services [5]. However, specific data on the coverage and determinants of TT vaccination is particularly scarce, hindering evidence‐based planning. This study aims to identify the sociodemographic, economic, and geographic determinants of TT protection among women of reproductive age in Somaliland.

Literature Review

Global efforts have significantly reduced the burden of MNT, largely through the implementation of routine immunization and clean delivery practices [6]. However, significant gaps in coverage persist, particularly in sub‐Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia, where health systems are often fragile [7, 8]. Antenatal Care (ANC) services serve as the primary and most effective platform for delivering TT vaccines to pregnant women [9]. ANC attendance is therefore a critical determinant of vaccination uptake, as it provides multiple opportunities for health workers to administer vaccines, provide health education, and build trust with mothers [10, 11].

Socioeconomic status is another powerful and consistent predictor. Studies across numerous developing countries have shown that maternal education and household wealth are strongly associated with the utilization of maternal health services, including TT vaccination [12, 13]. Educated women are more likely to be aware of the benefits of vaccination and have greater autonomy in making health‐related decisions for themselves and their children [14]. Similarly, higher household wealth can mitigate the financial barriers associated with accessing healthcare, such as transportation costs and lost income [14]. This study builds on this extensive body of literature by providing a focused analysis of TT vaccination in the unique context of Somaliland, addressing a critical evidence gap for the region.

Data and Methodology

Data Source

This study used data from the 2020 Somaliland Demographic and Health Survey (SLDHS) [15]. The survey covered all six administrative regions of Somaliland from 2019 to 2020. After applying inclusion criteria, a final analytical sample of 2630 women of reproductive age (15–49) with complete data on the variables of interest was included in this study.

Variable Description

Dependent Variable

Tetanus Toxoid (TT) protection status. While WHO defines long‐term protection based on a 5‐dose schedule, for the purpose of this cross‐sectional analysis using DHS data, we defined “Protection” as a binary variable. It was coded as “1” for women who reported receiving two or more doses of TT vaccine (Protected) and “0” for those who received fewer than two doses (Not Protected) during their lifetime, referencing the most recent birth.

Independent Variables

Independent variables were selected based on their established association with maternal health service utilization in the literature. These included: Maternal age (categorized as 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35+), place of residence (Rural, Urban), education level (No Education, Primary, Secondary/Higher), wealth index (in quintiles from Lowest to Highest), number of Antenatal Care (ANC) visits (None, 1–3, 4+), and administrative region of residence.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis began with descriptive statistics to summarize participant characteristics, reported as frequencies and percentages. Bivariate analysis using the Chi‐square (χ²) test was conducted to assess associations between each independent variable and TT protection status. Subsequently, a multivariate logistic regression model was built to identify the net effect of each predictor while controlling for others. Variables found to be significant at p < 0.20 in the bivariate analysis, along with variables of theoretical importance, were included in the initial multivariate model. The final model retained variables that remained statistically significant predictors. All analyses were performed using Stata version 16 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) and accounted for the complex multi‐stage cluster sampling design of the SLDHS by applying survey weights, strata, and primary sampling units to generate nationally representative estimates and robust standard errors. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (2‐sided).

Descriptive Statistics

The sociodemographic characteristics of the 2630 participants are presented in Table 1. The overall prevalence of TT protection (≥ 2 doses) was 14.9%. The majority of women had no formal education (82.9%) and had not attended any ANC visits (63.0%). There were strong, statistically significant associations (p < 0.001) between TT protection and education level, wealth index, region, and ANC attendance.

Discussion

This study identified that TT protection in Somaliland is critically low at 14.9% and is systematically associated with healthcare access, socioeconomic status, and geography. The key finding is the overwhelming impact of Antenatal Care on vaccination status. The odds of being protected were over 8 times higher for women with four or more ANC visits compared to those with none (AOR = 8.52; 95% CI: 6.31–11.51). This confirms that ANC is the primary, and perhaps only, platform through which most women access this life‐saving intervention. This finding aligns with extensive research from across sub‐Saharan Africa, which consistently identifies lack of ANC as a primary barrier to receiving maternal vaccinations [9, 10]. The fact that 63% of women in our sample reported no ANC visits represents a massive missed opportunity for MNT prevention.

Contextualizing these findings, the independent effects of education and wealth, which remained significant after adjusting for ANC, are consistent with the theory of social determinants of health [16]. Our analysis shows that women with secondary or higher education had nearly 3.5 times the odds of protection (AOR = 3.48; 95% CI: 2.11–5.74), and the wealthiest women had nearly 3 times the odds (AOR = 2.83; 95% CI: 1.98–4.05). This reinforces that empowered and economically stable women are more likely to overcome barriers to seek preventive care [12, 13, 17].

The stark regional disparities, with women in the Sool region having 55% lower odds of vaccination compared to the reference region (AOR = 0.45; 95% CI: 0.31–0.65), highlight subnational inequities in health service availability and quality. This is a common challenge in fragile health systems and may reflect deep‐seated challenges in Sool related to security, limited health infrastructure, or a higher proportion of nomadic populations who are harder to reach with routine services [5, 8]. The implications of these intertwined factors are clear: improving TT coverage requires a multi‐pronged approach that both strengthens the health system's capacity to deliver services through platforms like ANC and addresses the underlying socioeconomic barriers that prevent women from accessing them in the first place [18].

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

Use of a large, nationally representative dataset; robust diagnostics supporting model validity (high AUC); use of multivariate logistic regression to control for confounders; application of appropriate statistical methods to account for the complex survey design, enhancing the generalizability of the findings.

Limitations

The cross‐sectional design limits causal inference. Self‐reported vaccination status may be subject to recall bias. The definition of “protection” used (≥ 2 doses) aligns with short‐term protection during pregnancy but may not reflect full, long‐term immunity which requires a 5‐dose schedule. Furthermore, potentially relevant variables such as parity, media exposure, employment status, and distance to health facilities were not available or included in this analysis, which may lead to residual confounding.

Policy Recommendations

Based on these findings:

Strengthen and expand ANC services as the primary strategy to increase TT vaccination coverage, ensuring that every visit includes TT administration or scheduling.

Conclusion

This study found that ANC attendance, education, wealth, and region are significant predictors of TT protection in Somaliland. The alarmingly low coverage rate poses a serious risk for Maternal and Neonatal Tetanus. Policymakers must prioritize strengthening the primary healthcare system, with a focus on making ANC services accessible and utilized by all women, especially the most vulnerable. Further research should explore the specific health system and community‐level barriers that contribute to these disparities.
