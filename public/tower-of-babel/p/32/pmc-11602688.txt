Enhancing Long Stability of Solid‐State Batteries Through High‐Energy Ball Milling‐Induced Decomposition of Sulfide‐Based Electrolyte to Sulfur
Authors: Xitang Qian, Yuxiang Lyu, Siyu Zhou, Yican Qiu, Yan Sun, Yuan Yuan, Minhua Shao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetal sulfides are increasingly favored as cathode materials in all‐solid‐state batteries (ASSBs) due to their high energy density, stability, affordability, and conductivity. Metal sulfides often exhibit capacities exceeding their theoretical limits, a phenomenon that remains not fully understood. In this study, it reveals that this phenomenon is primarily due to the sulfur decomposition from sulfide‐based electrolyte. By employing the high‐energy ball milling (HEBM) technique, the deposition of sulfide‐based electrolyte onto sulfur is intentionally promoted, resulting in higher charge capacities compared to the discharge capacities and surpass theoretical limits of metal sulfides. Using chromium sulfide (Cr2S3) as the active material, the sulfur decomposed from sulfide‐based electrolyte transforms into lithium sulfide (Li2S) after discharge, resulting in an increased capacity by ≈439.6 mAh g−1 and improved cycling stability. Consequently, it demonstrates a specific capacity surpassing 1200 mAh g−1 with a capacity retention of over 80% after 650 cycles, maintaining cycling stability for more than 1900 cycles and achieving a Coulombic efficiency exceeding 99.9%. This versatile HEBM approach enables the fabrication of ASSBs utilizing various transition metal sulfides, such as molybdenum disulfide (MoS2), niobium disulfide (NbS2), and iron disulfide (FeS2), all exhibiting over theoretical limited capacities and prolonged cycling capabilities.Keywords: Cr2S3 active materials, decomposed sulfur, ehanced capacity, high energy ball milling, solid state batteries

Background

The escalating global energy consumption has led to increased attention on energy storage devices.[ 1 ] Among these devices, lithium batteries have emerged as the most promising and commercially available solutions.[ 2 ] Within the realm of lithium batteries, all‐solid‐state batteries (ASSBs) have garnered significant interest as an emerging class of rechargeable batteries, holding immense potential for the future of energy storage.[ 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] The primary advantages of ASSBs lie in their enhanced safety and higher energy density.[ 5 ] ASSBs significantly mitigate the risks associated with traditional lithium‐ion batteries, such as toxicity of electrolytes’ decomposition products, thermal runaway, and fire hazards,[ 5 , 7 ] by eliminating flammable liquid electrolytes (like dimethyl/diethyl‐ and ethyl methyl carbonates).

This enhanced safety profile makes ASSBs particularly attractive for electric vehicles and portable electronics. The ASSBs utilize sulfides, oxides, and polymer based solid‐state electrolytes (SSEs), offering several advantages over traditional liquid electrolyte‐based batteries. The oxides include NASICON‐type Li1.3Al0.3Ti1.7(PO4)3,[ 8 ] LISICON‐type Li14ZnGe4O16,[ 9 ] perovskite‐type La(2/3)‐x□(1/3)‐2xLi3xTiO3,[ 10 ] LiPON‐type Li2.88PO3.73N0.14 [ 11 ] and garnet‐type Li5La3M2O12 (M = Nb, Ta).[ 12 ] However, these oxides suffer from low lithium‐ion conductivity in the range of 10−6 to 10−3 S cm−1 and involve sophisticated sintering process and high sintering temperature (> 1000 °C). The oxides‐type SSEs exhibit poor contact with lithium metal, leading to increased resistance and hindered ion transport, and possess a high rigidity, limiting their ability to accommodate volume changes which further significantly impact battery performance and efficiency,[ 13 ] severely limiting their application in the ASSBs. The gel SSEs, an alternative to liquid electrolytes, fail to provide a comprehensive solution to the challenges associated with liquid‐based batteries.[ 14 ] Although gels offer some improvements for stability and leakage prevention, they still suffer from inadequate ion conductivity and compatibility issues with electrode materials.[ 14 ]

In contrast to oxides‐type and gel‐type SSEs, sulfide‐based SSEs offer solutions to several crucial limitations. The sulfide‐based SSEs are the most promising candidate for ASSBs due to their high Li ion conductivity (1–32 mS cm−1) at room temperature, which is comparable to that of commercial liquid electrolytes and in some case higher than those of liquid organic electrolytes.[ 5 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 ] Furthermore, due to the nature of solid phase, the lithium ion transference number of sulfide solid state electrolyte is close to 1, which is higher than that of liquid comparts of ≈0.5, facilitating the elimination of lithium dendrite growth. Due to their moderate mechanical strength, characterized by a Young's modulus of ≈20–30 GPa,[ 21 ] the electrolyte and electrode layers can be easily fabricated using a simple pressing and rolling process with polymers. This eliminates the need for high temperature sintering processes, enhancing the processability and assembly of pouch cells.[ 4 ] Sulfide‐based SSEs can enable the use of high‐capacity cathode and anode materials that are incompatible with traditional liquid electrolytes. By utilizing sulfide compounds as electrolyte, these ASSBs exhibit improved contact and compatibility with lithium metal, enabling efficient ion transport and minimizing resistance. Moreover, the flexible nature of sulfide‐based electrolytes allows for better accommodation of volume changes during cycling, leading to enhanced mechanical stability and extended battery life.

Transition metal sulfides (MSx, M = Cr, Nb, Mo, V, Fe, Cu, Co, etc., X = 0.5–3) have moderate operating voltage (2.5–3.0 V) and higher theoretical specific capacities (up to 800 mAh g−1) and energy density (over 1200 Wh kg−1) than that of layered oxide cathode materials (<250 mAh g−1 and ≈150–220 Wh kg−1), making them an ideal candidate for high‐energy‐density solid state batteries (SSBs).[ 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 ] In addition, MSx has similar redox potential to those of sulfide‐based solid electrolytes, which is beneficial to enhancing the compatibility and stability of the electrode/electrolyte interface and improving the electrochemical performance of SSBs.[ 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 ] However, one intriguing observation in the MSx‐based ASSBs is that MSx materials often exhibit higher charge capacities than discharge capacities when subjected to an initial discharge followed by charge, surpassing their theoretical limits.[ 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 ] This phenomenon is not yet fully understood and highlights the need for further investigation into the underlying mechanisms.

In this study, we reveal that this phenomenon is primarily due to the sulfur decomposition from sulfide‐based electrolyte. By employing the high energy ball milling (HEBM) technique, we intentionally promote the deposition of Li5.4PS4.4Cl1.6 (LPSC) electrolyte onto sulfur, resulting in a higher charge capacity compared to the discharge capacity and surpass theoretical limits of metal sulfides. Using Cr2S3 as the active material, the sulfur decomposed from LPSC transforms into lithium sulfide (Li2S) after discharge, increasing capacity of Cr2S3 ASSB by ≈439.6 mAh g−1 and improved cycling stability. As a result, it showcases a specific capacity surpassing 1200 mAh g−1 with a capacity retention of over 80% after 650 cycles, maintaining cycling stability for more than 1900 cycles and achieving a Coulombic efficiency exceeding 99.9%. Our versatile HEBM approach enables the fabrication of ASSBs utilizing various transition metal sulfides, such as MoS2, NbS2, and FeS2, all exhibiting over theoretical limited capacity and prolonged cycling capabilities.

To investigate the alternation of LPSC electrolyte (Figure S1, Supporting Information) after the HEBM process, Cr2S3, vapor‐grown carbon fiber (VGCF), and LPSC were selected as precursors (Figure 1a and detailed in the Methods). The Cr2S3 crystals (theoretical capacity of 776.2 mAh g−1) were synthesized through chemical vapor transport method, employing Cr and S powder at a temperature of 700 °C for over 4 days. The crystals exhibited a spherical structure (Figure 1b) with a particle size of ≈50 µm. The Cr2S3 sheets, with a thickness of several hundred nanometers, were found to grow together in a disordered manner (Figures S2 and S3, Supporting Information). The chemical and structural properties of the samples were assessed through high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), X‐ray diffraction (XRD), X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and Raman characterizations before and after the HEBM treatment. The lattice spacing of Cr2S3 crystals was 0.551 nm from HRTEM image, corresponding to the (002) facet (Figure 1c,d). In the study of HEBM Cr2S3, the Cr2S3 compound enveloped with LPSC showed a lattice spacing of ≈0.56 nm (Figure 1e,f). This lattice spacing closely mirrors that of crystalline Cr2S3 and aligns with the (002) crystal facet. The XRD spectrum revealed that both the Cr2S3 crystals and the HEBM Cr2S3 sample exhibited characteristic peaks of Cr2S3 (PDF#10‐0340), including distinct (002), (110), (112), and (114) diffraction peaks, indicating same crystalline structures (Figure 1g). However, the intensity of these peaks was reduced, indicating a decrease in particle size following the HEBM treatment. These XRD and HRTEM characterizations provide evidence that the Cr2S3 samples before and after the HEBM treatment are well‐matched.

Due to the high‐energy attack, some LPSC electrolytes disintegrated, leading to the production of sulfur. According to the Raman spectrum (Figure 1h), HEBM treated samples exhibited two distinct peaks at 489.5 and 395.7 cm−1 which are attributed to the S─S bonds of sulfur and the P2S7 4− group of LPSC besides the Eg peaks of Cr2S3 at 248.5 and 311.1 cm−1. As for the chemical state of Cr2S3 and HEBM Cr2S3 samples, they exhibited the same Cr chemical state, as evidenced by the Cr 2p XPS spectrum (Figure S4, Supporting Information), with a main peak at the state of 575.15 eV for the Cr 2p3/2 state, accompanied by two satellite peaks at 574.09 and 577.22 eV. Another main peak at 584.31 eV was observed for the Cr 2p1/2 state, with satellite peaks at 586.42 eV. In addition, the S 2p XPS spectrum (Figure 1i) of both Cr2S3 and HEBM Cr2S3 samples displayed two primary peaks at 161.32 eV (S 2p3/2) and 162.57 eV (S 2p1/2). A small peak at 164.2 eV, attributed to the ‐Sx‐ species in the HEBM Cr2S3 sample, confirms the production of sulfur after HEBM, corroborating the Raman and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Figure S5, Supporting Information) results. Furthermore, the generated sulfur, characterized by a lattice spacing of 0.61 nm and exhibiting the (111) facet, can be readily identified in the HRTEM images (Figure S6, Supporting Information). These measurements suggest that the HEBM treatment results in the disintegration of the LPSC electrolyte and the production of sulfur without altering Cr2S3 lattice and chemical state. The produced sulfur is expected to contribute to the specific capacity of ASSBs.

To investigate the electrochemical performance of the HEBM‐treated Cr2S3 materials, batteries were assembled using LPSC as the electrolyte (Figure S1, Supporting Information), HEBM Cr2S3 mixture as the cathode, and lithium‐indium (Li‐In) foil as the anode in a home‐made mold (Figure S7, Supporting Information). The Li‐In alloy, with a voltage of ≈0.6 V versus Li+/Li, was selected due to its good stability and compatibility with LPSC electrolyte under high‐areal‐capacity and high‐current‐density conditions. All the batteries were tested at 30 °C. As illustrated in Figure 2a, the HEBM Cr2S3 ASSB was first discharged to 0 V versus Li+/LiIn, exhibiting a specific capacity of 694.7 mAh g−1, and then charged to 2.6 V versus Li+/LiIn, with a specific capacity of 1134.3 mAh g−1. The surplus capacity of 439.6 mAh g−1 can be attributed to the conversion of sulfur decomposed from LPSC (Figure 1h,i), to Li2S after charge. During discharge, in addition to the transformation of Cr2S3 into Cr and Li2S, the lithium and sulfur resulting from the HEBM process undergo a conversion into Li2S on the cathode side (Figure S8, Supporting Information), which does not contribute to the overall capacity. Consequently, the Li2S and Cr transform into Cr2S3 and S after charge, as indicated by the plateau observed at 1.0 and 2.2 V versus Li+/LiIn (Figure 2a; Figures S9 and S10, Supporting Information). The phenomenon of higher excess capacity during charge than discharge is commonly observed in active transition metal sulfides and sulfide‐based electrolytes.[ 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 ] To elucidate the excess capacity contributed by sulfur produced by HEBM treatment, three kinds of batteries, including HEBM Cr2S3|LPSC|HEBM Cr2S3, HEBM Cr2S3|LPSC|Cr2S3, and Cr2S3|LPSC|Cr2S3 batteries were assembled (Figure 2b). The Cr2S3 used here was made by hand milling of Cr2S3, LPSC, and VGCF. Both HEBM Cr2S3 symmetric and Cr2S3 symmetric batteries first discharged to −0.7 V and then charged to 0.7 V. The HEBM Cr2S3|LPSC|HEBM Cr2S3 battery shows the discharge capacity of 13.1 mAh g−1 and charge capacity of 22.6 mAh g−1 whereas the Cr2S3|LPSC|Cr2S3 battery shows negligible capacity of 0.7 mAh g−1 and charge capacity of 1.4 mAh g−1. Then we tested HEBM Cr2S3|LPSC|Cr2S3 batteries under two separate discharge/charge and charge/discharge cycles, with each cycle ranging from −0.7 to 0.7 V (Figure 2b). The HEBM Cr2S3|LPSC|Cr2S3 battery that underwent charge first exhibited a charge capacity of ≈9.6 mAh g−1 and a discharge capacity of 12.9 mAh g−1, while the same battery that underwent discharge first showed no capacity. This indicates that the presence of sulfur decomposed from LPSC contribute to capacity enhancement in specially designed batteries. As a result, the production of sulfur through the decomposition of LPSC can explain the higher capacity during the charge process compared to the discharge process for ASSB in the first cycle (Figure 2a).

The cyclic voltammetry (CV) was then employed to investigate the Cr2S3 reaction kinetic mechanism on the cathode under the scanning rates from 0.1 to 5 mV s−1 (Figure 2c,d). The peaks at 1.14 V (peak A) and 2.16 V (peak B) correspond to the reduction of Cr and Li2S to Cr2S3, and Li2S to S.[ 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ] The peaks at 0.154 V (peak C) and 0.85 V (peak D) were attributed to the transition from Cr2S3 to Cr and Li2S, and S to Li2S. With the increase of scanning rates, the peaks A and B shift to higher potentials with larger area and peaks C and D shift to lower potentials, proving the greater voltage polarization and more sluggish reaction kinetics.

The cycling performance of the HEBM Cr2S3 ASSB was investigated, with an areal Cr2S3 mass loading of 1.91 mg cm−2 at a constant current density of 0.096 mA cm−2 (0.064 C, 1C = 776.2 mAh g−1 based on Cr2S3 active material) for the first five cycles and 0.7 mA cm−2 (0.47 C) for the following cycles between 0 to 2.6 V versus Li+/LiIn at 30 °C (Figure 2e; Figure S11, Supporting Information). The HEBM Cr2S3 ASSB was cycled stably for more than 200 cycles with an initial discharge and charge capacity of ≈694.7 and 1134.3 mAh g−1, respectively, which is higher than hand milling Cr2S3 sample (Figure S12, Supporting Information). The second discharge and charge capacities were 1146.0 and 1142.3 mAh g−1 with the Coulombic efficiency of 100.32%, proving that the produced sulfur can reversely convert, leading to stable charge and discharge capacities in the following cycles. After 200 cycles, the discharge capacity remained to 785.6 mAh g−1, corresponding to a capacity retention of 73.1% compared to the discharge capacity (1074.8 mAh g−1) at the seventh cycle. The HEBM Cr2S3 cathode shows a fading rate of 0.14% per cycle after 200 cycles. The corresponding electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results before and after cycling confirm that the battery shows a slight increase of the interfacial resistance (Figure S13, Supporting Information). Regarding the rate performance, the battery demonstrated high discharge specific capacities (1074.5, 1013.4, 908.6, 647.6, and 430.2 mAh g−1) and high Coulombic efficiencies (100.16%, 100.3%, 99.9%, 99.8%, and 99.8%) across various current densities and C‐rates ranging from 66.6 to 1333.33 mA g−2 and 0.086 C to 1.7 C, indicating good rater capability (Figure 2f; Figure S14, Supporting Information).

The HEBM Cr2S3 ASSB with an areal loading of 0.64 mg cm−2 was fabricated and investigated their cycling stability at 0.48 mA cm−2 (0.97 C) (Figure 2g; Figure S15, Supporting Information). The HEBM Cr2S3 ASSB cycled stably for over 1900 cycles with the discharge capacity of 1144.2 mAh g−1 and a low fading rate of ≈0.029% per cycle. The specific capacity gradually increased in the first 150 cycles to the maximum value of 1254.0 mAh g−1. It showcases a capacity retention of over 80% after 650 cycles, maintaining cycling stability for more than 1900 cycles and achieving a Coulombic efficiency exceeding 99.9% (Figure 2g). The Cr2S3 based ASSB is among the best compared to the literature regarding the MSx based ASSBs in terms of specific capacity and cycle stability (Figure S16 and Table S1, Supporting Information).

As shown in Figure 3c, the calculated DLi+ in HEBM Cr2S3 ASSB is mainly in the range of 10−12 to 10−10 cm2 s−1 during the discharge process, although lithium transport was restrained during the discharge plateaus at ≈0.252 V versus Li+/LiIn. To investigate the electrochemical changes in the battery during different electrochemical states, we conducted in situ EIS in the first cycle. During the discharge process, the impedance of the battery decreased from 50 to 12 Ω in the mid/low‐frequency region (Figure 3e). Conversely, during the charge process, the impedance increased from 12 to 85 Ω (Figure 3g). To gain a deeper understanding of the impedance evolution throughout the charge and discharge process, the distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis was carried out (Figure 3f,h). The peaks observed at 10−6−10−5, 10−4−10−3, 10−3−10−2, and 10−2−10−1 s represent the resistance of SSE, solid electrolyte interface and cathode electrolyte interphase, Rct1, and Rct2, respectively. As the voltage decreases from 1.28 to 0 V versus Li+/LiIn, the Rct slightly decreased due to the formation of highly conductive and evenly distributed Cr elements. In contrast, during the charge process, the conversion of Cr to Cr2S3 leads to an increase in Rct. The conversion of Cr2S3 and Cr during the charge and discharge process predominantly influences the Rct resistance.

The HEBM Cr2S3 ASSBs were disassembled and subjected to ex situ XPS measurement to disclose the component evolution of cathode active materials in the cathode with different states (Figure 3i; Figures S17 and S18, Supporting Information), including HEBM Cr2S3 cathode, HEBM Cr2S3 cathode in the first discharge state, HEBM Cr2S3 cathode in the charge state, and Cr2S3 cathode in the 10th cycle. Among the different states, the P 2p exhibited two distinct peaks at 131.5 and 132.9 eV across various states, indicating the stability of the LPSC electrolyte. Upon initial discharge, the S 2p spectrum revealed a peak at 160.5 eV, corresponding to Li2S formation, thus confirming the conversion of sulfur produced from HEBM Cr2S3 into Li2S. Subsequent battery charge led to the reversion of Li2S to sulfur, with parts of Li2S remaining in the cathode. Notably, after 10 cycles, complete conversion of sulfur to Li2S was observed upon discharge, as evidenced by the absence of sulfur peaks in the XPS spectrum (Figure 3i).

To demonstrate the efficacy of HEBM in integrating sulfur into various MSx materials and to evaluate their versatility as cathode active materials for high‐performance ASSBs, MoS2, NbS2, and FeS2 with theoretical capacities of 670 (0.6–3.6 V versus Li+/LiIn), 682.8 and 893.4 mAh g−1 were subjected to the same treatment as Cr2S3. For the HEBM MoS2 ASSB (Figure 4a), the cycling performance was investigated with an areal loading of 1.91 mg cm−2 at constant current densities of 0.127 mA cm−2 (0.1 C, 1C = 670 mAh g−1 based on MoS2 active material) at first fifth cycles and 0.382 mA cm−2 (0.3 C) at following cycles between 0.4 to 2.9 V versus Li+/LiIn. The MoS2 cathode cycled stably for more than 600 cycles, with an initial discharge and charge capacity of ≈288 and 663.5 mAh g−1 and Coulombic efficiency of 43.4% (Figure 4a,b). Then the discharge and charge capacity in the second cycle were 632.9 and 666.6 mAh g−1 with the Coulombic efficiency of 105.32%. After 600 cycles, the discharge capacity remained 416.3 mAh g−1, corresponding to a capacity retention rate of 76.2% compared to that at seventh cycle (546.4 mAh g−1). The HEBM MoS2 ASSB shows a fading rate of 0.05% per cycle after 600 cycles. With a lower areal loading of 0.64 mg cm−2 cycled at 0.306 mA cm−2 (0.72 C), the battery was cycled stably for over 1400 cycles (Figure 4c; Figure S19, Supporting Information). After 1400 cycles, the discharge capacity remained 556.8 mAh g−1, corresponding to a capacity retention rate of 88.8% compared to that at seventh cycle (627.0 mAh g−1) (Figure 4c).

The NbS2 cathode with an areal loading of 1.91 mg cm−2 cycled stably for more than 400 cycles, with an initial discharge and charge capacity of ≈440.0 and 779.2 mAh g−1 and Coulombic efficiency of 56.5% (Figure 4d). After 500 cycles, the discharge capacity remained 616.0 mAh g−1, corresponding to a capacity retention rate of 70.3% compared to that at seventh cycle (876.0 mAh g−1). The HEBM FeS2 ASSB an areal loading of 0.64 mg cm−2 cycled stably for over 1400 cycles. The corresponding EIS results before and after cycling confirm that the battery using HEBM NbS2, FeS2, and MoS2 shows a slight increase of the interfacial resistance (Figure S22, Supporting Information). The similar increase capacity during the charge process and stabilized cycling performance in the HEBM FeS2, MoS2 and NbS2 ASSB were also observed, proving the key role of converted sulfur from LPSC through HEBM, same as result from Cr2S3 battery.

In summary, we reveal that surpass theoretical capacity of MSx was primarily due to the sulfur decomposition from sulfide‐based electrolyte. By employing the HEBM technique, we intentionally promote the deposition of LPSC electrolyte onto sulfur, resulting in a higher charge capacity compared to the discharge capacity and surpass theoretical limits of metal sulfides. Using Cr2S3 as the active material, the sulfur decomposed from LPSC transforms into Li2S after discharge, leading to an enhanced capacity of ≈439.6 mAh g−1 and improved cycling stability. It showcases a specific capacity surpassing 1200 mAh g−1 with a capacity retention of over 80% after 650 cycles, maintaining cycling stability for more than 1900 cycles and achieving a Coulombic efficiency exceeding 99.9%. Our versatile HEBM approach enables the fabrication of ASSBs utilizing various transition metal sulfides, such as MoS2, NbS2, and FeS2, all exhibiting over theoretical limited capacity and prolonged cycling capabilities. The ability to exceed theoretical limits and achieve outstanding performance parameters brings us closer to realizing more efficient and sustainable energy storage solutions to meet the growing demands of our modern world. These batteries hold promise for a wide range of applications, including electric vehicles, renewable energy integration, portable electronics, aerospace, and beyond.

Methods

The Synthesis of Transition Metal Sulfide and LPSC

To synthesize the transition metal trisulfide including Cr2S3, MoS2, NbS2, and FeS2, the chemical vapor transport method was used by annealing the pure transition metals power and sulfur in the quartz ampoule under the vacuum condition. In details, the Cr, Mo, Nb, Fe, and S powers (Aladdin reagent, 99.9%) were weighted according to the molar ratio in the Cr2S3, MoS2, NbS2, and FeS2, with total amount of ≈1 g. Then the powder mixtures were kept in the muffle furnace at 700 °C for more than 48 h after sealing the precursor powders in the quartz ampoule under the high vacuum condition. The transition metal sulfides were obtained after smashing the quartz ampoule and stored in the glovebox for subsequent usage.

For the synthesis of LPSC, the precursors including Li2S (Ganfeng Lithium Co., Ltd. 99.9%), P2S5 (Sigma‐Aldrich, 99%), and LiCl (Aladdin reagent, 99.99%)) with molar ratio of (1.9: 1: 1.6) were mixed and sealed in the ZrO2 based ball milling tank to prevent the leakage of air during the ball milling process. After 400 to 600 min HEBM (Spex 8000 M), the grey colored powders were collected and pressed them into pellets, which process was carried out in the glovebox. These pellets were put into muffle and annealed at 500 °C for 8 h and then converted to LPSC crystals. The pellets were grounded into fine powers for subsequent usage.

The Fabrication of Composite Cathode

The Cr2S3, LPSC, LiI, and LiBr with weight ratio of 12: 18: 3: 1 were weighted in the glove box and dissolved in the acetonitrile solution, which was mixed through water‐sonication for 30 min. Then the acetonitrile was evaporated at 80 °C overnight and completely removed by annealing these powders at 200 °C for 2 h. Then the mixture and VGCF with weight ratio of 40: 3 were transferred to ball milling tank. To prevent potential contamination, zirconium oxide‐based ball milling tanks were used and sealed by vacuuming the tank in a small chamber within the glove box. 20 zirconia balls, each with a diameter of 5 mm and a combined weight of 9.54 g, were mixed with 2 g samples in the Spex 8000 m Mixer/Mill. This setup was then operated at 1080 cycles per min (60 Hz model) for 60 min. Following a cooling period of at least 1 h, the zirconia tank was taken out and returned to the glove box for sample collection. As for the other cathodes like MoS2, NbS2, and FeS2, the weight ratio and treatment process of these cathodes are same as that of Cr2S3.

The Assembly of all‐Solid‐State Battery

The all‐solid‐state batteries were assembled by employing the LPSC solid electrolyte in combination with Cr2S3 mixture cathode as active materials and a LiIn alloy anode in the argon‐filled glovebox. First, ≈80 mg of LPSC powder was placed into a PEEK cylinder with diameter of 10 mm and pressed at 300 MPa for 1 min to make the powder into pellet. Then on the side of LPSC pellet, ≈2–5 mg of composite cathode mixture was weighted and pressed at 360 MPa for 3 min. On the other side of the pellet, a thin indium foil (10 mm in diameter and 100 µm in thickness, 99.99% purity) was attached and ≈2 mg Li foil was cut with the diameter of 3 mm and placed on the surface of indium foil. The cell was placed into a home‐made stainless‐steel casing with a constant applied pressure of 50 MPa during the cycling. The FeS2, MoS2, and NbS2 with cathode weight of ≈2–5 mg based all‐solid‐state batteries were assembled by same process with that of the Cr2S3 based all‐solid‐state batteries. All batteries underwent cycling and rating performance tests using the NEWARE Battery Test System in a thermostat‐controlled environment at a constant temperature of 30 °C.

The applied specific current and the measured specific capacity are based on the mass of sulfur in the cathode. The EIS spectrum was obtained on the CHI 760 and Gamry electrochemical station form 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz under the potentiostatic mode with a bias of 30 mV at room temperature. The CV test of Cr2S3 battery was carried out on the CHI 760 electrochemical station with a scan rate from 0.1 to 5 mV s−1 and voltage between 0 to 2.6 V versus Li+/LiIn. For the GITT measurements, the mold cells were first finished full discharge process and charge process between 0–2.6 V versus Li+/LiIn and then tested with a pulse current of 0.13 mA cm−1 for 5 min and a resting time for 2 h at room temperature.

Structural Characterization

The structures of LPSC, Cr2S3, and Cr2S3 composite cathode were characterized by XRD (using Cu Kα radiation). An XRD air‐sensitive sample holder with sealed tape was used to load the sample in the argon‐filled glovebox and then transferred out of glovebox for XRD characterization. The TGA experiment was conducted on Q50 Thermogravimetric Analyzer (TA Instruments). The morphology of Cr2S3 crystals, Cr2S3 cathode before and after cycling, were characterized by SEM (Joel, 10 kV/5 kV) and EDS mapping. XPS measurements were performed in an ESCALAB 250 spectrometer using monochromatic Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV). To test the XPS spectrum and the depth analysis of Cr2S3 based all‐solid‐state batteries under different charge and discharge conditions. The sample was first sealed in the blue silk mouth glass bottle in the glovebox and then transferred to the XPS chamber within 1 min to prevent them reacting with the water in the air. The disassembled batteries were taken and placed over the conductive carbon tape. To determine the content of Li ions in the Cr2S3 phase after high‐energy ball milling, the Cr2S3 and LPSC powders with weight ratio of 1:1 was first obtained by high‐energy ball milling for 60 min. The mixture was then put into deionized water and vacuum‐filtered to remove excess LPSC after rinsing with deionized water for more than three time. Then the resided powers on the surface of filtration membrane were attached to the conductive carbon tapes for the subsequent XPS characterization.

The Raman spectrum of the Cr2S3 powder and in situ Raman experiments were characterized by using equipment (Micro Rman, InVia (Renishaw), using the 514.5 nm laser). The TEM (JEOL, JEM 2010, 200 kV) of LPSC and HEBM Cr2S3 samples were prepared by dissolve the LPSC particles in the toluene solution and then dripped on the Cu grids in an argon‐filled glove box (H2O < 0.1 ppm and O2 < 0.1 ppm). The grids were then transferred to the TEM holder and inserted into TEM vacuum chamber as soon as possible to prevent their exposure to humidified air.
