Dimensionality-driven optical and electronic evolution of naphthalenediimide-templated hybrid metal oxides
Authors: Madeline C. Schearer, Ilya S. Sosulin, W. Lakna N. Dayaratne, Aliaksandra Lisouskaya, Adam Jaffe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We generate hybrid molybdenum oxides featuring naphthalenediimide (NDI) derivatives, whose redox-activity enable emergent optoelectronic behavior that depends on inorganic dimensionality. We isolate an extended NDI-templated 2D molybdenum oxide—that features the largest-known interlayer spacing for any such hybrid oxide—and a 0D NDI-polyoxomolybdate, allowing us to directly compare molecular, cluster, and extended systems. Combined light absorption/emission and temperature-, light-, and power-dependent EPR studies suggest photogenerated NDI radical anions in molecular and cluster systems, with the latter featuring enhanced photoluminescence. In contrast, the extended structure stabilizes light-independent spins that couple strongly to the lattice. This work expands the physical and electronic boundaries of 2D hybrid molybdenum oxides and points toward addressable spin and charge states for quantum material applications.

Introduction

Hybrid organic–inorganic metal oxides integrate the stability and electronic versatility of solid-state inorganic oxides1–3—stemming from extended metal–oxygen bonding4–6—with the diverse chemical utility of molecular functional groups.7–16 During these hybrids' assembly, molecular attributes such as steric profile, charge density, and intermolecular interactions like H-bonding direct the local coordination, connectivity, dimensionality, and topology of the resulting metal oxide component(s) (Fig. 1),16,17 yielding 0D clusters,17–19 1D chains,20 or 2D layered architectures.7–10,16 Once assembled as hybrid systems, emergent behavior arises: molecular species retain physical and chemical behaviors derived from their functional groups that are inaccessible to inorganic metal oxides, while the molecules' proximity to metal oxide units can allow extraction or delivery of charge that is necessary for generation of unpaired spins, catalytic turnover, or production of photocurrent—uncommon characteristics for molecules alone.

(C) (4-pyNDI)Mo4O13. H atoms and disorder are omitted for clarity. Gray, blue, red, and teal spheres are C, N, O, and Mo atoms, respectively.

Molybdenum oxides and their mixed-valence “bronzes”2—so-named for their metallic luster—represent intriguing parent oxides from which to derive organic–inorganic hybrids. They feature in numerous fields owing to their favorable charge transport and light absorption,21–23 ion exchange,24–27 catalytic activity,28,29 and usefulness as model systems for solid-state physical phenomena.5 Molybdenum-based hybrids form a range of metal–oxygen-based dimensionalities.2,30–32 Discrete molybdenum oxide clusters represent finite (zero-dimensional) MoOn building blocks whose electronic structures are described by localized molecular orbitals based on Mo 4d and O 2p states.17–19 One-dimensional systems16,17,20 feature continuous Mo–O arrays along one axis, yielding more extended electronic structures (bands). However, strong confinement effects limit long-range electronic communication. Two-dimensional systems, including parent α-MoO3, possess extended inorganic sheets that produce solid-state behaviors distinct/enhanced from clusters or chains: multidirectional band-type electronic structures,4,5 facile charge transport,32,33 magnetism and long-range (dis)order,34,35 and (photo)catalytic activity.28,29 Such two-dimensional motifs are exciting for hybrid design because they still allow theoretically infinite interlayer expansion to accommodate large molecules, while retaining favorable extended electronic structure. Building on others' seminal work,9–11,13,20,36 we recently expanded the phase space of layered hybrid molybdenum oxides7—demonstrating synthetic control of band structure and charge-carrier concentration and, consequently, electronic conductivity and infrared/visible light absorption via the generation of hybrid bronzes.37,38 In a limited capacity, we introduced redox-/photo-active molecular ligands such as disulfides and azo compounds as proofs-of-concept. Importantly, since organic species within these layered molybdenum oxides are charge-neutral, this class of materials is not beholden to charge-balancing requirements for molecular packing density, in contrast to many other prominent hybrid systems like halide perovskites39,40 and metal–organic frameworks (MOFs).41,42 Yet, the vast molecular potential of hybrid metal oxides remains underexplored.

Naphthalenediimides (NDIs) are planar aromatic molecules featuring attractive redox and photoactive properties for inducing, studying, and leveraging phenomena such as charge transfer, charge transport, and photoluminescence.43,44 NDIs have been explored in the context of electrochromics,45 photocatalysis,46 and photovoltaics,47,48 and since their extended π systems can support radical species,49 they can stabilize spins critical for quantum information processing.50,51 Not only do their high electron affinity and planarity promote π–π interactions, leading to emergent properties in their molecular solids,52,53 but they can also be integrated into hybrid materials—e.g., MOFs54,55 and halide perovskites56—wherein they can exhibit photoinduced electron transfer between inorganic and organic components. However, it should be noted that rarely has atomic-level information, i.e., crystal structures, been reported for such hybrids and though these systems exhibit exquisite tunability and technological potential, they can suffer from solvent and temperature instability, do not generally support electronic doping of the inorganic component, and tend to feature less extended and more localized electronic structures.

In this work, we integrate naphthalenediimide derivatives into hybrid metal oxides—producing the first example of their incorporation within a 2D metal oxide. Not only does this work redefine size boundaries for molecular incorporation in hybrid oxides by exhibiting an interlayer spacing more than 1.5 times greater than the largest-known reported example, but it provides a tunable framework for the elucidation of phenomena such as electron transfer and stabilization. We systematically study how dimensionality impacts structural, optical, and electronic behavior by contrasting our two-dimensional NDI-based metal oxide with its zero-dimensional oxide cluster analog and the molecular NDI species. Such fundamental understanding will aid future implementation in electrochromic devices, photovoltaics, quantum information processing systems, and beyond.

Results and discussion

To incorporate NDI moieties within layered hybrid molybdenum oxides, we targeted a derivative featuring terminal pyridyl units, given their commonality within this structural family.7 We isolated 4-pyridyl-naphthalenediimide (4-pyNDI) as previously reported.57 Combining 4-pyNDI, MoO3, and Mo metal in a 1 : 1 : 4 molar ratio under hydrothermal conditions at 105 °C for two days afforded crystals suitable for X-ray structure determination of (4-pyNDI)0.5MoO3 (Fig. 1B). This two-dimensional hybrid crystallizes in the orthorhombic Pbca space group, featuring molybdenum oxide layers pillared by 4-pyNDI. The pyridyl rings are twisted by 63.2(5)° relative to the NDI core. Nearest-neighbor NDI planes are not parallel—they are angled 37.4(3)° relative to each other—yet π–π interactions are likely, given a distance of 3.234(5) Å from one NDI core to the nearest carbon of the next molecule. As in our previous reports,7 we find hydrothermal conditions are not required to isolate bulk solids—the precursors can be stirred under mild aqueous conditions (see SI) to afford the desired microcrystalline powder (Fig. S1). Vibrational spectra show peaks attributable to 4-pyNDI, as well as features matching those reported for bridging and terminal Mo–O modes (Fig. S2–S3).37

By altering the reactant molar ratio of 4-pyNDI : MoO3 to 1 : 2, removing Mo metal, and raising the temperature to 150 °C for three days, crystals suitable for structure determination were isolated for the zero-dimensional hybrid solid: (4-pyNDI)Mo4O13. This triclinic P1̄ structure features Mo8O26 polyoxomolybdate clusters surrounded by protonated 4-pyNDI molecules (Fig. 1C). Each metal center is octahedrally coordinated by six oxygen atoms that include terminal as well as bridging (µ2, µ3, and µ5) oxos. Bulk diffraction patterns match the simulated pattern from single-crystal data (Fig. S4), and vibrational spectra again confirm the presence of organic and metal oxide components (Fig. S2–S3).

We employed X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy to interrogate the oxidation state(s) of metal centers. Interestingly, in the molecular cluster, (4-pyNDI)Mo4O13, the Mo 3d region indicates both Mo(v) and Mo(vi) species with an approximate composition of 60% and 40%, respectively (Fig. S5). We attribute this mixed valence to in situ reduction from 4-aminopyridine liberated from the hydrolysis of molecular 4-pyNDI under high-temperature high-pressure aqueous conditions. Similar in situ reduction by aliphatic and aromatic amines has been observed in many such hydrothermal syntheses.7,13 In contrast, (4-pyNDI)0.5MoO3 exhibits only features corresponding to Mo(vi), indicating an oxidized inorganic lattice.

We collected diffuse reflectance spectra for microcrystalline 4-pyNDI, (4-pyNDI)Mo4O13, and (4-pyNDI)0.5MoO3 (Fig. 2, S6 and Table S1). The spectrum of 4-pyNDI shows intense absorption above ca. 2.95 eV that we assign as an NDI-based π–π* transition.58,59 The 0D (4-pyNDI)Mo4O13 displays similar absorption ca. 2.91 eV, suggesting a similar electronic transition and implying the metal oxide cluster's HOMO–LUMO gap is larger than that of 4-pyNDI. Extended (4-pyNDI)0.5MoO3 features a lower-energy onset ca. 2.31 eV, consistent with excitation across a band gap defined by filled O 2p states and empty Mo 4d states, similar to band gaps reported for other hybrid molybdenum oxides.37 Higher energy absorption ca. 2.9 eV supports excitation of the molecular NDI, tracking with the other two compounds.

Normalized diffuse reflectance spectra (pseudoabsorbance = α/S; α and S are absorption and scattering coefficients, respectively) of hybrid molybdenum oxides and 4-pyNDI.

We measured photoluminescence (PL) for the molecular and hybrid solids (Fig. 3). When excited with 3.06 eV (405 nm) light, 4-pyNDI shows weak, broad emission maximal ca. 430 nm (2.88 eV), likely corresponding to the π–π* transition. Contrastingly, the (4-pyNDI)Mo4O13 cluster exhibits peaks at 2.85 eV (435 nm) and 2.66 eV (465 nm) as well as a shoulder at 2.52 eV (492 nm) that collectively show qualitatively enhanced emission intensity compared to 4-pyNDI. Excitation–emission maps (Fig. S7) indicate that these features correlate with maximum excitation at 3.02 eV (410 nm). The 435 nm emission likely corresponds to the unperturbed π–π* transition, while the 465 nm and 492 nm emission bands may correspond to the progression of vibronic states described for other NDI derivatives.60,61 Here, favorable solid-state packing with the polyoxomolybdates may avoid aggregation-induced PL quenching that could explain the poor emission from molecular 4-pyNDI. Alternatively, these features could arise from states stabilized by coupling to the clusters themselves.62,63 It is unlikely these lower-energy bands in (4-pyNDI)Mo4O13 are generated from 4-pyNDI aggregation; such emission features tend to be more significantly redshifted and broadened,61,64 and, furthermore, the NDI units are relatively separated within the hybrid cluster compound, with no evidence of π–π interactions. We collected time-resolved single photon counting measurements, exciting at 371 nm and monitoring emission at 435 and 465 nm (Fig. S8). Emission at both wavelengths in both compounds exhibits biexponential decay, with faster time constants ca. 0.3–0.6 ns and slower ones from 2.2–3.3 ns. The similar decay constants for the 435 and 465 nm bands suggest they originate from the same molecular π–π* electronic transition—with the redshifted peak corresponding to a vibrationally coupled transition. Similar emission profiles are observed for NDI species in solution without proximity to polyoxometalates,60,64 supporting that in this case the inorganic clusters may serve to prevent nonradiative transitions by diminishing intermolecular quenching. Photoluminescence is almost entirely quenched in the extended solid, indicating drastically different ground and/or excited state behavior.

Photoluminescence spectra of hybrid molybdenum oxides and 4-pyNDI (λex = 405 nm).

Given the sharp contrast between the photophysical responses of molecular 4-pyNDI, 0D (4-pyNDI)Mo4O13, and 2D (4-pyNDI)0.5MoO3, we examined 4-pyNDI carbonyl CO bond distances and vibrational frequencies for evidence of bond weakening associated with the formation of radical anions (Tables S4–S6 and Fig. S2–S3). No significant differences are observed crystallographically and the extended solid only exhibits minor differences in its FT-IR spectrum such as splitting of a higher-frequency feature to two peaks at 1729 and 1714 cm−1 as well as a small redshift of its broad intense feature to 1665 cm−1 compared with ca. 1675 cm−1 in the other two compounds (Fig. S3B). However, we note that these spectroscopic differences may simply be associated with the 4-pyNDI incorporation in the rigid extended lattice instead of a molecular solid. Further, literature reports for NDI-containing solids suggest that crystallography and vibrational analysis are not always sensitive to NDI oxidation state (see SI discussion).

We therefore employed electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements as a sensitive probe to evaluate the existence and behavior of unpaired spins. At room temperature (Fig. 4), 4-pyNDI shows a structureless singlet at g ∼ 2.0036, aligning well with reported solid-state NDI-based radical anions.49 Meanwhile, (4-pyNDI)Mo4O13 shows a weaker signal with a main component near g ∼ 2.0038 and shoulders near g ∼ 2.0026 and g ∼ 2.0060. Finally, (4-pyNDI)0.5MoO3 exhibits the strongest signal: a dominant g ∼ 2.0038 feature and a shoulder near g ∼ 2.0026. We do not detect signals attributable to reduced metal centers or oxygen vacancies,65 even at low temperature. Instead, spectral evolution from 195–355 K is largely temperature independent; the primary changes occur in signal intensity (Fig. S9–S10). The extended solid displays near-perfect Curie-like behavior over the full temperature range, indicating a stable population of localized S = ½ centers. 4-pyNDI appears Curie-like above ca. 275 K, while lower-temperature deviations are consistent with partial saturation. The cluster compound's temperature-dependent behavior is less definitive due to low signal intensity. However, some S = ½ centers are likely given that the component at g ∼ 2.0038 exhibits Curie-like behavior.

EPR spectra of hybrid molybdenum oxides and 4-pyNDI under ambient conditions.

Broadband photoirradiation produces markedly different sample responses (Fig. S11–S14). For 4-pyNDI and (4-pyNDI)Mo4O13, the g ∼ 2 peak-to-peak intensity (maximum-to-minimum difference) increases under irradiation, then returns to its initial value within hours in the dark, demonstrating reversible photogeneration of paramagnetic species. In contrast, (4-pyNDI)0.5MoO3 shows no appreciable light dependence of EPR intensity at room temperature, indicating its dominant paramagnetic population pre-exists in the dark and is likely not light-dependent (see SI discussion). Microwave power saturation measurements at room temperature (Fig. S15–S17) estimate effective T1 (spin-lattice) and T2 (spin–spin) relaxation parameters for the dominant spectral components: 4-pyNDI yields the slowest relaxation (T1 ∼ 3417; T2 ∼ 33 ns)—which falls in the typical range for organic radicals.66–68 Shorter T1 and slightly longer T2 were obtained for (4-pyNDI)Mo4O13 (T1 ∼ 515; T2 ∼ 44 ns) and (4-pyNDI)0.5MoO3 (T1 ∼ 308; T2 ∼ 110.5 ns).

Conclusions

Taken together, the light absorption and emission results along with temperature-, light-, and power-dependent EPR analysis support assignments for optoelectronic behavior in the molecular and extended systems. In 4-pyNDI, the single EPR line that grows under irradiation and decays in hours is consistent with a photo-generated radical anion (4-pyNDI˙−) whose presence may partially suppress π–π* radiative transitions. In (4-pyNDI)Mo4O13, weak photoinduced growth of a three-component EPR spectrum likely indicates photoinduced charge separation and trapping in at least three distinct environments. The similar EPR decay kinetics to 4-pyNDI and weaker intensity are consistent with lower steady-state photoinduced 4-pyNDI˙− yield and additional exchange-coupled/interfacial contributions from the polyoxomolybdate that potentially stabilize radiative transitions such as π–π* emission. In the extended (4-pyNDI)0.5MoO3 framework, the intense, light-independent Curie-like EPR signal indicates a large, stable population of ground-state localized S = ½ centers whose markedly faster relaxation relative to the molecular compounds supports coupling to the lattice—consistent with the metal–ligand bonds and the absence of photoluminescence. Given our reports of H+ intercalation to charge-balance mixed-valence metal oxide layers in hybrid bronzes,7,37,38,69 we hypothesize that similar proton insertion without metal reduction provides charge balance for stable molecular radical anions, which is supported by our XPS spectra showing no Mo(v). Future investigation could allow for stable, controllably ordered and coupled spins within an easily synthesized hybrid material—an exciting prospect for next-generation quantum materials.
