Extraskeletal Ewing's Sarcoma/Primitive Neuroectodermal Tumor of the Breast in a Young Woman
Authors: Shejuti Sharmin, Fahad Mohammad Sadat, Farzana Hafiz, Md. Mahabub Alam, Ishtiaq Ur Rahim, Marzia Afrin Reme, Nazirum Mubin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExtraskeletal Ewing's sarcoma/primitive neuroectodermal tumors (EES/PNET) of the breast are rare malignant soft‐tissue tumors associated with a high recurrence rate. We present the case of a 17‐year‐old female who presented with a lump in the left breast. Ultrasonography and mammography revealed a large, ill‐defined, hypoechoic, heterogeneous, dense lobulated lesion without calcifications. Fine needle aspiration cytology showed atypical cells. Computed tomography of the brain, chest, and abdomen ruled out extra‐mammary origin and metastasis. Wide local excision of the lesion followed by histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses confirmed the diagnosis of primary EES/PNET of the breast. The patient underwent adjuvant chemotherapy. Early diagnosis and multidisciplinary treatment are essential to improve prognosis and survival rates.

Summary Primary extraskeletal Ewing's sarcoma/primitive neuroectodermal tumors of the breast is extremely rare but should be considered in young patients with rapidly growing breast masses. Early diagnosis through imaging, histopathology, and immunohistochemistry, followed by multimodal therapy (surgery, adjuvant chemotherapy) and regular follow‐up, is crucial for better prognosis and long‐term survival.

Introduction

Ewing's sarcoma (ES) is the third most common primary malignant bone tumor following osteosarcoma and chondrosarcoma [1, 2]. Ewing's Sarcoma Family Tumors include classical Ewing's sarcoma of bone, extraskeletal Ewing's sarcoma (EES), Askin tumors, and peripheral primitive neuroectodermal tumors (pPNET) arising from soft tissues [3]. It mostly affects the skeletal structures of adolescents and young adults, although it is occasionally observed in adults. EES is an uncommon, aggressive, and malignant soft‐tissue neoplasm. EES/PNET of the breast was reported with fewer than 1% of occurrences in the literature [4]. Its diagnosis requires immunohistochemical study and the detection of a translocation (11;22) (9q24;q12) [5]. Management typically involves a multimodal approach including chemotherapy, surgery, and radiotherapy, depending on the stage of the cancer.

Here we discuss a rare instance of EES/PNET of the breast in a 17‐year‐old female, who had a painless and progressively enlarging breast lump. The patient's family granted their approval for the case report.

Case History

A 17‐year‐old female presented with a painless mass in the left breast, progressively increasing in size over 7 months, and becoming painful during the last 3 months. There was no history of fever, weight loss, nausea, vomiting, or nipple discharge. She had no significant past medical history or familial predisposition to breast or ovarian cancers.

Physical examination revealed a firm‐to‐hard mass measuring 60 × 50 mm in the upper‐outer quadrant of the left breast, mobile with respect to the underlying structure and overlying skin, with no nipple retraction or axillary lymphadenopathy. The right breast and axilla examination was unremarkable.

Methods

USG identified a large ill‐defined hypoechoic, heterogeneous mass, measuring about 62.1 × 59.0 × 53.4 mm in size (volume‐102.44 cc) along the upper and lower outer quadrant near the areolar region (Figure 1). Doppler study showed increased central as well as peripheral vascularity. No ductal changes or left axillary lymphadenopathy were noted. Moreover, no such changes were noted in the right breast including the right axilla. Mammography revealed a large dense lobulated lesion with heterogeneously dense parenchyma with no micro or macro calcifications (Figure 2).

An initial diagnosis of suspected fibroadenoma/phyllodes/epidermal inclusion cyst/mammary carcinoma could not be ruled out without a biopsy. On fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) atypical cells were seen. Based on these finding, the patient underwent wide local excision of the tumor, and the sample was sent for a frozen section and excisional biopsy.

Histopathological examination showed a malignant tumor composed of small round blue cells (Figure 3A) arranged in sheets and islands separated by dense fibrous tissue. Cells have round to oval nuclei and a high nuclear‐cytoplasmic ratio, and ill‐defined cytoplasm. Focally, Homer‐Wright pseudorosettes were seen. Cells in mitosis were frequent (~40 per 10 HPF). Areas of hemorrhage, hyalinization, and necrosis were present. The edges of the tumor were moving outward, and it did not spread into the nearby mammary parenchyma. There was no identified in situ carcinoma component. Lympho‐vascular invasion and perineural invasion were not identified. Resection margins were free from tumor.

(A) Malignant tumor composed of small round blue cells arranged in sheets and islands separated by dense fibrous tissue; (B, C) immunohistochemical staining showed that the tumor cells are diffusely and strongly positive for CD 99 (MIC2); (D) immunohistochemical staining showed that Pan‐Cytokeratin (PanCK) was patchy positive; (E) CD45 was immunonegative and (F) Synaptophysin tumor was negative.

Immunohistochemical staining showed that the tumor cells are strongly positive for CD 99 (MIC2) (Figure 3B,C), vimentin, and Pan‐Cytokeratin (PanCK) (Figure 3D) were patchy positive. CD45 (Figure 3E), desmin, and synaptophysin tumor (Figure 3F) were negative. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) of NKX2.2, Ki‐67, and FISH tests were not done. Computed tomography scans of the brain, thorax, and abdomen excluded metastasis and extra‐mammary origin. The patient was identified as a primary case of EES/PNET of the breast based on the aforementioned findings.

Conclusions and Results

Due to the tumor's rarity, genetic counseling and oocyte preservation were discussed before chemotherapy initiation. The patient has just completed 17 cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy using the VAC regimen (vincristine, adriamycin, and cyclophosphamide) alternating with the IE regimen (ifosfamide, and etoposide) every 3 weeks. Gonadotropin‐releasing hormone analog was administered concurrently. Regular follow‐up was maintained during chemotherapy, and no major side effect was noted. A follow‐up CT scan is scheduled after 3 months.

Discussion

EES was first described by Angervall and Enzinger in 1975 [6]. Common primary sites for EES include the chest wall, paravertebral region, retroperitoneal space, lower limbs, and gluteal area. In addition to its typical sites, EES/PNET has been documented in a diverse array of uncommon anatomical locations. These encompass visceral organs like the kidneys, breasts, gastrointestinal tract, liver, pancreas, and adrenal glands; elements of the genitourinary system such as the urinary bladder, ureter, penis, seminal vesicle, testis, ovary, vulva, vagina, and uterine cervix; in addition to structures in the head and neck region, including the maxillary sinus, parotid gland, and trachea. There has also been evidence of rare involvement in the brain and lungs [7, 8]. EES is known for its aggressive clinical behavior characterized by a high rate of recurrence and distant metastasis, thereby significantly impacting patient prognosis [9].

EES commonly affects adolescents and young adults aged 10–20 years [9]. Although primary breast involvement by EES is exceptionally rare, several cases involving individuals older than 30 years have been documented [10, 11, 12, 13]. Patients typically demonstrate discomfort and swelling in the afflicted location [3]. The majority of patients appear with a distinct and well‐defined breast lump; however, manifestations may range from small cystic lesions to inoperable large tumors [11, 14, 15, 16, 17]. EES presenting as a primary breast lesion in the elderly is exceptionally rare and poses a diagnostic challenge. The differential diagnosis includes fibroadenoma, phyllodes tumor, and malignancy. USG (USG) and mammography results may vary. Similar cases in the literature have been recorded and first misclassified as fibroadenoma, phyllodes tumor, and breast carcinoma. This diagnostic dilemma delays the treatment [13].

The emergence of EES/PNET within breast tissue is exceedingly improbable to be detected at the initial presentation. Imaging modalities such as mammography, USG, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography‐computed tomography (PET‐CT) aid in diagnosis; nevertheless, the imaging findings are nonspecific. Results from mammography and USG of breast pictures may differ, potentially indicating a hypoechoic mass with posterior enhancement or a heterogeneous mass with a necrotic region [10, 15]. The diagnosis is often validated through histological and immunohistochemical investigation [9]. Standard histological and immunohistochemical analyses are adequate to achieve an accurate diagnosis of EES/PNET in the majority of instances. Genetic analysis is highly beneficial when morphology is inconclusive or when cancers manifest in atypical clinical contexts. Over 85% of ES is defined by the occurrence of the characteristic translocation t (11;22) (q24;q12). Immunophenotyping further confirms the diagnosis of EES/PNET, demonstrating positive for vimentin, Fli‐1, and CD99 (MIC2) [5]. In this instance, CD99 (MIC2) and vimentin were identified as positive. Molecular approaches employed for the identification of ES translocations linked to PNET are essential instruments for definitive diagnosis. The techniques encompass DNA‐ and RNA‐based polymerase chain reaction, Southern blotting, and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) [18].

The diagnosis of Ewing Sarcoma/PNET in this case is strongly supported by classic histopathological features, including small round blue cell morphology, presence of Homer‐Wright pseudorosettes, and diffuse membranous CD99 positivity—hallmarks of Ewing Sarcoma. Additional immunohistochemical findings, such as patchy Pan‐Cytokeratin (occasionally seen in the PNET variant) and the exclusion of mimickers like lymphoma (CD45‐negative), rhabdomyosarcoma (desmin‐negative), and neuroblastoma (synaptophysin‐negative), further strengthen the diagnosis. Age, tumor location, and radiological features further support the diagnosis. Although molecular tests such as NKX2.2 IHC, FISH, or RT‐PCR could increase diagnostic specificity, they were not performed due to cost and limited availability in Bangladesh. In current local clinical practice, especially within resource‐constrained settings in Bangladesh and neighboring countries like India, diagnosis based on morphology and a targeted IHC panel remains a valid and accepted approach [19, 20].

Currently, no standardized treatment guidelines specifically tailored to primary breast EES/PNET exist due to its rarity. Therefore, therapeutic strategies have been adapted from protocols established for soft‐tissue sarcomas in general. Surgical excision aiming for clear margins constitutes the cornerstone of local disease control. Breast‐conserving surgery supplemented by radiation therapy is considered an acceptable approach, provided negative margins can be ensured. Axillary lymph node dissection is typically not indicated, given the hematogenous rather than lymphatic route of spread associated with EES/PNET [21]. When tumor‐free margins are difficult to achieve, mastectomy remains a viable option.

In cases where consensus among reference centers is absent, smaller tumors have been treated with adjuvant radiotherapy after a thorough discussion involving the oncologist, pathologist, and surgeons [21]. Adjuvant chemotherapy plays a crucial role in improving outcomes for high‐risk patients by delaying both distant and local recurrence. Furthermore, research suggests that combination regimens offer significant benefits for individuals facing advanced cancer. However, there is a lack of compelling evidence suggesting that adjuvant treatment improves overall and relapse‐free survival rates [21]. In the study by Kim et al., the patient presented with a well‐defined lesion and subsequently underwent a mastectomy, followed by adjuvant therapy that included vincristine, Adriamycin, and cyclophosphamide [17]. It is recommended that alkylating drugs and anthracyclines serve as the primary treatment option [21]. In cases where tumors are inoperable or patients show signs of distant metastases, chemotherapy serves as the main treatment approach [15, 21]. De Silva et al. presented a case involving primary inoperable breast PNET that showed a positive response to a chemotherapy regimen consisting of cisplatin, etoposide, and doxorubicin, followed by radiation, leading to a disease‐free survival period of 2 years [15]. Over the last four decades, advancements in localized therapy and multiagent adjuvant chemotherapy have markedly improved the treatment of localized disease, boosting the 5‐year survival rate from below 20% to above 70%. However, it is important to note that the recurrence rate remains high. Nevertheless, most cases are found locally, and subclinical metastatic disease is apparent in almost every instance. Roughly a quarter of individuals who initially present with localized illness will ultimately face a recurrence. Recurrent and refractory EES/PNET lacks a well‐established treatment, with survival rates of less than 30% for patients with isolated lung metastases and < 20% for those with bone and bone marrow involvement. Given the harmful nature and poor survival outcomes linked to metastatic disease, there is a pressing need to develop new treatments for EES/PNET of the breast [3].

In summary, EES/PNET of the breast are exceedingly rare and frequently present diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Histopathological confirmation is essential for all enlarging breast tumors, regardless of seemingly benign clinical and radiographic features. In cases of concern, immunohistochemical analyses must be conducted for an appropriate diagnosis of EES/PNET. Early detection of the disease and prompt treatment through a multidisciplinary approach are crucial for enhancing prognosis.
