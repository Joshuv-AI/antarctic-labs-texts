Impact of microvascular decompression on wall shear stress in responsible arteries in trigeminal neuralgia and hemifacial spasm
Authors: Toru Satoh, Yoichi Sawada, Isao Date, Takao Yasuhara, Kentaro Fujii
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Microvascular decompression (MVD) has proven efficacy in trigeminal neuralgia (TN) and hemifacial spasm (HFS). This study utilized computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to investigate the impact of MVD on wall shear stress (WSS) of responsible arteries (RAs) at the neurovascular contact (NVC). A total of 21 cases (10 TN, 11 HFS) were analyzed, involving RAs at NVC validated through intraoperative photographs. Hemodynamic parameters (WSS, vessel diameter, flow rate, pressure drop) was calculated using CFD for the RAs based on 3D silent-magnetic resonance angiograms. The NVC was segmented into NVC-proximal, NVC-site, and NVC-distal portions using simulated 3D CFD images that correlated with surgical observations. WSS ratios of NVC-site to NVC-proximal (NVC-site/proximal) was calculated both before and after MVD. Prior to MVD, WSS in the RA at the NVC displayed a peaked curve with a maximum at NVC-site; however, post MVD, it presented a smooth curve without peaks. The WSS ratio exhibited a significant decrease after MVD. The impact of MVD on WSS of RAs at NVC was evaluated in both TN and HFS cases. Analyzing the hemodynamics of RAs through CFD and identifying WSS peaks at NVC portions before MVD provided a more detailed and localized understanding of the morphologically depicted NVC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73217-x.

Introduction

While the precise pathogenesis of trigeminal neuralgia (TN) and hemifacial spasm (HFS) remains elusive, these conditions often result from the compression of the trigeminal and facial nerves by responsible arteries (RAs)1. Microvascular decompression (MVD) effectively alleviates symptoms in TN and HFS patients by relieving the nerve compression caused by RAs. Neurovascular contact (NVC) induced by RAs has been traditionally assessed morphologically through surgical procedures and preoperative simulation imaging findings2–4. However, pinpointing more specific locations within the NVC has proven challenging. Recently, the hemodynamics of RAs at NVC have been examined, revealing that RAs exhibit high wall shear stress (WSS) in these locations5–9. This study aimed to evaluate RAs at NVC in TN and HFS, both before and after MVD, using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to assess the impact of MVD on RAs at NVC. Evaluating NVC from a hemodynamic perspective before and after MVD aimed to provide a more detailed and localized understanding of the morphological NVC. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first report examining the hemodynamic characteristics of RAs at NVC in TN and HFS, both before and after MVD.

Results

Statistical analysis results in TN (n = 10)

WSS exhibited relatively high values at the NVC-s. The postoperative ratio of NVC-s/NVC-p (1.096 ± 0.108 Pa) significantly decreased compared to preoperative values (1.307 ± 0.147 Pa) (Z = − 2.50, p < 0.05, effect size: r = 0.79, post-hoc power 58.1%). However, no significant change was observed in the NVC-s/NVC-p ratio for vessel diameter (before MVD: 0.938 ± 0.057 mm, after MVD: 0.972 ± 0.038 mm, Z = − 1.07, non-significant, effect size: r = 0.34), flow rate (before MVD: 1.001 ± 0.011 mL/min, after MVD: 1.000 ± 0.010 mL/min, Z = − 0.15, non-significant, effect size: r = 0.05), and pressure drop (before MVD: 1.331 ± 1.278 mmHg, after MVD: 1.108 ± 0.606 mmHg, Z = − 0.46, non-significant, effect size: r = 0.15) (Fig. 1). As for NVC-s/NVC-d, no significant changes were observed before and after MVD in any of the hemodynamic parameters, including vessel diameter (before MVD: 0.976 ± 0.037 mm, after MVD: 0.970 ± 0.052 mm, Z = − 0.15, n.s., effect size: r = 0.05), flow rate (before MVD: 1.001 ± 0.010 mL/min, after MVD: 0.998 ± 0.008 mL/min, Z = − 0.76, n.s., effect size: r = 0.24), WSS (before MVD: 1.206 ± 0.191 Pa, after MVD: 1.200 ± 0.272 Pa, Z = − 0.05, n.s., effect size: r = 0.02), and pressure drop (before MVD: 1.346 ± 0.960 mmHg, after MVD: 1.497 ± 0.513 mmHg, Z = − 0.56, n.s., effect size: r = 0.18).

Statistical analysis results in HFS (n = 11)

WSS demonstrated relatively high values at the NVC-s. The postoperative WSS ratio of NVC-s/NVC-p (0.952 ± 0.109 Pa) significantly decreased compared to preoperative values (1.275 ± 0.159 Pa) (Z = − 2.93, p < 0.05, effect size: r = 0.88, post-hoc power 72.5%). However, no significant changes were observed in the NVC-s/NVC-p ratio before and after MVD for vessel diameter (before MVD: 0.932 ± 0.049 mm, after MVD: 1.000 ± 0.102 mm, Z = − 1.96, non-significant, effect size: r = 0.59), flow rate (before MVD: 0.999 ± 0.010 mL/min, after MVD: 0.999 ± 0.012 mL/min, Z = − 0.09, non-significant, effect size: r = 0.03), and pressure drop (before MVD: 2.530 ± 3.762 mmHg, after MVD: 1.343 ± 0.884 mmHg, Z = − 0.62, non-significant, effect size: r = 0.19) (Fig. 2). Regarding NVC-s/NVC-d, the WSS ratio after MVD (0.947 ± 0.226 Pa), it significantly decreased compared to preoperative values (1.345 ± 0.454 Pa) (Z = − 2.31, p < 0.05, effect size: r = 0.70, post-hoc power 53.1%). However, no significant changes were observed in the NVC-s/NVC-d ratio before and after MVD for vessel diameter (before MVD: 0.962 ± 0.090 mm, after MVD: 0.985 ± 0.055 mm, Z = − 0.62, non-significant, effect size: r = 0.19), flow rate (before MVD: 1.005 ± 0.014 mL/min, after MVD: 1.000 ± 0.010 mL/min, Z = − 0.18, non-significant, effect size: r = 0.05), and pressure drop (before MVD: 3.814 ± 4.594 mmHg, after MVD: 1.111 ± 1.502 mmHg, Z = − 1.78, non-significant, effect size: r = 0.54).

Post-hoc power analysis

Post-hoc power analysis of the conducted significance tests suggested that to detect a statistically significant difference with 80% power, at least 1.2 to 1.7 times more participants (13 to 19 individuals) would be needed in both TN (n = 10) and HFS (n = 11) analyses. However, even if we take a conservative approach (even if we commit a Type II error), the observation of statistical significance in WSS within a small sample size does not necessarily negate the findings of this study.

Clinical outcome

We conducted a three-year follow-up to evaluate the clinical effects of MVD. As a result, in all cases of TN (n = 10), pain disappeared shortly after MVD, and no recurrence was observed during the three-year follow-up period. In HFS cases (n = 11), facial spasms disappeared shortly after MVD in all but one case, and no recurrence was observed during the three-year follow-up period. In one HFS case, mild facial spasms recurred six months after the surgery. Therefore, the effectiveness of MVD in both TN and HFS was generally deemed successful.

Discussion

Before planning MVD for TN and HFS, identifying the RAs compressing the nerves at the NVC is crucial. Conventional methods like computed tomographic angiography (CTA) and X-ray digital subtraction angiography (DSA) are invasive and carry risks.

In this investigation, we employed a non-intrusive methodology utilizing silent MRA with a silenz scan sequence instead of time-of-flight (TOF)-MRA. The vessels depicted by TOF-MRA do not represent the true vascular geometry but rather show images influenced by flow voids related to the peak systolic velocity of blood flow through the vessel lumen. Particularly at the NVC site, RAs in contact with surrounding structures or those that are curved may be depicted as excessively narrowed or inadequately visualized. In contrast, silent MRA, which uses blood protons as tracers, provides a more accurate depiction of the blood morphology within the vessel lumen, making it superior for visualizing the morphology of the responsible vessels at the NVC site10,11. Since CFD analysis strongly depends on vascular morphology, silent MRA was considered to have advantages over TOF-MRA in accurately depicting the morphology of the responsible vessels Additionally, MRC was employed to visualize anatomical elements at the NVC, distinguishing cerebrospinal fluid, arteries, veins, cranial nerves, and brain parenchyma based on signal intensity. The 3D multifusion images from silent MRA and MRC vividly depicted the anatomical relationship between RAs and trigeminal and facial nerves at the NVC5,8. These images served as a reference for comparing and analyzing CFD of the RAs at the NVC before and after MVD.

CFD analysis involves the utilization of various parameters, including streamlines, vessel diameter, flow rate, WSS, pressure drop, wall pressure, and oscillatory shear index. Among these parameters, WSS stands out as the most commonly used5. WSS signifies the tangential frictional force exerted by the flowing blood on the vessel wall and is quantified in Pascals (Pa). WSS values fluctuate with the flow rate, with an increase in velocity as the flow approaches vessel constriction resulting in elevated WSS. However, after passing through the constriction, WSS decreases. In sections of the vessel with curvature, the eccentricity of flow influences these phenomena, causing an increase in WSS on the outer curve and a decrease on the inner curve.

Recent studies have revealed distinctive hemodynamic patterns in RAs at the NVC in cases of TN and HFS5–9. Notably, within the NVC, RAs demonstrate a tendency for higher WSS compared to the proximal and distal portions around the NVC. Within the NVC, RAs come into contact with the trigeminal and facial nerves, potentially leading to morphological changes such as deformation and indentation due to nerve compression5,11. Simultaneously, the movement of RAs may be constrained by influences from cranial nerves. Consequently, both RAs and cranial nerves at the NVC exhibit limited ranges of motion, suggesting that in RAs, this may induce hemodynamic features related to constriction, characterized by suppression and immobility5,8. This constriction, coupled with an increase in flow velocity, results in a rapid and concentrated flow within the vessel lumen. The intricate interplay between RAs and cranial nerves at the NVC implies specific hemodynamic patterns for RAs. From a hemodynamics perspective, this observation indicates the potential to facilitate a more detailed and localized identification of NVC beyond morphological considerations.

In this study, we conducted CFD analysis on RAs at NVC both before and after MVD for TN and HFS. Hemodynamic parameters, including streamlines, vessel diameter, flow rate, WSS, and pressure drop, were measured for each individual case. The RA within the NVC portion was segmented into three distinct portions—NVC-p, NVC-s, and NVC-d based on simulation images corresponding to the surgical findings. Ratios of NVC-s to NVC-p and NVC-s to NVC-d were calculated before and after MVD. Subsequently, we calculated the relative ratios of NVC-s/NVC-p and NVC-s/NVC-d and statistically analyzed the changes in these parameters before and after MVD for each case.

The results demonstrated elevated WSS values in NVC-s/NVC-p before MVD, with a significant decrease post-procedure. Conversely, no significant alterations were noted in the diameter, flow rate, or pressure drop in NVC-s/NVC-p. These patterns were consistent not only across cases of TN but also for HFS and the overall dataset. Despite potential anatomical and morphological distinctions in NVC between TN and HFS, the hemodynamic characteristics of RAs showed no discernible differences. Moreover, MVD effectively alleviated the contact and compression of cranial nerves by RAs at the NVC, resulting in similar hemodynamic changes in RAs for both TN and HFS. Additionally, the observed hemodynamic peak state in RA at NVC before MVD disappeared after the procedure, revealing a relatively uniform flow pattern without peak in RA after MVD.

Benefits and perspectives of CFD analysis of RAs in NVCs

In this study, non-contrast silent MRA was used for CFD analysis of RAs at the NVC site, rather than invasive CTA or DSA. While MRA has been widely used for non-invasive visualization of cerebral vasculature, accurately representing luminal morphology as depicted in DSA or CTA is challenging with TOF-MRA due to flow information related to peak systolic flow velocities within the vessels. In contrast, silent MRA depicts the flowing blood within the arteries themselves, allowing for the visualization of fine luminal morphology. In this regard, the volume data from Silent-MRA are useful for conducting stereolithography of CFD analysis based on arterial luminal morphology information8,12.

Neurovascular compression syndromes such as trigeminal neuralgia, hemifacial spasm, and glossopharyngeal neuralgia are believed to be caused by RAs compressing the trigeminal, facial, and glossopharyngeal nerves, respectively. Regarding trigeminal neuralgia, the etiology and pathophysiological mechanisms are not yet fully understood4. In classical idiopathic trigeminal neuralgia, MRI examinations often reveal NVC at the nerve root entry zone of the trigeminal nerve in 80–90% of cases, which is considered a characteristic finding of nerve compression injury13. It has been reported that in the trigeminal nerve compressed by RA, nerve displacement and distortion frequently occur. The sensitivity and specificity of MRI for diagnosing NVC are reported to be 66% and 90%, respectively, while in cases of nerve atrophy, sensitivity and specificity are 54% and 97%, respectively, and in cases of nerve dislocation, sensitivity and specificity are 39% and 97%, respectively. In cases of root entry zone contact with atrophy, sensitivity and specificity are reported to be 52% and 100%, respectively14.

However, morphological MRI findings of NVC are often observed in many asymptomatic individuals at any location along the trigeminal nerve, from the brainstem to Meckel’s cave. In asymptomatic NVC, the NVC of the unaffected trigeminal nerve is usually less severe than that of the affected nerve, and it does not cause nerve displacement or distortion as frequently15. While affected trigeminal nerves in TN patients tend to have severe NVC, asymptomatic nerve NVC in healthy individuals or patients is mainly reported as simple contact forms11.

Distinguishing between symptomatic and asymptomatic NVC based solely on morphological information obtained from MRI is often challenging. Additionally, determining the site of nerve compression by RA at the NVC in symptomatic TN cases can also be difficult morphologically. The gold standard for NVC is confirmation during surgery, where MVD surgery is performed to completely relieve nerve compression by RA through extensive displacement or prosthesis insertion. However, even during surgery, it is often difficult to identify which part of the RA running parallel to the nerve is the compression point of NVC based solely on intraoperative morphological findings15.

In this study, RAs involved in NVC were subjected to CFD analysis. As a result, RA sites causing WSS peaks at the NVC site were observed, and after MVD surgery to release compression, symptom improvement was observed, and postoperative CFD analysis confirmed the resolution of WSS peaks in the RA. Thus, retrospective identification of hemodynamic NVC points was possible. Therefore, preoperative CFD analysis of NVC sites in RA can prospectively reveal the hemodynamic status of the RA lumen, allowing for the inference of hemodynamic collision points not obtained from morphological information, including MR images and surgical findings. The success of MVD appears to be confirmed by the relief of nerve compression by RA, disappearance of symptoms, morphological findings, and hemodynamic findings obtained through CFD analysis. In the future, sophisticated CFD analysis is expected to provide new opportunities to evaluate NVC and offer insights into the pathogenesis and pathophysiology of neurovascular compression syndromes.

Limitation

When evaluating changes in WSS in RA before and after MVD using CFD analysis, several limitations must be considered. Firstly, the sample size in this study was limited, and future research should aim to include a larger number of cases. Although the three-year clinical outcomes following MVD were generally favorable, longer-term follow-up is necessary. The CFD analysis relies on several assumptions that approximate the in vivo state, such as modeling blood as a Newtonian fluid, assuming rigid arterial walls, predefined outflow conditions, and non-patient-specific flow boundary conditions. These assumptions highlight the inherent constraints of hemodynamic evaluation through CFD. Additionally, the analysis focuses on arteries (RA) compressing cranial nerves, excluding cases of venous compression or cases without compressing vessels. Another limitation is the challenge of accurately selecting RAs and cranial nerves constituting NVC from silent MRI volume data when creating multifusion images for surgical simulation and stereolithography for CFD analysis. In this study, RAs were identified with a signal intensity threshold of 300 or higher in MRA, while cranial nerves were identified with a threshold of 1200 or lower in MRC. Moreover, the segmentation of RAs at the NVC regions, including NVC-p, NVC-s, and NVC-d, depends on simulation images or surgical photos, which may not perfectly correspond to the actual NVC regions. Lastly, the WSS values of RA are influenced by the individual blood flow conditions of each patient, making direct comparison of absolute values between different patients, or even within the same patient, challenging. To address this, the study utilized relative ratios to compare WSS in RA before and after MVD.

Conclusion

In this study, we assessed the hemodynamic impact on RAs at NVC in TN and HFS before and after MVD. Prior to MVD, the WSS ratio at the NVC (WSS-s/WSS-p) in RAs exhibited a high value, which significantly decreased after MVD. This reduction in the WSS ratio in RAs was attributed to the resolution of focused blood flow and vascular constriction in RAs resulting from the release of compression through MVD. Analyzing the hemodynamics of RAs through CFD and identifying WSS peaks at NVC portions before MVD provided a more detailed and localized understanding of the morphologically depicted NVC. This was deemed valuable for preoperative assessment of MVD.

Methods

Patient population

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Ryofukai medical corporation (Approval Number: IRB: 2022-03, approval date: August 2022). All research procedures adhered to the applicable guidelines and regulations, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients involved in the study. Between September 2017 and August 2022, 36 patients diagnosed with TN (25 cases) and HFS (11 cases) underwent microvascular decompression (MVD) performed by a single operator (I.D.) at our hospital (Table 1). Within the TN cases, 7 experienced recurrences, 6 had venous compression, and 2 had adhesions, leading to the exclusion of 15 cases. This study focused on the 10 TN patients (10/25 cases) who underwent initial MVD and exhibited RAs at the NVC. The median age of the TN patients (4 males, 6 females) was 65.0 years (38–80 years), with neuralgia occurring on the right side in 6 patients and on the left side in 4 patients. RAs included the trunk of the superior cerebellar artery (SCA) in 7 cases and a caudal segment of the SCA in 3 cases. For HFS, all 11 patients (5 males, 6 females) who exhibited RAs at the NVC during the initial MVD were included. The median age was 63.0 years (28–73 years), with the right side affected in 4 patients and the left side in 7 patients. RAs included the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) in 6 cases, the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) in 4 cases, and the vertebral artery (VA) in 1 case. Postoperatively, pain disappeared in all 10 cases of trigeminal neuralgia. Among 11 cases of hemifacial spasm, recurrence occurred in one at 6 months postoperatively, while the rest experienced resolution of spasms after the initial surgery.

Imaging procedures

The imaging by the silent MRA with silenz scan sequence (zero-echo time and arterial spin-labeling tagging) and the heavily T2-weighted 3D fast spin-echo MR cisternography (MRC) was performed in the same session on a 3T unit (Signa Pioneer; GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, Wisconsin)5,8. The MRA was performed with the following parameters: TR/TE, 880/0.016 ms; flip angle, 5°; FOV, 180 3 180 mm; matrix, 200 3 200; spokes per segment, 320; section thickness, 1.0 mm; NEX, 1.5; bandwidth, 31.25 kHz; acquisition time, 12 min 48 s. A total of 400 continuous axial source images were acquired. The MRC was performed with the following parameters: TR/TE, 1900/100 ms; FOV, 180 3 180 mm; matrix, 3,563,256; section thickness, 1.2 mm; NEX, 1; bandwidth, 31.25 kHz; echo-train length, 128; acquisition time, 5 min 53 s. A total of 60 continuous axial source images were acquired.

Image processing

The volumetric datasets of silent MRA and MRC were transferred to a workstation (Ziostation-2; Ziosoft-AMIN Corp., Tokyo)8,12. For 3D silent MRA, the vascular structures were reconstructed using a perspective volume rendering algorithm. Arterial lumen selection was based on the opacity chart of the dataset, where arteries were identified with higher signal intensity (threshold ≥ 300) compared to the background. 3D MRC was reconstructed from the entire dataset, including areas with lower signal intensity of cerebrospinal fluid (threshold ≤ 1200), depicting anatomical elements constituting the NVC, such as RAs, trigeminal and facial nerves, and brain parenchyma. The integration of MRA and MRC into a single 3D image was achieved using the 3D multifusion imaging technique, combining MRA and MRC 3D images. At 3 months post MVD surgery, images from silent MRA and MRC were re-examined, and postoperative 3D multifusion images were created.

CFD analysis

CFD analysis was conducted using a commercial package (Hemoscope v1.4, EBM & AMIN Corp., Tokyo)5. Patient-specific geometries were generated in a stereolithography format using pre- and post-operative 3D silent MRA, encompassing the proximal end of the VA, basilar artery, PICA, AICA, SCA, and the proximal segment of the posterior cerebral artery. Blood was modeled as an incompressible Newtonian fluid with a density () of 1050 kg/m3 and viscosity () of 0.004 Pa/s. The vascular geometries were reconstructed and filled with unstructured hexahedral cells, primarily. The mesh size in far-wall regions was set to 0.25 mm, and the near-wall mesh consisted of three layers, with a width of 0.125 mm and a height of 0.05 mm at the wall-nearest mesh. Unsteady pulsatile simulations were carried out, adjusting the time step to maintain a Courant number less than one. For boundary conditions, a time-averaged flow rate () was initially estimated using the formula = (⁄32) 3, where represents the magnitude of WSS ( = 1.5 Pa)16, and is the diameter of the inlet artery. The distribution of flow rate at vascular bifurcations was set based on the ratio of the cubic diameters of the branches. A physiological pulsatile waveform was imposed on the steady flow rate17.

The vascular geometry of the RA at the NVC portion was segmented into NVC-p (proximal portion just before NVC), NVC-s (NVC site), and NVC-d (distal portion just after NVC) based on simulation images corresponding to the surgical findings. CFD was then employed to analyze hemodynamic features specifically on the RA at the NVC. Various hemodynamic parameters, including streamlines, vessel median diameter (mm), cycle-averaged flow rate (mL/min), cycle-averaged mean WSS (Pas), and cycle-averaged pressure drop (mmHg) at the segmented portions of the RA, were individually and respectively analyzed before and after MVD.

Identification of NVC before and after MVD

The anatomical relationship between the RA and the cranial nerve at the NVC was simulated using 3D multifusion images2,3,5,8. The NVC was then meticulously compared and identified, utilizing corresponding surgical findings obtained from operative photographs or video recordings during MVD. With reference to the 3D multifusion images, the NVC-p, NVC-s, and NVC-d portions of the NVC were delineated and identified on the computer-generated 3D CFD images. The alignment of NVC location between 3D multifusion images and 3D CFD images underwent independent review by four Japanese board-certified neurosurgeons (T.S., I.D., T.Y., K.F.), with any disagreements resolved through consensus.

Statistical analysis

Four numerical hemodynamic parameters obtained from CFD analyses, comprising vessel median diameter (mm), cycle-averaged flow rate (mL/min), cycle-averaged mean WSS (Pas), and cycle-averaged pressure drop (mmHg), were measured at each portion of NVC-p, NVC-s, and NVC-d. The relative ratios of NVC-s/NVC-p and NVC-s/NVC-d were calculated for each individual case. Changes in the relative ratios for each parameter before and after MVD underwent statistical analysis. Statistical significance was assessed using two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction for comparison of hemodynamic parameters before and after MVD. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS for Windows version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and G*Power 3.1.9.7, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.
