Heterogeneous causal mediation analysis using Bayesian additive regression trees
Authors: Chen Liu, Xu Qin, Victor B Talisa, Jiebiao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCausal mediation analysis provides insights into the mechanisms through which treatments affect outcomes. While mediation effects often vary across individuals, most existing methods focus solely on population-average effects, overlooking individual-level heterogeneity. To address this limitation, we propose a Bayesian regression tree ensemble method that flexibly models nonlinear relationships and captures treatment-by-mediator interactions in the mediation process. Using hierarchical posterior sampling, our approach provides credible intervals with nominal coverage rates for inferring heterogeneous mediation effects. Additionally, we leverage regression tree summaries to identify subgroups with distinct mediation effects and employ SHapley Additive exPlanation values to highlight key moderators and their influence on the mediation process. Comprehensive simulations demonstrate the method’s accuracy in estimating and inferring heterogeneous mediation effects. Finally, we apply our method to investigate the heterogeneous mediation role of Alzheimer’s disease pathology burden in the effect of apolipoprotein E genotype on late-life cognition.

Introduction

Understanding complex causal pathways is essential in scientific research, especially in answering questions about how, for whom, and under what conditions a treatment affects an outcome. Mediation analyses address the question of how by uncovering the pathways through which a treatment effect (TE) is generated. Under the classical mediation framework (eg, Baron and Kenny, 1986), a treatment influences a mediator, which subsequently impacts the outcome. The total TE can be decomposed into an indirect effect transmitted through the mediator and a direct effect that operates directly or through other unspecified pathways. Across disciplines, empirical researchers use mediation analysis to uncover the mechanisms through which a treatment affects an outcome, thereby informing the design of targeted interventions and policies.

Notably, the direction and magnitude of the mediation effects are often heterogeneous across different subgroups and settings, known as moderators. Therefore, it is important to further address the questions of for whom and under what conditions a treatment works, beyond the question of how, through a heterogeneous (moderated) mediation analysis (eg, Edwards and Lambert, 2007). The treatment or exposure impacts the outcome through the mediator, while moderators may change the direction or strength of one or more paths that transmit the treatment’s effect on the outcome.

However, most existing causal mediation methods focus primarily on population-average effects, potentially obscuring important variations in causal mediation mechanisms across different individual and contextual characteristics. Investigating these variations can help researchers improve and tailor interventions or policies to specific individuals and contexts. Qin and Wang (2024) addressed this gap by incorporating moderators into the regression-based causal mediation analysis. They developed the general definition, identification, estimation, and sensitivity analysis for causal moderated mediation (MM) effects under the potential outcomes framework (Rubin, 1978). Unlike the traditional MM analysis (eg, Preacher et al., 2007), Qin and Wang (2024) considered the treatment-by-mediator interaction when defining indirect and direct effects, accounted for observed confounders of the relationships among the treatment, mediator, and outcome, and evaluated the sensitivity of analysis results to unmeasured confounders. However, their method relies on parametric assumptions about the relationships among the variables. The causal effect estimates may be biased if the models are misspecified. Additionally, their method requires prior knowledge to select appropriate moderators, which can be challenging when such knowledge is limited or when the choice of moderators is not straightforward.

To address these limitations and enable more robust and flexible modeling, it is desirable to develop a machine learning method for heterogeneous causal mediation analysis. Among the machine learning methods used in causal inference, Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART) (Chipman et al., 2010) have consistently ranked among the top-performing methods in the Atlantic Causal Inference Conference Data Challenge over several years (Dorie et al., 2019). Hahn et al. (2020) and Carnegie et al. (2019) explored BART’s capabilities for examining TE heterogeneity and identifying subgroups with different levels of TE. Compared to other methods, BART-based methods are more likely to detect subgroup signals without overfitting (Thal and Finucane, 2023) and perform substantially better in real data (Chipman et al., 2006). Moreover, BART’s ability to provide credible intervals offers a unique advantage for inferring causal effects.

Linero and Zhang (2025) extended the application of BART to causal mediation analysis, enabling robust estimation, and inference of the mediation effects in the presence of nonlinear relationships. While they focused on estimating population-average mediation effects, Ting and Linero (2025) further considered estimating heterogeneous causal mediation effects with BART. They introduced a linear varying-coefficient model based on BART to identify and regularize heterogeneous causal mediation effects. The method allows for flexible investigations of the heterogeneity of causal mediation effects. However, it has several limitations. First, their model assumes no interaction between the treatment and mediator, which can lead to bias when such interactions are present and influential. Second, the method relies on linear varying-coefficient models for the outcome, which may not adequately capture potential nonlinear relationships between the mediator and outcome. Third, they assume small and mostly homogeneous mediation effects, which could make the method conservative in estimating heterogeneity in practical applications.

To overcome these limitations, we develop a nonparametric BART model for heterogeneous causal mediation analysis, termed Heterogeneous Mediation with BART (HMBART). HMBART allows for the treatment-by-mediator interaction, accommodates nonlinear relationships, and can capture large and heterogeneous mediation effects. It extends the approach proposed by Linero and Zhang (2025) to address the more challenging problem of estimating individual mediation effects. Through various simulation settings, we demonstrate that HMBART outperforms existing methods in reducing bias, achieving the nominal coverage rate (CR), and maintaining adequate statistical power.

We organize this article as follows. In Section 2, we introduce our method for conducting heterogeneous causal mediation analysis with BART. In Section 3, we present a series of simulations that demonstrate the superior performance of our method. Section 4 illustrates the application of HMBART in a real-data analysis, in which we assess the heterogeneous mediating roles of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology burden underlying the relationship between the apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype and late-life cognitive function. Finally, Section 5 discusses the advantages and limitations of our proposed method.

Identification of the mediation effects

We observe \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Y_i(1, M_i(1))$\end{document} for the treated group and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Y_i(0, M_i(0))$\end{document} for the control group, whereas \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Y_i(1, M_i(0))$\end{document} remains counterfactual and unobserved for all. BART models

Based on the identification results in Equation 3, we estimate the conditional expectation of each potential outcome and, correspondingly, the conditional causal mediation effects defined in (1) and (2), by fitting models for the treatment, mediator, and outcome. We fit the models through BART, which allows us to flexibly capture nonlinear interactions, including the treatment-by-mediator interaction, and to provide inference for the effects.

In the BART fitting process, prior specifications of the model parameters play an important role. Using naive regularization priors in the presence of confounding can inadvertently lead models to attribute causal effects more to the treatment and mediator than to the confounders (Hahn et al., 2020; Linero, 2024). As a result, the discovery of truly influential confounders can be distorted by regularization, which is known as regularization-induced confounding (RIC). An effective way of mitigating RIC is through clever covariate adjustments (Hahn et al., 2020; Linero and Zhang, 2025; Ting and Linero, 2025). This involves adjusting for the propensity score of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $T$\end{document} in the mediator and outcome models and adjusting for the prediction of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $M$\end{document} in the outcome model, known as clever covariates. The clever covariates serve as summary measures of the confounders in the treatment-mediator, treatment-outcome, and mediator-outcome relationships, encouraging the models to incorporate confounders and thus reducing RIC.

$M_i$\end{document} under both treatment conditions as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} Both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m_{i}(0)$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m_{i}(1)$\end{document} are included to account for the difference in the sets of confounders of the mediator-outcome relationship across treatment conditions.

Bayesian estimation

BART employs an ensemble of Bayesian regression trees to predict response variables. Within this Bayesian framework, the sum-of-trees model requires prior specifications both for regularization and to prevent overfitting (Hill et al., 2020). A detailed prior specification for BART models is provided in Web Appendix A. We denote the sets of priors for models of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $T$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $M$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Y$\end{document} as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\tau _T$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\tau _M$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\tau _Y$\end{document}, respectively. To optimize the models and enhance robustness, we tune these prior specifications via cross-validation, as demonstrated by Chipman et al. (2006) and Dorie et al. (2019).

With specified priors, we can fit the BART models via the Bayesian backfitting MCMC procedure. In each iteration, a posterior sample of tree parameters is generated, based on which the posterior predictions of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $T$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $M$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Y$\end{document} (including the propensity score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\pi$\end{document} and clever covariates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m(0)$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m(1)$\end{document}) are generated. Figure 2 shows the detailed fitting process. We implement BART using the R package bartMachine (Kapelner and Bleich, 2016), which provides fast and reliable model fitting, prediction, and diagnostic checking.

Inference of the mediation effects

We apply the Monte Carlo \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $g$\end{document}-computation approach to estimate mediation effects in nonlinear settings. This method computes the necessary counterfactuals by predicting potential outcomes under a given treatment level, while setting the mediator to its predicted value under the same or a different treatment level. To implement this \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $g$\end{document}-computation framework within our nonparametric Bayesian context, we develop a two-stage hierarchical posterior sampling strategy. This strategy constitutes the core of our inference: we first draw posterior samples from the mediator model and then from the outcome model. To explicitly account for uncertainty arising from the BART predictions, we incorporate both the predicted expectations and associated errors. The errors from the mediator model contribute to the prediction of the outcomes and are thus non-negligible. Therefore, when sampling the potential mediators, we add a random draw from the mediator model’s error distribution to ensure our estimates reflect the full variability in the data.

The step-by-step procedure is as follows. First, we fit the treatment model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\theta _T({\bf X})$\end{document} with all the covariates as predictors and the treatment as the target. Then we estimate propensity scores \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\pi _i$\end{document} for the treatment and construct augmented covariates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\bf X}^{+}_i = (\pi _i, {\bf X}_i)$\end{document} by including the propensity scores in the original covariates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\bf X}_i$\end{document}. This helps balance the distribution of covariates across treatment groups. Then, we fit the mediator model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\theta _M(T, {\bf X}^{+})$\end{document} with both the treatment \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $T$\end{document} and the augmented covariates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\bf X}^+$\end{document} as predictors. The predictions of the mediator for individual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $i$\end{document} under both treatment levels, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m_{i}(0)$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ m_{i}(1)$\end{document}, are included in constructing augmented covariates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\bf X}_i^{++} = (\pi _i, m_{i}(0), m_{i}(1), {\bf X}_i)$\end{document}. They serve as clever covariates to improve the precision of the outcome model. Finally, we fit the outcome model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ \theta _Y(T, M, {\bf X}^{++})$\end{document}.

Given the fitted models, the two-stage hierarchical posterior sampling is formulated as follows. In the first stage, we draw \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $J$\end{document} posterior samples of the potential mediators for each individual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $i$\end{document} under both treatment conditions from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\theta _M(T_i, {\bf X}^{+}_i)$\end{document}. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $j$\end{document}th posterior sample representing the potential value of the mediator for individual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $i$\end{document} is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m_{i}^{(j)}(t), t = 0, 1$\end{document}. Note that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m_{i}(t)$\end{document} used in the clever covariates is the average of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m_{i}^{(j)}(t)$\end{document} across \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $J$\end{document} posterior samples. Specifically, we draw \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m_{i}^{(j)}(t) = \displaystyle \theta _M^{(j)}(t, {\bf X}_i^{+}) + \epsilon _{M_i}^{(j)}$\end{document} from their marginal distributions, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\epsilon _{M_i}^{(j)}$\end{document} are random draws from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mathcal {N}(0, \widehat{\sigma }_M^{2(j)})$\end{document} representing the mediator model’s error terms shared across treatment levels to reduce Monte Carlo variability (Rene et al., 2023), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\widehat{\sigma }_M^{2(j)}$\end{document} is the BART-estimated variance associated with the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $j$\end{document}th posterior sample. With \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m_{i}^{(j)}(t)$\end{document}, the predicted expectations and associated errors of mediators can be incorporated into the prediction of the outcomes.

In the second stage, we draw \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $K$\end{document} posterior samples of the predicted potential outcomes for each of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $J$\end{document} posterior samples of the predicted potential mediators. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $k$\end{document}th posterior sample of the potential outcomes obtained conditional on the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $j$\end{document}th posterior sample of the potential mediators is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $y_{i}^{(jk)}(t, t^{\prime }), t, t^{\prime } = 0, 1$\end{document}. Specifically, we compute \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $y_{i}^{(jk)}(0, 0) = \theta _Y^{(k)}(0, m_{i}^{(j)}(0), {\bf X}_i^{++})$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $y_{i}^{(jk)}(1, 0) = \theta _Y^{(k)}(1, m_{i}^{(j)}(0), {\bf X}_i^{++})$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $y_{i}^{(jk)}(1, 1) = \theta _Y^{(k)}(1, m_{i}^{(j)}(1), {\bf X}_i^{++})$\end{document}.

This creates a nested structure that propagates the uncertainty from the mediator model to the final outcome. Eventually, we have \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $J\times K$\end{document} posterior samples of each potential outcome for each individual. Following Equations 1 and 2, each posterior sample of the heterogeneous mediation effects is calculated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\widehat{\mathrm{NDE}}_i^{(jk)} = y_{i}^{(jk)}(1,0) - y_{i}^{(jk)}(0,0)$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\widehat{\mathrm{NIE}}_i^{(jk)} = y_{i}^{(jk)}(1,1) - y_{i}^{(jk)}(1,0)$\end{document}. The point estimates of the heterogeneous mediation effects are then obtained as an average of all posterior samples of the effects:

\setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $100(1-\alpha )\%$\end{document} credible intervals of the individual effects \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\widehat{\mathrm{NDE}}_i$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\widehat{\mathrm{NIE}}_i$\end{document} are then obtained by taking the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha /2$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $1-\alpha /2$\end{document} quantiles over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $J\times K$\end{document} posterior samples of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\widehat{\mathrm{NDE}}_i^{(jk)}$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\widehat{\mathrm{NIE}}_i^{(jk)}$\end{document}, respectively.

In addition, to mitigate high estimation uncertainty in small samples, we use empirical Bayes shrinkage to borrow strength across similar individuals and stabilize noisy estimates. Empirical Bayes shrinkage is applied to the individual estimates to produce final estimates and corresponding credible intervals (see Web Appendix B). Algorithm 1 details the procedure for fitting sequential models, predicting potential outcomes, and inferring the corresponding heterogeneous mediation effects. After estimating heterogeneous mediation effects and their credible intervals, we further examine how covariates contribute to this heterogeneity. Web Appendix C outlines the procedure for identifying influential covariates and subgroups.

Simulations

We evaluate the performance of our proposed HMBART method by comparing it with two alternative approaches for heterogeneous causal mediation analysis: regression-based causal MM (Qin and Wang, 2024) and varying-coefficient Bayesian causal mediation forest (VCBCMF) (see Web Appendix D for detailed model comparisons).

We generate datasets with 250, 500, and 1000 independent observations. Each covariate in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\bf x} = (x_1, \cdots , x_p)^T$\end{document} is independently drawn from either a uniform or normal distribution, as detailed for each case below. The treatment \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $t$\end{document}, mediator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m$\end{document}, and outcome \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $y$\end{document} are respectively generated from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $t \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\mathrm{logit}^{-1}(t(\boldsymbol{x})))$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m \sim m(t, \boldsymbol{x}) + \mathcal {N}(0, 1)$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $y \sim y(t, m, \boldsymbol{x}) + \mathcal {N}(0, 1)$\end{document}. For each sample size, we examine four cases to generate linear or nonlinear functions, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $t(\boldsymbol{x})$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m(t, \boldsymbol{x})$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $y(t, m, \boldsymbol{x})$\end{document}, leading to a total of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $3 \times 4 = 12$\end{document} scenarios. The detailed simulation settings are provided in Web Appendix E.

Simulations are conducted at the individual level, with evaluation metrics computed for each individual and then averaged across the population over all replications. We evaluate the accuracy of the estimated heterogeneous mediation effects and frequentist properties of their associated credible intervals. For each simulation scenario, we run 200 replications and compare the performance of the three methods based on various evaluation criteria, including the difference between the corresponding average mediation effect and the true effect (bias), root mean squared error (RMSE), proportion of 95% credible intervals containing the true value (CR), and the proportion of 95% credible intervals not containing 0 (power). In addition, we report the variance estimation. For MM, the reported variance is the mean of the estimated mediation-effect variances across all individuals. For VCBCMF and HMBART, the variance is calculated as the variance across all posterior samples of mediation effects for each individual, and then averaged across individuals.

The results demonstrate that HMBART achieves nominal CRs and optimal inferential performance for heterogeneous mediation-effect inference, outperforming existing methods, particularly in nonlinear scenarios. In nonlinear cases, HMBART consistently yields lower bias and RMSE than MM and VCBCMF, emphasizing its capability to handle complex nonlinear mediation mechanisms with heterogeneous effects. As the sample size increases, HMBART exhibits clear improvements over competing methods, with reduced bias and RMSE, improved CRs, and greater power. This indicates consistent improvement in performance as sample size increases.

In scenarios with large, heterogeneous mediation effects, such as Cases 3 and 4, HMBART exhibits higher variance than other methods. However, this larger variance reflects a more conservative assessment of uncertainty, which ensures that the type I error rate is not inflated. In contrast, MM and VCBCMF, with lower variances, struggle to achieve nominal coverage, suggesting their estimates may be overly shrunk.

Overall, these findings highlight the flexibility and effectiveness of HMBART, especially in scenarios involving nonlinear relationships and larger datasets. In contrast, the performance of VCBCMF is inferior because it overlooks treatment-by-mediator interactions, and its linear varying-coefficient framework lacks the flexibility needed to capture complex relationships. In addition, VCBCMF may overshrink the effects when the true mediation effects are large and heterogeneous. HMBART not only offers reduced bias and ensures valid inference but also demonstrates superior flexibility in modeling nonlinear relationships, making it a strong candidate for complex heterogeneous causal mediation analyses.

To assess efficiency, average runtimes were measured for Cases 3 and 4 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $n=500$\end{document}, 10 CPU cores). Using 2000 burn-in and 500 sampling iterations, MM, VCBCMF, and HMBART averaged 6.50, 4.87, and 6.05 min, respectively.

In this study, we examine the relationship between the APOE genotype and late-life cognition, mediated by AD pathology, using data from the Religious Orders Study and the Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP). Discussion

We introduce a novel framework for heterogeneous causal mediation analysis that can flexibly capture complex nonlinear relationships and the treatment-by-mediator interaction in mediation processes. Compared with existing methods, our approach achieves more accurate estimation with lower bias and RMSE for nonlinear data structures. Our method is designed to assess heterogeneity in mediation effects, providing credible intervals with nominal CRs that competing methods often fail to achieve. We implement HMBART as a user-friendly R package. To the best of our knowledge, there is currently a lack of comprehensive software packages specifically tailored for nonlinear heterogeneous causal mediation analysis. The package also includes interpretive tools, such as SHAP variable importance, dependence plots, and regression tree summary plots, to assess the influence of moderators on the magnitude, direction, and significance of a mediation effect.

Future research can extend our approach in several directions. First, sensitivity analysis tools can be developed to assess robustness against unmeasured pretreatment confounding. Second, the framework can be expanded to handle continuous or multi-valued treatments, mediators, and outcomes. Third, more rigorous methods for assessing the importance of moderators and identifying subgroups can be explored. Finally, we focused on one single mediator and required moderators to be pretreatment covariates. Future work will consider a more comprehensive mediation framework involving multiple mediators that may interact with one another. Overall, HMBART is a useful tool that provides low-bias point estimates and valid inference for heterogeneous mediation effects.
