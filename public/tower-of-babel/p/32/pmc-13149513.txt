Optimizing the potential use of waste palm oil clinker powder in cementitious grouts for semiflexible pavements using response surface methodology
Authors: Nasir Khan, Muslich Hartadi Sutanto, Muhammad Imran Khan, Nura Shehu Aliyu Yaro, Rania Al-Nawasir, Arsalaan Khan Yousafzai, Kaffayatullah Khan, Muhammad Arsalan Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCement is one of the major industrial sources of global CO₂ emissions, which highlights the need for sustainable alternatives in pavement construction. In this context, the present study examines the partial substitution of cement with waste palm oil clinker powder (POCP) to develop cementitious grouts for semi-flexible pavements (SFP). In this study, POCP was used at different proportions (0–30%) across water–cement ratios (W/C) ranging from 0.40 to 0.55, in the presence of a superplasticizer to improve grout flowability. The performance of the grout was assessed by measuring flowability and compressive strength at 1, 7, and 28 days. The experiments were designed using Response Surface Methodology (RSM), which was used to analyze statistical correlations and determine the optimal POCP content and W/C. The optimized grout mixture with 20% POCP was subsequently used to produce SFP specimens (PSFP), which were assessed for marshall stability, moisture susceptibility, indirect tensile stiffness modulus, cantabro loss, and resistance to fuel spillage, and then compared with control SFP (CSFP) and conventional hot mix asphalt (HMA). Microstructural analysis using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) indicated that the addition of POCP increased the porosity relative to the control sample. The marshall stability for both CSFP and PSFP was more than twice that of conventional HMA with a moisture-induced loss of about 9%, compared to about 16% in HMA. However, the rigid nature of SFP and the interface between the grout and bitumen-coated aggregates, which creates a weak zone, led to greater mass loss under impact loading than in HMA, highlighting the need for higher viscoelasticity in SFP samples.Keywords: Semi-flexible pavement, Palm oil clinker powder, Response surface methodology, Cantabro loss, Fuel spillage resistanceSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Due to the growth of the global economy, the consumption of cement has grown 2.7-fold between 1998 and 2020 (4.2 billion tons)1. The production of such a large quantity of cement accounts for about 25% of industrial CO2 emissions and 8% of total anthropogenic CO2 emissions, making it the second-largest industrial CO2 emitter globally2,3. These emissions pose serious environmental challenges, not only in the form of CO₂ but also as toxic gases such as nitrogen oxides, sulfur oxides, methane, and heavy metals such as nickel, mercury, cobalt, chromium, and lead4. Such pollutants not only contribute to global warming and climate change but also to serious health problems5–8. Moreover, these emissions affect air quality, forming haze and acid rain, thereby acidifying lakes and streams, which significantly damages aquatic life and lowers fish populations9.

To overcome these emissions, researchers are taking measures to overcome the high consumption of cement by introducing geopolymer mixtures, applying alternative pozzolanic materials, and replacing cement with readily available industrial wastes10–12. Recent research on semi-flexible pavements (SFP) has explored the use of various waste materials as partial replacements for cement in the grouting phase, aiming to enhance sustainability while maintaining mechanical performance13. Several authors have demonstrated that treated waste PET can be incorporated (at low-to-moderate dosages) into cementitious grouts with limited loss of flexural performance and with potential gains in flowability when used alongside fly ash or other SCMs. More recent experimental work has extended the waste palette: waste glass powder and date-palm seed ash have been successfully blended into grout mixes (15–30%), showing promising mechanical behavior and added benefits such as reduced surface temperatures and improved material densification. However, long-term traffic durability still needs substantiation14,15. Likewise, ceramic/porcelain waste powders and marble dust have been trialed as partial cement substitutes in grout formulations, with several studies reporting that modest replacement levels (commonly 10–25%) can maintain acceptable flow and 28-day strength when mix proportions and water-to-cement ratios are optimized16,17. Pumice stone dust has likewise been experimentally evaluated, with 5–20% replacement found effective and an optimum range of 10–15% yielding balanced flowability and strength18. Taken together, the literature indicates that replacing cement with waste materials in SFP grouts is technically feasible for many waste materials, such as PET, glass, ceramic/porcelain, marble dust, pumice ash, fly ash, silica fume, GGBS, etc. However, to date, palm oil clinker (POC) has not yet been evaluated as a cement replacement specifically in cementitious grouts for semi-flexible pavements.

In recent decades, Malaysia’s palm oil sector has experienced significant growth, making the country as the world’s second-largest exporter of palm oil19. From its modest beginnings in the early 20th century, when plantations covered only about 400 ha20, the cultivated area has expanded dramatically, reaching approximately 5.6 million hectares by 202421. According to the Malaysian Palm Oil Board (MPOB)21, the total crude palm oil (CPO) production in 2024 was approximately 19.34 million tonnes. Based on the estimation by Vijaya et al.22, which reported that about 0.02 tonnes of palm oil clinker (POC) are generated per tonne of CPO, the corresponding total POC generation in 2024 was approximately 386,800 tonnes. This waste POC has been used as fine and coarse aggregates, as well as a replacement for ordinary Portland cement (OPC) by up to 30%23. However, as highlighted by Vijaya et al.22 this by-product is currently disposed of predominantly through landfilling, underscoring the need to identify sustainable, value-added applications for POC.

Recent studies have continued to highlight the growing shift toward sustainable cementitious and concrete systems through the incorporation of waste-derived materials, optimized mix design, and data-driven performance evaluation in the concrete industry24–26. Likewise, recent research has increasingly focused on sustainable pavement materials. However, to date, no studies have analyzed the use of POCP in cementitious grouts for SFP. Most existing investigations on POCP have been limited to its use in conventional concrete or mortar, leaving its behavior in highly flowable grout systems and its potential contribution to SFP performance unknown. Literature also lacks systematic optimization of POCP-based grout mixtures using advanced statistical methods such as RSM, which are essential for identifying efficient and reliable mix proportions. To address these gaps, the present study investigates the partial replacement of cement with POCP at different W/C ratios and employs RSM to optimize grout properties in terms of flowability and compressive strength across multiple curing ages. The optimized grout is then incorporated into SFP specimens and assessed through a series of mechanical and durability tests. By comparing the performance of POCP-modified SFP (PSFP) with control SFP (CSFP) and conventional hot-mix asphalt (HMA), this study provides new evidence on the feasibility and performance benefits of using POCP in SFP. Overall, the work introduces a novel application of POCP and presents a structured optimization approach that supports the development of more sustainable semi-flexible pavement systems. Materials

The POC is a grey, lightweight, porous material discarded into landfills, as presented in the Fig. 2a. Various researchers have reported pozzolanic properties in POC while using them as a replacement for coarse, fine, and filler in pavement and concrete27–29. Mix preparation and testing method

Following ASTM C30531, a Hobart mortar mixer was used to prepare the cementitious grouts. Following a sequential procedure, the dry POCP and cement were mixed at a moderate speed for about one minute. In the next phase, around two-third of the required water was added to the dry mix, which was then stirred at low speed for another minute. After this, Superplasticizer (SP), as reported in various studies32–34, was mixed with the remaining water, added to the grout, and mixed for 5 min at a low speed. After this, the grout was stirred at high speed for 3 min, followed by scraping the adhesive off the bottom and sides of the mixing basin. Finally, to ensure homogeneity, the grout was mixed at high speed for 1 min. The well-stirred cementitious grout was poured into a standard flow cone to record the flow time. Finally, the grout was poured into molds of size 50 mm x 50 mm x 50 mm for compressive strength testing at different curing ages.

Flowability

The flowability of fresh cementitious grout was measured using a standard flow cone following ASTM C939/C939M35. To measure the flowability, the flow time of 1725 ml of freshly mixed grout was recorded for the grout to flow out of a standard flow cone shown in Fig. 3. According to Hassan et al.36, the flow time should be between 11 and 16 s to ensure proper filling of voids in the open-graded asphalt mixture. Following this procedure, the flow time of all the grout mixtures with varying combinations of POCP and W/C, following the response methodology (RSM) design, was recorded.

Response surface methodology (RSM) design

RSM is a flexible statistical approach commonly used to plan experiments, study relationships among variables, optimize processes, and verify experimental results. Within the concrete industry, it has been widely adopted for structuring experimental programs, developing predictive models, and examining how different independent factors influence key performance outcomes37. For instance, a study by Khan et al.38 considered SP content and the water–cement (w/c) ratio as the primary input variables, while flowability and compressive strength were treated as the output responses. Another study by Khan et al.39, exploring the use of PET as a partial cement replacement, selected the PET content and fly ash percentage as the key input variables. The study evaluated flowability and compressive strength at 1, 7, and 28 days as the response variables. This research adopted a full factorial design for the experiment and factor analysis, using Design-Expert 13.0 40. Flow time (sec), 1-day compressive strength (CS₁d), 7-day compressive strength (CS₇d), and 28-day compressive strength (CS₂₈d) were measured for 20 unique combinations of POCP content and W/C ratio (POCP% (0–30) and W/C (0.35–0.55) in the presence of 1% SP following the schematics given in Table 2. Each combination was repeated 4 times to ensure the reliability of the results, yielding a total of 80 experimental runs.

The selected POCP replacement levels (0–30%) were determined based on preliminary experimental trials and evidence from the literature. Higher replacement levels during preliminary testing (up to 50%) resulted in noticeable reductions in compressive strength and cohesion, making them unsuitable for semi-flexible pavement applications. Two different studies conducted by Nayaka et al.41,42 have also reported that POCP replacement beyond moderate levels leads to strength reduction due to dilution of cementitious phases, while replacement levels around 30% can still maintain acceptable mechanical performance. On the other hand, the W/C ratio range (0.35–0.55) was selected based on RSM studies and conventional mix design practices, in which W/C is identified as a key factor controlling both rheological and mechanical properties. Previous studies have shown that W/C is typically varied within practical limits (0.35–0.60) to balance flowability and strength, making the selected range suitable for semi-flexible pavement grout systems16,43,44. The SP dosage was fixed at 1% (by weight of binder) to ensure adequate flowability and consistent dispersion across all mixtures. Previous RSM-based studies on cementitious grouts for semi-flexible pavement applications have similarly treated SP as a constant parameter while optimizing other variables. Since the optimum SP dosage depends on the material system and typically ranges between 0.5% and 1%, a representative value of 1% was adopted in this study to focus on optimizing the main variable rather than diluting its effect with additional variables in the mix34,45.

Compressive strength of grouts

Cubic samples of size 50 mm × 50 mm × 50 mm were prepared from each type of grout, and their CS₁d, CS7d, and CS₂₈d were determined. The compressive strength was determined in accordance with ASTM C10946 by applying a load at a rate of 0.90 kN/s using an ELE Universal Testing Machine (UTM). It is important to note that no standardized guideline currently defines the acceptable compressive strength range for cement grouts used in semi-flexible pavement applications. Consequently, target compressive strength values of 7 MPa at 1 day, 15 MPa at 7 days, and 35 MPa at 28 days were adopted in this study based on previous research and industry recommendations16,40,47–49. Marshall testing

Marshall stability and flow tests play a crucial role in assessing the deformation resistance of bituminous mixtures and their capacity to withstand repeated traffic loading. Marshall stability represents the mixture’s maximum tensile strength, indicating its ability to resist rutting at elevated service temperatures. Conversely, Marshall flow quantifies the permanent deformation at the point of failure, providing insight into the mixture’s susceptibility to rutting59. At least three Marshall samples were prepared for CSFP, PSFP, and HMA using a coring machine with an approximate diameter of 101 mm from a slab measuring 305 × 305 mm. However, for HMA, the samples were prepared following D6926-2060. Each HMA specimen was compacted using the standard Marshall hammer, applying 75 blows to each side of the sample. Before testing, the specimens were conditioned in a 60 °C water bath for 25–30 min to replicate field service temperatures. The Marshall test was then performed at a constant deformation rate of 50.8 mm/min. The peak load sustained before failure was reported as the Marshall stability (kN), while the corresponding deformation at this load was recorded as the Marshall flow.

Moisture resistivity

Moisture intrusion has a significant impact on the long-term performance of asphalt pavements, making it essential to evaluate their resistance to moisture-related damage61. The indirect tensile strength (ITS) test is commonly used for this purpose, as it allows assessment of a mixture’s susceptibility to moisture by comparing the strength of conditioned and unconditioned specimens through the tensile strength ratio (TSR). Higher ITS values indicate improved resistance to moisture-induced distresses such as stripping and loss of adhesion between asphalt and aggregates. Previous studies have generally considered a TSR value of 80% or higher as indicative of acceptable moisture resistance37,62. Two sets of specimens were prepared for ITS testing. The first set was placed in a 25 °C water bath for 30 min before testing (ITS dry). The second set was conditioned in a 60 °C water bath for 24 h, followed by 30 min in a 25 °C water bath before testing (ITS wet). ITS values for CSFP, PSFP, and HMA samples were calculated using Eq. 3, with loading applied at 50.8 mm/min until failure, in accordance with ASTM D693163. The TSR was then computed as the ratio of ITS wet to ITS dry, expressed as a percentage (Eq. 4), representing the mixture’s sensitivity to moisture after prolonged conditioning at elevated temperatures.

Indirect tensile stiffness modulus testing

The Indirect Tensile Stiffness Modulus (ITSM) is a key parameter for assessing an asphalt mixture’s resistance to deformation under tensile loading, serving as an indicator of its elasticity and overall stiffness. Because ITSM is highly temperature-dependent, it provides valuable insight into how the material responds under various climatic conditions, including its vulnerability to thermal cracking at low temperatures and rutting at elevated temperatures64. In cold regions where temperatures fall below freezing, excessively high ITSM values may indicate that the mixture is overly stiff, increasing the likelihood of cracking and reducing pavement service life. Conversely, low ITSM values suggest insufficient stiffness, which may lead to permanent deformation under traffic loads. For these reasons, evaluating ITSM across different temperatures is essential for establishing a suitable mix design for specific environmental conditions. In this study, the stiffness modulus of CSFP, PSFP, and HMA specimens was measured at 5 °C, 15 °C, 20 °C, and 25 °C in two loading orientations (0° and 90°), following the procedures outlined in EN 12697-2665. Fuel resistivity using the partial immersion test

Fuel resistivity may enable the researchers to test the mixtures on a small scale and then implement them in places where the probability of the fuel spillage is high, such as parking areas, rest stations on highways, airports, toll stations, etc. As per the standard procedure as provided in BS EN 12697-43, the marshall samples of CSFP, PSFP, and HMA were subjected to 24 h in a diesel depth up to 35 mm. Each sample weight was measured before it was partially immersed and recorded as M1. The samples were removed and rinsed with clean water after 24 h of immersion. They were then left to dry at room temperature for 24 h. At this stage, the samples were weighed once again and recorded as M2. The samples were then subjected to a steel brush to assess the mixes’ resistance to abrasion. The brush followed the epicycloid motion while applying abrasion, attached to the Hobart mixer. The cycles of steel-brush abrasion were applied for 30, 60, and 120 s. The mass of each sample was recorded at the end of each cycle and recorded as M3, M4, and M5, as shown in Fig. 6. Contabro loss

The Cantabro abrasion test was conducted to evaluate the susceptibility to raveling of the dense HMA, CSFP, and PSFP, as it is the most commonly used test method to evaluate the raveling potential of porous asphalt mixture68–70. The test was performed following the general procedure outlined in Tex-245-F Standard “Test Procedure for Cantabro Loss”71. Three replicas of cylindrical Marshall specimens for every combination were conditioned at room temperature before testing. Each specimen was placed inside the Los Angeles abrasion drum without steel charges and subjected to 300 revolutions at a rotation speed of 30–33 rpm. The initial mass (m₁) of the specimen was recorded, and the final mass (m₂) was obtained after brushing off detached particles after the test. The Cantabro loss was calculated as the percentage difference between m₁ and m₂ using Eq. 9. For dense HMA, the test serves primarily as a reference since its low void content limits aggregate loss. In contrast, for CSFP and PSFP, the test provides a reliable measure of the effectiveness of grout filling and the resistance of the composite structure against particle detachment under repeated impacts. According to ASTM standards, the maximum Cantabro loss be 20% for unaged specimens and 30% for aged specimens68,72.

Results

Flowability of fresh grout

The results in Fig. 7 show that both the W/C and POCP replacement significantly affect grout flowability. At lower W/C, mixtures without POCP exhibited relatively poor workability, while the inclusion of POCP consistently reduced flow time and improved flowability. Similarly, as the w/c ratio increased, all mixes exhibited improved workability, with flow times falling within the target range of 11 to 16 sec36. The improvement in flowability observed with increasing POCP content can be attributed to its lower water absorption compared to cement. During mixing, it was observed that the POCP particles exhibited limited wetting, thereby reducing water uptake and increasing the availability of free water in the mixture. This improves lubrication between particles and enhances the grout’s overall flowability.

Effect of POCP % and W/C on flow time.

POCP-modified grouts generally performed better than the control mix, particularly at intermediate replacement levels (10–20%), where the balance between fluidity and cohesiveness is maintained. At a higher replacement rate (30%), the flow became too rapid, approaching the lower end of the acceptable range. From the figure, it can also be observed that for mixes with W/C of 0.45 or lower, the flow time was too high than 16 s, specifically at lower POCP content.

Compressive strength

The results indicate that both the W/C and the partial replacement of cement with POCP strongly influence the compressive strength of the cementitious grouts. At all curing ages, the lowest W/C produced the highest compressive strength values, while increasing the W/C to 0.45, 0.50, and 0.55 progressively reduced strength. This behavior is consistent with established findings that higher water content increases porosity and weakens the hardened matrix68. The effect of POCP replacement was also notable. At each W/C, the control grout (0% POCP) exhibited the highest compressive strength, whereas the introduction of POCP resulted in a gradual decline, as observed by Elmagarhe et al.68. The reduction became more pronounced at 20% and 30% replacement, which can be attributed to POCP’s lower cementitious activity relative to portland cement. POCP’s inert and porous nature limits the extent of hydration and the formation of calcium silicate hydrates (C-S-H), which are the primary contributors to strength73. Besides, this reduction in compressive strength with increasing POCP content can also be attributed to associated changes in the grout matrix microstructure. As POCP content increases, the reduction in cementitious material and the presence of POCP lead to a less dense matrix. The SEM/EDS analysis further supports this effect, which is presented later, showing increased porosity and heterogeneity at higher POCP levels.

Despite this reduction, it is important to note that most of the mixes, including those with 20% and 30% POCP, achieved compressive strengths above the minimum threshold line indicated in the Figs. 8, 9 and 10. However, it is worth mentioning that mixes with POCP performed well in early strength gaining but lagged in achieving the target CS₂₈d, especially at higher W/C. Meaning that the margin of safety is clearly reduced at higher replacement levels, especially when combined with a high W/C. In other words, all mixes exhibited strength gain with curing age, but the relative performance pattern remained unchanged: lower W/C and lower POCP content consistently yielded superior results. In the case of CS₂₈d, the gap between the control and POCP-containing mixes widened, reflecting the diminished long-term hydration potential of grout material. Overall, the findings confirm that maintaining a low W/C is critical to achieving satisfactory strength, and that POCP replacement should ideally be limited to around 15–20% to balance sustainability benefits with mechanical performance. Beyond this range, the trade-off in strength becomes more pronounced. Overall, these observations align with a previous study by Nayaka et al. showing a decline in compressive strength with increasing POCP content in the mix41.

RSM design and grout optimization

After the data was analyzed, the optimization criteria were set to achieve the desired strength and flexibility. The W/C was set to minimize, as reducing W/C improves mechanical properties. POCP content was set to maximize POCP cement replacement. Flow time was set to stay in the range of 11 to 16 s while the CS₁d, CS₇d, and CS₂₈d were set to maximize as shown in Fig. 11.

Optimized ramps for features and targets based on RSM design.

The results suggest that the optimum W/C lies close to 0.46, while the corresponding POCP replacement level is approximately 20%. With this combination, a satisfactory balance between workability and mechanical performance is achieved. The flow value of 16 s falls within the desirable range for semi-flexible pavement grouts, indicating adequate fluidity for effective infiltration into the asphalt skeleton while avoiding excessive bleeding or segregation36. For mechanical properties, the optimized mix delivers compressive strength of 13.7 MPa at 1 day, 21.9 MPa at 7 days, and 42.9 MPa at 28 days, which was well above the required strengths of 7 MPa, 15 MPa, and 35 MPa, for CS₁d, CS₇d, and CS₂₈d respectively16,40,47–49.

Previous studies on waste-incorporated cementitious grouts for semi-flexible pavements have reported optimum replacement levels depending on the material type. For instance, glass powder and date-palm seed ash systems achieved optimal performance at approximately 20% GWP and 10% DPSA14, while separate studies on glass powder reported effective replacement levels around 15%15. Similarly, RSM-based optimization of marble dust identified an optimum replacement of 15%16, whereas ceramic waste demonstrated optimal performance at around 20% replacement17. Pumice-based systems have shown optimum replacement levels of approximately 17%, with an effective range between 10 and 15%18. In comparison, the present study identified an optimum POCP content of 20%. This indicates that POCP exhibits comparable behavior and can serve as a viable alternative for sustainable cement replacement in semi-flexible pavement grouts when optimized using RSM.

From the figure, it is evident that the flow time and compressive strength reduced with an increase in both W/C and POCP content. Microstructural analysis: SEM and EDS

Figure 15 presents SEM micrographs of the control cementitious grout at different magnifications together with the corresponding EDS spectrum. It can be observed that the control sample exhibits a dense and continuous matrix, indicating effective hydration and particle packing. Although few micropores appear at higher resolution, they are isolated rather than interconnected. The EDS spectrum shows high levels of oxygen, calcium, carbon, and silicon, and a low level of aluminum. Quantitative analysis shows that oxygen (45.7 wt%), calcium (23.9 wt%), carbon (22.3 wt%), and silicon (6.3 wt%) are the major constituents. This composition of elements is typical of traditional materials made of Portland cement and shows the dominance of the calcium-containing hydration products, as well as silica-carrying phases, related to the formation of C–S–H gels74,75. The relative proportions of Ca and Si, often expressed as the Ca/Si ratio, indicate C–S–H formation and matrix development. The observed elemental composition, therefore, suggests that C–S–H is the dominant hydration product contributing to the structural integrity of the grout76.

SEM and EDS analysis of control cementitious grout.

In contrast, SEM analysis of POCP-modified cementitious grouts revealed a porous and heterogeneous structure as presented in Fig. 16. The microstructure is composed of loosely packed hydration products and granular particles, some of which contain open pores left in the matrix. The pores appear larger and more frequent than those observed in the control sample, suggesting increased pore connectivity and a less compact internal structure. These characteristics indicate that incorporating POCP increases porosity and heterogeneity within the cementitious matrix, as reflected in the SEM observations. This increase in porosity is typically associated with a reduction in mechanical strength due to the loss of structural density. Such behavior is expected in systems with partial cement replacement and is consistent with the observed strength trends.

SEM and EDS analysis of POCP modified cementitious grout.

Analysis of the EDS spectrum displays peaks of oxygen, carbon, calcium, and silicon with minor amounts of aluminum and potassium. Quantitative analysis reveals that oxygen (43.9 wt%) and carbon (25.2 wt%) are the predominant components, with calcium (18.0 wt%) and silicon (10.1 wt%) as the next major constituents. The POCP-modified samples exhibit a relatively lower calcium content and higher silicon content. This increase in silica is likely due to unreacted or partially reacted POCP particles rather than to additional C–S–H formation. Consequently, the reduced availability of calcium for hydration limits the formation of C–S–H gel. Furthermore, the porous nature of POCP particles and the dilution effect resulting from reduced cement content contribute to increased voids and reduced structural density. As a result, the increase in silica content does not translate into matrix densification, leading to a more porous microstructure and reduced mechanical strength.

Grout saturation and voids analysis

Air voids play a crucial role in open-graded asphalt mixtures designed for semi-flexible pavement applications. When the air void content falls below 25%, the interconnected void structure is disrupted, restricting grout penetration and reducing the layer’s mechanical strength and load-bearing capacity36. Conversely, when the void content exceeds roughly 35%, the mixture requires a significantly larger volume of grout, which can result in a stiffer and more brittle composite. Previous studies have reported that effective void interconnectivity and uniform grout penetration can be achieved when grout saturation is maintained between 94 and 97%36,77,78. Cylindrical specimens were analyzed for void saturation by comparing the voids before and after grout saturation. Figure 17 presents the findings of all the specimens’ air voids for both the CSFP and PSCP. However, the average degree of saturation for PSFP was lower than that for CSFP. This slightly lower grout saturation in PSFP mixtures can be attributed to incomplete filling of the interconnected void structure of the asphalt skeleton or to the higher porosity of the POCP grout itself, resulting in increased residual voids and reduced structural density.

Marshall testing

The Marshall stability and flow of CSFP and PSFP were measured in accordance with ASTM D6927 and compared with dense-graded HMA samples as the reference. The Marshall stability in the case of both CSFP and PSFP was more than 2 times higher than that of the HMA sample, as presented in Fig. 18. The flow values observed in the PSFP case were similar to those of the HMA sample. The high marshall stability of CSFP and PSFP indicates the semiflexible mix’s resistance to permanent deformation. Previous studies have also reported similar differences in semiflexible and HMA samples16,54. Overall, the CSPF and PSCP performed much better than HMA in resisting permanent deformation. However, it is worth mentioning that the dense graded HMA samples also satisfied the stability requirements of higher than 8 kN, and flow requirements in the range of 2 to 4 mm as standardized by the Jabatan Kerja Raya, Malaysia57.

Marshall stability and flow of SCFP, PSFP, and HMA sample.

Resistance to fuel spillage

Resistance to fuel spillage was measured by subjecting the three replicas of CSFP, PSFP, and HMA samples to partial immersion in diesel for 24 h for seven cycles in accordance with BS EN 12697-4379. These samples were then subjected to a steel brush to check their resistance to abrasion after washing and drying for 24 h. The mass of all samples was monitored throughout the process to calculate the mass loss resulting from the durability test. The introduction of Marshall samples to diesel resulted in the reduction of the total mass of the sample. This reduction in mass was attributed to the stripping of aggregates from the total mix. Figure 19 presents the performance of HMA and SFP samples in terms of parameters A, B, and C. From the figure, it is evident that both CSFP and PSFP performed much better than HMA samples. A possible reason for such performance could be the lower bitumen content in SFP samples, resulting in less soluble material in diesel. The superior fuel resistance of SFP can be attributed to its composite structure, in which the cementitious grout serves as the primary load-bearing matrix. Unlike conventional HMA, which relies predominantly on bitumen as the binding phase, SFP contains a rigid grout network that is inherently resistant to diesel exposure. Although a small amount of bitumen is present in SFP, its role is mainly to maintain the initial aggregate skeleton. Even if the bitumen is partially affected by diesel, the hardened grout matrix preserves the structural integrity and prevents aggregate detachment. In contrast, HMA’s performance is more dependent on bitumen, making it more susceptible to material loss under fuel exposure.

Fuel resistivity over the cycle for of SCFP, PSFP, and HMA sample. (a) Parameter A, (b) parameter B, (c) parameter C.

Additionally, it is worth mentioning that, based on the limits defined by BS EN 12697-4366 and Bernhard Hofko67, HMA samples showed poor resistance to fuel spillage, especially after the 3rd emersion cycle and above (parameters A > 5%, B between 1 and 5%, and C between 6 and 10%). On the contrary, CSFP and PSFP outperformed HMA. The visual changes that happened in marshall sample as a result of the fuel spillage test can be seen in Fig. 20.

Moisture resistivity

Two sets of samples (dry and immersed in water) for CSFP, PSFP, and HMA were used to measure the indirect tensile strength using an automatic Marshall testing machine. The tensile strength of CSFP and PSFP samples was compared with that of HMA under dry and wet conditions to quantify the strength loss due to moisture absorption. A reduction of around 7% and 9% was observed for CSFP and PSFP, respectively. On the other hand, HMA showed a reduction of around 16% due to moisture infusion, as shown in Fig. 21. However, all three mixes satisfied the 80% strength retention requirement as recommended by previous studies62,80,81. Conclusively, the SFP samples in the current study outperformed the HMA samples in terms of resistance to moisture by showing strength retention higher than 90%, close to that reported by Weiguang et al.82.

Moisture resistivity of SCFP, PSFP, and HMA samples.

Indirect tensile stiffness modulus

Indirect tensile stiffness modulus (ITSM) helps researchers optimize the mix design based on the asphaltic mix’s response to tensile loading, in terms of resistance to permanent deformation and recovery from horizontal deformation, at different temperatures. In the current study, three replicas of CSFP, PSFP, and HMA were tested at two orientations (0 and 90 degrees) at four different temperatures (5, 15, 20, and 25 °C). Figure 22 shows that the CSFP had the highest ITSM among PSFP and HMA across all testing temperatures. However, it can also be observed that the ITSM values for all samples decreased with increasing temperature, indicating the viscoelastic behavior of both the HMA and SFP mixes. However, due to the higher viscoelastic behavior of HMA mixes, the drop in ITSM with temperature was more pronounced in HMA than in SFP at low temperatures.

Stiffness modulus of SCFP, PSFP, and HMA samples at different temperatures.

Cantabro loss

In the current study, the contabro mass loss test was conducted to assess the resistance of both HMA and SFP mixes to repetitive impact loading. At least three samples were tested on the Los Angeles machine, rotating at 30 to 33 revolutions per minute for 300 revolutions. The mass loss due to impact loading was determined by comparing the samples’ masses before and after the test. From Fig. 23, it can be observed that the HMA samples have the lowest mass loss as compared to CSFP and PSFP. The SFP samples experienced significant mass loss, possibly due to their high stiffness and rigidity. The HMA samples exhibited lower stiffness and a more pronounced viscoelastic response than the SFP mixes, as indicated by the ITSM. This viscoelastic behavior enables HMA to dissipate impact energy more effectively, reducing the extent of aggregate disintegration during the Cantabro test83. In contrast, CSFP and PSFP exhibited higher ITSM values, indicating a stiffer, more rigid structure. While this rigidity contributes to load-bearing capacity, it limits energy absorption and causes the applied impact load to be transferred across the specimen rather than absorbed or damped. These findings emphasize the trade-off between rigidity and toughness in pavement design, where optimizing stiffness without sacrificing durability under impact is critical. In addition to the higher stiffness of SFP, the interface between the cementitious grout and the bitumen-coated aggregate may act as a weak zone under impact loading, contributing to particle loss. By comparing CSFP with PSFP, the cantbro loss in the case of PSFP was higher than that of CSFP. This behavior may be attributed to increased porosity, as indicated by SEM analysis, which leads to a less dense internal structure and reduced resistance to abrasion.

Cantabro mass loss of SCFP, PSFP, and HMA samples.

Limitations

This study focuses on replacing cement with POCP using RSM, considering different POCP contents and W/C ratios. However, SP content was kept constant at 1% throughout the study to isolate the effects of POCP content and W/C ratio. The influence of varying SP dosage on the performance of the grout system was not explored and may be considered in future studies for further optimization. In addition, the durability evaluation in this study is limited to fuel resistance, moisture susceptibility, and Cantabro loss. Other long-term durability aspects, such as freeze–thaw resistance, sulfate attack, and fatigue behavior, were not considered and are planned to be investigated in the next phase of the study.

Conclusion and recomendations

The study explored the potential of waste palm oil clinker powder (POCP) as a cement replacement in the preparation of cementitious grout for semi-flexible pavement (SFP). Response surface methodology (RSM) was adopted to optimize the grout based on 80 experimental runs. Flow (Sec), 1-day compressive strength (CS₁d), 7-day compressive strength (CS₇d), and 28-day compressive strength (CS₂₈d) were predicted based on varying water-to-cement ratio (W/C) and POCP (%). POCP modified SFP samples (PSFP) were prepared and compared with control SFP (CSFP) and dense graded hot mix asphalt (HMA) samples using various testing, including Marshall testing, moisture resistance, indirect tensile stiffness modulus testing, fuel spillage resistance, and cantabro loss. Besides, microstructural characterization of the control and modified grout was also conducted. Based on the above analysis, the following conclusions have been drawn.

Replacing 20% of cement with waste POCP at a 0.46 W/C ratio in the presence of 1% superplasticizer was found to meet the strength requirements for both grouts and SFP mixes. This highlights the potential of POCP as a sustainable binder replacement for environmentally friendly pavements.

SEM–EDS analysis revealed that the incorporation of POCP introduces a more porous and heterogeneous microstructure compared to the dense C–S–H gel of the control grout. This increased porosity, attributed to the inherent nature of POCP and the dilution of cementitious phases, explains the observed reduction in compressive strength and the slightly higher Cantabro loss in PSFP.

This increased porosity, attributed to the inherent nature of POCP and the dilution of cementitious phases, explains the observed reduction in compressive strength and the slightly higher Cantabro loss in PSFP.

Grout saturation tests showed that POCP-modified grouts exhibited slightly lower void saturation compared to control samples. The slightly lower grout saturation observed in PSFP mixtures may be attributed to incomplete filling of the asphalt skeleton or the more porous nature of the POCP grout, resulting in reduced structural density. This condition can lead to the formation of weak zones, potentially reducing Marshall stability, facilitating moisture ingress and lowering moisture resistance, and increasing susceptibility to particle loss, as reflected in higher Cantabro values.

Although the SFP mixes performed exceptionally well in terms of load-bearing capacity and resistance to permanent deformation, their relatively low viscoelasticity led to higher mass loss under repetitive impact loading in the Cantabro test. These findings underscore the importance of achieving an optimal balance between rigidity and toughness in semi-flexible pavement design.

Overall, the study successfully demonstrated that replacing 20% of cement with waste POCP can produce grouts and SFP mixes that meet the required mechanical performance. However, further investigation is recommended to assess how variations in the air voids of the bitumen skeleton influence the toughness and rigidity of SFP mixes. Based on the current findings, it is suggested that targeting lower air void contents (closer to 25% rather than 35%) could enhance the viscoelastic behavior of SFP mixes. A more detailed study on this trade-off would provide deeper insights into optimizing the balance between void content and flexibility in semi-flexible pavements. Additionally, future studies should focus on establishing standardized compressive strength requirements for cementitious grouts used in semi-flexible pavement systems. In particular, further research is needed to identify the optimal balance between mechanical strength and viscoelastic compatibility, ensuring sufficient load-bearing capacity while maintaining flexibility within the asphalt–grout composite system.
