Parental involvement in patient safety events in paediatric emergency care: a multi-method analysis
Authors: Isobel Joy McFadzean, Lauren Donovan, Thomas Purchase, Emily Ball, Philippa Robb, Philippa Rees, Andrew Carson-Stevens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionParents are central to keeping children safe when receiving healthcare. National scrutiny of failures to recognise and respond to clinical deterioration in children led to the introduction of Martha’s Rule by National Health Service (NHS) England, which formalises families’ rights to request an urgent clinical review for children. Given rising paediatric emergency department (ED) attendances and evidence of healthcare-associated harm within EDs, we examined how parents contribute to and mitigate unsafe care in this care context.MethodsThis was an exploratory multi-method analysis of anonymised paediatric safety incidents from EDs. We sought reports reported by EDs between 2014 and 2020. 4000 reports were reviewed to identify whether a patient safety incident had occurred and whether there was parental involvement to mitigate or contribute to the harm. Reports were categorised by incident type (what happened), contributory (why it happened), mitigatory factors (actions taken to reduce or prevent harm), outcome (patient impact) and harm severity.ResultsOf 658 reports that met the definition of a patient safety incident and included parental involvement, 591 (90.0%) described parents mitigating harm and 67 (10.0%) described inadvertent contribution to incidents. Parents most commonly mitigated harms by advocating for their children, prompting reassessments where necessary and supporting care. However, poor communication involving clinical information, particularly allergies or pertinent medical history, contributed to harm, leading to multiple safety incidents, including the administration of contraindicated medication within EDs.ConclusionParents are important participants in paediatric safety within EDs, and we have identified diverse ways in which they prevent harm. As policy initiatives formalise escalation pathways, healthcare systems must strengthen mechanisms to support partnership working with families across healthcare settings, while addressing paediatric vulnerabilities that contribute to harm.

Introduction

Family members are central to keeping children safe within healthcare environments, given that children are dependent on others to recognise that they are unwell and may be unable to communicate their needs effectively. Research suggests that up to 35% of hospitalised children in a teaching hospital in Ohio experienced physical or psychological harm during medical care.1 However, estimates of adverse events in inpatient care vary considerably due to differences in measurement approaches and genuine variation across healthcare systems and contexts. Consequently, researchers have concluded that a reliable overall incidence of adverse events in inpatient care cannot be determined,2 but it is a frequent and burdensome problem.

Studies focused on vulnerable children have highlighted failures of health and social care systems to recognise children’s specific care needs and to plan effective solutions to enhance safety. Interventions to address this issue are reflected in new policies by the National Health Service (NHS) in England, such as ‘Martha’s Rule’,3 which aims to empower families to seek an urgent clinical review for their children within hospital settings when they have concerns about care or wish to seek a second opinion. An initial evaluation of this policy has demonstrated a positive impact on patient safety, with one-third of calls to the Martha’s Rule helpline supporting the identification of acutely deteriorating patients.4

In 2025, there were approximately six million attendances to emergency departments (EDs) in England by children aged 0–165, and NHS England reports highlight increased rates of child (<18 years old) presentations.6 Children are brought to EDs to receive emergency care due to family concern and actual or perceived difficulties receiving healthcare elsewhere, such as with their own general practitioner (GP).7 A study in Canada, involving over 6000 children in nine EDs, found that one in 33 children experienced safety concerns,8 with proposed reasons for this including overcrowding9 and the nature of ED care, requiring multitasking and frequent interruptions.10

While research has shown that many safety events within EDs are avoidable,11 and that legally, and when able to, parents should be encouraged to make decisions about their child’s healthcare,12 the nature of their involvement in paediatric safety events within EDs, known as incidents, remains underexamined. To address this, we aimed to explore how parents may prevent or influence paediatric patient safety incidents within EDs. Our objective was to describe ED paediatric incident reports, which included parental involvement in the form of contribution or mitigation of healthcare-associated harm.

Method

Study setting

This study involved a multi-method analysis of paediatric patient safety incidents reported from EDs within England and Wales. This was part of a wider exploration of the role of parents within paediatric safety incidents in different health settings including primary care13; and the study protocol has been published previously.14

Health services in EDs are free at the point of delivery as part of the NHS. Patients can choose to attend; they may be signposted to ED by triage telephone services such as NHS 111, their own GP or other healthcare professionals (HCPs); via the emergency telephone number 999; or may be conveyed by ambulance.15

Patient safety incident reports

Healthcare professionals (HCPs) are encouraged to report safety concerns within EDs through the completion of patient safety incident reports.16 These include question prompts for reporters to select categories or write free-text descriptions, which are then analysed locally, and later submitted to a national repository, formerly called the National Reporting and Learning System (NRLS)16 within England and Wales, now known as the Learn from Patient Safety Events (LfPSE) service.

Study population

Search terms such as ‘Parent’, ‘Mum’ and ‘Dad’ were used to identify reports with potential parental involvement submitted from paediatric settings (see online supplemental appendix 1) between September 2014 and February 2023 (n=183 700). To recognise the multifaceted family unit surrounding children,17 18 alternative search terms including ‘carer’ and ‘family’ were considered, but, as described within our protocol, they generated a high volume of records with limited relevance to the study inclusion criteria, resulting in a disproportionate screening effort relative to their likely yield.14 Consequently, carefully defined parental caregiver-related terms were used within this study.

We developed a strategy to sample reports submitted between 2014 and 2020, with similar proportions of children from different age groups (less than 1 year, 1–12 years and 12–18 years). All reports within each age group that included outcomes of death, severe harm and moderate harm were included, and the remaining sample consisted of reports of low harm and no harm in a ratio of 2:114 from the location category ‘ED’ (n=4000). Analysis

Descriptive analysis

The incident reports were screened independently by four clinicians (two academic general practitioners and two paediatricians) with training in patient safety and human factor principles, to reduce potential bias (IJM, LD, PRo and EB). Reports that met the inclusion criteria (Box 1) were analysed using the multi-axial PatIent SAfety (PISA) Classification System, developed at Cardiff University.19 This system ontologically aligns with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) International Classification for Patient Safety (ICPS) and its concepts for understanding safety.20

Each report was deconstructed using codes applied systematically and chronologically, adhering to the ‘Recursive Model of Incident Analysis’.21 This captured the primary incident (the issue or event that occurred before the described patient outcome), contributory incidents to the primary incident, contributory and mitigatory factors and outcomes to emphasise the sequential nature of storytelling evident in reports. In instances where multiple, unrelated incidents were described, those stories were coded separately. Coders sought explicit evidence within the free-text before attributing each code and were asked not to infer or make assumptions, to create coded sequences that directly mirrored what was described in the free-text. Consequently, this enabled the identification of the patterns between key classes of information necessary to understand the described safety event, where the most frequent relationships between codes formed the basis of purposive sampling for thematic analysis. Using guidance from Cimino regarding the design of a controlled healthcare vocabulary,22 the existing PISA Classification System was also updated, with focus on mitigatory factors, contributory and ameliorating actions by parents to support an understanding of safety. Reviewers documented reflexive memos about how the factors influenced the incident.

To appraise concordance between coders, 20% of the reports were independently double-coded (n=800), and inter-rater reliability was monitored using Cohen’s Kappa statistics. Any unresolvable discrepancies between reviewers led to discussion about coding decisions with the wider study team. Following final coding decisions, codes were downloaded, and descriptive summaries were compiled within Microsoft Excel. Cross-tabulations were used to explore the relationships between incident types, contributory factors (CFs), mitigatory factors and outcomes, to understand their frequency within the reports.

Subsequently, these data were analysed thematically, utilising principles from Braun and Clarke,23 to inform the development of a mitigatory coding framework and review of parental contribution. The software NVIVO V.12.0 was used to conduct the analysis. The memos and report free-text were read and reread to support data familiarisation by IJM and LD and coded inductively and deductively to understand patterns, themes and key relationships between patient safety incident reports involving mitigation, and then with the wider team, additional codes were developed to capture this learning.

Inter-rater reliability

The first Kappa coefficient between reviewers was 0.527. After arbitration, which included the removal of safeguarding reports that did not relate to healthcare, the Kappa coefficient was 0.968, indicating near perfect concordance.

Reports in which parents mitigated harm

From the analysed reports, most (591/658, 90%) detailed evidence of parents preventing or lessening harm. Within this sample, all 591 reports contained a primary incident, 252 (42.6%) contained two incidents, 28 (4.7%) contained three incidents and 5 (0.8%) contained four incidents. None of the reports contained additional independent safety incidents. In total, 876 incidents were coded; with single reports including descriptions of multiple safety incidents experienced by the same child (such as insufficient triage on arrival, a delayed clinical assessment, poor medical documentation and subsequent medication not administered in a timely fashion).

Reports described how parents intervened, before or following incidents involving their child’s medical diagnoses or clinical assessments (196/876, 22.4%), especially when focused on missed diagnoses (32, 16.3%). They also prevented or lessened the impact of harm from medication-related incidents (156, 17.8%), including when medication was being dispensed, reporting to staff that incorrect medication or doses were being dispensed (17/156, 10.9%) or that medication was incorrectly prescribed (17/156, 10.9%). Additionally, they mitigated risks posed to children as a result of poor communication between HCPs (114, 13.0%), highlighting concerns related to breakdowns in communication between healthcare teams (34/114, 29.8%), and by raising concerns when specialist physicians were not informed about their child’s attendance to ED, as well as by playing an important role to correct medical inaccuracies (see online supplemental appendix 2 for all coded incidents).

Over 75% of these reports contained one or more CFs (457/591, 77.3%), with 590 CFs coded in total. The most frequent type of CF included children experiencing long waits for care in EDs (74/591 reports, 12.5%), which impacted their initial assessment/triage (39/74 reports, 52.7%), followed by child-specific vulnerabilities (62/591 reports, 10.5%), for example, events where children experienced discomfort and needed family members to advocate on their behalf for analgesia (28/62 reports, 45%), or to stop physicians from causing pain, for example, during multiple, unsuccessful attempts to relocate fractures (4/62 reports, 6.5%).

These reports also highlighted family concerns about staff behaviour (44/591 reports, 7.4%) and family complaints (30/44, 68%). Guardians tried to protect their children when they perceived staff had acted inappropriately; for example, when HCPs contacted their children via social media, texting during consultations or making inappropriate comments to patients. A full list of CFs is found in online supplemental appendix 3.

Despite family advocacy and support, within a quarter of reports, children experienced delays in management, assessment or treatment (149/591, 25.2%), with reports evidencing harm prevented by families (131/591, 22.2%) or further harm lessened (114/591, 19.3%). All outcomes broken down by report category are found in online supplemental appendix 4.

The most frequently coded harm severity category was no harm (200/591, 33.8%). However, despite family efforts to prevent or minimise healthcare-associated harms, there were two severe harm outcomes (0.3%) and four deaths (0.6%). Such outcomes occurred despite families alerting staff to their child’s deterioration within ED waiting rooms, driving critically unwell children to EDs due to ambulance delays or seeking second opinions at different EDs following discharge. All harm severities are found in online supplemental appendix 5.

Reports in which families contributed to harm

In 10% of reports (67/659), families contributed to the development of safety incidents. From these reports, 16 (of 67, 23.9%) contained two incidents, and two reports (3.0%) contained three incidents. No reports in this sample contained additional independent safety incidents. In total, there were 86 incidents reported in which parents influenced events, for example, by giving incorrect patient details at ED check-in, despite receptionists stating that they had established the information given. This led to patients having medical consultations, and investigations including blood tests and sepsis bundles completed (including cannulation, blood cultures and administration of intravenous fluids and antibiotics) with the wrong patient’s details, delaying diagnoses and resulting in repeated investigations, delayed treatment and patient discomfort.

Families were often found to influence events involving medication (43/86, 50%). These reports were exacerbated by poor communication (10/86, 11.6%); for example, by family members giving incorrect information about medication administered to their children before ED arrival (5/6, 83%), which included paracetamol and ibuprofen (4/5, 80%), and salbutamol (1/5, 20%), or information about allergies (1/5, 20%) leading to contraindicated medication being administered.

Nearly all of these reports contained at least one CF (66/67, 98.5%), and 96 CFs were coded in total. The most frequently coded CFs related to the behaviour of family members (28/67, 41.8%); for example, when they refused medical care for children (6/28, 21.4%), including taking children home against medical advice (3/6, 50%) or refusing emergency treatment for conditions such as haemophilia (1/6, 16.7%).

There was also evidence that families did not always share clinical information with healthcare teams (13/67, 19.4%), with eight such incidents (61.5%) involving children receiving the wrong doses of medicine. The remaining cases involved information about their child’s health, including diagnoses (4/7, 57.1%) and other clinical information (3/7, 42.9%) such as confusion surrounding injury sites, leading to inappropriate radiation of the wrong limb during X-rays.

The next most frequent CF included evidence that the family had a poor understanding of care plans or instructions, which contributed to harm (12/67, 17.9%), often with medication (10/12, 83.3%), such as families not understanding Salbutamol regimes and giving 60+ inhaled doses in ED overnight, which went unnoticed by HCPs.

All reports had a coded outcome (67/67, 100%), and 76 outcomes were coded in total. Within these, reports were unclear outcomes for patients (12/67, 17.9%), followed by children requiring repeated tests, procedures or additional treatment (8/67, 11.9%) or experiencing delays in management or treatment (7/67, 10.4%).

Even with family members contributing to patient safety incident reports, reports resulted in no harm (28/67, 41.8%). There were three moderate harms (3/67, 4.5%), including an event in which family members took a child with suspected sepsis home against medical advice, requiring police involvement to return the child, and one severe harm (1/67, 1.5%), which included a family not advising ED staff that their baby was unconscious and therefore needed an emergency review. No deaths were associated with family contribution within this sample.

Parental mitigation and contribution coding framework

We categorised parental mitigation and contribution following the review of reflexive memos from 562/591 (95.1%) mitigation reports, and all 67 reports involving contribution. Mitigatory actions included seeking further healthcare input (179/562, 31.9%) and raising concerns about waiting times within EDs (62/179, 34.6%) or care provision (17, 9.5%). Parents were also seen to take responsibility for management or self-care (104, 18.5%), such as returning to an ED with their children for another medical review (56/104, 53.8%) or transporting their child to other locations while experiencing delays for ambulance conveyance (34/104, 32.7%).

The most frequent actions that contributed to harm involved the provision of inaccurate information to ED teams (35/67, 52.3%), with the majority involving paracetamol or ibuprofen administered prior to arrival (22/35, 66.7%) or booking children at reception under the wrong details (6/35, 17.1%).

Discussion

This study has characterised the nature of paediatric patient safety incidents involving parents. Of this sample, 658 (of 4000, 16.5%) reports met the definition of a patient safety incident and included parental involvement, and 591 (of 658, 90.0%) described parents mitigating harm, and 67 (10.0%) described inadvertent contribution to incidents.

The majority of reports described a breadth of ways parents can prevent or lessen the impact of harm. These reports were often related to medication incidents, sharing pertinent information, advocating for their children by expressing concerns about care and playing an integral role in medical management. A much smaller proportion of reports involved parents contributing to healthcare-associated harms, by providing inaccurate information, inappropriate care or by refusing care, which predominantly influenced healthcare management and medication provision.

Using reflexive memos developed during the analysis of reports, a coding framework was developed, highlighting parental mitigatory and contribution actions, which, if addressed, could support safer paediatric care in EDs.

Strengths and limitations

Nearly half of all reports within our initial sample were excluded from descriptive analysis because they did not meet our definition of a patient safety incident, and ~1% of reports could not be analysed because they contained insufficient information. There are other well-recognised limitations of patient safety incident reports,24 including selection bias (systematic distortion in reported safety data, where reports may not reflect all safety incidents)25 and hindsight bias (the tendency to assume that those involved in an incident should have recognised it as harmful).26 As reports were written from the reporter’s perspective, they may not fully capture the complexity of incidents or wider contextual factors. Nevertheless, the patterns identified, and their strong face validity within our multidisciplinary team, support their value for hypothesis generation. As highlighted by Purchase et al, patient safety incident reports can provide an important first step in organisational learning,27 supporting signal detection within safety data and informing subsequent enquiry using methodologies better suited to exploring contextual detail.

While we additionally recognise that parents are not the only family members to care for children, given our pilot work found that broader search terms would not have been proportionate to yield,14 we worked closely with NHS England to identify a unique and large sample of reports to overcome some of these limitations, and this is the largest characterisation of reported patient safety incidents in EDs describing parental involvement. It has supported an understanding of how parents may help, or influence the delivery of safer care, and highlights where and how their involvement could be encouraged in the future. We had high rates of inter-rater concordance during analysis and utilised our research team’s experience of patient safety and paediatrics. We have also adopted a systems approach, enabled through the use of the PISA classification system aligned to the WHO’s ICPS ontology.

Comparison with other literature

Parental involvement within paediatric patient safety

The role of parents and families is of growing interest within paediatric patient safety. O’Hara et al highlights that parents may identify safety signals that are not obvious to HCPs, that they play a vital role in maintaining continuity of care28 and they can support the concept of system resilience29 in overcoming unexpected and unintentional events such as patient safety incidents within healthcare systems.28 Similarly, the importance of family members within settings such as paediatric surgery has been previously considered, notably, how families can ‘rescue’ paediatric patients, and how they interpret information, co-ordinate care, bridge gaps in healthcare delivery and recognise when their children are critically unwell.30

Our findings extend this literature in several ways. First, we have used the national database of patient safety incident reports to identify a large corpus of reports from paediatric EDs in England and Wales, whereas prior studies have focused on a smaller number of safety events through interviews or qualitative accounts of family experience. Similarly, we have demonstrated that parental involvement, when examined through patient safety incident reports, was mostly associated with harm mitigation, with parents prompting reassessment, correcting inaccurate information or seeking further care, while only a minority of patient safety incident reports described their contribution to incidents. This suggests that parents function not only as observers of safety risks but as active mitigators of healthcare-associated harm.

Supporting family members to speak up

Within our reports, we saw examples of how families prevented or reduced harms by sharing their concerns about safety incidents. This is a concept frequently encouraged within patient safety efforts in the NHS, such as the ‘Freedom to speak up’ programme,31 which supports HCPs to share care concerns and aims to ensure that reporters are listened to, and their issues are acted on. This is in keeping with NHS campaigns focused on patients and families, such as ‘NHS Putting Things Right’ by NHS Wales32 with emphasis on supporting them to raise complaints or concerns.

Martha’s rule additionally empowers families to raise concerns, as well as helping rapid response teams and services to respond if families feel that the response is not sufficient.3 However, a study in the United Kingdom, which aimed to explore patients’ and family members’ experiences of speaking up about safety concerns, carried out focus groups and interviews with ~150 patients, and participants described how raising safety concerns can be difficult, and they would speak up only if they thought the situation was serious, they were confident that there was an issue, and there were no consequences of reporting them.33 This was echoed by researchers in Norway, who found that a family’s willingness to raise concerns is shaped by their confidence and organisational responsiveness.29 Health systems may, therefore, struggle to support parents and patients to speak up in EDs in the absence of ‘psychological safety’, which should encourage reporting.34 Research has considered how healthcare settings embody this feeling of safety, and while the focus is often on staff, as opposed to the patients/families themselves, it is known that transparent and positive reporting cultures,35 and removing the fear of retribution36 are necessary. These are transferable concepts for potential adoption in EDs to support families and are necessary, with Mills et al, identifying that parental concerns are potentially a better indication of paediatric deterioration than other signs such as clinical observations monitored within hospital settings, subsequently recommending a proactive assessment of parental concerns as a standard component of clinical care.37

Preventing medication incidents

Within our reported sample, families contributed to medication incidents within EDs, leading to harm. While a systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that it is not possible to currently provide an estimated rate of paediatric avoidable harm,2 wider research advises high frequencies of paediatric patient safety incidents commonly involving medication, for example, an analysis of patient safety incident reports in primary care found that over 30% (of over 2000 reports) involved medication and vaccines.38 Recognising concerns with medication-related harms, an interventional study within a paediatric neurology ward in Germany developed an educational programme for parents and HCPs and found that this was an efficient means of reducing medication-related safety incidents,39 by implementing short teaching sessions (2–5 min), accompanied by information leaflets for 30 parents.39

Implications for practice and further research

Our study demonstrates the different ways parents influence, intervene and mitigate harm to their children in the ED context. By developing a mitigatory and contributory framework derived from these incident reports, we have provided a structured account of how parents influence safety events within EDs, which are likely transferable to other healthcare settings.

How best to encourage and support parental and other family member-led interventions, openly and safely within EDs and other healthcare settings, is currently unclear. Our characterisation of paediatric safety incidents highlights the existing efforts of parents to keep their children safe. Studies signal that healthcare-associated harm is experienced regularly by children,13 but despite our efforts to learn from what happens when things go wrong in healthcare, further focus should be on best practice. This is especially true with regards to near miss incidents, when it is likely that, in the absence of a parent, the outcome could be very different for children while receiving healthcare. So, while we can take important steer and insight from these data, they are likely the tip of the iceberg. These observations, in combination with learning yielded from other data sources used to measure and understand paediatric patient safety, including retrospective medical record review, use of global trigger tools,2 public-reported experience and outcome data, interviews and ethnography, would help with important strategic efforts like the implementation of ‘Martha’s Rule’.40 Nevertheless, as with the realisation of other vital safety efforts,41 further system-level, workforce, technological and cultural improvements are needed to create safer care and greater support for guardians to be effective advocates in healthcare settings.

Our findings would be supported by further research, particularly understanding the experiences of family members from all backgrounds, and the perceptions of HCPs, to understand how EDs can optimise support for family members to effectively act as safety advocates, to feel psychologically safe to raise concerns, determine what is feasible within the NHS and how good practice can be supported. This work would support the debate regarding levels of responsibility, what is appropriate to expect from parents, and which approaches enhance safety efforts, while encouraging parents to speak up about healthcare concerns.

Conclusion

Parents play an important role in preventing and influencing safety incidents while children receive care in EDs. This work highlights priority areas with a mitigatory framework and mitigatory actions to deliver safer paediatric care and support parents to act as child safety advocates. Working closely with family members within EDs could support paediatric patient safety and reduce healthcare-associated harms, emphasising the importance of advocacy efforts and where and how families can support healthcare systems to enhance care.
