Whole testicular organ and seminal cryopreservation in the malarial mosquito Anopheles gambiae (Diptera: Culicidae)
Authors: Courtney C Grula, Arun Rajamohan, Melanie Tischleder, Joseph P Rinehart, Julian Hillyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Anopheles gambiae (Giles) is a medically important mosquito species and a vector of the malaria Plasmodium parasite. There have been many advancements in vector control research that involves genetic manipulation of the mosquito. Malaria vector research requires rearing continuous mosquito colonies that are costly and carry risks to maintain. Therefore, we studied the feasibility of germplasm cryopreservation as a way to maintain genetic background as well as restore mosquito colonies. One part of germplasm cryopreservation is the cryopreservation of the whole germinal organ such as the testis as this would preserve half the genome. The aim of this study was to develop a simple method to cryopreserve whole testicular-seminal vesicle complex from which one could derive both the viable spermatozoa as well as primordial and stem cells from male mosquitos. This is the first study which demonstrates the successful cryopreservation of a whole organ in an insect system. In this study, various cryopreservation protocols were tested to determine which resulted in the highest testicular cell survival. The methods tested included different freezing rates, different incubation protocols, and use of different non-permeating cryoprotectants. The cooling rate that was found to have the highest survival was cooling to −40 °C at a rate of 1 °C min−1 followed by a plunge into liquid nitrogen, although there was little difference in survival based on cooling rate.Keywords: cryopreservation, mosquitoes, germ tissue

Introduction

Cryopreservation is a tool for the long-term preservation of plant and animal genetic material and has become increasingly important for maintaining laboratory insect colonies (Leopold 2007). By cooling biological material to very low temperatures, this approach enables long-term storage in liquid nitrogen. In insects, cryopreservation offers a cost-effective alternative to continuous colony maintenance (Benedict 1997, Leopold 2007) and potentially reduces the risk of accidental loss of the colony or genetic drift. The preservation of germinal tissue is a promising method for protecting genetically distinct strains and allowing for the ability for strains to be revived when needed. There are many examples that have established the feasibility of germline cryopreservation across diverse insect taxa (Rajamohan and Leopold 2007, Rajamohan et al. 2020, Campion et al. 2021, Grula et al. 2024).

Although cryopreservation protocols are well developed for many insect taxa, mosquitoes have been largely underrepresented, leaving a gap in our understanding of preservation techniques for this species. Anopheles gambiae (Giles) is the primary insect vector of the human malaria Plasmodium parasite. Despite their medical importance, mosquitoes, including An. gambiae are understudied in cryopreservation research. An. gambiae is found throughout Africa and is major threat to human health. In 2024, there were 610,000 reported deaths attributed to malaria, and 282 million cases reported globally (WHO 2025). Majority of the concern for malaria transmission is in Africa, and there is recent concern of malaria vectoring mosquitos and cases of malaria being reported in the United States (CDC 2023). Only female mosquitos transmit malaria because they require blood meals for egg production. While male mosquitos do not transmit the disease, they are crucial for colony maintenance and are important targets for vector control strategies. Interest in genetic modification technologies has increased in response to rising insecticide resistance (WHO 2019). Advancements in gene editing for malaria control has expanded the number of mosquito strains needed to be maintained in laboratory colonies (Imam et al. 2014, Kauffman et al. 2017).

Within the order Diptera, successful cryopreservation efforts demonstrate the feasibility of long-term preservation (Rajamohan et al. 2003, Rajamohan and Leopold 2007, Leopold et al. 2010). Notably, the New World Screwworm (Cochhomyia hominivoraxis) is an example of a medically important dipteran colony that was successfully cryopreserved (Leopold et al. 2001). Twelve strains were cryopreserved to serve as a reserve for existing strains, as well as for strain recovery (Leopold et al. 2001). These strains were then successfully recovered after a period of colony collapse. The success of strain cryopreservation as a backup and recovery strategy in the New World Screwworm highlights its potential as a tool for maintaining mosquito colonies, where long-term preservation of genetically distinct strains is important. Previous successful dipteran cryopreservation procedures have used the late embryonic stages (Leopold and Rinehart 2010). This has been proven to be difficult in the case of mosquitos because their embryo stage is aquatic and not susceptible to the permeabilization procedure to allow entry of cryoprotectants into the system, due to the presence of an adaptive and dynamic endochorion. However, there are proof of concept studies that show that a very early stage embryo in specific strains of mosquitos could be cryopreserved (James et al. 2022) as well as larvae (Joshi et al. 2026). Pending the extension of the protocol to various strains and species of mosquitoes, the alternative to embryo and larval cryopreservation is germplasm tissue cryopreservation. Preservation of male reproductive tissue offers the potential to maintain genetic material at the strain level while circumventing some of the developmental constraints associated with embryo-based protocols. Germplasm tissue cryopreservation also has other applications such as in cloning (Howard et al. 2016, Novak et al. 2025).

The success of insect germplasm cryopreservation depends on how cells respond to freezing-induced stresses. During cryopreservation, freezing can cause cells to experience multiple forms of damage such as the intracellular ice crystal formation which can physically cause damage to the cells by rupturing cell membranes (Białkowska et al. 2020, Roeters et al. 2021). The damage could also be caused by dehydration from prolonged exposure to the growing extracellular ice, which causes water to leave the cell along the partial pressure gradient, resulting in higher intracellular solute concentration (Storey et al. 1991, Fuller 2004). This could damage sensitive biochemical pathways. There are other forms of freeze-damage including, membrane damage, protein denaturation, oxidative damage, and disruption of metabolic processes (Grgac et al. 2022, Toxopeus et al. 2019). It is important for post-cryopreservation survival to reduce the probability of intracellular ice nucleation and reducing osmotic shock which occurs from the increase of solutes.

The cryopreservation protocol attempts to reduce the damage caused by freezing through addition of the optimized concentration of cryoprotectant agents (CPAs) and cooling-rates to reduce damaging intracellular ice damage (Mazur 2004) while avoiding toxicity and dehydration (Blanco et al. 2000, Holt 2000, Watson 2000, Medeiros et al. 2002, Anger et al. 2003, Dinneys et al. 2007, Vuthiphandchai et al. 2007). CPAs function through multiple mechanisms to limit ice crystal formation and osmotic stress, primarily by interfering with hydrogen bond formation among water molecules and lowering the freezing point of water. Both the type and concentration of CPA are important for cell viability. There are two types of CPAs; permeating and non-permeating. Permeating CPAs, such as Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), are small molecules that can pass through the cell membrane. Permeating CPAs can directly interact with intracellular ice crystal formation during the freezing process. DMSO (MW 78.13), for example, is amphiphilic and is capable of crossing the cell membrane due to its water miscibility as well as lipid interactions; Ethylene glycol (62.68) or propylene glycol (76.1) can only permeate at very high concentrations due to their hydrophilicity and exerts more dehydrating effects than the DMSO. Non-permeating CPAs are too large to enter the cell and function extracellularly. One of the most common non-permeating CPAs is trehalose which works as a dehydrating agent that that reduces the amount of intracellular ice formation by drawing water out of the cell prior to freezing and can act as a vitrifying agent in high concentrations. Trehalose as a cryoprotectant has been used in the cryopreservation of whole-testis in other organisms (Seki et al. 2024).

Cooling rate is equally as important as CPA selection in preventing the formation of intracellular ice (Hunt 2017). Conventional or slow freezing is the controlled and gradual reduction in temperature, thereby minimizing cellular injury by reducing the risk of intracellular ice formation and osmotic stress (Mazur et al. 1970, Wolkers and Oldenhof 2021). Another cooling approach is vitrification which is the ultra-rapid cooling utilizing high CPA concentrations, which bypasses ice crystal formation and the liquid in the cell or sample turns into an amorphous, glass-like state (Rall and Fahy 1985). While both methods aim to protect cellular integrity, they differ in their mechanisms. Slow freezing achieves this through incremental temperature reduction whereas vitrification relies on near-instantaneous cooling.

This study demonstrates the first successful cryopreservation of a whole organ in an insect. We consider 50% or higher cell viability post-cryopreservation to be successful. We determined the best method for cryopreservation of whole testicular-seminal vesicle complex from male An. gambiae mosquitos through manipulation of CPAs, cooling rates, and types of vessels used for cooling samples was investigated. These factors are all important to achieving successful cryopreservation of complex tissue structures. We employed a step-wise protocol to determine the best protocol for germ tissue survival, as well as a protocol for general use in non-cryo specific laboratories.

Methods

Mosquito Rearing

Adult An. gambiae (G3 strain) male mosquitoes were obtained from the Center for Disease Control and Prevention, Atlanta, GA. Mosquitos were fed a 10% sucrose solution which contained 0.2% methyl paraben and were allowed to feed ad libitum. They were stored in a 24 °C incubator with 70% humidity.

Dissection and Cryopreservation Preparation

Testis were obtained by slowly pulling apart the last abdominal segments from the rest of the body which resulted in the evisceration of the testis and accessory gland complex (Fig. 1). Testis along with the accessory glands were then moved to a drop of saline in a 3 cm petri plate (Falcon, USA) (Fig. 1E). The testes were then separated from the accessory glands and placed in a drop of ice cold cryoprotective solution in another sterile 3 cm petri plate on ice. Thereafter the testes were picked onto a 1.27 mm (inner diameter) copper cryoloop affixed to the inner lid of a 1.8 ml cryovial (Nunc, Finland) while suspended in the CPA. The lid and loop were secured on to the cryovial and placed in a controlled rate freezer or frozen by other methods described below. The copper used for the cryoloop has a plastic coating that minimizes toxicity to the sample.

Dissection protocol for An. gambiae testis. Panels A to C show removal of the posterior abdominal segments to begin exposing the testis. Panel A shows a male mosquito prior to dissection. Panel B shows the beginning of the dissection protocol in which the abdominal segments are beginning to be removed from the rest of the body. Panel C shows the removal of the accessory glands, while panel D and E show the testis which are located slightly anterior to the accessory glands within the organism.

Cryopreservation

This study used a basal medium of Grace’s insect cell culture medium or a 0.75% sodium chloride. Grace’s insect medium contains amino acids, vitamins, inorganic salts, and other components (osmolality: 367 mOsm/kg; pH: 6.0) (11605094, Gibco ThermoFisher Sci., USA). Grace’s insect medium is supplemented with lactalbumin hydrolysate, yeastolate, L-glutamine, and sodium bicarbonate in addition to the general components of Grace’s medium. The cryoprotects used included 10% DMSO and 0.5M trehalose. These cryoprotectants and protocols for vitrification have been previously established for spermatozoa cryopreservation (Campion et al. 2021). Trehalose has been demonstrated to be successful in the cryopreservation of cells and tissues larger than a single-cell, and the addition of BSA has successfully been used in the cryopreservation honeybee spermatozoa. Further details are as described below.

Determination of Optimal Cooling Rate

The first protocol tested an optimized cooling rate. We tested rate-controlled cooling using the Freeze Control® cryopreservation system (CryoLogic, Victoria, Australia) to determine an optimized cooling rate by measuring the success of freezing at a rate of 0.5 °C min−1 (n = 31), 1 °C min−1 (n = 36), 3 °C min−1 (n = 38), and 5 °C min−1 (n = 31). Prior to cooling, testes were placed in 10% DMSO in Grace’s medium on ice. Samples were then placed at 4 °C in the automated controlled rate freezer and chilled at 0.5, 1, 3, or 5 °C min−1, after samples reached −40 °C, they were plunged into liquid nitrogen and stored in a liquid nitrogen storage dewar.

Determination of Optimal Incubation and Rate-Cooling Protocols

Incubation protocols were assessed using three different techniques, first using a refrigerated pre-incubation step prior to direct plunging into liquid nitrogen, second using a passive cooling device (PCD) (CoolCell™, Corning, USA) prior to direct plunging into liquid nitrogen, and third directly plunging samples into liquid nitrogen. Testes were incubated in Grace’s medium containing 10% DMSO. The first subsample was incubated at 4 °C for 20 min, then plunged directly into liquid nitrogen (n = 5). Another subsample was placed in the PCD and moved to a −80 °C freezer which lowers the temperature 1 °C min−1 once samples reached −80 °C, they were then directly plunged into liquid nitrogen (n = 5). Control samples were vitrified by directly plunging in liquid nitrogen after the addition of 10% DMSO in Graces cell culture medium to the organ (n = 5).

Vitrification CPA Assessment

DMSO-free non-permeating CPAs were assessed by incubating the whole testis in the media for no longer than 10 min (7–10 min) prior to plunge freezing in liquid nitrogen. The equilibrated testis was loaded on a 1.27 mm copper cryoloop as described above and placed into the cryovial prior to plunging into liquid nitrogen. CPAs and mixtures evaluated included Grace’s medium (control) (n = 5), 0.5 molar (M) trehalose in Grace’s medium, 0.5M trehalose in 0.75% aqueous sodium chloride (n = 4), and 0.5M trehalose in Grace’s containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (n = 4). The vitrification capacity of 0.5M trehalose in Grace’s medium was confirmed using differential scanning calorimetry (Hitachi DSC7000X, Gurgaon, India), which demonstrated that the solution successfully vitrified.

Thaw and Viability

We assessed testicular tissue survival post cryopreservation after storage in liquid nitrogen after 7 days. Samples were rapidly thawed by placing directly into room temperature Schneider’s Drosophila Medium and incubated in SYBR14 (Invitrogen; L7011) and propidium iodide (Invitrogen; L7011). These dyes differentially permeate and bind the nuclei in live and dead cells. Images of thawed testis were visualized and captured using a live cell fluorescent imaging microscope (Lionheart FX, Agilent-Biotek, Santa Clara, CA) using fluorescent sources appropriate for each stain (Fig. 2). The proportion of live and dead cells was measured using ImageJ software using the Color Pixel Counter plug-in (Pichette 2010, Rueden et al. 2017).

Example of testis that were stained using a live/dead stain. Cells fluorescing green are live cells, and cells fluorescing red are dead cells. The control (left) image shows what a testis looks like post-dissection and without going through the cryopreservation process. This image shows some mechanical damage from handling. The cryopreserved testis (right) has gone through the cryopreservation process through vitrification by directly plunging in liquid nitrogen with 0.5M trehalose and BSA and has been thawed. Damage can be seen around the perimeter tissue, but the majority of the organ is intact with live sperm/testicular tissue cells.

Statistical Analysis

Dunn test for multiple comparisons (with Holm-Šídák correction) was used to determine significantly efficient methods of cryopreservation yielding the highest proportion of testicular cell survival.

Results

To determine an optimal cooling rate that resulted in the highest number of viable sperm cells in the seminal vesicle, the testis was chilled at 0.5, 1, 3, and 5 °C min−1. The highest testicular tissue and spermatozoa survival was noted when chilled at a rate of 1 °C min−1 down to −40 °C then plunged into liquid nitrogen (0.55 ± 0.11 proportion of live cells) (Fig. 3, 1 vs 0.5 °C min−1 P = 0.054, 1 vs 3 °C min−1 P = 0.003, 1 vs 5 °C min−1 P = 0.009).

Proportion of viable cells in An. gambiae testis after cryopreservation. The whole testis in 10% DMSO was loaded on a copper cryoloop and chilled to −40 °C at 0.5, 1, 3, and 5 °C min−1 using a programmable freezer. Letters represent significant differences as determined by the Dunn’s post-hoc test.

Second, we tested different incubation and rate-cooling protocols prior to cryopreservation. Cooling of the sample in 10% DMSO after a 20-min equilibration at 4 °C had the highest viability (0.62 ± 0.1 proportion of live cells). However, this procedure was not statistically different than non-equilibrated plunging or freezing using a −80 °C freezer chilled coolers (Fig. 4, P = 0.249). In fact, the −80 °C chilled coolers which are purported to freeze at 1 °C min−1 resulted in the lowest viability (0.51 ± 0.9 proportion of live cells).

Proportion of viable cells in An. gambiae testis after utilization of different controlled-rate cooling protocols. The whole testis in 10% DMSO in Grace’s Medium was loaded on a copper cryoloop and cooled by different methods. First using a refrigerated pre-incubation step prior to direct plunging into liquid nitrogen, second, directly plunging samples into liquid nitrogen, and third using a PCD prior to direct plunging into liquid nitrogen.

Third, we assessed DMSO-free vitrification with non-permeating CPAs. These CPAs included 0.5M trehalose in saline; 0.5M trehalose in Grace’s medium, and 0.5M trehalose in Grace’s medium with 1% BSA. Directly plunging the sample into liquid nitrogen without equilibration with the standard Grace’s insect cell culture medium yielded higher viability (Fig. 5, 0.71 ± 0.21 proportion of live cells) than the other treatments containing non-permeating cryoprotectants such as trehalose and BSA. Tissue cryopreserved in trehalose with BSA contained more viable cells (0.52 ± 0.11 proportion of live cells) than the tissue cryopreserved without the added BSA (0.51.3 ± 0.11 proportion of live cells) but they were statistically not differentiable from the samples cryopreserved in Grace’s medium (P = 0.676, P = 0.5, respectively). Similarly, trehalose in saline was less effective (0.46 ± 0.06 proportion of live cells) than trehalose in Grace’s medium (51.4 ± 0.1 proportion of live cells) although, they were not significantly different (P = 0.6).

Proportion of viable cells in An. gambiae testis after directly plunging into liquid nitrogen in different vitrifying cryoprotectants and Grace’s medium as the control.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated multiple cryopreservation approaches to identify conditions that maximized An. gambiae testicular and seminal cell survival. Experimental variables included cooling rate, incubation protocols, and the use of different non-permeating cryoprotectants. Cell survival varied little among cooling rates; however, the highest survival was observed at the cooling rate of 1 °C min−1 to −40 °C followed by a plunge into liquid nitrogen. We found that other enhancements to the cryopreservation protocol did not significantly improve cell survival, but survival overall with all treatments was relatively high.

The combination of cryoprotectants and cooling rates is important for successful cryopreservation and cell survival. This is even more true with respect to tissues due to variable permeability and water loss characteristics. In general, cells such as the spermatozoa with very minimal cytoplasm, tend to tolerate a wide range of freezing rates. However, these cells are still sustainable to damage from osmotic effects which could be due to rapidly changing solute concentrations with non-compatible freezing rates (Mazur et al. 1972). Sperm cells are fragile enough to be damaged by the physical forces from the extracellular ice as well as osmolarity resulting in broken sperm cells and irreversibly coiled or twisted tails that would impede the cell’s motility during the fertilization process (Morris et al. 2012). Hence this study investigated the viability of testicular tissue containing a distinct region of mature sperm cells in the associated seminal vesicle after exposure to a cryopreservation protocol which included a variety of cooling rates and cryoprotectants. The base treatment used 10% DMSO as cryoprotectant, as it is one of the most common cryoprotectants utilized for tissue cryopreservation (Friedler et al. 1988, Siebzehnruebl et al. 1989, Boquet et al. 1995, Ock and Rho 2011, Whaley et al. 2021). DMSO is a commonly used permeating cryoprotectant due to its low cytotoxicity especially at lower doses and temperatures (Lovelock and Bishop 1959) and has been shown to have a low toxicity in mosquitoes (Campbell et al. 2021). DMSO can be especially useful in the cryopreservation of sperm, and germinal tissues (Lovelock and Bishop 1959, Lee et al. 2013).

In this study, a controlled rate cooler was used to assess the effects of freezing rates on the male germinal tissue and to accurately assess the optimal cooling rate. A rapid cooling rate, which is achieved by a controlled-rate cooler, can help avoid cellular damage (Mazur 1984, 2004). In the current study, the cooling rate that was found to have the highest survival was 1 °C min−1 through the use a of a programmable freezer, which is a commonly applied freeze rate for cellular cryopreservation (Mazur 1963), although there was little difference in overall tissue survival based on rate of cooling. This is interesting because freezing rates higher than 1 °C min−1 often apply to tissue structures less than 1 mm3 (Meryman 1956). The approximate volume of the testis-seminal vesicle complex in An. gambiae was calculated assuming the organ to comprise of a hemisphere and a cone. The volume was determined to be ∼4.1 × 107 µm3 which is equivalent to 0.041 mm3. That the 3° and 5° min−1 did not yield higher number of intact sperm cells indicates that the testicular tunic offers significant resistance to water movement from the organ.

There are two types of equipment that can be used to cryopreserve samples at predictable/programmable rates including rate-controlled devices and passive cooling devices (PCDs). The current study results show that 1 °C min−1 was the most successful cooling rate using a controlled-rate cooler. Thereafter, a PCD which cools samples at a near-linear rate of 1 °C min−1 was assessed. This device is relatively inexpensive and can be used as a controlled rate cooler for cryopreservation in many laboratories using an available −80 °C storage freezer. The device essentially cools the sample using a thermo-conductive alloy heatsink core combined with foam insulation which allows cooling the samples at a rate of ∼1 °C min−1 when placed in a −80 °C freezer. PCDs have been used successfully in other organisms for stem cells (Hunt 2017) and tissue cryopreservation (Huang et al. 2017), including germ tissue (Lierman et al. 2020). Although 1 °C min−1 was found to have the best survival outcome in our controlled rate cooler experiment, it did not translate to samples cooled in the PDC, which had a lower testicular tissue survival than samples which were directly plunged in liquid nitrogen. The reasoning for this may be due to the logistics of transitioning samples from the −80 °C freezer to the liquid nitrogen. While the amount of time the PDC was exposed to room temperature was minimal, it may have been enough time to cause a thaw event before reaching the liquid nitrogen. Samples cooled in the PCD may have also experienced a cooling rate that is slower near the end of the cooling period, which differs from the linear cooling rate that controlled-rate coolers achieve, as cooling in a PCD is asymptotic and cooling rates are slower when reaching the end point temperature, while cooling an a controlled-rate freezer is linear (Hunt 2017). Another possibility for the difference in testicular tissue survival may be due to the different temperatures that the samples were brought to before being plunged in liquid nitrogen. In the controlled rate cooler, samples are brought down to −40 °C before being plunged into liquid nitrogen. The PCD brings the samples down to −80 °C as they are being placed in a −80 °C freezer for cooling. Once samples are −80 °C they are then plunged into the liquid nitrogen. Exposing cells to −80 °C prior to plunging into liquid nitrogen may cause more freeze damage to the cells. Although, there was still a high survival of cells in the PDC treatment, and survival was not significantly different than samples in the controlled rate freezer, this may still be a viable option for labs interested in cryopreserving germline tissue as it is the simplest and most cost-effective option.

Finally, we assessed whether testicular tissue would vitrify without the use of xenochemicals such as DMSO. Vitrification is a process that forms a glass-like substance by utilizing high concentrations of cryoprotectants to prevent ice crystal formation (Rall and Fahy 1985). We tested the differences in cell survival using different vitrifying cryoprotectants. We did not find any significant difference in the proportion of cellular survival among the treatments. Although, our results show that Grace’s medium had a high proportion of live cells, even higher than some of the vitrification solutions. The use of Grace’s medium was intended to be used as a negative control in our study because it was used in all of the cryoprotectant solutions. It is not believed to have vitrifying properties, but testis that were frozen in Grace’s medium alone survived the cryopreservation protocol. This may be due to the formulation of Grace’s medium which contains sugars such as sucrose, as well as many amino acids such as lysine, histidine, arginine, beta alanine, and proline all which have cryoprotectant properties (Fukumori et al. 2022). The treatment that included 0.5M trehalose in saline (not Grace’s) had the lowest cell survival. This reiterates the protective ability of Grace’s medium. High cell survival in this treatment may be due to low toxicity during the thawing process, in comparison to other CPAs used such as DMSO and BSA (Campbell et al. 2021, Nesbitt et al. 2021). These CPA’s need to be diluted out of the system directly after thawing to avoid toxicity effects. Grace’s medium is a cell growth medium and has a low toxicity.

Cryopreservation of An. gambiae germplasm is a tool for advancing malaria research and long-term vector control strategies. As the primary vector of human malaria, An. gambiae is important for research into parasite transmission, insecticide resistance and vector competence. The ability to cryopreserve male germline material preserves half of the mosquito genome and potentially enables recovery of genetic lines through artificial insemination. The development of cryopreservation protocols for An. gambiae also provides a framework that can be adapted to other medically important mosquito species. The primary criteria in this study was whether, post cryopreservation, the distinctly shaped sperm cells that appeared viable based on a membrane intactness assessment could be isolated and pooled from multiple testis and further used for artificial insemination endeavors. This is one of the optimal the future strategies for conservation of mosquito strains withstanding the inability to cryopreserve the late stage embryos of Anopheles sp.
