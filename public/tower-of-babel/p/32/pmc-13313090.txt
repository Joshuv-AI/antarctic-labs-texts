Patient‐Reported Swallowing Outcomes ≥5 Years After Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy and Transoral Robotic Surgery for HPV‐positive Oropharyngeal Carcinoma
Authors: Sandhya Ganesan, Sana Smaoui, Leena Zitoun, Tyler P Tufano, Esther Lee, Punam Thakkar, Arjun Joshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate long‐term swallowing outcomes in patients with HPV‐associated oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (HPV + OPSCC) following neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) and transoral robotic surgery (TORS) (NAC + S).Study DesignRetrospective cross‐sectional study.SettingSingle academic institution.MethodsPatients completed the MD Anderson Dysphagia Inventory (MDADI) at various time points following the completion of treatment. Patients requiring adjuvant treatment, experiencing recurrent disease, or who did not complete an MDADI at least 5 years following treatment were excluded from the study. A linear regression model was explored to determine the relationship between time since treatment and MDADI score.ResultsA total of 21 patients treated with the NAC + S protocol were included. The average time between treatment and the most recent follow‐up was 101.5 months. 1 patient had a composite MDADI score indicating poor function (<60), 3 indicating adequate function (≥60 to <80), and 17 indicating optimal function (≥80). There was no significant change in overall MDADI score (P = .469), global score (P = .163), emotional score (P = .528), and physical score (P = .903) over time. A small yet significant improvement in functional score of 1.06 points per year was seen over time (P = .0096).ConclusionPatients who were successfully treated with NAC + S maintain their favorable swallowing outcomes on long‐term follow‐up. A small improvement in functional MDADI sub‐score may represent compensatory strategies or rehabilitation over time.Keywords: HPV+ head and neck cancer, quality of life, swallowing function

The increasing prevalence of human papillomavirus (HPV) infection worldwide has led to an increase in HPV‐positive oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC). Patients with HPV‐associated OPSCC (HPV + OPSCC) often demonstrate improved prognosis and treatment response when compared to HPV‐negative OPSCC. 1 , 2 OPSCC treatments vary; however, standard treatment involves chemoradiation, often resulting in long‐term functional impairments as a result of tissue edema and fibrosis. 3 , 4 , 5 Given the significant morbidity associated with chemoradiation in addition to the favorable prognosis of patients with HPV + OPSCC, de‐escalated treatment strategies have been explored to preserve oncologic outcomes while minimizing treatment‐related toxicity. 6 , 7

Various de‐escalation protocols are currently under investigation, one of which includes neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) followed by transoral robotic surgery (NAC + S). This protocol combines systemic escalation of treatment with 3 cycles of neoadjuvant platinum‐based chemotherapy, followed by locoregional de‐escalation with a reduced primary surgical resection margin and reservation of radiation for salvage treatment. Previous studies exploring this protocol have found the de‐escalated treatment to improve 5‐year disease‐free survival and reduce dependence on feeding tubes compared to stage‐matched patients undergoing standard treatments. 8 , 9 , 10

In addition to maintaining oncologic outcomes, an important component of de‐escalated treatment is to reduce treatment‐related morbidity. In head and neck cancer patients, compromised swallowing function is one of the main factors influencing quality of life following treatment. Long‐term studies of traditional treatment therapies, specifically chemoradiation, often result in functional swallowing impairment. 11 This is particularly relevant for patients with HPV + OPSCC, who tend to be younger and have good prognosis, making the long‐term effects of cancer treatment impactful. Preserving swallowing function and overall quality of life is, therefore, an extremely important consideration in this patient population. 5

A 2‐year follow‐up study demonstrated that patients treated with the NAC + S protocol maintained satisfactory patient‐reported swallowing outcome measurement using the MD Anderson Dysphagia Inventory (MDADI). 12 This finding suggests that NAC + S treatment may offer a long‐term functional benefit by preserving swallowing ability while maintaining favorable oncologic control. However, given the potential for delayed treatment‐related effects in head and neck cancer survivors, it is imperative to investigate long‐term swallowing function. We aim to report long‐term patient‐reported swallowing outcomes in the same cohort at least 5 years following treatment to determine whether these favorable outcomes persist with extended follow‐up.

Methods

Study Population

Patients with OPSCC who received surgery at the George Washington University Hospital between 2010 and 2020 were identified. The inclusion criteria are as follows: treatment‐naive patients diagnosed with HPV + OPSCC who received NAC followed by transoral robotic surgery (TORS) (NAC + S) as primary treatment. The exclusion criteria are as follows: patients who are diagnosed with HPV‐ OPSCC, patients who required adjuvant radiotherapy (RT) or concurrent chemoradiation therapy (CCRT), and/or patients with less than 5 years of follow‐up. Only patients in the initial study conducted at George Washington University Hospital by Lee et al were approached and included if they consented to the study. 12 The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (NCR202928) of the George Washington University.

Treatment Protocol

The NAC + S protocol begins with 3 cycles of taxane‐based chemotherapy, consisting of docetaxel (75 mg/m2) and cisplatin (75 mg/m2). Chemotherapy infusions were completed once every 3 weeks, and patients were followed closely by medical oncology to manage any treatment toxicities. Between each cycle of chemotherapy, the operating otolaryngologist assessed patients for treatment response through fiberoptic nasopharyngoscopy and point‐of‐care ultrasound examinations. At least 3 weeks following the completion of the third cycle of chemotherapy, patients underwent TORS in addition to either unilateral or bilateral neck dissection. 12

Patient‐Reported Swallowing Function Assessment

The MD Anderson Dysphagia Inventory (MDADI) was collected from patients at various time points, either via telephone, email, or clinic‐administered survey, following the completion of NAC + S treatment. Patients without an MDADI at ≥5 years following completion of NAC + S treatment were excluded. The MDADI is a 20‐item, patient‐reported questionnaire that evaluates the effect of dysphagia on quality of life in head and neck cancer patients. It reports four subscores: global (1 item), emotional (6 items), functional (5 items), and physical (8 items). A composite score is calculated from the subscores and ranges from 20 to 100, with higher scores indicating better swallowing function. Composite scores are categorized based on scoring criteria: <60 indicating poor function, ≥60 to <80 indicating adequate function, and ≥80 indicating optimal function.

Statistical Analysis

A mixed‐effects linear regression model was fitted to the data to predict composite and MDADI subscores. As individual patients had multiple MDADI responses included in the analysis, a random intercept was included for patient identity. The regression was fitted as a function of time in months. All data analysis was completed in the R statistical computing environment (R Core Team 2021).

Results

During the study period, as described in Lee et al, a total of 127 patients underwent NAC followed by surgery (NAC + S) as primary treatment for OPSCC at our institution. Of these, 48 patients (37.8%) received adjuvant radiation therapy based on surgical pathology and were excluded. Among the remaining 79 patients treated with NAC + S alone, 49 were diagnosed with HPV‐positive OPSCC and treated at least 2 years prior to the study period. Of the original cohort of 37 patients followed in Lee et al, 2 patients were deceased, and 14 patients did not complete routine MDADI surveys and were thus excluded from the study. A total of 21 patients were then included in the study for statistical analysis. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 66 years, and the majority of patients were male (86%). Most patients identified as White (76%), with the remainder identifying as Black (19%) or Hispanic (5%). The most common tumor subsite was the tonsil (67%), followed by the base of tongue (29%) and the soft palate (4%). Clinical staging, based on the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition, showed that most patients presented with stage II disease (71%), followed by stage I disease (24%) and stage III disease (5%). No patients experienced recurrence of their OPSCC or required further adjuvant treatment following completion of NAC + S treatment.

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate the persistence of optimal patient‐reported swallowing outcomes more than 5 years after treatment of HPV‐associated OPSCC using the NAC plus transoral robotic surgery (NAC + TORS) protocol. This pathologically driven treatment approach aims to help eligible patients avoid or minimize the long‐term adverse effects of radiation on swallowing function. Patients with HPV‐positive OPSCC tend to be younger and healthier, presenting with a more favorable prognosis. Thus, efforts have focused on developing treatment strategies that maintain long‐term quality of life while maintaining favorable oncologic outcomes. 8

NAC has been shown to induce HPV‐specific T‐cell immunity, and when followed by TORS resection, it significantly reduces the number of patients requiring adjuvant radiation. 13 , 14 This treatment approach has demonstrated favorable oncologic outcomes, with disease‐free and overall survival rates equivalent to those seen with primary chemoradiation (CCRT) or TORS followed by adjuvant radiation. 8 , 15 Silver et al examined quality of life in patients treated with the NAC + TORS protocol and found significant improvements in global quality of life following treatment, with functional scores returning to or exceeding baseline levels. 16 Lee et al evaluated swallowing function at 2 years posttreatment and reported favorable patient‐reported outcomes. 12 The present study builds on that work, demonstrating that these favorable patient‐reported swallowing outcomes are sustained at longer‐term follow‐up of 5 years or more.

The durable swallowing outcomes observed in this cohort contrast with those typically seen in patients treated with definitive radiation‐based therapy. Radiation to the head and neck is often associated with late‐onset dysphagia due to fibrosis, neuropathy, xerostomia, edema, and stricture formation in the aerodigestive tract. These complications are known to contribute to long‐term feeding tube dependence and reduced quality of life. 3 , 4 , 5 In a cohort of p16+ OPSCC patients treated with definitive CCRT, 7.5% developed late progressive dysphagia and 15.1% experienced moderate dysphagia 2.5 years posttreatment. 17 Objective assessments using videofluoroscopic swallow studies (VFSS) have also shown significant long‐term swallowing dysfunction in this population. 18 The literature appears to suggest that the addition of radiation treatment is associated with poorer functional outcomes. Despite these previous findings, a recent meta‐analysis did not identify a statistically significant difference in swallowing outcomes between TORS and CCRT. 19

Lee et al reported that patients treated with NAC + TORS had superior patient‐reported swallowing outcomes compared to those treated with definitive CCRT at the 2‐year mark. 12 At their 2‐year follow‐up, the median composite MDADI score of the present cohort was 98.9. As described by Hutcheson et al, this is a clinically significant difference of over 10 points greater than patients treated with CCRT, whose median MDADI scores are reported to range between 68.4 and 87.4. 20

At 2 to 5 years posttreatment, between 11.1% and 32% of patients treated with radiation have been reported to have poor swallowing function, compared to 8.1% in our NAC + TORS cohort. 21 , 22 In another study assessing long‐term swallow function in a cohort of patients treated with definitive CCRT, the mean MDADI score was 68, representing a clinically significant reduction in function at the 5‐year mark and beyond. 23 In contrast, the sustained improved patient‐reported swallowing trend appears to continue beyond 5 years in our study cohort, with a median composite MDADI score of 94. Additionally, studies have shown that patients with lower cranial nerve palsy following CCRT have significantly worse swallowing scores (median MDADI scores of 68) compared to those without palsy (median MDADI score of 80.2). 24 Both of these scores remain substantially lower than those seen in the current NAC + TORS cohort. Long‐term swallowing outcomes after TORS alone have been reported infrequently in the literature; however, a comparative analysis conducted by Mehta et al demonstrates that TORS alone has been associated with greater MDADI scores and more favorable swallowing outcomes compared with TORS plus adjuvant RT or chemoradiotherapy, with differences exceeding the MCID (88.0 vs 73.1‐74.9), highlighting the functional cost of adjuvant radiation. 25

Interestingly, we observed a small but statistically significant increase in the functional MDADI subscore over time, with an estimated average improvement of 1.06 points per year posttreatment. This change may reflect ongoing adaptation, including compensatory strategies or long‐term improvements in function. At our institution, patients treated with NAC + TORS are referred to and followed up with by speech‐language pathologists in the immediate postoperative period for diet texture modification and compensatory maneuvers to alleviate any dysphagia‐related risks (eg, head turn, chin tuck). Generally, these patients do not require continued dysphagia intervention beyond the initial healing period and do not require percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy feeding tube insertion, unlike many patients who undergo definitive CCRT, who often require extensive rehabilitation. 26 , 27 However, interpretation of this trend is limited given that diet information was not collected as part of this study. It remains possible that ongoing dietary adjustments contributed to maintaining MDADI scores.

This study is not without limitations. Most notably, we highlight that objective swallowing assessments using imaging (eg, VFSS or FEES) were not performed to corroborate these patient‐reported outcomes. Additionally, pre‐treatment MDADI scores were not collected, precluding quantification of the exact magnitude of change in patient‐reported swallowing function. Additionally, the study relied on voluntary participation, which introduces potential bias due to selective participation, and although all patients included in our analyses completed the MDADI at least 5 years posttreatment, responses were not obtained at standardized intervals due to variations in follow‐up timing and clinic appointments.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate long‐term preservation of patient‐reported swallowing function for patients with HPV + OPSCC treated with the NAC + TORS protocol, reinforcing its value as a function‐sparing treatment approach. Given the established oncologic efficacy of this protocol, our results highlight its ability to also preserve swallowing‐related quality of life. Future research in this space should compare long‐term functional outcomes between NAC + TORS, standard chemoradiation, and other de‐escalation protocols using objective measures of swallowing, such as FEES or VFSS, which may also improve our understanding of the mechanisms underlying post‐treatment dysphagia to help tailor patient‐centered treatment strategies.
