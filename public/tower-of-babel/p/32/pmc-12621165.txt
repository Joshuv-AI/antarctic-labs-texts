Navigating early allergenic food introduction: Practices and barriers among parents and caregivers
Authors: Wan Shen, Phil Welch, HeeSoon Lee, Jayden Parrott
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly introduction of allergenic foods to infants starting 4–6‐months of age has been shown to effectively prevent the development of food allergies. This project examines the practices and barriers among parents and caregivers toward feeding infants allergenic foods.MethodsA cross‐sectional online survey was conducted from January to March 2024 with primary focus on the Midwest area of the United States. The survey was advertised through local health departments and social media platforms. Parental practices on feeding allergenic foods to their infants, as well as perceived barriers were collected. Descriptive and inferential statistical analyses were used to summarize participant demographics, parental willingness and feelings toward allergenic food introduction, and the actual practices. Thematic analysis was employed to identify qualitative data such as parental concerns and barriers.ResultsThe study included 563 valid responses from parents and caregivers of children aged 5 years or younger. The surveyed infants were predominantly white (84.9%) and female (58.1%), with a mean age of 7.5 months. Among families who had introduced solid foods to their infants (n = 529), eggs (72%) and wheat (63%) had the highest introduction rates by age one, while shellfish (44%) and sesame (52%) had the lowest. Only 55.9% of families reported receiving guidance from healthcare providers on allergenic food introduction. Thematic analysis revealed concerns about inconsistent advice, fear of allergic reactions, cultural beliefs, social media influence, and safety concerns such as choking hazards and inconsistent allergen labeling.ConclusionParents and caregivers exhibited caution in feeding allergenic foods to their infants by age one. Barriers included lack of awareness, cultural practices, conflicting guidance, and social media influences.

Key messageHealthcare provider support is limited, with only 55.9% of parents reporting receiving guidance on allergenic food introduction, highlighting a need for improved education and standardized recommendations. Cultural and religious dietary restrictions impact allergenic food introduction. Providing culturally tailored education and suitable food alternatives, such as fortified soy products or canned fish, can help parents incorporate allergenic foods while respecting their traditions. Many parents and caregivers are unaware of updated allergen introduction guidelines, often due to inconsistent healthcare messaging and misinformation. Healthcare providers should enhance early education efforts, including prenatal counseling and daycare‐based awareness programs, to ensure parents receive reliable, evidence‐based information. Registered dietitians (RDs) play a crucial role in educating parents on safe allergenic food introduction. They can provide individualized strategies to manage food refusal, mitigate allergy risks, and ensure children receive proper nutrition while preventing unnecessary food avoidance.

INTRODUCTION

Immunoglobulin E (IgE)‐mediated food allergies are life‐threatening health conditions, that affect approximately 1 in 13 children, 1 and 1 in 10 adults 2 in the United States. The disease impacts nearly 85 million, or 1 in 4, Americans' dietary practices. 3 The top 9 common allergens in the United States are eggs, dairy, and peanuts, tree nuts, fish, shellfish, wheat, sesame, and soy, though any food has the potential to cause an allergic reaction. Food allergies are suspected to have a genetic and environmental etiology, but the exact cause for the development is not yet well understood. Before 2008, guidelines from the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) advised parents and caregivers to delay the introduction of allergenic foods to young children to avoid potential fatal allergic reactions. 4 Unfortunately, the Center for Disease Control (CDC) reported a 50% increase in rates of peanut allergies among children from 1997 to 2011, 5 , 6 and the rates for peanut or tree nut allergies among children increased more than 3 fold from 1997 to 2008. 7

Based on the observation that countries, notably Israel, had higher rates of early introduction of peanuts, results from the Learning Early About Peanut Allergy (LEAP) study showed that early introduction of peanut and peanut‐containing products to infants before 12 months of age could decrease the risk of the development of IgE‐mediated peanut allergy later in life. 8 More specifically, consistent dietary exposure to peanuts from infancy through 5 years of age was associated with at least a 70% reduction in the development of peanut allergies, regardless of whether children had positive or negative skin‐prick test results before 1 year of age. 8 This protective effect persisted among those who adhered to the regimen, even when peanuts were avoided during the sixth year of life. 9 Other research findings also indicate that the introduction of dairy, 10 , 11 peanuts, 12 , 13 and eggs 12 , 14 as early as 4–6 months lowered the risk of developing these specific food allergies. As a result of these findings, the current National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Disease (NIAID)‐endorsed Addendum Guidelines for the Primary Prevention of Peanut Allergy guidelines 15 encourage the early introduction of peanut‐containing foods to infants with severe eczema or egg allergy starting at 4–6 months. Similar practice guidelines are published in the AAP 16 and the Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025 17 which endorse the early introduction of allergenic foods to infants at around 6 months. As of now, the early introduction of allergenic food is considered an effective strategy to prevent the development of food allergies. 18

Despite these changes in recommendations, pediatricians have been slow in updating their practices in the U.S. 19 This creates confusion and hesitancy among parents/caregivers concerning the early introduction of allergenic foods. 20 , 21 , 22 Common barriers and hesitancies that hindered parents and caregivers from following the updated AAP guidelines included limited information and guidance from pediatricians, 23 , 24 fear of allergic reactions, 25 infant refusal, 25 practical problems such as food preparation to age‐appropriate consistency, 25 and being low socioeconomic status. 26 It is important to note that the above propositions were either from research results collected almost 10 years ago or from countries other than the United States. This project examines the practices in feeding allergenic foods to infants among parents and caregivers, and pinpoints specific barriers and hesitancies in introducing the top 9 allergenic foods to infants within the first year of life. We designed an online survey intended to collect both quantitative and qualitative data on current or planned parental practices and barriers on early allergenic food introduction to infants before 1 year old and advice from primary pediatricians regarding this practice.

METHODS

Study design

Between January and March 2024, parents and caregivers of children were invited to complete an online survey regarding allergenic solid food introduction. Recruitment flyers were posted at local health departments and on social media platforms (Facebook, WeChat, and X). Eligibility was restricted to individuals with at least one child aged 5 or younger at the time of the survey. Parents and caregivers who agreed to participate submitted informed consent forms using processes approved by the Institutional Review Board at the affiliated institution. The survey was distributed online using the Qualtrics survey system (Qualtrics, Provo, UT, USA). Participants were entered into a drawing for 1 of 50 Amazon gift cards (valued at $20 each) to improve the survey response rate. An a priori power analysis was conducted using free, online statistical software developed by the Raosoft corporation. 27 This analysis yielded a suggested sample size of 386 based on a 5% margin of error, a 95% confidence level, a population size >20,000, and a 50/50 response distribution.

Survey questions

The study instrument contained 35 items. Face and content validity of the instrument was established via literature review and expert review by three professionals in the survey research, qualitative research, and nutrition fields. The experts provided suggestions for changes which were incorporated into the final instrument. Participants were prompted to provide data specifically about their youngest child or infant. Demographic information (age, sex, race/ethnicity), detailed family (biological parent, grandparent or sibling) histories of allergies, and risk factors associated with food allergies (presence of eczema, asthma or hay fever) were collected. Participants were asked about if the child has ever been diagnosed with a food allergy by a medical professional and options include yes, no, not sure and prefer not to answer; then questions related to whether the allergen was outgrown by the time of the survey were asked. Additionally, questions about feeding practices, including the timing of the initial introduction of allergenic foods and the current frequency of consumption of the top nine allergens were asked. The willingness of parents or caregivers to introduce allergenic foods around the age of 6 months was assessed by a single item using constructs from the Precaution Adoption Process Model. 28 Possible responses were “I have already started to follow this recommendation”, “I have heard but not thought much about the recommendation”, “I am undecided about this recommendation”, “I have not heard about this recommendation”, “I will start following this recommendation as soon as possible”, and “I thought about it and decided not to follow this recommendation”. An additional item used Instrumental and Experiential attitude constructs from the Integrated Behavioral Model 29 to gage participants' feelings toward introducing allergenic foods to their child along a five‐point sliding scale with the following response options: “Easy (1) to Hard (5)”, “Safe (1) to Unsafe (5)”, “Beneficial (1) to Harmful (5)”, and “Not Scary (1) to Very Scary (5)”. A total score was calculated for each participant and labeled “Apprehensive” to follow the new guidelines ranging from low apprehensiveness (4) to high apprehensiveness (20). There was one multiple‐choice item for perceived barriers and three items to gage interest in and format preference for future educational programming about early introduction of allergenic foods.

Qualitative data regarding pediatricians' guidance on solid food introduction were collected using the following open‐ended prompt: “When your youngest child's primary care doctor was discussing feeding your youngest child allergenic foods, what guidance did they give?”. The last survey question was an open‐ended prompt asking “Do you have any additional concerns, questions, or comments about early introduction of allergenic foods?”

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 28 was used for descriptive and inferential statistical analyses. The qualitative data were examined independently by the researchers and grouped by themes. Thematic analysis was used to analyze the qualitative data. 30

RESULTS

Participants

A total of 968 responses were collected. After inspection, 31 405 responses were omitted due to lack of data and/or suspicion of automated bot‐generated responses (e.g., repetitive phrases and/or nonsensical errors; n = 335), low total completion rate (50% or less of items completed; n = 38), implausible responses with inconsistent reported information (n = 22), and rapidly completed surveys (i.e., a few seconds) that unlikely reflected thoughtful or thorough answers (n = 10). After cleaning, the final dataset comprised 563 valid responses.

Current or planned practices on allergenic food introduction

Most participants (n = 536, 95.2%) had already introduced solid foods to their infant/child at the time of the survey. As shown in Figure 2, among the families that completed self‐reported food introduction timing (n = 529), eggs (n = 381, 72.0%) and wheat (n = 331, 62.6%) had the highest introduction rates by the time the infants reached 1 year of age. In contrast, shellfish (n = 235, 44%) and sesame (n = 273, 51.6%) had the lowest introduction rates before the infants turned one. When assessing parental adherence to current NIAID guidelines among children with self‐reported eczema, asthma, or hay fever (n = 248), 19.8% (n = 49) introduced peanut‐containing foods between 4 and 6 months of age, 32.3% (n = 80) introduced peanuts between 7 and 12 months, and 20.2% (n = 50) delayed introduction until the child was 1 year old or older. In the subgroup of children with self‐reported eczema, asthma, or hay fever along with egg allergy (n = 97), only 14.4% (n = 14) had peanut‐containing foods introduced between the ages of 4 and 6 months. Twenty‐seven responses (n = 27, 4.8%) reported their infants had not been introduced to solids at the time of the survey. Among these, sixteen infants were under 5 months of age at the time, five infants were at 6 months of age, three were at 7 months of age, two were at 8 months of age and one was between 13 and 24 months of age.

The participants were also asked about the current feeding frequency of the nine allergenic foods. For families whose children were aged between 6 and 9 months of age, eggs (n = 71, 63.4%), cow's milk (n = 62, 55.4%) and wheat (n = 62, 55.4%) were the top foods that were included in the diet at least once a week, followed by fish (n = 54, 48.2%), tree nuts (n = 53, 47.3%), sesame (n = 53, 47.3%), peanuts (n = 51, 45.5%), soy (n = 50, 44.6%), and shellfish (n = 44, 39.3%). A plurality of participants (n = 269, 47.9%) reported already following the new guidelines or being ready to begin following the new guidelines as soon as possible. Approximately 12% (n = 67) had not heard about the new recommendations. The remaining participants (n = 220, 39%) had heard about but not thought much about the recommendations, were undecided about them, or had decided not to follow the recommendations. Received guidance on allergenic food introduction from health professionals

Roughly half of participants (n = 315, 55.9%) reported receiving guidance from their primary healthcare provider about what age to introduce allergenic foods to their infant. Thematic analysis revealed key concerns among parents/caregivers including the need for consistent advice about when to introduce allergenic foods, fear of allergic reactions, the influence of cultural beliefs and social media on feeding habits, and safety concerns such as choking hazards and inconsistent allergen labeling. DISCUSSION

Self‐reported food allergy rates were remarkably high in the present study (nearly 44%), with the top reported pediatric food allergies in this study being egg, peanut, and cow's milk, aligning with general trends observed in the pediatric population. 1 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 It is important to note that IgE‐mediated allergies to cow's milk and egg often develop during infancy, typically within the first 24 months of life, and tend to resolve by approximately 6 years of age. In contrast, peanut and tree nut allergies are usually diagnosed later in childhood and are more likely to persist into adulthood. 35 Consequently, the prevalence of food allergies caused by specific allergens may vary depending on the age group being surveyed.

The early introduction of allergenic foods has been demonstrated to be the most effective strategy for preventing the development of food allergies to date, particularly for high‐risk infants. 8 , 9 , 18 , 36 Delayed introduction of allergenic food to infants older than 10 months old is associated with a higher risk of developing food allergies (OR [95% CI] = 2.46 [1.77; 3.42]). 37 According to self‐reported data from this study, the introduction rates of the top nine allergens to infants by 6 months of age ranged between 15% and 30%. This rate increased to 30%–60% by 9 months of age and 45%–70% by 12 months of age, which was comparable to a recently published national survey on food allergen introduction patterns among infants by Venter, et al. 38 Based on the observation from this study, the introduction rates for peanuts, tree nuts, fish, shellfish, and sesame were lowest among the top nine allergens, accounting for 55.9%, 55.6%, 56.1%, 44.4%, and 51.6% to infants by 12 months of age, respectively. These numbers were similar to or higher than introduction rates (58.5%, 26.3%, 28.1%, 15.8%, and 26.9% for peanuts, tree nuts, fish, shellfish and sesame) found by Venter, et al. 38 and the peanut introduction rate of 27% found by Yakaboski, et al. among a convenience sample of multicenter cohorts across the U.S. from 2011 to 2014. 39 These U.S. introduction rates lag our peer nations. For example, Australia enjoys a 90% peanut introduction rate among infants by 12 months of age. 40

Beyond the timing of introduction, consistently exposing children to allergenic foods is a potential difficulty experienced by parents and caregivers according to a statement paper from the Canadian Society of Allergy and Clinical Immunology in 2023. 41 In this study, between 30% and 60% of caregivers indicated that one or more of the allergens were not consistently in their infant's diet. More research is needed to determine the quantity of food to be included in the diet to maintain allergy tolerance. Parents should be reminded that potentially allergenic foods are typically nutritious and calorically dense which benefits growing children. For example, fish and shellfish are excellent sources of protein, omega‐3 fatty acids, and other micronutrients such as iodine and selenium, 42 , 43 while nuts and peanuts can provide healthy unsaturated fatty acids, vitamin E, and magnesium. 44

Despite these benefits, parents and caregivers in the present study reported several perceived barriers to introducing allergenic foods to their children, including but not limited to cultural or religious practices, advice from medical professionals, and family members having food allergies. Several barriers—such as parental fear, infant food refusal, and practical challenges—have also been reported in other studies on early allergenic food introduction, 45 as well as during the reintroduction phase following negative oral food challenges. 46 Based on the findings of this study, we propose several potential strategies that may help address these barriers. These strategies could inform future research and public health efforts aimed at promoting timely and consistent allergenic food introduction.

Address cultural and religious practices

Cultural and religious practices were identified as a significant barrier to allergenic food introduction in this study. Creating culturally sensitive approaches 45 including education that respects these practices is essential to overcoming this challenge. For example, in cultures where dietary soy exposure is traditionally limited, families can be encouraged to incorporate soy‐based foods like fortified soy milk, tofu, or tempeh, which are nutritionally equivalent alternatives that align with allergenic food introduction guidelines. Similarly, in communities where shellfish or fish are not accessible, alternative strategies can include preparation of meals that use canned fish or frozen shellfish. By providing culturally appropriate substitutions and tailored education, healthcare professionals can bridge gaps in allergenic food exposure while respecting cultural norms. Further research is needed to identify and develop additional approaches that align allergenic food introduction with cultural practices.

Integrate registered dietitians into pediatric healthcare teams

We advocate for the integration of registered dietitians (RDs) into pediatric healthcare teams to address these challenges. RDs with pediatric health care providers can play a critical role in educating parents and caregivers on the appropriate timing and methods for introducing allergenic foods, particularly for high‐risk infants. Nutritional interventions by RDs should include evidence‐based strategies for safely introducing complementary and allergenic foods, providing guidance on mitigating risks associated with food exposure, and offering safe, nutritious alternatives when food elimination is necessary. 46 While food refusal is a common developmental stage in childhood that can cause parental anxiety, RDs and pediatric health care teams can develop tailored strategies to manage or mitigate these issues. For example, introducing allergenic foods in different forms—such as yogurt instead of liquid milk or baked quiche instead of scrambled eggs—can help overcome taste aversions.

Implement awareness and education campaigns

It has been shown that guidance from healthcare providers plays a crucial role in shaping parental decisions and practices related to the introduction of complementary foods. 47 A significant number of participants (12%) had not heard about the recommendations regarding early allergenic food introduction suggesting a need for more effective communication between parents and health professionals. Furthermore, recent reviews highlighted improper patient education as a key challenge in early allergenic food introduction. One possible explanation is that some healthcare providers may be slow to adopt updated recommendations from the AAP or may lack adequate continuing education on the subject. 19 , 47 , 48 , 49 Additional research is warranted to understand these gaps and provide solutions to improve provider education. Moreover, misinformation from online sources and conflicting family advice were reported as barriers. Health professionals including pediatric physicians, nurses, and dietitians can be valuable assets in addressing these barriers by providing reliable, evidence‐based education on allergenic food introduction to parents and caregivers at an early age of their children or to be included in the prenatal visits. In addition, daycare centers also play a central role in many children's early development and provide an excellent platform for awareness raising and educating caregivers, staff, and parents about the early introduction of allergenic foods. Cultural awareness and understanding of beliefs and practices related to nutrition, including dietary habits and sources of food, are essential for effectively educating parents and caregivers. 50 By fostering collaborative relationships between parents and childcare practitioners and integrating allergenic food education into daycare policies and routines, these centers can serve as places to address misconceptions, ensure a safe environment for allergen exposure, and support consistent dietary practices aligned with current guidelines. 51

Lastly, apprehensiveness toward following the new guidelines was higher when the child in question was younger. This could be due to a new parent/caregiver being overly protective or anxious about their first child. Parents and caregivers who had heard about the new recommendations but had not thought much about them also showed higher apprehension scores. These findings highlight the need for targeted, tailored interventions to promote timely, consistent allergenic food introduction in infants. For example, parents who are expecting their first child and have not thought much about the new recommendations may need more convincing than parents/caregivers of multiple children.

STUDY LIMITATIONS

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the study participants reported on infants that were predominantly white (85%) and female (58%), which introduces response bias and limits the generalizability of the findings due to a lack of racial, ethnic, and gender diversity. Additionally, the online recruitment strategy may have excluded participants with limited internet access, particularly those from lower socioeconomic or rural backgrounds. The use of self‐reported data for food allergy diagnosis, allergenic food introduction, and pediatrician guidance is also subject to recall bias, which could influence the accuracy of responses. Notably, we did not provide participants with standardized criteria for IgE‐mediated food allergy, which may have further impacted the accuracy of reported diagnoses. In this study, the self‐reported food allergy rate is higher than the national data on pediatric food allergy rates (8%) as reported by Dr. Gupta. 52 Surveys such as ours are more likely to be completed by people with heightened concerns about allergies, which may introduce additional self‐selection bias. Previous studies have shown that self‐reported food allergy prevalence often exceeds that confirmed through clinical evaluation (i.e., physician diagnosed). 52 , 53 , 54 The discrepancy between the self‐reported food allergy rate and national data on food allergy rates could also result from parental confusion between food intolerance and the presence of other non‐IgE mediated allergies such as food protein induced enterocolitis syndrome (FPIES) versus true IgE‐mediated allergies. The relatively high percentage of reported food allergy in the present study should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Overall, these methodological limitations highlight the need to interpret our findings within the context of these biases and in relation to broader epidemiological evidence. Our intent is not to generalize these findings to the entire pediatric population but to inform strategies relevant to families at heightened risk or concern. Future studies should aim for more diverse and representative samples, employ longitudinal designs to assess long‐term trends in allergenic food introduction, and incorporate objective measures such as standardized IgE‐mediated food allergy criteria or clinical testing. Moreover, exploration of cultural practices, healthcare access disparities, and pediatrician training may provide further insight into allergenic food introduction trends.

STUDY CONCLUSION

In conclusion, parents and caregivers were cautious in adopting the current clinical guidelines in the early introduction of allergenic foods to infants. Barriers included lack of awareness, cultural practices, conflicting guidance, and social media influences. The study advocates for integrating additional allied health professionals such as registered dietitians into pediatric healthcare teams to provide clear and consistent guidance on allergenic food introduction.
