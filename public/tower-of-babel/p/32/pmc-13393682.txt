Multilevel in silico structural analysis of WNT proteins
Authors: Konstantin Midlovets, Natalia Volkova, Mykyta Peka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWNTs are a family of signaling proteins involved in numerous biological processes, including morphogenesis, oncogenesis, cell migration and proliferation, cellular specialization, and tissue regeneration. WNT proteins are characterized by a distinctive structure comprising two domains resembling a “thumb” and an “index finger,” which enables their interaction with cellular receptors. Despite the identification of 19 WNT proteins in humans, three-dimensional structural data are available for only a subset, highlighting the importance of studying WNT proteins using in silico approaches. This study presents the in silico structural analysis of three human WNT family members — WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A — across multiple levels of protein organization. The primary structure of these proteins was analyzed for amino acid composition, and secondary structure predictions for α-helices, β-strands, and loops were correlated with domain structures characteristic of WNT proteins. Tertiary structures were modeled using homology modeling and deep-learning algorithms, and structural properties were investigated through molecular dynamics simulations. The ability of WNT proteins to adopt several closed and open conformational states was demonstrated, as well as the role of the flexibility of the β-hairpin forming the “index finger” in mediating conformational transitions. Thus, by applying a comprehensive bioinformatic approach to WNT proteins, the study highlights the advanced capabilities of in silico methods for analyzing protein structures at multiple levels of organization.Keywords: WNT proteins, Protein structure prediction, Molecular dynamics simulation, Protein conformation, Computational biology

Introduction

WNT proteins comprise a family of secreted, lipid-modified glycoproteins involved in regulating morphogenesis, oncogenesis, cell migration, proliferation, specialization, and tissue regeneration (Aros et al., 2021; Maeda et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2023). These single-chain molecules have a molecular mass of approximately 40 kDa and range from 350 to 420 amino acid residues in length (Mehta et al., 2021). As proteins secreted into the extracellular space, they primarily perform paracrine roles in tissue physiology regulation, categorizing them traditionally as short-range signaling molecules (Sedgwick and D'Souza-Schorey, 2016; Nam et al., 2014). WNT proteins activate canonical β-catenin signaling pathway by binding to cell surface receptors such as Frizzled (FZD) and coreceptors like Low-density lipoprotein-related receptors 5 and 6 (LRP5/6). In addition to the canonical β-catenin pathway, WNT proteins can act through β-catenin-independent (non-canonical) pathways, stimulating target cells via transcription factors such as AP-1 or NFAT (Wolf and Boutros, 2023).

The WNT family includes 19 proteins in humans (Yu et al., 2023). This study focuses on WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A based on their key roles in both canonical and non-canonical WNT signaling pathways. These pathways regulate a wide range of physiological processes in the musculoskeletal, nervous, cardiovascular, and immune systems (Liu et al., 2022). WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A function through both synergistic and antagonistic interactions, influencing biological regulation from the molecular to the tissue level. Moreover, these three WNT family members are among the most frequently studied WNTs in scientific literature indexed in NCBI PubMed, underscoring their significance in current research.

WNT1 is essential for embryonic brain and central nervous system development, as well as for osteoblast function, bone development, and cell homeostasis (Joeng et al., 2017; Navarro-Garberi et al., 2016; Peng et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019). WNT3A is involved in the proliferation and differentiation of neural and hematopoietic stem cells and supports processes such as trophoblast migration and invasion, and the proliferation and motility of various cell lines, including HEK293 and HC11 cells (He et al., 2015). WNT3A is also a major regulator of osteogenesis, influencing osteoblast formation and differentiation (Lin et al., 2024). Additionally, both WNT1 and WNT3A are involved in the development of melanocytes from neural crest cells, possibly acting at different differentiation stages, thereby demonstrating functional selectivity within a common cellular lineage (Dunn et al., 2005).

WNT5A predominantly activates β-catenin-independent signaling but can also engage β-catenin pathways, thereby modulating cell polarity, migration, proliferation, survival, and immune responses (Kumawat and Gosens, 2016). WNT3A and WNT5A are considered the most extensively studied WNT proteins regulating the canonical and non-canonical signaling, respectively (Sahu et al., 2015). In the immune system, WNT3A exhibits anti-inflammatory activity, whereas WNT5A regulates pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (Schaale et al., 2011; Nie et al., 2021). In the nervous system, WNT3A induces WNT5A expression in dorsal root ganglion neurons, contributing to peripheral sensitization and the transmission of nociceptive signals to the spinal cord (Simonetti and Kuner, 2020). WNT3A also provides neuroprotection by preventing Aβ-induced neuronal death (Arrázola et al., 2015), while WNT5A promotes mitochondrial fission in the postsynaptic region (Arrázola et al., 2015).

In the cardiovascular system, these WNT proteins are implicated in disease pathology. Elevated WNT1 levels have been observed after ischemic cardiac injury, whereas WNT5A is significantly upregulated in individuals with atherosclerotic lesions, particularly in the macrophage-rich regions (Foulquier et al., 2018).

Understanding the functions of WNT proteins closely correlates with knowledge of their tertiary structures. Historically, determining these structures has been a complex and lengthy process, as it took decades to isolate a pure and active protein, crystallize it, and obtain a high-resolution structure (Willert and Nusse, 2012). The first WNT structure resolved was that of Xenopus laevis WNT8, which unveiled the two-domain structure unique to WNT proteins (Janda et al., 2012). In WNT proteins, the amino-terminal domain (NTD) and carboxy-terminal domain (CTD) form a “thumb” and “index finger” structures, respectively, which mediate interaction with the FZD receptor (Willert and Nusse, 2012; Janda et al., 2012). These domains are connected by linker regions and interface motifs (Hirai et al., 2019; Chu et al., 2013). The NTD includes a β-hairpin structure and a W-loop, forming the “thumb” (Wolf and Boutros, 2023; Willert and Nusse, 2012; Zhong et al., 2021; Li et al., 2020). The CTD features another β-hairpin that serves as an “index finger”. In structural studies, the NTD β-hairpin and the CTD “index finger” are designated hairpin-1 and hairpin-2, respectively (Zhong et al., 2021), while the W-loop is sometimes classified as an additional β-hairpin and is occasionally referred to as “finger-3” (Nygaard et al., 2021; Hillier et al., 2024). This structural organization has been also confirmed for WNT3 (Hirai et al., 2019), WNT3A (Zhong et al., 2021; Qi et al., 2023), WNT7A (Qi et al., 2023), and WNT8A (Nygaard et al., 2021). However, for most WNT proteins, including WNT1 and WNT5A, the structure remains undetermined. Given the complexity and limitations of instrumental methods for structural elucidation, in silico prediction of secondary and tertiary structures remains a promising research direction.

Protein structure prediction can be achieved through homology modeling, threading methods, and various ab initio protein modeling algorithms (Bongirwar and Mokhade, 2022). Notably, recent advances in deep-learning algorithms significantly improve prediction accuracy and now allow for the prediction of protein structures even when no structural data exists for homologous proteins or proteins with similar folding patterns (Bertoline et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2023).

This study conducts a comprehensive analysis of WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A across multiple levels of structural organization, including the primary, secondary, and tertiary structures. The analysis focuses on examining structural motifs and topology, predicting three-dimensional structures, and evaluating structural changes during molecular dynamics. Throughout the study, bioinformatic methods employing different algorithms are applied simultaneously, allowing for comparison of results across alternative approaches and enhancing confidence in structural predictions for WNT family proteins.

Materials and methods

Genomic and proteomic databases, including NCBI (Sayers et al., 2022), UniProt (UniProt Consortium, 2023), and Ensembl (Harrison et al., 2024), were analyzed. The accession numbers for each database are as follows: for human WNT1, NP_005421.1 in NCBI, P04628 in UniProt, and ENST00000293549.4 in Ensembl; for human WNT3A, NP_149122.1 in NCBI, P56704 in UniProt, and ENST00000284523.2 in Ensembl; for human WNT5A, NP_003383.4 in NCBI, P41221 in UniProt, and ENST00000264634.9 in Ensembl. UniProt reference sequences for WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A were used for further structural analyses of these proteins at the primary, secondary, and tertiary levels.

During primary sequence analysis of the WNT family proteins, Taylor's classification was employed to categorize amino acids based on their physicochemical and structural properties (Taylor, 1986). As the primary structure alone does not reveal whether a specific cysteine in the protein chain participates in disulfide bond (CS-S) formation, all cysteines were assumed to be in the reduced form (CSH). Amino acids were grouped accordingly, and the content of each group within the proteins was calculated. Based on complete amino acid sequences of WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A, the aliphatic index (Ikai, 1980), grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) (Kyte and Doolittle, 1982), and instability index (Guruprasad et al., 1990) were determined using the ProtParam interface (Gasteiger et al., 2003). Pairwise alignments of the amino acid sequences of WNT proteins were performed using the Needleman–Wunsch algorithm (Needleman and Wunsch, 1970) with the BLOSUM62 substitution matrix (Henikoff and Henikoff, 1992), implemented in the EMBOSS Needle tool (Madeira et al., 2024).

To predict the secondary structures of WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A, multiple online services were employed to identify α-helical, β-strand, and coiled elements based on the primary amino acid sequences of these proteins. Specifically, SOPMA (Geourjon and Deléage, 1995; Combet et al., 2000), PSIPRED (Jones, 1999; Buchan et al., 2024), and PSSpred (Yan et al., 2013) were used for structure prediction, and the results were subsequently compared to ensure accuracy and consistency across platforms. Among the proteins studied, only WNT3A has known three-dimensional structure coordinates, available in the RCSB PDB (Burley et al., 2023), with a corresponding entry in PDBsum (Laskowski et al., 2018). The WNT3A model in the RCSB PDB and PDBsum has the identification number 7DRT (Zhong et al., 2021). Additional secondary structure predictions for WNT3A were derived using data of tools available through PDBsum, specifically the HERA topology prediction tool (Hutchinson and Thornton, 1990) and the PROMOTIF tool for structural motif identification (Hutchinson and Thornton, 1996). These tools allow an estimation of WNT3A's secondary structure elements based on the coordinates of its experimentally determined tertiary structure.

Homology modeling and ab initio prediction methods were employed to model the three-dimensional structures of WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A proteins. SWISS-MODEL (Waterhouse et al., 2018) was used for homology modeling, with template selection based on Global Model Quality Estimate (GMQE) rankings (Benkert et al., 2011). Additionally, modeling services utilizing deep-learning algorithms and capable of ab initio protein modeling were explored, including AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al., 2024; Jumper et al., 2021), D-I-TASSER (Zheng et al., 2021, 2026; Zhou et al., 2022a), RoseTTAFold via the Robetta server (Baek et al., 2021), and DMPfold via the PSIPRED server (Buchan et al., 2024; Greener et al., 2019).

The geometric correctness of the resulting tertiary models was assessed using several scoring approaches tools: MolProbity (Williams et al., 2018), ModFOLD9 (McGuffin and Alharbi, 2024), ERRAT (Colovos and Yeates, 1993), VoroMQA (Olechnovič and Venclovas, 2019), ProQ3D (Uziela et al., 2017), and QMEANDisCo (Studer et al., 2020). The scores from ModFold9, VoroMQA, ProQ3D, and QMEANDisCo naturally range from 0 to 1, with a value of 1 indicating the most accurate model. The scores from MolProbity and ERRAT were normalized to the same range, after which a mean normalized score was calculated using the scores from all tools. The most accurate model for each WNT protein was selected based on the highest mean normalized score. The open-source PyMOL (https://www.pymol.org) was used to visualize the three-dimensional models and perform structural alignment. To further compare the models generated using different tools, pairwise calculations of the Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), TM-score, Global Distance Test - Total Score (GDT_TS), and Local Distance Difference Test (lDDT) were performed (Waterhouse et al., 2024; Zhang and Skolnick, 2004).

Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using the GROMACS 2023.3 software package (Abraham et al., 2015) with the CHARMM36m force field (Huang et al., 2017). The proteins were simulated in the apo state without additional modifications as modeled; however, prior to simulations, N-terminal signal peptide regions were removed to represent the mature forms of WNT proteins in accordance with UniProt annotations, with the aim of focusing on intrinsic conformational dynamics of the protein core. Disulfide bonds, protonation states, and N- and C-termini were treated using the default pdb2gmx topology generation workflow. Then proteins were placed in dodecahedron boxes with 1.8 nm distances from box edge for WNT1, and 2.4 nm for WNT3A and WNT5A. Then boxes were filled with water and NaCl to achieve a 150 mM salt concentration. The systems underwent energy minimization followed by equilibration at 310 K and 1 bar in three stages: 1 ns of NVT equilibration using a V-rescale thermostat with a 0.1 ps time constant for temperature coupling; 1 ns of NPT equilibration with a V-rescale thermostat and a C-rescale barostat, employing time constants of 0.1 ps and 2.0 ps for temperature and pressure coupling, respectively; and 50 ns of NPT equilibration using the same thermostat and barostat, with time constants of 1.0 ps and 5.0 ps, respectively. Production runs of 500 ns were then performed, maintaining the thermostat and barostat parameters from the final equilibration phase. Other molecular dynamics parameters were chosen according to the GROMACS documentation and recommendations for the selected force field. Simulations for each WNT protein were repeated three times. All trajectories were verified to ensure that the protein did not interact with its periodic images, in accordance with the minimum image convention, by applying a cutoff of 1.2 nm for non-bonded interactions. Post-molecular dynamics analyses included calculations of root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) (Bondi, 1964; Eisenhaber et al., 1995). Trajectories from replicate simulations of each protein were concatenated, followed by principal component analysis (PCA) and free energy landscape (FEL) analysis to evaluate structural variability and the capacity of WNT proteins to adopt diverse conformational states. The presence and localization of secondary structure elements in the in silico models of WNT proteins were compared before and after molecular dynamics simulation. This analysis was conducted using the STRIDE tool, which assigns secondary structures based on atomic coordinates (Heinig and Frishman, 2004).

Open-source PyMOL (https://www.pymol.org) was used for molecular structure visualization. The graphical abstract was prepared using structure visualizations generated by the authors in PyMOL; additional graphical elements were created using icons obtained from SVG Repo (https://www.svgrepo.com) and Bioicons (https://bioicons.com), with the α-helix icon originating from DBCLS resources via Bioicons.

Results

Primary structure analysis

The human WNT1 protein is encoded by the gene located at positions 48,978,322–48,982,620 on chromosome 12 (human genome assembly GRCh38.p14). The sequence encoding WNT1 is contained within four exons, resulting in a 2422 bp transcript that translates into a protein of 370 amino acid residues. The human WNT3A protein, encoded by a gene at positions 228,006,998–228,061,271 on chromosome 1, also has a coding sequence divided among four exons, resulting in a 2996 bp transcript that encodes a 352-residue protein. The gene encoding human WNT5A is located on chromosome 1 (positions 55,465,715–55,490,539) and contains five exons, resulting in a 5841 bp transcript and a protein with 380 amino acid residues.

For WNT1, only a single isoform is known. However, WNT3A, according to UniProt, has two isoforms: the main isoform (P56704-1) and a second isoform (P56704-2) with a lengthened sequence of 385 residues, including an additional 33 residues at the C-terminus. WNT5A, on the other hand, is presented by several isoforms in UniProt. The main isoform (P41221-1) has 15 additional N-terminal residues compared to the second isoform (P41221-2). A third WNT5A isoform, identified separately in UniProt with C9J8I8 identification number, lacks the same 15 N-terminal residues as well as a substantial C-terminal fragment of 151 residues.

This study offers an analysis of the primary isoforms of human WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A proteins. Pairwise alignment of these amino acid sequences (see Supplementary File 1) revealed that the sequence identity between WNT1 and WNT3A is 42.4%, between WNT5A and WNT3A is 41.2%, and between WNT1 and WNT5A is 37.6%. Then, to evaluate the amino acid composition of these proteins, the fractions of amino acid residues with distinct physicochemical properties were calculated, with the summarized data provided in Table 1.

The data in Table 1 reveal a high proportion of hydrophobic amino acids — over 60% in each of the WNT family proteins studied. The GRAVY indices, derived from the amino acid sequences, are −0.347 for WNT1, −0.378 for WNT3A, and −0.290 for WNT5A. Given these negative GRAVY values, each protein exhibits overall hydrophilic properties. Each proteinogenic amino acid has a unique hydropathicity value, leading to varying contributions to the overall GRAVY index. This variation partly accounts for why these proteins can be regarded as hydrophilic, despite having a larger proportion of hydrophobic amino acids in their total composition. Furthermore, according to Taylor's classification, certain amphiphilic amino acids are categorized as hydrophobic.

Over a quarter of the amino acids in WNT proteins are charged, including histidine, lysine, and arginine (positively charged), as well as aspartic and glutamic acids (negatively charged). Together with polar but uncharged amino acids, the polar amino acids comprise over 50% of each WNT protein. This is particularly important, as polar amino acids enable protein hydrogen bonding capabilities.

Aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, and histidine) represent 10.81%, 13.35%, and 11.58% of WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A, respectively, while aliphatic amino acids contribute 17.84%, 15.63%, and 17.11% to each respective protein according to Taylor's classification. The aliphatic indices for WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A are 71.46, 62.93, and 68.03, respectively (the aliphatic index also includes the contribution of alanine). The instability indices for WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A are 62.61, 50.65, and 39.15, respectively. Despite being generally non-reactive, aliphatic amino acids are likely important for maintaining protein thermostability. Given its high aliphatic index and low instability index, WNT5A is likely to exhibit the greatest stability among the proteins studied.

Secondary structure analysis

The secondary structure analysis employed a combination of bioinformatic tools to enhance the accuracy of predictions regarding secondary structure elements, such as α-helices and β-strands, in the studied WNT proteins. The results generated by the different prediction tools were aligned and are presented in Fig. 1. More detailed data, including the individual outputs from each secondary structure prediction tool, data on smaller structural elements (e.g., β-turns reported by PSIPRED), and prediction confidence scores, is provided in Supplementary File 2.

Secondary structures of WNT3A (a), WNT1 (b), and WNT5A (c)

SOPMA, PSIPRED and PSSpred tools were used for prediction of secondary structures of all studied proteins, while ProMotif and HERA predictions were available only for WNT3A.

The analysis begins with the WNT3A protein, as an experimentally derived model of this protein (PDB ID: 7DRT), obtained through electron microscopy, is available in the RCSB PDB and is documented in PDBsum. Data on the topology, estimated using the HERA tool, and the secondary structure elements determined through PROMOTIF were available through PDBsum and are presented in Fig. 1a and Table 2. Both the HERA and PROMOTIF estimates rely on WNT3A coordinate data from the 7DRT model. The positions of the α-helices and β-strands, as estimated by these two tools, are very close (differing by a few amino acid residues). Both HERA topology and PROMOTIF indicate the presence of 11 α-helices and 7 β-strands in WNT3A. This study will reference the HERA estimate for WNT3A when comparing it with data obtained from other bioinformatic tools. Fig. 2 provides a visualization of the WNT3A structure from the 7DRT model, illustrating the domain structure (NTD, CTD, and linker region) and characteristic structural motifs (e.g., β-hairpins, W-loop), allowing for comparison of their localization with identified key elements of the secondary structure (such as α-helices, β-strands, and coils).

The secondary structure of WNT3A was further assessed using tools (SOPMA, PSIPRED, and PSSpred) that predict structure based solely on the primary protein sequence and do not require experimental model coordinates. The results from these tools are also presented in Fig. 1a, Table 2, and in Supplementary File 2. A comparative analysis reveals that these sequence-based predictions generally reproduce the HERA topology derived from experimental coordinates. SOPMA, PSIPRED, and PSSpred are largely able to identify, with minor differences (typically several amino acids), most of the α-helices in the NTD, particularly those spanning residues I22–V28, V49–Y59, I62–F81, G99–K104, T106–E130, E160–A170, D171–R173, and D178–H198. However, these sequence-based tools fail to recognize the α-helix in the G29–Y32 region within the NTD (except SOPMA) and the sole α-helix in the CTD located at G306–C311.

Regarding other structural motifs in WNT3A, all tools identified the W-loop in the G131–I159 region, while the β-hairpin in the “index finger” (composed of β-strands R319–C327 and Q340–V348) was recognized only by PSIPRED and PSSpred. In contrast, the β-hairpin in the “thumb,” which includes β-strands H200–H206 and V214–S220, went undetected by the sequence-based tools; either both β-strands were not simultaneously identified, or one strand was incorrectly classified as a helical element. Additionally, these tools showed limited accuracy in detecting the β-hairpin in the linker region; the first β-strand at V253–R259 was not identified, though the second strand at L271–Y273 was.

The SOPMA tool's calculation of the relative content of different secondary structure elements in WNT3A reveals that α-helices comprise 41.19% of the protein, β (extended) strands and β-turns account for 10.23% and 2.56%, respectively, and random coils constitute the remaining 46.02% (Supplementary File 2).

Unlike WNT3A, there are no experimental models available for the WNT1 and WNT5A proteins in the RCSB PDB. Consequently, no data on topology or secondary structure elements are available from the HERA and PROMOTIF tools for these proteins. Thus, their secondary structure was predicted based solely on the amino acid sequences using SOPMA, PSIPRED, and PSSpred. The alignment of the primary sequences of WNT1 and WNT5A with WNT3A (Supplementary File 1) provides a basis for inferring the likely localization of characteristic secondary motifs, such as the W-loop and β-hairpins.

For WNT1, the secondary structure predictions from SOPMA, PSIPRED, and PSSpred (Fig. 1b, Table 3, Supplementary File 2) are generally consistent in identifying the localization of α-helices. Additionally, all tools recognize the G146–C170 region as primarily composed of random coils, consistent with the expected location of the W-loop. Based on the sequence alignment of WNT1 with WNT3A, β-strands are anticipated in the R215–H221 and V229–R235 regions, which would form a β-hairpin in the “thumb.” However, none of the tools detect these specific β-strands.

In the C-terminal region, each tool predicts the presence of β-strands, though there is some variation in their number and length. PSIPRED's prediction appears most plausible, as it identifies an even number of β-strands suitable for forming a β-hairpin in the “index finger”: T336–R344, T348–W351, H354–S356, and N359–E368.

According to the SOPMA analysis, the composition of secondary structure elements in WNT1 includes 37.30% α-helices, 12.43% β-strands, 1.89% β-turns, and the remaining 48.38% as random coils (Supplementary File 2).

For WNT5A, the secondary structure predictions from SOPMA, PSIPRED, and PSSpred are also generally consistent regarding the localization of α-helices (Fig. 1c, Table 4, Supplementary File 2). All tools identify the G157–C182 region as a random coil, consistent with the expected location of the W-loop. Based on the sequence alignment of WNT5A with WNT3A, β-strands are expected in the D235–H241 and V249–Q255 regions, forming a β-hairpin in the “thumb.” However, only PSIPRED and PSSpred recognize one of these β-strands, locating it at K239–C240 and V236–C240, respectively; the second β-strand goes undetected by all tools.

Tertiary structure analysis

During the modeling process, three-dimensional structures of the WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A proteins were obtained. The structures of each protein were modeled using five services, incorporating both deep-learning algorithms (AlphaFold3, RoseTTAFold, D-I-TASSER, DMPfold) and homology modeling (SWISS-MODEL). For SWISS-MODEL, the WNT3A protein chain from the 7DRT model in RCSB PDB was selected as the template for all proteins, as this template had the highest GMQE values. AlphaFold3, RoseTTAFold, and D-I-TASSER generated sets of five models for each protein, while DMPfold and SWISS-MODEL produced one model each. Consequently, a total of 17 models were generated for each protein.

To identify the models closest to the natural three-dimensional structure, geometric assessments of the models were conducted using six tools: MolProbity, ModFOLD9, ERRAT, VoroMQA, ProQ3D, and QMEANDisCo. Since sets of models were obtained for each WNT protein using AlphaFold3, RoseTTAFold, and D-I-TASSER, the best model within each set was initially selected based on geometric evaluations (Supplementary File 3). Subsequently, the geometry accuracy of WNT models produced by different approaches was compared (Table 5).

The analysis indicates that for the WNT1 protein, the model generated by AlphaFold3 demonstrated the highest accuracy, based on the highest mean normalized score, followed by the model generated by RoseTTAFold, which also achieved a comparably high mean normalized score (Table 5). These were followed by the SWISS-MODEL homology-based structure, and then by the models produced by D-I-TASSER and DMPfold. A similar ranking pattern was observed for the WNT3A and WNT5A models, as the AlphaFold3 and RoseTTAFold models also emerged as the most accurate (Table 5). Similar patterns were observed in the analysis of local scores and errors, with the AlphaFold3 and RoseTTAFold models exhibiting fewer regions of high inaccuracy along the amino acid chains compared to other models (Supplementary File 4).

When comparing score distributions across the evaluation tools, MolProbity and ERRAT identified the RoseTTAFold models for each WNT protein as having the most accurate structures. Meanwhile, AlphaFold3 models were ranked highest by ModFOLD9, VoroMQA, and ProQ3D. Based on QMEANDisCo, the highest scores were assigned to the SWISS-MODEL structures for WNT1 and WNT5A, and to the AlphaFold3 model for WNT3A.

In analyzing the models obtained using SWISS-MODEL — the only homology-based modeling service in this study — it is noteworthy that the WNT3A protein model, whose amino acid sequence is identical to the 7DRT template, outperformed the WNT1 and WNT5A models in each of used geometry estimation tools. The partially amino acid sequences of WNT1 and WNT5A (without N-terminal region) share 44.84% and 44.35% identity with the template, respectively. This trend suggests that model accuracy decreases as the similarity between the target sequence and template declines. It is also important to note that SWISS-MODEL generated only partial protein chains, excluding regions corresponding to the N-terminal sequences due to their absence in the template.

Molecular dynamics

Based on geometry evaluations of the models generated across various services, AlphaFold3 structures were selected for molecular dynamics simulations. To represent the molecular dynamics of proteins without signal sequences, the WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A structures were shortened by removing their N-terminal signal regions in accordance with UniProt data on signal region lengths in WNT proteins.

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed in triplicate for each studied protein to capture the diversity of dynamic behaviors characteristic of WNT protein structures. Comparative analysis of trajectories from repeated simulations revealed that WNT proteins can adopt multiple metastable conformational states. These include a reference open state with a U-shaped conformation, consistent with known experimental structures (Janda et al., 2012; Hirai et al., 2019; Zhong et al., 2021), as well as an extended open state in which the C-terminal “index finger” is displaced from the NTD. In addition, several closed conformations were identified. The first closed state, that we term the “bent finger” conformation, is characterized by the approximation of the β-hairpin structure of the CTD “index finger” toward the β-hairpin of the NTD “thumb”. The second, referred to as the “core-associated” conformation, involves folding of the CTD onto the central α-helical core of the protein.

Fig. 4 depicts the molecular states at the beginning, at several of the points in the middle of molecular dynamics (in particular, the energetically most stable state is shown), and after 500 ns of molecular dynamics. Fig. 5 presents secondary structure element localization within WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A, as identified by the STRIDE tool at the beginning and end of the simulations. Because STRIDE uses tertiary structure coordinate data for evaluations, it was able to detect α-helices in the NTD of each WNT protein, as well as the W-loop, β-hairpin of the “thumb” in the NTD, linker region, and β-hairpin of the “index finger” in the CTD. Although individual structural motifs showed relative positional shifts due to molecular dynamics (Fig. 4), the general localization and length of major secondary structure elements remained largely stable, changing only minimally by a few amino acid residues (Fig. 5).

For WNT1, the three replicate simulations demonstrated convergence after approximately 240 ns, as evidenced by consistent RMSD behavior across trajectories (Fig. 6a). Across different intermediate conformations, all simulations ultimately favored the closed “bent finger” state (Fig. 4a–c), which appeared to represent the most energetically favorable conformation and dominated a substantial portion of the simulation time. This compact state was further associated with a reduction in the Rg (Fig. 7a), supporting its structurally condensed nature.

For WNT3A, the three independent simulations revealed the existence of multiple metastable conformational states. In simulation #1, the protein adopted the “bent finger” closed conformation by approximately 200 ns (Fig. 4d), which it retained for the remainder of the simulation. This state was associated with moderate RMSD values (Fig. 6c) and a reduced Rg relative to the reference structure (Fig. 7c), indicating a compact and stable configuration. In contrast, simulation #2 led to the formation of a distinct compact conformation, referred to as the “core-associated” closed state (Fig. 4e). This conformation was marked by substantial deviation from the reference structure, as evidenced by elevated RMSD values (Fig. 6c), while also displaying low Rg values (Fig. 7c), consistent with its highly condensed nature. Simulation #3 illustrated a different trajectory in which the protein initially remained in a U-shaped conformation similar to the reference structure, characterized by moderate RMSD and Rg values. After approximately 340 ns, the structure transitioned into an extended open conformation (Fig. 4f), which was maintained until the end of the simulation. This state was characterized by increased RMSD and Rg values, reflecting its expanded architecture. Together, these results underscore the conformational plasticity of WNT3A and demonstrate that different metastable states can be preferentially adopted depending on the simulation trajectory.

For WNT5A, simulations #1 and #2 exhibited a tendency toward adopting the “bent finger” closed conformation (Fig. 4g and h). However, the time required to reach this compact state, as indicated by reductions in RMSD and Rg fluctuations, was notably longer in simulation #1 compared to simulation #2 (Figs. 6e and 7e). In contrast, simulation #3 was dominated by the extended open state (Fig. 4i), which was reflected in the highest RMSD and Rg values among all simulations, as well as in the large amplitude of fluctuations in both parameters. These observations highlight the structural variability of WNT5A across simulation trajectories.

RMSF plots reveal that the CTD regions, corresponding to β-hairpins in the “index fingers” of the WNT proteins, exhibit the highest flexibility (Fig. 6b, d, f). This movement in the “index fingers” contributes significantly to RMSD fluctuations during the simulations, and forming of different conformational states. Compared to the CTDs, other regions of the proteins exhibit lower flexibility; however, fluctuations in the W-loops, β-hairpins of the “thumb” in NTDs, and linker regions contribute smaller peaks in the RMSF plots.

Analysis of the Rg throughout the molecular dynamics simulations of WNT proteins indicated similar average Rg values for WNT1 across the three replicates (Fig. 7a, c, e): 2.29 ± 0.09 nm, 2.28 ± 0.05 nm, and 2.36 ± 0.11 nm. These results suggest a consistent level of overall compactness of WNT1. In the case of WNT3A, the average Rg values showed greater variability, measured at 2.36 ± 0.12 nm, 2.44 ± 0.13 nm, and 2.56 ± 0.13 nm, which can be attributed to the predominance of different conformational states in each simulation. The most pronounced differences were observed for WNT5A, where the average Rg values across the three simulations were 2.43 ± 0.12 nm, 2.26 ± 0.07 nm, and 2.61 ± 0.11 nm. This variability also reflects the presence of distinct metastable states with varying degrees of structural compactness.

Changes in SASA for WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A are shown in Fig. 7b, d, and f, respectively. The average SASA values over the course of the simulations were 188.86 ± 6.15 nm2, 190.71 ± 4.11 nm2, and 194.59 ± 4.38 nm2 for the three replicates of WNT1; 193.54 ± 3.33 nm2, 194.76 ± 5.36 nm2, and 200.20 ± 4.12 nm2 for WNT3A; and 199.24 ± 5.86 nm2, 191.14 ± 4.96 nm2, and 201.28 ± 4.75 nm2 for WNT5A. Since both the “index finger” and “thumb” regions of the WNT protein structure remain exposed to solvent across different conformational states, the SASA profiles for the replicate simulations show a high degree of overlap and do not exhibit abrupt shifts associated with major conformational transitions.

Fig. 8b–d displays the corresponding projections for each replicate individually. The first and second principal components account for 50.12% and 16.26% of the total positional fluctuations, respectively. Fig. 8e–g presents the FEL results for each of the WNT1 simulations.

In simulation #2, the minimum corresponds to the “core-associated” closed conformation, also shown in blue in Fig. 4e. The FEL analysis of simulation #3 reveals two broad energy minima. This energy minima corresponds to the extended open state, which WNT5A occupies for the majority of simulation #3, accompanied by some structural variability (Fig. 4i). Discussion

The functional characterization of WNT proteins depends heavily on knowledge of their structures, yet experimental data remain available for only a small subset of the 19 human WNT proteins. This reflects a broader disparity between the rapidly expanding number of known protein sequences and the comparatively limited number of experimentally resolved structures (Bertoline et al., 2023). Structural determination by X-ray crystallography, NMR, cryo-EM, and related techniques remains technically demanding and is particularly challenging for proteins containing flexible or disordered regions (Bertoline et al., 2023; Nguyen and Gonen, 2020; Liebschner et al., 2019; Lieutaud et al., 2016; Evans et al., 2023; Konrat, 2014; Maiti et al., 2024; Metskas and Rhoades, 2020; Schiavina et al., 2024; Djinovic-Carugo and Carugo, 2015). Although spectroscopic approaches can provide valuable information on secondary-structure composition, they do not yield atomic-resolution structural models (Sun et al., 2025; Beć et al., 2022; Izutsu et al., 2006; Chen et al., 2022; Rasheed et al., 2020; De Meutter and Goormaghtigh, 2021; Gao et al., 2025). Recent hybrid approaches that combine cryo-EM density maps with AI-based structural modeling, including ModelAngelo, DEMO-EM, EMBuild, EModelX, and related methods, have partially addressed these limitations, improving reconstruction of incomplete regions and analysis of structurally heterogeneous macromolecules (Zhou et al., 2019, 2022b, 2022c, 2026; Malhotra et al., 2021; He et al., 2022, 2023; Cui et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2022, 2024, 2025; Jamali et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Nevertheless, fully computational prediction methods remain indispensable for investigating proteins, for which experimental structural information is scarce, and are increasingly used both for structural characterization and protein engineering applications (Kuhlman and Bradley, 2019).

Earlier, a more general bioinformatic analysis of the WNT family as a whole was performed (Midlovets et al., 2025), whereas the present work focuses on a detailed comparative analysis of WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A across primary, secondary, and tertiary levels of organization. WNT family proteins play critical roles in embryonic development, contribute to the maintenance of physiological processes in the postnatal period, and are implicated in the pathogenesis of various diseases (Alrefaei et al., 2020; Rashid et al., 2018; Thompson and Williams, 2018). Structurally, WNT proteins exhibit a characteristic architecture consisting of two domains resembling a “thumb” and an “index finger” (Willert and Nusse, 2012). While the WNT family includes 19 proteins (Yu et al., 2023), this study focuses on WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A for specific reasons: they are involved into the regulation of wide range of biological processes and their functions are well-studied, while among the three, only WNT3A has structural coordinates available in the RCSB PDB (model 7DRT). Although no experimental structures exist for WNT1 or WNT5A, RCSB PDB entries for WNT8 and WNT8A exhibit relatively high sequence similarity to WNT5A, potentially facilitating WNT5A in silico modeling compared to WNT1. Therefore, this study includes three WNT proteins with varying degrees of data availability on secondary and tertiary structures.

Primary sequence analysis of WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A indicates that these proteins have comparable lengths and similar distributions of amino acid residue types (hydrophobic, polar, aromatic, aliphatic, charged, etc.). This similarity in composition suggests, at a preliminary level, that the proteins may share some physicochemical properties; however, it does not allow for definitive conclusions regarding structure or function. Structural modeling using this primary amino acid sequences with DMPfold, for instance, yielded models with low resemblance to established WNT protein structures. This discrepancy highlights the limitations of primary sequence data alone in predicting higher-order protein organization and underscores the challenges of ab initio three-dimensional structure modeling methods.

The analysis of secondary structure elements in WNT proteins was performed using a combination of structure-based and sequence-based bioinformatic tools. Structure-based approaches, including HERA, ProMotif, and STRIDE, rely on three-dimensional coordinates and are therefore restricted to proteins with experimentally determined structures or high-quality in silico models. In this study, HERA and ProMotif were applied only to WNT3A, for which experimental coordinates were available, while STRIDE was additionally used for predicted models. In parallel, sequence-based predictors (SOPMA, PSIPRED, and PSSpred) were used for all three proteins, as they infer secondary structure directly from primary amino acid sequences. Comparison using WNT3A showed that sequence-based predictions were largely consistent with structure-derived annotations, suggesting that such approaches can provide reliable secondary-structure estimates for WNT1 and WNT5A in the absence of experimental coordinates.

The SOPMA, PSIPRED, and PSSpred tools successfully identified α-helices and W-loops in the NTDs of WNT proteins and β-strands in the CTD region corresponding to the “index finger”, although they were not always accurate enough to confirm β-hairpin formation. However, these tools typically failed to detect the β-hairpin corresponding to the “thumb” in the NTD. In contrast, the STRIDE tool accurately identified all secondary structure elements characteristic of WNT proteins, but its requirement for tertiary structure coordinates limits its applicability. Overall, using multiple predictors improves robustness when discrepancies arise.

SOPMA, PSIPRED, and PSSpred are based on evolutionary information derived from multiple sequence alignments and position-specific scoring matrices. Although newer deep-learning and protein language model-based approaches have recently become available (Lin et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025; Leclercq and Droit, 2026; Ismi et al., 2022), alignment-based methods remain widely used and provide competitive accuracy (Weissenow and Rost, 2025). The agreement among these predictors, and their consistency with structure-based analyses of WNT3A, supports the reliability of the inferred secondary-structure organization. Nevertheless, future studies could incorporate modern protein language model-based predictors as an additional source of validation.

For tertiary structure modeling of WNT proteins, both homology-based and deep-learning algorithms were employed (Qin et al., 2024). Among the latter, AlphaFold3 and RoseTTAFold produced the most accurate full-size models. These findings align with CASP15 data indicating the accuracy of AlphaFold and RoseTTAFold models comparing to other bioinformatic tools (Simpkin et al., 2023). SWISS-MODEL, a well-established homology-based server, also generated high-quality models; its models for WNT3A surpassed or was close to those from AlphaFold3 in terms of MolProbity, ERRAT, and QMEANDisCo scores. However, these models were truncated because of the limited sequence length of the template used. Considering that WNT proteins have a multidomain organization, the application of methods specifically designed for multidomain structure assembly is also relevant. In particular, the D-I-TASSER approach used in the present study incorporates strategies from the DEMO framework, in which the relative orientation of domains is determined using analogous templates identified through domain-level structural alignment, combined with deep learning-predicted inter-residue restraints (Zhou et al., 2019, 2022b). The models generated by D-I-TASSER also performed well in structural quality assessments and were generally able to predict the structural organization of WNT proteins.

The modeling of N-terminal sequences in WNT proteins deserves special consideration. AlphaFold3 consistently modeled these N-terminal fragments as disordered regions, while RoseTTAFold and D-I-TASSER represented them as ordered structures with varying spatial positions relative to the main molecule in different models. Notably, there are no RCSB PDB structures for WNT proteins that include the N-terminal fragments. As these regions serve as signal sequences and are likely truncated (Willert and Nusse, 2012) in functional proteins, their impact on WNT functionality may be limited. Nevertheless, modeling these regions at the tertiary level remains valuable. In this study, due to the substantial modeling discrepancies both among different in silico tools and within individual tools on repeated modeling, only limited conclusions can be drawn regarding the tertiary structure of these regions.

The comparative analysis of homology-based and deep-learning methods for protein structure modeling suggests that while deep-learning methods are more advanced and capable of addressing complex structural challenges, homology modeling remains valuable when high-similarity templates are available for the target protein, particularly when full-length modeling is not critical to the research objectives. It should be noted that most current structure prediction methods are primarily designed to predict representative static conformations. However, protein function often depends on transitions between multiple structural states (Niazi, 2025). Recent developments in machine learning and integrative structural biology increasingly focus on modeling conformational ensembles and protein dynamics, particularly through the analysis of cryo-EM data and generative approaches for conformational sampling (Cui et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2023; Madani and Tarakanova, 2026). The conformational states identified in this study by molecular dynamics simulations therefore complement static structure predictions and provide additional insight into the dynamic behavior of WNT proteins.

The molecular dynamics simulations indicate that the studied WNT proteins can adopt multiple metastable conformational states with varying degrees of compactness. Although all simulations were initiated from an open “U-shaped” conformation similar to that observed in experimental structures (Janda et al., 2012; Hirai et al., 2019; Zhong et al., 2021), the proteins diverged toward distinct dynamic regimes. Under the simulation conditions, WNT1 transitioned to a closed conformation, referred to in this study as the “bent finger” state. WNT3A was capable of adopting both the “bent finger” closed state and an alternative “core-associated” closed state, as well as an extended open conformation. WNT5A exhibited transitions to either the “bent finger” closed state or the extended open conformation. Collectively, findings suggest that WNT family members differ in intrinsic conformational plasticity, likely reflecting variations in constraints imposed by their conserved two-domain architecture (Willert and Nusse, 2012).

The molecular dynamics trajectory analysis indicates that the β-hairpins, which constitute the “index fingers” within the CTDs of all three WNT proteins studied, were the most dynamic regions. These flexibility patterns can be attributed to structural characteristics: following the comparatively rigid NTD, there are loops within the linker region and CTD, enabling the “index finger” to move in relation to the NTD. This flexibility results in various conformations that range in compactness. In addition to the “index fingers,” other flexible regions were identified, including W-loops, β-hairpins of the “thumb” (especially short loops that connect two β-strands within the β-hairpin structure), and loops located within or adjacent to the linker region. These secondary structural elements contribute additional flexibility to the WNT proteins, which may be essential for their conformational adaptability and may underlie the ability of WNT proteins to engage in diverse molecular interactions (Wang et al., 2016; Das et al., 2012; Niehrs, 2012).

Beyond the current research, the molecular dynamics of WNTs have been explored in some previous studies. Mirza et al. (2017) studied the molecular dynamics of WNT3 and WNT3A proteins with different numbers of disulfide bonds for 20 ns and found the flexibility of the “thumb” and “index finger”, with the “index finger” also being the most flexible region. Fakhar et al. (2018) conducted 120 ns simulations of both apo-WNT1 and complexes of WNT1 with Klotho-derived peptides. Their study highlighted larger fluctuations in the binding pocket and peripheral regions of WNT1. However, the RMSF plots from this study did not demonstrate an increased flexibility of the CTD “index finger” in comparison to other regions of the protein. In contrast, a study by Mondal et al. (2022) observed the transition of WNT1 to a closed conformation after 20 ns of simulation, attributed to the CTD moving closer to the NTD. This is consistent with our findings, as we also observed similar behavior with the formation of a closed conformation of WNT1 in all three replicate simulations. Additionally, the RMSF plot in Mondal et al. (2022) also indicated greater flexibility in the CTD of ligand-free WNT1 during the first 20 ns. It is also noteworthy that several previous studies investigating other members of the WNT family, such as WNT4 (Azam and Mirza, 2014) and WNT2B (Midlovets et al., 2025), have demonstrated their capacity for structural fluctuations, with the “index finger” of the CTD contributing most significantly to these conformational changes. Similar to the study (Midlovets et al., 2025), the current research indicates that movement of the “index finger” governs conformational transitions to the extended open conformation. However, whereas for WNT2B the transition to an extended open conformation was observed only upon O-acylation of serine, WNT3A and WNT5A reached an extended open conformation even in the unmodified state in some molecular dynamics replicates, alongside closed “bent finger” and “core-associated” states.

Notably, this study represents the first molecular dynamics-based comparative analysis of WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A performed within a unified framework, and the first to explicitly characterize the dynamic behavior of WNT5A at the atomic level. This simultaneous analysis reveals distinct conformational regimes across the three proteins, providing new insight into intra-family variability in WNT structural dynamics. Specifically, WNT1 predominantly adopted a closed conformation that appeared to be energetically stable, whereas WNT3A and WNT5A exhibited broader conformational plasticity, sampling both open and closed states. These observations suggest distinct molecular dynamics patterns and the potential for diverse conformational transitions among WNT proteins. Such structural properties of WNTs may have implications for their biological functions, in particular, for interactions with FZD proteins, the primary WNT receptors (Wang et al., 2016), and with Wntless (Wolf and Boutros, 2023; Zhong et al., 2021; Das et al., 2012), as well as with other molecules. In total, WNTs couple with more than 15 receptors and co-receptors across seven protein families (Niehrs, 2012), making these interactions significantly important for numerous biological processes in humans and other animals. Although data from in vitro studies on conformational transitions of WNT proteins are very limited, this highlights the importance of the present in silico investigation. Several previous studies suggest that the ability of WNT proteins to adopt different conformational states may have functional implications. For instance, studies of WNT3 and WNT3A, which share high sequence similarity, revealed distinct orientations of hairpin-1, hairpin-2, and the W-loop when bound to different partner molecules, FZD and Wntless, respectively (Hirai et al., 2019; Zhong et al., 2021), with these elements reorienting by approximately 30°, 60°, and 90° between the two bound states (Hirai et al., 2019; Zhong et al., 2021). In this nomenclature, hairpin-1 is the lipid-bearing “thumb” of the NTD and hairpin-2 is the CTD “index finger” that dominates the conformational fluctuations in current simulations, so its marked reorientation is consistent with the central role of “index finger” mobility that are observed in this study. Notably, the “thumb” and, most prominently, the W-loop also reorient substantially (30° and 90°), indicating that the NTD loop and hairpin regions are likewise conformationally plastic – in line with the additional, lower-amplitude fluctuations detected in these elements during the molecular dynamics trajectories in the current study and pointing to motions that are functionally relevant during WNT-receptor engagement. Similarly, structural comparisons of human WNT8A and Xenopus Wnt8 in complexes with Wntless and FZD, respectively, showed differing hairpin orientations (Janda et al., 2012; Hillier et al., 2024). Together, these observations suggest that transfer of WNT proteins from Wntless to cell-surface FZD receptors is accompanied by induced-fit rearrangements of loop and hairpin structures (Zhong et al., 2021). Furthermore, members of the WNT family differ in their affinities for specific receptors. A certain degree of interaction selectivity among various WNT/FZD pairs has been demonstrated in vitro (Dijksterhuis et al., 2015). Additionally, several in silico studies have investigated the binding affinities of different WNT proteins for FZD receptors (Agostino et al., 2017; Agostino and Pohl, 2019) and for LRP5/6 (Sahu et al., 2015).

Thus, there is relationship between the structure of WNT family proteins and their capacity to fulfill biological functions within signaling pathways, although this connection remains insufficiently explored. The present study contributes new insights into the conformational flexibility of several WNT proteins, highlighting the role of the “index finger” in mediating structural fluctuations and conformational transitions. Further studies on the structures of other members of the WNT family at the secondary and tertiary levels remain important. Conducting molecular dynamics simulations, particularly in complexes with receptors and ligands, is relevant for investigating other structural features and the potential behavior of these important regulatory proteins. Moreover, complementary in vitro studies are necessary to confirm the ability of WNT proteins to undergo conformational transitions and to clarify the functional significance of these dynamics in molecular interactions and signaling processes.

Conclusions

This study provides a comparative bioinformatic characterization of WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5A across multiple levels of organization, including their primary, secondary, and tertiary structures. The presence of secondary structure elements in these proteins was correlated with the formation of a two-domain structure, consisting of “thumb” and “index finger”, characteristic of WNT proteins. The results of modeling the tertiary structures of WNT proteins demonstrated the effectiveness of both homology and deep-learning algorithms, with deep-learning methods showing an advantage in producing full-length models without explicit template dependence. Molecular dynamics simulations provided novel insights into potential conformational plasticity of WNT proteins, particularly within the β-hairpin corresponding to the “index finger”, suggesting its potential role in functional structural transitions.

Future research should extend structural and dynamical analyses to additional WNT family members and incorporate experimental validation to support computational predictions. In particular, analysis of WNT complexes with receptors may be necessary to clarify the functional relevance of the observed conformational states. These efforts will be essential for bridging static structural models with the dynamic mechanisms underlying WNT signaling.
