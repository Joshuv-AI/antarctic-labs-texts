Is MUC5B rs35705950 promoter polymorphism associated with chronic lung allograft dysfunction?
Authors: Adèle Sandot, Ibrahima Ba, Clément R. Massonnaud, Raphaël Borie, Benjamin Renaud-Picard, Benjamin Coiffard, Xavier Demant, Loïc Falque, Antoine Roux, Jérôme Le Pavec, Adrien Tissot, Claire Merveilleux, Thomas Villeneuve, Christiane Knoop, Vincent Bunel, Nicolas Carlier, David Lair, Pierre Mordant, Caroline Kannengiesser, Jonathan Messika, COLT consortium.
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) is the main cause of death following lung transplantation (LT). Its pathophysiology and risk factors remain poorly understood. Donor or recipient genetic characteristics could be involved. MUC5B promoter rs35705950 polymorphism is one of the major genetic factors associated with pulmonary fibrosis. We aimed to correlate the MUC5B genotype of recipients and donors with LT outcomes.MethodsRecipient and donor blood samples from the Cohort in Lung Transplantation biobank were used. MUC5B status was determined by quantitative PCR using probes for rs35705950 polymorphism. CLAD occurrence and phenotype were blindly adjudicated.Results210 recipient–donor pairs were analysed. At 5 years, 68 patients (32.4%) had CLAD. The MUC5B rs35705950 polymorphism, whether in the donor or the recipient, was not associated with CLAD at 5 years (OR 1.07 (95% CI 0.53–2.11), p=0.8, and OR 0.79 (95% CI 0.37–1.60), p=0.5, respectively), CLAD-free survival or CLAD phenotype. The prevalence of the recipients’ T allele differed among underlying respiratory diseases (20.0% in interstitial lung disease, 8.4% in emphysema, 13.4% in cystic fibrosis and 5.6% for others, p=0.03). Risk of antibody-mediated rejection (AMR) was independent of the donor MUC5B polymorphism genotype, whereas the presence of the T allele in the recipient was associated with a reduced occurrence of AMR (OR 0.26 (95% CI 0.08–0.69), p=0.015).ConclusionMUC5B rs35705950 polymorphism in the donor or the recipient does not affect LT outcomes. The significant association between the recipients’ polymorphism and reduced AMR occurrence has yet to be confirmed.

Introduction

The protein encoded by the MUC5B gene is a mucin expressed by the bronchial epithelium [1]. A common polymorphism, located in the gene promoter, is associated with increased expression of MUC5B protein in the epithelium and mucus [1, 2]. The “T allele” or “minor allele” of the MUC5B rs35705950 polymorphism has been reported to increase the risk of interstitial lung disease (ILD), particularly usual interstitial pneumonia, whether idiopathic (sporadic or familial idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)) [1, 2] or secondary to rheumatoid arthritis, asbestosis or hypersensitivity pneumonitis [3–5]. Expression of MUC5B protein in the epithelium is increased in presence of the MUC5B polymorphism, even in healthy subjects unaffected by ILD [2].

Lung transplantation (LT) is a valuable therapeutic option, particularly for ILD [6]. Despite ongoing improvements in its management, the prognosis after LT remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate of 60% [7]. Chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) [8] is the main complication beyond the first year. It significantly impairs the prognosis of LT recipients, occurring in 50% of cases after 5 years [9], and is the leading cause of death in this population [10]. The pathophysiology and risk factors of CLAD have yet to be clarified. Nevertheless, recent data suggest that epithelial mucins are involved in its occurrence [11], with levels of broncho-alveolar lavage MUC5B associated with CLAD progression. Moreover, two main phenotypes of CLAD have been described [8], the most frequent being obstructive or bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS), characterised by small airway damage and inflammation. The restrictive phenotype or restrictive allograft syndrome (RAS) is associated with airways, pleural and parenchymal tissue damage, and carries a poorer prognosis, with faster lung function decline [12]. The risk factors associated with BOS or RAS onset are yet to be elucidated. Recent data suggest the potential implication of donors’ and recipients’ genetic traits in the occurrence of RAS [13, 14]. Due to the similarities between RAS and ILD, MUC5B rs35705950 polymorphism was analysed in a single-centre cohort of 746 LT recipients. The study found no association between the presence of the minor allele T (homozygous TT or heterozygous GT) and CLAD onset [15]. However, the impact of the presence of the T allele on the risk of CLAD had yet to be found, leaving a question mark over the donor's involvement in CLAD onset.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate whether the donor's MUC5B rs35705950 polymorphism was associated with CLAD onset, using data from a national multicentric biobank. Secondary objectives were to evaluate 1) if the donor's polymorphism was associated with a restrictive or mixed phenotype of CLAD, 2) if the recipient's polymorphism was associated with a higher rate of restrictive phenotype of CLAD 3) and what the frequency of polymorphism was in LT recipients, according to each underlying respiratory disease.

Methods

Study cohort

We used blood samples from the Cohort in Lung Transplantation (COLT) [16]. COLT is a French national, multicentre, prospective study initiated in 2009 which aims to identify risk factors for CLAD (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00980967). Patients were recruited between September 2009 and December 2018, in 10 French centres (Paris-Bichat, Paris-Cochin, Marie Lannelongue, Foch, Toulouse, Marseille, Bordeaux, Nantes, Grenoble and Lyon) and one in Belgium (Brussels). The COLT biocollection is made of whole blood, serum and DNA from the donor and the recipient at various time-points pre- and post-LT. The COLT study received the approval of the Ethic Committee on 24 February 2009 (CPP Ouest 1-Tours, 2009-A00036-51) and authorisation from the French regulatory authority (AFSSASP/ANSM) on 5 February 2009. The protocol was registered by the CNIL (National Committee for Data Processing and Liberties) and approved on 16 September 2011. The biobank has been subject to regulatory declarations (declared to the Ministry of Research under DC-2011-1399). We included all double-lung and heart–lung recipients included in the COLT study and their respective donors, between January 2009 and December 2014, with at least one pre-transplant serum and DNA samples available from both donor and recipient. Patients with missing clinical data, who received a single lung, with a missing donor or recipient pre-transplant sample, or those who did not consent to genetic studies were excluded. For each recipient demographic characteristic, underlying respiratory disease type and severity, and associated comorbidity data were collected. For each donor, demographic characteristics, cause of death and lung graft assessment data were collected. For each donor and recipient, peripheral blood was collected by participating centres pre-operatively at the time of LT and centralised. The main outcome was CLAD and its phenotype, adjudicated by an independent committee as per the design of the COLT study and according to International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) guidelines [8].

The study protocol was approved and validated by the COLT executive committee on 8 March 2021 and by the Institutional Review Board of the French Learned Society for Respiratory Medicine – Société de Pneumologie de Langue Française (SPLF, CEPRO 2023-037). Signed informed consent to participate in the COLT study had been obtained from recipients. The Committee for Patients’ Protection waived the need for specific consent authorising genetic analysis from donors.

Definitions

CLAD was defined according to ISHLT guidelines [8] as a decline in forced expiratory volume in 1 s less than 80% of baseline. BOS or RAS phenotype were described according to ISHLT guidelines [8]. CLAD onset, its phenotype and its delay after LT were determined by an independent adjudication comity, as per the design of the COLT study, after a follow-up of 5 years. We defined the primary end-point as the occurrence of CLAD at 5 years post-transplant.

Patients’ clinical characteristics and outcome

For the recipients, patients’ characteristics included age and date of LT, gender, type of LT (double LT or heart–lung LT), and cytomegalovirus status. Underlying diseases were classified into four sub-groups: COPD/emphysema, ILD, cystic fibrosis (CF) and “other aetiologies”. The subtype of ILD was not available for analysis. The donors’ and recipients’ ethnicity were not available due to French law governing research. Clinical follow-up included primary graft dysfunction (PGD), acute cellular rejection (ACR) onset and antibody-mediated rejection (AMR) onset, defined according to ISHLT guidelines [17, 18].

Blood sampling

For each donor, a peripheral blood sample was collected in an EDTA tube in the hours prior to organ procurement. For each recipient, the peripheral blood sample was collected in an EDTA tube at the time of inclusion or on the day of the transplant at the latest. All the blood samples were centralised and stored at −80°C until DNA extraction with the DNA extraction kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany).

MUC5B genotyping

Genotyping of the MUC5B rs35705950 polymorphism was performed using an allelic discrimination technique based on Taqman real-time quantitative PCR (assay ID C_1582254_20 Thermo Fisher Scientific), with two probes specific to each allelic variant. Each plate included a blank, two homozygous controls (GG and TT) and a heterozygous control (GT). Each well included a 10 µL reaction volume consisting of 5 µL of TaqMan Genotyping Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.3 µL of Taqman SNP Assay containing the fluorescent probes and primers, 4.3 µL of water and 0.4 µL of DNA at 50 ng·mL−1 (20 ng) per well. PCR amplification was performed on a FAST 7500 thermocycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The “at risk” genotype was defined by the presence of the minor T allele (TT or GT genotype).

The minor allele frequency (MAF) was used to describe the lower allele frequency and its variation in the LT population [19]. It was calculated in the donor group and in each subgroup of recipients by dividing the number of minor T allele occurrences by the total number of copies of the allele of interest in the cohort.

Statistical analysis

For descriptive statistics, the results are presented as number (percentage) for categorical variables or as median (interquartile range) for continuous variables. Genotype frequencies were analysed in the donor and recipient groups (homozygous dominant GG, recessive TT and heterozygous GT genotype frequencies). Clinical characteristics and outcomes of participants were compared based on the presence of the MUC5B T allele in LT donors and recipients, as well as the recipients' underlying respiratory diseases, using Pearson's Chi-squared test, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. The associations between donor and recipient genotypes and recipient outcomes at 5 years (CLAD, RAS/mixed phenotype, ACR and AMR) were first estimated using univariate logistic regression, then adjusted for donor and recipient age and gender, respectively. CLAD-free survival over 5 years, according to donor or recipient genotype, was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and associations were estimated using multivariate Cox models (adjusted for age and gender). Additionally, a multivariate Cox model was fitted to estimate associations between CLAD occurrence within 5 years and recipient genotype, donor genotype, donor age and gender, and history of AMR, ACR or PGD. In the recipient group, patients who had undergone redo-LT were excluded from the analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2) and Prism software (version 8.4.3.686, GraphPad Software, San Diego, California). The significance level was considered for a two-sided p<0.05. No correction for multiplicity was applied; therefore, statistically significant results should not be interpreted as evidence of causal effects.

Results

LT recipient characteristics

Between 2009 and 2018, 1874 patients were included in the COLT cohort. Among the 1200 biocollection participants, blood samples were available from 263 donor–recipient pairs, with recipients engrafted between 2009 and 2015, i.e. with at least 5 years of follow-up. 20 recipients were excluded from the analysis because they received a single LT, six because they received a redo-LT and 27 because the DNA sample quality from the donor or recipient was impaired, leaving a total of 210 donor and recipient pairs with successful genotyping (figure 1). The underlying respiratory disease in the recipients was COPD/emphysema in 95 (45.2%) cases, any form of ILD in 30 (14.3%), CF in 58 (27.6%) and other respiratory diseases in 27 (12.8%). 205 patients received a double LT, while five received a cardio-pulmonary graft. Influence of the MUC5B polymorphism in donors and recipients on post-transplant outcome

After 5 years of follow-up, 187 (89.0%) patients were alive and CLAD was diagnosed in 68 patients (32.4%), 54 of whom had an obstructive phenotype. Presence of the T allele in the donor or the recipient was not associated with CLAD occurrence at 5 years, with an OR of 1.09 (95% CI 0.53–2.16; p=0.8) for donors and an OR of 0.74 (95% CI 0.35–1.52; p=0.4) for recipients. Similarly, no association was found with a restrictive or mixed CLAD phenotype (OR 0.85 (95% CI 0.12–3.63), p=0.8, for donors and OR 1.24 (95% CI 0.26–4.57), p=0.8, for recipients) (figure 2). CLAD-free survival over 5 years was not associated with donor genotype (hazard ratio (HR) 1.10 (95% CI 0.63–1.94), p=0.7) or recipient genotype (HR 0.76 (95% CI 0.41–1.39), p=0.4) (figures 2, 3a and 3b).

Outcomes risks according to donors’ or recipients’ MUC5B polymorphism. Outcomes considered were antibody-mediated rejection, acute cellular rejection, restrictive or mixed allograft syndrome (logistic regressions), and chronic lung allograft dysfunction (Cox model). Presence of MUC5B polymorphism in recipients’ genotypes was significantly associated with the protection from antibody-mediated rejection (OR 0.24, 95% CI 0.07–0.65; p=0.01).

No statistical relationship was evidenced between donor or recipient MUC5B polymorphism and PGD, nor with ACR at 5 years (figure 3 and table ESM2). Risk of AMR was independent of donor MUC5B polymorphism genotype (OR 0.72 (95% CI 0.30–1.58), p=0.4), but recipient T allele was associated with reduced AMR occurrence (OR 0.24 (95% CI 0.07–0.65), p=0.01). In the model evaluating the associations between CLAD-free survival over 5 years and recipient genotype, donor genotype, donor age and gender, history of AMR, ACR or PGD, and history of ACR was found to be associated with an increased risk of CLAD occurrence (HR 2.11 (95% CI 1.15–3.90), p=0.017) (table ESM 3).

Discussion

We screened a multicentre biobank for MUC5B rs35705950 promoter polymorphism in 210 donors and 210 recipients of lung allograft. Our main results can be summarised as follows: we did not find any significant association between donor or recipient MUC5B promoter polymorphism and CLAD occurrence, CLAD phenotype or 5-year survival; whereas the reduction of risk for AMR was associated with the presence of recipients’ T allele and, finally, the recipients’ MUC5B polymorphism significantly differed according to the underlying respiratory disease.

MUC5B rs35705950 polymorphism is gaining interest in respiratory diseases. It has been shown to be a strong risk factor for IPF occurrence [4] and for ILD onset in rheumatoid arthritis [3]. Furthermore, its presence has been associated with a better survival and transplant-free survival in IPF [20], while it was not associated with rheumatoid arthritis–ILD prognosis [21].

In LT recipients, two previous studies have failed to evidence any significant association between CLAD occurrence and recipients’ rs35705950 polymorphism [15, 22] and we confirmed these findings in the present study. We nevertheless aimed to investigate the donors’ rs35705950 polymorphism, as chimerism between the donor's and the recipient's cells is known to occur only in limited anatomical sites after LT [23], and the allele expressed in the lung allograft is therefore that of the donor. We aimed to evaluate whether its presence in the lung allograft was associated with the LT recipient's prognosis. We found no evidence of an association between the donor's rs35705950 polymorphism and subsequent CLAD onset.

Moreover, the presence of the minor allele was not associated with an increased risk of a restrictive phenotype of CLAD, although associated with ILD [4, 21]. While BOS is limited to small distal airways, RAS shows various patterns including sub pleural and parenchymatous damage, with a histological pattern that can be similar to some ILD [12]. In a previous study by Goos et al. [15], the “T allele” of 746 LT recipients was found to be associated with the underlying respiratory disease, being higher in ILD patients (reaching 17.6%) compared to other respiratory diseases. In our cohort, ILD patients had the higher rate of “T allele”, reaching 20.0%, compared to 13.4% in CF, 8.4% in COPD and 5.6% in other underlying respiratory disease. The observed difference differed significantly (p=0.03). Nevertheless, it must be underlined that the subtype of ILD was not available as per the COLT database design, preventing this analysis. We also aimed to evaluate whether the presence of the minor allele of rs35705950 in donors impacts the prognosis of LT recipients and found no association between the donor polymorphism and CLAD onset or CLAD phenotype. These data are consistent with two previous studies [15, 22].

We investigated the association between this polymorphism with other outcomes in LT recipients. We did not find any association with ACR occurrence; however, surprisingly, recipient polymorphism was associated with a lower risk of AMR occurrence (OR 0.26 (0.08–0.69), p=0.015). These results should be assessed with caution, as the diagnosis of AMR was raised by the physicians in charge (who followed ISHLT guidelines [18]) and was not independently adjudicated, as per the design of the COLT study. Nevertheless, this diagnosis was made blindly from MUC5B rs35705950 promotor polymorphism. Previous studies in the thoracic transplantation area have demonstrated the association of common single nucleotide polymorphisms and the risk of AMR. In heart transplantation, two quite widespread recipient polymorphisms associated with innate immunity and the complement pathway have been found to be associated with a higher risk for AMR [24]. Okamoto et al. [25] report a higher C3 expression associated with MUC5B promoter TT genotype in IPF patients [26]. One could hypothesise that this genotype is involved in the antibody-mediated immune response, which is partly complement-dependent [27]. The other hypothesis could be a polygenic protective characteristic, with the association of other protective genes linked to MUC5B expression. Obviously, these statements are purely speculative and deserve specific complementary investigations.

Although being, to our knowledge, the first study reporting the influence of MUC5B minor polymorphism of the donor in CLAD occurrence after LT, this study has several limitations. Although the multicentric COLT study is the largest French biobank of LT donors and recipients, the number of patients with ILD limited the power of detection of rs35705950 polymorphism frequency. The limited number of ILD patients could be explained by the conditional 5-year survival. Moreover, we were unable to analyse the genotype frequency according to the ethnic origin of donors and recipients, as French legislation does not allow the collection of the ethnic origin of patients. The underlying respiratory disease is referenced according to the reason for listing, therefore limiting inter-centre variability, but does not allow a precise insight into ILD classification. The design of the COLT study is nevertheless a strength for our findings. The presence of an independent adjudication committee allows a reliable determination of the diagnosis of CLAD and its phenotype; moreover, the long follow-up period supports the drawing of solid conclusions.

In summary, we found a frequency of MUC5B rs35705950 promotor polymorphism similar to previous data in the general Caucasian population, among donors and recipients. CLAD onset or its restrictive phenotype were not associated with rs35705950 polymorphism, neither in the recipient (as previously described), nor in the donor. Surprisingly, rs35705950 polymorphism of the recipient appeared to be associated with a reduced AMR occurrence. These findings remain to be confirmed in further studies and the potential interplay between MUC5B rs35705950 promotor polymorphism and AMR needs to be elucidated.
