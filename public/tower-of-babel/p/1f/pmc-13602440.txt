When Enzymes Mislead: Assessing the Value of MRCP in Suspected Choledocholithiasis
Authors: Renato Pitesa, Wai Yan (Rachel) Yuen, Nadia Meyer, Garth Poole, Primal Parry Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe diagnosis of choledocholithiasis (CDL) requires balancing timely intervention against the risks of unnecessary invasive procedures. Although liver function tests (LFTs) are widely used for risk stratification, their static values and short‐term trends remain poorly defined in predicting persistent common bile duct stones. This study evaluated the association between static and dynamic LFTs and CDL and assessed the diagnostic performance of magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP).MethodsA retrospective review was conducted at Middlemore Hospital involving 504 acute inpatient MRCP scans within 2022. The collected clinical variables included patient demographics, LFTs and imaging modalities. 2 × 2 tables were constructed for those who had both MRCP and ERCP to assess the diagnostic utility of MRCP. Logistic regression and linear mixed‐effects models were used to analyse the association between LFTs and CDL. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic performance of each LFT.ResultsOf 504 MRCPs, 131 (26%) were positive for stones. Bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT) were significantly higher in the CDL group on admission and repeat testing. Paradoxically, CDL was associated with a downward 24‐h trend in ALP and GGT. Elevated GGT demonstrated the strongest independent association with CDL (adjusted OR: 5.27, 95% CI: 2.46–13.21; p < 0.001). Discriminatory performance of individual enzymes was modest (AUC: ALP 0.69; GGT 0.68; bilirubin 0.63). Among 157 patients undergoing both MRCP and ERCP, MRCP demonstrated high sensitivity (91.4%) but low specificity (32.7%).ConclusionStatic and dynamic LFTs provide limited discrimination for persistent CDL and improving biochemical trends do not reliably exclude stones. This study underscores the diagnostic utility of MRCP to prevent both missed CDL and avoidable ERCP, and the need for standardised diagnostic protocols.

Introduction

Choledocholithiasis (CDL), the presence of gallstones in the common bile duct (CBD), is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality, increasing the risk of severe complications such as cholangitis and pancreatitis if not promptly diagnosed and treated [1]. The management of CDL involves balancing the need for accurate diagnosis with the costs of imaging like magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) and the risks of invasive procedures like endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) or intraoperative cholangiogram (IOC) with possible CBD exploration [2]. In patients deemed high‐risk for CDL, MRCP offers a definitive, non‐invasive diagnosis and a detailed anatomical roadmap, often obviating ERCP and assisting surgical planning. Thus, improving non‐invasive diagnostic tools, like blood tests, is essential to prevent unnecessary procedures while ensuring timely intervention.

Liver function tests (LFTs) are routinely used to assess biliary pathology with a characteristic increase in bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT) suggestive of obstructive biliary pathology [3]. Disappointingly, their sensitivity (Sn) and specificity (Sp) in this context are suboptimal. Recent evidence suggests dynamic changes in these enzyme levels during the first 48 h may provide additional diagnostic clues suggesting a spontaneous passing of CDL or persistent blockage [4]. Therefore, an imaging modality that can reliably ‘rule in’ CDL when biochemical risk is high and ‘rule out’ when not is indispensable: MRCP fills this gap.

Despite numerous studies examining the utility of LFTs in predicting CDL, there is still no consensus on their optimal use. A recent scoping review has highlighted the heterogeneity of LFT cut‐off values, suggesting no consensus has been reached [5]. This heterogeneity underscores why many centres now default to MRCP once LFT derangement crosses predefined thresholds. Additionally, static and dynamic tests, and the interplay between different liver enzymes and their combined predictive value, are poorly understood. This study seeks to address these gaps by evaluating both static and dynamic LFTs and their correlation with CDL, and to determine the diagnostic yield and clinical utility of MRCP in diagnosing CDL.

Methods

This study received HDEC (Health and Disability Ethics Committee) approval through the expedited pathway (2023 EXP 13846) and locality approval at Middlemore Hospital (Approval #1757).

Study Design/Participants

A single‐centre retrospective review of all patients who underwent acute inpatient MRCPs at Middlemore Hospital over a 12‐month period between 1 January 2022 and 31 December 2022 was done. Our exclusion criteria included elective or outpatient MRCPs and inpatient MRCPs where the primary indication was not CDL.

Admission diagnoses reflected routine acute general surgical presentations prompting investigation for suspected CDL. These included biliary colic, acute cholecystitis, gallstone pancreatitis and suspected biliary obstruction or cholangitis. However, admission diagnosis was inconsistently documented in an extractable format limiting the ability to reliably stratify patients. MRCP was requested at the discretion of the treating surgical team when there was clinical concern for CBD stones or obstruction based on symptoms, an increasingly cholestatic picture and/or a dilated CBD on USS, irrespective of the admitting diagnosis. As the study cohort comprised a heterogeneous group of biliary‐related presentations with differing baseline risks of CDL, admission diagnosis was not used as an inclusion criterion to reflect real‐world clinical decision‐making.

Middlemore Hospital is a tertiary‐level public Hospital with 900 beds, serving a catchment of 500 000 people. Management of CBD stones at our institution is surgeon‐dependent, but general preference is for preoperative ERCP following radiological confirmation of CDL on MRCP. IOC and laparoscopic CBD exploration are performed selectively rather than routinely.

Variables/Outcomes

The clinical variables collected included patient ethnicity, height and weight on admission, admission and repeat blood samples (including liver enzymes, inflammatory markers and lipase), imaging modalities such as biliary ultrasound (USS), MRCP and ERCP, whether a stone was identified, and whether a laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) was done during index admission. All repeat blood samples were collected within 24 h of the initial set. Data was also collected for a third set of blood samples in patients with CDL.

Our primary outcome was liver enzyme patterns and trends for patients with confirmed CDL. Our secondary outcomes were false negative and false positive rates of MRCPs and ethnic variations in the incidence of CDL.

Statistical Analysis

Mean and standard deviation were used for normally distributed continuous variables, median and interquartile range for non‐normally distributed continuous variables, and frequency and percentages for categorical variables. The association between liver enzyme levels and their dynamic change with CDL was analysed using logistic regression models, adjusting for potential confounding variables such as age, sex, ethnicity and body mass index (BMI). The dynamic changes in liver enzyme patterns in the context of CDL were analysed using linear mixed‐effects models, depending on the data distribution. Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate the association between liver enzyme patterns and CDL based on age, sex and ethnicity.

Diagnostic performance of MRCP was assessed using a 2 × 2 contingency table with ERCP as the gold standard. Sn, Sp, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), diagnostic accuracy, false positive and false negative rates were derived from this table. Confidence intervals (CI) for proportions were calculated using Wilson score intervals.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic performance of each liver enzyme in predicting CDL. The area under the curve (AUC) was then calculated to quantify accuracy. Optimal cutoff values for each enzyme were then determined using the Youden index, maximising the sum of Sn and Sp. All statistical analyses were performed using R studio statistical software (version 4.3.1, Boston, MA). A two‐sided p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Missing Values

Under the assumption of missingness at random, missing data in key blood tests were addressed using multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) with predictive mean matching (PMM) [6]. Five imputed datasets were generated with 50 iterations, and the first complete dataset was used for analysis.

Results

This study identified 898 MRCPs that were performed during the study period. Of the 898 scans, 354 were excluded due to being outpatient and 40 were excluded due to the primary indication not being CDL. Descriptive statistics of the final cohort (n = 504) are summarised in Table 1. Age was the only statistically significant demographic factor between the two groups, with the CDL group being older. Ethnicity was heterogeneous across the cohort, with NZ European being the most common ethnicity in both non‐CDL (28.8%) and CDL groups (26.2%), followed by NZ Māori, Indian and Pacific ethnicities. There was no statistically significant difference in ethnicity between groups (p = 0.738). Sex and BMI were also similar at baseline between both groups (Table 1).

Three hundred and ninety‐one patients (78%) had biliary ultrasound scans (USS) before their MRCP. The median (IQR) CBD diameter on USS of those with stones was greater than that of those without stones, which was statistically significant (8 mm [6–10 mm] vs. 6 mm [5–9 mm] p = 0.002) (Table 1). Of the 504 patients, 201 received an index LC. For the 201 who had an index LC, the median (IQR) time from admission to MRCP was 2 days (1–3 days), and the median time from MRCP request to MRCP was 1 day.

Table 1 summarises the mean liver enzyme values taken on admission, the following day and the trend between the two. Bilirubin, ALP and GGT all showed statistically significant differences between the two groups, both on admission and repeat testing. These were all higher on admission, while ALP and GGT showed a significant downward trend on repeat testing (Figure 1).

Table 2 shows a 2 × 2 contingency table of MRCP results (index test) against ERCP results (gold standard) for all patients who had both procedures (n = 157). MRCP demonstrated a Sn and Sp of 91.4% (95% CI: 84.4%–95.5%) and 32.7% (95% CI: 21.3%–46.5%) respectively. PPV and NPV were 73.3% (95% CI: 65.1%–80.2%) and 65.4% (95% CI: 46.2%–80.6%). The false negative rate was 8.6% (95% CI: 4.5%–15.6%) and the false positive rate was 67.3% (95% CI: 53.5%–78.7%). Overall diagnostic accuracy was 72% (95% CI: 64.5%–78.5%).

Figure 5 represents the diagnostic performance of each liver enzyme test in predicting CDL. Each test showed modest diagnostic accuracy, with ALP and GGT outperforming the other enzymes (AUC: 0.69 [95% CI: 0.63–0.75] and 0.68 [95% CI: 0.63–0.74], respectively) and bilirubin having the poorest with an AUC of 0.63 [95% CI: 0.57–0.69]. The cutoff values optimizing the balance between Sn and Sp for ALP and GGT were 188 IU/L and 276 IU/L, respectively, with bilirubin having a cutoff value of 30 μmol/L.

Discussion

This was a retrospective review of patients receiving acute inpatient MRCPs, which aimed to identify LFT values and trends that can be used to reliably predict the presence of CDL. Despite biochemical uncertainty, MRCP demonstrated high diagnostic utility, confirming stones in 25% of patients with markedly deranged LFTs. Bilirubin, ALP and GGT were significantly higher on admission in the CDL group, with ALP and GGT showing a significant downward trend on repeat testing. Additionally, the best AUC curve values of 0.69 for ALP and 0.68 for GGT individually also suggest that these are modest predictors at best.

Notably, bilirubin did not demonstrate a significant change between admission and repeat testing in the control or CDL group (1.86 and 1.77 μmol/L, p = 0.8). This was surprising, given bilirubin is the only biochemical test that is listed as both a strong and very strong predictor in the ASGE guidelines [7]. The lack of a bilirubin rise in the CDL group within the first 24 h of hospital admission possibly suggests a lag in a detectable accumulation of conjugated bilirubin within hepatocytes in response to biliary obstruction. Nevertheless, it is important to note that persistently elevated bilirubin levels, particularly greater than twice the upper limit of normal, may still indicate ongoing biliary obstruction and should increase clinical suspicion [8].

Although a downtrend in liver enzymes following admission is expected with standard resuscitation practices, an interesting finding was the persistence of CDL despite the downward enzyme trends. This goes against the current consensus on how liver enzyme tests are interpreted as well as existing data, whereby ongoing CBD obstruction results in worsening cholestasis and hepatocellular damage, and thus worsening liver enzymes, whilst a downtrend represents possible spontaneous stone passage [9, 10]. There are several potential reasons why contrary findings were observed. Reportedly, 12%–39% of people with CDL are asymptomatic, therefore a number of patients may have been asymptomatic for some time with their obstruction prior to admission, accounting for why the initial spike in liver enzymes in response to the onset of obstruction is not captured on hospital laboratory results [11, 12]. Secondly, down trending liver enzymes may be influenced by admission resuscitation, particularly improvement in hepatic perfusion via intravenous fluid administration and antibiotic therapy which can reduce associated hepatocellular inflammation independent of true biliary clearance [13, 14]. Lastly, the degree of biliary obstruction may only be partial or intermittent, resulting in a more varied biochemical picture over time. Given the low physiological baseline pressure of the biliary system (~1–7 mmHg), even minor transient degrees of obstruction can lead to significant biochemical fluctuations [15]. The factor of sampling bias is possible but unlikely, given this inverse relationship has been described in other studies. For example Bidarmaghz et al. found that downward trends in liver enzyme values did not reliably exclude persistent CDL, with approximately two‐thirds of patients exhibiting CBD stones on ERCP despite biochemical improvement [16]. Hence, enzyme downtrends cannot be relied upon solely to exclude CDL, reinforcing MRCP as a decisive non‐invasive imaging modality with previous studies reporting a Sp of up to 98% [17].

Additionally, while concurrent cholecystitis is common in patients with gallstone disease and is associated with liver enzyme derangement, our study found no significant relationship between inflammatory markers (white blood cells, C‐reactive protein and neutrophils) and CDL. This suggests that the observed liver enzyme patterns are less likely to be driven by inflammation alone, and more indicative of underlying biliary obstruction. These findings support the interpretation that the dynamic changes in LFTs seen in our cohort are predominantly related to the pathophysiology of CDL rather than confounded by concurrent inflammatory processes. Zgheib et al. similarly found that patients with acute cholecystitis (AC) and concurrent CDL had lower mean WBC values, were less likely to have leucocytosis, and were less likely to be septic compared to those with AC and no CDL [18].

Liver enzyme tests used in isolation may have limited utility in diagnosing CDL but remain clinically relevant in determining the need for further specialised imaging. The median time from admission to MRCP for those undergoing index LC at our centre was 1 day, allowing imaging‐guided actionable management of deranged liver enzymes. A 25% positivity rate reflects appropriate case selection, balancing diagnostic yield with resource utilisation and is comparable to expected rates for imaging in this patient population with a similar yield of 37% by Robinson et al. and 26.8% by Wee et al. [19, 20]. The relatively high false positive rate 67.3% (95% CI: 53.5%–78.7%) is likely explained by spontaneous stone passage in the interim time between MRCP and ERCP. While the high Sn of MRCP for CDL is well‐established, the challenge lies in accurately identifying patients that require imaging when biochemical markers are improving or mildly abnormal. This study addresses a gap in the literature by demonstrating that both static and dynamic liver enzyme patters, including improving trends traditionally interpreted as stone passage, fail to reliably exclude persistent CDL. In this context, MRCP serves as a necessary safeguard in those whose liver enzyme picture may falsely reassure clinicians. Additionally, MRCP offers an important adjunct for surgical planning, aiding in risk stratification and operative decision‐making to minimise bile duct injuries and conversion rates.

Moreover, the role of IOC and single session management of CDL at LC is increasingly supported in the literature with reported reductions in LOS and cost [21, 22, 23]. Given 75% of MRCPs were negative, single session management of stones could potentially represent significant reductions in LOS and associated costs. However, such approaches require institutional expertise in laparoscopic bile duct exploration to reduce the risk of iatrogenic bile duct injuries and ready access to intraoperative imaging. In such settings, MRCP remains a key preoperative tool with a low false‐negative rate (8.6%).

The current study has several limitations. As a retrospective, single‐centre review, this introduces an inherent degree of sampling and information bias and reduces the generalisability of results. Secondly, there is the issue of confounders such as medications, underlying biliary disease and temporal uncertainty. From this, it is difficult to confidently conclude whether the inverse relationship observed between dynamic liver enzyme trends and CDL is a pathophysiological mechanism or a consequence of unmeasured confounders. Additionally, it is difficult to control for in‐hospital delays in accessing scans, which may affect the relationship between MRCP findings and blood test timing.

Conclusion

In conclusion, MRCP has useful diagnostic utility in patients suspected to have CDL regardless of their biochemical picture. While liver enzymes and their trends provide valuable clinical insight, their utility in diagnosing CDL alone remains limited and may reflect different pathophysiological mechanisms we do not fully appreciate. These contrary findings emphasise the need for further investigation into how CDL manifests biochemically to streamline and standardise diagnosis and management.
