Linking multipoint folding and stability with functional regulation in the mitochondrial transmembrane β-barrel Sam50
Authors: Roshika Ravi, Swadha Gupta, Jyoti Kumari, Aadish Rawat, Radhakrishnan Mahalakshmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondrial outer membrane proteins (β-OMPs) serve as the first line of communication with the cellular milieu. A crucial β-OMP, the sorting and assembly machinery Sam50, is a 16-stranded transmembrane β-barrel highly conserved in all eukaryotes. Sam50 dysregulation is lethal; yet, molecular elements that regulate Sam50 remain poorly understood. Here, we identify and characterize residues that regulate Sam50 structure and function. Using single-molecule electrophysiology, in vivo function, and stability measurements, we demonstrate that the POlypeptide-TRansport Associated domain is dispensable for Sam50. Complete characterization of the folding mechanism using 165 Xaa→Ala substitutions reveals that Sam50 folds through parallel pathways, with at least two transition states. The folding nucleus is towards the N-terminus, whereas frustrated folding at the C-terminal region kinetically traps the structure. We correlate this unexpected folding of Sam50 with its SAM-assisted assembly. Additionally, our per-residue stability measurements show that destabilizing hotspots in Sam50 are linked to its gating function. Our findings suggest how the dynamic structure of Sam50 offers a functional advantage, with specific residues that regulate folding, stability, and function, also determine the protein’s sensitivity to mutations.

Introduction

Mitochondria, the site of cellular bioenergetics, are dual-membrane organelles. The mitochondrial outer membrane (mOM) is uniquely enriched with transmembrane β-barrel OM proteins (β-OMPs). β-OMPs regulate metabolite flux, transport ions, proteins, and lipids, form mitochondrial-endoplasmic reticulum (ER) contacts, and maintain mitostasis1. β-OMPs are nuclear-encoded and cytosolically synthesized. They are then imported into the intermembrane space (IMS) of mitochondria, where they undergo a highly regulated directional insertion into the mOM. This β-OMP insertion is mediated by the Sorting and Assembly Machinery (SAM)2–4. Sam50, the core SAM component, is a 16-stranded β-OMP that facilitates substrate insertion into the mOM, with one N-terminal POlypeptide TRansport Associated (POTRA) domain located in the IMS that mediates precursor release (Fig. 1a)2,5,6. Yeast Sam50 additionally binds to peripheral subunits Sam35/Sam37 through its cytosolic loops3,4. Sam50 differs functionally from ATP-dependent chaperones in its ability to scaffold the folding of nascent β-OMPs (including itself) independent of electrochemical energy and membrane composition. In humans, Sam50 also regulates mitochondrial dynamics, and is involved in lipid scrambling and mitochondrial quality control1,7,8. Insights into how the complex process of yeast Sam50-mediated β-OMP folding has been obtained from a stable structural intermediate captured using cryo-EM3,4. Here, the β1 seam of the Sam50 transmembrane domain (TMD) associates dynamically with β16 (Fig. 1a). This association allows β1 to template the C→N assembly of nascent β-OMPs into the mOM. The N-terminal lateral gate (β1-β3) undergoes structural interconversion between gate-closed (resting) and gate-open (substrate-bound) states, while the POTRA domain and Sam35/37 remain unaltered3,4,10,11. Interestingly, however, the thermodynamics regulating such structural dynamics remains poorly understood. More importantly, the mechanism of how Sam50 itself folds, the folding pathways adopted by Sam50, and the residue-specific regulation of transition state structures remain unclear.

Here, we present atomistic details linking Sam50 folding, stability, and function. We show that Sam50 TMD folds and functions independent of its POTRA domain, SAM complex, and Sam35/37. The folding is stepwise, directed by the primary sequence, and proceeds through parallel pathways. The transition state structures are similar, where strands β3–β6 nucleate folding, followed by β7–β11 assembly. Residues at β13–β15, form non-native interactions, exhibit late assembly, but are vital for Sam50 function in vivo. Our findings suggest spontaneous membrane insertion of the pre-assembled N-terminal strands, and a SAM complex-assisted insertion of the C-terminal strands. Folded Sam50 is destabilized thermodynamically at β4-β6, and this is mechanistically relevant for the “open↔closed” interconversion of the Sam50 lateral gate during its function. We suggest this stability-function trade-off as a possible advantage allowing Sam50 to efficiently fold nascent β-barrels, while also performing additional essential functions in the mitochondrial membrane.

Results

Folding and function of POTRA-less Sam50 resembles full-length Sam50

Sam50 is believed to have evolved from the primordial β-barrel assembly machinery (BamA-BAM) of Gram-negative bacteria. Here, all extramembranous POTRA domains (except one) are non-essential for mitochondrial Sam50 function5. First, we asked if the TMD was sufficient for Sam50 folding, stability, and function. To address this, we generated a POTRA-less variant of yeast Sam50 (Sam50Δ120, only TMD; referred to hereafter as Sam50) (Fig. 1a). We examined if the structural and functional features of the full-length Sam50 (Sam50FL) were retained in POTRA-less Sam50. Similar to observations with Neurospora crassa Tob556, BamA12 and other β-OMPs13,14, both Sam50FL and Sam50Δ120 can fold spontaneously in artificial membranes without SAM assistance (Supplementary Figs. S1–S5). Screens of folding and stability of both proteins in large unilamellar vesicles (LUVs) yielded PC:PE LUVs (90% DMPC, 10% DMPE) as optimal for path-independent assembly (details in SI Methods, Supplementary Table S1).

Folded Sam50 in PC:PE membranes resist proteolysis by proteinase K, and forms a β-rich structure as revealed by far-UV circular dichroism spectropolarimetry (comparable to NcTob556 and 16-stranded EcOmpF15). Folded Sam50 also shows a 10 nm blue shift in Trp fluorescence to 336 nm, with a twofold increase in fluorescence intensity (Fig. 1b–e and Supplementary Figs. S1–S6). These observations show that Sam50 can fold into a stable β-barrel independent of its POTRA domain. Sam50 has no identified role in conducting ions; yet, it can exhibit channel-like behavior in electrophysiology experiments (Fig. 1f–h and Supplementary Fig. S1e–p), with unknown in vivo significance6,11,16. Comparison of both full-length and POTRA-less constructs using single molecule electrophysiology6,11,13,16 revealed channel-like transitions between two conductance states, O1 and O2 (Fig. 1f), as reported earlier11, with the removal of POTRA favoring the lower conductance O1 state in Sam50Δ120. Both proteins also exhibit similar changes in voltage-dependent conductance (Fig. 1g) and step-wise channel insertion events (Fig. 1h). We additionally validated the in vivo function of Sam50 by complementation of yeast lacking the nuclear-encoded SAM50. Survival of cells carrying pFL39-SAM50FL and pFL39-SAM50Δ120 was similar in both fermentable (glucose) and non-fermentable (glycerol) carbon sources at permissive (25 °C, 30 °C) and non-permissive (37 °C) temperatures (Fig. 1i). Taken together, our results show that the TMD is sufficient for Sam50 folding and function, both in vitro and in vivo.

Sam50 exhibits thermodynamic stability in PC:PE membranes

The complex interplay between Sam50 and LUVs precludes a simple interpretation of its thermodynamic parameters. Unlike bacterial OMPs18–22, we found that Sam50 β-barrel is less stable thermodynamically. We measured a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Delta G}_{{{{\rm{F}}}}}^{0,{{{\rm{WT}}}}}$$\end{document}ΔGF0,WT of – 6.66 ± 0.04 kcal mol−1, a folding cooperativity (MF) of – 3.01 kcal mol−1 M−1, and a mid-point of chemical denaturation (Cm) of 2.21 ± 0.01 M (Supplementary Table S2). Next, we generated a library of 165 Xaa→Ala substitutions in the TMD (Fig. 2b), and deduced the contribution of each side chain to β-barrel stability, by measuring the energetics for each variant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Delta G}_{{{{\rm{F}}}}}^{0,{{{\rm{Mut}}}}}$$\end{document}ΔGF0,Mut) (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Figs. S7–S8 and Supplementary Table S2). The lower entropic cost of introducing Ala (with fewer rotamers) provided us with a per-residue thermodynamic map of Sam50 (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. S9). Here, most substitutions did not alter the overall β-barrel stability. Despite its moderate stability, we found that the Sam50 structure is tolerant to point mutations.

Intrinsically destabilizing Sam50 residues (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Delta \Delta G}_{{{{\rm{F}}}}}^{0}$$\end{document}ΔΔGF0 > + 0.45 kcal mol−1) primarily involve Gly, Pro, and polar residues (Fig. 2d, maroon). They are distributed in the β-barrel’s lipid and lumen face, midplane, and the interface, and are marginally higher at β4–β6. Specifically, Ser, Thr, Asn, and Gln destabilize Sam50 when they are located at β1–β8 lumen over β9–β16. We inferred that such a polarized destabilization localized at the N-terminal strands correlates with Sam50 function. Opening of Sam50 lateral gate4, and repositioning of β1–β3 in the mOM for substrate assembly, requires hinge-like conformational displacement at β4–β6. Therefore, strategically positioned residues at β4–β6 with lowered stability (Fig. 2d, highlighted region) could facilitate the interconversion of “closed” and “open” states of Sam50. Supporting our observation, we found only two residues (W240, F455) as intrinsically stabilizing (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Delta \Delta G}_{{{{\rm{F}}}}}^{0}$$\end{document}ΔΔGF0 < – 0.45 kcal mol−1). Other transmembrane aromatic residues do not alter Sam50 stability (Supplementary Table S2), suggesting that the contribution of W240 and F455 is location-specific.

Our experiments show S170 (β4), N175 (β4), N415 (β13), and P462 (β15) are destabilizing hotspots (Supplementary Table S2). The folding cooperativity is altered when N175 or P462 are additionally mutated, possibly due to the accumulation of a folding intermediate (Supplementary Figs. S8–S10). These residues form intra-β-barrel interaction (N415 with L6), or with other SAM complex proteins (P462 with Sam37; S170 with Mdm10)3, and can be functionally relevant. The intrinsic destabilization of N415 affects the stability of the C-terminal strands during substrate assembly, which is overcome by L6 docking interactions23. Whether intrinsic Sam50 destabilization can also promote oligomerization in vivo remains to be determined.

β-Barrel stability allosterically regulates protein dynamics

Next, we characterized Sam50FL and Sam50Δ120 in PC:PE membranes using all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to map the protein’s dynamics (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S11–S15). We found larger spatio-temporal dynamics and conformational states in Sam50FL over Sam50Δ120. This is due to the intrinsic dynamicity of the POTRA domain, which increases the radius of gyration (Rg) (Supplementary Figs. S14, S15). The root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of the TMD is marginally lower in Sam50FL than Sam50Δ120 (Fig. 3a, c Supplementary Fig. S13a). Particularly, the strand tilt at β1-β3 (w.r.t. bilayer normal) is lower in Sam50FL than Sam50Δ120, causing structural compaction at the lateral gate in Sam50FL, while Sam50Δ120 β1-β3 are partly open (Fig. 3a). WF-Sam50Δ120 (W240/F455→A; intrinsically stabilizing residues) showed a broader free energy landscape (FEL) than Sam50Δ120, due to larger TMD dynamics (Supplementary Fig. S15). In addition, W240/F455→A increases the β-barrel midplane and T3 (P218-P234 linking β6-β7) distance by 0.5–1.5 nm when compared with Sam50Δ120 (Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Figs. S12–S15). Our simulations revealed that this increase in dynamicity of T3 of only 18 residues –unlike longer loops (L6: 41 residues; L4: 31 residues)– is due to A240 (on β7) and A455 (on β15) causing an allosteric effect on T3. Hence, the midplane W240 (β7) and F455 (β15) anchor the TMD, help form structured extramembranous segments, and are vital for Sam50 structural integrity. The loss in membrane anchoring explains the destabilization we observed when W240 or F455 is replaced (see Fig. 2d).

In contrast, SNNP-Sam50Δ120 (S170/N175/N415/P462→A; intrinsically destabilizing residues) shows a narrow FEL, with an increase in TMD structural order. Here again, T3 dynamicity is high, suggesting T3 conformational entropy is sensitive to various thermodynamic perturbations in the TMD (Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Figs. S12–S15). Our simulations establish that the TMD residues of Sam50 exhibit allosteric modulation of the dynamics of extramembranous regions, and may thereby influence cytosolic Sam35/37 binding, N-terminal gating, substrate interaction, or Sam50 function.

Sam50 β-barrel follows parallel pathways for assembly

Next, we studied how per-residue energetics influenced Sam50 folding and structure(s) of the transition state (TS) ensemble formed during folding. We addressed this by combining ultrafast mixing with kinetic measurements of the change in Trp fluorescence. Sam50 kinetics is multiphasic. Fits of change in Trp fluorescence provided three folding (kf1, kf2, kf3) and two unfolding (ku1, ku2) rate constants (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S16–S18). Φ-values define the local structure of each residue in the transition state, with ΦF ranging from ~ 0 (unfolded in TS) to ~ 1 (folded in TS). Values outside the 0-1 range (ΦNN = – 0.3 to – 3.01; ΦNN = + 1.301 to + 4.0) represent non-native interactions formed transiently during folding, but are absent in the end-state structure. To obtain a simplified map of how Sam50 folds, we depicted the ΦF1/2 on a two-dimensional topology diagram (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Figs. S25, S26). We colored each residue that was structured in the TS as blue, unstructured as red, and forming non-native interactions as gray. The results showed strands β3–β6 as structured in the TS in both pathways, suggesting that β3–β6 assembly is a common early nucleation state during Sam50 folding (Fig. 5, zone (i)). Residues forming native-like interactions are also seen in β7–β11 (Fig. 5, zone (ii)), interspersed with non-native interactions, suggesting that these strands are also partially structured during Sam50 assembly. Non-native interactions localized at β14–β16 (Fig. 5, zone (iii)) revealed the assembly of C-terminal strands as the rate-limiting step in Sam50 folding.

In the fast pathway (ΦF1; TS1), several residues of β6-β12 were unfolded or formed non-native contacts (Fig. 5a, zone (ii)). Such non-native contacts can form when transfer of charged residues of L4/L6 across the membrane is energetically disfavored. In contrast, in the slow pathway (ΦF2; TS2), β7-L4-β8 and β10-L6-β12 assembled correctly (Fig. 5b, zone (ii)). Here, several residues are “folded” at β9–β10, facilitating assembly of this hairpin, which can then lower the energy barrier for membrane transfer of L4/L6. In both pathways, folding is nucleated at β3–β6. Subsequently, the fast and slow pathways are demarcated by the extent of local structure formed at the mid-barrel (β7-L4-β8 and β10-L6-β12).

The βT of ~ 0.5 can stem from structural compaction following non-native interactions at the C-terminal β-strands. In vivo, the SAM complex is believed to template the structuring of C-terminal strands and β-barrel closure4. In our experiments, these processes progress slowly in the absence of SAM, also explaining the transient hysteresis during early time points of our thermodynamic measurements (see Supplementary Figs. S4, S5). Whether the SAM complex also accelerates the membrane transfer of L4/L6 is, however, unclear.

Put together, we find that assembly at β3-β6 is spontaneous, and may be nucleated in the IMS in the holdase-bound state or the membrane vicinity (see Supplementary Figs. S31, S32). The post-folding structural destabilization we measured at β4-β6 (Fig. 2d; highlighted) reveals a kinetic role for these β-strands in nucleating Sam50 folding. Folding is retained in Sam50Δ120, emphasizing the intrinsic ability of the β-barrel and extramembranous POTRA domains to fold independently.

Trade-off between residues vital for Sam50 folding and function

Our thermodynamics and kinetics measurements identified key residues that cause frustrated folding, impede rapid β-barrel formation, and affect Sam50 scaffold stability (Figs. 2d, 5a, b, Supplementary Figs. S9, S25, S26). To assess if these residues were retained evolutionarily for superior function, we examined their in vivo relevance. We monitored the survival of yeast YPH499 sam50::ade226, complemented with pFL39-SAM50FL, pFL39-SAM50Δ120, and their mutants, under non-permissive conditions (37 °C, non-fermentable carbon source, oxidative stress). We found Sam50 (FL and Δ120) as tolerant to point mutations (Supplementary Fig. S27). Therefore, we next determined the functional relevance of TMD strands, T3, and L4/L6, through multiple Xaa→Ala substitutions.

Surprisingly, complementation was successful only for a subset of variants (Supplementary Fig. S27). Non-permissive mutants mapped to residues at the midplane and cytosolic interface of β10-β14 (L315(β10)/Y390(β12)/T440(β14); K341/V420) (Supplementary Fig. S28). Our in vitro measurements showed that β10 (L315) contributes to mid-β-barrel assembly, and β10-β13 is essential for membrane transfer of L6. Therefore, we propose that these multiple mutations negatively affect the correct positioning of L6 and subsequent β15-L6-L7-Sam35 interactions. L315 (β10) is essential in both Sam50FL and Sam50Δ120, supporting a POTRA-independent functional role for this residue. Others, such as K341 and V420, located at the neck of L6 and L7, can control loop dynamics and regulate pore size of the Sam50 lumen. T440 can stabilize β15-Sam35 interaction3 at the bilayer interface through vicinal residues (e.g., E437). The other Sam50 subset that did not support yeast survival included I185(β5)/I277(β8)/G443(β14)→A. We attributed this to a combined loss in structural features involving T3 (I185) and L4 (I277) dynamics, and β14 curvature nucleated at G443 (the central hinge residue with unusual ϕ-ψ values; discussed later). These mutations were lethal also in Sam50FL, and POTRA could not rescue this lethality.

Taken together, residues displaying frustration in Sam50 folding also appear vital for its biogenesis and its function in the cell. Many of these residues are conserved between BamAEc, Sam50Sc, and human Sam50 (Supplementary Figs. S29, S30). Our study revealed that, despite the successful evolutionary divergence of Sam50 for adaptation to the mitochondrial membrane and its interactome in the specific eukaryotic cell, the retention of select residues from bacteria to humans emphasizes their functional importance. For example, β14-G443 is necessary for the hinge-like structural gating motion of Sam502,4. Despite causing folding errors, sequences such as β14–β16, or the stereo-selective positioning of residues such as G443, are retained evolutionarily for their vital role in Sam50 function in vivo.

Discussion

Mitochondrial Sam50 exhibits a multipoint assembly that correlates with its function in vivo. We identified the existence of residue-specific regulators of Sam50 thermodynamic stability and β-barrel assembly. These residues correlate inversely with functional regulators of Sam50 (Fig. 6). Sam50 self-assembly is intrinsic to its primary sequence and is independent of the POTRA domain, Sam35/37, or a preformed SAM complex. Thus, the β-barrel domain has retained the evolutionary capacity to fold independently. We captured at least two major fluxes for Sam50, with the accumulation of on-pathway partially assembled structures (Fig. 6[aIII-aIV], Supplementary Fig. 31). Lateral gate opening of Sam50 of the SAM complex and formation of a substrate-Sam50 hybrid β-barrel is unfavorable thermodynamically, and can mechanically perturb the OMM3,4,10. Consequently, bilayer insertion and folding of a nascent Sam50 must be kinetically accelerated. The non-native interactions we observed in the C-terminal strands (Fig. 5, zone (iii)) correlate well with the requirement of a chaperone-assisted Sam50 folding in vivo4. Here, a pre-formed SAM complex templates the membrane insertion and correct formation of β16 → β13 of a nascent Sam50, through local membrane perturbations, lowering the energy barrier for membrane insertion, and forming H-bonding interactions. SAM-assisted folding also suppresses non-native interactions of nascent Sam50 at β13–β16. Simultaneously, local structuring at zone (i) and zone (ii), as captured in TS1 and TS2, can occur in the IMS, proximal to the OMM or the holdase-bound state. This enhances β-strand propagation in nascent Sam50 folding prior to or along with its membrane insertion (Fig. 6[aI-IV]), thereby accelerating rapid folding in vivo. The formation of such local structures is important in β-barrels that have limited distal contacts in the end-state structure.

We show that β-strand formation prior to membrane insertion –displayed only by a few bacterial OMPs19,20,28,29– is likewise retained in mitochondrial OMPs. Our findings that N-terminal and mid-barrel β-strands can assemble spontaneously, and is kinetically accelerated, is also supported by previous studies, which suggest that SAM-assisted folding only templates the structuring of the C-terminal β-hairpins4,10. Notwithstanding the energy penalty of loop insertion, Sam50 displays features of unassisted folding. The spontaneous membrane insertion we observed for β3–β11 should occur at orders of magnitude faster in vivo with SAM-guided templating of β13–β16. While the unassisted folding mechanism of Tom40 and VDACs remains to be solved, we envision that both β-OMPs may adopt similar assembly pathways in vitro.

Kinetic contributions also exist in Sam50. The two (un)folding pathways with TS1 and TS2 we observed for Sam50 suggest that β-barrel energetics involve both thermodynamic and kinetic contributions. Long-chain lipids increase Sam50 stability, but with hysteresis (Supplementary Fig. S2), suggesting that lipids can kinetically trap the β-barrel structure in vivo. Similar kinetically trapped states are known in bacterial OMPs18–21,24,30,31. Furthermore, the measured stability of Sam50 was lower than bacterial OMPs19,21,22,32. Interestingly, despite the low sequence conservation, several residues that lower Sam50 stability are also found in BamA (Supplementary Fig. S29), linking their evolutionary retention with functional relevance. We suggest that kinetic contributors to Sam50 stability are essential for: (i) supporting structural dynamicity in Sam50, (ii) conferring tolerance to mutations, (iii) allowing Sam50 to remain folded when dissociated from the SAM complex, (iv) synergistically achieving a transiently stable gate-open conformation in the OMM, and (v) helping Sam50 remain functional despite mitochondrial insults.

Kinetic contributions can also promote Sam50 oligomerization in vivo (homodimerization, heterodimerization with Sam35/37)2,3. Here, Sam50-Sam35 interactions that are vital for Sam50-mediated folding of nascent β-OMPs33, occur through β7-L4/L6-β11 of Sam50. The unique physico-chemical elements we identified at β7-β11 as essential for Sam50 folding (Fig. 5 zone (ii), Fig. 6[aII]), point to a mechanism for Sam50 quality control in the OMM. We speculate that when Sam50 remains “unfolded” for prolonged durations at β7–β11, it may trigger degradation of this protein34.

We also identify an allosteric link between the dynamics of extramembranous loops and turns with the TMD. Sam50 cytosolic loops L4 and L6 interact with the β-barrel lumen and with Sam35/37. Cryo-EM and mutagenesis studies have revealed how L4/L6 maintains Sam50 structural integrity2,3,10. L6 interacts with β16 and the β-barrel lumen, and guides the substrate to the lateral gate3,10. Hence, L4 and L6 are important for Sam50 structure and function. We found that L4/L6 impedes the rapid folding of Sam50Δ120 in the fast pathway through non-native intra-protein interactions, and membrane transfer penalties of L4/L6 charged residues. The likely association of Sam50 with Sam37 during Sam50 folding could lower the intrinsic energetic barrier for correct membrane transfer of these functionally important loops.

T3 is less understood and unresolved in cryo-EM structures3 due to its intra-luminal positioning. We observed T3 as largely rigid in Sam50FL and Sam50Δ120, with conformational degrees of freedom increasing with (de)stabilizing mutations (S170/N175/N415/P462→A or W240/F455→A) that reduce the inherent strain on T3. Our simulations indicated that the TMD can allosterically fine-tune T3 entropy over 1.5–3.0 nm. Interestingly, T3 residues are not conserved by position between Sam50 and BamA (Supplementary Fig. S29). We propose that restricted T3 conformational sampling is inherent to the Sam50 sequence, crucial for its function, and is allosterically modulated both by TMD residues (WF and SNNP) and POTRA (Fig. 3b). As POTRA is dispensable in vivo, T3-POTRA interaction may serve other unknown roles.

A functional significance for Sam50’s POTRA domain is linked only to precursor release and efficient β-OMP biogenesis2,5. Our limited analyses from simulations established that POTRA residues ~ 30–120 remain well-folded, static, and POTRA does not block the Sam50 lumen (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S12–S14). POTRA may help in switching the TMD between open and closed states (Fig. 3a). Further, POTRA can influence the channel-like characteristics6,11,16 of Sam50. Indeed, our single-channel electrophysiology measurements show that POTRA influences TMD dynamics, altering the residence time and interconversion between O1 and O2 states (Fig. 1f). Interaction of POTRA with TMD and turn residues can help retain the β-barrel in the O2 state (in line with previous findings11), while POTRA-less Sam50Δ120 preferentially samples O1 (Fig. 1f). We attribute this change in conductance we observe for Sam50Δ120 to an increase in dynamicity and reduced β-barrel compactness following POTRA domain deletion. Our findings from electrophysiology measurements are consistent with our MD simulations (Fig. 3a).

Interestingly, POTRA can influence the membrane insertion of Sam50. Sam50FL inserts predominantly as a single channel with conductance of 1-2 nS, while Sam50Δ120 inserts both as single and as more than two channels (accounting for the broad conductance range of 1-5 nS; Supplementary Fig. S1g, h), signifying the role of POTRA in regulating Sam50’s oligomeric state during membrane insertion. Further, and unlike the ~3.7 nS conductance of NcTob556, previous11 and our findings with ScSam50 show a reduced average conductance of ~ 1-2 nS. Such differential behavior is not surprising, given the < 40% sequence similarity between both proteins. Differences in conductance are known for other β-OMPs with near-identical sequences (e.g., human VDACs13 with > 87% similarity).

Multi-point substitutions that are deleterious in vivo are also largely POTRA-independent (Supplementary Fig. S28). The unassisted folding mechanism we mapped for Sam50 links well with in vivo assembly, and collectively, this process may also not require the POTRA domain. It is conceivable that POTRA may become essential during mitochondrial stress, though this remains to be verified.

Sam50’s gating conformations are linked to its stability. Our per-residue energetics measurements support a moderately stable Sam50 –a feature common in several functional proteins19. We found an asymmetric distribution of largely destabilized residues at the N-terminal, and a comparatively stable C-terminal region. This observation links with Sam50 function and interaction in vivo (Fig. 6b). A crucial step in assembling nascent β-OMPs involves a structural change of Sam50 from gate-closed to gate-open state4, leading to Sam50-nascent β-OMP hybrid β-barrel formation. The “open” conformation is achieved by displacement of β1-β3 of Sam50 by 45° at the β4-β7 hinge (Fig. 6c)2,4. Our finding that intrinsically destabilized residues populate β4-β6 provides a thermodynamic explanation for how controlled structural interconversion between “open” and “closed” Sam50 states is achieved at this essential hinge (Fig. 6c). Cryo-EM snapshots of Sam50 also supported rearrangement at β16 (requiring β14-G443) and H-bond reformation between these two states. From our findings, we propose that W240 and F455 help stabilize the β7–β15 zone during such structural interconversions.

Residues forming non-native interactions during Sam50 folding are also relevant in vivo (Fig. 6b(II)). Consistent with previous observations35, we found that substituting β5-Y195 and β11-K341 cause cytotoxicity (Supplementary Figs. S27, S28). In addition, we found β13-V420 and β14-T440 as vital, with β11-K341 particularly crucial in β11 structural integrity, defining protein-bilayer interface through snorkeling. Furthermore, destabilization through Y390→A at β12 (K341/Y390 double mutant) triggered cytotoxicity.

We identified G443 (β14), which resides at the center of the β14 strand kink, as vital for kinetic and functional regulation of Sam50 structure (Fig. 6c). The constrained ϕ-ψ geometry at this site mandates glycine, explaining its conservation from bacteria to humans. Substitution at G443 –when present with other mutations– is lethal in vivo. Our findings help understand how evolution attained structural fine-tuning through key structures and residues (including β4–β6, Y195 (β5), W240 (β7), K341 (β11), D385 (β12), V420 (β13), F455 (β15), G443 (β14), P462 (β15), and T3), which also direct function, stability, and regulation in vivo.

In summary, low copy numbers of Sam50 (~ 1100–1500 per cell36) necessitate that nearly all molecules in the OMM remain functional for β-OMP assembly and lipid homeostasis at mitochondria-ER contacts37,38. The molecular energetics and functionally essential Sam50 residues we identified link folding and structural stability with a POTRA-independent in vivo function. While the SAM complex facilitates folding of a nascent Sam50 in the OMM, we found signatory elements in the Sam50 primary sequence that can kinetically accelerate this process. The multipoint folding that we captured bears some mechanistic similarity to β-barrel swinging39 for the Sam50 N-terminus, and threading40 and β-augmentation41,42 mechanisms for the C-terminus. The Sam50 destabilization we detected upon mutation of key residues can substantially alter SAM complex function, affecting mitochondrial health. The identification of residues tailored for stability and functional efficacy for vital proteins, such as what we report for Sam50, can unveil mechanistic correlations of β-OMPs under both health and disease states, furthering the design of possible therapeutic molecules.

Methods

Cloning, mutant library generation, and protein production

Saccharomyces cerevisiae SAM50 cloned in pGEM4 was a kind gift from Prof. Doron Rapaport (University of Tübingen, Germany). The complete gene was PCR amplified and re-cloned in pET3a between NdeI and BamHI, downstream of the T7 promoter. Full-length pET3a–SAM50 was used as the template for generating the POTRA deleted variant (pET3a–SAM50Δ120). This was achieved through conventional mutagenesis and gene manipulation, wherein the first 360 nucleotides at the 5′ end of SAM50 were excised, and the gene re-ligated in-frame to obtain the POTRA–less variant. Both pET3a–SAM50FL and pET3a–SAM50Δ120 were used as the templates for generating all the mutant variants described in this study.

The modeled structure of Sam5010 was used as the guide to map residues constituting the transmembrane domain of this protein. The codon for each non-Ala transmembrane residue of SAM50Δ120 was mutated systematically to Ala using transfer-PCR19,43. A total of 165 variants of Sam50Δ120 were thus generated, and incorporation of the mutation was verified in each case using Sanger sequencing. Chemically competent Escherichia coli C41 cells were transformed independently with pET3a–SAM50Δ120 and its 165 mutant variants, and protein expression in the form of inclusion bodies was achieved upon induction for 4 h with 1 mM (final) isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside. Inclusion bodies were extracted and purified ( > 85% purity) by treatment with 2.75% Triton X-100 followed by 0.5% sodium deoxycholate14,19,44. The preparations for Sam50Δ120 was additionally subjected to fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC) denaturing purification on a 20 mL HiPrep SP-FF cation ion exchange column using an increasing 1 M NaCl gradient (Buffer B) prepared in 50 mM acetate buffer pH 5.0 containing 8 M urea (Buffer A). Fractions containing the purest form of Sam50Δ120 were pooled, and dialyzed against water to remove urea, residual buffers, and salts. All protein preparations (from inclusion bodies and chromatographic purification) were stored at – 86 °C as lyophilized powders. All 166 variants were produced tag-free and without a mitochondrial targeting sequence(s), and checked for protein integrity on 12% SDS-PAGE before use.

Preparation of large unilamellar vesicles

Lipid vesicles contained the 14-C saturated phosphatidylcholine DMPC (1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine) doped with a defined concentration (10%) of the 14-C non-lamellar lipid DMPE (1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine). For folding screens, the 16-C phosphatidylcholine DPPC (1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine) and cholesterol were also used. Defined molar volumes of DMPC, DMPE, DPPC, and cholesterol, in required ratios and concentrations, were aliquoted in glass vials. Chloroform, methanol, and trace solvents in the lipid suspensions were removed by drying under a stream of dry N2 gas. These lipid films were dried further by lyophilization for 4 h, following which they were stored at – 86 °C and used within one week of preparation.

Lipid resuspension (final concentration = 40 mM) was achieved by hydrating the dried film in the desired buffer (listed in Supplementary Table S1), followed by alternating heating–vortexing cycles at 54 °C for 45 min to obtain a suspension of 40 mM lipid. Large unilamellar vesicles (LUVs) of uniform 100 nm diameter were prepared using 40–50 cycles of extrusion at 35 °C through a 0.1 μm polycarbonate membrane using the mini-extruder assembly (Avanti Polar Lipids, Inc.).

Preparation of Sam50Δ120 stocks

Different conditions were screened to obtain the buffer, lipid composition, lipid:protein ratios, temperature, pH, and dilution factor that supported complete folding of Sam50 (details in SI “Methods”). 125 μM Sam50Δ120 was prepared in 8.0 M GdnHCl in 50 mM citrate buffer pH 5.0 and 10 mM DTT (unfolded stock). Protein quantification was achieved using a molar extinction coefficient for Sam50Δ120 of 53400 M−1 cm−1 at 280 nm (https://www.expasy.org/resources/protparam). In the first step, this unfolded protein stock was diluted 10-fold to achieve 12.5 μM Sam50Δ120 in 20 mM LUVs (90% DMPC + 10% DMPE), 50 mM citrate buffer pH 5.0, 4.0 M GdnHCl, and LPR = 1600:1. Next, this reaction was diluted 5-fold either in buffer (to prepare the folded stock) or 4.0 M GdnHCl (to prepare the unfolded stock). Both stock preparations were incubated for 12–14 h at 28 °C, with rotational mixing. These folded and unfolded stocks contained final concentrations of 2.5 µM protein, 4 mM LUVs (90 % DMPC + 10 % DMPE), 50 mM citrate buffer pH 5.0, 0.2 mM DTT, and 0.8 M GdnHCl (folded stock) or 4.0 M GdnHCl (unfolded stock). The final LPR was 1600:1. These preparations were diluted an additional 6-fold in the equilibrium thermodynamics and kinetics experiments. This stepwise protocol was also followed for the preparation of Sam50FL.

Pulse proteolysis

Unlike bacterial outer membrane proteins, Sam50Δ120 does not exhibit differential mobility on cold SDS-PAGE. Therefore, to assess folding efficiency, the folded protein stock prepared as described above was subjected to proteolysis using the robust protease proteinase K (PK)45. Protein stocks were diluted with 50mM citrate buffer, pH 5.0, and PK was diluted in PK buffer. PK was added so as to achieve different protein:PK ratios (5:1 and 1:3.5) in the final reaction. Samples were drawn at different time intervals and the reaction quenched by addition of phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (final concentration = 8 mM or 1.2 mM). Samples were loaded on 12% SDS-PAGE and checked for protease protection by comparing with the appropriate controls (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. S1b).

Far-UV circular dichroism (CD) measurements

The secondary structure content of the folded protein was assessed on a JASCO J-815 far-UV CD spectropolarimeter (JASCO Inc., Japan). The folded stock protein (2.5 μM protein folded in 4 mM LUVs) was subjected to wavelength scans from 210–260 nm at 1 nm increments and scan speed of 50 nm/min, at 28 °C, using a quartz cuvette of 1 mm path length. The contribution from buffer, GdnHCl and lipid were nullified using appropriate blank corrections, and the raw ellipticity values were smoothened using the means–movement method45. The averaged raw ellipticity (θ, mdeg) from three accumulations was converted to molar ellipticity (ME) at each wavelength19, and plotted (Fig. 1d). Per-residue ellipticity was calculated by dividing the ME at each wavelength by the total number of residues. The signal at ~ 255–260 nm was set to 0 mdeg, to nullify effects of scattering from LUV preparations and plotted as normalized θ (mdeg) for the mutants (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Transmission electron microscopy

To check the integrity and size of vesicles, TEM images were obtained. Protein-free vesicles (0.5 mg/ml) recovered after rotational mixing (similar to the stock preparations) at 1100 rpm for 14 h at 28 °C, were immobilized by coating on a carbon–coated copper grid, followed by staining with 2% uranyl acetate, washing with milliQ, and drying at 28 °C for 4 h. TEM images were obtained on a ThermoFisher Scientific TalosTM F200S (S)TEM operated at an accelerated voltage of 200 kV at – 196 °C.

Electrophysiology measurements

Sam50 is a β-barrel with a central luminal pore with a sufficiently large diameter to facilitate the movement of ions. We used this feature of the protein to check if Sam50Δ120 can form a complete functional β-barrel without its soluble N-terminal POTRA domain. To study the channel-forming properties, both Sam50FL and Sam50Δ120 were first assembled in LDAO micelles by rapid 10–fold dilution of the unfolded protein in 6.0 M GdnHCl, and 10.0 mM DTT, to achieve 25 μM protein, 65 mM LDAO, 10 mM DTT, 100 mM NaCl, in 50 mM phosphate buffer pH 7.2. Samples were incubated for 12 h at 4 °C using rotational mixing. Post-incubation, samples were centrifuged at 16,600 x g for 1 h to remove trace amounts of aggregates. The supernatant was quantified, mixed with 0.1% cholesterol and 1% triton X-100, and incubated on ice for 15–20 min, before use in the experiment. 0.5–1.5 μL of this sample was added on the cis side of a single DiPhPC bilayer (containing 0.1% cholesterol) painted across a 150 μm aperture of a polysulfone cup44. Both sides of the bilayer membrane contained 10 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 5 mM CaCl2, and 1 M KCl. The potential across the black lipid membrane was maintained at + 10 mV using an 8-pole Bessel filter (Warner Instruments, Harvard Apparatus Inc.) and Ag/AgCl electrodes. Protein was added only after a tight membrane with a near-zero conductance was obtained.

Insertion of the protein in the DiPhPC membrane was achieved through slow stirring. For capturing the stepwise insertion events, the protein was added to the cis side of the assembly, and the solution was magnetically stirred, allowing for various protein molecules to be inserted into the DiPhPC membrane consecutively. A holding potential of + 10 mV was maintained throughout. For voltage ramp measurements (both single channel and multichannel experiments), the applied voltage across the membrane was varied systematically from 0 mV → + 100 mV → – 100 mV → 0 mV (at 3.33 mV/s), and the voltage-dependent characteristics of the Sam50 β-barrels were acquired using a filtering frequency of 400 Hz. Similarly, for holding voltage experiments, the potential across the bilayer membrane was maintained at a defined voltage, and the system was monitored for stable channel-forming behavior. The voltage was increased stepwise by 10 mV, to capture channel closing events. Channel-like flickering behavior of both proteins at holding voltages of + 10 mV and + 50 mV were captured. Recordings for multichannel voltage-ramps were carried out with a 15 kHz filtering frequency. The data was acquired using Clampex 10.3.1.5 (Molecular Devices, LLC) and processed using Clampfit 10.6.2.2 (Molecular Devices, LLC).

Interrupted (un)folding measurements

For interrupted folding experiments, the unfolded protein stock comprising 5.0 or 8.75 µM protein, 8.0 or 14.0 mM LUVs (90% DMPC + 10% DMPE), 50 mM citrate buffer pH 5.0, and 6.0 M or 4.0 M GdnHCl, respectively, was incubated overnight at 28 °C with rotational mixing. In the first step of the interrupted folding measurements, the unfolded stock was diluted 6-fold or 3.5-fold with buffer to initiate folding of Sam50Δ120, and the reaction was allowed to age for defined delay times (tage). Folding was interrupted by 2-fold or 6-fold dilution of this reaction in unfolding buffer (3.75/ 4.25/ 4.5/ 5.0/ 5.5 M GdnHCl in 50 mM citrate buffer, pH 5.0), while simultaneously capturing the unfolding kinetics using change in Trp fluorescence (as described above). Three technical replicates were recorded at 28 °C at each tage and each final [GdnHCl]. Each decay profile was processed independently by fitting to a double exponential decay function (Eq. 5b), to derive ku1 and ku2 for each tage.

For interrupted unfolding experiments, the folded protein stock comprising 8.75 µM protein, 14 mM LUVs (90% DMPC + 10% DMPE), 50 mM citrate buffer pH 5.0, and 0.8 M GdnHCl, was incubated overnight at 28 °C with rotational mixing at 1100 rpm. In the first step of the interrupted unfolding measurements, the folded protein stock was diluted 3.5-fold with 4.0 M GdnHCl (final concentration in the delay loop) to initiate unfolding. The reaction was aged for defined delay times (tage). Unfolding was interrupted by a 6-fold dilution of this reaction with buffer (final GdnHCl in the cuvette was 0.67 M). The refolding kinetics were captured using a change in Trp fluorescence (as described above), and three technical replicates were recorded at 28 °C for each tage. Each decay profile was processed independently by fitting to a triple exponential rise function (Eq. 5a), to derive kf1, kf2 and kf3 for each tage.

In vivo yeast survival assays

Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain YPH499 with the chromosomal copy of SAM50 deleted (YPH499 sam50::ade2 [Yep352-MET25pr-Sam50-CYC1t]; MATa ura3-52 lys2-801_amber ade2-101_ochre trp1-Δ63 his3-Δ200 leu2-Δ1) complemented by the YEpSAM50 plasmid expressing Sam50 constitutively (control)26, was a kind gift from Dr. Nils Wiedemann, University of Freiburg, Germany. The shuffle strain (also obtained from Dr. Wiedemann) was created by the Wiedemann laboratory in collaboration with Dr. Bernard Guiard (Center de Génétique Moléculaire, CNRS, France)26. pFL39 E. coli–yeast shuttle vector carrying the SAM50 gene11 was also provided by Dr. Nils Wiedemann. Here, the yeast SAM50 gene is expressed constitutively and in low copy numbers by being cloned between its native promotor and native terminator sequences11. pFL39–SAM50Δ120 was generated by overlapping PCR43. In addition, point mutants were generated both for SAM50FL and SAM50Δ120 by transfer-PCR43 or cloned from pET3a–SAM50 to pFL39 using assembly PCR. All genetic manipulations were confirmed in pET3a and pFL39 by Sanger sequencing.

The pFL39-SAM50 full-length, Δ120, and all mutant variants were transformed in the YPH499 sam50::ade2 strain26, followed by plasmid shuffling on 5-fluoroorotic acid (5-FOA) media10. Cells that successfully underwent plasmid shuffling were identified by their selective growth only in yeast synthetic dropout media w/o Trp (these cells failed to grow in dropout media w/o Trp and Ura).

Serial spot dilution assays to measure survival of YPH499 sam50::ade2 (SAM50 deletion strain) carrying pFL39-SAM50 were carried out using the drop test44. Briefly, cells were grown in the synthetic dropout medium w/o Trp until an OD600 = 1.5 ± 0.1 was achieved. Cells were harvested, washed twice with milliQ, and resuspended to an OD600 = 0.5. Cells were then serially diluted 10–fold (fold-dilution = 0, 10, 100, 1000, 10000) (Fig. 1i) and 25–fold (Supplementary Fig. S27), and spotted on (i) YPD (1% (w/v) yeast extract, 2% (w/v) bactopeptone, 2% (w/v) glucose), (ii) YPG (1% (w/v) yeast extract, 2% (w/v) bactopeptone, 3% (w/v) glycerol), and (iii) YPDA (1% (w/v) yeast extract, 2% (w/v) bactopeptone, 2% (w/v) glucose, 40 mM acetic acid) plates additionally containing 2% agar, and incubated at 25 °C, 30 °C, and 37 °C. Plates were imaged at 48 h (YPD), 55/72 h (YPG), 72 h (YPDA). Here, the YPH499 sam50::ade2 strain transformed with the yeast episomal plasmid YEpSAM50FL (expressing SAM50FL constitutively) was used as the control. Complementation of Control (YPH499 sam50::ade2 with YEpSAM50FL) was then done with pFL39-SAM50FL (expressing full-length SAM50 constitutively), followed by curing of YEpSAM50FL. Similarly, complementation of Control (YPH499 sam50::ade2 with YEpSAM50FL) was done with pFL39-SAM50Δ120 (expressing POTRA-less SAM50 constitutively), followed by curing of YEpSAM50FL.
