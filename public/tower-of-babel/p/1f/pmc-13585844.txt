Cardiac Arrests During Inpatient Gastrointestinal Endoscopic Procedures: A United States Nationwide Assessment
Authors: Spencer R Goble, David Wong Arias, Joseph A Akambase, Thomas M Leventhal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMajor cardiac events can complicate endoscopic procedures. However, the true incidence and outcomes of these events are not well defined.AimsWe aimed to define the true incidence of cardiac arrest during inpatient endoscopic procedures and the associated outcomes.MethodsWe performed a cross-sectional retrospective analysis of the National Inpatient Sample from 2016 to 2023. Endoscopic intraprocedural cardiac arrests were identified using ICD-10 diagnostic and procedural codes. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess patient and procedural factors associated with cardiac arrest. Outcomes among patients experiencing endoscopic intraprocedural cardiac arrest were compared with those of all other cases of in-hospital cardiac arrest.ResultsAmong 11,137,008 inpatient endoscopic procedures, 1,110 were complicated by cardiac arrest, corresponding to one cardiac arrest per 10,125 procedures. Among assessed patient factors, congestive heart failure had the strongest association with cardiac arrest (aOR = 2.78, 95% CI 2.04–3.78; p < 0.001). Compared with upper endoscopy, colonoscopy was associated with a lower risk of cardiac arrest (aOR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.36–0.69; p < 0.001), while endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography had a similar risk (aOR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.49–1.15; p = 0.183). In-hospital mortality was lower among patients with endoscopy-associated cardiac arrest compared with other cases of in-hospital cardiac arrest (30.6% vs. 71.0%; aOR-0.17, 95% CI 0.13–0.23; p < 0.001).ConclusionsIntraprocedural cardiac arrest is an uncommon complication of inpatient endoscopy. Upper endoscopy carries a higher risk than colonoscopy, and patients with congestive heart failure are at increased risk. Outcomes following endoscopy-associated cardiac arrest are more favorable than for other in-hospital cardiac arrests.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10620-026-10094-6.Keywords: Cardiac arrest, Endoscopy, Gastrointestinal, Inpatients, Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, Anesthesia, Intravenous

Introduction

Endoscopy is an essential screening, diagnostic, and therapeutic tool commonly used in modern practice: with over 20 million endoscopic procedures performed in the United States every year [1, 2]. While generally considered safe as an interventional procedure, there are several known potential complications of endoscopy. Bleeding, mucosal perforation, and, in the case of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), pancreatitis are all frequently cited complications [3–6]. Adverse cardiopulmonary events, most commonly driven by sedation-related side effects, can also complicate endoscopic procedures and have been estimated to comprise over one half of all complications during endoscopy [5, 7, 8].

Among cardiopulmonary complications, cardiac arrest represents the most catastrophic adverse event that could occur with endoscopic procedures. Understanding the risk and outcomes of this devastating complication is important to endoscopists and patients alike. For endoscopists, a comprehensive understanding of procedural risk is needed to determine whether a procedure is overall beneficial for a given patient. This warrants particular attention in the inpatient setting, where patients frequently have a high comorbidity burden that may place them at increased risk for cardiac events [9, 10]. For patients, an understanding of procedural risks is necessary to provide truly informed consent [11]. Additionally, for patients with do-not-resuscitate (DNR) orders, the decision of whether to reverse their DNR status for an endoscopic procedure should be informed by an understanding of the actual risk of cardiac arrest and the outcomes for those who arrest.

The rate and outcomes of inpatient cardiac arrest during endoscopic procedures in the United States remain poorly defined. While prior studies have assessed cardiopulmonary complications of endoscopy, there has not been a contemporary analysis capable of providing generalizable estimates for the United States inpatient population [12, 13]. To address this, we utilized the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) to analyze inpatient endoscopic procedures. We report the incidence of intraprocedural cardiac arrest among patients undergoing endoscopy and the subsequent outcomes for these patients.

Methods

Study Design and Database Description

This was a retrospective cross-sectional analysis of hospitalizations within the NIS from 2016 to 2023. The NIS is the largest all-payer inpatient database in the United States and captures approximately 20% of all U.S. hospitalizations using a systematic, stratified sampling design. The NIS is managed by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ). Standardized weighting procedures supplied by AHRQ were applied in accordance with recommended guidelines to generate national estimates representative of the U.S. inpatient population.

The NIS includes demographic and clinical data for each hospitalization, including in-hospital mortality, length of stay (LOS), and cost in U.S. dollars. Each hospitalization contains one primary diagnosis (the condition chiefly responsible for admission) and up to 39 secondary diagnoses, defined using ICD-10 diagnostic codes. Billable procedures are recorded using ICD-10 procedure codes and include the hospital day on which each procedure occurred. The NIS is publicly available and deidentified, and Institutional Review Board approval was waived.

Study Sample and Variables

Adult hospitalizations from 2016 to 2023 with a cardiac arrest occurring during an endoscopic procedure were identified. Endoscopic procedures were defined using relevant ICD-10 procedure codes and categorized as upper endoscopy, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, or colonoscopy (Table S1). Endoscopic ultrasound was not analyzed as a separate category because ICD-10 procedure codes do not reliably indicate its use. Additionally, ICD-10 procedure codes do not reliably distinguish the extent of lower endoscopy; therefore, all lower endoscopy codes were included in the colonoscopy group and a separate flexible sigmoidoscopy group was not created.

Inclusion criteria required documentation of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) on the same hospital day as an endoscopic procedure along with a diagnostic code for intraprocedural cardiac arrest. Hospitalizations with missing data for the day of CPR or the day of endoscopy were excluded (n = 105). Remaining hospitalizations were manually reviewed to identify other procedures performed on the day of cardiac arrest. Incident hospitalizations were excluded if another procedure occurred on the same day as endoscopy and could reasonably have caused an intraprocedural cardiac arrest (n = 130). For example, hospitalizations with both endoscopy and exploratory laparotomy documented on the same day were excluded. Procedures resulting in exclusion are listed in the supplemental materials (Table S2).

For comparison, a cohort of all hospitalizations with an endoscopic procedure was created. Hospitalizations excluded from the endoscopic cardiac arrest cohort were also excluded from this endoscopy cohort. Additionally, a cohort of all in-hospital cardiac arrest hospitalizations, defined by the presence of an ICD-10 procedure code for CPR, was constructed for comparison. The same exclusion criteria were applied to this cohort.

Study Objectives

The primary objective was to determine the frequency and outcomes of cardiac arrest during inpatient endoscopic procedures. Outcomes included in-hospital mortality, need for renal replacement therapy (RRT), need for tracheostomy, LOS, and cost. Secondary objectives included identifying patient factors associated with cardiac arrest during endoscopic procedures and comparing outcomes between cardiac arrest occurring during endoscopy and all other in-hospital cardiac arrests. Variables assessed for association with cardiac arrest included age, sex, cirrhosis, coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, gastrointestinal malignancy, gastrointestinal bleeding, sepsis, and type of endoscopic procedure.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are presented as proportions and continuous variables as means. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess factors associated with cardiac arrest during endoscopic procedures, with results reported as odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Multivariable logistic regression was also used to compare clinical outcomes between cardiac arrests occurring during endoscopic procedures and other in-hospital cardiac arrests. LOS was analyzed using multivariable negative binomial regression and restricted to patients surviving to discharge to avoid bias from terminal admissions. Cost was analyzed using a multivariable generalized linear model. LOS and cost results are reported as rate ratios representing arithmetic mean ratios. Age, sex, and Charlson Comorbidity Index were included in all multivariable models due to their known associations with cardiac arrest outcomes [14–16]. All statistical computations were performed using STATA, version 17.0.

Results

Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 1,110 hospitalizations with a cardiac arrest documented during an endoscopic procedure were identified. The mean age of the cohort was 69.2 years. Most patients were male (59.0%) and White (60.2%). The mean Charlson Comorbidity Index was 4.0, reflecting a high burden of comorbidity. Among assessed conditions, congestive heart failure was the most prevalent, present in 46.8% of patients with cardiac arrest.

Upper endoscopy was the most frequently documented procedure and occurred without another endoscopic procedure in 68.9% of hospitalizations. Both upper endoscopy and colonoscopy were documented on the day of cardiac arrest in 8.1% of hospitalizations (Table 1). Over one third of cardiac arrests occurred on the day of admission, and the mean hospital day on which cardiac arrest occurred was 2.9.

Clinical Factors Associated with Cardiac Arrest During an Endoscopic Procedure

Across the full study population, an endoscopic procedure was documented in 11,137,008 hospitalizations, corresponding to approximately one cardiac arrest for every 10,125 endoscopic procedures. On univariable logistic regression analysis, age ≥ 75 years, coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and sepsis were each significantly associated with intraprocedural cardiac arrest (Table S3). Female sex and colonoscopy, when compared with upper endoscopy, were associated with lower rates of cardiac arrest.

After adjustment for age, sex, and Charlson Comorbidity Index, the following conditions remained independently associated with intraprocedural cardiac arrest: coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and sepsis (Fig. 1). Among these factors, congestive heart failure demonstrated the strongest association with cardiac arrest during endoscopy in both univariable analysis (OR = 3.90, 95% CI 2.99–5.10, p < 0.001) and multivariable analysis (aOR = 2.78, 95% CI 2.04–3.78, p < 0.001). Despite this strong relative association, the absolute risk difference associated with congestive heart failure was small, corresponding to an increase of 0.2 cardiac arrests per 1,000 endoscopic procedures.

Outcomes Compared with Other In-Hospital Cardiac Arrests

During the study period, 1,081,565 hospitalizations included an in-hospital cardiac arrest. Cardiac arrest occurring during an endoscopic procedure accounted for approximately one in every 975 in-hospital cardiac arrests. Compared with all other in-hospital cardiac arrests, patients who experienced cardiac arrest during an endoscopic procedure had substantially lower mortality on the day of arrest (13.5% vs. 41.0%; OR = 0.22, 95% CI 0.15–0.33, p < 0.001) as well as lower overall in-hospital mortality (30.6% vs. 71.0%; OR = 0.18, 95% CI 0.14–0.24, p < 0.001) (Table S4). These mortality differences remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (Table 2).

Rates of renal replacement therapy and tracheostomy did not differ significantly between patients with cardiac arrest during an endoscopic procedure and those with other in-hospital cardiac arrests. Among patients who survived to discharge, mean LOS was shorter in the endoscopic cardiac arrest group (10.7 vs. 15.0 days, p < 0.001). Hospitalization costs were also significantly lower in this group (p < 0.05).

Discussion

In this United States nationwide assessment, spanning eight years and including over 11 million endoscopic procedures, we found that intraprocedural cardiac arrest complicated one in every 10,125 procedures. The majority (69.4%) of those who experienced a cardiac arrest during endoscopy survived to discharge. Our results, generalizable to the entire United States inpatient population, have considerable implications for the assessment and discussion of risks of endoscopic procedures.

Our finding of roughly one cardiac arrest per every 10,000 procedures falls within the range of previously reported rates. In a study using the Clinical Outcomes Research Initiative database, Sharma et al. found CPR documented in 0.6 per 10,000 procedures [13]. Notably, the cohort evaluated by Sharma et al. was predominantly comprised outpatient procedures who received conscious sedation [13]. This may explain the higher rate of cardiac arrest noted in our analysis, as complication rates tend to be higher in inpatient endoscopy versus outpatient [17, 18]. On the contrary, a single-center retrospective analysis of 73,029 outpatient procedures by Goudra et al. found a cardiac arrest rate of 3.92 per 10,000 procedures [12]. However, an important point of difference is that cardiac arrest was defined by any intraprocedural arrest or arrest in the post-procedure area, which may explain the increased rate [12]. Additionally, the study was a single-center assessment with only 20 recorded cardiac arrests, which does limit generalizability [12].

Of note, in our assessment, as well as those by Sharma et al. and Goudra et al., cardiac arrest was defined in part by the documentation of CPR [12, 13]. In this design, patients who experienced cardiac arrest during endoscopy would not be captured if CPR was not attempted. It has previously been reported that between 6 and 16% of inpatients have a DNR order [19, 20]. However, in a national survey of gastroenterologists, the majority of gastroenterologists reported reversing a DNR order to full code for over 75% of procedures [21]. Given this reported high rate of code status reversal, the number of cardiac arrests events not captured in our work is likely minimal.

Pre-existing cardiac disease was strongly associated with cardiac arrest in our cohort: those with congestive heart failure having a nearly fourfold increased risk of cardiac arrest. This is unsurprising as pre-existing cardiac disease has been consistently identified as a risk factor for cardiovascular complications across a wide variety of procedures [22, 23]. Patients with congestive heart failure can be sensitive to the hemodynamic changes associated with sedation leading to increased rates of intraprocedural hypotension [24, 25]. They are also at risk for pulmonary edema with subsequent hypoxic arrest [26]. While the relative increase in cardiac arrests in those with pre-existing cardiac disease appeared quite impressive, it is important to note that cardiac arrest during endoscopy is rare, and the absolute risk difference is quite low, with just an increase of 0.2 per 1,000 procedures in those with congestive heart failure.

Cardiac arrest events were increased in upper endoscopies compared to colonoscopies in our cohort. This is in contrast to the study by Sharma et al. which found higher rates of cardiac events during colonoscopies [13]. Several factors may have contributed to this. Respiratory failure, ultimately leading to cardiac arrest, may be increased in upper endoscopies secondary to aspiration events. Sedation differences also may have contributed. We hypothesize that patients undergoing upper endoscopy were more likely to receive propofol-based sedation than those who underwent colonoscopy and that this may have contributed to the increased risk in upper endoscopies. Sharma et al. only evaluated patients who received conscious/moderate sedation [13]. Goudra et al., on the other hand, evaluated both patients who received conscious/moderate sedation and those who received propofol-based sedation and found the upper endoscopies had an increased rate of cardiac arrests compared to colonoscopies [12]. Every cardiac arrest in the upper endoscopy group from Goudra et al. received propofol sedation, supporting our hypothesis that type of sedation contributes to cardiac arrest rates among different endoscopic procedures [12]. Unfortunately, as the NIS does not provide details on the sedation received, we are unable to further explore this theory.

For patients who had a cardiac arrest during an endoscopic procedure, in-hospital mortality was less than half the mortality in all other cases of in-hospital cardiac arrest. There is precedence for this finding as perioperative and operative mortalities for cardiac arrest have previously been shown to be lower than other manners of in-hospital cardiac arrest [27–29]. We suspect that the improved outcome may be due to more reversible causes being the underlying etiology and faster initiation of CPR. Early initiation of CPR is crucial to survival in cases of cardiac arrest and patients undergoing endoscopy likely benefit from their cardiac arrest being witnessed with subsequent prompt initiation of CPR [30–32].

Our study benefits from its large, generalizable inpatient population. Several limitations are worth noting. The NIS is an administrative database and does not include narrative assessments that would allow for identification of the precise cause of cardiac arrest. We are unable to determine what type of sedation patients received, the experience level of the sedation provider, and the ASA class of patients. Use of the Charlson Comorbidity Index did allow us to adjust our assessments for general patient comorbidity burden; however, this does introduce the potential for collinearity or partial overadjustment in our assessment of individual comorbidities such as congestive heart failure. The NIS does not include procedure duration, location of the procedure, indication of the procedure (therapeutic vs. diagnostic) or urgency of the procedure (emergency vs. elective). Without more detailed information on the procedure itself, we cannot account for overall procedural complexity. Our inclusion criteria, requiring both a diagnostic code for intraprocedural cardiac arrest and CPR on the same day as endoscopy with no other potential causative procedures, were quite strict. While this likely leads to some underestimation of the true rate of cardiac arrest during endoscopic procedures, we do feel that it overall improves the validity of our results to ensure we are truly capturing cardiac arrests that occur during endoscopic procedures.

In the first United States nationwide assessment of cardiac arrest occurring during inpatient endoscopic procedures, we found that one in every 10,125 procedures is complicated by an intraprocedural cardiac arrest. Encouragingly, 69.4% of these patients survive discharge. These results provide valuable insight into the risks of endoscopic procedures and can guide risk and benefit discussions with patients at high risk for cardiac events.
