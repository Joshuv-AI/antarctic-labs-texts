Experience of Australian rheumatologists managing polymyalgia rheumatica: results from a national survey
Authors: Jessica L. Leung, Victor Yang, Jem V. Ninan, Carlee Ruediger, David F. L. Liew, Russell R. C. Buchanan, Helen I. Keen, Catherine L. Hill, Claire E. Owen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolymyalgia rheumatica is the commonest inflammatory condition affecting adults over 50 years of age, however it has been under‐researched and management varies in everyday clinical practice.AimThis national survey of rheumatologists and trainees managing polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) in Australia sought to determine current diagnostic and management approaches and establish a baseline for future development of a disease‐specific clinical care standard.MethodsFollowing ethics approval, an online survey was circulated to the membership of the Australian Rheumatology Association. Relevant demographic information about respondents was collected, along with answers to multiple‐choice questions on the clinical presentation, diagnosis, management and prognosis of PMR. Free‐text responses were also permitted at the end of each section. The survey remained open between 25 March 2022 and 11 November 2022. Data analysis involved descriptive statistics for multiple‐choice questions and thematic analysis for free‐text responses.ResultsSeventy‐nine clinicians completed the survey, of whom the majority were qualified rheumatologists who regularly managed patients with PMR. Most respondents relied upon clinical diagnosis, but some did not recognise peripheral joint involvement or normal inflammatory markers as possible features of PMR. Access to imaging investigations like 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography combined with computed tomography was limited but desirable in circumstances of diagnostic uncertainty. Management predominantly involved glucocorticoid monotherapy; however, long‐term outcomes were diverse and almost every clinician had prescribed a conventional synthetic DMARD for steroid‐sparing purposes. The use of adjunctive physical therapy was inconsistent.ConclusionVariability remains in the diagnosis and management of PMR among Australian clinicians, with gaps in recognition of atypical presentations, imaging access and incorporation of physical therapy, ultimately supporting the need for standardised, evidence‐based clinical care guidelines.

Introduction

Polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) is a common chronic rheumatic disease characterised by abnormal inflammation at key musculotendinous structures throughout the body. 1 Clinically, this results in the patient experience of profound pain and stiffness at the neck, shoulders, lower back, hips and knees. PMR is also closely related to the serious large‐vessel vasculitis, giant cell arteritis (GCA), with an overlap existing between the two conditions in ~25% of cases. 2

Better understanding of PMR's unique pathology has only happened recently courtesy of imaging modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography combined with computed tomography (18F‐FDG PET/CT). 1 In everyday practice, diagnosis remains clinical based upon the patient's reported symptoms and elevation of non‐specific systemic markers of inflammation. 3 Abnormalities detected on ultrasound at the shoulders and/or hips have been demonstrated to marginally improve the sensitivity of the 2012 EULAR/ACR classification criteria 4 ; however, a gold‐standard test to aid practitioners evaluating suspected cases of PMR in the community does not yet exist.

Unlike analogous chronic rheumatic diseases, PMR is still managed with glucocorticoid monotherapy in the form of a weaning course of oral prednisolone. 5 Little high‐level evidence exists to support the routine use of conventional synthetic disease‐modifying anti‐rheumatic drugs (csDMARDs). Instead, agents such as methotrexate or leflunomide are introduced for their steroid‐sparing effects following repeated disease relapse or serious glucocorticoid‐induced complications.

In Australia, no medication is currently approved by the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) for the treatment of PMR, although sarilumab, an interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) receptor blocker, became the first drug listed by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in the United States for the indication of relapsing PMR following the landmark SAPHYR trial. 6 Given there has been an increasing number of randomised controlled trials investigating novel therapeutic agents since that time, it seems probable that PMR's antiquated treatment paradigm will soon be revised.

This study therefore aimed to delineate the current diagnostic and therapeutic approach of Australian rheumatologists and trainees to a suspected case of PMR. The results are intended to provide insights into the existing quality of care available to patients living with PMR and provide the basis for future development of a disease‐specific clinical care standard.

Methods

An online survey was developed by a group of clinician‐researchers with expertise in PMR (Supporting Information). Survey questions explored how individual clinicians approached the diagnosis and management of patients with suspected PMR and provided the opportunity for free‐text responses at the end of each section. Relevant demographic information about the practitioners completing the survey was also collected (gender, clinical experience, type of practice, frequency of managing patients with PMR in the past 12 months).

Following ethics approval from the Central Adelaide Local Health Network Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/17/TQEH/50), a link to the survey was circulated to all rheumatologists and trainee members of the Australian Rheumatology Association via the organisation's monthly e‐bulletin. Each respondent provided online consent. Study data were collected using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) tool, 7 , 8 hosted and managed by the Central Adelaide Local Health Network. Responses were collected between 25 March 2022 and 11 November 2022.

Multiple‐choice questions were analysed with Stata 16.1 to generate descriptive statistics. Free‐text responses were examined using an inductive approach to thematic analysis. First, two researchers (J. L. Leung and C. E. Owen) familiarised themselves with the data and independently generated codes, which were then refined iteratively through discussion until a final consensus was reached.

Results

Responses were received from 79 clinicians, of which 57 (72%) answered the survey to completion. Most respondents were qualified rheumatologists (89%), with the remaining 11% being advanced trainee doctors completing specialist training in rheumatology. The majority of respondents reviewed a patient with PMR at least once per fortnight (75%) (Table 1).

Clinical presentation and diagnosis of PMR

Bilateral shoulder involvement was felt to be ‘always’ present in untreated PMR by only 23% of respondents, although the majority (76%) did believe this occurred ‘most of the time’. Bilateral hip involvement was thought to be present ‘most of the time’ or ‘always’ by 56% and 6% respectively. Approximately one in three clinicians believed that elevated inflammatory markers were prerequisite to make a diagnosis of PMR. Conversely, 59% did not feel this was necessary but acknowledged that PMR with normal inflammatory markers was an uncommon entity. The majority (80%) of respondents did not believe that peripheral joint involvement precluded a diagnosis of PMR, but 13% believed that it did and 7% were unsure.

Symptoms at the hands and wrists were the predominant polarising issue in the free‐text responses. Some clinicians expressed doubt about the accuracy of conventional teaching that peripheral synovitis excluded a PMR diagnosis, while others remained steadfast in this approach, considering hand and/or wrist involvement to be an indication of PMR‐onset seronegative rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Frequent reference was made to the presentation of PMR being routinely classic, with atypical features such as normal inflammatory markers considered rare.

The majority (79%) of clinicians reported not routinely arranging any imaging when making a diagnosis of PMR, but 72% believed that imaging could be useful if diagnostic uncertainty was present. Imaging modalities that were commonly utilised included ultrasound (52%), 18F‐FDG PET/CT (39%), X‐ray (34%) and MRI (24%).

Free‐text responses emphasised the value of imaging in improving diagnostic clarity, particularly in cases of atypical symptomatology or a suboptimal response to prednisolone therapy. The ability of imaging to exclude alternate diagnoses, specifically malignancy or an overlap with large‐vessel GCA, was highlighted. Several respondents additionally expressed frustration regarding their inability to access 18F‐FDG PET/CT, necessitating the use of alternative imaging modalities instead.

Management of PMR

The median prednisolone dose prescribed at treatment initiation was 15 mg (range 10–25 mg). There was consensus (75% agreement) to wean this by 2.5 mg per month until reaching a daily dose of 10 mg, followed by reductions of 1 mg per month. Free‐text responses suggested similar approaches of an initial more rapid wean followed by a longer, slower taper with variable increments. Two respondents reported a 1‐ or 2‐year pause in weaning once a prednisolone dose of 5 mg daily is reached, with one respondent opting to continue this dose indefinitely in patients over 75 years of age based upon their anecdotal experience of a high relapse rate in this demographic. In the case of relapse, 59% of clinicians increased prednisolone to the last effective dose prescribed, while 27% tailored the dose according to symptom severity.

Significant heterogeneity existed about the likelihood of physical therapy being recommended to complement pharmacological management, either by providing advice around exercise or referral to an appropriate allied health practitioner. Approximately one in four clinicians reported never having done this, while another one in four specifically addressed this for >75% of patients with PMR.

Almost every clinician (98%) reported having prescribed a csDMARD as a steroid‐sparing agent for PMR, with half noting that this was required in at least 25% of patients (Fig. 1). Recurrent relapse (≥2) was the main indication nominated by prescribers (49%), with 79% of respondents selecting concern for steroid‐related adverse events as their ‘other’ reason for utilising these medications. Methotrexate was considered the first‐line choice, with a median target dose of 20 mg weekly (range 10–25 mg). Having said this, most respondents (86%) indicated that there was a lack of quality evidence to support its use in PMR.

Leflunomide was recognised as another appropriate steroid‐sparing option by 27% of respondents, although a lack of research to support this approach was acknowledged once again. Other medications that clinicians had prescribed to manage PMR included hydroxychloroquine, tocilizumab, azathioprine and cyclophosphamide.

Prognosis of PMR

Clinicians most frequently identified steroid‐related side effects as the biggest issue affecting PMR patients, followed closely by musculoskeletal pain and stiffness (Fig. 2). Fatigue and sleep disturbance were also recognised as common problems by 79% and 57% of clinicians respectively.

Discussion

To the authors' knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the experience of rheumatologists and trainees managing PMR. Results indicate that many clinicians continue to characterise PMR's clinical presentation as remarkably uniform and its natural history as self‐limiting. However, most respondents had prescribed csDMARDs to PMR patients for their steroid‐sparing effect and identified glucocorticoid‐related side effects as the primary issue affecting this population. Free‐text responses further highlighted difficulties clinicians faced making a PMR diagnosis in patients reporting hand and/or wrist symptoms and their frustrations about inadequate access to imaging modalities like 18F‐FDG PET/CT when important differentials like concomitant GCA, infection or malignancy were being considered.

Peripheral joint involvement proved a particularly contentious topic, with 13% of respondents indicating that the presence of hand and/or wrist symptoms precluded them from making a diagnosis of PMR. Comments indicated that these clinicians would instead classify the patient as PMR‐onset seronegative RA. In fact, distal manifestations occur in up to half of all PMR patients, with synovitis and tenosynovitis appreciated on MRI. 9 , 10 , 11 Remitting seronegative symmetrical synovitis with pitting oedema (RS3PE) syndrome has also been recognised as part of the clinical spectrum of PMR. 12 Notably, the cytokine profile of so‐called PMR‐onset RA has been demonstrated to more closely resemble PMR, suggesting a shared pathogenesis, than elderly‐onset cases of RA. 13 The positive predictive value of peripheral joint involvement for RA among older patients with bilateral shoulder symptoms is similarly poor. 14 In such circumstances, imaging can assist in differentiating between the two diseases, with an extra‐capsular pattern of inflammation observed on MRI and 18F‐FDG uptake at key musculoskeletal sites on PET/CT corresponding with PMR's characteristic musculotendinous pathology. 15 , 16 Accordingly, 72% of survey respondents agreed with the use of imaging in cases of diagnostic uncertainty.

Clinical presentations accompanied by atypical features similarly posed a more substantial challenge. A third of clinicians indicated that they expected all patients with PMR to exhibit elevated systemic inflammatory markers at the time of diagnosis; however, the available literature previously established that 4%–22% of cases will have a normal ESR and/or CRP. 4 , 17 , 18 While this group does not represent the majority, it is crucial for clinicians to remain open to the possibility of a PMR diagnosis and not automatically disregard it based on this assumption.

The approach to initial treatment of PMR was relatively uniform. Prednisolone tapering by most clinicians was in line with the 2010 BSR/BHPR and 2015 ACR/EULAR guidelines, indicating that adoption of this approach has improved over time. 5 , 19 , 20 The impact of glucocorticoid‐related side effects upon patients with PMR was keenly appreciated, with a majority of respondents having prescribed csDMARDs as steroid‐sparing agents. Of note, almost half of the clinicians surveyed indicated this approach was necessary in some 25%–50% of patients. These figures are consistent with other international studies and ultimately refute the characterisation of PMR as a completely steroid‐responsive, monophasic condition. 20 , 21 , 22 In accordance with treatment guidelines, the main indications nominated for the use of a steroid‐sparing medication were disease relapse or steroid‐related side effects.

Methotrexate and leflunomide represented the most frequently utilised csDMARDs for steroid‐sparing in PMR, despite most clinicians acknowledging the lack of evidence supporting their efficacy. Five randomised controlled trials have evaluated the effectiveness of methotrexate to date, yielding mixed and therefore inconclusive results. 23 , 24 Leflunomide has never been the subject of a randomised controlled trial in PMR, with supporting evidence limited to a cohort study and case series. 23 In a large, multi‐centre observational cohort by Vinicki et al., leflunomide was recently found to outperform methotrexate in achieving complete withdrawal of glucocorticoid therapy. 25 In recent years, there has been an increasing number of successful phase 2 and 3 clinical trials evaluating biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs in PMR, most notably sarilumab and recently secukinumab. 6 , 23 , 26 However, these agents are not currently available to clinicians for these indications in Australia. Furthermore, trials have variably included new or, more commonly, relapsing disease; therefore, uncertainty remains regarding the optimal timing of initiation.

Physical therapy was inconsistently incorporated into the management of PMR despite the 2015 ACR/EULAR management guidelines recommending an individualised exercise program ‘aimed at the maintenance of muscle mass and function and reducing risk of falls especially in older persons on long‐term glucocorticoids as well as in frail patients’. 5 Though there is a lack of disease‐specific clinical data to substantiate this approach, emerging evidence now indicates persistent disability among treated PMR patients compared with controls for key functional outcomes like gait speed, muscle strength and balance. 27 Rates of frailty (5.6% cf. 0% for controls) and pre‐frailty (60.7% cf. 34.4% of controls) are also substantially higher, with another recent study documenting sarcopenia in 26% of American patients with PMR. 28 The development and testing of an exercise intervention specific to PMR therefore represents a significant research priority within this field.

Substantial disagreement among clinicians was noted regarding the prognosis of PMR patients, both in terms of relapse rates and the likelihood of achieving glucocorticoid‐free remission. As outlined, the traditional view of PMR as a short‐lived, benign disease entity is being increasingly challenged by the available literature, as is the duration of glucocorticoid therapy necessary to absolve it completely. Population‐based data now suggest a median time to permanent prednisolone discontinuation of 5.95 years, with 42% of patients still receiving glucocorticoid therapy after 10 years; average cumulative glucocorticoid exposure 2 and 5 years following diagnosis was 4.0 ± 3.5 and 6.3 ± 9.8 g respectively. 29 It accordingly seems unsurprising that survey respondents’ perceptions regarding long‐term outcomes for PMR were so varied given the apparent disconnect that exists between conventional teaching and modern clinical experience.

Although not addressed by either the 2010 BSR/BHPR or 2015 ACR/EULAR management guidelines, fatigue and sleep disturbance were also recognised by clinicians as pertinent issues. 5 , 19 Importantly, this aligns with key priorities expressed by patients and follows recent cohort studies that have demonstrated significantly increased rates of severe fatigue and poor sleep quality among PMR cases compared to age‐ and sex‐matched controls. 30 , 31 , 32 Despite the high regard of patients and clinicians, less than one in six PMR clinical trials have included patient‐reported outcome measures for fatigue to date. 33 Moving forward, it is imperative that complications of the disease and its treatment like these be thoroughly evaluated, particularly when investigating novel therapeutic agents.

There are several limitations to our study. Results only reflect the management of PMR by rheumatologists and rheumatology trainees, but research indicates 40%–75% of PMR patients are treated solely in primary care. 34 , 35 Further, referral to a rheumatologist is often limited to diagnostic confirmation or to cases that have experienced recurrent disease relapse or glucocorticoid‐related adverse events, a subgroup that may not be generalisable to the broader PMR population. Finally, we cannot exclude selection bias arising from clinicians with an academic interest in PMR being more likely to respond to the online survey.

Conclusion

In summary, this survey of the experience of Australian rheumatologists managing PMR has shed significant light on the complexities faced by clinicians in everyday clinical practice. Many responses challenged established perceptions of PMR as a benign and self‐limiting condition, reflecting recent developments in our understanding of this common chronic rheumatic disease. There was broad agreement about the need for improved access to imaging in circumstances of diagnostic uncertainty and appropriate recognition of key priorities for patients. While good adherence to existing treatment recommendations was noted among respondents, the overall inadequacy of current management strategies remained abundantly clear. It is hoped that these insights will provide a starting point for future improvements in the standard of care available to patients living with PMR in Australia.
