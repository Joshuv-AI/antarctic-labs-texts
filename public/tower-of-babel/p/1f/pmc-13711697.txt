Association of the preoperative MRI LAMBDA sign with long-term patient-reported outcomes after isolated ATFL surgery: a ten-year retrospective cohort study
Authors: Pengtao Shi, Shengxuan Li, Chen Yang, Yu Wei, Zhidong Zhao, Qi Quan, Yanpeng Zhao, Min Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe LAMBDA sign has been reported to be associated with syndesmotic-region injury on MRI, but its relationship with long-term outcomes after isolated ATFL surgery remains unclear.ObjectiveTo evaluate the association between the preoperative MRI LAMBDA sign and long-term patient-reported outcomes in patients undergoing isolated anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) repair or reconstruction.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients who underwent ATFL repair or reconstruction at a single institution between 2014 and 2016. A transparent cohort flow was documented, comparing included and excluded patients to assess for selection bias. The primary endpoint was the Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS) at final follow-up. At surgery, all patients were diagnosed clinically as having isolated ATFL injuries without chondral lesions, and the syndesmosis was not examined. Preoperative MRIs were reanalyzed, and patients were classified according to the presence or absence of the LAMBDA sign. LAMBDA-positive patients were additionally stratified by distal tibiofibular joint effusion height (DTJEH ≥ 8 mm vs. < 8 mm). Baseline characteristics and long-term patient-reported outcomes (FAOS, Karlsson, Tegner, VAS, satisfication) were collected. Adjusted effects, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were reported. Rater reliability (ICC/κ) for LAMBDA and DTJEH was assessed with CIs and exact methods.ResultsSeventy-nine patients were included (mean follow-up, 112.2 ± 8.0 months): 46 LAMBDA-positive and 33 LAMBDA-negative. LAMBDA-positive patients had significantly higher preinjury Tegner scores compared with the negative group (6.0 [6.0–7.0] vs. 5.0 [4.5–6.5]; P < 0.05), but worse long-term outcomes. Specifically, FAOS was lower in the LAMBDA-positive group (80.0 [75.0–85.0] vs. 86.0 [81.5–88.0]; mean difference, −6.1; 95% CI, −9.3 to −3.0; P < 0.001), as were Karlsson scores (80.0 [75.0–85.0] vs. 85.0 [84.0–90.0]; mean difference, −6.1; 95% CI, −9.5 to −2.7; P = 0.001). Additionally, LAMBDA-positive patients showed greater decline in Tegner activity levels (2.0 [1.0–3.0] vs. 1.0 [0.5–2.0]; P < 0.05). However, despite these statistically significant differences, they did not exceed established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds for FAOS and Karlsson scores (FAOS: 11.7; Karlsson: 10.0).ConclusionA preoperative MRI LAMBDA sign was associated with slightly inferior long-term patient-reported outcomes after isolated ATFL surgery. Given the limitations of static MRI and the lack of intraoperative syndesmotic evaluation, these findings should be viewed as associations rather than causal relationships. Further studies incorporating dynamic or intraoperative assessments are warranted.Level of Evidence: III (retrospective cohort).Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-026-06977-z.

Introduction

Lateral ankle sprains are among the most common musculoskeletal injuries, and concomitant syndesmotic involvement can occur in a subset of cases [1–5]. Syndesmotic injury may coexist with anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) injury and is challenging to diagnose accurately, particularly when functional instability is subtle or dynamic in nature [6, 7]. Although clinical examination and stress radiographs can suggest syndesmotic pathology, these methods have limited sensitivity and specificity, and functional syndesmotic instability may remain undetected [8–10].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can detect structural abnormalities of the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis, though static imaging does not necessarily reflect dynamic instability [11, 12]. One specific MRI feature of interest is the LAMBDA sign, a qualitative finding characterized by fluid or high signal intensity around the syndesmosis that resembles the Greek letter lambda on coronal sequences, indicating regional involvement [13, 14]. Prior studies suggest this sign may reflect edema or hemorrhage in acute injuries, or synovitis, fibrotic changes, and recurrent micro-trauma in chronic cases [13, 14].

Similarly, distal tibiofibular joint effusion height (DTJEH) has been proposed as another relevant MRI feature for evaluating syndesmotic-region injury [15]. However, the temporal behavior and prognostic significance of both the LAMBDA sign and DTJEH remain incompletely understood, especially in cohorts with variable injury chronicity and disparate MRI acquisition timing. Because clinical presentations can be heterogeneous (e.g., acute-on-chronic) and specific injury episodes are often difficult to define uniformly, these MRI findings are best viewed as structural features rather than phase-specific lesion markers.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the association between preoperative MRI features (the LAMBDA sign and DTJEH) and long-term patient-reported outcomes following isolated ATFL repair or reconstruction. By retrospectively re-evaluating preoperative MRIs from a historical cohort of patients treated between 2014 and 2016, we aimed to clarify the potential clinical relevance of these structural imaging features. Importantly, this study focuses on associations with long-term outcomes rather than establishing direct predictive or causal relationships.

Method

Study design and ethics

This was a single-center, retrospective comparative cohort study (level of evidence, Ⅲ). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board of our institution (approval No. S2021-637-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before enrollment.Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Patient enrollment and follow-up

This retrospective single-center cohort study reviewed consecutive patients who underwent isolated anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) repair or reconstruction for lateral ankle instability at our institution between January 2014 and December 2016. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Because this cohort was derived from historical index surgeries performed between 2014 and 2016, occult syndesmotic lesions without frank radiographic diastasis were not routinely explored or stabilized at the time of ATFL surgery. Therefore, it should be emphasized that “isolated” refers to the clinical management strategy at the time of index surgery, not definitive exclusion of all syndesmotic pathology. Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate whether preoperative MRI findings indicative of syndesmotic involvement were associated with long-term outcomes after isolated ATFL surgery, rather than validate functional syndesmotic instability. The presence of a preoperative LAMBDA sign on MRI is treated in this study as a structural imaging feature, and this research is designed to investigate its association with long-term patient-reported outcomes, rather than to determine its independent prognostic value or confirm functional syndesmotic instability.

Patients were eligible if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age 14–65 years; (2) clinical diagnosis of lateral ankle instability requiring ATFL repair or reconstruction ; and (3) availability of a preoperative ankle magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examination obtained within 1 month before surgery. Although a standardized injury-to-MRI interval could not be reliably determined, we recorded the interval from first injury to surgery as the only available surrogate timing variable. Postoperative follow-up duration was analyzed separately.

Patients were excluded if they had concomitant osteochondral lesions of the ankle, no preoperative MRI, loss to final follow-up, or a new severe ankle sprain during follow-up. To reduce confounding from medial-sided instability, patients with clinical or MRI evidence of deltoid ligament injury were also excluded. A ‘new severe ankle sprain’ was operationally defined as a recurrent ankle injury during follow-up that required revision surgery due to failure of conservative treatment. We acknowledge that this definition is based on treatment outcome rather than intrinsic injury severity, which could introduce selection bias if patients with more severe recurrent injury were preferentially excluded. After application of the eligibility criteria, 79 patients were included in the final analysis. The patient selection process and MRI-based grouping workflow are summarized in Fig. 1.

MRI protocol and evaluation

All preoperative MRIs were obtained within one month prior to surgery using a 3.0-T scanner (Magnetom Skyra, Siemens, Germany) in accordance with the institutional ankle MRI protocol. The routine protocol included T1-weighted (repetition time/echo time, 450/13 ms; matrix, 512 × 512) and T2-weighted (repetition time/echo time, 3700/68 ms; matrix, 512 × 512) dual spin-echo sequences.

Because this retrospective cohort included both acute and chronic presentations, a standardized interval from the clinically relevant injury event to MRI acquisition could not be reliably determined. Consequently, “MRI timing” in this study refers to the interval between MRI acquisition and surgery, rather than a uniform injury-to-MRI interval. We acknowledge this as an important limitation, as the presence of the LAMBDA sign or the extent of DTJEH may vary depending on the time elapsed since the initial injury. Therefore, differences observed between groups might partially reflect variations in MRI timing rather than intrinsic injury patterns alone.

Preoperative MRI scans were independently reviewed in a blinded fashion by two experienced readers who were not involved in clinical outcome assessments. These reviewers evaluated the presence or absence of the LAMBDA sign and measured the distal tibiofibular joint effusion height (DTJEH). To measure DTJEH, the coronal image displaying the maximal effusion was selected; a reference line was drawn along the tibial plafond, and a perpendicular line was dropped from the highest point of the effusion to this reference line (Fig. 2). The vertical distance between these two lines was recorded as the DTJEH, with a value of 0 mm assigned if no effusion was present. Any disagreements between the primary readers were resolved by a third senior musculoskeletal radiologist.

To evaluate measurement consistency, inter-rater reliability was assessed for both MRI variables. Agreement for the qualitative LAMBDA sign was quantified using Cohen’s kappa, while reliability for the continuous DTJEH measurement was calculated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), both reported with 95% confidence intervals. Based on these final MRI assessments, patients were primarily categorized into LAMBDA-positive or LAMBDA-negative groups. For a secondary exploratory analysis, the LAMBDA-positive cohort was further subdivided using a prespecified DTJEH threshold of 8 mm (≥ 8 vs. <8 mm).

Surgical management and baseline clinical data

All operations were performed at our institution by a senior orthopedic sports medicine surgeon. The choice between ATFL repair and reconstruction was made according to intraoperative findings and patient-specific clinical considerations. The study cohort was limited to patients undergoing isolated ATFL surgery; no routine syndesmotic exploration, dynamic stress testing, or syndesmotic stabilization was performed at the index procedure in the absence of frank radiographic diastasis.

To comprehensively contextualize these procedures, baseline demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), injured side, time from injury to surgery, and surgical approach (repair vs. reconstruction). Relevant associated findings documented in the medical record were also reviewed to characterize the injury pattern and reduce confounding from additional ankle pathology.

Outcome measures and follow-up assessment

The primary endpoint was the Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS) at final follow-up [16]. Secondary outcomes included the Karlsson–Peterson Ankle Score [17], visual analog scale (VAS) pain score [18], current Tegner activity scale [19], patient satisfaction, and decline in Tegner activity level.

To systematically evaluate these endpoints, baseline patient-reported outcome measures, including FAOS, Karlsson score, and VAS pain score are collected preoperatively within 1 day before surgery. Preinjury activity level was assessed using the Tegner activity scale at the baseline interview, and current activity level was reassessed at final follow-up. Tegner decline was defined as the difference between the preinjury Tegner score and the current Tegner score at final follow-up. Correspondingly, at the final follow-up, patients completed the FAOS, Karlsson score, VAS pain score, and Tegner activity scale again. Patient satisfaction was also assessed at final follow-up using a 10-point scale ranging from 0 (completely dissatisfied) to 10 (completely satisfied).

These long-term follow-up data were obtained through outpatient review and medical record reassessment. Complications, including recurrent injury, infection, and additional surgery, were recorded. Because standardized serial structural imaging was not available for all patients at long-term follow-up, the present study focused on long-term clinical and patient-reported outcomes rather than radiographic or MRI progression over time.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent-samples t test. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median with interquartile range and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test.

The primary between-group comparison was performed between the LAMBDA-positive and LAMBDA-negative groups. Because baseline height and preinjury Tegner activity level differed between groups, adjusted analyses were additionally performed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to account for these imbalances. Adjusted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals were reported for the principal outcome measures. Within the LAMBDA-positive cohort, DTJEH subgroup comparisons (≥ 8 vs. < 8 mm) were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating because of the limited subgroup sample size.

Correlations between continuous DTJEH values and long-term outcome measures were assessed using Spearman rank correlation analysis. Inter-rater reliability for the binary LAMBDA sign was reported as Cohen’s kappa with 95% confidence intervals, and reliability for continuous DTJEH measurements was reported as the ICC with 95% confidence intervals. All statistical tests were 2-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In accordance with reviewer concerns regarding interpretation of statistically significant but small between-group differences, effect estimates were interpreted together with 95% confidence intervals and minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds where available, rather than on the basis of P values alone.

Result

Of the 145 patients with ankle sprain who underwent isolated ATFL surgery, 79 patients were enrolled in this study. Patients were divided into two main groups based on the presence of the LAMBDA sign in the anterior talofibular ligament region on preoperative MRI (Fig. 2): the LAMBDA positive group (n = 46) and the LAMBDA negative group (n = 33). Within the LAMBDA positive group, patients were further subdivided based on the distal tibiofibular joint effusion height (DTJEH): the DTJEH ≥ 8 mm group (n = 35) and the DTJEH < 8 mm group (n = 11).Some patients underwent postoperative imaging as part of routine clinical care; however, standardized longitudinal imaging suitable for structural outcome analysis was not available for the cohort.

Baseline demographic characteristics were generally similar between groups, except for height and preinjury Tegner scores, which were significantly higher in the LAMBDA-positive group (height, 166.9 ± 8.5 cm vs. 172.2 ± 8.7 cm, p = 0.009; preinjury Tegner, 6.0 [6.0–7.0] vs. 5.0 [4.5–6.5], p = 0.032). No other statistically significant differences were observed (Table 1).The preoperative functional scores for both groups are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Patients in the DTJEH ≥ 8 mm group demonstrated relatively poorer long-term postoperative clinical outcomes, with greater loss of motor function compared to the DTJEH < 8 mm group. DTJEH correlation analysis

Correlation analysis between DTJEH and clinical outcomes revealed important findings. In the Spearman rank correlation analysis, the classification of distal tibiofibular joint effusion height (DTJEH) was not correlated with patient-reported outcomes (P > 0.05 for all). Among all patients with a positive LAMBDA sign, no correlation was observed between the height of the distal tibiofibular joint effusion and clinical outcome scores. This lack of correlation suggests that simply assuming higher effusion height indicates greater injury severity may not be accurate. When the effusion height in negative LAMBDA sign patients was recorded as zero, several clinical outcome scores showed a negative correlation with the effusion height. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of patients who underwent isolated ATFL repair or reconstruction [20], a positive preoperative MRI LAMBDA sign was associated with slightly worse long-term patient-reported outcomes. Specifically, the LAMBDA-positive group had lower FAOS and Karlsson scores and a greater decline in Tegner activity level than the LAMBDA-negative group. However, although these between-group differences were statistically significant, they did not exceed established MCID thresholds, indicating that the long-term impact at the group level was modest.

The MCID analysis is particularly important for clinical interpretation. Although FAOS and Karlsson scores differed significantly, the magnitude of differences remained below MCID thresholds (FAOS: 11.7; Karlsson: 10.0). This suggests that the observed association is modest rather than clinically dramatic. The greater decline in Tegner score may indicate that LAMBDA-positive patients are somewhat less likely to maintain preinjury activity levels, even if their absolute final activity score is similar to LAMBDA-negative patients [20].

The principal implication is therefore narrow. While a positive LAMBDA sign may identify a subgroup with slightly inferior long-term outcomes, our data do not establish causation. The study was not designed to determine whether the LAMBDA sign reflects occult syndesmotic instability, a more complex injury pattern, or other unmeasured factors.

A central issue is the distinction between structural abnormality and functional instability [7, 21]. MRI is highly sensitive to soft-tissue abnormalities around the syndesmosis, but static imaging alone cannot determine pathological motion under physiological load [11, 12]. None of the patients underwent routine arthroscopic or dynamic evaluation at surgery, which limits the ability to infer functional instability. Recent techniques, such as needle arthroscopy, highlight the limitations of relying solely on static MRI [22–25].

Syndesmotic instability is a key determinant of symptoms and long-term prognosis [6, 26, 27]. However, the index surgeries were performed more than a decade ago, when occult syndesmotic pathology was not routinely assessed [28]. Therefore, the retrospective analysis is primarily from an imaging perspective: a positive LAMBDA sign may indicate syndesmotic-region injury, but it should not be interpreted as proof of functional instability. Conversely, a negative LAMBDA sign reduces the likelihood of injury but does not exclude it. After identifying suboptimal outcomes in some patients, we retrospectively analyzed preoperative MRIs to explore associations with structural abnormalities, emphasizing that the study focuses on association, not direct evaluation of functional instability.

The findings should not be overinterpreted. Worse outcomes in LAMBDA-positive patients do not necessarily indicate that the imaging sign itself has independent prognostic value. It may instead mark patients with greater injury complexity or untreated syndesmotic lesions [3, 20]. Because occult lesions were not routinely explored or stabilized, differences may reflect untreated pathology rather than intrinsic prognostic power [6].

Emerging data on AITFL lesion morphology suggest structural syndesmotic injury can be clinically meaningful even without overt functional instability. A positive LAMBDA sign is generally associated with syndesmotic-region injury [5, 13, 14], and combining LAMBDA with distal tibiofibular joint effusion height (DTJEH) may improve detection [15]. Structural injury does not always equate to functional instability but may influence long-term outcomes through altered joint mechanics [29]. MRI-detected syndesmotic abnormalities may thus still be clinically relevant, even if they do not directly indicate instability.

In this context, a positive LAMBDA sign is best interpreted as a structural MRI finding rather than a validated indicator of functional instability. Imaging findings alone cannot fully represent functional status. Our rationale was that semiquantitative MRI assessment might still reflect underlying injury patterns, and the purpose of the analysis was to explore correlations with long-term outcomes.

The higher preinjury Tegner activity level observed in LAMBDA-positive patients may offer context but must be interpreted cautiously. More active patients may have greater repetitive loading and complex injury patterns, or they may delay surgery until damage is more severe. Alternative explanations are plausible, including different functional demands. We deliberately avoid mechanistic claims regarding the origin of the LAMBDA sign.

The clinical implications should remain conservative. A positive LAMBDA sign may raise awareness of a potentially more complex injury pattern. It should not be used alone to indicate syndesmotic instability or guide stabilization [8, 10]. Correlation with symptoms, physical examination, and surgeon judgment remains essential [1, 9]. Intraoperative assessment or dynamic testing may be considered when clinically indicated [12, 23].

Our exploratory DTJEH analysis adds nuance but should not be overstated. In LAMBDA-positive patients, DTJEH ≥ 8 mm was associated with lower FAOS and greater Tegner decline, but no consistent linear relationship with outcomes was observed. Effusion may reflect inflammation, synovial change, prior traction injury, or persistent fluid, rather than ligament insufficiency [15]. DTJEH should therefore be regarded as exploratory, hypothesis-generating, and limited by the small number in the < 8 mm subgroup.

Despite the lack of a standardized injury-to-MRI interval, the two groups showed no significant difference in disease course from initial sprain to surgery. MRI was obtained within 1 month before surgery, so disease course serves only as an approximate surrogate; time-dependent effects cannot be excluded.

Several limitations should be emphasized. First, the study relied entirely on static preoperative MRI without a reference standard for functional syndesmotic instability [11, 23]. Second, MRI timing was not standardized, and systematic differences between groups could not be determined. Third, the cohort was restricted to patients treated with isolated ATFL surgery during a period when occult syndesmotic lesions were not routinely explored or stabilized, introducing treatment and selection bias. Fourth, despite long follow-up, no standardized longitudinal structural assessments were performed; outcomes were based primarily on final follow-up, and preoperative functional scores were reconstructed retrospectively without between-group differences. Fifth, the retrospective single-center design and modest sample size limit generalizability and preclude causal inference. Finally, the outcome measures evaluate overall ankle function and may not isolate deficits specific to the syndesmosis.

Future studies should prospectively integrate structural MRI with dynamic or intraoperative syndesmotic assessment and longitudinal imaging follow-up [30]. Larger studies with multivariable adjustment are needed to determine whether the LAMBDA sign independently contributes prognostic information after accounting for confounders. Accordingly, the apparent association between LAMBDA sign, DTJEH, and outcomes may partly reflect temporal variation in MRI appearance rather than a stable prognostic effect.

In summary, a positive preoperative MRI LAMBDA sign was associated with slightly worse long-term patient-reported outcomes after isolated ATFL surgery, but differences were modest and below established MCID thresholds. The sign should be interpreted as a possible marker of more complex injury rather than definitive evidence of functional syndesmotic instability. Confirmation requires studies incorporating dynamic validation and longitudinal structural assessment.

Conclusion

In patients who underwent isolated ATFL repair or reconstruction, a positive preoperative MRI LAMBDA sign was associated with slightly inferior long-term patient-reported outcomes at approximately 10 years after surgery. Although these differences were statistically significant, they were clinically modest and did not exceed established MCID thresholds. Furthermore, because MRI timing relative to the clinically relevant injury event was not standardized, the LAMBDA sign must be interpreted cautiously. It should be viewed as a preoperative structural imaging feature associated with outcome, rather than as a validated time-independent predictor or definitive evidence of functional syndesmotic instability. Consequently, further prospective studies incorporating standardized imaging timing, dynamic validation, and longitudinal structural follow-up are warranted.
