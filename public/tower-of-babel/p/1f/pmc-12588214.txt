BREAST AI-PLAN: Prompt-Driven AI Assistance for Breast Surgery Planning - A Retrospective Single-center Study
Authors: MARTINA COSSU, LETIZIA CUNIOLO, RAQUEL DIAZ, MARTINO OLIVA, CHIARA CORNACCHIA, FEDERICA MURELLI, FRANCESCA DEPAOLI, MARCO GIPPONI, CECILIA MARGARINO, CHIARA BOCCARDO, SIMONETTA FRANCHELLI, MARIANNA PESCE, REBECCA ALLIEVI, AMANDINE CAUSSE D’AGRAIVES, SAAD ABDALLAH, PIERO FREGATTI
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimPreoperative planning in oncoplastic and reconstructive breast surgery requires synthesizing patient- and tumor-specific variables with aesthetic considerations. Variability in documentation and expertise may hinder reproducibility across centers. Large language models (LLMs) can process structured data into consistent outputs. We assessed whether a prompt-engineered artificial intelligence (AI) assistant (BREAST AI-PLAN) could replicate multidisciplinary team (MDT) decisions and aesthetic scores from routinely collected clinical data.Patients and MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 30 women undergoing breast cancer surgery. Each case was recorded in a standardized form covering demographics, comorbidities, breast anatomy, tumor biology, and staging. This form was used to generate structured prompts for the AI assistant. The AI returned eight outputs: 1) neoadjuvant therapy suitability; 2) planned surgery; 3) surgical approach; 4) reconstruction type; 5) direct-to-implant feasibility; 6) implant type; 7) adjuvant therapy; and 8) an aesthetic score (BCCT.core-aligned). Clinical MDT decisions were the reference standard. Agreement was measured using Cohen’s κ, with linear and quadratic weights for aesthetics.ResultsMean age was 59.2±9.8 years; median body mass index (BMI) 24.2 (20.9-26.8). One patient carried a BRCA2 mutation; 13.3% had prior radiotherapy; 33.3% were smokers. Tumors were mainly Luminal A (43.3%) and Luminal B (26.7%). Agreement was very good for neoadjuvant therapy (κ=0.91; observed 96.7%) and good/moderate for planned surgery (κ=0.58; observed 70.0%), surgical approach (κ=0.43; observed 60.0%), reconstruction (κ=0.36; observed 57.1%), and adjuvant therapy (κ=0.29; observed 64.3%). Aesthetic scoring showed high observed concordance (0.778-0.896) but only slight weighted κ (0.14-0.16).ConclusionBREAST AI-PLAN reproduced MDT planning elements and aesthetic assessments with moderate-to-very-good agreement. Transparent, auditable AI assistance may standardize surgical planning and support training. Prospective validation with imaging integration is warranted.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequent malignancy in women worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality. Advances in early detection and multimodal treatment have improved survival, shifting focus toward individualized treatment and quality of life (1,2). Surgical management has evolved from radical mastectomy to breast-conserving and oncoplastic approaches, aiming to balance oncologic control with cosmetic and psychological outcomes (3).

Preoperative planning in breast surgery is highly complex. It must incorporate tumor biology (e.g., Luminal A/B, HER2+, triple-negative), clinical stage, breast volume and density, prior treatments such as radiotherapy, comorbidities, and patient preferences. Multidisciplinary tumor boards (MDTs) play a central role, yet variability exists across institutions and surgeons. Standardization is further challenged by heterogeneous documentation and the subjective nature of aesthetic assessment (4).

Artificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated potential in generating structured outputs from unstructured data, synthesizing guidelines, and supporting clinical decision-making. While AI has been tested in radiology, pathology, and risk prediction, its role in surgical planning remains underexplored. In oncoplastic breast surgery, an AI assistant could provide transparent, reproducible support, enabling education and standardization across centers (5,6).

We developed BREAST AI-PLAN, a prompt-driven AI workflow using structured clinical forms. This study compared AI-derived surgical recommendations with real-world MDT decisions in a cohort of women undergoing breast surgery.

Patients and Methods

A retrospective single-center observational study was conducted at the Breast Surgery Unit of IRCCS Ospedale Policlinico San Martino, Genoa (Italy). Thirty consecutive women undergoing breast cancer surgery between January 2024 and May 2025 were included.

Inclusion criteria were female sex, age ≥18 years, histologically confirmed ductal/lobular carcinoma or Ductal In Situ Carcinoma (DCIS), stage ≤cT3, nodal status cN0-cN1, suitability for surgery with or without reconstruction. Exclusion criteria were metastatic disease (M1), locally advanced, inoperable disease (T4), uncontrolled comorbidities, contraindications to surgery/anesthesia, extreme Body Mass Index (BMI) (>40 or <16), active heavy smoking with high risk of necrosis.

Each patient was evaluated using a tailored form capturing the following: Demographics: age, BMI, smoking, prior radiotherapy, vascular disease, BRCA1/2 status; Breast anatomy: volume classes II-VII; BIRADS density A-C; subcutaneous thickness; Tumor biology: histology, ER/PgR, HER2, Ki-67, molecular subtype; Staging: cT, cN; Surgical planning: feasibility of conserving vs. mastectomy, reconstruction options.

The structured data were transcribed into a guideline-oriented prompt requesting eight outputs: 1) Neoadjuvant therapy suitability; 2) Planned surgery (codes: A-E, A+D, A+E, C+F, D+F); 3) Surgical approach (I-IV); 4) Reconstruction (types 1-6); 5) Direct-to-implant feasibility; 6) Implant type (anatomical vs. round); 7) Adjuvant therapy; 8) Aesthetic score (BCCT.core-aligned 1-4).

MDT decisions were the gold standard. Aesthetic outcomes were assessed with the BCCT.core-based scale used routinely at the center. Agreement between AI and MDT was assessed using observed vs. expected concordance and Cohen’s κ with 95% confidence intervals (Cis). Weighted κ (linear and quadratic) was applied for aesthetic scoring. Descriptive statistics are reported as mean±SD or median (Interquartile range-IQR).

Results

Patients had mean age 59.2±9.8 years and median BMI 24.2 (20.9-26.8). Ten (33.3%) smoked, four (13.3%) had prior chest radiotherapy, and one (3.3%) had vascular disease. One (3.3%) was BRCA2+. Breast volumes were predominantly III-V; BIRADS density mainly B, known as a standardized way to describe the proportion of fibroglandular (dense) tissue versus fatty tissue seen on a mammogram. Most were cN0 (90%). Tumor subtypes: Luminal A (43.3%), Luminal B (26.7%), HER2+ (16.7%), TNBC (13.3%) (Table I, Table II).

To assess the consistency between the initial clinical evaluations and the AI-based reassessments, Cohen’s Kappa coefficient was used (Table III). This index measures the degree of agreement between two raters while accounting for agreement expected by chance. Kappa values were interpreted according to the Landis and Koch (1977) scale: <0.00: Poor; 0.00-0.20: Slight; 0.21-0.40: Fair; 0.41-0.60: Moderate; 0.61-0.80: Substantial; 0.81-1.00: Almost perfect.

Planned surgery. The analysis of concordance between the initially planned surgery and the AI-based reassessment revealed an observed agreement of 70.00%, with an expected agreement of 28.33%. The Kappa value was 0.5814 [standard error (SE)=0.0972], indicating moderate agreement. This result was highly significant (Z=5.98; p<0.001), suggesting that the observed agreement exceeded that expected by chance.

Aesthetic score. As the aesthetic score is an ordinal variable, weighted Kappa was calculated to account for the degree of disagreement between ratings. Using linear weights, the observed agreement was 80.95%, with an expected agreement of 77.81% (Kappa=0.1418; SE=0.1464). With quadratic weights, the observed agreement increased to 91.27%, with an expected agreement of 89.60% (Kappa=0.1608; SE=0.1763). In both cases, agreement was classified as slight according to Landis and Koch and was not statistically significant (linear weights: Z=0.97, p=0.1665; quadratic weights: Z=0.91, p=0.1809).

Surgical access. For surgical access, the observed agreement was 60.00%, with an expected agreement of 30.33%. The Kappa value was 0.4258 (SE=0.1125), indicating moderate agreement. This finding was statistically significant (Z=3.79; p<0.001).

Reconstruction. The concordance between initial and AI-reassessed reconstruction showed an observed agreement of 57.14%, with an expected agreement of 33.16%. The Kappa value was 0.3588 (SE=0.0906), corresponding to fair agreement. Statistical analysis confirmed the significance of this result (Z=3.96; p<0.001).

Neoadjuvant therapy. Concordance for neoadjuvant therapy was very high, with an observed agreement of 96.67% and an expected agreement of 62.44%. The Kappa value was 0.9112 (SE=0.1819), indicating almost perfect agreement. This result was statistically significant (Z=5.01; p<0.001).

Adjuvant therapy. For adjuvant therapy, the observed agreement was 64.29%, with an expected agreement of 49.62%. The Kappa value was 0.2911 (SE=0.0961), indicating fair agreement according to Landis and Koch. The observed agreement was significantly higher than that expected by chance (Z=3.03; p=0.0012).

Discussion

This pilot study demonstrates that an AI assistant can approximate MDT breast surgery planning when provided with structured clinical data. Notably, decisions regarding neoadjuvant therapy showed near-perfect agreement with MDT recommendations, likely reflecting the clear criteria provided by current guidelines, including tumor subtype, nodal status, and clinical stage. This suggests that AI performs particularly well when decision-making is guided by objective, well-defined parameters.

In contrast, surgical planning and reconstruction decisions achieved only moderate agreement (κ), highlighting the influence of institutional protocols, individual surgeon preferences, and subtle patient-specific variables that are difficult to capture in structured data alone, such as skin quality, degree of breast ptosis, and patient wishes. The AI’s tendency to suggest a higher rate of mastectomies may reflect conservative biases present in its training dataset. Similarly, aesthetic scoring demonstrated high observed concordance but low Kappa values, likely due to the skewed distribution of scores and the inherently subjective nature of cosmetic assessment. Incorporating imaging or 3D simulations into AI assessment tools could help capture these outcomes more realistically in the future.

Applications of AI in breast surgery planning represent a rapidly evolving area of research and clinical development. AI-driven technologies -including computer vision, machine learning, and neural networks- are being utilized to support preoperative planning, anatomical measurements, symmetry assessment, and outcome simulation (7-9). These systems can help generate personalized treatment plans, improve precision, and enhance patient consultations by providing predictive modelling and real-time feedback (10-12).

Recent studies describe AI models capable of automating breast symmetry evaluation, streamlining decision-making for benign breast conditions, and assisting in patient selection for cosmetic procedures (13). These tools have shown high concordance with expert surgeon assessments and can significantly reduce processing time, although further validation is required before widespread clinical implementation. Explainable AI approaches are also being developed to increase transparency and trust in surgical decision-support systems (14).

In summary, while AI-assisted planning for aesthetic breast surgery aligns with ongoing developments in the field, a named system or protocol called BREAST AI-PLAN does not currently exist in the medical literature (15). AI-based planning tools are emerging, but their integration into routine clinical practice is still under evaluation, with ongoing attention to ethical considerations, patient privacy, and clinical validation challenges (16).

The educational potential of BREAST AI-PLAN is substantial. By standardizing input parameters and providing reproducible outputs, it offers a valuable resource for resident training and MDT preparation. The system allows explicit comparison with both guideline-based recommendations and peer decisions, fostering reflective practice and enhancing learning opportunities. Key strengths of this study include the use of a structured input ontology, the direct comparison with MDT decisions, and the transparency and reproducibility of the AI outputs.

Study limitations. The study was conducted on a small, single-center cohort and relied primarily on textual clinical data rather than imaging, which may have limited the AI’s performance. Additionally, potential transcription biases cannot be excluded.

Future directions should focus on developing multimodal AI models that integrate imaging, genomic signatures, and patient-reported outcomes to provide more accurate, patient-tailored guidance. External validation across multiple centers with larger datasets will be essential to ensure generalizability and reliability.

Conclusion

BREAST AI-PLAN successfully replicated MDT planning with moderate-to-very-good agreement across multiple surgical domains, highlighting its potential to complement expert clinical decision-making. While the system is not intended to replace the nuanced judgment of experienced surgeons, it offers a valuable tool for standardizing decision-making processes, supporting resident education, and facilitating research in breast surgery. By providing reproducible and transparent outputs, AI can serve as a benchmark for evaluating guideline adherence and identifying areas of variability in clinical practice.

Furthermore, the integration of AI into clinical workflows could enhance efficiency in MDT meetings, allowing teams to focus on complex or ambiguous cases while routine decisions are streamlined. Prospective validation across larger, multicenter cohorts will be essential to confirm generalizability, and the incorporation of imaging modalities, genomic information, and patient-reported outcomes will likely improve the system’s accuracy and clinical relevance. Rigorous safety governance, ethical oversight, and continuous performance monitoring will also be critical to ensure that AI recommendations are reliable, unbiased, and patient-centered.
