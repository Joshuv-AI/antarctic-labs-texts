The Effect of Writing Direction and Task‐Specific Experience on Mapping Time and Numbers on Space
Authors: Mikhail Ordin, Dina Abdel Salam El‐Dakhs, Leona Polyanskaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMapping time and numbers on space is affected by the writing direction. In two experiments, we searched for an explanation of how writing scripts can influence the mental representation of time and number axes. We presented to participants either 2 months (written in their native language) or two numbers (Arabic numerals), one on the left and the other on the right of the screen, and asked them to judge which number is larger or which month comes later in the year. For the months, Arabic speakers responded more accurately and faster when the correct answer was shown on the left, and the pattern was reversed for the Portuguese. However, this effect was not detected when the numbers were compared. We suggest that the observed effect is not rooted in mental mapping of time on space but is accounted for by automatized behavioral patterns related to habitual information processing. We also found that mathematicians performed better with numbers and worse with month names than nonmathematicians, confirming the direct effect of experience on cognitive processing without involving mental representations of number or time axes.

Introduction

Despite universal biases in judgment and decision‐making, which are rooted in a common evolutionary history shaped by natural and social environments, there is remarkable variability in how people perceive the world, select which stimuli to attend to, decide what to encode in memory, and how to structure and represent information, potentially leading to different conclusions from the same priors. A portion of this variability can be accounted for by differences in language properties [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. One domain in which language properties can affect universal cognitive biases is the perception of directionality [7].

The directionality bias influences, for example, how people map temporal or magnitude relationships onto a mental axis in space [8]. Using space to conceptualize the flow of time or magnitude relationships (e.g., from back in the past forward in the future; from lower magnitude on the left toward higher magnitude on the right) is sometimes considered a cognitive and biologically determined universal, although the specific details of how people map space onto time and magnitude are culture‐specific [9, 10]. The mental representation of numerical magnitude tends to align along a left‐to‐right horizontal axis, with larger numbers being positioned to the right [11, 12, 13]. Since it was first described in the 19th century [14], this left‐to‐right mapping of magnitude and its origin has persisted as a topic of ongoing scientific fascination.

This bias has been observed in preverbal infants [15, 16, 17], in vertebrates that are evolutionarily very distant from mammals, for example, such as chickens [18, 19] or Clarks's nutcrackers (Nucifraga columbiana) [20], and even in insects such as honeybees [21]. It suggests deep evolutionary roots of a general cognitive predisposition.

However, some researchers have suggested that this behavior in nonhuman animals and preverbal (unculturated) infants can be accounted for without recourse to a mental number line [22]. For example, if the high spatial frequency stimuli are faster perceived in the right visual field [23], animals are more likely to turn to different directions depending on the number of dots in the right and left visual fields, and although such a response is biologically determined indeed, it is not based on the magnitude concept. Moreover, in adult humans, this bias may be affected by language properties, for example, by writing directionality [7, 9]. Although larger numbers elicit faster responses by the right hand or when they appear on the right side of the screen [11, 12], Arabic speakers, who write in the opposite direction and scan the visual space from right to left during reading, show the reverse effect [24]. Boroditsky [2] showed that script directionality also influences reaction times (RTs) when judging the temporal order of two consecutively presented images. When participants are asked to judge whether the second image refers to an earlier or later event on the timeline than the first image, native English speakers make the judgment faster when they use the left response key, whereas Hebrew speakers show the reverse pattern.

Zebian [25] reported that such effects are not observed in illiterate Arabic speakers, who conform to the general bias of aligning numbers from right to left in magnitude order, which suggests that the bias is reversed through with literacy, through many hours of training that require moving gaze and orient temporal and information flow from right to left. However, she also did not observe this bias in English monolinguals and, contrary to predictions, the effect was stronger in Arabic−English bilinguals, who are fluent readers in both directions, than in Arabic monolinguals, who are not accustomed to left‐to‐right reading direction.

Gevers et al. [26, 27] reported that this general cognitive bias is not restricted to numbers and magnitudes but also extends to ordinal sequences of months, weekdays, and letters. Dutch speakers responded faster to months at the beginning of the year or to letters earlier in the Latin alphabet when they had to register the response with the left hand. They interpreted this finding as evidence that time can also be mapped onto space and that the flow of time is represented from left to right. Further studies strengthened this hypothesis through cross‐language comparisons. When people are asked to arrange cards with images in temporal order (e.g., breakfast‐lunch‐dinner; tadpole‐froglet‐frog; or images depicting scenes from a story in chronological order), they follow the convention of the writing direction of their language [28]. Speakers of alphabetic Indo‐European languages arrange the cards from left to right, whereas speakers of Semitic languages arrange them from right to left (except for Hebrew speakers, who exhibit a mixed pattern, potentially because of exposure to Indo‐European languages and close cultural ties with societies using alphabetic left‐to‐right writing scripts). Speakers of Chinese languages arrange the cards vertically, from top to bottom, with the effect being stronger in Taiwanese than in Mandarin speakers [29, 30, 31]. Park et al. [32] studied English−Arabic bilinguals and reported that the language effect can be transient. If bilinguals communicate with a monolingual Arabic interlocutor and then perform the task, they arrange the images from right to left in chronological order. If, however, they interact with an English interlocutor and perform the task in English, they arrange the images from left to right.

Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether the reversal of the directionality bias is constrained to written language and can be observed only when the automaticity of reading underlies information processing, or whether it generalizes beyond reading and writing and influences the way we think about and represent the flow of time. The reversal of number‐line directionality is more difficult to explain in terms of script direction because Arabic numerals are written from left to right, even when they are embedded in Arabic text that is written from right to left. Moreover, some studies have failed to replicate the language effect on the spatial mapping of numbers and time [33, 34, 35, 36].

If the reversal directionality effect is indeed explained by automatized practices of information processing (with reading and writing directionality being a particular case of such automatized practices), then we should also observe it in populations, in which the directionality of information processing is reversed in regular habitual activities unrelated to written language. This could potentially demonstrate that directionality effects in speakers of Semitic or Sinitic languages can be explained without invoking intrinsic general cognitive biases or mental representations of number and time axes, but rather by habitual ways of processing information that become automatized through constant use [37].

In this study, we pursued three objectives: (1) to replicate the reversal of the directionality effect in Arabic monolingual speakers who are fluent readers and writers, who use right‐to‐left script, in contrast to Portuguese monolingual speakers, who use left‐to‐right script (Experiment 1); and (2) to determine whether the reversal can be related to patterns of information processing that become automatized through practice, without recourse to mental time line or magnitude axis (Experiment 2).

Experiment 1

Method

Participants

We recruited 40 native Arabic speakers (13 females), all students at Prince Sultan University in Riyadh, who were not studying foreign languages or linguistics, because we wanted to minimize the possible effect of proficiency in foreign languages with left‐to‐right alphabetic scripts. In addition, we recruited 40 native Portuguese speakers (14 females) who were not familiar with Semitic or Sinitic languages. After preprocessing the data, we excluded one Portuguese participant and one Arabic participant because they terminated the experiment early. One Portuguese and two Arabic participants were excluded for responding randomly: their numbers of correct responses did not differ from chance. Two Portuguese participants were left‐handed, but their data (accuracy and RTs) fell within the central 50% range of the sample distributions; therefore, their data were retained for analysis.

Procedure

The experiment was administered on a computer using the left and right arrow keys on the keyboard to register responses. Participants saw either 2‐month names or two numbers (digits), one on the left and the other on the right side of the screen. When they saw 2‐month names, they had to indicate whether the month on the left or the month on the right came later in the year (e.g., if participants saw “March” on the left and “November” on the right, they should press the right arrow). Horizontally, the stimuli were presented within the middle 20% of the screen, and vertically, they were centered in the left and right quarters of the display. We used a 15.5‐inch screen. In total, there were 132 trials with month names (each month was paired with every other month, with the correct answer appearing equally often on the left and on the right, yielding 132 possible combinations). When participants saw numbers (108 trials, with an equal number of trials in which the correct answer was on the left and on the right), they had to indicate whether the number on the left or the number on the right was larger. The two‐digit numbers were mirror images (e.g., 23 vs. 32). Trials with month names and trials with numbers were intermixed, and their order was randomized for each participant. Participants were instructed to use the index finger and either the middle or ring finger (whichever was more convenient) of their dominant hand to press the arrow keys on the keyboard. Once a participant responded by pressing either the left or right arrow, the next stimulus pair was presented immediately. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly as possible.

The experiment lasted about 15 min on average, with an additional 5 min for instructions, obtaining informed consent, and a short training session to familiarize participants with the procedure. For the training set, we used weekday names (from Monday to Thursday) and one‐digit numbers. The training data were not analyzed. Consent was obtained digitally: before the main experiment began, participants read statements confirming (a) voluntary participation, (b) anonymous data collection with no collection of personal information, (c) their right to terminate the experiment at any time, and (d) that they had been informed about the procedure, had completed a training session, and had had the opportunity to ask questions before the main experiment. Participants were asked to press a button below these statements if they agreed and wished to begin the experiment. The generated anonymous data file was time‐stamped with the exact moment at which the consent button was pressed. No personally identifiable information was collected.

Analysis

We analyzed accuracy rate (percentage of correct responses) and RT as a function of stimulus type (month names or numbers), the side on which the correct answer was displayed (left or right), and group (Arabic or Portuguese). Stimulus type and side were treated as within‐subject factors, and group was treated as a between‐subject factor. Because the number of month‐name trials (N = 132) exceeded the number of number trials (N = 108), the number of correct responses was converted into a percentage for each side and stimulus type.

To run a within‐subject ANOVA with 2 within‐subject factors and 2 independent groups (native speakers of a language with right‐to‐left writing direction vs. native speakers of a language with left‐to‐right writing direction), the required sample size is 40 subjects per group to detect significant effect with alpha = 0.05 and beta = 0.8 (conventional type I and type II error margins in social sciences), given high correlations between repeated measured (exceeding 0.7, which we assumed as adequate, because we expect that if one person is more accurate and faster with letter trials than another person, he will also be more accurate and faster with number trials), even if the effect size is small (f = 0.1 or larger).

Interim Discussion 1

The data showed that RTs were shorter and accuracy was higher on number trials (when participants had to indicate whether the number on the left or right side of the screen was larger) than on month‐name trials (when they had to read the names of the months and indicate whether the month on the left or right came later in the year). Trials with letters (month‐names) are processed more slowly and elicit more errors in Arabic speakers than in Portuguese speakers, whereas no such difference emerged for number trials. This might suggest that processing syllabic opaque Arabic script is more cognitively demanding than processing the more transparent Portuguese alphabetic script. The differences in accuracy and RT are probably due to writing systems rather than intergroup differences in general efficiency of information processing; otherwise, we would also expect performance differences on number trials. As Arabic and Portuguese speakers perform on number trials equally well, we suggest that they do not differ in general information processing efficiency; rather, the writing system adds cognitive load when reading is involved.

Importantly, Arabic speakers are faster and more accurate on month‐name trials when the correct response was on the left than when it was on the right, whereas the pattern—both for accuracy and RT—was reversed for Portuguese speakers. For the numbers, no difference in RT or accuracy was observed in either group between the stimuli presented on the right and left. Overall, the results do not support the claim that writing direction influences the directionality of the mental number line. Moreover, the data do not confirm the existence of such a line, because we did not observe evidence that larger numbers were processed more accurately when they are presented on the right.

The existence of mental time arrows is likewise dubious. Months later in the year indeed elicit faster responses on the right, and the accuracy rate is higher in the Portuguese population, in accordance with the assumed cognitive bias, and this bias is reversed in the Arabic population. However, whether this finding truly indicates a reversal of a general cognitive bias in the perception of time flow is not conclusive because the result can more easily be accounted for by reading habits. Arabic speakers read the word on the right first and then the word on the left. If the correct response is on the left, they can respond as soon as they finish reading the second word; if the correct response is on the right, they must still retrieve the right‐side word to direct their response, which lengthens RT. The same explanation can account for the Portuguese pattern without recourse to an intrinsic general cognitive bias. This interpretation is also in line with Eviatar [37], who showed that reading habits influence subjects’ responses to stimuli in the visual field on the side opposite to the scanning direction while reading. The result pattern can, therefore, emerge via familiarization and practice in processing left‐to‐right script in the course of many hours of reading. The numbers are shorter (have fewer characters) and hence less cognitively demanding; therefore, we do not observe any lateralization effect on the trials with numbers.

Arabic numerals make an especially interesting case because of their formatting. In Arabic, two‐digit numerals are transcoded in a way similar to Slovenia and German: in that units precede decades in verbal output (UD format, i.e., “23” is transcoded as three and twenty), while in most languages the format is DU, with decades preceding units (“23” is transcoded as twenty and three). Double conversion happens because in a right‐to‐left text, the numeral is inserted as a left‐to‐right sequence of digits, which then has to be converted back to the UD format. This is cognitively taxing [38, 39] and adds to repeated practice of switching the order between larger and smaller place values. Moreover, native Arabic speakers who are more proficient in Hebrew (in Hebrew, the numerals are presented in DU format) than in English are also more fluent in processing numerals verbally presented in Hebrew than in English, even though the required place value inversion into Arabic is identical in both cases [40]. This suggests that numeral processing and their spatial organization are related to habit and practice [39].

If the result is indeed a matter of automatized practices, we should expect to observe a similar reversal effect in Portuguese participants who have experience processing information from right to left. This would confirm that the explanation is better framed in terms of unconscious, automatized practices of information processing than in terms of mental time or number arrows or intrinsic general cognitive bias, and that these practices need not affect the underlying representation of temporal or magnitude directionality. To test this hypothesis, we chose professional mathematicians (students of pure mathematics in the second and third years of their university studies).

Mathematicians who routinely handle complex functions decode them from right to left because nested functions in calculus are decoded from inside toward outside, with outer functions, which are processed later, to the left: y = f(x(g(k)))—the first function to be processed is g(k), then the output is fed into x(output), and then the new output is fed into the outer function f(new output). Matrix operations may also require directional sequencing, because order matters even when the same two matrices are involved: for example, multiplying A × B is not the same as multiplying B × A. Thus, the direction in which two matrices are processed, one on the left and the other on the right, is important. Professional mathematicians may, therefore, automatize right‐to‐left sequencing of information even though the writing direction of their native language, Portuguese, is left to right. If mathematicians show a reversal of directionality bias relative to nonmathematicians from the same linguistic and cultural population, this would provide stronger evidence that directionality in information processing is modulated by automatized practice.

Experiment 2

Method

Participants

We recruited 40 Portuguese mathematics students from the second and third years (13 females) after they had completed the calculus 1 and calculus 2 courses, and linear algebra. Additionally, we recruited a control group of 40 Portuguese native speakers (33 females), not included in the Portuguese sample in the previous experiment. A new group was collected because we did not want to use the same data twice, and, more importantly, we wanted to collect Portuguese‐speaking participants from the same campus as mathematicians. In Experiment 1, the participants were drawn from those studying the social sciences. The control group for mathematicians included students of technology‐oriented subjects but with less experience in pure mathematics (particularly in manual—pen‐and‐paper—manipulation of matrices and functions). This allowed us to better match the backgrounds of the mathematicians and the Portuguese‐speaking control participants.

After data screening, we had to remove two participants from each group as outliers (outliers are defined as participants whose accuracy rate or RT values differed more than 3 SDs from the mean of the group to which they belong). All participants were right‐handed.

The procedure was identical to that of Experiment 1.

Accuracy Rate

Repeated‐measures omnibus analysis of variance (RM ANOVA) did not reveal a significant effect of group, F(1,74) = 0.76, p = 0.784, ηp2 = 0.001, or side, F(1,74) = 1.92, p = 0.17, ηp2 = 0.025. We observed a significant effect of stimulus type, F(1,74) = 58.281, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.441 (the accuracy rate was greater for trials with numbers than for those with letters). Interim Discussion 2

The data revealed that the stimuli with letters were processed less efficiently than those with numbers in both groups, recapitulating the pattern observed in Experiment 1 (which compared Arabic and Portuguese social sciences students). This can be accounted for by shorter numerical stimuli (consisting of two characters) than month‐names, which contained a larger number of characters. Although we did not observe a main effect of group (mathematicians and controls do not differ in averaged accuracy or speed of response), mathematicians are more accurate and faster with numbers and less accurate and slower with letters than controls. We did not observe any difference in the accuracy rate or RT between the trials when the correct stimuli were shown on the left versus on the right, neither when the stimuli were letter‐based nor when they were numerical.

This pattern, again, does not support the existence of a mental time arrow or mental number line. Rather, it supports the hypothesis that information processing is shaped by experience. Getting more expertise with numbers hones the processing of numerical values, whereas more frequent engagement of the cognitive mechanisms underlying reading improves the processing of written information. Direction‐specific mapping of number order onto space is, therefore, better understood as a product of behavioral patterns and correlates with the structures of the world that need to be processed [9, 41].

A puzzling result is the discrepancy between the Portuguese‐speaking group in Experiment 1 and the Portuguese control group in Experiment 2. In the first sample, we observed a right‐side advantage for month‐name stimuli (when the task engaged reading, a higher accuracy rate and shorter RT were observed when the correct response was shown on the right). This advantage was not observed in the control group in Experiment 2. We suggest two possible explanations for this discrepancy, both boiling down to the automatization of information processing through practice. First, Portuguese participants in Experiment 1 were social sciences students, who might spend more time reading than students in technology‐oriented disciplines; the effect of writing direction may, therefore, have been stronger in that group. Lower engagement of reading‐related cognitive mechanisms by individuals from the control group in Experiment 2 might have had a less prominent effect on the processing of text‐based information. Second, although individuals from the control group were not mathematicians, they nevertheless studied mathematics in greater depth and for more hours than social sciences students, and familiarity with interpreting complex functions from inside toward outside might have reduced the effect caused by the writing script directionality.

General Discussion

This study replicated the reversal of the directionality effect on the stimuli with letters (when processing engages reading mechanisms): responses are given faster and more accurate when the correct answer is shown on the right side of the screen in the Portuguese population and on the left side of the screen in the Arabic population. Replicating this reversal was the first objective of the study. Earlier studies attributed this to the spatial organization of time in mind, that is, the mental time arrow, which is reversed due to the right‐to‐left direction in writing script.

Our second objective was to determine whether this reversal can instead be explained by patterns of information processing that become automatized through practice [9, 41]. We propose that the reversal effect is explainable without referring to mental representations of time and number axes. Arabic speakers begin reading on the right, and they can respond once they have finished reading the word on the left. To verify whether the word on the right is indeed a correct answer, however, they must finish reading the word on the left and then retrieve the word on the right again into their attention focus, leading to a longer RT. Verification failures may lead to a lower accuracy rate. It is easier to explain the reversal effect by automatized reading practice rather than the reversal of the representation of the mental timeline and time‐to‐space mapping. Moreover, the second experiment showed that practice in processing numerical information leads to better performance on trials, in which participants had to decide whether the number on the right or left was greater, and that practice with reading leads to a stronger effect of writing directionality. This pattern strengthens our proposed explanation.

Based on the data we collected, we did not observe evidence that the mental number line exists; there was no right‐side advantage for larger numbers. Most robust evidence for the mental number line arises from experiments in which people need to respond to larger numbers with their right hand and to smaller numbers with their left hand, for example, when people see one number on the screen and have to judge whether it is larger or smaller than a given reference number that stays the same across all trials. In the present design, by contrast, the reference changed continuously, both in magnitude and in lateralization. Participants had to scan for a smaller number and use it as a reference for selecting a larger one. This may lead to a mental number line—if it exists at all—to constantly and unpredictably switch direction, preventing the use of the reference as an anchor. Consequently, the effect reported in previous studies appears to be task dependent, and any intrinsic general cognitive bias, if it exists, interferes with the task performance only when the conditions allow for the bias to manifest itself. This interpretation also aligns with Eccher et al. [42], who showed that the cognitive bias is task‐dependent and emerges when the culturally determined habits are transferred to the task performance.

The spatial–numerical association is often claimed to be a reliable phenomenon [11], yet it is not always replicated [35], or attenuated [25]. For example, Zebian [25] did not observe a processing advantage for larger numbers on the right in English monolinguals, and a stronger reverse effect (i.e., a processing advantage for larger numbers on the left) was observed in Arabic–English bilinguals than in Arabic monolinguals. Hebrew speakers sometimes do not show processing advantage for larger numbers on the left [43], as would be predicted from script directionality [7, 10], and in some studies, they even show a contrary‐to‐prediction pattern identical to that found in readers of European alphabetic scripts [44, 45]. These inconsistencies undermine the purely conceptual link between the mental number line and reading practice and instead strengthen the view that number–space mapping is associated with automatized behavioral practices that are not limited to reading. Taken together, our results suggest that spatial–numerical and spatial–magnitude associations depend on experience and on behavioral routines that become automatized through regular practice and start guiding habitual ways of information processing. Reading is one of such practices because it involves many hours of scanning from left to right or from right to left. Yet reading is not the only activity that may guide information processing behaviors.

The absence of a right‐side advantage for larger numbers may also reflect a limitation of the present study, namely, the use of short two‐digit numbers. Future experiments using numbers with more digits may provide a stronger test. Choosing the larger number requires detecting which number has the larger leftmost digit, so the task relies on visual search for the first digit that differs between the two presented numbers. Another informative task would be to explore the short‐term effects of mapping numbers onto space through priming. For example, mathematicians can be asked to multiply matrices A*B from left to right and then perform the test with numerical stimuli, followed by performing the same multiplication from left to right and again performing the test. This can reveal whether a mental number line truly exists and whether it can be reverted dynamically. So far, more evidence is needed to link the writing script direction and the reversal of mental representation of the temporal flow or number line, and to identify the conditions under which these mental axes influence performance in cognitive tasks.
