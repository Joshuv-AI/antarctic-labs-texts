MAIT cells promote cancer progression and regulatory T cell accumulation in bladder tumor microenvironment
Authors: Inbar A Habaz, Xuejin Ou, Nicole I Wang, Angela Schincaglia, Valeryia Shydlouskaya, Agetha Mahendran, Francisco M Martinez-Santiesteban, Dylan Doolabi, Yige Bao, Ahmad R Movasseghi, Julius Haruna, Nicholas E Power, Timothy J Scholl, Melissa J Huynh, Alp Sener, S M Mansour Haeryfar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMucosa-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells represent a unique population of innate-like T lymphocytes capable of detecting non-peptide antigens in the context of monomorphic antigen-presenting molecules. Due to their abundance in barrier tissues, reactivity to local inflammatory cues, and cytotoxic and regulatory functions, MAIT cells are poised to shape the dynamics of various tumor microenvironments. Growing evidence suggests that MAIT cells can exert protumor and/or antitumor effects in cancers arising from or metastasizing to mucosal tissues. However, MAIT cell roles in bladder cancer (BCa) remains unclear.MethodsTo begin to identify MAIT cells in BCa, we stained bladder tumor biopsies for T cell receptor (TCR) Vα7.2+ cells. We then refined a human MAIT cell signature, which enabled us to interrogate a bulk RNA sequencing dataset and conduct correlation analyses linking intratumoral MAIT cell abundance and mortality from BCa. To extend our work to an in vivo setting, we employed a clinically relevant mouse model in which Mr1+/+ B6-MAITCAST (MAIT-sufficient) and Mr1−/− B6-MAITCAST (MAIT-deficient) mice were exposed to N-butyl-N-(4-hydroxybutyl)nitrosamine, a chemical carcinogen associated with tobacco smoke. In additional experiments, MAIT cells were functionally removed through acetyl-6-formylpterin (Ac-6-FP) administration. Effector and regulatory cell types were phenotyped by flow cytometry, and BCa tumor burden and progression were assessed by MRI and/or H&E and Ki67 staining.ResultsTCR Vα7.2+ cells were readily detectable in several BCa biopsies, and our bioinformatic analyses correlated heavier MAIT cell presence in BCa tumors with poorer overall survival. Similarly, we found higher tumor burdens in Mr1+/+ B6-MAITCAST mice than in Mr1−/− or Ac-6-FP-treated animals. Bladder MAIT cells from tumor-bearing mice exhibited phenotypic MAIT17 bias based on transcription factors they harbored along with increased interleukin-17A and tumor necrosis factor-α production capacities upon stimulation. Finally, FoxP3+ regulatory T (Treg) cell frequencies were elevated in Mr1+/+ mouse bladder tumors, likely contributing to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, a finding that could be recapitulated in our transcriptomic studies on human BCa.ConclusionsMAIT cells are abundant in BCa tumor microenvironments where they potentiate Treg cell accumulation and play protumor roles.Keywords: Bladder Cancer, T regulatory cell - Treg, T cell, Tumor microenvironment - TME, Cytokine

Background

In 2023, bladder cancer (BCa) was ranked as the fourth most common malignancy and the eighth leading cause of cancer-related deaths in men in the USA.1 The single most important risk factor for BCa is smoking. Tobacco smokers have a threefold risk increase for developing BCa compared with those who have never smoked,2 with smoking estimated to be responsible for approximately 50% of BCa cases.3

Non-metastatic, organ-confined BCa can be categorized as either non-muscle-invasive BCa (NMIBC) or muscle-invasive BCa (MIBC) depending on the depth of bladder wall invasion. NMIBC and MIBC make up approximately 70–75% and 25–30% of newly diagnosed BCa cases, respectively.4 The management of BCa depends largely on the type of BCa and the stage of the disease. Almost all patients with NMIBC are initially treated with transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT). Patients who are found to have high-risk NMIBC may be treated with intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) to stimulate a local immune response, which may in turn prevent tumor recurrence and its progression to MIBC.5 6 Patients diagnosed with MIBC are often treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by radical cystectomy or with trimodal therapy consisting of maximal TURBT and concurrent chemoradiation.7 The US Food and Drug Administration has approved the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as nivolumab, in the treatment of high-risk MIBC and metastatic BCa.8 The application of immunotherapy in the treatment of both NMIBC and MIBC highlights the need for in-depth investigations into the BCa immune landscape as they may shed light on novel targets with therapeutic potentials.

Mucosa-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells are a subset of innate-like T cells with remarkably potent immunomodulatory properties. Unlike mainstream T cells, MAIT cells express highly conserved, semi-invariant T cell receptors (TCR) (TRAV1-2-TRAJ33/12/20 in humans, and Trav1-Traj33 in mice), and are not major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-restricted.9 MAIT cells recognize vitamin B2 metabolites of microbial origin presented by the monomorphic MHC-related protein 1 (MR1).10 They also respond to inflammatory cytokine signals in the presence or absence of TCR triggering.11 Following activation, MAIT cells promptly release T helper (TH)1-type and/or TH17-type cytokines as well as cytotoxic effector molecules. Found abundantly in various mucosal tissues, as well as in the human liver,12 MAIT cells occur in a poised state to contribute to the outcome of various malignancies.

The presence of MAIT cells in primary and metastatic tumors and their roles in cancer progression or anticancer immune surveillance have been a subject of recent investigations. To this end, several groups, including ours, have reported the significance of MAIT cells in liver,13 14 lung,15 16 colorectal17 18 and ovarian19 cancers. Depending on the tumor microenvironment (TME) in which MAIT cells operate, these cells can serve protumor or antitumor functions.20 MAIT cells are able to release potent cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interferon (IFN)-γ, and interleukin (IL)-17, which in turn modulate the activity of surrounding immune cells.12 IL-17-producing MAIT cells are often associated with tumor-promoting effects, while IFN-γ-producing MAIT cells are usually expected to promote antitumor immunity.20 MAIT cells can also exhibit cytotoxic effector functions via cell surface-bound receptors, such as NKG2D,21 22 and/or through degranulation to release perforin, granzymes and granulysin.23 24 Despite extensive research on MAIT cells in numerous cancers, their biological behavior in BCa remains far from clearly understood. For instance, Zhang et al25 found TCRVα7.2+CD161+ T cells, the vast majority of which should be MAIT cells, in BCa tumors. However, they did not report any possible relationships between the frequency of these cells and disease severity.

In this work, we used bladder tumor biopsies, a publicly available human BCa dataset, and a clinically relevant mouse model to investigate MAIT cell roles and characteristics in BCa.

Methods

Bulk RNA dataset analysis

Bulk transcriptomic data and corresponding clinical data for The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) urothelial BCa dataset were retrieved from UCSC Xena (http://xena.ucsc.edu/) and cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/), respectively.26 To define MAIT cells, we used an eleven-gene MAIT cell signature, which we established before,27 along with TRAV1-2, which encodes the human MAIT cell TCR α-chain. We then applied variance stabilizing transformation on these genes (SLC4A10, KLRB1, ME1, TMIGD2, IL23R, NCR3, LST1, COLQ, RORC, ZBTB16, TLE1, TRAV1-2) using the R package DESeq2. Non-parametric and tie-robust Kendall’s rank correlation coefficients (Kendall’s τ) were determined for each of these genes to produce a correlation matrix. Of the above-listed 12 genes, we selected only those that (1) correlated with T cell markers CD3E, CD3G and CD3D; (2) correlated with well-documented MAIT cell markers SLC4A10, ZBTB16 and TRAV1-2; and (3) correlated with one another.27 A refined six-gene signature (SLC4A10, KLRB1, ZBTB16, TRAV1-2, NCR3, LST1) emerged as a result and was used for downstream analyses.

The above genes, as well as CD3E, CD3G and CD3D, were z-standardized to their mean and variance across all individuals. For each tumor sample, we averaged the standardized gene expression values to calculate a temporary MAIT cell score. Similarly, we averaged the standardized gene expression values of the three T cell genes for a T cell score. As the final normalization step, we fitted a linear regression model, with the MAIT cell score regressed on the T cell score (MAIT cell score ~ T cell score). The final MAIT cell scores were defined by the residuals of this model—the difference between modeled values and actual data points. Tumor samples above the 66th quantile were classified as “High MAIT”, and samples below the 33rd quantile as “Low MAIT” for survival analyses, which were visualized with a Kaplan-Meier curve.

Samples were similarly analyzed to assign a regulatory T (Treg) cell score. Using a signature of five genes typically expressed by Treg cells (CTLA4, FOXP3, IL2RA, TNFRSF18, CD4) and a linear regression model (Treg cell score~T cell score), samples were assigned a Treg cell score based on the residuals of this model. Tumor samples above the 66th quantile were classified as “High Treg”, and samples below the 33rd quantile as “Low Treg”.

Based on the above-described scores, samples above the 66th quantile and below the 33rd quantile for both MAIT cell and Treg cell scores were classified as “High MAIT, High Treg” and “Low MAIT, Low Treg,” respectively, for survival analyses.

Immunofluorescence staining of bladder tumor biopsies

Six human primary tumor specimens were collected from patients with BCa who underwent TURBT at the West China Hospital (Sichuan, China). Demographic data are detailed in online supplemental table 1.

Specimens were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after collection. They were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura Finetek) and sectioned at 8 µm thickness using a cryostat microtome. Sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After Avidin/Biotin blocking, sections were incubated with 5% goat serum for 15 min, rinsed with PBS, and then incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-human TCRVα7.2-PE (clone REA179; Miltenyi). Tissue sections were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Servicebio) and mounted onto slides with anti-fade mounting medium (Fluoromount-G, SouthernBiotech). A Pannoramic SCAN II Scanning Platform (3DHISTECH) and ImageJ were used to scan and analyze the slides, respectively.

BCa mouse model

Mr1+/+ C57BL/6 (B6)-MAITCAST and Mr1−/− B6-MAITCAST mice28 were provided by Dr Olivier Lantz (Institut Curie, Paris, France) and bred in-house. They will, hereafter, be referred to as Mr1+/+ and Mr1−/− mice, respectively. Adult wild-type (WT) B6 mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (St Constant, Canada). Animals were housed in the same pathogen-free barrier facility. We used male mice throughout this study since BCa is more prevalent in males.29 Mouse experiments were performed following Animal Use Protocols 2019–093 and 2023–139, which were approved by the Animal Care Committee of Animal Care and Veterinary Services at Western University.

To induce BCa, 0.05% N-butyl-N-(4-hydroxybutyl)nitrosamine (BBN) was administered orally in drinking water. Animals at 7–8 weeks of age were randomly assigned to naïve or BBN-treated cohorts. The latter received BBN in their drinking water for 12 weeks with weekly replenishments followed by standard drinking water for an additional 6 weeks. Naïve mice received standard drinking water.

In several experiments, cohorts of BBN-treated Mr1+/+ mice were injected intravesically with 50 nmol of acetyl-6-formylpterin (Ac-6-FP) in 100 µL of saline (or with saline alone) once a week for 4 weeks starting on week 18 post-BBN. Treatments were conducted under isoflurane anesthesia. Mice were euthanized 3 days after the final injection was given.

MRI

Tumor progression in BBN-treated mice was longitudinally monitored at weeks 10, 14 and 18 using MRI. Images were captured using a GE Discovery MR 750 3T scanner (General Electric Healthcare) equipped with a high-performance gradient insert coil and a custom-built 12-rung low-pass RF birdcage coil for whole-body mouse imaging. One hour before imaging, mice were deprived of water in order to reduce bladder size variation from water consumption. Animals were then subcutaneously injected with 200 µL of sterile saline and anesthetized with 2% isoflurane. During imaging, mice were positioned in a tray warmed to 38°C. Balanced steady-state free precession MR images were obtained with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 7 ms, echo time (TE) = 3.5 ms, flip angle = 35°, bandwidth = 62.5 kHz; number of phase cycles = 8, matrix size 200 × 200, field of view = 4 cm, phase field of view = 70%, 200 µm isotropic resolution, and acquisition time ≈ 35 min/mouse. Acquired data was zero-padded to a final DICOM reconstruction resolution of 0.0781 mm × 0.0781 mm × 0.1 mm. The resulting T2/T1-weighted images were stored in DICOM format for analysis.

We used ITK-SNAP V.3.8.0 for the segmentation and analysis of MRI images to calculate tumor volumes. In a blinded manner, each tumor region, characterized by irregularly thickened tissue morphology, was manually segmented to ensure accurate delineation of tumor boundaries. The total number of tumor voxels was then multiplied by the voxel volume (6.09961 × 10−4 mm) to obtain the approximate tumor volume. Images that did not clearly delineate tumor from surrounding tissue were excluded from analysis.

Mouse tissue processing

After cervical dislocation, mouse blood, spleens, lungs and bladders were collected. Heparinized blood was subjected to treatment with ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) lysis buffer to deplete erythrocytes. Spleens were mechanically homogenized before being exposed to ACK lysis buffer. Lungs were finely minced using scissors before enzymatic digestion in RPMI-1640 medium containing 0.5 mg/mL collagenase type IV (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 hour at 37°C and treatment with ACK lysis buffer. Bladders were finely minced using scissors and subjected to two rounds of enzymatic digestion in RPMI-1640 medium containing 5% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1 mg/mL of collagenase D (Roche), and 0.17 µg/mL of Liberase (Roche) for 30 min at 37°C. To obtain sufficient cell numbers for flow cytometric analysis, two bladders were often pooled per sample. High cellular viability was confirmed by trypan blue staining.

Flow cytometry

To prevent non-specific binding of fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) to Fcγ receptors, cells were incubated for 15 min at 4°C with 5 µg/mL of an anti-CD16/CD32 mAb (clone 2.4G2). Surface staining was conducted in PBS containing 2% FBS for 30 min at 20°C in the dark. Dead cells were stained with either 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) (Thermo Fisher) or Fixable Viability Dye (Thermo Fisher). We used the FOXP3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher) to stain intracellular proteins. Cells were fixed for 20 min at 20°C before mAb staining in permeabilization buffer for 30 min at 4°C. To detect MAIT cells, we used mouse MR1 tetramers loaded with 5-(2-oxopropylideneamino)−6-D-ribitylaminouracil (5-OP-RU). MR1 tetramers loaded with 6-formylpterin (6-FP) were used as a negative staining control. Both tetramers were provided by the National Institutes of Health Tetramer Core Facility. A detailed list of dyes, fluorochrome-conjugated mAbs and tetramers used in this study is provided in online supplemental table 2. Samples were read on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer, and data was analyzed with FlowJo V.10.0.7 (TreeStar).

In several experiments, non-urothelial bladder mononuclear cells were stimulated with 50 ng/mL of phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, Sigma-Aldrich) and 500 ng/mL of ionomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) in the presence of 10 µg/mL of Brefeldin A (Sigma-Aldrich) in 96-well U-bottom plates. After 4 hours at 37°C, cells were washed and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Histopathological analyses

Bladders were harvested from mice that had received intravesical Ac-6-FP therapy (or saline) for their BBN-elicited tumors. To retain tissue structure during fixation, bladders were filled with 50 µL of 10% neutral buffered formalin and suture-ligated. Bladders were then fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin before they were embedded in paraffin wax and sliced into 5 µm-thick sections for histopathological analyses.

Five representative sections from each bladder were taken at 500 µm increments and stained with H&E. Slides were scored by a blinded veterinary pathologist for inflammation, hyperplasia, tumor presence, and tumor invasiveness. The extent of inflammation was determined across all bladder tissue layers, while hyperplasia was assessed in the urothelium only. Invasive carcinoma was defined as a tumor invading the underlying muscular layer, deep adipose/connective tissue, and serosa. We used previously published scoring criteria30 31 with slight modifications. Accordingly, hyperplasia was recorded as minimal (a score of 1), moderate (a score of 2) or severe (a score of 3) when 3–4, 5–6 and >6 cells were detectable in the urothelial layer, respectively. Scores for each bladder were averaged.

Immunohistochemical staining was performed by incubating sections with a mAb against Ki67 (Abcam). Slides were imaged at 40× magnification. Ki67+ cells were quantified using QuPath software V.0.5.1. Percent Ki67+ area was determined using ImageJ V.2.16.0/1.54p.

Statistical analyses

Log-rank tests, Student’s t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, two-way analysis of variance tests, or Spearman correlation tests were used for statistical comparisons as appropriate, using R or GraphPad Prism V.9.5.1. Normality was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk tests.

Results

A heavier presence of MAIT-like cells in bladder tumors is associated with poorer overall survival

Although the bladder contains a mucosal layer, limited information is available on the presence of MAIT cells in this organ. Using publicly available human single-cell RNA sequencing datasets, our group previously identified eleven MAIT cell-associated genes (SLC4A10, KLRB1, ME1, TMIGD2, IL23R, NCR3, LST1, COLQ, RORC, ZBTB16, TLE1), forming a signature that could be used to distinguish between MAIT cells and other T cell populations.27 For instance, we used this signature to interrogate the TCGA Pan-Cancer dataset in a previous study.27 However, BCa was excluded from our analyses due to the low sample size at the time and also to the poor expression of SLC4A10, an otherwise well-established MAIT cell marker, in BCa. Therefore, we refined our approach to study bladder MAIT cells in the current investigation. To this end, we identified a bladder-specific MAIT cell gene signature—SLC4A10, KLRB1, ZBTB16, TRAV1-2, NCR3 and LST1—using the TCGA Urothelial Bladder Carcinoma dataset (figure 1A).

Using this new bladder-tailored signature, we scored the degree of MAIT cell presence in each tumor sample as described in Materials and Methods and consistent with our previous work in this area.27 Samples were subsequently stratified as either “High MAIT” or “Low MAIT” if they scored above the 66th or below the 33rd MAIT cell score quantile, respectively. This exercise revealed that BCa samples with high MAIT scores corresponded with poorer overall survival, whereas samples with lower MAIT scores were associated with significantly improved survival (figure 1B). These results suggested that MAIT cells play a detrimental role in BCa.

The above transcriptomic findings prompted us to assess the presence of TCRVα7.2+ cells within human BCa tumor specimens. Six biopsies, which had been obtained from patients who underwent TURBT, were stained with DAPI and an immunofluorescent mAb against TCRVα7.2, an antigen receptor that is primarily, although not exclusively, expressed by MAIT cells.32 While the abundance of these “MAIT-like” cells in tumor biopsies was variable, Vα7.2+ T cells were detected in at least three of the six samples, thus confirming that “MAIT-like” cells can infiltrate BCa tumors (figure 1C).

BCa tumor growth is reduced in the absence of MAIT cells

Following up on our findings from bulk transcriptomic analyses and human tumor biopsies, we sought to enumerate MAIT cells in an in vivo model of BCa. Given the low abundance of MAIT cells in standard laboratory mouse strains,28 33 we first assessed the presence/absence of MAIT cells in healthy bladder tissues from WT B6, Mr1−/− and Mr1+/+ mice. We found MAIT cells to be abundant in the bladders of Mr1+/+ mice, comprising ⁓15% of all T cells in this organ on average (online supplemental figure 1 and figure 2A–C). In comparison, as expected, MAIT cells were scarce in WT B6 mice, accounting for only ⁓3.2% of all T cells, and absent in Mr1−/− mice (figure 2A,B). Interestingly, MAIT cells were highly enriched in the bladders of Mr1+/+ mice relative to the other peripheral organs tested, including the spleen, lymph nodes and lungs (figure 2C).

MAIT cells exhibit a type-17 phenotype in BBN tumor-bearing mice

To assess the phenotype and functionality of MAIT cells in our in vivo mouse model, we isolated and evaluated non-urothelial mononuclear cells from the bladders of Mr1+/+ mice. We found MAIT cells to be approximately three times less frequent in the bladders of BBN-exposed mice compared with naïve animals. However, this is likely a reflection of increased non-MAIT T cell numbers in tumor-bearing bladders (online supplemental figure 2), consistent with previous reports.31 Therefore, it was not surprising that absolute MAIT cell numbers remained stable (figure 4A).

MAIT cells can be placed into several categories based on their phenotypic and functional characteristics. In mice, MAIT cells fall under functional MAIT1 or MAIT17 subsets capable of producing TH1- or TH17-type cytokines, respectively. MAIT1 cells can be defined by their ability to express the transcription factor T-bet (T-box expressed in T cells), the master transcriptional regulator of TH1 cell differentiation. By comparison, MAIT17 cells are recognized through RORγt (retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor γt), the key transcription factor involved in the regulation of TH17 cells.35 In the blood, spleens and lungs of both naïve and BBN-treated Mr1+/+ mice, the most abundant MAIT cell subsets were RORγt−T-bet− (double-negative (DN)) and RORγt+T-bet− (MAIT17) cells, while RORγt+T-bet+ (double-positive (DP)) and RORγt−T-bet+ (MAIT1) cells formed only very small fractions of bulk MAIT cells (online supplemental figure 3). In contrast, bladder MAIT cells in naïve Mr1+/+ mice consisted mainly of MAIT17 and DP cells (figure 4B and online supplemental figure 4). Importantly, we observed a phenotypic shift in the bladder of BBN-exposed animals, whereby a RORγt+T-bet− MAIT17 bias became apparent in the BCa TME (figure 4B and online supplemental figure 4). This was corroborated by MAIT cells’ cytokine production capacities. Upon ex vivo stimulation with PMA and ionomycin, bladder MAIT cells from BBN-treated mice showed an increase in the frequency of IL-17A+ cells when compared with naïve mice (figure 4C). Also notably, BBN-treated mouse bladder MAIT cells showed increased TNF-α expression, as judged by both the frequency of TNF-α+ cells and the geometric mean fluorescence intensity of TNF-α staining on a per-cell basis (figure 4C).

Collectively, the above results indicate that MAIT17 cells accumulate in the bladder of BBN-treated animals.

Tumor growth in Mr1+/+ mice is accompanied by elevated bladder Treg cell frequencies

IL-17-producing cells, including MAIT17 cells, are thought to contribute to cancer progression, for instance, by recruiting various regulatory cell types to TMEs, among other activities.20 Our examination of the BBN-induced TME demonstrated numerical increases in most effector cell types, including total TCRβ+ cells, CD8+ T cells, natural killer cells, natural killer T cells and γẟ T cells (online supplemental figure 5A–E). Unsurprisingly, however, cell types conducive to supporting an immunosuppressive environment, such as M2 macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and Treg cells, were also enriched in tumor-bearing bladders (figure 5A,B and online supplemental figure 5F–H). These immunosuppressive cell types were similar in abundance in Mr1+/+ and Mr1−/− mice, with the notable exception of Treg cells that were significantly more frequent in the former strain (figure 5A,B). This was not a global finding since we found no differences between tumor-bearing Mr1+/+ and Mr1−/− mice in terms of peripheral blood, splenic and pulmonary Treg cell percentages (figure 5C).

Increased Treg cell infiltration in human bladder tumors positively correlates with MAIT cell abundance and poor survival

To extend our Treg cell analyses to patients with BCa, we resorted to the TCGA Urothelial Bladder Carcinoma bulk RNA sequencing dataset again. To this end, we used a Treg cell signature marked by common Treg cell markers (CTLA4, FOXP3, IL2RA, TNFRSF18, CD4) to generate a Treg cell score for each sample. We found that similar to “MAIT High” BCa tumor samples, those with a high Treg cell score belonged to patients with poorer overall survival (figure 5D). Also, interestingly, there was a positive correlation between MAIT and Treg cell scores (figure 5D). Moreover, samples with both high MAIT cell and high Treg cell scores had even poorer overall survival (online supplemental figure 6). Therefore, the co-enrichment of MAIT and Treg cells appears detrimental in BCa.

Intravesical Ac-6-FP administration reduces tumor burden in Mr1+/+ mice

To assess the therapeutic efficacy of MAIT cell targeting in BBN-induced BCa, Mr1+/+ mice were injected with Ac-6-FP or saline intravesically (figure 6A). Therefore, these studies enabled us to test a pharmacological approach to experimental BCa immunotherapy. Moreover, we evaluated bladder tumors by H&E and immunohistochemical staining for Ki67 as additional readouts. We found animals in the Ac-6-FP cohort to display lessened bladder tissue inflammation (figure 6B) and urothelial hyperplasia (figure 6C). In addition, fewer mice presented with carcinoma and invasive carcinoma (figure 6D). Most notably, Ac-6-FP-treated mice had less Ki67+ cells in their bladder relative to saline-treated animals, a finding that was reproducible across several independent experiments (figure 6E). A similar trend was also noticeable when the proportion of Ki67+ areas within mouse bladders was quantified (online supplemental figure 7). Taken together, the above findings indicate that MAIT cells can be efficiently targeted in a preclinical model of BCa to reduce tumor burden.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that MAIT cells are present in both mouse and human bladder tumors. More importantly, they are associated with poor overall survival in patients with BCa and increased tumor burden in mice. We propose that the protumor effects of MAIT cells can be attributed to their augmented IL-17A and TNF-α secretion capacities as well as their co-enrichment with Treg cells in BCa TMEs.

Our group and others have previously reported the presence of MAIT cells in a variety of primary and metastatic tumors.13,1936 Our findings from bioinformatic analyses and immunofluorescent staining of resected human BCa tissue specimens demonstrate the presence of MAIT-like cells in bladder tumors. We also notably observed that the abundance of these cells, at least at a transcriptomic level, negatively correlates with overall patient survival, highlighting their potential impact on BCa progression and prognosis.

To date, the roles of MAIT cells in an in vivo BCa setting have not been sufficiently addressed to our knowledge. Here, we used the BBN model of BCa to fill this knowledge gap. This model is clinically relevant for several reasons. First, BBN is a potent bladder-selective chemical carcinogen derived from compounds found in tobacco smoke,37 which is the primary risk factor for BCa.2 3 Second, BBN and its metabolites exert their effects through DNA alkylation, resulting in a mutational landscape similar to that of human BCa.34 In fact, examination of TCGA and other repositories has demonstrated that similarly to human BCa, BBN tumors contain high mutational burdens with prevalent mutations in Trp53, Kmt2c and Kmt2d.34 Third, unlike most other mouse BCa models, BBN-elicited tumors can arise in immunocompetent mice, thus permitting the assessment of procancer and anticancer responses in the context of an intact immune system. Lastly, male mice are more susceptible to BCa from exposure to BBN and develop BBN-induced tumors much faster than do female animals,38 reminiscent of the reported sexual dimorphism in human BCa incidence.29

According to our findings, MAIT cells exacerbate BBN-elicited tumor burdens, while their absence confers increased protection. These results are in contrast with those of Zhang et al, who reported a protective effect for exogenously administered MAIT cells in a T24 cell-derived BCa model in nude mice.25 While the artificial T24 BCa model rapidly generates a human BCa-derived tumor, it develops subcutaneously rather than in the bladder, omitting the influence of cells resident in or recruited to the bladder during BCa progression. Additionally, using T cell-deficient nude mice precludes T cell subset analyses and crosstalk between different immune cell types, including MAIT cells in TMEs. Therefore, the BBN model overcomes the limitations of cell line-derived bladder tumors and offers more relevant information on BCa complexities.

In our study, MAIT cells isolated from BBN-treated mice exhibited a predominantly MAIT17 phenotype based on their transcription factors. Additionally, they produced higher levels of IL-17A and TNF-α upon stimulation. These findings are similar to a previous report that found elevated IL-17A and TNF-α levels in 5-OP-RU-stimulated MAIT cells isolated from the pulmonary metastases of B16-F10 melanoma-bearing mice.39 IL-17A is a potent immunomodulatory cytokine associated with increased IL-6, IL-8 and vascular endothelial growth factor production—promoting neoangiogenesis.20 IL-17A also facilitates the accumulation/expansion of various suppressor cell populations within TMEs.20 40 In a similar fashion, TNF-α has been demonstrated to support angiogenic activity and the recruitment of MDSCs to the mouse fibrosarcoma TME.41 Moreover, IL-17A and TNF-α can work cooperatively to promote tumors by stimulating glycolysis, growth factor production, and secretion of the chemoattractant CCL20, which recruits CCR6-expressing TH17 and Treg cells.42,44 This likely creates a positive feedback loop to attract these cells to the tumor bed. While TH17 and Treg cell recruitment may limit inflammation and prevent tissue damage, they simultaneously promote cell proliferation and tumor growth.

It is noteworthy that according to Zhang et al,25 PMA/ionomycin-stimulated peripheral blood MAIT cells produce more IFN-γ, TNF-α and IL-2, but not IL-17A, in patients with BCa when they are compared with healthy donor MAIT cells. However, circulating MAIT cells may not accurately represent bladder tissue-resident or intratumoral MAIT cells. It is therefore not surprising that no positive or negative correlations were noted between peripheral blood MAIT cell frequencies and tumor numbers, size, grade or invasiveness in patients with BCa studied by Zhang et al. In our investigation, a clinically relevant in vivo model demonstrated that MAIT cells do indeed impact tumor progression in BCa.

TMEs contain a complex network of effector and regulatory cells that influence cancer progression. We found the presence of MAIT cells in tumor-bearing bladders to be associated with an increase in Treg cells, as demonstrated by both our in vivo mouse model and our analyses of a human BCa dataset. Treg cells have long been recognized as potent immunomodulatory cells, capable of suppressing anticancer immunity. In human BCa specifically, higher infiltration of Treg cells has often been linked to poorer clinical outcomes, including reduced recurrence-free survival and progression-free survival following TURBT.45,47 Therefore, the suspected ability of MAIT cells to drive Treg cell infiltration into the BCa TME can constitute a mechanism through which MAIT cells contribute to tumor progression.

A recent study by Awad et al demonstrated that chemical compounds found in cigarette smoke (CS) modulate MR1 expression and MAIT cell functions.48 Also intriguingly, in vivo exposure to CS augmented IL-17 expression by MAIT cells, which is thought to contribute to the pathogenesis of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). In fact, the authors found Mr1 deficiency to partially protect against CS-associated COPD manifestations in a mouse model. Whether CS ingredients similarly induce an MAIT17 bias in the bladder mucosa, thus potentially attracting Treg cells to bladder TMEs, will be an interesting research avenue to explore.

Collectively, our work demonstrates that MAIT cells promote tumor progression, likely through secretion of IL-17A and TNF-α, and recruitment of immunosuppressive Treg cells. Therefore, MAIT cells should be regarded as an active participant in shaping the tumor immune landscape in BCa. Furthermore, intravesical treatment with Ac-6-FP showed efficacy in decreasing the BBN tumor burden, lending support to the therapeutic potentials of MAIT cell targeting in BCa. Given the monomorphic nature of MR1,49 MR1/MAIT cell-based interventions should work across genetically diverse patient populations.50 Our ongoing studies are focused on additional mechanisms underlying MAIT cell-mediated immunosuppression in BCa and other urogenital malignancies and on experimental strategies that may remedy such shortcomings.
