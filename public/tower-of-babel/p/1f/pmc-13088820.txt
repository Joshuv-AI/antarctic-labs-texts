The effect of school-based educational session on knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs of cervical cancer among adolescents in mixed day high schools in Embu County: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Lucy W Kivuti-Bitok, Caroline Wanja Mugo, Dorothy Mituki, Christine Marete, Irene Ngune, Jessica Wells
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Cervical cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related death among women in Kenya. In the year 2022 alone, Kenya recorded 3591 deaths from the disease, with a probability of other missed cases [1]. Notably, projections indicate that Kenya may experience a 120% increase in cervical cancer cases over the next two decades, a burden that will largely affect more than 13 million individuals in the adolescent population reported in 2023 [2, 3]. Studies have reported low knowledge levels regarding cervical cancer among both adults and adolescents in sub-Saharan Africa [4] and specifically in Kenya, where 30% of adolescents were reported to have not heard about cervical cancer [5]. This low level of cervical cancer literacy is a significant concern, prompting the WHO’s Global Strategy for the Elimination of Cervical Cancer to emphasize the importance of health literacy in elimination efforts [6]. A heightened focus on adolescents in matters of cervical cancer literacy should leverage adolescents at a critical stage of life that allows for malleability and plays a critical stage in the acquisition of lifestyle skills [7]. Adolescence represents a critical period for lifestyle-related behaviors, including those associated with cancer risk.

Targeted sexual health education among adolescents has been shown to reduce engagement in risky sexual behaviors [8]. Adolescents who have had formal sources of health education tend to be more cautious and engage in less risky behavior, whereas attendance of formal schools in itself has been associated with lower rates of transactional and age-disparate sex, inconsistent condom use, and engagement with multiple partners [8]. Even though the role of teachers in health education cannot be overemphasized, some educators in African settings report discomfort in discussing sensitive topics in sexual matters, potentially leading to misinformation or avoidance of the subject [8]. Hence, partnering with other health educators for school programs is vital.

In Kenya, women with lower education levels (primary and secondary school) are less likely to have ever heard about cervical cancer [5]. This could be attributed to a lack of deliberate effort to educate primary and secondary school students on cervical cancer. Education sessions targeting both adolescent boys and girls could be instrumental in addressing this knowledge gap, hence contributing to the WHO’s goal of eliminating cervical cancer by 2030 [9]. Additionally, adolescents are likely to share this information with their peers, parents and guardians, leveraging the potential ripple effect and a multilevel approach to cervical cancer prevention and early detection among the broader community [10].

The intervention was informed by the Health Belief Model and the Social Cognitive Theory, which suggest that knowledge, perceived susceptibility, self-efficacy and social modelling are key drivers of preventive health habits [11, 12]. By improving knowledge on cervical cancer and HPV and modelling open discussion through near-peer education, the intervention session aimed to increase knowledge and improve confidence to engage in preventive actions such as vaccination and dissemination of the information to family.

This study hypothesized that a structured health education session would increase cervical cancer-specific knowledge among adolescents, as school-based interventions have proven effective in enhancing knowledge and altering attitudes toward cervical cancer among adolescents [13, 14].

Few studies have focused on cervical cancer knowledge among adolescents in Kenya. There are few studies on the effect of school-based education interventions on cervical cancer. In a limited resource setting where the majority of adolescents do not have access to communication devices, alternative methods of dissemination need to be explored. To address this gap, our proposed study aimed to assess the effect of a school-based health education session on cervical cancer literacy among adolescents in Embu County, Kenya.

Using a repeated cross-sectional pre-post study design, we assessed changes in attitudes, beliefs and knowledge of cervical cancer among adolescents following a school-based educational session in mixed-day high schools in Embu County.

We aimed to contribute to efforts to eliminate cervical cancer by empowering adolescents with the knowledge needed to prevent cervical cancer and reduce its burden in Kenya.

Methods

The study used a single-group, repeated cross-sectional pre-post intervention design to assess the effects of a structured health education session on cervical cancer among adolescent boys and girls in mixed-gender secondary schools in Embu County. Data were collected from independent samples of students before and after the intervention; responses were not paired at the individual level. The two schools were conveniently sampled (one was located near an urban center, while the other was located in a rural setting). Year one and year two high school students were purposefully sampled, as they were more likely to fall within the WHO definition of adolescents.

Ethical clearance was obtained from the UoN/KNH ERC (P438/5/2023). The KNH-UoN ERC adheres to the ethical principles outlined in the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki. The informed consent to participate was obtained from the parents and the school administration, whereas assent was obtained from the respondents.

All the students were assembled in a common hall. After introductions and communication on the intended lesson on cervical cancer, the students were given the option of staying for the lesson or leaving.

Assent forms were distributed to the respondents, and the preintervention questionnaire was subsequently administered. The students could choose not to answer the preintervention questionnaire.

We adopted the CEC-61 (Conocimientos en Cáncer Cervicouterino—Knowledge in Cervical Cancer, 61 items) [15]. The questionnaire was available in English and did not need to be translated. The data were collected via a manual self-administered questionnaire. The students took an average of 30 min to complete the preintervention questionnaire.

This was followed by a 30–45-minute lesson covering different aspects of cervical cancer. The materials and contents (Appendix 1) were adopted and modified from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [16, 17] and WHO Fact sheets [18].

In line with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [16], the lesson was conducted as a PowerPoint presentation. The slides were projected, and the presenter read the content aloud. After each slide, the presenter invited the students for comments or questions. The subsections of the lecture covered 10 different domains related to anatomical location of the cervix, diagnosis of cervical cancer, risk factors for cervical cancer and its prevention, HPV transmission, symptoms of cervical cancer, testing for HPV and cervical cancer screening and the HPV vaccine and dosage. Each domain was covered for an average of 5–6 min.

The lessons were conducted by 2 trained research assistants, who were two registered nurses in their late 20 s. The choice of the research assistants was guided by closeness to the respondents’ age group. It was assumed that the respondents are likely to feel more comfortable asking research assistants of similar age to themselves questions. The research assistants had mock presentations to the rest of the research team; they read the presentation several times and out loudly to be familiar and comfortable with the content [16]. While one assistant conducted the lesson, the other stayed at the back of the classroom to monitor the class environment.

The postintervention questionnaire was administered immediately after the lesson. The postintervention questionnaire had an extra 4 questions that covered how the respondents intended to use information gained from the Education session and 3 questions about their view on the education session. In line with their ascent forms, the students had the option of not answering the postintervention questionnaire.

Each item in the questionnaire that was used to assess knowledge about cervical cancer was scored on a scale from 0 to 4, where 0 = “I do not know” (indicating no knowledge), 1 = “Strongly disagree,” 2 = “Disagree,” 3 = “Agree,” and 4 = “Strongly agree” (indicating full understanding of the item). The overall cervical cancer knowledge score was computed by summing the scores for all the individual items, with higher total scores indicating greater knowledge of cervical cancer.

Because pre- and post-intervention responses were not linked at the individual level, the study design is considered a repeated cross-sectional rather than a paired pre-post design. Accordingly, unpaired (independent-samples) t-tests were used to compare mean knowledge scores between the pre-intervention and post-intervention groups.

The collected data were processed in Microsoft excel and statistical analysis conducted using R version 4.2.2. Continuous variables are summarized as the means and standard deviations (SDs), and categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages. Welch’s two-sample t-test to compare pre and post- intervention groups scores. This is because the approach does not assume equal variances and is robust to unequal sample sizes. Effect sizes were estimated using Cohen’s d with 95% confidence intervals. For inferential statistical analysis, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

All students in the classroom participated in the educational session. Table 1 summarizes the sociodemographic characteristics of the respondents. The number of respondents in the pre- and posttest was 284 and 280, respectively. The average age of the girls in the pretest was 16.2 years, and that in the posttest was 16.0 years. Most of the respondents were in form 2, with 61.8% in the pretest and 58.99% in the posttest. Many of the respondents had 73.9% of both parents in the pretest and 76% in the posttest, implying that approximately 25% of the participants came from single-parent households or were in the care of grandparents. The majority of those who participated were not vaccinated. The majority of those who responded to the pretest were female (47.5%), whereas in the posttest, the majority were male (46.8%).

As shown in Table 2, there was a notable improvement in knowledge scores across all demographic groups following the training. Although males had higher initial scores, females had greater increases in their scores (26.5 points). Learners from rural schools had greater increases in knowledge (39.2 points) than did those from urban schools (16.6 points). The differences by school location indicated moderate gains among students attending urban schools (d = 0.39) and large gains among those in rural schools (d = 0.86).

There was a significant improvement in adolescents’ knowledge of cervical cancer across all the assessed domains following the educational intervention. Specifically, knowledge about the anatomic location of cervical cancer improved notably from a pretest mean score of 6.64 ± 3.66 to 7.86 ± 3.15, t (560.45) = 4.28, p < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.19–0.52). Similarly, significant increases were observed in the understanding of diagnostic methods (pre: 5.94 ± 3.50; post: 6.66 ± 3.44; t (568.96) = 2.50, p = 0.013; d = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.04–0.37) and awareness of cervical cancer risk factors (pre: 8.76 ± 4.09; post: 9.93 ± 4.08; t (568.76) = 3.40, p = 0.001, d = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.12–0.45). Furthermore, adolescents exhibited substantial gains in their knowledge of risks and preventive measures (pre: 16.46 ± 7.11; post: 19.46 ± 7.52; t (565.32) = 4.24, p < 0.001; d = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.19–0.52), demonstrating the program’s effectiveness in enhancing practical knowledge crucial for prevention. Improvements were also observed in domains directly associated with human papillomavirus (HPV), including HPV transmission (pre: 5.98 ± 3.18; post: 7.43 ± 3.18; t (568.67) = 5.45, p < 0.001;) d = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.29–0.62), and HPV-related symptoms (pre: 12.85 ± 7.93; post: 16.87 ± 7.08; t (564.64) = 6.38, p < 0.001; d = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.37–0.70), reinforcing the intervention’s role in addressing essential gaps in adolescent understanding of HPV. The greatest absolute improvement appeared in knowledge regarding the Papanicolaou (Pap) test, with the mean score increasing significantly from 27.38 ± 16.90 to 36.05 ± 16.80 (t (568.72) = 6.15, p = 0.001, d = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.35–0.68). Additionally, significant improvements in understanding the HPV vaccine (pre: 13.67 ± 8.67; post: 17.98 ± 9.12; t (565.85) = 5.78, p < 0.001; d = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.32–0.65) and awareness of the correct dosage of the HPV vaccine (pre: 7.54 ± 5.40; post: 9.99 ± 5.52; t (567.74) = 5.36, p < 0.001; d = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.28–0.61) were observed.

Across constructs, effect sizes ranged from small to moderate, indicating consistent improvements in cervical cancer–related knowledge following the intervention.

Overall, the respondents showed increased willingness to seek HPV vaccination, with notable differences across genders and vaccination statuses. After the educational session, 70.2% of the respondents said that they would ask their parents or guardians to take them for HPV vaccination. Among the male participants (all of whom were unvaccinated), 54.3% said they would ask to be vaccinated. Among the girls who had already been vaccinated, 56.9% were willing to request vaccination again. While 41.7% of the unvaccinated individuals were willing to be vaccinated, 31.3% reported being unwilling to be vaccinated, 6.3% were unsure, and 20.8% did not respond.

The respondents’ views of the educational session and their intended use of the information gained

The feedback on the session itself was largely positive, with most students agreeing that the delivery method was effective and that they would welcome a repeat of the lesson. Additionally, the educator was well received, although a few participants found the lesson content somewhat complicated following the educational session, many adolescents indicated a willingness to engage in discussions about cervical cancer, with several expressing intent to talk with friends and 40.99% strongly agreeing to talk to the parents about the topic (Table 4). Some participants also reported that they would ask their parents to take them for the HPV vaccine, suggesting increased motivation to take preventive health action.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the effect of a one-off structured educational session on HPV and cervical cancer among adolescents in selected mixed-gender day schools in Embu County, Kenya. We also sought to establish how the respondents intended to use the information gained during the educational session.

The findings of this study are consistent with the hypothesis that structured health education is associated with increased knowledge of cervical cancer among adolescents [19].

Statistically significant gains were observed in knowledge of cervical cancer tests (p < 0.001), awareness of risk factors (p < 0.001), and preventive measures (p < 0.001). These results suggest that the intervention may contribute to enhanced knowledge across key areas, including HPV infection and prevention strategies.

The findings also suggest that structured educational programs can improve health literacy and potentially promote informed health behaviors among adolescents. This aligns with the WHO’s Global Strategy for the Elimination of Cervical Cancer, which prioritizes adolescent health literacy as critical to achieving long-term public health goals [20].

The overall knowledge score increased from a pretest mean of 105.23 to posttest mean of 131.8, which was a statistically significant change t (567.66) = 6.89, p < 0.0001), with a 95% confidence interval for the mean difference of 19.02 to 34.18. This is comparable to the findings of [21] and [22] in a school-based health education programme where knowledge of cervical cancer among young girls in two schools in Ghana improved with educational intervention, even though the tool used by Dedey et al., [22] was different and much simpler.

An overall increase in knowledge scores was observed across all sociodemographic groups, with greater improvement noted among the respondents from rural schools. Compared with male students and Form One students, female students and Form Two students achieved greater gains. A significant increase in knowledge was noted in all the domains, but the greatest increase was detected in the Pap smear test and HPV-related symptoms. These results mirrored other studies that noted that targeted education improved the understanding of Pap smears and HPV, which are often poorly understood prior to targeted instruction [23, 24].

At baseline, fewer than half of the participants in our study reported having received the HPV vaccine, yet following the intervention, 70.22% of all the respondents and 49.7% of those who had not been vaccinated expressed a clear intention to request vaccination. These findings suggest that tailored educational efforts can play a critical role in stimulating vaccine uptake among adolescents. The significant shift from fewer than half having been vaccinated to nearly two-thirds expressing intent to be vaccinated indicates that targeted health education can positively influence vaccine-related decision-making and potentially drive higher future uptake rates. A study performed in North Uganda among adolescents revealed that students attending schools with health programs were more than twice as likely to understand that cervical cancer is preventable, highlighting the value of structured educational interventions in enhancing awareness [25]. These findings reinforce the notion that targeted health education, especially when delivered through schools, can significantly influence adolescent girls’ awareness, attitudes, and health-seeking behaviors, particularly with respect to HPV vaccination [23, 24, 26]. However, this study established that 58.3% of the unvaccinated girls either did not agree to be vaccinated, were unsure, or did not respond. This hesitancy has been echoed among young girls in Ethiopia [27] as well as among adolescents receiving comprehensive HIV care in Kenya [28]. This illuminates an area of concern that requires further exploration to understand the underlying reasons for hesitancy. Our study also revealed that awareness of cervical cancer risk improved. The willingness to discuss cervical cancer with friends and family and to pursue vaccination suggests a positive shift in health-seeking attitudes, a key goal in adolescent health promotion. This shift could be due to the active learning approach undertaken in the intervention, as demonstrated in a study performed in China that revealed that school-based education health education significantly improved adolescent awareness and positive attitudes, suggesting the potential for an improved vaccination rate [26].

The family structure data revealed that close to one-third of the participants resided with single parents, mostly mothers or grandparents. These household dynamics are relevant, as the literature indicates that adolescents from single-parent or lower-education households may have reduced access to health-related information [29]. By delivering accurate, structured content in the school setting, this intervention helped bridge informational gaps that might otherwise persist due to family-related disparities.

In addition to knowledge acquisition, the intervention positively influenced behavioral intentions. Many participants reported a willingness to share what they learned with peers and family members, echoing findings from previous studies [30] on the ripple effects of adolescent-led health education. This peer-to-peer and adolescent-to-parent information sharing can be instrumental in increasing community-level awareness and encouraging health-promoting behaviors beyond the school environment.

The in-class-based delivery method appeared more acceptable, particularly in comparison with mainstream media campaigns and social media, which may be less accessible to students in low socioeconomic areas [31]. Although some students find the content complex, many express a strong interest in additional sessions. This feedback indicates a clear demand for ongoing learning and underscores the importance of training teachers to deliver sensitive health content with confidence. As George et al., [8]. and Dedey et al., [22] suggest, such training could support the integration of sustainable health education into existing school systems and curricula.

Conclusion

Our results suggest that a structured, school-based educational interventions was associated with increased knowledge of cervical cancer and preventive intentions among adolescents in the short term. The findings indicate short-term improvements in knowledge and behavioral intentions. Future studies with control groups and longitudinal follow-up are needed to assess retention and broader community impact. To sustain and expand these benefits, future initiatives should include longitudinal follow-up to assess knowledge retention and explore innovative, scalable methods for delivering engaging and age-appropriate health education in schools.

Study limitations

This study adopted a repeated cross-sectional design, in which pre- and post-intervention data were collected from independent samples of students. Therefore, paired statistical tests were not applicable and between-group comparisons using independent-samples t-tests were employed. A similar study using a paired t test is recommended.

The CEC-61 questionnaire [15] was originally validated among adolescents in Chile, an upper-middle-income country, whereas this study was conducted in a lower-middle-income country. These contextual differences pose further limitations to the generalizability of the results.

Additionally, a significant number of respondents reported difficulty understanding certain items in the CEC-61 questionnaire, which was originally developed and validated among adolescents in Chile, a setting with linguistic, educational and cultural contextual differences. This highlights limited cross-cultural applicability of the tool in its current form underscoring the need to develop a culturally adapted, age-appropriate and comprehension-level-appropriate version of the questionnaire for adolescents in Kenya and similar settings. Future research should prioritize the co-designing of a locally relevant version of the questionnaire, ideally involving input from local adolescents, educators and health professionals to ensure clarity, relevance and contextual appropriateness.

Furthermore, the absence of control group limits the ability to solely attribute the observed changes to the intervention as external factors cannot be ruled out. Repeated cross- sectional design with unpaired data also precludes the assessment of individual change overtime. The study also relied on self-reported behavioral intentions rather than observed actions e.g. vaccine uptake or dissemination of information. Future studies should therefore incorporate control groups, longitudinal paired designs and objective behavioral measures to strengthen causal inference and assess real-world impact.

Although multiple statistical tests were conducted across knowledge domains and participant subgroups, formal adjustments for multiple comparisons were not applied. While outcomes were pre-specified and analyses were largely exploratory, the possibility of inflated type I error cannot be fully excluded. To support interpretation of the results, effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals were reported alongside p-values. Future studies employing individual-level longitudinal designs and confirmatory analytic frameworks are needed to validate and extend these findings.
