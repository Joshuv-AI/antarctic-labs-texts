Ceramic‐on‐Ceramic or Metal‐on‐Polyethylene: The Bearing of Choice after Ceramic Component Fracture in Total Hip Arthroplasty along with Concise Follow‐Up of the Previous Cohort
Authors: Hong Seok Kim, Han Jin Lee, Soong Joon Lee, Jeong Joon Yoo
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe previously reported the questionable result of reoperation using metal‐on‐polyethylene (MoP) bearing after the fracture of the ceramic component. After the report, we abandoned the use of MoP; instead, we used ceramic‐on‐ceramic (CoC) bearing. This report aimed to present the outcome of reoperation to CoC bearing after ceramic component failures along with the longer‐term outcome of the previously reported cohort with an MoP bearing.ResultsThe mean follow‐up of the MoP cohort was extended from 4.3 years to 8.8 years. Metallosis had occurred in three of nine patients of the previous cohort, which all required re‐reoperations. Two hips from 11 patients with revision to CoC had re‐reoperations due to ceramic liner fracture and non‐union of the osteotomized fragment, respectively. No metallosis and wear occurred in the cohort revised with CoC articulation. Otherwise, there were no adverse changes in radiographs and no impairment in functions.MethodsWe conducted an extended prospective evaluation of a previous cohort consisting of six head fractures and three liner fractures. In this cohort, the patients underwent a bearing change to metal‐on‐polyethylene. Additionally, we retrospectively analyzed 11 cases of third‐generation ceramic bearing fractures, comprising nine head fractures and two liner fractures. These cases were subsequently treated with a bearing change to fourth‐generation CoC. We assessed clinical and radiological outcomes, including complication rates, in both groups.ConclusionWe recommend the latest CoC as the bearing of choice in reoperation after the fracture of ceramic components. In the case of an MoP bearing after the ceramic component fracture, the risk of metallosis seemed high in the early postoperative period.

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) bearings play a crucial role in the success and longevity of hip replacement surgeries. 1 Over the years, the development of bearing materials has undergone significant advancements to address the challenges associated with wear and friction. In the past, metal‐on‐metal bearings were utilized, but their use has significantly decreased due to concerns over metallosis and adverse local tissue reactions, prompting a shift towards alternative bearing materials. 2 Then, metal‐on‐polyethylene (MoP) bearings were widely used and proven its good longevity; however, concerns regarding polyethylene wear and the release of metal debris causing metallosis emerged as possible drawbacks of this material. 3

Ceramic‐on‐ceramic (CoC) bearing THA has proven its lowest wear rate that leads to minimal osteolysis and longer implant survival. 4 , 5 , 6 Also, ceramic bearings offer better biocompatibility than metal bearings. 7 However, ceramic‐specific complications such as noise, stem neck notching, malseating, and ceramic fracture were still a concern. 8 , 9 , 10 Among them, ceramic fractures are catastrophic and inevitably require reoperation.

Ceramic component fractures have been extensively studied, with reported fracture rates ranging from 0% to 10% for alumina ceramics. 11 The introduction of the fourth generation alumina matrix composite ceramic (AMC) has significantly reduced the incidence of ceramic fractures. Hallan et al. reported comparable rates of ceramic fracture in the Norwegian Arthroplasty Register, with an incidence of 0.15% for alumina femoral head fracture, 0.01% for AMC head fractures, and 0.08% for AMC liner fractures. 12 However, as the number of CoC‐bearing THA procedures increases, so does the number of patients experiencing ceramic fractures.

We previously reported the questionable result of changing the bearings to MoP after ceramic component fractures. 13 Massive metallosis due to the third body wear by remnant ceramic particles was first reported in 1998. 14 Further, elevated cobalt or chromium ion levels in serum by metallosis could lead to fatal systemic complications. 15 In the same context, our previous study reported that reoperation with an MoP bearing after ceramic component fractures showed disastrous results with the occurrence of metallosis. We abandoned the use of an MoP bearing; instead, we utilized a CoC bearing in case of fracture of the ceramic bearing. Though an implicit consensus exists on using a CoC bearing after ceramic articulation failure, there was a scarcity of reports due to the rarity of the complication.

The aim of this study was: (i) to update the longer‐term outcomes of the previously reported reoperation THA with an MoP bearing to further investigate bearing‐related complications; and (ii) to present and compare the outcome of reoperation with CoC bearings after ceramic component failures with those of revisions with MoP bearings.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

Institutional review board approval of our hospital was obtained for this study (H‐2205‐123‐1327). This study comprised two cohorts, both of which underwent reoperation following ceramic component fracture. The first cohort consisted of patients with an extended follow‐up from a previously reported study, while the second cohort included patients who visited our hospital specifically for ceramic component fractures after the first cohort.

Previous Cohort

We conducted the meticulous follow‐up of the previous cohort. The patient cohort from the previous study consisted of nine individuals who underwent reoperations due to ceramic bearing fractures. These five male and four female patients were treated at our institution between November 1999 and December 2013, and the follow‐up period was extended until July 2020. Six femoral heads and three liners of the original third generation alumina‐on‐alumina bearing were found to be fractured. The primary THAs were performed at an average age of 45.8 ± 21.9 years (range, 19.6–63.4 years). Diagnosis of ceramic fracture occurred at a mean interval of 6.9 ± 4.2 years (range, 2.0–14.3 years) after the index surgery. The etiology for the primary THA was osteonecrosis in six patients, femoral neck fracture in two patients, and degenerative arthritis in the remaining patient.

The mean follow‐up duration for this previous cohort was extended from 4.3 ± 1.9 years (range, 2.0–7.3 years) to 8.8 ± 5.4 years (range, 2.4–15.5 years). Previously, we worried about subsequent ceramic component fractures by damaged taper surfaces or by fractured ceramic particles. Thus, we changed the bearing to metal‐on‐polyethylene bearings to retain well‐fixed implants without concerns about ceramic refracture. The 28‐mm metal head composed of cobalt‐chrome alloy and a PE liner were used. Radiological and clinical evaluations were conducted using the same methodology as for the novel cohort.

The Novel Cohort of Reoperation with Delta Ceramic‐on‐Ceramic Bearing

Between April 2008 and July 2020, an additional 11 alumina ceramic fracture patients – nine ceramic heads and two ceramic liners – visited the emergency department. Primary THA was performed at the mean age of 44.4 ± 13.4 years (range, 25.2–60.1 years). The etiology of primary THA was osteonecrosis in 10 patients, and degenerative arthritis in the remaining patient. In nine hips, Plasma cups (Aesculap AG, Tuttlingen, Germany) with BiContact Stems (Aesculap AG) were implanted. The other two patients underwent THAs with Duraloc cups (DePuy, Leeds, UK) with AML stem (DePuy) and Coren cup (Corentec, Cheonan, Korea) with Coren stem (Corentec), respectively. All ceramic heads and liners were made of third‐generation alumina ceramic (BIOLOX® Forte; CeramTec, Plochingen, Germany). Reoperation of Ceramic Fracture

After the diagnosis of ceramic fractures, reoperation was planned and undergone immediately. The fourth‐generation alumina matrix composite (AMC) ceramic bearing (BIOLOX® Delta; CeramTec) was used in all patients. During reoperation, macroscopic ceramic particles were removed; thorough synovectomy was performed as previously described; 13 and pulsatile lavage was done. All stems were securely fixed and retained during reoperation. In one patient with a liner fracture, the cup was removed and revised for the complete removal of ceramic particles. We thoroughly investigated the trunnion of the stem. If there was no damage or little damage on the trunnion, then revision to a regular ceramic head was preferred. If a few scratches or suspected damages to the trunnion were noted, BIOLOX® delta option ceramic femoral head (CeramTec) – a metal‐sleeved ceramic head ‐ was used. If there was a definite ablation of the trunion, then the revision of the stem was planned. Finally, BIOLOX® delta liner was inserted. Clinical Evaluation

Patients were followed up at postoperative 6 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year. After the postoperative 1 year, patients were followed up annually. Clinical evaluations were performed with the use of the modified Harris hip score (mHHS) 16 and a questionnaire that included items regarding any noise during daily activity (the presence of noise; the nature, time of onset, and frequency of the noise as well as activities associated with the noise; awareness of the noise by other people; the intensity of the noise over time; associated pain; and the effect of the noise on the quality of life).

Radiographic Evaluation

Radiographs were evaluated by two independent observers (HSK and HJL) with respect to component stability, 17 radiolucent lines, 18 calcar resorption, osteolysis, 19 and metallosis. 20 The zones described by Gruen et al. 21 and those described by DeLee and Charnley 22 were used to assess the location and extent of radiolucent lines and osteolysis. Osteolysis was defined as a periprosthetic cystic or scalloped lesion with a diameter exceeding 2 mm that had not been present on the immediate postoperative radiograph. 19 We also checked serum chromium (Cr) levels in patients with suspicious findings of metallosis.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using a t‐test. Categorical data are presented as counts (percentages) and were analyzed using chi‐square or Fisher's exact test. p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0. (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Longer‐term Outcome of the Previous Cohort of MoP Bearings

Regarding metallosis, no further related complications and/or re‐reoperations due to metallosis were observed in either the previously diagnosed patients or the novel cohort. We reported three re‐reoperations due to metallosis, and each patient was followed up more for an additional 6.6, 7.8, and 8.8 years, respectively; there was no sign of additional metallosis. Previously, elevated serum Cr level was stabilized in three patients. The mean duration of follow‐up of the previous cohort was extended from 4.3 years to 8.8 years with no other complications noted. Two patients (Patients #4 and #7) who cannot visit the clinic due to medical comorbidities were interviewed by telephone. Patient #4 did not have any complaints. Patient #7 who had repeated debridement for periprosthetic joint infection has also been free of symptoms for 10.5 years.

Outcome of the Cohort of Reoperation with CoC Bearing

For the cohort of reoperation with CoC bearing, there was no radiologic sign of osteolysis, loosening, or metallosis during the mean follow‐up period of 4.5 ± 2.3 years. In clinical evaluation, the mean mHHS improved from 41.6 (range, 37–45) points preoperatively to 92.1 (range, 85–100) points at the final follow‐up. Complications of the Cohort of Reoperation with CoC Bearing

There were two complications noted in the cohort of reoperation with CoC bearing. One patient suffered another subsequent ceramic liner fracture after 3 months of reoperation. During the first reoperation, both the head and liner were changed to AMC bearing. Three months after the reoperation, he slipped and fell with his operated hip hyperextended. On the simple radiograph and CT scan, the broken ceramic liner fragment was found, and re‐reoperation was planned and performed with another AMC bearing with a longer neck ceramic head (Figure 3).

The other patient had a re‐reoperation due to trochanteric non‐union 2 months after the first reoperation. The trochanter was re‐fixed with cerclage wire and grip plate. The patient was without complaint at the latest follow‐up (Figure 4).

Discussion

The use of MoP bearings following the ceramic component fracture should be avoided, rather the CoC bearings were recommended as the preferred bearing of choice. Previously, catastrophic results of metallosis were reported; no additional metallosis was noted in the extended follow‐up. In the present study, the reoperations with CoC bearing have demonstrated encouraging results with good clinical and radiographic outcomes without any sign of metallosis. Nevertheless, the fracture of the revised ceramic component is still a concern.

Incidence of Ceramic Component Fracture

The incidence of ceramic component fractures has been continuously studied. Massin et al. 11 reported fracture rates range between 0% and 10% with a median close to 0 for alumina ceramic. Howard et al. analyzed the National Joint Registry for England, Wales, Northern, Ireland, and the Isle of Man and found the fracture rate of 0.1% of alumina heads and 0.01% of AMC heads, and the risk for liner fracture was 0.1% for both ceramic types. 23 Recently, Hallan et al. studied the Norwegian Arthroplasty Register from 1997 to 2017, and reported comparable rates of ceramic fracture; the incidence of alumina femoral head fracture was 0.15%, that of AMC head fractures was 0.01%, and that of AMC liner was 0.08%. 12 As reported, the introduction of AMC ceramic has dramatically decreased the incidence of ceramic fracture from an already low incidence. However, as the number of CoC bearing THA accumulates, so does the number of patients with ceramic fractures.

Metallosis

Metallosis as a complication of bearing change to MoP after ceramic component fracture was considered to be due to third‐body wear. The retrieved metal head and polyethylene liner from the patient with metallosis exhibited scratches on the surface and embedded tiny ceramic particles, respectively. 13 The metallosis was still continuously reported, whose presentation mimicked periprosthetic joint infection or pseudotumor. 14 , 24 , 25 , 26 Not only the fact that metallosis requires re‐reoperation but also the possibility of cardiotoxicity due to heavy metal poisoning was the major problem. 15 However, numerous reports indicated that there were no more metallosis‐related events after re‐reoperation with bearing change to CoC or ceramic‐on‐polyethylene. 24 , 26 In our analysis of extended follow‐up of the previous cohorts, also no subsequent complications were noted in the re‐reoperated patients with metallosis. If metallosis was suspected in reoperated patients with MoP bearings after ceramic component fracture, immediate debridement, thorough synovectomy, and bearing change to CoC would prevent subsequent complications and improve the survival of revised implants.

MoP versus CoC: The Bearing of Choice after Ceramic Component Fracture

In the available literature, there has been poor consensus on the long‐term outcome of revision for fracture of ceramic components; some advocate both MoP and CoC bearing, 27 whereas others reported the poor outcome of MoP articulation. 28 , 29 The strength of this study lies in that we directly compared the results of those revised with MoP and with CoC bearing within the same protocol of the single institution. The result of our study was comparable to the recent studies from Im et al. 30 and Trebse et al.;31 however, the emphasis on the detrimental consequence of metallosis did not seem to be sufficient. We observed a disparity in the follow‐up periods of the two groups, which can be attributed to our decision to utilize CoC bearings as the preferred choice following complications associated with MoP cases. Consequently, the duration of follow‐up differed between the two groups. However, it is important to highlight that most major complications in the MoP revision cases occurred during the early stages of follow‐up (6.9 months, 3.1 years, and 5.7 years for metallosis, and 3 years for periprosthetic joint infection); no other complications were observed during the extended follow‐up analysis of the previous cohort. Hence, our study suggests that patients with CoC bearings exhibited superior outcomes compared to the other cohort, with a lower re‐reoperation rate. Even with timely reoperation, extensive synovectomy, and pulsatile lavage, metallosis due to the third body wear of MoP articulation cannot be fully avoided.

Trunnion Damage

Trunnion damage in ceramic head fracture was a concern, but little has been studied. The incidence and mechanism of trunnion damage were too complicated to characterize, and the scoring system was hard to apply clinically. 32 Also, it was difficult to discriminate true trunnion damage by ceramic particles from fretting and corrosion due to repetitive motion in the surgical field. Recently, ceramic heads with titanium adapter sleeves showed less severe fretting and corrosion with non‐inferior clinical results in revision THA. 33 , 34 This is comparable in our cohort; we used metal‐sleeved ceramic heads in most cases in bearing change to CoC bearing and found no related complications during follow‐up. When trunnion damage was suspected, a titanium adaptor sleeve ceramic head would be a reliable option, with minimal radiological and clinical complications related to metal‐sleeve and trunnion interactions. If ablation of the trunnion was noted, then femoral stem revision was advised.

Limitations

We note the limitations of this study. First, the study design was retrospective without randomization, and the two cohorts were operated in separate periods. Second, the number of patients was inevitably small due to the rarity of ceramic bearing failure. Third, though this report consisted of an extended follow‐up of the previous report, still the follow‐up duration was relatively short. A study with longer‐term follow‐up of a larger cohort would be warranted in the future.

Conclusion

In cases of ceramic component fracture, reoperation with MoP bearing should be avoided due to the high rate of metallosis. Instead of MoP bearing, we recommend the latest CoC as the bearing of choice, which showed encouraging clinical and radiological outcomes. When any trunnion damage was suspected, a ceramic head with a titanium adaptor sleeve should be considered.
