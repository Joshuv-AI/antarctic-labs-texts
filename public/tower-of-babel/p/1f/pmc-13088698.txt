Dynamic changes in the corpus callosum in a case of HIVEP2-related disorder: a case report and literature review
Authors: Zetong Zhu, Mingshuang Ma, Xiaoyu Cui, Jianbo Shu, Yang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAutosomal dominant intellectual developmental disorder type 43 (MRD43) is a rare neurodevelopmental disorder caused by mutations in the HIVEP2 gene. Although previous cases have established core phenotypic features, detailed descriptions of neuroimaging evolution and effective epilepsy management strategies remain limited. This case presents novel findings: it identifies a previously unreported pathogenic variant, documents dynamic changes in the corpus callosum, and details a successful combination antiepileptic drug regimen, providing comprehensive insights into the natural course and clinical management of this disorder.Case PresentationWe report a 14-year-old male who presented with global developmental delay, intellectual disability, and epilepsy. An initial brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) suggested slight thinning of the corpus callosum (involving the genu, rostrum, and body), though a subsequent review found no abnormalities. Genetic testing identified a novel de novo pathogenic variant in the HIVEP2 gene (NM_006734.4: c.3412 C > T, p.Q1138X), confirming the diagnosis of MRD43. His seizures were effectively controlled with a combination of levetiracetam and lamotrigine, which was accompanied by improvement in his electroencephalogram findings.ConclusionsThis case expands the clinical and radiological spectrum of MRD43. The observation of evolving corpus callosum abnormalities suggests a previously underappreciated progressive neurodevelopmental component to the disorder. Furthermore, the positive response to combination antiepileptic therapy offers a practical management reference for clinicians. This report underscores the importance of longitudinal neuroimaging and tailored treatment in managing HIVEP2-related disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06663-3.

Introduction

Autosomal dominant intellectual developmental disorder 43 (MRD43, OMIM: 616977) is a rare autosomal dominant disorder caused by heterozygous mutations in the HIVEP2 gene. Since Ranch et al. [1] first reported a novel HIVEP2 gene variant in a 21-year-old female patient in 2012, a limited number of additional cases have further outlined the clinical spectrum of this disease. Core features include developmental delay and intellectual disability, often accompanied by hypotonia, nonspecific dysmorphic facies, behavioral abnormalities, and seizures [2, 3]. Nevertheless, the full molecular and phenotypic spectrum of MRD43 remains incompletely characterized due to the scarcity of reported cases [3].

HIVEP2(human immunodeficiency virus type 1 enhancer binding protein 2), also known as Schnurri-2, is located on chromosome 6q23–q24, and encodes a large zinc finger–containing transcription factor highly expressed in brain regions such as the frontal cortex and hippocampus [4–6]. This protein participates in dopaminergic signaling and regulates several neurodevelopmental pathways, including NF-κB, somatostatin receptor II, and c-Myc, and other pathways [4–7]. Haploinsufficiency of the HIVEP2 gene has been identified as a major pathogenic mechanism, consistent with observations in mouse models—where deletion of this gene induces hyperactivity, anxiety-like behaviors, and schizophrenia-associated endophenotypes [8, 9]. These findings underscore the critical role of HIVEP2 in normal brain development and function.

The purpose of this work is to describe the case of a 14-year-old male carrying a novel de novo pathogenic variant in HIVEP2. His clinical presentation was characterized by intellectual disability, developmental delay, and epilepsy. Notably, dynamic changes in the corpus callosum observed on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and his seizures were successfully controlled with a combination of levetiracetam and lamotrigine, accompanied by improved electroencephalogram (EEG) findings. Furthermore, we provide a review of all cases published to date, with a focus on molecular data and phenotypic implications.

Case presentation

Clinical presentation

The patient is a 14-year-old male, the second child of non-consanguineous healthy Chinese parents, with an unremarkable birth and negative family history. His developmental milestones were delayed: he walked independently at age 2, began speaking at age 4, and was diagnosed with language delay at a local hospital at age 5, subsequently receiving long-term special education.

At age 7, he presented to our neurology department with a 4-month history of intermittent, afebrile seizures. The episodes manifested as behavioral arrest, blank staring, and impaired consciousness during wakefulness (particularly upon waking), sometimes accompanied by forward bending or involuntary smiling. Each spell lasted 4–5 s, occurring in clusters over 2–10 min, with a maximum daily frequency of 7–8 times.

Physical examination at admission revealed age-appropriate motor skills but notably delayed language abilities, with expression limited to short phrases and dysarthria. He could follow commands and communicate simply, despite his overall language performance falling below age level. No other abnormalities were noted.

Supportive tests and treatment course

Comparison of serial sagittal T1-weighted brain MRIs revealed dynamic changes of the corpus callosum: from mild thinning of the genu, rostrum, and body at age 3 to complete normalization at age 7. Video electroencephalogram (VEEG) at age 7 showed background slowing with sharp and sharp-slow wave discharges in the left central region. Metabolic screening revealed elevated plasma levels of methionine (Met) and tryptophan (Trp), as well as an increased arginine-to-ornithine ratio (Arg/Orn). Renal ultrasound indicated left pyelectasis (5 mm). All other investigations, including routine laboratory tests and additional organ ultrasounds, were unremarkable.

The patient was initially diagnosed with epilepsy (focal seizures), intellectual disability, and left pyelectasis. Treatment with valproate was initiated but provided inadequate seizure control. The antiepileptic regimen was therefore adjusted to a combination of lamotrigine and levetiracetam, which resulted in complete seizure control. A follow-up VEEG at age 13 showed an abnormal adolescent pattern characterized by background slowing.

Genetic findings and final diagnosis

Following informed consent and ethical approval, trio whole-exome sequencing (trio-WES) was performed on peripheral blood samples from the proband and his parents, with Sanger sequencing confirmation. A novel de novo heterozygous nonsense mutation, c.3412 C > T (p.Q1138X), was identified in exon 5 of the HIVEP2 gene (Fig. 1). This variant is absent from previous literature. According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines [10], it was classified as pathogenic (criteria: PVS1, PS2, PM2). The variant highly correlates with the patient’s core phenotype, including intellectual disability, seizures, developmental delay, and corpus callosum abnormalities, thereby establishing the definitive diagnosis of MRD43. Discussion and conclusion

As of February 28, 2025, a comprehensive literature search using the keywords “HIVEP2”, “MRD43”, and “Autosomal dominant intellectual developmental disorder 43” across CNKI and PubMed databases identified 24 reported cases. These include 21 de novo variants (nonsense, missense, and frameshift), 2 inherited variants from pedigrees, and 1 variant of unknown origin [2, 3, 5, 8, 11–20].

As summarized in Table 1 [2, 3, 5, 8, 11–20], the consolidated clinical spectrum across 23 pathogenic/likely pathogenic cases (including ours) is characterized by universal intellectual disability and language delay, with most patients (91.3%) also exhibiting motor delay. Associated manifestations include nonspecific dysmorphic features (56.5%, e.g., smiling face, hirsutism); behavioral abnormalities such as attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorders (ASD), anxiety, and more; hypotonia (47.8%); and neuroimaging abnormalities (43.5%) including corpus callosum thinning, myelin hypoplasia, and reduced brain volume as well as one case of external hydrocephalus [3]. Less common findings involved gastrointestinal (e.g., reflux, constipation) and ocular manifestations (e.g., strabismus, hyperopia). Seizures were reported in five patients, with variable semiology. Notably, recent reports suggest a broadening phenotypic spectrum. One case of rapidly progressive cerebral atrophy indicates potential neurodegenerative severity, while an adult with a HIVEP2 frameshift variant developed parkinsonism at age 49, coinciding with earlier reports of tremor and possible parkinsonism in a pediatric case [8, 11, 14]. Supporting this clinical observation, a recent study found that circHIVEP2—a circular RNA derived from HIVEP2—is significantly downregulated in Parkinson’s disease and may influence cognitive function [21]. These data may suggest that HIVEP2-related disorders may confer lifelong neurological vulnerability, including risk for progressive Parkinsonian syndromes. Therefore, given this emerging evidence of adult-onset and progressive manifestations, we recommend implementing a proactive long-term follow-up protocol. This protocol should include: (1) Neurological monitoring to detect functional decline or new-onset seizures; (2) Regular cognitive and behavioral assessments to track developmental trajectories and identify late-onset deficits; (3) Monitoring for movement disorders, particularly symptoms of Parkinsonism, dystonia, or ataxia.

At the mechanistic level, the transcription factor Schnurri-2 encoded by HIVEP2 serves as a crucial regulator for maintaining neural development and function. This protein not only acts as an endogenous inhibitor of the NF-κB signaling pathway but also directly regulates the expression of glutamate receptors, thereby modulating synaptic plasticity. Animal studies have shown that upregulation of Schnurri-2 enhances the expression of relevant glutamate receptor subunits [22], while its deficiency leads to activation of the NF-κB pathway, resulting in mild chronic brain inflammation [23]. Furthermore, in Schnurri-2 knockout mice, impaired maturation of hippocampal dentate gyrus granule cells, abnormal dendritic spine morphology, weakened short-term synaptic plasticity, and impaired GABAergic neurotransmission were observed [23, 24]. These abnormalities collectively disrupt the excitatory/inhibitory balance in neural networks, which clinically manifests as complex phenotypes including intellectual disability, behavioral abnormalities, and epilepsy.

Building on this, the potential role of HIVEP2 in white matter development, particularly myelination, also warrants attention. Animal model studies indicate that Schnurri-2 deficiency leads to downregulated expression of myelin markers 2’,3’-cyclic nucleotide 3’-phosphodiesterase (CNPase) and myelin basic protein (MBP), accompanied by cortical thinning [23]. Clinically, corpus callosum thinning is also a known imaging feature of this disorder. Within this context, the observed dynamic normalization of the corpus callosum on serial MRI is a novel finding in MRD43. While the mechanism is unclear, potential explanations include activity-dependent axonal pruning, as demonstrated in animal models [25], or delayed myelination given that this process can continue through adolescence [26]. This phenomenon parallels the documented reversibility of white matter abnormalities in other conditions, such as phenylketonuria with early intervention [27] and the “catch-up” development observed in preterm infants [28], suggesting the possibility of a common, yet unverified, repair pathway.

Seizures represent a rare and underreported phenotype among HIVEP2-associated conditions. Previous reports, including those by Steinfeld [14] and Hauser [11], have described seizures in a few cases but lacked detailed descriptions of symptoms and treatment records. Notably, one case exhibited inadequate control with valproate [20], while another achieved good control using levetiracetam monotherapy [18]. Compared to this limited evidence, our case establishes the efficacy of combined treatment with levetiracetam and lamotrigine, thereby providing a valuable therapeutic reference for managing epilepsy associated with MRD43.

The efficacy of levetiracetam and lamotrigine in controlling seizures in this case may be mechanistically linked to glutamatergic system dysfunction observed in HIVEP2-related disorders. Animal models of HIVEP2 deficiency demonstrate impaired glutamatergic signaling by reducing expression of key receptor subunits (such as GluA1 of α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptors (AMPARs)), weakening their function, and impairing N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (NMDARs) activation [24, 29]. These findings are further supported by evidence that Schnurri-2 promotes the expression of GluA1 and GluN2D subunits [22], collectively indicating that HIVEP2 deficiency disrupts excitatory-inhibitory balance which may result in compensatory neuronal hyperexcitability. Building on these established findings and the known mechanisms of the drugs, we propose a testable hypothesis. Given that Levetiracetam, by binding to synaptic vesicle protein 2 A (SV2A), and lamotrigine, by blocking voltage-gated sodium/calcium channels, both converge on inhibiting this excessive presynaptic glutamate release [30, 31], we hypothesize that their combined inhibition of glutamate release counteracts the network hyperactivity caused by HIVEP2 mutations, thereby restoring stability and controlling seizures. This hypothesis requires further validation in targeted studies.

A question that worth further exploration is why seizures become a prominent phenotype in only a minority of patients with HIVEP2 mutations. A plausible explanation is that the emergence of seizures may depend on the convergence of two key conditions: the mutation must cause irreversible damage to core functional domains essential for maintaining neural network stability, and the individual’s genetic background must be insufficient to compensate for this loss of function. Specifically, seizure-associated variants (such as p.Q1138X in this case), are located within or adjacent to the coding regions predicted to encode the nuclear localization signal or DNA-binding zinc finger domains [6]. Structurally, these variants likely cause complete loss of transcriptional regulatory capacity, significantly reducing neural network stability. In contrast, many other mutations occur outside or downstream of core functional domains. Their protein products may retain partial function or be partially compensated by homologous proteins like HIVEP1/3, resulting in lower independent risk of seizures induction [6]. Furthermore, the penetrance of the seizure phenotype is strongly modified by individual genetic background [32], such as mutations (e.g., p. Arg2223*) exhibiting phenotypic heterogeneity across different patients. Therefore, seizures can be understood as a “conditional penetrance” phenotype, whose occurrence requires not only severe impairment of the HIVEP2 mutation to its critical transcriptional regulatory function, but also the addition of an individual’s unique genetic susceptibility background. The synergistic interaction of these two factors ultimately exceeds the compensatory threshold of the neural network.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single case report, the findings of this study require validation in larger cohorts to confirm their generalizability. Second, the proposed mechanistic hypotheses—including the “presynaptic compensation” model explaining treatment response and the “conditional penetrance” model elucidating epilepsy rarity—remain speculative despite being grounded in published literature and clinical observations. These hypotheses urgently require validation through direct functional testing and evaluation in additional patients. Finally, the long-term neurodevelopmental trajectory and optimal management of HIVEP2-related disorders remain to be defined through prospective studies.

Despite these limitations, this study identified a novel de novo nonsense mutation in the HIVEP2 gene (c.3412 C > T (p.Q1138X)) as the causative variant, reports the first evidence of dynamic normalization of the corpus callosum as a unique neuroimaging phenotype in this disorder, and demonstrates the efficacy of the levetiracetam and lamotrigine combination therapy for HIVEP2-related epilepsy. These findings significantly expand the genotype and phenotype spectrum of MRD43, providing critical insights for the diagnosis and management of future cases.
