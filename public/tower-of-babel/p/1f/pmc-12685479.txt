Is Condylar Constrained Knee Prosthesis Necessary for Femoral Condylar Avulsion Fractures in Primary Total Knee Arthroplasty?
Authors: Wei Ji, Peng Zhang, Lianping Wan, Shengtao Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveIntraoperative femoral condylar avulsion fractures during total knee arthroplasty (TKA) are rare but potentially lead to joint instability and poor outcomes if not properly managed. However, the necessity of using condylar‐constrained prostheses in these cases remains controversial. This retrospective study examines the incidence, management approaches, and radiological outcomes of these fractures.MethodsA total of 47 patients (11 males, 36 females; mean age 67.1 ± 7.0 years) with femoral condylar avulsion fractures were identified from 4290 TKAs performed between January 2008 and December 2022, matched with nonfracture patients at a 1:1 ratio by age, gender, and BMI. Intraoperative fractures were treated using cancellous bone screws or nonabsorbable sutures based on the size of the fracture fragment, without the insertion of condylar constrained prostheses. All patients underwent outpatient follow‐up, with data collected on preoperative diagnosis, body mass index (BMI), knee range of motion (ROM), and type of prosthesis used. Key radiological indicators assessed included proximal tibia varus angle (PTVA), distal femoral valgus angle (DFVA), joint line congruence angle (JLCA), hip‐knee‐ankle angle (HKA), and preoperative subluxation status.ResultsThe incidence of femoral condylar avulsion fracture in primary TKA was found to be 1.1%. Over a follow‐up period of 1.5–3 years, no instability was noted in any patients. Significant differences were observed between fracture and nonfracture groups in PTVA (82.02 ± 3.39 vs. 85.32 ± 1.87, p = 0.01), DFVA (85.53 ± 2.73 vs. 87.51 ± 5.29, p = 0.02), and HKA (8.81 ± 3.30 vs. 6.53 ± 2.21, p = 0.01). However, the Knee Society Score (KSS) at last follow‐up showed no statistical difference (p = 0.05).ConclusionFemoral condylar avulsion fractures during primary TKA may be linked to joint deformities. Fixation methods using cancellous bone screws or nonabsorbable sutures, combined with a hinged knee brace, resulted in favorable clinical and radiological outcomes, with no need for prosthesis modification.Keywords: femoral condylar avulsion fractures, incidence, joint deformities, radiological follow‐up, total knee arthroplasty (TKA), treatment methods

Introduction

Intraoperative fracture in primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a rare clinical complication, with an incidence of 0.39%–2.2% [1, 2]. Such fractures may lead to displacement and joint instability if neglected or improperly treated, potentially resulting in premature prosthesis failure. With the growing demand for TKA, rare complications appear to be more common in daily clinical practice. Femoral condyle fracture caused by avulsion of the medial collateral ligament (MCL) and lateral collateral ligament (LCL) remains an important type of intraoperative fracture in TKA, yet there is a paucity of literature detailing its risk factors and underlying mechanisms [3, 4, 5]. AsMCL and LCL greatly contribute to the coronal stability of knees, improper handling of avulsion fractures may lead to laxity of MCL and LCL, thereby triggering early repair surgery due to knee instability, increased insert wear, and loss of function.

Femoral condyle fracture caused by avulsion of MCL and LCL during primary TKA mainly occurs on the femoral side at the time of surgical exposure, osteotomy, trial prosthesis installation, and polyethylene insert [1, 2, 6, 7]. Femoral condyle fracture may be associated with osteoporosis, obesity, imbalance between flexion and extension gaps, and femoral intercondylar osteotomy. Current treatments for femoral condyle fracture include screw fixation, replacement of condylar constrained prosthesis, selection of PLUS INSERT, rivet suture, thickened insert, and artificial material or allograft reinforcement suture [8, 9, 10, 11].

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate whether intraoperative femoral condylar avulsion fractures during primary TKA can be effectively managed without the use of a condylar constrained knee prosthesis, by assessing the incidence, treatments, and radiological follow‐up outcomes. The secondary objective is to explore the risk factors associated with such fractures.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

We obtained approval for this retrospective study from the ethics committee of our hospital in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Approval No. KYLL‐2024023). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. A total of 4290 TKAs from January 2008 to December 2022 at the department of joint surgery were completed. We recruited 47 patients with femoral condylar avulsion fracture in TKA and matched them with nonfracture patients for gender, age, and BMI, with a ratio of 1:1. All surgeries were performed by Dr. Gao and Dr. Zhang, each with over 15 years of experience in joint replacement surgery. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with femoral condylar avulsion fracture in primary TKA; (2) intraoperative screw or nonabsorbable suture fixation. Patients were excluded if (1) they had a history of knee joint surgery; (2) they had a history of MCL or LCL injuries. Knee prostheses used included GEMINI PS (LINK, Germany), Attune PS (Depuy, USA), Genesis II PS (Smith & Nephew, USA), Vanguard PS (Biomed, USA), and A3 (AIKANG, China).

According to the hospital information system (HIS), among the 47 patients with femoral condylar avulsion fracture, 11 were males and 36 were females, with an average age of 67.15 ± 7.02 years and body mass index (BMI) of 25.80 ± 3.95. In the matched control group, there were 15 males and 32 females, with an average age of 67.09 ± 6.76 years and BMI of 27.12 ± 4.09. Preoperative knee range of motion (ROM) was recorded. Imaging Evaluation

All patients underwent preoperative X‐rays of both lower extremities in a standing position including hip and ankle, as well as knee joint in the standing position of both anteroposterior position and lateral directions. All imaging data were measured and stored by the Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS) (Infinitt Healthcare, South Korea).

Through the full‐length X‐rays of both lower extremities, we measured and recorded proximal tibia varus angle (PTVA), distal femoral valgus angle (DFVA), joint line congruence angle (JLCA), hip‐knee‐ankle angle (HKA), and the presence of preoperative coronal subluxation. Besides, deep dish deformity and posterior tibial slope were identified by lateral radiographs of the knee. DFVA is defined as the valgus angle between the femoral mechanical axis and the distal femoral anatomical axis (Figure 1A). PTVA is defined as the medial angle between the tibial articular margins and a line perpendicular to the tibial (Figure 1B). JLCA is defined as the angle between the distal femur joint orientation line and proximal tibia joint orientation line (Figure 1C). “Deep dish” deformity is defined as the distal end of the femoral condyle protruded into the tibial plateau causing pitting of the tibial plateau (Figure 1D). Coronal subluxation is defined as a distance greater than 3 mm between the midpoint of femoral and tibial joint lines.

(B) Proximal tibial varus angle (PTVA), assessed as the medial angle between the tibial articular margin and a line perpendicular to the tibial shaft axis. (C) Joint line congruence angle (JLCA), showing the angle between the distal femoral and proximal tibial joint orientation lines. (D) Lateral X‐ray indicating “deep dish” deformity, characterized by distal femoral condyle indentation into the tibial plateau.

Surgical Techniques

All surgeries were performed by the same team of joint surgeons. The operation was conducted using an anterior midline knee incision; a medial parapatellar approach was used to incise the joint capsule and to expose the joint cavity, carefully cleaning the osteophyte with an osteotome, and a deep release of the MCL was done under the periosteum. Osteotomy was carried out according to the same amount of osteotomy technique, and bone cement was selected to fix the knee prosthesis after gap balance, and the patella was not replaced during the operation.

Treatments for femoral condylar avulsion fracture were as follows: (1) medial and lateral coronal stability was assessed, and 3.5 mm cancellous bone screws or No. 2 nonabsorbable sutures (ETHIBOND, Johnson & Johnson, USA) were fixed for patients with bone fragments greater than and less than 5 mm in thickness, separately; (2) after fixation, re‐assessed coronal stability through Bohler's test. Patients were asked to wear a hinged knee brace in situ for 6 weeks after surgery without restriction of knee flexion and extension, and weight‐bearing activities were allowed with crutches.

Rationale for Treatment Selection

The selection of each treatment method in this study was guided by established clinical practice guidelines and supported by literature evidence [12, 13]. Treatments were chosen for their proven efficacy and safety profiles in the context of chronic nonspecific low back pain. Additionally, considerations such as accessibility, clinical feasibility, and ethical suitability in our institution played a role in the final selection. These methods represent commonly adopted and evidence‐based therapeutic options for the target population.

Follow‐Up

Follow‐up visits were conducted at 2, 6, and 12 weeks after surgery, and an annual outpatient follow‐up as well. Knee Society Score (KSS) was recorded. Knee instability was measured by Bohler's test at 0° and 30° of knee flexion, and an opening greater than 5 mm was defined as knee instability. All radiological data were obtained from the PACS, while clinical information was collected from follow‐up visits. All 47 patients finished outpatient follow‐up, including physical examination and plain X‐rays.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent‐sample t tests, while nonnormally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and compared using the chi‐square test. All analyses were conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 20 (IBM Corp., USA), and a two‐tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Regression analyses were not performed due to the limited sample size to avoid model overfitting.

Results

Incidence of Femoral Condylar Avulsion Fracture in TKA

From January 2008 to December 2022, a total of 47 femoral condylar avulsion fractures (incidence 0.01%) occurred in 4290 TKAs, including 35 medial and 12 lateral femoral condylar avulsion fractures, separately. All patients were followed up for 1.5–3 years.

Comparison Between Fracture and Nonfracture Groups

In order to minimize selection bias, the 47 patients were matched with contemporaneous patients without intraoperative avulsion fractures for gender, age, and BMI, with a ratio of 1:1.

Among the patients with avulsion fractures, there were 13 cases of rheumatoid arthritis, one case of traumatic arthritis, and 33 cases of osteoarthritis. In the control group, there were 31 cases of osteoarthritis, 15 cases of rheumatoid arthritis, and one case of traumatic arthritis. No statistically significant differences were found in age, gender, and BMI between the two groups. Compared with the control group (7.57 ± 1.55 days), the fracture group (9.96 ± 3.67 days) had significantly longer lengths of hospital stays (p = 0.01).

Intraoperative Fracture Characteristics and Treatment

Of the 47avulsion fractures, 16 cases occurred at the time of intraoperative anterior tibial dislocation, 15 occurred at the insertion of polyethylene liners, and 16 occurred at the completion of prosthesis placement. All fractures were uncontrollable fractures that occurred unexpectedly in either the medial or lateral femoral condyles and were connected to either MCL or LCL. Bone fragments were irregular, ranging in thickness from 3 to 12 mm, with an average of 4.88 ± 2.05 mm. Depending on the size of the fracture fragment, 20 patients were treated with screw fixation and 27 patients with suture fixation. For patients with medial femoral condyle avulsion fractures, the average preoperative HKA angleis 15.91° ± 5.63°, with lateral femoral condyle avulsion fractures, the average preoperative HKA angle is 14.67 ± 6.03, There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (p < 0.05).

Radiological Parameters

Significant differences were found in PTVA (82.02 ± 3.39 vs. 85.32 ± 1.87, p = 0.01), DFVA (85.53 ± 2.73 vs. 87.51 ± 5.29, p = 0.02), and HKA (8.81 ± 3.30 vs. 6.53 ± 2.21, p = 0.01) between fracture and nonfracture groups. However, the two groups had comparable JLC (5.94 ± 1.42 vs. 5.53 ± 1.13, p = 0.41). Besides, the fracture group had a significantly higher incidence of coronal subluxation (15 cases vs. six cases) and “deep dish” deformity (31 cases vs. 20 cases) than the control group, with p values of 0.02 (Table 2). In terms of KSS at the last follow‐up, no statistical difference was reported between the fracture group (88.91 ± 2.50) and nonfracture group (87.87 ± 2.62) (p = 0.05). According to Bohler's test, no knee instability was observed in all patients during the follow‐up period.

Discussion

Our study aimed to investigate the incidence, risk factors, and management of intraoperative femoral condylar avulsion fractures during primary TKA. We found that the incidence of such fractures was 1.1%, and preoperative severe varus deformity, knee subluxation, and “deep dish” deformity were identified as potential risk factors. In addition, fixation with cancellous screws or nonabsorbable sutures combined with a postoperative hinged knee brace provided good stability and favorable clinical outcomes, without the need for conversion to a condylar‐constrained knee prosthesis.

Risk Factors and Mechanisms of Femoral Condylar Avulsion Fracture

Ligament injuries resulting from femoral condylar avulsion fractures remain a critical contributor to postoperative instability. Most iatrogenic injuries of the MCL and LCL occur due to intraoperative technical factors such as saw blade injury, excessive ligament release, or overexposure of the joint capsule [8, 14, 15, 16]. While direct injury is potentially preventable through surgical refinement, avulsion‐type fractures remain difficult to avoid in patients with significant deformities. In our cohort, femoral condylar avulsion fractures occurred in only 47 out of 4290 primary TKAs, underscoring their rarity.

These fractures were predominantly observed in patients with severe coronal deformity and extra‐articular malalignment. Correction of such deformities often necessitates aggressive osteophyte removal and extensive medial ligament release. During anterior tibial dislocation or prosthesis trialing, realignment to neutral axis can result in excessive medial tension, increasing the risk of avulsion. This risk may be heightened in Asian populations where extra‐articular deformities are more common and severe [17].

Our results pointed out significantly greater PTVA, DFVA, and HKA in the fracture group than the nonfracture group, predicting a higher incidence of femoral condylar avulsion fracture among patients with more pronounced deformity. Specifically, increased PTVA and HKA reflect enhanced varus stress on the joint, while reduced DFVA may indicate altered femoral geometry. These alignment abnormalities increase tension on the collateral ligaments during critical intraoperative steps, such as anterior tibial dislocation and prosthesis trialing. In addition, preoperative knee subluxation and “deep dish” deformity can further exacerbate ligament stress by restricting joint mobility and complicating reduction maneuvers. Improper surgical handling under these conditions may lead to excessive ligament tension and subsequent avulsion. Finally, though bone mineral density testing was not performed in this study, the role of disuse osteoporosis, common in patients undergoing long‐term conservative treatment in China, should not be overlooked.

After retrospectively analyzing the timing of avulsion fractures, 16 cases occurred at the time of intraoperative anterior tibial dislocation, 15 occurred at the insertion of polyethylene liners, and 16 occurred at the completion of prosthesis placement. The above results suggest that in patients with severe varus deformity, knee subluxation, extra‐articular deformity, and “deep dish” deformity, careful operation is required to avoid excessive tension of the MCL and LCL, and if necessary, collateral ligaments can be fully released to prevent the occurrence of fractures.

Treatment Strategies and Clinical Outcomes

For the management of collateral ligament injuries during primary TKA, Lee et al. [15] applied a high constrained knee prosthesis in 30 patients. Out of the seven knees without constraint, four were revised due to instability within an average of 7 months. Patients who were treated with an additional constraint did not require revision. Poor functional scores were noted in patients treated without constraint, and patients with MCL injury were advised to use increased constraints. Our study showed different results: after combined use of screw fixation or nonabsorbable suture and a 6‐week postoperative hinged knee brace in situ, stable knee joints were observed after fracture healing, and we found good clinical outcomes in the medium‐term follow‐up. Several recent studies have emphasized constrained prosthesis use as a default strategy following collateral ligament compromise. For instance, Moussa et al. [18] and Urbain et al. [19] reported reliable mid‐ and long‐term outcomes using constrained condylar knees in complex TKA. However, these studies focused primarily on ligamentous ruptures or severely deficient knees. Our data contribute to a more nuanced understanding by suggesting that isolated avulsion injuries, when anatomically stabilized, may follow a distinct recovery pathway.

This contrast suggests that, unlike midsubstance ligament ruptures, avulsion fractures—when securely fixed—may not require prosthetic constraint. Moreover, the use of constrained prostheses carries drawbacks, including increased mechanical stress at the cement‐bone‐implant interface [20, 21], greater costs, and the necessity for stemmed components, which compromise bone stock for future revision procedures.

The key point for treating collateral ligament injury during TKA in previous literature is to increase constrained prosthesis [9, 10, 22, 23] which can enhance immediate stability during surgery. However, on the other hand, it can lead to a growing incidence of aseptic loosening due to more stress transmitted at the cement‐bone‐implant interface [18, 19, 23, 24]. At the same time, constrained prosthesis is designed to be used with a stem, increasing patient costs and causing host bone loss in future joint repair surgery. Therefore, our results support a conservative, bone‐preserving treatment strategy for femoral condylar avulsion fractures, which diverges from the prevailing emphasis on constrained prostheses in the literature.

Limitations and Strengths

There are some limitations in this study. First, the retrospective design might result in some bias in the fracture group. Second, femoral condylar avulsion fracture was not uniform in size and extent. Third, only intraoperative Bohler's test was used to identify subjective laxity after screw or suture fixation, with the lack of objective measurements. Finally, we failed to perform a bone mineral density test to further detect the presence of osteoporosis in both groups. We were unable to perform a multivariable analysis, such as logistic regression, to identify independent risk factors and calculate odds ratios (ORs). This was primarily due to the complex interdependencies and high collinearity among the potential risk factors in our cohort. Attempting to fit such a model would have yielded unstable and clinically uninterpretable results. Subsequent studies are needed to explore factors associated with femoral condylar avulsion fracture. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more granular data are needed to disentangle these complex relationships and build robust predictive models.

However, this study also has notable strengths. It is one of the few investigations to focus on intraoperative femoral condylar avulsion fractures during primary TKA, with systematic radiological evaluation and consistent surgical management by experienced surgeons. These findings provide valuable insights and support a conservative, bone‐preserving strategy for managing this rare complication.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that a combination of intraoperative screw fixation or nonabsorbable suture and postoperative hinged knee brace in situ could achieve good clinical and radiological results among patients with femoral condylar avulsion fracture in primary TKA, without the need for a condylar‐constrained knee prosthesis. In addition, the preoperative presence of severe varus deformity, knee subluxation, and “deep dish” deformity might increase the risk of femoral condylar avulsion fracture, highlighting the importance of careful intraoperative technique to prevent this complication.
