Information quality and reliability of pheochromocytoma-related videos on tiktok and Kwai: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jun Luo, Ziliang Chen, Runfu Cao, Kaihong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundShort-video platforms are major sources of health information, yet the quality of pheochromocytoma-related content is unclear. We assessed and compared the quality and reliability of related videos on TikTok and Kwai.MethodsUsing the Chinese keyword “嗜铬细胞瘤,” we searched TikTok and Kwai (24–25 Dec 2025) with a newly created account and retrieved the first 120 videos from each platform under the default “comprehensive” ranking. After exclusions, 178 videos were included (TikTok, n = 100; Kwai, n = 78). Video metrics, uploader characteristics, and content features were extracted. Quality and reliability were assessed using GQS (1–5) and mDISCERN (0–5) by two blinded reviewers, with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer. We compared video characteristics and GQS/mDISCERN scores between TikTok and Kwai, and performed subgroup analyses by uploader identity and physician specialty. Nonparametric tests and Spearman correlation analyses were conducted using R 4.1.3.ResultsMedian video length was 73.0 s; median GQS and mDISCERN were both 2.0, indicating generally low quality and reliability. Most videos were posted by healthcare personnel (81.5%), predominantly urologists (50.6%). TikTok videos were longer and had higher likes, comments, and collections (all P < 0.001). mDISCERN was higher on TikTok (P = 0.047), while GQS did not differ (P = 0.580). Video length correlated with GQS (r = 0.46) and mDISCERN (r = 0.41), whereas engagement metrics were not reliable proxies for credibility.ConclusionsPheochromocytoma-related short videos on TikTok and Kwai show limited quality and reliability despite professional authorship. Improvements should prioritize verifiable sources and decision-relevant risk framing, supported by platform-level quality labeling and distribution strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12894-026-02242-x.

Introduction

Pheochromocytoma is a neuroendocrine tumor arising from chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla [1]. It can synthesize and secrete excessive catecholamines, with an estimated annual incidence of approximately 2–8 cases per million population [2]. Typical clinical manifestations include the classic triad of headache, palpitations, and diaphoresis, as well as paroxysmal or sustained hypertension; in severe cases, hypertensive crisis and related cardiovascular complications may occur [3]. Surgical resection remains the mainstay of treatment [4]. With timely recognition and standardized perioperative management, the prognosis is generally favorable for most patients. However, when the disease is unrecognized or inadequately managed, fatal perioperative events may still occur [5]. Previous studies reported perioperative mortality as high as 20%–45% among patients with functional pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PPGL) [6], highlighting the clinical importance of early identification and risk management. Although diagnostic and therapeutic guidelines have improved in recent years, public awareness of this disease remains limited, and delayed diagnosis and insufficient risk communication continue to be important contributors to adverse outcomes [7]. Therefore, improving risk recognition among the public and non-specialists, and promoting early medical consultation and standardized management, are essential components of prevention and health management [8].

Access to high-quality health information is uneven across populations because of regional disparities in healthcare resources and individual financial constraints. Some individuals may not obtain adequate diagnostic clues and guidance for care in a timely manner [9]. Effective health communication can facilitate risk recognition and changes in health behaviors, and the internet and social media provide additional dissemination pathways. With the widespread adoption of social media, the public has increasingly shifted from passive recipients to active seekers of health information [10]. Compared with text-based formats, short videos reduce barriers to information acquisition through the use of images, audio, and contextualized presentation, which may offer distinct advantages for health education [11].

Nevertheless, the quality of health information on social media is highly variable. Inaccurate or misleading content may prompt audiences to make health decisions based on unreliable information, thereby increasing potential risks [12]. Accordingly, evaluating and guiding the quality of health information disseminated via social media has become a shared challenge in public health and healthcare management [13]. Existing evidence indicates that short-video content on multiple disease topics often has limitations in source attribution, coverage of key messages, and communication of appropriate risk boundaries [14]. However, the quality of short-video health information related to pheochromocytoma has not been systematically evaluated. Given the large volume of related content on short-video platforms such as TikTok and Kwai, an objective assessment of its quality and reliability is warranted. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the information quality and reliability of pheochromocytoma-related short videos on TikTok and Kwai, and to compare differences in quality scores and dissemination characteristics between platforms, thereby providing evidence to support optimization of health communication for rare diseases and platform content governance.

Methods

This study did not involve clinical data, human specimens, or experimental animals. All data were obtained from publicly available videos posted on TikTok and Kwai. No interaction with platform users occurred during data collection, and no personally identifiable information was collected, disclosed, or reported. Accordingly, the study posed no privacy or human-subject research risks and, under institutional and routine practice, did not require ethics committee review.

Search strategy and inclusion/exclusion criteria

We searched TikTok and Kwai using the Chinese keyword “嗜铬细胞瘤” (pheochromocytoma). To minimize potential bias introduced by personalized recommendations, a newly registered account was used for all searches. Searches were conducted on 24–25 December 2025, and the top 120 ranked videos from each platform were retrieved.

Both platforms provide multiple sorting options (e.g., “comprehensive,” “most recent,” and “most liked”). To ensure comparability, we selected the “comprehensive” ranking on both platforms and did not restrict publication date, video duration, or search scope.We excluded (1) duplicate videos (n = 24), (2) videos unrelated to the study topic (n = 15), and (3) advertising or marketing content (n = 23). A total of 178 videos were included for data extraction and statistical analyses (screening flow shown in Fig. 1).

Data extraction and content coding

Video-level data were extracted and coded in Microsoft Excel. The basic metrics collected included video length, number of likes, comments, shares, and collections. Uploaders were categorized as healthcare personnel, patients, or organizations/others. Healthcare personnel were further classified by specialty, including urologists, cardiologists, oncologists, radiologists, general surgeons, pathologists, orthopedic surgeons, and other specialties. Video content was coded according to predefined thematic domains, including epidemiology, etiology, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, treatment, prevention, and advertising/other. A multi-label coding scheme was used: a single video could be assigned to more than one content category (e.g., both diagnosis and treatment) if the corresponding topics were covered. Therefore, percentages across content categories are not mutually exclusive and may sum to more than 100%.

Instruments for assessing quality and reliability

Video quality and reliability were evaluated using the Global Quality Score (GQS; Supplementary Table 1) [15, 16] and the modified DISCERN (mDISCERN; Supplementary Table 2) [17], respectively. GQS is a 5-point scale designed to assess the overall quality of health-related videos, ranging from 1 (poor quality) to 5 (excellent quality) [18] and mDISCERN assesses the reliability of health information using five dichotomous (yes/no) items. Each “yes” response is assigned 1 point, yielding a total score from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater reliability [19].

To minimize subjective bias, we used an independent, blinded rating procedure. Two reviewers independently scored all videos using GQS and mDISCERN and were unaware of each other’s ratings until all assessments were completed. Discrepancies were subsequently resolved through discussion, with adjudication by a third researcher to determine the final scores. Pre-adjudication inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa for GQS and mDISCERN based on the two reviewers’ independent ratings.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.1.3). Continuous variables (including video length, numbers of likes, comments, collections, and shares, as well as GQS and mDISCERN scores) are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Because engagement metrics and rating scores on social media are typically non-normally distributed, non-parametric methods were used for between-platform comparisons. Differences in continuous variables between TikTok and Kwai were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Categorical variables (e.g., uploader category, whether specific content themes were covered, and the distribution of GQS categories) are presented as frequencies and percentages; between-platform differences were evaluated using the chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test when any expected cell count was < 5. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess associations between baseline video metrics and quality/reliability scores. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition to platform comparisons, subgroup analyses compared GQS and mDISCERN across uploader identity (healthcare personnel vs. organizations/others vs. patients; Kruskal–Wallis test) and physician specialty among healthcare personnel (urologists vs. non-urologists; Mann–Whitney U test).

Results

Study selection

After applying the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 178 pheochromocytoma-related short videos were included: 100 from TikTok (56.18%) and 78 from Kwai (43.82%) (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics

Overall, videos were brief, with a median duration of 73.00 s (IQR 47.25–121.75). Median engagement metrics were 55.50 likes (IQR 16.00–175.75), 6.00 comments (IQR 2.00–23.00), 10.00 collections (IQR 3.00–47.00), and 11.00 shares (IQR 3.00–43.00) (Table 1). The overall quality and reliability were generally low, with median GQS and mDISCERN scores of 2.00 (IQR 2.00–3.00) for both instruments (Table 1; Fig. 2). The distribution of overall GQS scores is presented in Table 2. Item-level reliability indicators assessed by mDISCERN are summarized in Table 3, showing that most videos were understandable, whereas source transparency (reliable sources and additional resources) was limited and uncertainty was rarely addressed. Pre-adjudication inter-rater agreement was high for both instruments (Cohen’s κ = 0.913 for GQS and κ = 0.892 for mDISCERN).

Uploader characteristics and content features

Most videos were uploaded by healthcare personnel (145/178, 81.46%), followed by organizations/others (20/178, 11.24%) and patients (13/178, 7.30%) (Table 1; Fig. 3b). Among healthcare personnel, urologists constituted the largest subgroup (90/178, 50.56%) (Table 1; Fig. 3c). Regarding content domains, treatment was most frequently addressed (87/178, 48.88%), followed by diagnosis (63/178, 35.39%) and clinical presentation (62/178, 34.83%) (Table 1; Fig. 3d).

Platform comparison

In between-platform comparisons, TikTok videos were significantly longer than those on Kwai [85.00 (IQR 56.75–159.25) vs. 59.50 (IQR 39.25–93.00) s; P < 0.001]. TikTok also showed higher counts of likes, comments, and collections (all P < 0.001) (Table 4). For information reliability, mDISCERN scores were higher on TikTok than on Kwai (P = 0.047), whereas GQS did not differ significantly between platforms (P = 0.580). The difference in shares was not statistically significant (P = 0.071) (Table 4; Fig. 4). Subgroup analyses further showed that GQS differed across uploader identity (P < 0.001), with patient-uploaded videos scoring lowest, whereas mDISCERN did not differ significantly across uploader groups (P = 0.155) (Supplementary Table 3). Among healthcare personnel, GQS and mDISCERN did not differ between urologists and non-urologists (P = 0.246 and 0.254, respectively) (Supplementary Table 4).

Correlation analysis

Video duration was moderately and positively correlated with GQS (r = 0.46) and mDISCERN (r = 0.41), and weakly positively correlated with likes, collections, comments, and shares (r = 0.08–0.13) (Fig. 5). Engagement metrics were strongly intercorrelated, including likes with comments (r = 0.85), likes with shares (r = 0.66), and collections with shares (r = 0.95). In addition, GQS and mDISCERN were positively correlated (r = 0.62) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Short-video platforms have become a major channel through which the public accesses health information [20]. This is particularly relevant for pheochromocytoma, a condition characterized by low incidence yet potentially severe consequences [21]. Excess catecholamine secretion can lead to hypertension and the classic triad of headache, palpitations, and diaphoresis [22]. Because symptoms are often episodic and non-specific, pheochromocytoma may be misattributed to common hypertension or stress-related complaints, thereby delaying further diagnostic evaluation and guideline-concordant management [23]. In addition, failure to recognize the disease or inadequate perioperative preparation can increase the risk of serious cardiovascular complications [24]. Therefore, public-facing educational content should go beyond a general disease overview and instead deliver structured, actionable messages—such as recognition of warning signs, appropriate care-seeking pathways, key diagnostic tests, treatment principles and perioperative risk considerations, and follow-up management [25]. Against this background, an objective appraisal of the quality and reliability of pheochromocytoma-related short-video content has immediate practical relevance. Notably, by jointly evaluating content quality and reliability and comparing dissemination characteristics across two major short-video platforms, this study provides platform-specific evidence for rare-disease health communication and content governance.

In this study, GQS reflects the overall quality and usefulness of the video (e.g., coherence and patient-oriented utility), whereas mDISCERN evaluates reliability-related indicators such as source transparency, balance, and communication of uncertainty. Importantly, these instruments assess structured quality and reliability rather than performing statement-by-statement verification against clinical guidelines. Therefore, lower scores should be interpreted as indicating deficits in verifiability, structure, and boundary communication, rather than necessarily implying factual incorrectness. Overall, pheochromocytoma-related short videos on TikTok and Kwai demonstrated generally low information quality and reliability, with GQS and mDISCERN scores concentrated in the lower range. This pattern is consistent with prior evaluations of short-video health content across multiple disease topics. For example, cross-platform assessments of short videos on pancreatic cancer [26]and gastrointestinal bleeding [27] have similarly reported low median GQS and mDISCERN scores, suggesting that health education content on short-video platforms often lacks sufficient structure, clear source attribution, and decision-relevant key points. Such content may benefit from improved review and recommendation strategies to enhance the visibility of high-quality professional information. Collectively, these findings indicate that high reach and engagement do not necessarily translate into high-quality health information provision.

A noteworthy finding is that most videos were uploaded by healthcare personnel, with urologists representing a substantial proportion, yet overall quality and reliability scores remained low. This suggests that limitations in short-video health communication are not determined solely by the professional status of the uploader, but rather by the extent to which the content adheres to basic principles of transparent and structured health communication. Item-level patterns indicate that while some videos were relatively easy to understand, they frequently lacked key elements such as citation of credible sources, provision of additional information resources, and explicit communication of uncertainty and appropriate boundaries [28]. Several factors may explain why videos produced by healthcare professionals still receive low scores. Short-video platforms favor concise, engaging storytelling, which can encourage creators to streamline content and omit decision-relevant details (e.g., when to seek further evaluation, perioperative risks, and follow-up). In addition, clinical expertise does not always translate into structured health communication: references to guidelines, links to additional resources, and explicit discussion of uncertainty are often missing. The short-video format also limits how much nuance can be delivered without sacrificing clarity, making balanced risk framing and boundary conditions difficult. Finally, outside the clinical setting, clinicians may avoid individualized recommendations or detailed caveats, and the lack of peer review or standardized templates means that even professional videos may not consistently provide verifiable sources or well-structured key points.

For pheochromocytoma, these omissions may carry heightened cognitive and behavioral risks: in the absence of verifiable sources and clear risk framing, audiences may inappropriately treat fragmented messages as diagnostic cues or treatment recommendations, potentially influencing care-seeking behavior and risk appraisal. Prior work on prostate cancer has similarly noted that even content produced by health professionals may remain incomplete, underscoring the broader challenge of delivering structured, decision-relevant information within a short-video format [29]. Accordingly, improving comprehensibility is necessary but insufficient; strengthening verifiability and providing an information structure that supports safe decision-making are arguably more critical [30].

Platform comparisons further suggest that dissemination characteristics and information quality do not necessarily move in parallel. Although TikTok and Kwai differed in video length and engagement metrics, differences in quality scores were limited, and longer duration did not automatically translate into higher-quality content. Correlation analyses showed positive associations between video length and both GQS and mDISCERN, suggesting that longer videos may allow more complete explanations and better logical flow/coherence (consistent with GQS), while also offering more opportunity for reliability-related disclosures (e.g., sources and uncertainty) captured by mDISCERN; however, duration alone is not a sufficient condition. Without transparent sourcing and a structured presentation of key points, gains in information quality are likely constrained. Similar positive associations between duration and quality scores have been reported in evaluations of short videos on thyroid nodules [30]and breast cancer [31], supporting the interpretation that longer videos are more likely to cover essential content domains.

In addition, engagement metrics were highly intercorrelated, reflecting the coupled nature of platform popularity signals; importantly, such metrics cannot substitute for assessments of information reliability. These findings also highlight the presence of multiple confounding factors in short-video health communication, including ranking algorithms and engagement-oriented presentation styles. As a result, viewers may overinterpret fragmented messages as diagnostic cues or treatment guidance, potentially contributing to unnecessary or inappropriate care-seeking. This underscores the importance of verifiable references and explicit boundary/uncertainty communication, as short videos may support initial awareness but cannot replace clinical evaluation. Prior studies in other clinical topics (e.g., orthopedic content) have reported inconsistent relationships between engagement indicators and information quality/reliability, emphasizing that popularity is not equivalent to quality [32]. A plausible explanation is that platform ranking mechanisms primarily optimize for viewing and engagement behaviors (e.g., completion rate and interaction) rather than for source transparency or risk framing; as a result, even professionally produced content may converge on styles that are engaging yet insufficiently verifiable. Consequently, if viewers use likes or shares as heuristics for credibility, low-quality content may achieve disproportionate influence. Platform-level mechanisms that provide clearer quality labeling and preferential distribution of high-quality information may therefore be necessary to mitigate the popularity-driven amplification of low-quality, insufficiently verifiable health information.

Based on these findings, several practical directions can be proposed. At the content-production level, a “minimum essential elements” checklist may help standardize messaging. Videos should present a structured set of actionable information, including warning signs, appropriate scenarios for seeking medical care, key diagnostic tests, core treatment principles and perioperative risk considerations, and follow-up recommendations. To enhance verifiability, creators could provide traceable references (e.g., clinical guidelines, consensus statements, or information from authoritative institutions) in the video description or pinned comments. At the platform level, baseline quality could be strengthened by introducing templated fields (e.g., information source, date of last update, target population, and risk warnings) alongside routine sampling audits, and by increasing the algorithmic visibility of high-quality professional content. Recommendations to optimize ranking mechanisms and strengthen content review have also been suggested in prior work on inflammatory bowel disease [33] and diabetes [34], supporting this governance approach. At the public education level, it is important to reinforce the boundary that short videos may facilitate initial awareness but cannot replace clinical decision-making, and to encourage timely medical evaluation when warning signs are present, thereby reducing the risk of self-diagnosis or delayed care.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, video retrieval may have been influenced by platform ranking and recommendation algorithms, introducing potential selection bias. Second, the GQS and mDISCERN instruments emphasize structured quality and reliability indicators rather than performing statement-by-statement verification against clinical guidelines; therefore, medical accuracy at the individual-claim level was not formally adjudicated. Third, we did not assess audience comprehension or downstream behavioral outcomes, and thus cannot directly quantify how content quality affects care-seeking decisions. Future studies should expand the platforms and time windows examined and incorporate claim-level accuracy verification, audience surveys, and more granular frameworks for content completeness to better characterize the real-world public health impact of short-video education on rare-disease recognition and health literacy.

Overall, the findings suggest that governance efforts should move beyond improving understandability alone and place greater emphasis on verifiability and explicit risk-boundary communication to reduce the potential harms of low-quality information in rare, high-risk conditions such as pheochromocytoma.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the overall video quality and information reliability of pheochromocytoma-related short videos on TikTok and Kwai using GQS and mDISCERN. Overall, GQS scores were low (median 2.0) on both platforms and did not differ significantly between TikTok and Kwai (P = 0.580), indicating limited overall coherence and patient-oriented usefulness of the available content. Reliability-related indicators assessed by mDISCERN were also generally low, although mDISCERN was slightly higher on TikTok than on Kwai (P = 0.047). Video duration was positively associated with both GQS and mDISCERN, whereas popularity indicators (e.g., likes) should not be used as proxies for credibility. These findings suggest that rare-disease short-video education should move beyond being merely understandable toward being verifiable and decision-relevant, supported by structured key-point checklists for creators, platform-based quality labeling and distribution strategies, and public education to reinforce appropriate boundaries and timely medical consultation.
