A Systematic Analysis of Coronary to Pulmonary Artery Fistula and Its Associated Aneurysm in Adults
Authors: Chaoneng Wu, Shayne Joseph, Rakesh Devireddy, Marco Kaldas, Maxim Zlatopolsky, Omar Qaqish, Yunguo C Gong, Sonela Blaceri, Chadi Saad, William E Harder, Fadi A Saab, Sujata Kambhatla, Ashok Kondur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWe conducted a systematic review to evaluate the clinical features, diagnosis, and treatment of coronary artery to pulmonary artery fistula (CPAF) and its associated aneurysms.MethodsOur search encompassed MEDLINE/PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Collaboration Database from 1970 to 2025.ResultsA total of 461 cases were analysed. The volume of publications steadily increased after 2010. The left anterior descending coronary artery was the most common site of origin for CPAF. A total of 457 cases underwent coronary angiography confirming CPAF, with computed tomography coronary angiography being the most frequently used non-invasive imaging modality (190 cases, 41.2%). Stress tests were conducted in 78 cases (16.9%), revealing a coronary steal phenomenon associated with CPAF. Particularly, when comparing aneurysm CPAF to non-aneurysm CPAF, we found that older age is an independent risk factor for developing an aneurysm. For interventions, the symptomatic patients were more likely to close the fistula. We identified that 218 cases (49.7%) underwent surgical treatment, while 121 cases (27.6%) received percutaneous embolization. There were 8 reported deaths, resulting in a mortality rate of 1.8%.ConclusionsThe clinical presentations of CPAF are often non-diagnostic. Computed tomography coronary angiography is the preferred method for visualizing the fistulas and their surrounding structures. Surgical treatment is beneficial in cases of aneurysm CPAF or with other surgical indications. Transcatheter closure of CPAF may be considered for selected individuals with favourable anatomy (PROSPERO Number CRD42025643603).Clinical Registration NumberPROSPERO Number CRD42025643603.Keywords: coronary artery to pulmonary artery fistula (CPAF), aneurysm, adults, systematic review

INTRODUCTION

Coronary artery to pulmonary artery fistula (CPAF) is a rare coronary anomaly characterized by a fistulous connection between the coronary artery and pulmonary artery. It was first reported by Krause in 1865,1 with an incidence of 0.002% among the general population, 0.1% among all patients undergoing cardiac catheterization,2 and 4% to 15% of cases of young sudden cardiac death identified during autopsy examinations.3,4 CPAF is primarily congenital; however, it can also be secondary to diseases, such as Kawasaki disease or Takayasu arteritis, or it may result from chest radiation or iatrogenic procedures. Most patients with CPAF remain asymptomatic or exhibit variable symptoms that mimic other cardiovascular diseases, leading to an underestimation of its true incidence. The classical description of CPAF includes a triad of a heart murmur, a left-to-right shunt, and vessel tortuosity with or without aneurysm formation.5 CPAF-associated aneurysm can lead to medical emergencies such as cardiac tamponade and mediastinal haemorrhage secondary to aneurysm rupture, thrombosis, or embolism.6 Failure to recognize CPAF can lead to misdiagnosis with altered management and long-term cardiovascular consequences, such as coronary steal phenomenon, pulmonary hypertension, and/or congestive heart failure.

Advancements in imaging modalities, particularly computed tomography coronary angiogram (CTCA)7 and coronary catheter angiography (CCA), have increased awareness of CPAF in cardiac pathophysiology. With the increase in cardiac surgeries, coronary interventional procedures, and chest radiation globally, the identification of sporadic and acquired CPAF is anticipated to rise. However, there is limited literature to systematically analyse the clinical features and management of CPAF. To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review by collecting all reported cases of CPAF, particularly, those associated with aneurysms.

METHODS

Data sources and search strategy

Data were reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement. The literature was searched on Scopus, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Collaboration Database of Randomized Trials. The search strategy, exclusion criteria, and data collection were described in Table S1. This search was conducted up to March 28, 2025. The protocol for this study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, CRD42025643603). A PRISMA flow diagram was provided (Figure S1).

Statistical analysis

The comparison of continuous variables was performed using the independent samples t‐test, and the analysis of the categorical variables using the Chi‐square test or Fisher’s exact test (where applicable). Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine the risk factors contributing to aneurysm formation. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0. Figures were created using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics, publication trends, and coronary origins

A total of 461 cases were analysed, consisting of 289 case reports and 28 case series (Table S1, Consort Figure S1). The average age of the participants was 56.3 years, with a female to male ratio of 0.96 (Table 1). The major clinical presentations were chest pain and/or dyspnoea on exertion (DOE) or palpitation (326 cases, 70.7%), followed by angina pectoris (42 cases, 9.1%), chance observation of fistula (26 cases, 5.6%), incidental finding of murmur (22 cases, 4.8%), syncope (15 cases, 3.3%), or other various manifestations (30 cases, 6.5%) (Figure 1). Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest was observed in 3 cases, cardiogenic shock in 4 cases, and chest or mediastinal mass in another 3 cases (Table S1).

Imaging modalities

Out of the 461 cases, 457 (99.1%) patients reported a coronary fistula through cardiac catheterization/CCA examination, but 6 patients did not undergo the CCA study (Table 1). There were 190 cases (41.2%) that received CTCA examinations showing fistula(s) (Table 1). There was an increasing trend in CTCA examinations. From 2000 to 2009, the number of cases was 4.1 times higher than the 10 years from 1990 to 1999. From 2010 to 2019, it was 2.8 times higher than the previous 10 years. In the 5 years from 2020 to 2025, it was more than half of the previous 10 years (Figure 2).

A total of 271 cases (59%) had echocardiography (ECHO) reports (Table 1). Among them, 45 patients (8.3%) showed positive fistula and/or aneurysm, indicating a lower sensitivity of ECHO in detecting fistula/aneurysm. Notably, 12 cases showed pericardial effusion (Table S1), implying an underlying ruptured or micro-ruptured aneurysm. Decreased left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was observed in 31 cases and systolic pulmonary artery pressure (SPAP) over 35 mmHg in 12 cases (Table S1). Additionally, 21 cases (7.8%) had coexisting heart structure or valvular abnormalities, such as bicuspid aortic valve, mitral valve prolapse, and others (Table S1).

Among the 78 patients (16.9%) undergoing stress tests (treadmill, ECHO, or radionuclide myocardial perfusion imaging [MPI], or single photon emission computed tomography [SPECT]), all due to symptoms, 65 cases (83.3%) showed positive ischaemia. However, only 14 cases (21.5%) exhibited obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD), while 51 cases (78.5%) had non-obstructive CAD based on the CCA study (Table S1). This suggests a coronary steal phenomenon in CPAF.

Differences between aneurysm and non-aneurysm CPAF

Among 461 CPAF cases, 110 (23.9%) had an aneurysm and 351 (76.1%) did not (Table 1). The mean age was older in the aneurysm group (62.2 ± 12.2 vs 54.5 ± 14.9, P = .013). The aneurysm group had a higher female ratio (67.3% vs 42.9%, P = .001). There were 75 (16.3%) patients presenting with murmurs, yet no significant difference between the 2 groups (18.2% vs 15.7%, P = .355). The non-aneurysm group had a higher ratio of obstructive CAD (25.8% vs 7.3%, P < .001). There were 50 patients undergoing right heart catheterization, with 40 cases (48.2%) having SaO2 step-up and an average Qp/Qs of 1.32 ± 0.23, yet no significant difference between the 2 groups (Table 1).

In terms of imaging features, among the 190 patients (41.2%) with CTCA tests showing CPAF, the aneurysm group was twice as higher as the non-aneurysm group (72.7% vs 31.35%, P = .001, Table 1), suggesting a higher usability of CTCA in aneurysm cases. Among the 269 cases receiving ECHO, there was no significant difference in using ECHO between the 2 groups (64.5% vs 58.4%, P = .15). We identified 31 cases (11.4%) undergoing ECHO with depressed LVEF, yet no difference between these 2 groups (11.3% vs 11.4%, P = .259).

There were 426 cases with data on interventional approaches: 329 cases underwent percutaneous transcatheter or surgical closure, while 97 cases did not attempt closure and instead received only medical treatment or watchful observation. The group of patients receiving closure interventions had a significantly higher percentage of being symptomatic (96% vs 88.7%, P = .006, Table 2).

Among the 218 out of 439 cases (49.7%) that underwent surgical procedures, the number of cases having surgical procedures was significantly higher in the aneurysm group compared to the non-aneurysm group (65.4% vs 44.6%, P = .001, Table 1). Similarly, out of the 121 cases (27.6%) receiving percutaneous embolization, the non-aneurysm group had a relatively higher ratio (29.5% vs 21.5%, P = .004). Lastly, the non-aneurysm group had a higher ratio of watchful observation (19.6% vs 7.5%, P = .003).

Logistic regression analysis revealed that older age is an independent risk factor for developing aneurysm CPAF (odds ratio [OR] = 1.035, 95% CI = 1.009 to 1.063, P = .009, Table 3). Interestingly, having obstructive CAD is negatively associated with aneurysm CPAF (OR = 0.154, 95% CI= 0.052 to 0.457, P = .001). Furthermore, it consistently revealed that patients with aneurysm CPAF have a higher chance of undergoing CTCA (OR = 4.628, 95% CI= 2.331 to 9.190, P < .001) and are more likely to have surgery (OR = 2.582, 95% CI= 1.337 to 4.989, P = .005).

Mortality

There were 8 death cases, resulting in a mortality rate of 1.8% (8 out of 439 cases): 2 patients experienced sudden cardiac death,8,9 2 had ruptured aneurysms,10,11 1 died from an embolic stroke following surgery,12 2 expired due to failed percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI),13,14 and 1 succumbed to left main dissection.15

DISCUSSION

Our study revealed that the average age at diagnosis of CPAF was 56.3, with a female to male ratio of 0.96, which aligns with literature reports of an average age of 55.8 years and no gender predilection.16 More than two-thirds of patients with CPAF presented with non-specific symptoms, such as chest pain, DOE, or palpitations, while another approximately 10% exhibited typical angina symptoms. These findings suggest that there are no particular clinical symptoms that suggest a CPAF diagnosis. A fistulous connection between vascular structures may produce a murmur. However, our data showed that only 75 cases (16.3%) presented with either a systolic or continuous murmur. It is a fact that small or tortuous fistulas may not generate audible murmurs. Therefore, CPAF can be commonly misdiagnosed as CAD due to similar presentations and age range, and relying solely on clinical features can result in misdiagnosis. However, CPAF can lead to life-threatening complications necessitating increased awareness and early detection. In our study, we observed 3 cases complicated by out-of-hospital cardiac arrest due to ruptured aneurysms or lethal arrhythmias,10,17,18 and another 4 cases of cardiogenic shock.19–22 Additionally, a chest or mediastinal mass may also indicate a CPAF-associated aneurysm as shown in our study.23–25

CTCA offers advantages in diagnosing CPAF and visualizing its adjacent structures, especially in aneurysm CPAF. In our study, 42.1% of patients with CTCA-detected CPAF exhibited coronary aneurysms, which is a lower percentage compared to the literature reports of aneurysm formation at 59.9% in CTCA-detected CPAF.26 CTCA also revealed that 12 patients (19.7%) had pericardial effusion, which may indicate a rupture or micro-rupture of the coronary aneurysms, a complication of CPAF with no definitively established rate of occurrence currently. CPAF-associated aneurysm micro-rupture followed by haemorrhagic pericardial effusion, especially in patients receiving anticoagulation therapy, is a problem that has been overlooked; and deserves increased awareness.27 Another advantage of CTCA is its effectiveness in identifying the drainage sites. The drainage site can exhibit various shapes, such as jet sign, smoke sign, pierced sign, and isodensity sign, which correlate with shunt flow and influence management decisions.26 Failure to establish the drainage site complicates the operation leading to prolonged operative duration and an increased risk of post-operative complications.28 Further investigation into drainage morphology is necessary for a comprehensive understanding of CPAF in the future. CCA or cardiac catheterization is considered the gold standard for diagnosing CAD, but is limited by its invasiveness and 2-dimensional imaging nature which may miss the drainage sites of the CPAF. Combining CTCA with CCA provides more precise information about the fistula, its drainage site and the adjacent structures.

ECHO has limitations in delineating the origin and anatomy of a fistula. However, it is advantageous in assessing coexisting heart structural or valvular abnormalities along with impaired heart function or pulmonary hypertension. Our data revealed that 11.4% of cases developed depressed LVEF among those who had an ECHO study, which is lower than the literature report showing a 19% incidence of heart failure.29 This situation is caused by various coexisting cardiovascular diseases besides shunt flow volume. There was a rate of 4.4% of cases having elevated SPAP, yet we lack literature reports for comparison.

In terms of stress imaging, vasodilators have little effect on dilating the fistulous artery. However, our data revealed that among the 78 patients who underwent stress tests, 83.3% of cases were positive for ischaemia, with 78.5% having non-obstructive CAD according to the CCA examinations. Although the true incidence of the coronary steal phenomenon in CPAF has remained unknown, our data suggest a coronary steal phenomenon that causes myocardial ischaemic manifestations.

Our data have revealed that older age is an independent risk factor for developing aneurysm CPAF. Furthermore, aneurysm CPAF was found to be negatively related to obstructive CAD. These findings suggest that CPAF as a congenital anomaly may develop complications such as aneurysms over the course of the disease. Additionally, we lack evidence to indicate that aneurysm CPAF is a secondary pathogenesis of collateral blood flow following severe CAD.30 Our data consistently suggest that patients with aneurysm CPAF are more likely to undergo CTCA examinations, highlighting the value of CTCA in diagnosing and treating CPAF. The major risks associated with aneurysm CPAF are the rupture of aneurysms or thrombosis, which can lead to increased mortality.31 This is supported by our data, which show no correlation between shunt flow and aneurysm formation but 3 out of the 4 non-procedural related decreased cases had aneurysm rupture.10,17,18

The clinical decision to treat CPAF depends on the anatomy of the fistula, age, complications, and comorbidities. Our data revealed that patients with symptoms were more likely to undergo transcutaneous or surgical interventions to close the fistula. This aligns with the guidelines recommendation of closing small- or medium-sized CPAF in cases of cardiac symptoms, including myocardial ischaemia, arrhythmia, ventricular dysfunction, ventricular dilation, or endarteritis.2 Large fistulas should be closed regardless of symptoms.2

Surgical correction have been reported to be a safe and effective approach with negligible associated mortality or morbidity.21 In our study, surgical correction was the most commonly employed approach. Specifically, the percentage of patients undergoing surgical interventions was much higher in the aneurysm group compared to the non-aneurysm group. It is widely accepted that complex patients should be treated with surgical ligation with a patch in the pulmonary artery if the drainage site is large, or with aneurysmorrhaphy or CABG when indicated. A history of bacterial endocarditis and a high risk of rupture of enlarged aneurysms are 2 additional indications for surgical correction.32 Literature also suggests that coronary aneurysms larger than 8 mm are at high risk of rupture, and surgical closure is preferred to minimize complications during transcatheter fistula closure and reduce the risk of embolic ischaemic myocardial injury.33 Complex fistulas may necessitate cardiopulmonary bypass and cardioplegic arrest; however, the use of cardiopulmonary bypass should be avoided whenever possible.22,34

The criteria for percutaneous transcatheter closure include favourable anatomy, accessibility to the distal portion, a narrowed drainage site, and the absence of surgical indications, which may be more cost-effective. Contraindications include significant obstructive CAD hindering access and closure, severe comorbidities, active infections, and inability to comply with post-procedure surveillance.35 Coil embolization is the most common method used, while graft-covered stent implantation can be employed for treating plexiform fistulas with multiple origins. Complications may include failed embolization13,14 and LM dissection15 as revealed in our study, or coil migration with distal embolization. A recommended course of 3 to 6 months of post-procedural antiplatelet therapy is advised.36

Limitations

This study may have potential publication bias, as only subjects with abnormal findings were included. Additionally, a precise evaluation of the association between the anatomy and morphology of the CPAFs with interventions and outcomes was not possible due to heterogeneous and incomplete data. Lastly, long-term outcomes of post-procedure or conservative treatment are uncertain due to incomplete follow-up.

CONCLUSION

The clinical presentations of CPAF are often non-diagnostic. CTCA is the preferred method for visualizing the fistulas and their surrounding structures. Surgical treatment is beneficial in cases of aneurysm CPAF or with other surgical indications. Transcatheter closure of CPAF may be considered for selected individuals with favourable anatomy.
