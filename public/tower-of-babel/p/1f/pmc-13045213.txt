Nurse‐Led Clinic for Patients With Decompensated Liver Cirrhosis
Authors: Elsebeth E. Kelstrup, Sara H. Andersen, Tine L. Poulsen, Karen L. Thomsen, Konstantin Kazankov, Palle Bager
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsPrognosis for patients with decompensated liver cirrhosis is poor and readmissions are frequent. Closer and more structured follow‐up by nurses may prevent readmissions and stabilize liver‐related complications. We aimed to test the hypothesis that establishing a nurse‐led clinic for patients with decompensated liver cirrhosis could prevent hospital admissions.MethodsA quasi‐experimental study including complex patients with decompensated cirrhosis and complications. The intervention was a patient‐centered and nurse‐led clinic. Contacts to the participants were mainly by phone or face‐to‐face visits in the main outpatient clinic. In addition, the whole cohort of patients with cirrhosis followed in the department was monitored prior to, during, and after the intervention. Data was evaluated using interrupted time series.ResultsA total of 55 patients were enrolled in the clinic between January 2022 and February 2024. Most (55%) became able to self‐manage and were discharged from the clinic hereafter. They were characterized by having lower clinical scores and having fewer mental problems. In contrast, 18% were unable to benefit from the clinic. Data on cohort‐level showed decreases in outpatient visits (p < 0.05) and admissions (p < 0.005) while the clinic was active.ConclusionsEstablishing a nurse‐led clinic for patients with decompensated cirrhosis is feasible and followed by reduced in‐ and outpatient contacts while the clinic was active.

Introduction

The mortality rate for patients with liver disease is high and rising while the mortality rates for other major diseases are declining [1]. Around half of the patients with decompensated cirrhosis die within 5 years of diagnosis. However, the risk of death depends on how well complications are managed and prevented [2]. A liver transplantation is the only curative treatment for advanced cirrhosis but is highly limited by organ availability. As a result, patient care is primarily focused on identifying, preventing, and managing factors that lead to complications like ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy (HE), malnutrition, and infections. Key initiatives to support this include promoting lifestyle changes, enhancing self‐care and health literacy, and improving patient adherence to treatment and care plans [3].

Cirrhosis progresses gradually over time, often going undiagnosed for years until complications arise. In the early stage, known as compensated cirrhosis, patients may experience a good quality of life with few or no symptoms of liver disease. However, as the disease advances to decompensated cirrhosis, overt clinical signs such as jaundice, ascites, variceal bleeding, or HE become prominent, significantly impacting the patient's health and quality of life, as well as prognosis [4]. Once any of these complications occur, cirrhosis generally progresses more rapidly toward death unless the patient receives a liver transplant.

Patients with decompensated cirrhosis have an average readmission rate of three times per year, with 20%–37% of patients readmitted within 30 days of discharge [5, 6, 7]. Unstructured follow‐up in outpatient settings has not proven effective in preventing frequent readmissions due to recurrence of cirrhosis complications. Factors such as drug‐related side effects, non‐adherence to self‐care and medical treatment, and relapse of alcohol intake may play a role. Previous studies suggest that closer and more structured outpatient follow‐up could prevent up to one‐third of these readmissions [5, 8]. In addition to the limited treatment options, many patients with cirrhosis are characterized by low health literacy, lack of social support, and significant barriers to managing their disease [9, 10, 11]. This highlights the need for tailored information and personalized guidance.

Nurses can have an important role in the holistic care for patients with cirrhosis, including running specialized nurse‐led clinics. A personalized, face‐to‐face approach may be essential in enhancing the patients' understanding of their condition. A mixed‐method study concluded that the patients' quality of life improved through an increased awareness of each patient's need for support and better information and education of the patients [12]. A review from 2023 describes several existing and potential tasks for nurses in the care for patients with decompensated cirrhosis [13]. In addition to the management of cirrhosis complications, these tasks include general health issues, for example, assessment of frailty and risk of falls, focus on healthy lifestyle, diet, and self‐management. Self‐management has been described as an individual's ability to manage symptoms, treatment, physical and psychosocial consequences and lifestyle changes inherent in living with a chronic condition [14].

Several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have explored the efficacy of nurse‐led patient‐centered intervention for patients with decompensated cirrhosis, with limited clinical effects shown [15, 16, 17]. In two studies, there was no significant difference in the primary endpoint of readmission rates when comparing the intervention to standard of care [15, 16]. Another RCT reported that the participants' perceived quality of care rate was higher in the adjunctive nurse‐based care arm compared to the standard care arm after 1 year, but this difference was not sustained after 2 years [17]. This finding aligns well with the conclusion of a 2023 systematic review, which included 11 controlled studies and five prospective studies [18]. The review demonstrated improvements across various interventions; however, the studies were of moderate to low quality, and no specific type of nurse‐assisted post‐discharge intervention could be definitively recommended.

RCTs are perceived as the methodology of choice for causal inference and are widely employed in medical research. However, it remains uncertain as to what extent the methodology used in the RCT studies mentioned above could have biased the results. In unblinded RCTs, healthcare staff are required to treat participants in the intervention group differently than those receiving standard of care, which can inadvertently affect the quality of care provided. This inherent bias suggests that alternative research methods may be more suitable for exploring the topic effectively. Consequently, we chose to design a real‐life study using a quasi‐experimental design to explore the implementation and possible effect of a nurse‐led clinic.

The primary aim of the study was to test the hypothesis that establishing a nurse‐led clinic for patients with decompensated liver cirrhosis could prevent hospital admissions. The secondary aim was to identify patients who would benefit the most from the intervention.

Material and Methods

Intervention Study

This intervention study was an investigator‐initiated, quasi‐experimental study, where patients with decompensated cirrhosis, who were assessed as having a high burden of complex symptoms and low self‐efficacy, were invited. The assessment was done individually by liver nurses who had comprehensive clinical experience. The same nurses assessed patients who exhibited non‐compliance, cognitive deficits, or limited need for additional intervention from the specialized liver nurses. Patients with such a profile were excluded. The individual clinical score was not an inclusion criterion. All participants were recruited from the Department of Hepatology and Gastroenterology, Aarhus University Hospital, Denmark between January 2022 and February 2024.

Cohort

In parallel with the intervention study, data were collected from the entire cohort of patients diagnosed with liver cirrhosis at the hospital. Participants in the intervention study were embedded within this larger cohort. The cohort was dynamic, with patients exiting due to death, relocation, or other factors, while new patients were continuously added over time. Thus, the number of patients in the cohort remained stable throughout the study period, with approximately 400 active patients at any given time.

Patient and disease‐related data have been anonymized and continuously collected at hospital level since February 2019. To evaluate the possible effect of the intervention on hospital contacts, we used the interrupted time‐series (ITS) method. ITS is described as a robust quasi‐experimental longitudinal design for assessing the impact of an intervention in real‐world settings [19]. In an ITS design, data are collected at multiple intervals over time. In this study, monthly data were collected before, during, and after the intervention to establish a causal relationship between the intervention and the outcome.

Data from the dynamic cohort were analyzed from February 2019 to December 2024. The analysis aimed to evaluate potential changes in liver‐related hospital contacts following the introduction of the nurse‐led liver clinic in January 2022. The ITS method evaluates trends before, during, and after the intervention to determine whether the observed changes could be attributed to the new nurse‐led care model. The cohort approach allowed us to account for both the natural progression of the disease and any factors that could affect patient outcomes over time.

Prior to the intervention, it was assumed that the overall treatment and supportive care for patients with cirrhosis remained unchanged throughout over time. Accordingly, the cohort was considered consistent in terms of disease progression, outcomes, and complication rates across the past, present, and foreseeable future. Any significant changes in the trajectory of the patients' condition within the study period could thus be reasonably attributed to the intervention itself, as no other external factors were expected to have influenced the outcomes.

Intervention

The intervention introduced was a patient‐centered, nurse‐led clinic for patients with decompensated liver cirrhosis. The clinic was staffed and run by 3–5 senior hepatology nurse specialists. Two hepatologists were also directly affiliated with the clinic. The clinic offered individualized consultations for patients and their relatives by the nurses. The initial visit was conducted face‐to‐face, while subsequent visits could take place either by telephone or in person at the clinic. The duration and type of contact would depend on the specific circumstances.

Initially, the clinic service was open for appointments and telephone consultations twice a week; however, additional contacts could be arranged as needed.

The clinic focused on monitoring signs of decompensation and goal‐directed care, aiming to address patients' specific concerns and issues related to their condition. As the aim of the study was to prevent hospital readmissions, the intervention focused on early identification of cirrhosis‐related complications, and equipping patients and relatives with tools to support better self‐management. This could be tight self‐monitoring of body weight when having ascites or dose regulation of lactulose when having HE.

We anticipated that some patients might not respond to the intervention, and that others may already possess a level of self‐management that could not be significantly improved through contact with the clinic. Those patients were excluded prior to inclusion or later.

Hepatologists provided support to the nurses in the clinic as needed. Additionally, visits to the nurse‐led clinic were paralleled by clinical physician consultations as part of the standard outpatient follow‐up. The participating nurses were able to prescribe common medication to the patients enrolled. This became possible after specific training of the nurses and subsequently described in a local prescription framework. The head of department had the overall responsibility for prescriptions done by nurses in accordance with the Danish law.

Measures and Analysis

On cohort level, monthly data on liver disease‐related admissions and outpatient visits were extracted from the hospital's dynamic cohort database, covering the period from February 2019 to December 2024.

On individual level, patient data included demographics, liver disease etiology, comorbidities, and main health issues related to the liver disease. Furthermore, index scores for Model for End‐stage Liver Disease (MELD) [20], Child‐Pugh classification [21], and Clinical Frailty Score [22, 23] were calculated. Finally, individual data related to the liver clinic included the number and type of contacts to the clinic and reasons for termination of participation.

Data were presented using descriptive statistics. Parametric tests were used to test for significant differences. A two‐tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

This study was registered in the ISRCTN registry (ISRCTN20391899). As this study did not collect human tissue or investigate medical products, ethical approval was not required. The research adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Istanbul, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to their participation.

Results

Establishing a nurse‐led clinic for patients with decompensated liver cirrhosis was correlated with fewer hospital admissions and fewer outpatient contacts. Fifty‐five patients were enrolled from January 2022 until February 2024. The last patient's final visit took place in May 2024. Baseline characteristics of the enrolled patients are shown in Table 1. Most patients (78%) had alcohol‐related cirrhosis and were classified in Child Pugh class B or C (89%). Other aetiologies of liver cirrhosis included primary biliary cholangitis, autoimmune hepatitis, cryptogenic cirrhosis, and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease. Seven (13%) of the patients had alcoholic hepatitis at inclusion. Other co‐morbidities included hepatocellular carcinoma, pancreatitis, kidney disease, heart disease, and diabetes. The most common liver‐related problems for the patients were ascites (87%) and malnutrition (85%). The median number of problems for the individual patient was 4 (IQR 2–5).

Service and Outcome

The number and type of visits to the liver clinic are shown in Table 2. Patients had a median number of 7 (IQR 3–11) contacts to the clinic. The median follow‐up time for the participants was 142 (IQR 43–224) days. The most frequent contact form was telephone calls followed by face‐to‐face appointments in the clinic. Most patients (55%) were discharged from the liver clinic once they became capable of self‐managing their condition while affiliated with the clinic. We aimed only to include patients in the liver clinic if they were expected to benefit from the service, by consensus of the participating specialized nurses and hepatologists. However, 18% appeared to be unable to benefit from the service due to non‐adherence, ongoing severe alcohol intake, or cognitive impairment and were therefore discharged from this clinic and followed in the standard of care outpatient service. Furthermore, nine patients (17%) died, three (5%) underwent a liver transplant, and three (5%) were lost to follow up. In Table 3, baseline data for subgroups of patients stratified by the reason for discharge are compared. Patients who became able to self‐manage were characterized by having less comorbidity, lower MELD‐ and Child‐Pugh scores, and fewer mental problems than the patients who couldn't benefit from the service and the patients who were lost to follow up. Most patients (27 of 43; 63%) included with alcohol‐related liver cirrhosis became able to self‐manage. In addition, more females compared to males (64% vs. 47%) became able to self‐manage after having been affiliated with the liver clinic.

Impact on Hospital Contacts

Hospital data showed a decrease in mean monthly admission rates among cirrhosis patients in general from 4.5 ± 1.0 per 100 patients in 2022 to 3.1 ± 1.0 in 2023 (p < 0.005), (Figure 1). In 2024, when the clinic was closing, the number rose to 4.1 ± 1.1 (p < 0.05). Similarly, outpatient contacts decreased from 16.8 ± 3.1 per 100 patients in 2022 to 14.7 ± 4.6 in 2023 (p < 0.05). The number of mean monthly inpatients per 100 patients decreased between 2019 (4.7 ± 1.5) and 2024 (4.1 ± 1.1). However, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.29).

Discussion

Our results showed significant reductions in the number of both in‐ and outpatient contacts after the intervention was established. However, the number increased again as the intervention was about to terminate. Our findings demonstrate how patient‐related goal‐directed care can be effectively delivered by hepatology specialist nurses. The data also highlight a broad spectrum of patient‐specific health challenges and individual support needs addressed by the clinic. Importantly, our findings offer valuable insight into the patient profiles most likely to benefit from such interventions. This aligns with the recent EASL guidelines and extends beyond them, emphasizing that complications associated with decompensated cirrhosis can be mitigated through secondary preventive measures and individualized support [4, 24].

Unsurprisingly, patients with lower clinical scores at baseline made up the majority of those who became able to self‐manage following their consultations at the clinic. Furthermore, patients with severe co‐morbidities and those experiencing mental health issues appeared to be more difficult to engage through the clinic. It is also notable that nearly all patients who became able to self‐manage had alcohol‐related cirrhosis, and alcohol was reported as a problem in two‐thirds of these individuals. This finding may challenge certain existing preconceptions, particularly as these patients are often assumed to struggle more with adherence and self‐care. Some patients failed to benefit from the clinic. This highlights the need for further exploration of individual barriers to engagement. In this regard, being male or having significant co‐morbidities appears to be of importance and may represent areas for greater focus in future iterations of the nurse‐led liver clinic. Thus, the potential for even more individualized care could be considered. However, it is also important to acknowledge that, despite best efforts, some patients may not be able to benefit from any intervention.

For methodological reasons, we refrained from conducting an RCT or including a control group. Instead, our study represented a real‐life complex intervention, with efforts continuously adapted during its implementation [25]. This adaptability could be regarded as a strength, as it allowed the intervention to remain dynamic and responsive to practical circumstances. However, it also represents a limitation, as the intervention became more difficult to describe in a standardized manner, and no control group was available for comparison.

In the study, we sought to assess the impact of the clinic in terms of hospital contacts using data from the hospital database. Our findings indicate a correlation between a decrease in patient contacts and the introduction of the liver clinic. Notably, the number of contacts increased again from 2023 to 2024, when the clinic began reducing its activities, as illustrated in Figure 1. However, it is a weakness that these data are prone to a great degree of uncertainty, largely due to the unexpected pre‐intervention period, which was affected by the COVID‐19 lockdown, as data from this period are considered abnormal compared to any other historical period.

Two recent studies have reported reductions in readmissions following the introduction of nurse‐led clinics [26, 27]. Despite the significant findings, the authors of both publications suggest further research on this subject. In a retrospective matched design, Giles et al. compared readmission rates between patients seen in a nurse‐led clinic vs. standard follow‐up [26]. Kalo et al. also compared patients followed in a nurse‐led clinic to standard care [27]. However, the patients were not randomized and allocation to the nurse‐led clinic was not fully described. Nevertheless, the authors specifically propose a large multicentre RCT aiming to investigate the long‐term impact of such intervention [27]. The suitability of RCTs as the ideal methodology in this context is questionable, as discussed above. Furthermore, if a large multicentre or even international study yields statistically significant results demonstrating the effectiveness of nurse‐led clinics, real‐life patient populations are not uniform, as also highlighted by our study. Additionally, the competencies and resources of healthcare services vary considerably between hospitals, and even more so between countries.

Although the current evidence can be challenging to compare systematically, the findings within the body of published studies illustrate that nurse‐led clinics for patients with decompensated cirrhosis are both feasible and beneficial [13, 15, 17, 18, 26, 27, 28]. Our study also confirms this. How this knowledge is implemented will largely depend on local conditions, and it is unlikely that large RCTs would significantly alter the rationale for establishing nurse‐led clinics or the process of implementation of such clinics. Rather than focusing solely on generating more data, resources may be better spent on establishing patient‐centered services that directly address the needs of this complex and vulnerable patient group.

In conclusion, establishing a nurse‐led clinic for patients with decompensated cirrhosis is feasible in a real‐life setting. Patients who benefit the most were characterized by moderate clinical disease scores. Notably, alcohol‐related liver cirrhosis does not appear to be a barrier to achieving beneficial outcomes from clinic affiliation, whereas severe co‐morbidity and male gender may present a barrier. Furthermore, it may be worth considering if RCT's are the most suitable study design for generating evidence in support of this type of intervention.
