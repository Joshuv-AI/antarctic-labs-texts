HPV vaccination intention among guardians of female secondary school students in Chongqing, China
Authors: Ningpei Bai, Qing Wang, Jianchao Shao, Lei Chen, Chenglian Wang, Xiao Xiao, Linling Yu, Binyue Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: In China, Chongqing is the first province implement a province-wide HPV vaccination free program for female students. Given the HPV vaccination of adolescents is largely dependent on the intent of their guardians, this study examined the factors associated with the parental intention to vaccinate their daughter against HPV. And we could explore how to change the factors to incentivize more guardians to vaccinate their children.Methods: This study used a cross-sectional survey designed by multi-stage non-randomized sampling. Study participants were guardians of female students of second year of junior high in all districts and counties of Chongqing, who were most knowledgeable about the immunization status of the adolescents. Data was collected by an online survey platform between December, 2022 to March, 2023.Results: We collected 20, 642 valid samples. Among the participating guardians, nearly 40% guardians had never heard of HPV and HPV vaccine. 68.8% guardians considered vaccinating their children against HPV, 12.5% guardians had vaccinated or vaccinating HPV vaccine for their children, and only 16.7% were reluctant to vaccinate for their children right now. Some individual characteristics of guardians (e.g. age, number of the children) were associated with the intention of HPV vaccines. Sufficient knowledge of HPV and HPV vaccine would promote the HPV vaccination, and vaccine hesitancy prevented guardians from vaccinating their children.Conclusions: Majority of guardians held a positive intention to vaccinate their daughter, higher than the foreign uptake. Exploring the methods of social propaganda to promote HPV-related knowledge and reduce the safety concerns of guardians could help improve HPV vaccination intention.KEYWORDS: Human papillomavirus vaccine, guardians, vaccine intent

Introduction

Cervical cancer poses a significant global public health challenge, particularly burdening numerous in many low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), resulting in an annual mortality of approximately 300,000 women and affecting nearly 600,000 women.1–3 Cervical cancer is widely regarded as preventable due to highly effective primary (human papillomavirus vaccine) and secondary (screening) prevention measures. However, these measures have not been equitably implemented both internationally and domestically.3 To prevent cervical cancer, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended that the primary target population for human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination is girls aged 9–14 years before they become sexually active.4 It has been confirmed that vaccinating pre-adolescent girls is typically cost-effective for cervical cancer prevention.5,6

In 2018, the Director-General of WHO called for action toward the elimination of cervical cancer.7 In 2022, the WHO Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer as a Public Health Problem recommended that HPV vaccines be included in all national immunization programs and reach 90% of all girls by age 15 by 2030.4,8 By November 2022, 125 countries had introduced the HPV vaccine into their national immunization programs for girls, and 47 countries had also included boys.9 However, HPV vaccination is not yet a part of the national immunization program (NIP) in China and still relies on private funding by guardians.

In response to the WHO’s call to accelerate the elimination of cervical cancer, the Chinese Government announced the Healthy China Action (2019–2030) in 2019 and the Accelerated Cervical Cancer Elimination Action Plan (2023–2030) in 2023.10,11 Consequently, Chongqing launched an HPV vaccination project for female second-year junior high students in November 2022. The project offered free vaccinations for the domestic bivalent HPV vaccines (200 yuan–300 yuan per dose) and provided a financial subsidy for other HPV vaccines (600 yuan–1,300 yuan per dose) to increase the affordability of the vaccine. All eligible girls could receive the HPV vaccine at specified community healthcare centers. However, the project only commenced in February 2023 due to the COVID-19 epidemic and vaccine shortages. Disease control personnel and vaccination staff had received training before the project began. Nevertheless, the initiation and completion of the HPV vaccination series by students largely depend on their guardians’ intent.12–14 All guardians were required to sign an informed consent indicating whether they wished to vaccinate their children.

The aim of this cross-sectional study was to examine how factors such as guardians’ education, knowledge of HPV, and place of residence affect their intention to vaccinate their daughters against HPV. We also aimed to explore how these factors could be changed to incentivize more guardians to vaccinate their children. Our study focused on guardians of daughters, as HPV vaccination was recommended for girls only at the time of program creation.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study used a cross-sectional survey with a multi-stage non-randomized sampling design. An online survey was conducted from December 2022 to March 2023, targeting guardians of female second-year junior high students in Chongqing Municipality, China. The age of female second-year junior high students was between 13 and 15 years old, which corresponded to the age for HPV vaccination by the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China. In China, Chongqing was the first province to implement a province-wide vaccination subsidy program. The province had a total population of 32 million, with female students making up about 47.4% of the total students in regular junior secondary school, slightly higher than the national average (46.5%).

Due to the feasibility restriction, two schools were identified in every district and county of Chongqing for participants recruitment. The schools were selected by the disease control personnel in each district based on the position, size, and so on. According to the information in the vaccination system, the HPV vaccination rate of female students in the second year of junior high was nearly 5% before the project began.

Based on the results of our pilot study and to achieve a larger sample size, we estimated the hesitancy rate at 23%, with a design efficiency of 2.5. This experiment design allowed us to investigate the actual vaccine hesitancy with a margin of error of 0.01 and a significance level cutoff of 0.05. Under the calculation formulas of multi-stage non-randomized sampling, the required sample size of this cross-sectional survey was calculated as 6,800. Assuming no more than 10% loss to follow-up, the required total sample size was calculated as 18,900.

Ethics statement

This study received approval from the ethics committee of the Chongqing Center for Disease Control and Prevention (KY-2022-013). Informed consents were obtained from all participants at the beginning of the survey.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was provided in Chinese. Based on previous research and expert consultations, the questionnaire included information about socio-demographic characteristics (such as age, number of children, place of residence, marital status, income, relationship with children, educational level, and occupation) as shown in Table 1. Additionally, it covered the awareness of HPV and the HPV vaccine, the extent of vaccine hesitancy, preferences for the type of HPV vaccine, and other relevant information. The awareness of HPV and the HPV vaccine toward vaccination was measured by some general knowledge questions. Vaccine hesitancy was measured by the Vaccine Hesitancy Scale (VHS) which was developed by the Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (SAGE) Working Group on Vaccine Hesitancy.15 The primary outcome was the intention of guardians to vaccinate the children against HPV. The options included “already vaccinated,” “intended to accept HPV vaccination,” “refused HPV vaccination,” and “had not considered HPV vaccination recently.” Only one choice could be selected, and we combined the last two responses, both of which indicated no intent to receive the HPV vaccine recently. That is exactly the object we wanted to incentivize. Each variable was explained in detail, and the questionnaire was set up with jump and logic controls to reduce the measurement bias.

Before the questionnaire was made available to all participants, researchers surveyed a small portion of parents to ensure the questionnaire was easily understood and completed.

Data collection

The final survey was distributed online via the mini application ‘Wen Juan Xing,’ a widely used online survey platform with functions equivalent to Amazon Mechanical Turk. Researchers commissioned class teachers to distribute the questionnaire in WeChat groups, the primary communication method among Chinese guardians and teachers. The questionnaire began with informed consent. Participants could proceed to survey items only after agreeing to continue. The completion of the questionnaire was tracked using the region and school type variables. For the unfinished schools, teachers supervised guardians in filling out the questionnaire. The data of 21,411 guardians who completed the questionnaire were recorded in our study, while the other uncompleted questionnaires cannot be recorded by the platform. All the records were anonymous and without any personal identifiers.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for socio-demographic characteristics, the awareness rate of HPV and the HPV vaccine, the degree of vaccine hesitancy by number and percentages. Knowledge of HPV and HPV vaccine was assessed using knowledge score (KS). One point was assigned to each question with only one correct option. The overall KS was categorized with a quantile value as a threshold, resulting in three classes of ‘low,’ ‘moderate’ and ‘high.15 Vaccine hesitancy was assessed using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1(extremely disagree) to 5 (extremely agree).16 The total score for vaccine hesitancy ranged from 10 to 50, and the degree of vaccine hesitancy was categorized as ‘low’ and ‘high,’ using the median value for bivariate analysis. Willingness to get a vaccination was assessed by the question ‘Would you like to get the HPV vaccine for your child now?.’ Logistic regressions were utilized to identify factors linked to awareness and knowledge of the HPV vaccine. Odds ratio (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) were presented. Statistical significance was determined by two-tailed tests with a significance level of 0.05.

Results

A total of 21,411 guardians completed the survey. We excluded respondents who provided logically inconsistent answers, such as guardians whose age was too young and provided inconsistent answers. The final sample consisted of 20,642 guardians (including 15,151 mothers, 4,451 fathers, and 1,040 other relatives) with girls in their second year of junior high.

Characteristics of guardians

Table 1 presents the characteristics of both guardians and children. The average age of the guardians was 41.0 ± 7.2, and 58.5% of them resided in urban areas. The majority (74.1%) were aged 45 and below, more than half (66.3%) had a junior high school diploma or lower, while 11.1% held a college degree or higher. Additionally, 88.6% were married, 21.2% worked in the production manufacturing industry, 19.7% worked in agriculture, and 18.2% worked in the business industry. Only 1.7% were employed in the medical field. And 80.3% had an annual household income below 100,000 yuan. Most of the participants in the survey had two children, and 60.0% were only girls.

Awareness of HPV

Among 20,642 guardians who participated in the survey, a large majority (87.1%) had heard of cervical cancer and 69.3% had heard of HPV. More than half knew HPV through television/network/newspaper/magazine or families/friends, and nearly half knew through doctors or public lectures in hospital and school (Figure 1). We devised eight questions to evaluate guardians’ awareness of HPV, assigning one to each correct response. Among guardians who had heard HPV, the mean score was 3.3 ± 2.2, with a minimum score of 0 and a maximum score of 8. The KS was dichotomized into three classes, ‘low’ (scores less than three), ‘moderate’ (scores between three and five), and ‘high’ (scores more than five). About 35.9% guardians who had heard HPV with low cognitive about HPV, 45.6% were moderate and 18.4% were high.

Ways of acquiring relevant knowledge of HPV and HPV vaccine ranked from the most to the least. The questions were multiple choices in the survey.

Awareness of HPV vaccine

In the survey, more than half (67.4%) of guardians had heard of the HPV vaccine. Among guardians who had heard of the HPV vaccine, 56.3% knew about the HPV vaccine through television, network, newspapers, and magazines, and 47.8% were informed by doctors in hospitals or schools (Figure 1). We devised six questions to measure guardians’ awareness of HPV vaccine, with one point awarded for each correct response. Among guardians who had heard of the HPV vaccine, the average score was 3.5 ± 1.7, with the minimum score of 0 and the maximum score of 6. The overall KS was divided into three categories, ‘low’ (scores less than three), ‘moderate’ (scores between three and four), and ‘high’ (scores more than four). About 26.0% of guardians who had heard of the HPV vaccine had low cognitive understanding about it, 39.6% had moderate understanding, and 34.4% had high understanding.

Degree of vaccine hesitancy

WHO’s SAGE on Immunization defined vaccine hesitancy as the act of delaying or refusing vaccines despite vaccine availability.17 In the study, the VHS developed by the WHO SAGE Working Group on Vaccine Hesitancy was used to assess vaccine hesitancy. The VHS consists of 10 items that are rated on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree.’ Among 20,642 guardians, the average score was 23.5 ± 4.1, with a minimum score of 10 and a maximum score of 42. The degree of vaccine hesitancy was categorized into two groups, ‘low’ indicating a score less than 26, and ‘high’ indicating a score more than 25. Among our survey guardians, 69.9% had low vaccine hesitancy and 30.1% had high hesitancy.

Attitude of the HPV vaccine

A majority (68.8%) of the guardians considered vaccinating their children against HPV, while 12.5% had already vaccinated or were in the process of vaccinating their children, and only 16.7% were reluctant to vaccinate their children right at the moment. Among guardians who refused vaccines, 50.1% believed that their child was too young to be vaccinated, 42.7% were fearful of serious adverse reactions, 39.4% worried about negative effects on the child’s growth and development of, and 33.3% had concerns about the effectiveness of the vaccine in preventing HPV infection. When guardians were asked about the stimulus promoting them to vaccinate their children, 56.3% chose “Scientific evidence confirms that vaccination works,” while over 40% of the guardians indicated that they would accept recommendations from a doctor, school, or government. About the reasons of those who were willing to vaccinate their children but still not, the top three were “Unknown where to get vaccinated,” “Vaccines are scarce and not available” and “Expensive.”

Among guardians who had vaccinated or were vaccinating their children with the HPV vaccine, 41.4% chose the bivalent, 20.3% chose the nine-valent, and 15.2% chose the quadrivalent vaccine. In terms of guardians who were willing to vaccinate their children, 29.1% preferred the bivalent vaccine, 24.8% preferred the nine-valent, only 8.6% preferred the quadrivalent vaccine, and 37.3% did not know how to choose. We also found that 68.3% of guardians would like to vaccinate themselves or their wives with the HPV vaccine, but only 8.1% had received the vaccine.

Factors associated with guardians’ willingness to vaccinate their children

Table 2 shows the results from the binary logistic regression of associations between vaccination intention and individual characteristics. The regression revealed that guardians living in urban areas were 0.812 (95% CI, 0.750–0.879, p < .001) times less likely than those in rural areas to be willing to vaccinate their children with the HPV vaccine. Guardians aged over 45 years old were 0.881 (95% CI, 0.809–0.959, p = .004) times less likely than those under 45 years old to be willing to vaccinate their children with the HPV vaccine. Compared to guardians who had children only girls, guardians who had children of both genders were 0.728 (95% CI, 0.671–0.791, p < .001) times less likely to be willing to vaccinate their children with the HPV vaccine. Additionally, guardians who had two children were 1.167 (95% CI, 1.043–1.307, p = .007) times more likely than guardians with one child to be willing to vaccinate their children with the HPV vaccine. Guardians who had a relative with cervical cancer were 1.704 (95% CI, 1.317–2.205, p < .001) times more likely than guardians who were not willing to vaccinate their children with the HPV vaccine, and guardians who had self-funded vaccination inoculation for children were also 1.133 (95% CI, 1.050–1.223, p = .001) times more likely than guardians who were not willing to vaccinate their children with the HPV vaccine.

Discussion

We conducted a cross-sectional survey in Chongqing, China, to understand guardians’ intentions regarding HPV vaccine inoculation for second-year junior high girls. Among unvaccinated girls, 21.4% of guardians were currently reluctant to vaccinate, comparable to 58.0% in the United States and 23.2% in Ethiopia.18,19 Collectively, guardians’ attitudes might be a combined effect of many factors. For example, guardians who are 45 years old or younger, guardians with two children, guardians with children only daughters, guardians living in rural settings, guardians who had a relative with cervical cancer, and guardians that had self-funded vaccination inoculation for children were more willing to vaccinate their children against HPV.

Additional health education and communication might be necessary, as we discovered knowledge gaps among guardians regarding HPV and the HPV vaccine. Although the refusal rate for HPV vaccine was low (2.1%), 32.6% of the guardians had never heard of HPV vaccines, and 37.3% said they did not have enough information to make an informed choice. Similar phenomena have been found in previous studies.13,20,21 Sufficient knowledge correlated with HPV vaccination uptake, and we also found that guardians with greater awareness of HPV and HPV vaccine were more likely to vaccinate their children.22 Lack of knowledge may have contributed to the low vaccine coverage observed in mainland China.

We demonstrated that vaccine hesitancy among guardians could hinder HPV vaccination for their children. The WHO now recognizes vaccine hesitancy as one of the greatest threats to global health.23 Common concerns among study participants included potential side effects of the HPV vaccine and the perception that their children were too young for it, echoing findings in existing literature.13,18,19,24–26 Significant declines in HPV vaccine coverage due to safety concerns have been noted.27 Scientific evidence significantly influences guardians’ decisions to vaccinate their children, and recommendations from healthcare providers play a crucial role in enhancing guardians’ intent to vaccinate children against HPV.21,28 Exploring ways to strengthen social propaganda, for instance organizing parent–child discussion sessions to develop effective communication strategies, could help reduce vaccine hesitancy among guardians. In the context of medical and preventive integration, publicity about HPV vaccines should focus on communication with clinical doctors. This includes incorporating information about the HPV vaccine into training for the prevention and control of infectious diseases. Conducting academic research and organizing publicity activities about the HPV vaccine with clinic doctors were also methods worth exploring.

In our study, although 81.3% of the guardians expressed willingness to vaccinate their children against HPV, only 12.5% had actually done so. Implementation barriers include vaccine availability issues (shortage of vaccine or lack of awareness about vaccination sites) and high cost. We believe that providing financial subsidies for eligible girls would encourage guardians to consider vaccinating their children, as originally intended in the Chongqing project. Regarding the choice of HPV vaccine type, most guardians opted for the bivalent vaccine. This preference stemmed from the fact that children could receive the bivalent HPV vaccine at age 9, and it was the only domestically produced HPV vaccine in China. In addition, the supply of the bivalent HPV vaccine was more plentiful than the others. The guardians who wanted the high-valent HPV vaccine had been waiting, but since June 2023, with the use of the appointment system and the increase in supply of high-valent HPV, most people have been vaccinated.

Notably, female second-year junior high students may not complete the full vaccination course after the initial dose. A survey in the USA indicated nearly a quarter of adolescents who received the first HPV vaccine dose had no plans to complete the series.18 Despite the HPV program’s launch in Chongqing, only about 35% female second-year junior high students had completed their HPV vaccine schedule by June 2023. Although updates to the WHO’s HPV vaccination schedule expand the target population, the government will continue to focus on second-year junior high female students, considering the impact of sexual activity and project feasibility. Enhancing logistical support (e.g., school-based vaccination campaigns) may be necessary to increase HPV vaccine coverage across all age groups in the future.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional study design allows identification of but not causality. Secondly, sample biases may exist due to accessibility factors. Although the survey protocol aimed to include participants from various school types, some respondents may not have adhered to this protocol. Thirdly, the use of the nine-valent HPV vaccine was not expanded during the study period. Therefore, some guardians may have delayed vaccination to wait for availability of this vaccine. Non-random data collection may have led to an underestimation or overestimation of parameters in our study. Despite these limitations, the key strength of our study is its coverage across all regions of Chongqing, allowing targeted interventions based on survey findings to support HPV vaccine implementation. This could serve as a reference for other provinces regarding factors influencing guardians’ intentions to vaccinate in China.
