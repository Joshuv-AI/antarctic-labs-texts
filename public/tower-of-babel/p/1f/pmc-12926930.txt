Clinical Practice Variations in Interventional Treatment of Chronic Lumbosacral Radicular Pain Among Registered Dutch Pain Specialists: A National Survey
Authors: Marius R van Ooijen, Sezai Özkan, Kris C P Vissers, Koen van Boxem, Sandra A S van den Heuvel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis questionnaire study aims to analyze the current clinical practice of the minimally invasive treatment of chronic lumbosacral radicular pain among accredited pain specialists in the Netherlands.MethodsParticipants received an online questionnaire regarding differences in clinical practice comprising definitions and clinical criteria for diagnosis; indications for interventional treatment; technical approaches to treatment; and outcome evaluation in the management of chronic lumbosacral radicular pain.Results and ConclusionA total of 125 pain specialists (response rate: 29%) completed the questionnaire. The most notable finding was that only 27% reported routinely performing prognostic selective nerve root blocks with local anesthetics prior to pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) treatment in patients with chronic lumbosacral radicular pain. Lidocaine was the preferred local anesthetic (n = 72, 63%). PRF was identified as the cornerstone of therapeutic interventional treatment by 81% of respondents (n = 93), and 40% (n = 46) reported combining PRF with corticosteroid administration.Keywords: back pain with radiation, dorsal root ganglion, nerve block, pain, pulsed radiofrequency

Introduction

Lumbosacral radicular pain (LSRP) is a prevalent and often debilitating condition, with annual prevalence rates varying from 9.9% to 25% and lifetime prevalence estimates up to 43% [1]. It is characterized by radiating pain in one or more lumbar or sacral dermatomes, often accompanied by other radicular symptoms such as sensory deficits, motor dysfunction, or both. Most patients recover spontaneously or with minimal ambulatory supportive treatment [2, 3], though the effectiveness of many non‐operative treatments remains subject to debate [3, 4].

However, 20% to 30% of patients with LSRP develop chronic (> 12 weeks) LSRP, persisting for more than 1 year [2, 3]. Chronic LSRP significantly impacts patients' quality of life and poses a considerable burden on healthcare systems due to frequent treatment interventions and the complexity of managing long‐term pain. For these patients, clinical guidelines suggest interventional pain treatment when conservative treatment proves insufficient [5]. In Europe, pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) of the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) is one of the main interventional treatment modalities for chronic LSRP. This procedure is typically preceded by a prognostic selective nerve root block with local anesthetics, with a positive outcome defined as at least 50% pain reduction [5, 6].

Despite the availability of clinical guidelines, their implementation in clinical practice remains challenging and may hinder effective treatment and compromise patient outcomes [7, 8]. The extent to which these guidelines for patients with chronic lumbosacral radicular pain are followed, particularly regarding the use of prognostic selective nerve root blocks prior to PRF, remains unclear. Peer conversations suggest that for the routine use of prognostic selective nerve root blocks, implementation is often hindered by logistical challenges, insufficient studies examining associations between prognostic selective nerve root blocks and clinical outcomes, and clinicians' concerns about patient burden.

This study aims to analyze the current clinical practice of the minimally invasive treatment of chronic lumbosacral radicular pain among accredited pain specialists in the Netherlands. Specifically, we conducted a national survey to investigate:

The diagnostic approach and criteria used to establish indications for interventional treatment in patients with chronic lumbosacral radicular pain.

Methods

Study Design

This cross‐sectional study was conducted between March and June 2024 and was exempt from Institutional Review Board approval by the CMO Radboudumc and the METC Oost‐Nederland, due to its non‐interventional nature and the anonymized nature of the data collection (3 January 2024, file number 2023‐16949).

Eligible participants were board‐certified pain specialists practicing in the Netherlands. Inclusion criteria were: [1] having a medical practice in the Netherlands; [2] being a practicing pain specialist; and [3] holding a board certification in pain management from the Dutch Society of Anesthesiology (DSA). In March 2024, 431 pain specialists were registered in the Netherlands, of whom 320 were members of the DSA. Due to privacy restrictions, individual email addresses of pain specialists were not accessible via the DSA. Direct individual approach was therefore not feasible.

Recruitment

To maximize participation, we employed the approach for questionnaire distribution into three parts. First, we distributed our questionnaire (Appendix A) to the participants through the research‐section of the quarterly e‐mail of the pain section of the DSA. Second, we contacted all registered pain institutes in the Netherlands by telephone to request their general email addresses. Pain centers were then asked to distribute our questionnaire internally to all affiliated pain specialists. Lastly, all pain specialists within the researchers' network were approached directly via e‐mail, phone, or personal contact to participate in our questionnaire. There was no (financial) reward for participation.

Questionnaire

After written informed consent, participants provided socio‐demographic data and completed our online questionnaires using Castor data management software (www.castoredc.com). Socio‐demographic variables included age, sex, main specialty, years in practice since board‐qualification, fellowship degree, employment status, and institution.

The questionnaire was developed by the authors who collectively have over 70 years of clinical experience in pain management. It was based on clinical observations, expert discussions, and key questions regarding prognostic selective nerve root blocks. The questionnaire (Appendix A) assesses differences in clinical practice concerning definitions and clinical criteria for diagnosis; indications for interventional treatment; technical approaches to treatment; and outcome evaluation in the management of chronic lumbosacral radicular pain. For some questions, multiple answers were allowed.

The final questionnaire (Appendix A) was reviewed for clarity and relevance by a panel of senior pain specialists, followed by pilot testing.

Statistical Analysis

Given the descriptive nature of the study, no comparative analyses were performed.

Diagnosis and Indication for Interventional Pain Treatment

Among Dutch pain specialists, 69% initiate interventional pain therapy for chronic lumbosacral radicular pain 12 weeks after symptom onset. The decision to begin treatment is primarily based on medical history (n = 111, 92%), followed by findings from medical imaging (n = 103, 85%); prior analysis by a referring specialist (n = 84, 69%); and physical examination (n = 78, 64%). Administered local anesthetic dosages for a prognostic selective nerve root block varied from less than 0.5 mL (n = 8, 7.1%) to more than 1.0 mL (n = 14, 13%). Lidocaine is the preferred local anesthetic used (n = 72, 63%). PRF was reported as the cornerstone of therapeutic interventional treatment by 81% (n = 93) of participants. Forty percent (n = 46) combine PRF with corticosteroid administration. For PRF procedures, a duration of 4 min was the most applied protocol (n = 80, 73%).

Outcome Assessment of Selective Nerve Root Block and PRF Treatment

Outcome assessment of prognostic selective nerve root block and PRF treatment are presented in Table 4. Over half of the respondents (n = 57, 51%) assess the outcome of prognostic selective nerve root block directly after the procedure. A reduction in pain of 50% or more is the primary criterion for a positive outcome (n = 88, 73%). Of the five participants who selected ‘other’ parameters to assess positive outcomes, two use patient satisfaction as a parameter; one used “100% pain reduction” as the threshold; and two incorporated additional aspects of the patient's medical history.

Discussion

This national survey highlights the variability in the clinical practice of Dutch pain specialists in terms of indication, execution and evaluation of interventions for chronic LSRP. These results underscore both the challenges and opportunities in aligning clinical practice with current guidelines to optimize patient outcomes.

Several results of this survey need to be accentuated. One of the most notable findings was that only one third (27%) of respondents routinely perform prognostic selective nerve root blocks before PRF treatments in patients with chronic LSRP, despite guideline recommendations advocating routine use for patient selection [5, 6]. Although this was not subject of our study, this deviation raises important questions about the perceived value of prognostic selective nerve root blockades in clinical practice. While prognostic selective nerve root block is intended to identify patients likely to benefit from PRF, only one study has explicitly linked positive prognostic selective nerve root block outcomes to successful PRF treatment [12]. The limited evidence base, as well as potential patient burden, patient preferences and economic costs of performing the procedure, might contribute to questions regarding the utility of prognostic selective nerve root blocks and could partially explain the observed variability in clinical practice.

Even among specialists who use prognostic selective nerve root blocks, techniques and interpretation are inconsistent. For example, half of the respondents assess immediately after selective nerve root block the outcome of their patient in the outpatient clinic, despite the widespread use of lidocaine as the preferred short‐acting local anesthetic. This introduces the potential for recall and interpretation bias, undermining the reliability of selective nerve root blocks as a prognostic tool. Standardizing the timing, technique, and criteria for outcome assessment is essential to ensure the clinical utility of prognostic selective nerve root block and facilitate meaningful research.

The observed variability in prognostic selective nerve root block practices has broader implications for patient care. If prognostic selective nerve root blocks are clinically valuable, the lack of standardization and suboptimal adherence to guidelines may delay effective treatment, increase healthcare costs, and expose patients to unnecessary interventions. On the other hand, if selective nerve root blocks have limited prognostic value, their routine use may unnecessarily burden patients with invasive procedures, further complicating care for a condition that already imposes significant functional and psychological burdens.

The variability also reflects a broader issue of guideline implementation. Clinical guidelines aim to reduce practice heterogeneity and improve patient outcomes, but their adoption often encounters barriers such as logistical constraints, reimbursement, differences in levels of evidence, clinician skepticism, and the lack of standardized training and examination (e.g., FIPP examination by the World Institute of Pain) in prognostic selective nerve root block procedures [8]. Addressing these challenges through education, streamlined protocols, training, resource allocation and solid scientific research (e.g., randomized controlled trials on this specific topic) could facilitate greater alignment with evidence‐based recommendations.

These findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, due to privacy constraints, direct contact with Dutch pain specialists was not possible. This survey was sent indirectly through a newsletter of the DSA, general e‐mail addresses of all Dutch Pain Centers, and informal networks. While multiple reminders were sent via e‐mail and phone, the indirect nature of the distribution might have contributed to a lower response rate (29%) and might have impacted the diversity an representativeness of the sample. Response rates to surveys vary between medical specialties [13] and our response rate aligns with similar survey studies conducted among Dutch anesthesiologists [14]. It is also possible that specialists who participated may differ systematically from non‐respondents in their adherence to guidelines or attitudes toward interventional pain management. This is a limitation of the nature of questionnaire studies and the inherent response bias that may always be present in these studies. Second, this study focuses exclusively on Dutch pain specialists. Practices may differ in other countries due to variations in healthcare systems, education, and resource availability. This may limit the external validity of our findings.

This study emphasizes the need for further research to clarify the role of prognostic selective nerve root blocks in the interventional management of chronic LSRP. Future research should focus on validating the prognostic value of SNRBs, investigating their cost‐effectiveness as part of the prognostic pathway for PRF, and standardizing techniques to ensure meaningful comparison across centers. Additionally, studies should explore patient‐centered outcomes, such as satisfaction and quality of life, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of treatment efficacy.

In summary, this study highlights the limited use of prognostic selective nerve root blocks among Dutch pain specialists, raising important questions about the alignment of clinical practice with guideline recommendations. Standardization of SNRB techniques and further research into their prognostic value are necessary to optimize the care of patients with chronic LSRP. Clinicians should consider more consistent use of prognostic selective nerve root blocks in line with guideline recommendations, and future studies should focus on validating the benefits of this approach.
