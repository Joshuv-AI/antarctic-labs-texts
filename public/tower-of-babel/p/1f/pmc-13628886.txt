Deep learning-based treatment decision support framework for multi-vessel coronary artery disease using integrated coronary angiography and clinical data
Authors: Byeolhee Kim, Junhee Kim, Young-Hak Kim, Tae Joon Jun, Jung-Min Ahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTreatment selection between percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) for multi-vessel coronary artery disease remains challenging, requiring careful consideration of both anatomical and clinical factors.MethodsWe developed a deep learning framework that automatically analyzes coronary angiography videos and integrates clinical data to support revascularization decisions. The framework consists of three key modules: (1) a video filtering module for quality screening, (2) a representative frame selection module based on curriculum learning, and (3) a treatment classification module combining imaging features with clinical characteristics. The framework was evaluated using 5,647 patients’ data from a single center, with cross-validation.ResultsOur framework demonstrated superior performance with a mean AUC of 0.8275 ± 0.0167 in 5-fold cross-validation, significantly outperforming traditional machine learning approaches (baseline AUC: 0.66 ± 0.007, p < 0.001). Ablation studies showed sequential improvements: representative frame selection improved performance over baseline by 3.69% (AUC: 0.6657 to 0.7026), video quality filtering provided additional 0.56% improvement (AUC: 0.7026 to 0.7082), and clinical information integration achieved final enhancement of 1.35% (AUC: 0.7082 to 0.7217). For frame selection specifically, curriculum learning outperformed supervised learning by 6.3% (AUC: 0.9067 to 0.9637).ConclusionsThis study provides a promising approach for objective, data-driven decision support in complex coronary revascularization cases. The framework’s multi-modal integration strategy and automated analysis capabilities demonstrate potential for improving the consistency and efficiency of treatment selection while maintaining high standards of clinical care.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

The myocardium is supplied with blood through three coronary arteries: the left anterior descending artery, the left circumflex artery, and the right coronary artery. Coronary artery disease (CAD) occurs when one or more of these arteries become narrowed or blocked, presenting a spectrum from stable angina to non-ST segment elevation acute coronary syndrome (NSTE-ACS) and ST segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). Coronary angiography (CAG) remains the gold standard for examining the coronary arteries and determining the extent of the disease.

Treatment options for CAD include medical therapy, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). Although CABG has traditionally shown superiority in the treatment of multivessel disease with lower rates of mortality and major adverse cardiac events (MACE) [1], recent advances in PCI techniques and drug-eluting stents have expanded its role [2], making treatment selection increasingly complex.

Current clinical decision-making relies on several complementary assessments. Patient-level risk scores, such as SYNTAX II and SYNTAX 2020 [3, 4], integrate anatomical and clinical variables to predict mortality and major adverse cardiac events. Lesion-specific techniques, including intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) [5] and optical coherence tomography (OCT) [6], provide micro-architectural and functional insights beyond conventional angiographic assessment. However, clinical implementation requires clinicians to synthesize these heterogeneous data sources, weigh them against patient-specific comorbidities, and reach consensus through multidisciplinary discussion. This process, while comprehensive, remains inherently subjective and resource-intensive, with potential for inter-observer variability and cognitive burden.

Our multimodal deep learning framework addresses these limitations by integrating raw angiographic video data with structured clinical variables, enabling the discovery of complex interactions that traditional risk scores and isolated imaging parameters cannot detect. The model generates probabilistic, reproducible recommendations with real-time efficiency, minimizing observer bias while maintaining the comprehensive perspective of multidisciplinary evaluation. Through unified processing of anatomical and clinical information within a single computational framework, this approach provides objective, scalable decision support to enhance clinical practice.

Related works

Deep learning in coronary artery disease

Deep learning has become an essential tool for imaging-based assessment of coronary artery disease (CAD). A recent systematic review by Tu et al. (2024) showed that deep networks achieved pooled accuracies of 0.79 (95% CI 0.64–0.94) for detecting < 50% stenosis and 0.73 (0.58–0.88) for ≥50% stenosis, confirming their utility in both coronary angiography (CAG) and coronary CT angiography (CCTA) [8].

The earliest deep-learning applications focused on vessel segmentation, a prerequisite for any quantitative analysis. AngioNet added an Angiographic Processing Network to a U-Net backbone and markedly improved segmentation performance across multiple datasets [9]. In parallel, Yang et al. demonstrated real-time segmentation of major coronary vessels in X-ray angiography, reporting an average F1 score of 0.917 with minimal image preprocessing [10]. These studies established reliable lumen masks and centrelines, providing the anatomical groundwork for downstream tasks.

Building on these advances, recent research has progressed toward fully automatic quantitative coronary analysis (AI-QCA). For example, Chen et al. combined accurate segmentation with morphometric measurement to grade stenosis severity on 1,322 coronary angiograms, achieving precision, recall, and F1 scores of 0.866, 0.897, and 0.879, respectively [11]. Such AI-QCA pipelines demonstrate that deep learning can deliver rapid and consistent evaluations of lesion severity, paving the way for objective decision support in routine clinical practice.

Key frame selection in medical video analysis

The selection of representative frames from medical videos has been extensively studied in coronary angiography analysis, where optimal visualization timing is critical for accurate diagnosis. Traditional methods have relied on contrast enhancement detection, vessel visibility metrics, and template matching approaches to identify frames with maximum vessel clarity and minimal motion artifacts. However, studies have demonstrated that frame selection significantly impacts quantitative coronary angiography (QCA) results, with inter-observer variability affecting measurements of minimal lumen diameter and lesion length [12].

To address these challenges, several automated approaches have been developed. Movahedi et al. proposed a two-stage algorithm that combines histogram analysis with cardiac phase detection to extract diagnostically suitable frames from angiogram sequences [13]. Their method first eliminates background and illumination variations through fuzzy c-means clustering, then determines cardiac phases to identify optimal frames. Zhou et al. introduced CDKD-w+, a keyframe recognition method based on w+ space encoding that specifically addresses the problem of coronary artery deformation due to cardiac motion in DSA sequences [14].

More recently, deep learning approaches have emerged to improve frame selection accuracy. Zhang et al. developed an approach using long short-term spatiotemporal attention mechanisms combined with image-to-patch contrastive learning for locating key frames in X-ray coronary angiograms [15]. Their method leverages temporal dependencies and spatial attention to identify frames with optimal vessel contrast and clarity, demonstrating the potential of machine learning techniques to reduce operator dependency and improve reproducibility in coronary angiography analysis.

Multimodal approaches in cardiovascular medicine

Multimodal integration has emerged as a promising approach in cardiovascular medicine, combining imaging data with clinical information to enhance diagnostic and treatment decision-making. Several studies have demonstrated the value of integrating different data modalities for improved cardiovascular assessment.

Recent work has shown that combining imaging features with clinical parameters can significantly improve diagnostic accuracy in cardiovascular applications. For example, studies integrating echocardiographic features with patient clinical histories have demonstrated enhanced classification of cardiovascular conditions [16]. Similarly, approaches that combine imaging data with structured clinical reports have shown improved diagnostic precision through cross-modal feature learning [17].

In the context of coronary intervention decision-making, multimodal approaches face unique challenges due to the dynamic nature of angiographic imaging and the complexity of clinical decision factors. Our work addresses these challenges by developing a framework that systematically processes coronary angiography videos through automated quality assessment and optimal frame selection, while effectively integrating the extracted imaging features with comprehensive clinical data to support PCI versus CABG treatment decisions.

Methods

The representative frame classification dataset was built from 23,611 videos, where each video contained a single frame index annotated as the representative frame. Considering the continuous nature of angiographic sequences, we extended the representative frame labels to neighboring frames. Our rationale was that frames adjacent to the labeled representative frame would show similar vessel structures and contrast enhancement patterns. Therefore, we marked ±10 frames around the annotated frame as representative samples. For negative samples, we selected frames from the video start and end sections, as these portions typically show no contrast enhancement and thus contain no visible vessel structures. This approach provided a clear distinction between frames with and without diagnostic value. Videos in which the representative frame window overlapped with the negative sample window were excluded to maintain dataset integrity. As shown in Table 2, we additionally included 29,506 unlabeled videos for semi-supervised learning.

For the Treatment Classification Dataset, we selected CAG videos taken within 3 months before the treatment date, excluding follow-up CAGs and post-procedure videos. As detailed in Table 2, this dataset comprised 4,518 patients (42,945 videos) for training, 565 patients for validation, and 564 patients for testing. For the final framework evaluation, we reorganized the entire dataset into 5-fold cross-validation sets to ensure a more robust assessment of generalization performance.

Data preprocessing

All CAG videos were processed through standardized preprocessing steps, including frame extraction and image quality screening. The clinical data was standardized with appropriate handling of missing values. Throughout the dataset construction, we maintained patient-level separation across all subsets to ensure unbiased evaluation of the framework’s performance.

Video processing pipeline

The quality of coronary angiography videos is critical for accurate treatment prediction, necessitating a robust filtering process to ensure that only diagnostically relevant videos are analyzed. As illustrated in Fig. 1, our approach involves two main components implemented in modules A and B: video quality filtering and optimal frame selection.

Video quality filtering through LCA/RCA classification

Our video filtering module (Fig. 1A) employs a binary classification approach to assess video quality and exclude unsuitable angiographic videos. The module utilizes a deep learning model based on the ResNet50 [18] architecture, which was pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset and subsequently fine-tuned to classify each frame as Left Coronary Artery (LCA) or Right Coronary Artery (RCA). As shown in the diagram, the network architecture consists of the ResNet50 backbone producing 2048-dimensional features followed by an LCA/RCA classifier head comprising two fully connected layers: FC(2048→512→2) with ReLU activation and a final softmax layer for probability output.

Representative frame selection

Selecting the most informative frames from CAG videos is essential for accurate treatment prediction. As depicted in Fig. 1B, our strategy uses a semi-supervised learning approach with curriculum progression to maximize the use of the available data (Fig. 3). The module architecture consists of a ResNet50 backbone pre-trained on ImageNet (extracting 2048-dimensional features), followed by a representative frame classifier head with two fully connected layers: FC(2048→512→2) with ReLU activation and softmax output. The adjacent sampling method shown in the diagram selects three consecutive frames (representative frame and its immediate neighbors) for subsequent processing. For training data preparation, we extended the representative frame labels to neighboring frames, marking ±10 frames around the annotated frame as representative samples. Given that our angiographic videos have an average of 60.01 frames per sequence, this ±10 frame window encompasses approximately 35% of the average sequence length, providing sufficient temporal context around the optimal contrast enhancement moment while maintaining selectivity for truly representative frames. The adjacent sampling method illustrated in Fig. 1B ensures that frames adjacent to the labeled representative frame capture similar vessel structures and contrast enhancement patterns.

The semi-supervised learning process follows the pseudo-labeling methodology proposed by Lee [19] and theoretically validated by Min and Tai [20]. The process consists of three sequential phases. In the first phase, the model is trained on labeled data using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-4, employing cross-entropy loss for binary classification. The second phase involves pseudo-labeling, where the trained model generates predictions for unlabeled data. These predictions are filtered based on confidence thresholds to maintain reliability. The final phase implements curriculum learning [21], where samples are ranked by their prediction confidence scores and gradually incorporated into the training process. Starting with the most confident pseudo-labeled samples (top 20%), the model progressively includes more challenging cases over time. This structured approach aims to establish a strong foundation with reliable samples before tackling more difficult cases, potentially improving the model’s generalization capability and robustness. Model updates occur at regular intervals throughout this progressive learning process.

Treatment classification

The final component of our framework (Fig. 1C) is designed to predict the optimal revascularization method (PCI or CABG) by integrating both imaging features from processed CAG videos and clinical characteristics. As illustrated in the diagram, the architecture consists of three main components: feature extraction from CAG frames using ResNet50 backbone, clinical data integration through feature fusion, and an ensemble decision mechanism via majority voting.

For CAG feature extraction, we use a modified ResNet50 architecture where the final classification layer is replaced with a feature extraction layer. For each video, we select three consecutive frames following the adjacent sampling method established in Module B: the representative frame identified by our frame selection module based on the highest probability score, and its adjacent preceding and following frames. These three frames are concatenated with standardized clinical characteristics before being processed through the treatment classifier head. While traditional video analysis might suggest using sequential models like LSTM or 3D-CNN for temporal information, we propose an approach of combining these three temporally adjacent frames into a three-channel input as shown in Fig. 1C.

This design choice is based on two key insights about coronary angiography characteristics. First, the frame identified as the most representative (highest probability) typically captures the optimal moment of vessel visualization, where contrast enhancement and motion stability align to provide the clearest view of coronary anatomy. Second, adjacent frames provide crucial temporal context about vessel filling patterns and subtle anatomical variations, while maintaining high visual coherence with the central frame. The temporal proximity ensures that vessel positions remain relatively stable across the three frames, allowing CNN to effectively learn subtle variations in contrast flow and vessel characteristics.

By processing these temporally coherent frames as channels rather than sequential inputs, we leverage the CNN’s ability to learn spatial correlations across channels while maintaining computational efficiency. The frames are processed through ResNet50’s convolutional layers, producing a 2048-dimensional feature vector through the global average pooling layer that encapsulates both optimal vessel visualization and local temporal dynamics.

As depicted in Fig. 1C, we incorporate clinical characteristics including demographic data (age, sex, BMI), cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes), and relevant medical history (previous revascularization procedures). These features undergo standardization and are concatenated with the 2048-dimensional imaging features through a feature fusion layer shown in the diagram. The combined feature vector then passes through the treatment classifier head consisting of three fully connected layers: FC(2048+clinical features→1024→512→2) with ReLU activation and batch normalization. Dropout layers (rate = 0.5) are implemented between fully connected layers to prevent overfitting.

The final prediction for each patient employs the comprehensive multiview analysis strategy illustrated through the majority voting mechanism in Fig. 1C. First, each CAG video is processed independently through the CNN backbone, generating a 2048-dimensional feature vector. These features are then combined with standardized clinical parameters through a feature fusion layer, followed by three fully connected layers with batch normalization and dropout (rate = 0.5). The model generates independent prediction probabilities for each video view, and the final decision is determined by majority voting on all available views. This approach not only mimics clinical decision-making where cardiologists examine multiple angiographic views but also provides inherent confidence measures through voting proportions. Additionally, the architecture incorporates residual connections between feature fusion and subsequent layers to maintain both low-level clinical features and high-level imaging features throughout the decision process.

Results

For model evaluation, we focus primarily on the Area Under the Curve (AUC) score as our key metric, supplemented by accuracy and F1 score. AUC was chosen as the primary metric due to its robustness to class imbalance and its ability to evaluate model performance across different decision thresholds, which is crucial in medical applications where the cost of false predictions varies significantly. In our context, AUC provides a more comprehensive assessment of the model’s ability to distinguish between cases requiring PCI versus CABG, regardless of the chosen probability threshold.

The model training used the Adam Optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1e-4 and a batch size of 32. Early stopping was implemented with a patience of 15 epochs to prevent overfitting.

The Full-sequence sampling method, while providing a broader view of the contrast dynamics, likely suffers from greater vessel position variability between frames. The Representative set sampling showed notably lower performance (AUC: 0.6376), suggesting that maintaining spatial continuity is more crucial than sampling multiple high-quality frames across the sequence.

This finding aligns with the fundamental characteristics of coronary angiography imaging: the quality of vessel visualization is heavily dependent on both the distribution of contrast agents and cardiac motion. The adjacent three-frame approach provides consistent vessel representation by capturing frames where the contrast enhancement is stable and cardiac motion-induced displacement is minimal. In configurations with wider frame intervals (5 or 7 channels), cardiac motion causes significant spatial displacement of the coronary arteries between frames, making it difficult to maintain consistent vessel alignment across the input channels. This spatial inconsistency is reflected in the performance degradation, with AUC dropping to 0.6867 and 0.6254 respectively for 5 and 7 channels, demonstrating that maintaining vessel position consistency across input channels is crucial for effective feature extraction.

Treatment classification performance

We evaluated the effectiveness of our framework through comprehensive experiments and cross-validation analysis. The baseline model using all frames achieved an AUC of 0.6657. Representative frame selection significantly improved performance (AUC: 0.7026, +3.69%, p < 0.01), demonstrating the advantage of focusing on optimal frames on entire sequences. LCA/RCA filtering provided an additional improvement (AUC: 0.7082, +0.56%, p < 0.05), confirming the value of automated quality assessment. The integration of clinical information yielded further enhancement (AUC: 0.7217, +1.35%, p < 0.01), highlighting the complementary nature of imaging and clinical data. These sequential improvements validate our multistage preprocessing approach, with representative frame selection showing the most substantial impact on model performance.

The addition of LCA/RCA filtering further enhanced performance to an AUC of 0.7082. Although this improvement appears modest in absolute terms, it shows that removing poor quality videos through automated quality assessment can contribute to more reliable predictions.

Finally, the integration of clinical information with the extracted imaging features yielded our best performance with an AUC of 0.7217. This final improvement serves to demonstrate the complementary and mutually reinforcing nature of imaging and clinical data. While our deep learning approach is capable of extracting visual information from angiographic images, the addition of clinical parameters provides crucial contextual information that serves to enhance the model’s capacity for decision-making.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates several key contributions to AI-assisted coronary intervention decision support, while also revealing important limitations and future directions for improvement. The framework achieved the performance (AUC: 0.8275 ± 0.0167) with statistically significant improvements (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{p} < 0.001$$\end{document}). This performance gain stems from our multimodal integration strategy that combines automated angiographic analysis with clinical characteristics, providing objective and reproducible treatment recommendations that could enhance clinical decision-making consistency.

A fundamental aspect of our approach is the analysis of complete angiographic images without lesion segmentation. This design choice preserves global anatomical context, enabling the model to capture spatial relationships between diseased segments and overall disease patterns that might be fragmented in traditional lesion-by-lesion analysis. The approach also enables unbiased feature discovery throughout the coronary tree, potentially identifying subtle vessel wall changes and perfusion patterns not captured by conventional metrics. Additionally, by eliminating multi-step processing pipelines, we achieve computational efficiency that facilitates real-time deployment.

Our three-channel temporal integration approach provides computational efficiency suitable for clinical deployment while capturing essential temporal context from angiographic sequences. The superior performance of adjacent frame sampling over wider frame intervals validates our focus on spatial coherence, which is crucial given the cardiac motion and contrast dynamics inherent in coronary angiography.

Several important technical limitations must be acknowledged. Our video filtering approach relies solely on LCA/RCA classification confidence rather than established clinical criteria for optimal angiographic visualization. Standard clinical assessment typically considers optimal angiographic angles, contrast concentration timing, and quantitative vessel visibility metrics. Our simplified approach may inadvertently include videos that, while enabling clear vessel identification, do not meet comprehensive clinical diagnostic standards.

In the subset of 823 patients for whom a SYNTAX score had been calculated, we correlated our model-predicted CABG probability with the recorded SYNTAX value. The Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.44 (p < 0.001), indicating that the two metrics overlap only partially. This moderate association suggests that our network may be leveraging image and patient-level information beyond the anatomical complexity captured by SYNTAX. To clarify these contributory factors, we plan to perform detailed Grad-CAM feature-attribution studies and patient-risk-factor importance analyses in future work. Our intention is not to replace SYNTAX, but to offer a complementary decision-support tool that enriches Heart-Team discussions when anatomical scoring alone is inconclusive.

The current architecture’s complexity may impact real-time processing capabilities, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Furthermore, while our Grad-CAM visualizations demonstrate that the model attends to clinically relevant regions, the lack of quantitative anatomical outputs represents a significant limitation for procedural planning. This trade-off between comprehensive pattern recognition and specific anatomical detail motivates future directions for hybrid approaches.

From a clinical implementation standpoint, our framework faces several challenges. The current single-center validation may not capture the full variety of clinical scenarios and institutional treatment approaches. Integration with existing clinical workflows requires careful consideration of human-AI interaction patterns, and the model’s recommendations need to be presented in a way that complements rather than replaces clinical judgment.

The black-box nature of deep learning models makes it challenging to understand feature importance and decision boundaries, which is crucial for clinical acceptance and trust. Additionally, reliance on high-quality angiographic videos limits applicability to cases with suboptimal imaging, which are common in emergency or resource-limited settings.

To address these limitations, we propose several specific development directions. Technical improvements should focus on developing a more consolidated framework that reduces the complexity of multiple preprocessing steps while maintaining performance. While our current three-channel temporal integration approach provides computational efficiency suitable for clinical deployment, it represents a relatively simple form of temporal modeling. Future research directions should explore more sophisticated contextual information modeling, including attention-based temporal mechanisms, recurrent neural networks, or 3D convolutional approaches that could more explicitly capture the dynamic patterns of contrast flow and cardiac motion in coronary angiography sequences. However, such approaches would need to be carefully balanced against computational requirements for real-time clinical applications, and their incremental benefits over our current efficient approach would need empirical validation.

For clinical integration, future work should prioritize developing an interpretable visualization system that highlights key vessel segments and features influencing the model’s decisions. This should be complemented by real-time confidence metrics that align with clinical decision-making processes. Additionally, incorporating established clinical criteria for video quality assessment, including optimal angiographic angles and contrast enhancement standards, would improve the framework’s clinical validity and reproducibility.

Validation efforts should focus on multicenter studies with diverse patient populations and imaging practices. Additionally, implementing privacy-preserving federated learning approaches would enable secure model updates across institutions while maintaining patient data protection. These developments aim to create a more robust and clinically applicable system while addressing both technical and practical challenges in real-world implementation. The ultimate goal is to provide a reliable, interpretable and efficient decision support tool that seamlessly integrates into clinical practice while maintaining high standards of patient care.

Conclusions

This study provides a data-driven decision support in complex coronary revascularization cases. While current limitations exist, particularly in video quality assessment and clinical validation scope, the framework’s multimodal integration strategy and automated analysis capabilities demonstrate significant potential for improving treatment selection consistency and efficiency in clinical practice.
