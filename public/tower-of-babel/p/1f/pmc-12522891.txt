Detection of tissue colonizing bacteria and their association with clinicopathological and molecular features of inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Xuanke Cao, Yuefang Sun, Hu Liu, Dexi Bi, Li Zhao, Tingting Ding, Lulu Chen, Wujianhong Liu, Shengrong Dong, Qing Wei, Yaohui Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccumulating evidence indicates that the occurrence, development, and treatment of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) are closely related to the intestinal microecology. However, whether Crohn’s disease (CD) and Ulcerative colitis (UC) are characterized by distinct gut microbial signatures and associated disease phenotypes remains to be determined. The present study investigates the correlation between potential pathogenic or protective intestinal bacteria and the clinical pathological characteristics of patients with IBD.MethodsA total of 274 tissue samples from patients with IBD, including 237 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples and 37 fresh samples, were detected by qPCR for Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Listeria monocytogenes, Akkermansia muciniphila, and Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis. The study identified significant correlations between specific bacteria and clinical features such as age, disease location, disease behavior, and immunohistochemical markers.ResultsAmong patients with CD, Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization was associated with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection, Ki-67 expression, disease behavior, and prognosis. Klebsiella pneumoniae colonization showed a significant association with EBV infection and lesion location. Listeria monocytogenes colonization was correlated with IgG4 levels and disease location. Moreover, as a potential probiotic, the colonization rate of Akkermansia muciniphila is significantly higher during the remission phase than in the active phase of CD. Notably, among patients with UC, these bacteria did not correlate well with clinical pathological characteristics. In addition, compared with healthy people, these bacteria in the feces of patients with CD and UC also exhibit different characteristics.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that, compared with UC, certain specific gut bacteria, such as Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, may play a more important role in the occurrence, development and prognosis of CD. In addition, different intestinal microbiota-based therapeutic strategies should be developed for CD and UC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07095-w.Keywords: Inflammatory bowel disease, Crohn’s disease, Ulcerative colitis, Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Listeria monocytogenes, Akkermansia muciniphila, Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis

Introduction

IBD is a group of chronic, non-specific inflammatory diseases of the gut [1]. In clinical practice, the main diseases among IBD are CD and UC [2]. Currently, the incidence of IBD varies widely across regions and populations [3]. Based on A systematic analysis based on the Global Burden of Disease Study, there were 4.9 million cumulative cases of IBD worldwide in 2019, with China accounting for the largest number of patients, at 91,405 cases. Due to the lack of clarity on the pathogenesis of IBD, the current treatment for IBD is mainly based on controlling inflammation and clinical symptoms, and is not adequately treated [4].

In a genome-wide association study, it was found that there are several susceptibility sites triggered by environmental factors in IBD-susceptible populations, and abnormalities in these loci may lead to congenital intestinal barrier disease [5], and those with susceptibility loci may develop an abnormal immune response stimulated by gut microbes. This, in turn, leads to the formation of IBD. Normally, the intestinal immune system and the microbial communities that inhabit the gut are in a state of dynamic equilibrium, with beneficial bacteria providing the body with the nutrients it needs, helping to digest food, and fighting colonization by foreign pathogens [6]. Unlike beneficial bacteria, the abnormal proliferation of opportunistic pathogens leads to an immune response and a compromised gut microbial barrier. From this, it can be concluded that the balance of the structure of the gut microbiota contributes to the maintenance of the relevant functions of the intestinal immune system [7].

Numerous studies have shown that the gut microbiota plays a prior role in the pathogenesis of IBD [8]. In general, an aberrant immune response between a genetically susceptible host and a commensal bacterium that disrupts the homeostasis of the host and microbes is thought to be one of the causes of IBD. Microbial diversity at sites of inflammation in IBD is reduced, making the host more susceptible to pathogen invasion and colonization [9]. Mucous membranes in IBD that are inflamed differ from healthy mucous membranes in terms of microbiota composition [10]. It is inferred from this that dysbiosis of the gut microbiome promotes further changes in the gut microbiota of individuals with IBD [11, 12]. Professor Claudia Veltkamp demonstrated that a sterile environment can prevent colitis in genetically susceptible mice [13]. Prof. Okayasu has experimentally demonstrated that the transfer of pro-inflammatory bacteria or microbiota from diseased mice to healthy mice can induce inflammation [14]. Prof. Britton proposed that colonization of gut microbiota mice from IBD donors exacerbates colitis by altering the immune response [15]. In addition, in a multibiome analysis research project that synthesized large-scale external datasets from multiple countries, UC and CD were found to have different bacterial signatures [16]. Some investigators have found that the microbiota of patients with CD is characterized by a decrease in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria [17]. At present, SCFA has been shown to have a protective effect against immune diseases such as IBD [18].

In current research, microorganisms that are highly involved in the development of IBD include but are not limited to Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Listeria monocytogenes, Akkermansia muciniphila, Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis, etc. Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis has been implicated in the etiology of human inflammatory diarrhea and associated symptoms [19]. In a study of germ-free mice, vaccination with Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis alone may cause colitis and also exacerbate colitis-related symptoms in mice treated with Dextran Sulfate Sodium (DSS) [20]. Klebsiella pneumoniae expresses broad-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) and causes more severe disease severity and symptoms in patients with IBD [21]. Listeria monocytogenes induces granulomatous responses and is considered a potential cause of CD [22, 23]. In animal experiments, Akkermansia muciniphila-derived extracellular vesicles can effectively prevent the development of colitis in mice induced by DSS [24]. Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis is thought to be associated with Johne’s disease and has the potential to infect humans and become the causative agent of CD [25].

A pretty number of studies have focused on the effects of gut microbiota on overall IBD, but there are relatively few bacterial studies on the specific effects of CD and UC and the clinical characteristics of IBD patients. The purpose of this study was to identify the intestinal bacterial species present in the lesions of CD and UC, namely Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Listeria monocytogenes, Akkermansia muciniphila, and Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis, and to analyze their correlation with the clinicopathologic characteristics of patients, and to explore their potential role in the pathogenesis of CD and UC.

Materials and methods

Human specimen collection

In this study, both patients with IBD were from the Tenth People’s Hospital of Tongji University. The pathologic examinations and clinical characteristics of these patients meet the definition of CD or UC. 37 fresh tissue samples, including 24 from patients with CD and 13 from patients with UC, were obtained from surgical operations or gastrointestinal endoscopy, immediately stored in a − 80 °C freezer for subsequent use in this study. 201 samples were processed by FFPE, including 99 from patients with CD and 102 from patients with UC, and were used in this study for testing. 36 FFPE tissues from IBD patients, including 24 from patients with CD and 12 from patients with UC, were divided into 18 pairs according to remission and active disease stages, and were used for detection in this study. The study has been approved by the Institutional Review Committee of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital.

DNA extraction and quantitative real-time RT-PCR

DNA was extracted from FFPE samples using the DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (AmoyDx Inc., CHINA), and the whole process was carried out in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA is stored in a − 80 °C freezer. Quantitative real-time RT-PCR is amplified by ABI 7500 detection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in combination with Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Takara Biomedical Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd., CHINA) using bacteria-specific primers. The 10 µl of Q-PCR mixture consisted of 4 µl of DNA and ddH2O, 5 µl of TB Green Premix Ex Taq, and 0.2 µl ROX Reference Dye Ⅱ, 0.4 µl of each primer (Total 10 µM). The PCR program was composed of an initial denaturation step for 30 s at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation for 5 s at 95 °C, annealing and extension for 34 s at 60 °C, and fluorescent signal acquisition. The presence of the bacteria in FFPE samples was determined based on cycle threshold (Ct) values, where each experiment was repeated at least three times, again in strict compliance with the manufacturer’s general protocol. The primer sequences used in this study are presented in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Tables S4).

GMrepo database analysis

GMrepo is a database dedicated to human gut microbiome data, and we extracted the relative abundance of Bacteroides fragilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Akkermansia muciniphila in the feces of healthy individuals and patients with IBD, UC, and CD from the GMrepo database (https://gmrepo.humangut.info). By consulting the data from relevant publications, the data’s quality was evaluated.

Statistical analysis

All statistics were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 software or the statistical software package of IBM SPSS Institute 27. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate whether the bacteria in the lesions had a significant impact on the incidence of IBD. Pearson’s chi-square test was used to evaluate the correlation between bacteria and disease characteristics in lesions, and Bonferroni correction was used for comparison between multiple groups. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to evaluate the correlation between bacterial presence and Ki-67. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Different characteristics of tissue-colonizing bacteria and gut microbiota and in patients with CD and UC

A total of 201 patients with clinically confirmed IBD were tested for an abundance of potentially relevant bacteria, including Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Listeria monocytogenes, Akkermansia muciniphila, Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis. We investigated the correlation between the clinical pathological characteristics of the patients with IBD and the above bacterial abundance in tissues (Supplementary Tables S1, S2, and S3). Among the tissues of these patients, Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis had the highest positivity rate. The positivity rate of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis and Listeria monocytogenes were higher in CD than in UC, whereas Klebsiella pneumoniae, Akkermansia muciniphila and Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis showed lower in CD than in UC (Fig. 1A). In addition, compared with the tissues of patients with UC, the bacterial abundances of Klebsiella pneumoniae and Listeria monocytogenes in the tissues of patients with CD were higher (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, we collected fresh tissue samples from IBD during surgical procedures or gastrointestinal endoscopy and detected the relevant bacteria. Despite the variability in positivity rates, all aforementioned bacteria were successfully identified in fresh tissues. The positivity rates of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Akkermansia muciniphila were even higher than those in paraffin tissues (Fig. 1C). Moreover, the bacterial abundance in fresh tissues was generally higher than that in paraffin tissues (Fig. 1D). These results further confirmed the presence of specific bacteria in the tissues of the patient’s lesion sites. Moreover, these results indicated that there is differences in the positive rates and abundances of the relevant bacteria between patients with CD and UC.

Studies have shown that different disease phases of IBD may induce differences in bacterial colonization [26]. Therefore, we further investigated whether disease phases could trigger changes in the positivity rates of relevant bacteria. We found that, compared to the active phase of CD, the positivity rate of Akkermansia muciniphila in remission-phase CD was significantly higher (P = 0.027) (Fig. 1E, F). Moreover, immunotherapy was associated with alterations in the positivity rates of target bacteria [27]. Our results demonstrated no significant correlation between immunosuppressant use and the positivity rates of tissue-colonizing bacteria relevant to patients with IBD (Fig. 1G).

To further confirm that the differences in these bacteria are caused by the dysbiosis of the microbiota in patients with IBD, we used the GMrepo database to further analyze the differences in the abundance of these bacteria in the feces of healthy individuals and patients with IBD. We further determined the relative abundance of Bacteroides fragilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Akkermansia muciniphila, which can be retrieved in the GMrepo database, in healthy individuals, patients with UC and patients with CD (Fig. 1H–J). The abundance of Bacteroides fragilis in the feces of patients with IBD is higher than that in healthy individuals, and the abundance in patients with CD is higher than that in patients with UC (Fig. 1H). The abundance of Klebsiella pneumoniae in the feces of patients with CD is higher than that in the feces of healthy individuals and patients with UC (Fig. 1I). The abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila in the feces of patients with UC is lower than that in healthy individuals, and there is no significant difference between patients with CD and those with UC (Fig. 1J). These data indicate that there may be different characteristics of the gut microbiota in patients with CD and UC.

The colonization of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis is associated with EBER, Ki-67, disease behavior, and prognosis in patients with CD, and is related to the age of patients with UC

We counted the correlation between Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis abundance results and clinicopathological information in patients with CD and UC (Fig. 2A, I). EBER is a hallmark molecule of EBV viral latent infection. We found that patients with EBER-positive patients with CD had a higher rate of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization compared with EBER-negative patients (P = 0.015) (Fig. 2B). We further analyzed the relationship between immunosuppressive agent use and EBV infection. Results showed no significant correlation between immunosuppressive agent use and EBV infection (Fig. 2C). These results indicated that EBV infection status is correlated with Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization in lesions in patients with CD, and this relationship may not be affected by immunosuppressive agents.

Among the statistics of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis abundance results and other pathological characteristics of CD, compared with patients with perianal disease (P), the rate of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization was significantly higher in patients with stricturing CD (B2) (P = 0.012) (Fig. 2F), suggesting that Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization is also associated with the disease behavior of patients with CD. Further analysis revealed that, compared to patients not colonized by Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, the proportion of Ki-67 positive cells was significantly higher in patients with CD colonized by Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (P = 0.004) (Fig. 2G), suggesting that Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization affects the proliferative state of cells. In addition, the recurrence time of patients with CD with positive Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis is significantly shorter than that of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis-negative patients, with the median recurrence time being 243 days and 428 days, respectively (Fig. 2H).

For patients with UC, there was a significant association between Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization and age (P = 0.041) (Fig. 2J), particularly with a notably higher rate of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization observed in individuals aged 40 years and older. In addition, no significant associations were found between Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis and other variables (Fig. 2D, E, K–M and Supplementary Fig. S1).

Klebsiella pneumoniae colonization is associated with EBER, the disease location, and extent in patients with CD

We counted the correlation between Klebsiella pneumoniae abundance results and clinicopathological information in patients with CD and UC (Fig. 3A, F). For patients with CD, there was a correlation between EBV infection status and Klebsiella pneumoniae colonization (P = 0.047) (Fig. 3B). At the same time, the rate of Klebsiella pneumoniae colonization was significantly higher when the lesion was located at L2 than at L3 (P = 0.006) (Fig. 3C), suggesting that there is also a significant correlation between the location of the lesion and Klebsiella pneumoniae colonization. Moreover, Klebsiella pneumoniae had no significant correlation with Ki-67 expression and the recurrence status of patients with CD (Fig. 3D, E). For patients with UC, there was no statistically significant correlation between Klebsiella pneumoniae colonization and other variables (Fig. 3G–J and Supplementary Fig. S2).

Listeria monocytogenes shows a correlation with IgG4 levels and the location of disease location in patients with CD

We counted the correlation between the abundance of Listeria monocytogenes and the clinicopathological information of patients with CD and UC (Fig. 4A, E). We found that the prevalence of IgG4 positivity was significantly lower in CD patients with Listeria monocytogenes colonization than in patients with CD without Listeria monocytogenes colonization (P = 0.041) (Fig. 4B), suggesting a potential link between Listeria monocytogenes colonization and regulatory immune response in patients with CD. In addition, compared to L3, the colonization rate of Listeria monocytogenes was significantly higher when the lesion was located at L2 in patients with CD (P = 0.003) (Fig. 4C), suggesting that there is a significant correlation between Listeria monocytogenes colonization and the location of the disease. In addition, in our statistical analysis of CD and UC, no significant association was found between Listeria monocytogenes and other variables (Fig. 4D, E, G and Supplementary Fig. S3).

Discussion

In this cohort study of 274 patients with IBD, we confirmed the presence of specific bacterial colonization at IBD lesion sites. Furthermore, distinct microbial profiles were observed between patients with CD and UC. The study further revealed that colonization by these specific bacteria was significantly associated with clinicopathological features in patients with CD, suggesting that specific bacterial colonization may play a critical role in the pathogenesis and progression of CD, while its impact on UC appears to be weaker. These findings support the hypothesis that gut microbiota imbalance contributes to the development and progression of IBD. This work provides a theoretical foundation for establishing a bacterial subtyping model in IBD patients and offers evidence for exploring microbiota-targeted therapeutic strategies [28, 29].

In addition, based on the data we retrieved from the GMrepo database, we found that the abundance of Bacteroides fragilis in the feces of patients with CD and UC is significantly higher than that in healthy individuals, and it is higher in patients with CD than in patients with UC. This conclusion is similar to our experimental results (Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis is an important branch of Bacteroides fragilis), indicating that Bacteroides fragilis may be associated with the pathogenesis of CD and UC, with a more pronounced correlation, particularly in the pathogenesis of CD.

Interestingly, in the paired active and remission phases of patients with CD, there was a significant difference in the colonization status of Akkermansia muciniphila. This finding suggests that the colonization of Akkermansia muciniphila may play a specific role in the remission process of CD. This is consistent with the conclusion that Akkermansia muciniphila is an intestinal probiotic in the previous study [30, 31], which leads to weakening of the protective role in the intestine and contributes to disease progression. Additionally, the abundance of Klebsiella pneumoniae is high only in patients with CD, while the abundance of Listeria monocytogenes is low only in patients with UC. These results suggest that these specific bacteria can serve as potential biomarkers for the auxiliary diagnosis of patients with CD and UC.

In our study, the EBV positivity rate was 18.6% in patients with CD. Similar to our findings, a study by Professor Van Kruiningen using PCR diagnostic tests showed that15.7% of patients with CD tested positive for EBV [32]. In this study, we reported for the first time that patients with EBV-positive CD had significantly higher rates of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization than patients with EBV-negative CD. Moreover, this correlation may not be affected by immunosuppressive therapy. This finding suggests a potential interaction between EBV and Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization. EBV infection may lead to damage to the intestinal mucosal barrier, promoting Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis invasion into intestinal epithelial cells and triggering an inflammatory response [33]. If the correlation between EBV infection and Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization in a population of CD patients is validated in a larger population, EBV may become a biomarker to predict the risk of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization and aid in early identification and intervention in high-risk patients.

In addition, there is a correlation between EBER results and Klebsiella pneumoniae colonization. Expression of Ki-67 as a marker of cell proliferation in patients with IBD may indicate increased disease activity [34]. We found a significant correlation between Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis and Ki-67 in CD patients, with a significantly higher proportion of Ki-67 positive cells in Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis-positive patients than in Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis-negative patients, suggesting that the presence of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis is associated with intestinal epithelial cell proliferation. IgG4 is an antibody primarily associated with regulatory immune responses, and in our data, Listeria monocytogenes-positive CD patients had a lower IgG4 positivity rate than Listeria monocytogenes-negative patients, suggesting that IgG4 positivity is associated with Listeria monocytogenes colonization.

Our results also found that patients with CD had a higher rate of colonization in Klebsiella pneumoniae and Listeria monocytogenes in patients with L2 lesions than those with L3 lesions. Patients with stricturing CD (B2) also have a higher rate of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization than those with perianal lesions (P). These results suggest that there may be differences in colonization rates of various bacteria among different Montreal classification subtypes in patients with CD.

In addition, among patients with UC, we found a higher proportion of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis positivity in patients aged 40 years and above, possibly implying that age is a factor influencing Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis colonization, particularly in people over 40 years of age. In addition, we did not find any correlation between these specific bacteria and the pathological features of UC, suggesting that these specific bacteria may not play a major role in the onset and progression of UC.

This study also has some limitations. First, our study is a single-center study with a limited number of cases, which may be biased in the statistical process. Second, as a retrospective study, there will be some missing data, and it is difficult to discover new biomarkers through multi-omics, which can only be used as a validation set. Finally, our study found the correlation between these bacteria and clinical features and molecules. In vivo and in vitro experiments are needed to validate our findings and explore potential mechanisms, such as the interaction mechanism between bacteria and intestinal epithelial cells or immune cells. Current studies suggest that Caco-2 and HT29-MTX can be used as cellular models of IBD to explore the interaction between bacteria and intestinal epithelial cells [35, 36]. The mouse model induced by DSS/TNBS and the organoid model of IBD can further simulate the pathogenesis and pathological characteristics of IBD [35, 36].

Conclusion

This study separately detected the abundances of Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Listeria monocytogenes, Akkermansia muciniphila, Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis in patients with CD and UC. By statistically analyzing the correlations between these bacteria and the clinical characteristics of patients with CD and UC, it clarifies the different microbiota characteristics and clinical significance of patients with UC and CD. Among these bacteria, Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis can serve as a biomarker for judging the prognosis of patients with CD. In addition, the correlation between EBV and Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis in patients with CD is confirmed for the first time. Moreover, our study suggests that the gut microbiota may have a greater effect on CD than on UC.
