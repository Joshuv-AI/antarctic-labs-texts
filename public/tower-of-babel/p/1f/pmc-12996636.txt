Machine Learning Models for Identifying Dental Pain in Adolescents
Authors: Luiz Alexandre Chisini, Luana Carla Salvi, Francine dos Santos Costa, Fausto Medeiros Mendes, Luiz Felipe da Silva Pinto, Maximiliano Sérgio Cenci, Tatiana Pereira-Cenci, Flávio Fernando Demarco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to identify dental pain using machine learning (ML) algorithms in Brazilian adolescents for public health screening purposes.MethodsData from 2 cross-sectional waves of the Brazilian National Survey of School Health (PeNSE) in 2015 and 2019 were used (schoolchildren aged 11 to 18). The outcome was dental pain in the last 6 months. Co-variables were 53 variables, including demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioral characteristics. The 2015 dataset was split (80:20) into training and test sets, while the 2019 dataset was used as a temporal external validation set. Nine ML models were evaluated.ResultsA total of 259,833 adolescents (97.0% of the sample) were included. Dental pain prevalence was 19.5% (95% CI, 19.2-19.8). Extra Trees (ET) was the model with the best metrics in the test and external validation sets. ET showed an AUC = 0.64 (95% CI, 0.63-0.65) and a Recall = 0.57 in the test, and AUC = 0.62 (95% CI, 0.62-0.63) and Recall = 0.57 in the external test, indicating a modest ability to discriminate adolescents with dental pain and to identify approximately 57 out of 100 affected individuals. Fairness estimations show lower accuracy for males, but a higher recall for this group. The model shows a higher accuracy for white adolescents but a lower recall for this group. The Shapley values showed that sex, alcohol consumption, and family violence were the most important variables in the algorithm's identification process.ConclusionThis study shows the potential of ML to identify dental pain in adolescents. Modest predictive performance and fairness limitations highlight the need for improvements before widespread adoption.Key words: Oral diseases, Diagnostic models, Dental pain

Introduction

Dental pain is defined as an unpleasant or distressing sensory experience resulting from damage or irritation to dental tissues, often accompanied by a significant emotional component,1 and is assessed by self-report.2 In most cases, dental pain results from the progression of dental caries or dental trauma,3 requiring dental services for pain clinical management.4 Dental pain is an important public health issue, with a worldwide prevalence of 32.7% among children and adolescents,3 negatively impacting the quality of life.5 Indeed, children who have experienced tooth pain have increased prevalences of sleep disorders6 and are 3.7 times more likely to be absent from school,7 often due to the need to seek emergency dental services,4 which results in poor academic performance.5,7

This burden is not evenly distributed across society.1 Dental pain disproportionately affects vulnerable and marginalized social groups,2,8,9 with individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds presenting the highest prevalence.2,8 For example, being born into a socioeconomically disadvantaged family establishes a higher trajectory of pain over the life course.2 This pattern highlights the strong influence of social, ethnic-racial, and behavioral inequalities on the occurrence of dental pain.2,8,9 Inequalities are not only the distal determinants of dental pain but also represent measurable characteristics that may be used to identify individuals with a higher likelihood of experiencing dental pain.2,8,9

Given the high prevalence of dental pain3 and its substantial implications for quality of life,5 socioeconomic2 and behavioral variables5,10 emerge as promising candidates for inclusion in predictive models aimed at screening populations at risk. Although population-based studies have consistently reported associations between these factors and dental pain,2 few have attempted to translate this knowledge into predictive models with an explicit screening purpose.11 Thus, the evidence on predictive modeling of dental pain remains scarce. One early study applied a neural network approach to predict dental pain.11 However, there was limited detail about the model structure, the set of behavioral input variables, and the absence of a description of the population. Moreover, the study did not incorporate broader socioeconomic determinants, nor did it address model applicability for population-level screening.11 Consequently, there remains a clear research gap in population-based studies that integrate socioeconomic and behavioral variables into robust predictive models aimed at identifying individuals at increased risk of dental pain, particularly using contemporary analytical approaches and in adolescent populations.

In this context, the use of machine learning (ML) algorithms may improve model performance, as they are designed to capture nonlinear and complex interactions among a large set of variables, potentially enhancing predictive ability.11 Indeed, recent advances in ML techniques have shown the potential to improve predictive tasks in various healthcare domains.12,13 ML algorithms use data from numerous characteristics to identify variables that detect outcomes, offering versatility and effectiveness in capturing nonlinear interactions within data sets.12

Therefore, assessing the associated factors and developing a ML model using socioeconomic and behavioral variables to identify dental pain can bring numerous advantages since it could be implemented as a screening tool connected or not to electronic medical records. By incorporating socioeconomic factors, the model may identify individuals at increased risk due to disparities, facilitating targeted interventions to address underlying social inequalities and promote equity in dental health.14 Thus, this study aims to identify dental pain using ML algorithms in Brazilian adolescents.

Materials and methods

This is a retrospective diagnostic predictive study.15 The study was reported according to the guidance for reporting models that use regression or machine learning methods TRIPOD+ AI statement.16

Data

Data from 2 cross-sectional waves of the Brazilian National Survey of School Health (PeNSE) were used: 2015, and 2019. PeNSE is a nationally representative, school-based survey of Brazilian adolescents enrolled in the 9th grade of public and private schools, with representativeness at the national level, across major regions, federative units, capital cities, and the Federal District.

PeNSE was promoted by the Brazilian Ministry of Health and the Brazilian Ministry of Education, and data was collected by the Brazilian Institute for Geography and Statistics (IBGE). It involves schoolchildren aged 11 to 18 from Brazil's public and private schools (urban and rural). The sampling strategy used a 2-stage cluster sample method: schools were initially selected, followed by the selection of student classes. The sample size aimed to allow parameter estimation for each of the states' 26 capitals, the Federal District, and the countryside. Data collection was performed employing smartphones with self-administered questionnaires. Complete information regarding the sampling methodology can be found in the publications of PeNSE for the respective years of 2015 and 2019, accessible through other sources (https://www.gov.br/saude/pt-br/composicao/svsa/inqueritos-de-saude/pense).

Participants

The study population comprised elementary school students who were officially enrolled during the academic years of 2015 and 2019, regularly attending both public and private schools in urban and rural regions across Brazil. The schools included were public and private, offering classes for the 9th year of elementary school, as reported in the preceding year's School Census. Schools with fewer than 15 students enrolled in the 9th year were excluded. The choice of the 9th year of primary school was justified by the minimum schooling required to answer the self-administered questionnaire and the proximity of the reference age recommended by the World Health Organisation (WHO), which is between 13 and 15 years old.

The selection of cities and capitals followed a randomized, equiprobable sampling method. One was randomly chosen in schools with two 9th-grade classrooms, while in schools with 3 or more classrooms, 2 were randomly selected. All students selected from these classrooms were invited to participate in the survey. Participants were recruited in the selected classrooms and were invited to voluntarily participate in the data collection by the IBGE interviewers. Individuals with incomplete dental pain data were excluded from the sample.

Sample size

The sample size within each stratum was determined to ensure accurate estimates of proportions (or prevalence) of specific characteristics of interest within the geographic strata. This aimed to achieve an approximate maximum error of 3% in absolute value at the 95% confidence level across all survey waves.

Outcome

The outcome of this study was dental pain, which was assessed by the same question in both survey editions through the following question: ‘Did you have dental pain in the last 6 months? (excluding toothache caused by orthodontic braces)’ with the possible answer: (a) yes or (b) no.

Co-variables

Based on prior literature and subject matter, it was used 53 variables were used from both waves. The full description of the variables included is displayed in Supplemental Material S1. Variables included: Brazilian regions (North, Northeast, Southeast, South, and Midwest), city location (countryside or capital), school type (public or private), adolescents’ sex (female or male), race (White, Black, Brown, Yellow, and American Indigenous), and adolescents’ age. Other variables included maternal schooling, sexuality, general health behaviors, physical activity, and consumption of alcohol, tobacco, and illegal drugs.

Three latent variables were constructed to summarize correlated socioeconomic and behavioral indicators, reducing dimensionality and multicollinearity while preserving theoretical relevance. The estimated latent scores were subsequently treated as continuous input features and combined with observed variables in the ML models. Thus, socioeconomic Status (SES) was a latent variable built through ‘lavaan’ package using nine variables (school type, having a cell phone, a laptop, internet access, a motorcycle, a housekeeper, number of home residents, and number of bathrooms at home). Dietary sweets consumption was also built as a latent variable by 2 variables: (1) Sweets consumption and (2) Soft drink consumption. Hygiene habits was also built as a latent variable by 3 variables: (2) Wash your hands before eating; (2) Wash your hands after using the bathroom, and (3) Soap use for washing hands. The factor loading and fit of latent variables are displayed in Supplemental Table S2.

Data preparation

Categorical variables with more than 2 categories were standardized by transforming them into binary variables using one-hot encoding. Numeric variables were transformed into Z-scores.

Missing data

Maternal education presented 22.4% of missing data, and a variable considering the missing data in this variable was built and included as a co-variable in the model. Additional missing values (less than 5.0%) in training data were imputed using Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations implemented (MICE) by the algorithm random forest.

Statistical methods

Descriptive and regression analyses were performed in Stata 17.0 using the command “svy” for survey data. The dental pain-associated factors were investigated utilizing logistic regression models. It was employed the backward stepwise method for variable selection in regression to ensure robustness. The odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were estimated. Only variables demonstrating a significance level of p<0.20 in the unadjusted analysis were incorporated into the adjusted models. E-values were estimated to provide insights into the robustness of the findings against potential uncontrolled confounding factors.

All subsequent analyses were performed using Python version 3.11.7 programming language. The data set of the 2015 wave was divided (80:20) into 2 independent sets: (1) training, and (2) test set. The full data from the 2019 wave was used to perform a temporal external test. Thus, nine ML algorithms were used, including Gradient Boosting Classifier, Ada Boost Classifier, Extreme Gradient Boosting, Light Gradient Boosting Machine, CatBoost Classifier, Extra Trees Classifier, Random Forest, Linear Discriminant Analysis, and Artificial Neural Network. Machine learning algorithms were selected as the primary modeling strategy given their flexibility in modeling complex and potentially nonlinear relationships among socioeconomic and behavioral variables. No resampling or class balancing techniques were applied. During training, hyperparameter tuning was addressed through 10-fold cross-validation using 50 iterations to optimize the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC). The comparative performance of the models was evaluated through the AUC, which indicates how well the model can differentiate between positive and negative outcomes. It corresponds to the area calculated under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC), which is generated by plotting the true positive rate (sensitivity) against the false positive rate (1 - specificity) across different decision thresholds. Confidence intervals of 95% were generated for AUC with 1,000 bootstraps. The accuracy, recall, precision, and F1 score were also obtained.17 AUC and the performance metrics were calculated separately for each fold in the cross-validation procedure, and for the test and temporal external validation set. DeLong analysed the differences between the curves. Shapley values were also calculated utilizing the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) algorithm for the most effective model, elucidating the contribution of each variable towards the outcome. To examine the algorithms’ fairness, an analysis stratified by sex and race was conducted using the algorithm with the highest AUC. The significance level for all analyses was established at 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study used publicly available, de-identified data from the Brazilian National Survey of School Health (PeNSE), conducted by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE) in partnership with the Ministry of Health. All participants signed the informed consent form.

Results

Of the 267,910 adolescents who participated in the 2015 and 2019 PeNSE studies, 259,833 (97.0%) had complete data on dental pain and were included in the present study. The sample was constituted mainly of females (51.2%) and brown (43.6%) adolescents, aged between 13 and 15 (74.3%), and with maternal education from 5 to 8 years (41.5%). The DeLong test showed that the AUC for the CatBoost model was significantly higher than the Extra Trees Classifier in the test (p = .007) and external test (p < .001). Discussion

Our findings demonstrate the potential of machine learning to identify dental pain in Brazilian adolescents. Although predictive performance was modest and some disparities in fairness were observed, these results provide a valuable starting point for developing more refined models that can integrate additional predictors and address equity concerns, ultimately supporting population-level screening efforts. While the findings reveal modest performance, almost no performance loss was observed when the model was applied to data from a follow-up conducted 4 years later. This indicates that the models retained their effectiveness in the temporal external test data.

This study presents some limitations that must be acknowledged. First, model performance was moderate, indicating that the variables included did not capture all relevant information needed to optimally predict dental pain. Relevant unmeasured factors—such as clinical oral health conditions (eg, dental caries) and access to dental care—may optimise dental pain prediction models. Second, the use of self-reported information may have introduced recall bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of reported behaviors and dental pain. Finally, although missing data were addressed through imputation strategies, the relatively high proportion of missing values for maternal education may have introduced residual bias.

Among the strengths, the study benefits from a large and nationally representative sample, encompassing data from over 250,000 adolescents across 2 significant time points, enhancing the generalizability of the findings to the Brazilian adolescent population. The comprehensive nature of the PeNSE survey allows for an in-depth analysis of various demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioral factors associated with dental pain, providing a nuanced understanding of its determinants. Additionally, using a robust statistical approach using machine learning to missing imputations and evaluating fairness, and the decision interpretation offers a multifaceted approach to identifying dental pain.

Dental pain represents a multifaceted and subjective experience that can significantly differ from person to person, influenced by various biological18,19 and psychological factors.20 Each individual has a distinct pain threshold, and individuals experiencing chronic pain tend to exhibit a lower pain threshold.21 Thus, this inherent pain subjectivity adds complexity to identifying dental pain, which cannot be exclusively attributed to observable clinical signs, such as the presence of dental caries.5,9,22 The difficulty of accurately detecting dental pain with models that lack oral health indicators highlights the limitations in achieving high performance without incorporating oral health data. While models predicting early childhood caries achieved an AUC of 0.75 over a 2-year follow-up using baseline clinical dental and socioeconomic data,23 our model demonstrates that machine learning in our data was only able to yield modest performance based on easily accessible interview data. Indeed, self-reported variables may introduce some degree of inaccuracy, potentially impacting model performance. However, this performance can be enhanced by incorporating variables related to dental caries from dental records, thereby strengthening the model's performance, as observed in a study that noted a significant improvement in model performance following the inclusion of medical history.24

Our model, developed using the Extra Trees Classifier, achieved a modest AUC of 0.640 in the test dataset, which showed a slight decrease when applied to data from another population not seen by the model (AUC = 0.624) during a temporal external validation test. This indicates that while the model can moderately differentiate between individuals with and without dental pain, further improvements are necessary for the enhanced model’s performance. Despite the statistically significant advantage of CatBoost in AUC, the selected model was the Extra Trees due to its superior balance between sensitivity and specificity, as evidenced by its higher recall of 0.57. This makes it particularly applicable in a healthcare setting where identifying true positive cases is critical. Moreover, even with modest AUC values, machine learning can add value over traditional regression approaches by capturing complex and potentially nonlinear interactions among the numerous socioeconomic and behavioral predictors, which would be difficult to model otherwise. This flexibility allows the model to leverage high-dimensional data effectively and provides a foundation for future refinements aimed at improving predictive performance and utility in population-level screening.

Fairness analysis in dentistry is still little explored.13,25,26 Our fairness analysis revealed performance discrepancies across gender and racial groups. The model showed higher recall but lower accuracy for males, suggesting that while it is more sensitive in identifying males with dental pain, it may also be more prone to false positives for this group. On the other hand, the model exhibited higher accuracy but lower recall for white adolescents, indicating a greater risk of missing true cases of dental pain among them. These imbalances in performance metrics highlight potential biases in the data and reflect underlying societal and healthcare inequalities.27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 Indeed, a Brazilian study has underscored the critical role of gender and race in dental pain, revealing that non-white girls—particularly those from public schools and lower educational backgrounds—experience higher prevalence rate of pain.8 Therefore, addressing these fairness issues is essential to ensure that the model does not reinforce existing disparities, especially in underserved populations. To address this, future studies should prioritize fairness as a key criterion in model development, ensuring that the model benefits all populations equitably.33 Techniques such as fairness-aware machine learning34 and stratified sampling can help mitigate bias and improve model performance for underrepresented groups.

In conjunction with the fairness analysis, Shapley values were used to enhance the understanding of the algorithmic decision-making process. The findings revealed that male adolescents had a lower risk of dental pain, which may be influenced by the observed patterns of dental health disparities between Brazilian boys and girls. While some studies indicate that girls experience higher rates of dental caries and self-reported dental pain, the literature remains controversial, with many studies showing no significant differences in untreated dental caries prevalence by sex.3,8,9,35 Furthermore, factors such as societal norms and gender stereotypes may lead to the underreporting of pain symptoms among males, potentially obscuring their true dental health status.36,37 Similarly, adolescents who reported alcohol consumption and those who experienced one or more episodes of family violence were classified as at higher risk of dental pain. Alcohol use during adolescence has been associated with lower oral health-related quality of life38 and reduced adherence to dental treatment.39 Exposure to family violence also emerged as a significant variable in the identification of dental pain. Adolescents living in violent family environments are more likely to experience neglect, emotional distress, and limited access to preventive healthcare.40 Such psychosocial stressors may not only exacerbate pain perception but also hinder adherence to oral hygiene practices. Therefore, the association between violence exposure and dental pain underscores the importance of considering social and behavioral determinants when interpreting the model’s predictions and developing preventive strategies.

Future research should prioritize improving model performance in accordance with a fairness-oriented approach and explore generalizability across demographic groups. A key next step to strengthen real-world applicability would be the integration of clinical oral health indicators together with socioeconomic and behavioral variables. This combined approach may enhance predictive performance while preserving the model’s potential use in population-based screening. Additionally, the use of longitudinal data could support the development of dynamic models capable of capturing changes in dental pain over time and improving risk stratification for early intervention.

In conclusion, this study shows the potential of machine learning to identify dental pain in adolescents. However, improvements in fairness and model performance are necessary before widespread adoption. By addressing these limitations and advancing future research, can be possible enhance the utility of such models in improving public health outcomes.
