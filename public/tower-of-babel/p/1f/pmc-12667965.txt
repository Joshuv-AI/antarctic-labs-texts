Alcohol and fear conditioning produce strain-specific changes in the dorsal hippocampal transcriptome of adolescent C57BL/6J and DBA/2J mice
Authors: Laurel R. Seemiller, Lisa R. Goldberg, Aswathy Sebastian, Sue Rutherford Siegel, Craig Praul, Dana Zeid, Istvan Albert, Jacob Beierle, Camron D. Bryant, Thomas J. Gould
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Adolescent sensitivity to alcohol is influenced by genetic background. Data from our laboratory suggested that adolescent C57BL/6J and DBA/2J inbred mice differed in susceptibility to alcohol-induced deficits in dorsal hippocampus-dependent contextual fear learning.Methods:To investigate the biological underpinnings of this strain difference, we examined dorsal hippocampus gene expression using RNA-sequencing after alcohol or saline administration followed by Pavlovian fear conditioning across male and female C57BL/6J and DBA/2J adolescents.Results:Strains exhibited dramatic differences in dorsal hippocampus gene expression. Specifically, C57BL/6J and DBA/2J strains differed by 3526 transcripts in males and 2675 transcripts in females. We identified pathways likely to be involved in mediating alcohol’s effects on learning, including networks associated with Chrna7, a gene encoding the nicotinic cholinergic receptor alpha 7 subunit, and Fmr1, a gene encoding the fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein.Conclusions:These findings provide insight into the mechanisms underlying strain differences in alcohol’s effects on learning and suggest that different biological networks are recruited for learning based on genetics, sex, and alcohol exposure.

INTRODUCTION

Understanding the basic mechanisms of individual sensitivity to alcohol is an important step in addressing the global health problem of Alcohol Use Disorders (AUD). The path to alcohol dependence or addiction starts with an individual’s first experience with alcohol, which usually occurs during adolescence (SAMHSA, 2012). Whether this first experience is positive or negative can predict the trajectory of lifetime alcohol use (Haertzen et al., 1983). Genetics can shape sensitivity to numerous consequences of alcohol, including reports of subjective intoxication (Heath et al., 1999) and hangover (Wall et al., 2000). Preclinical research indicates that genetic background can determine alcohol-related behavioral phenotypes, such as conditioned taste aversion (CTA) in adult and adolescent male mice (Broadbent et al., 2002). However, little is known about the genetic factors that mediate alcohol’s effects on learning and memory in adolescents.

Prior studies from our laboratory (Seemiller & Gould, 2021) found that adult and adolescent male and female C57BL/6J and DBA/2J mice were differentially affected by acute alcohol exposure prior to fear conditioning. DBA/2J mice were less susceptible to alcohol-induced contextual, but not cued, fear conditioning deficits than C57BL/6J mice. Because this strain difference was specific to contextual fear learning, this result suggested that the neural circuitry unique to the contextual learning task was differentially affected by alcohol exposure. The dorsal hippocampus is known to be uniquely involved in contextual, but not cued, fear conditioning (Maren & Fanselow, 1997; Moser et al., 1995). Further, hippocampal gene expression varies across inbred mouse strains (Fernandes et al., 2004), which could contribute to differences in learning and sensitivity to disruption of learning. Alcohol exposure and learning both cause changes in hippocampal gene expression (Izquierdo et al., 2002; McClintick et al., 2018). Learning leads to the expression of immediate early genes and a later phase of gene expression changes (Barrientos et al., 2004; Guzowski, 2002; Izquierdo et al., 2002). It has also been shown that alcohol induces differential changes in hippocampal gene expression between males of C57BL/6J and DBA/2J mouse strains (Kerns et al., 2005). Therefore, we reasoned that alcohol’s effects on dorsal hippocampus gene expression might differ by strain, which could contribute to differences in susceptibility to alcohol’s effects on contextual fear learning.

To test this prediction, male and female C57BL/6J and DBA/2J subjects underwent alcohol or saline treatment and fear conditioning or no conditioning (learning or no-learning) control conditions prior to dorsal hippocampus gene expression analysis. RNA-sequencing was chosen for unbiased identification of neurobiological factors altered in a strain-specific manner by alcohol. Based on prior reports, we expected pathway analysis to reveal alcohol-induced changes in neural gene expression broadly related to neuroplasticity (Kerns et al., 2005). Prior studies in male rats investigating adult hippocampal gene expression after prior adolescent alcohol exposure identified changes related to neurogenesis, long-term potentiation, and myelination (McClintick et al., 2018). We predicted that we could see similar dysregulated gene networks in adolescent mice, as well as adolescent-specific effects. We have observed sex differences in learning performance after alcohol exposure in C57BL/6J and DBA/2J adolescent and adult mice (Seemiller, Garcia-Trevizo, et al., 2023; Seemiller, Goldberg, et al., 2023; Seemiller & Gould, 2021). Sex differences in gene expression patterns induced by contextual learning or alcohol exposure have been reported in other studies (Antunes-Martins et al., 2007; Finn et al., 2018). Because of this, we also expected to see sex-specific gene expression patterns.

The following research examines how genetic background influences changes in gene expression pathways in the dorsal hippocampus after adolescent alcohol exposure, which may contribute to the predisposition or protection for individual cognitive effects of acute alcohol exposure during adolescence. Ultimately, the identification of genes and neurobiological networks associated with alcohol-induced changes in cognition may aid in the development of treatments and interventions for those experiencing alcohol-related learning deficits.

METHODS

Subjects

Subjects were male and female C57BL/6J and DBA/2J mice shipped in groups of four from The Jackson laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA; JAX). Mice arrived at the facility on PND 31 (±3) and were PND 38 (±3) at the beginning of fear conditioning. This adolescent age was chosen because of important developmental processes happening around this time and based on our prior study examining alcohol effects on fear conditioning in adolescent mice (Seemiller & Gould, 2021). Subjects were housed in groups of four, and each subject within a cage was allocated to a different treatment group from its cage mates. Final group sizes were 3–4 per sex per strain per treatment group. Mice were kept on a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7 a.m.) and given free access to food (LabDiet 5053, Lab Diet, St Louis, MO, USA) and water. All procedures occurred between 8 a.m. and 5 p.m., during the light phase. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All procedures were also approved by the Penn State Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Alcohol dose and route of administration

Mice were administered an acute intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 0.9% saline solution or 1.5 g/kg 20% w/v ethanol in 0.9% saline solution. The route of administration, dose, and concentration of alcohol were chosen to match our previous studies examining alcohol effects on learning in adolescent C57BL/6J and DBA/2J mice (Seemiller, Garcia-Trevizo, et al., 2023; Seemiller & Gould, 2021). In our previous work, this alcohol dose did not produce strain or sex differences in blood ethanol concentration. A reduction in freezing during the baseline (pre-training) phase of fear conditioning was observed (Seemiller, Garcia-Trevizo, et al., 2023). However, learning deficits were exhibited during contextual fear testing, which happened the day after training and alcohol administration. Thus, locomotor stimulation due to acute alcohol treatment would not have been present during the assessment of contextual learning.

Fear conditioning procedures

Fear conditioning training was conducted by our laboratory as it has been done previously (Gould, 2003; Gulick & Gould, 2007, 2008; Seemiller & Gould, 2021). Fans provided a 65 dB background noise in the room. Mice were placed into sound-attenuating chambers (18.8 × 20 × 18.3 cm; MED Associates, St Albans, VT, USA), and after 2 min, subjects were exposed to two CS-US pairings (CS: Conditioned Stimulus, tone, 30 s, 85 dB; US: Unconditioned Stimulus, 0.45 mA footshock) separated by 120 s. At the conclusion of training, subjects were removed from the training context and moved back to their cages. While an auditory cue was presented during training in order to replicate procedures from our prior work in which an auditory CS was present (Seemiller & Gould, 2021), the goal of the described experiments was to determine gene expression patterns associated with alcohol’s effects on a brain region critical for contextual fear learning—the dorsal hippocampus, and not cued fear learning. This is because the prior studies found greater deficits in contextual fear conditioning. Learning-associated freezing responses to this fear conditioning paradigm are similar between C57BL/6J and DBA/2J strains independent of alcohol exposure. However, strain differences in other fear conditioning behaviors have been noted (Seemiller & Gould, 2021).

Experimental timeline

On approximately PND 38, male and female C57BL/6J and DBA/2J mice were injected with saline or alcohol. Fifteen minutes later, mice in the learning groups were placed into conditioning chambers and underwent fear conditioning training. Thirty minutes after being removed from the fear conditioning chambers, subjects in the learning groups were euthanized via cervical dislocation. Subjects in the no-learning groups remained in their cages for 50 min and 30 s after saline or alcohol treatment before being euthanized. The no-learning groups had a 50 min and 30 s delay between treatment and tissue collection to mirror the 15 min period after saline or alcohol treatment, the 5 min and 30 s conditioning period, and the 30 min delay after fear conditioning experienced by the learning groups (see Figure 1 for timeline). Subjects in the learning and no-learning groups were kept in the same holding room prior to tissue collection. Sample collection was run across 2 days and groups were counterbalanced to account for potential circadian effects. No-learning control subjects were kept in their cages and not introduced to the training context because the goal of the study was to understand alcohol’s effects on contextual fear learning and we wanted to capture gene expression changes that were independent of a new context in our control group.

Contextual fear learning induces at least two waves of transcription and protein synthesis in the hippocampus (Igaz et al., 2002). During the first wave of transcription, the largest change in gene expression has been observed 30 min after fear conditioning in rodents (Peixoto et al., 2015). Other studies in adult subjects have found significant fear conditioning-induced gene expression changes at this timepoint (Mei et al., 2005; Poplawski et al., 2016), including studies in adolescent subjects (Schreiber et al., 2014). Our laboratory has also examined neurobiological markers at this timepoint, finding increased hippocampal CREB phosphorylation 30 min after fear conditioning in acute nicotine-treated adult mice (Kenney et al., 2012). Thus, we chose the 30 min post-fear conditioning training timepoint for our examination of alcohol’s effects on learning-related gene expression.

Tissue collection, RNA isolation, and RNA-sequencing

After euthanasia, the whole hippocampus was dissected, and the dorsal hippocampus was removed and placed in RNAlater (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). Samples were stored at 4°C for 48 h before being dry-blotted and stored at −80°C until later processing. Trizol and Qiagen RNeasy Kits (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) were used to extract RNA (Goldberg et al., 2017). Library preparation and sequencing was performed by the Penn State Huck Institutes of the Life Sciences Genomics Core Facility (RRID:SCR_023645). Uniquely dual indexed libraries were prepared using the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Illumina NextSeq 550 High Output 75 nt single read sequencing was performed (Illumina).

RNA-sequencing data analysis

The Penn State Bioinformatics Consulting Center oversaw RNA-sequencing data analysis for this project. Using HISAT2 (v2.1; Kim et al., 2019), FASTQ files were aligned to a mouse reference genome (GRCm38). Read counts and differential expression analysis were performed using Salmon (v1.4; Patro et al., 2017) or featureCounts (v2.0.1; Liao et al., 2014) and DESeq2 (v4.1.1; Love et al., 2014), using a standard FDR cut-off of 0.05 (Benjamini et al., 2001). Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA; run May 2022; www.qiagen.com/ingenuity; Qiagen, Redwood City, CA, USA; Krämer et al., 2014) and Enrichr Gene Ontology (GO) analysis (Kuleshov et al., 2016) were used to interpret data, as our laboratory has done previously (Goldberg et al., 2019). The IPA analysis specified pathways in mammalian nervous tissue or cell lines and the maximum was set to 35 molecules per network. Gene ontology plots were generated using GeneScape (Albert, 2024). Male and female subjects were analyzed separately due to sex differences in prior behavioral data and apparent sex-specific changes in gene expression. RNA-seq data were uploaded to Gene Expression Omnibus.

qPCR

Reverse transcription was performed with ~1 μg total RNA using the High-Capacity RNA to cDNA kit (ThermoFisher Scientific Applied Biosystems: USA; catalog # 4387406) following manufacturers’ instructions. Relative expression was conducted using a TaqMan Universal Mastermix II with UNG (ThermoFisher Scientific Applied Biosystems: USA; catalog # 4440038) to amplify Taqman inventoried primers for Chrna7 (Mm01312230_m1; ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) and Fmr1 (Mm01339582_m1; ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). ThermoFisher Scientific (Life Technologies, USA) custom made primers for Hprt1 (forward: GCTGGTGAAAAGGACCTCT and reverse: CACAGGACTAGAACACCTGC) were used as an endogenous control for each real time quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay. Samples were run in triplicate and averaged with the comparative Ct method to calculate fold change (Schmittgen & Livak, 2008). All amplifications were performed using a Rotor-Gene Q 5 plex HRM thermocycler (Qiagen). Statistical analysis [4-way (strain, sex, drug, and learning condition) ANOVA] was conducted using SPSS 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and figures were made in GraphPad Prism 9 (San Diego, CA, USA).

SNP analysis

Follow-up SNP analysis of genes of interest was conducted using a Mouse Phenome Database GenomeMUSter tool (Bogue et al., 2020; https://mpd.jax.org/genotypes).

RESULTS

Overlap of transcripts between alcohol and learning treatment groups

First, the transcripts regulated by alcohol, learning, and combined alcohol and learning exposures (Tables S1–S12) were compared within-strain and within-sex groups. Across males and females from C57BL/6J and DBA/2J strains, alcohol exposure led to 114–142 differentially regulated transcripts and fear conditioning led to 128–279 differentially regulated transcripts. Combined alcohol and learning led to 116–473 differentially regulated transcripts. Relatively few transcripts (3–7) were changed across all three conditions (Figure 2).

Individual impact of strain, alcohol, and learning on gene expression

Strain differences in gene expression

Separate pairwise comparisons were conducted to investigate the unique impacts of strain, alcohol exposure, and learning on dorsal hippocampal gene expression patterns. First, the influence of strain was assessed by comparing saline-treated C57BL/6J and DBA/2J that had not undergone fear conditioning (saline no-learning C57BL/6J vs. saline no-learning DBA/2J; Tables S13 and S14). Transcript analysis identified extensive strain differences, finding 3526 transcripts in males and 2675 transcripts in females that were significantly different between C57BL/6J and DBA/2J strains (FDR <0.05). A notable differentially regulated transcript between strains among male subjects was Chrna7, which encodes the nicotinic cholinergic receptor alpha 7 subunit and has previously been associated with alcohol-induced contextual learning outcomes (Wehner et al., 2004). IPA analysis identified a significant differentially regulated network associated with this transcript, described as functioning in “Cancer, Organismal Injury and Abnormalities, and Organismal Survival” (score = 27, Figure 3). Follow-up analysis of Chrna7 sequence in C57BL/6J versus DBA/2J strains using a Mouse Phenome Database GenomeMUSter tool revealed mainly intronic, synonymous, or UTR SNPs (Table S15). Follow-up qPCR analysis recapitulated strain differences in Chrna7 expression, although they were not significant (p > 0.05) likely due to low power due to small sample sizes (Figure S1). These results in untreated/untrained male strains implicate predisposing genes and networks that may influence subsequent susceptibility to alcohol deficits in learning.

Impact of alcohol or learning on gene expression

Next, comparisons within strain and sex groups assessed the impact of alcohol and learning on dorsal hippocampal transcript levels. As mentioned previously, alcohol exposure led to 134 (C57BL/6J male), 142 (C57BL/6J female), 141 (DBA/2J male), and 114 (DBA/2J female) differentially regulated transcripts (alcohol no-learning vs. saline no-learning; Tables S1, S4, S7, S10). Fear conditioning resulted in 279 (C57BL/6J male), 133 (C57BL/6J female), 128 (DBA/2J male), and 128 (DBA/2J female) differentially regulated transcripts (saline learning vs. saline no-learning; Tables S2, S5, S8, S11).

Disruption of learning-related gene expression by alcohol

A primary goal of transcriptome analysis of the dorsal hippocampus was to identify the set of fear learning-associated differentially expressed genes that were disrupted by alcohol. This goal was addressed by conducting pairwise comparisons between saline- and alcohol-treated subjects that had undergone fear conditioning training (learning groups only; C57BL/6J alcohol learning vs. C57BL/6J saline learning; Tables S16–S19). Separate analyses were conducted in C57BL/6J and DBA/2J strains to assess possibly divergent gene expression patterns associated with disruption of learning by alcohol in both strains. Comparisons between fear conditioned saline- or alcohol-treated C57BL/6J subjects found 176–190 differentially regulated transcripts. Significant GO Biological Pathways were axon regeneration (GO:0031103) and neuron projection regeneration (GO:0031102) in males, while none were significantly enriched in females. IPA analysis identified the following top associated canonical pathways, upstream regulators, and diseases and disorders implicated in transcript-level differences between groups (Tables 1 and 2; Table S20). While top canonical pathways and upstream regulators show pronounced sex differences, diseases and disorders associated with gene expression changes after alcohol were similar across sexes.

Comparisons between fear conditioned saline- or alcohol-treated DBA/2J subjects (DBA/2J alcohol learning vs. DBA/2J saline learning) identified 123–140 differentially regulated transcripts. GO Biological Function analysis did not identify any significantly enriched terms among differentially regulated transcripts in DBA/2J mice. IPA analysis found the listed top five canonical pathways, upstream regulators, and diseases and disorders associated with differentially regulated transcripts (Tables 3 and 4; Table S20). Again, there is only overlap between DBA/2J males and females in diseases and disorders associated with alcohol-regulated transcripts in fear conditioned mice. While there is no overlap in top canonical pathways and upstream regulators between C57BL/6J and DBA/2J analyses, diseases and disorders associated with transcriptional changes were similar between strains.

Strain differences in disruption of learning-related gene expression by alcohol

Another goal of transcriptome analysis was to understand why adolescent DBA/2J mice were less susceptible to alcohol-induced contextual fear learning deficits than C57BL/6J mice in our previous studies (Seemiller, Goldberg, et al., 2023; Seemiller & Gould, 2021). To investigate the individual and combined effects of alcohol, learning, and strain on gene expression, two sets of multifactor (2 × 2) analyses were conducted. First, within-strain and within-sex groups, drug and learning effects were assessed (saline or alcohol vs. learning or no-learning). Then, within sex groups, strain and combined learning and alcohol effects were compared (C57BL/6J or DBA/2J vs. alcohol and learning or saline and no-learning; Table S21). There were no significant interactions of drug and learning condition or strain and combined learning/drug conditions detected (FDR >0.05), likely due to small sample sizes (n = 3–4) when sexes were analyzed separately. Thus, simpler pairwise analyses were used to assess impacts of alcohol, learning, and strain on gene expression, as has been previously published (Baker et al., 2017; Silva et al., 2018).

Pairwise comparisons were made between C57BL/6J and DBA/2J mice that had undergone alcohol treatment and fear conditioning exposure (C57BL/6J alcohol learning vs. DBA/2J alcohol learning; Tables S22 and S23). This analysis identified 2816–3847 differentially regulated transcripts between strains. GO Biological Process analysis found ubiquinone biosynthetic process (GO:0006744), cotranslational protein targeting to membrane (GO:0006613), SRP-dependent cotranslational protein targeting to membrane (GO:0006614), cytoplasmic translation (GO:0002181), quinone biosynthetic process (GO:1901663), and protein targeting to ER (GO:0045047) significantly enriched in males, and regulation of focal adhesion assembly (GO:0051893) and neuron projection development (GO:0031175) was significantly enriched in females. Further, IPA analysis identified the following top canonical pathways, upstream regulators, and diseases and disorders associated with transcript differences (Tables 5 and 6; Table S20). Using GeneScape, the functions of upstream regulator genes (as listed in Table 6) were projected to be broadly involved in protein binding and protein-containing complex binding (Figure 4; Figures S3–S6). Similar to previous analyses, there was limited overlap between sexes among top IPA canonical pathways, but substantial overlap between diseases and disorders associated with gene expression differences. Htt and Fmr1 were shared upstream regulators identified across male and female mice. Follow-up analysis of Fmr1 sequence in C57BL/6J and DBA/2J mice found mainly intronic variants (Table S24). The qPCR analysis did not support a clear difference in Fmr1 expression between alcohol and learning groups (p > 0.05), supporting the importance of the broader Fmr1-associated network in determining alcohol and learning phenotypes (Figure S2).

DISCUSSION

We assessed strain differences in dorsal hippocampal gene expression patterns associated with fear conditioning, alcohol exposure, and their interaction. Major differences in gene expression were observed between strains. Notably, in alcohol and fear conditioning naive DBA/2J males, we observed lower levels of Chrna7 transcript than condition-matched C57BL/6J males. A prior study from our laboratory using males and females found that DBA/2J mice were less sensitive to the disruptive effects of alcohol than C57BL/6J mice (Seemiller & Gould, 2021). These results are in line with a previous study that found reduced alcohol-induced contextual learning deficits in Chrna7 knockout mice (Wehner et al., 2004). Alcohol also disrupted transcript levels differentially across strains and sex. Some notable pathways associated with alcohol’s actions were GDNF (glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor) family ligand-receptor interactions in female C57BL/6J mice and PKA (Protein Kinase A) in male DBA/2J mice. Further, it is possible that strain differences in alcohol-associated deficits in contextual fear learning were related to upstream regulator Fmr1, identified in both male and female analyses. Thus, alcohol may disrupt learning via interactions with pathways associated with Chrna7, GDNF, PKA, and Fmr1, which could be points of intervention for future studies.

Impact of strain, learning, or alcohol on gene expression

The main goals of this study were (1) To identify alcohol-disrupted learning-related gene expression in adolescents and (2) To understand why adolescent C57BL/6J and DBA/2J mice differed in contextual learning outcomes after alcohol exposure (Seemiller & Gould, 2021). We predicted that complex interactions between strain, alcohol treatment, and fear learning exposure contributed to observed fear learning outcomes described in prior research (Seemiller & Gould, 2021). However, before examining possible interactions among factors, we examined the impact of each factor individually (strain, learning, and alcohol) on the dorsal hippocampal transcriptome.

Among the three factors (strain, drug, and fear learning) that were examined, strain differences were, predictably, the most pronounced. In males and females, 3526 and 2675 transcripts, respectively, were differentially regulated between C57BL/6J and DBA/2J strains (FDR <0.05). These transcriptional changes were an order of magnitude greater than those seen due to alcohol or fear conditioning exposures alone, as alcohol exposure resulted in 114–142 differentially regulated transcripts and fear conditioning led to 128–279 differentially regulated transcripts within strain and sex groups. These gene expression differences likely correspond to known strain differences in fear conditioning phenotypes (Seemiller & Gould, 2021).

An exciting observation from pairwise comparisons across strains was that in male subjects, Chrna7 transcript levels were lower in DBA/2J subjects than in C57BL/6J subjects in saline no-learning control subjects. This was not the case in female subjects. Prior research established that Chrna7 knockout male and female mice are less susceptible to alcohol effects on contextual fear learning (Wehner et al., 2004). Thus, it is intriguing that DBA/2J males have lower Chrna7 transcript levels and are less susceptible to alcohol effects on contextual fear learning that C57BL/6J males (Seemiller & Gould, 2021). This could suggest an important role for Chrna7 and its protein product, the α7 subunit of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, in shaping susceptibility to alcohol’s cognitive effects. Specifically, having lower Chrna7 expression, and possibly fewer α7 subunit-containing nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, may predispose DBA/2J subjects to being less affected by alcohol in this learning task. Studies examining α7 subunits have shown unique roles for this subunit in modulating other alcohol phenotypes. For example, deletion of the α7 subunit (in null mutant male and female mice) produced exaggerated locomotor stimulation, sedation (LORR duration), and hypothermia phenotypes after alcohol exposure (Bowers et al., 2005). α7 knockout female mice have also been shown to consume smaller amounts of alcohol (Kamens et al., 2010). Our data fit into a broader narrative implicating alcohol effects on the cholinergic system as being critical for short- and long-term effects of adolescent alcohol exposure on learning and memory (Ehlers et al., 2011). However, our data suggest that this may not apply to female subjects, warranting more detailed follow-up studies in females. Chrna7 would also be an intriguing target to examine further in humans, as it is expressed throughout the nervous system and variation in this gene has been associated with other cognitive and drug-associated conditions, including nicotine dependence (Cameli et al., 2018).

Disruption of learning-related gene expression by alcohol

Relative to observed transcriptional differences between strains, there were far fewer alcohol-associated transcriptional changes (176–190 differentially regulated transcripts). Prior studies have demonstrated that acute alcohol exposure often leads to transcriptional changes in the hippocampus associated with neuroplasticity (Kerns et al., 2005). Because learning and memory relies on this plasticity (Ramirez & Arbuckle, 2016), we suspected that alcohol might disrupt contextual fear learning by inhibiting neuroplasticity in the dorsal hippocampus. Comparisons between saline- and alcohol-treated subjects that had undergone fear conditioning showed some support for this. For example, among female C57BL/6J mice, a top canonical pathway was GDNF (glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor) family ligand-receptor interactions; GDNF is thought to contribute to neuroplasticity and, consequently, learning and memory (Võikar et al., 2004). However, top canonical pathways did not consistently include GDNF across both strains and sexes examined. This further indicates that genetic mechanisms underlying learning after alcohol exposure differ due to strain and sex.

Another notable finding was that Protein Kinase A (PKA) was a top canonical pathway associated with alcohol exposure among fear conditioned male DBA/2J subjects. It has also been shown that PKA is important in learning and memory (Goosens et al., 2000; Schafe et al., 1999). Ablation of some PKA isoforms has been shown to disrupt hippocampal long-term potentiation in male and female mice (Huang et al., 1995), and inhibiting PKA activity impaired contextual and cued fear conditioning in male rats (Schafe et al., 1999). Furthermore, male rats treated with a low dose of alcohol shifted learning strategies from a hippocampus-based strategy to a striatal-based strategy, which suggests that alcohol changed how learning occurred (Sun et al., 2018). This shift in strategy was associated with decreased PKA activation. Thus, it is possible that alcohol regulates learning performance in DBA/2J males by dysregulating PKA signaling.

Strain differences in disruption of learning-related gene expression by alcohol

To assess why in prior work C57BL/6J and DBA/2J strains exhibited differences in contextual fear learning after alcohol exposure (Seemiller & Gould, 2021), dorsal hippocampal gene expression was compared across strains among subjects that had undergone alcohol and fear conditioning exposures. The number of transcript-level differences between groups was relatively high (2816–3847 differentially regulated transcripts), largely due to the drastic differences observed between strains independent of drug or fear conditioning exposures. The canonical pathways and diseases and disorders associated with these strain differences were variable across males and females, with some canonical pathways related to immune signaling seen in both males and females (Activation of IRF by Cytosolic Pattern Recognition Receptors, IL-3 Signaling).

While sex differences in the RNA-sequencing data were generally pronounced, there was some noteworthy convergence in upstream regulators associated with transcriptional differences across sexes in this analysis. In both males and females, transcript differences between C57BL/6J and DBA/2J mice that were treated with alcohol and fear conditioned were associated with upstream regulators, Htt (huntingtin) and Fmr1 (fragile X mental retardation syndrome 1). Htt, while associated with many differentially regulated transcripts, was not differentially expressed between strains in males or females. However, Fmr1 transcript levels were significantly greater in DBA/2J mice than in C57BL/6J in male and female comparisons, making it a desirable candidate for future investigation. Fmr1 encodes fragile X mental retardation protein (FMRP), which is likely an RNA-binding protein (Siomi et al., 1993). As its name suggests, it has been associated with fragile X syndrome and autism-like phenotypes (Crawford et al., 2001). Male and female Fmr1 knockout mice display numerous behavioral deficits depending on their strain background, including abnormal anxiety-like behaviors and social behaviors (Baker et al., 2010; Spencer et al., 2005) and impairments in learning tasks, including contextual fear conditioning (Baker et al., 2010). Further, in Drosophila, FMRP dysregulation has been associated with alterations in PKA activity (Sears et al., 2019), noted in a previous section for being dysregulated in alcohol-treated fear conditioned subjects. PKA and FMRP interact in a negative feedback loop (Sears & Broadie, 2020), suggesting that higher FMRP levels could be related to low PKA levels. This could contribute to learning deficits. Thus, it is possible that altered expression of Fmr1 due to alcohol and fear conditioning leads to dysregulation of Fmr1-associated networks, and this may drive observed fear learning differences after alcohol between C57BL/6J and DBA/2J strains. Importantly, the SNP analysis of Fmr1 did not indicate likely functional variants between strains, supporting that strain differences are likely the consequence of differential expression of Fmr1 rather than functional changes within the gene.

Limitations and future directions

RNA-sequencing enabled a discovery-based approach to examining alcohol- and learning-associated transcriptional patterns. Interpretation of these data was informed by existing literature examining mechanisms of alcohol’s behavioral effects. While it would have been informative to include numerous strains in the RNA-sequencing experiments, using two strains, C57BL/6J and DBA/2J, allowed for inclusion of more groups to examine separate alcohol and learning influences. We also chose to examine one time point in the gene expression study. Prior studies provide robust support for the value of this timepoint in assessing gene expression changes associated with fear learning in adult C57BL6/CBAF1 and C57BL/6J mice (Mei et al., 2005; Poplawski et al., 2016). Candidate genes identified by RNA-sequencing in our study may later be investigated using RT-qPCR in other strains, in adult subjects, or at different timepoints after learning or alcohol treatment. This may also be examined in future studies in specific cell types, as our study examined general differences across dorsal hippocampal tissue.

Further, strain differences in brain regions other than the dorsal hippocampus may have contributed to observed variation in fear learning after alcohol exposure. We chose to focus on the dorsal hippocampus because of the known role of the dorsal hippocampus in spatial learning (Hunsaker & Kesner, 2008), alcohol’s known effects on the hippocampus (McClintick et al., 2018; Nagel et al., 2005; Weitemier & Ryabinin, 2003) and the role of the hippocampus in numerous alcohol-related pathologies, such as Major Depressive Disorder (Brière et al., 2014; Nagel et al., 2005; Videbech & Ravnkilde, 2004). The ventral hippocampus, while important for fear learning, plays a broader role in fear association and expression (Kjelstrup et al., 2002). Because alcohol had greater effects on contextual fear conditioning when compared to cued fear conditioning, we focused on a brain region that contributes more specifically to contextual fear conditioning (Hunsaker & Kesner, 2008). While it is outside the scope of the proposed research, the roles of other brain regions, such as the ventral hippocampus and amygdala, in contextual learning deficits could be investigated in future studies. Future researchers will be able to compare their findings to ours, which are publicly available.

It is important to note that alcohol-associated gene expression was evaluated in fear conditioned subjects from both strains and sexes, but results of IPA analysis revealed little convergence across strain and sex groups in the functional enrichment of those transcriptional changes. This suggests it is likely that alcohol disrupts learning through unique mechanisms across the sexes and genetic backgrounds.

CONCLUSION

Understanding the biological basis of alcohol sensitivity is critical for understanding and preventing the development of AUD. Here, we describe how individual differences such as genetic background and sex shape susceptibility to alcohol’s effects during adolescence and shape differences in hippocampus-dependent learning. We found robust differences in dorsal hippocampal gene expression between strains and sexes. Alcohol and learning both altered gene expression and produced unique transcriptional patterns across groups. Thus, differences in hippocampal gene expression biological networks were seen across strains, sex, and alcohol exposure. This suggests that mechanisms of learning are not homogenous but are influenced by genetics, sex, and drug exposure, which could result in differences in learning strategies and outcomes. Within this dataset, we identified biological pathways that could be involved in alcohol’s effects on learning and memory, such as those associated with Chrna7 or Fmr1. Future research can followup on targets identified in this project to better understand how to modulate and possibly prevent alcohol-associated contextual learning impairments.
