Lactobacillus rhamnosus B16 regulates lipid metabolism homeostasis by producing acetic acid
Authors: Zhengping Che, Juan Huang, Shiyi Wen, Xingxing Chai, Shaobing Zhang, Haibo Yu, Bihua Lin, Yanfang Liang, Ziyu Ye, Jincheng Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHyperlipidemia, a chronic disorder caused by abnormal lipid metabolism, increases risks of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular diseases. Current single-target drugs have limited efficacy and side effects, prompting interest in probiotics such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus, though their mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsA novel Lactobacillus rhamnosus strain from healthy human feces was identified via API 50CH and ANI analysis. Integrated genomic and proteomic analyses were performed on the novel Lactobacillus rhamnosus B16 (L. rB16) strain to characterize its lipid metabolism-related traits. A cholesterol degradation assay, gastrointestinal fluid tolerance test, and biofilm formation assay were used to evaluate the functional properties of L. rB16, while foam cell models were employed to assess its cholesterol-lowering effects. Introduction

Hyperlipidemia is a systemic chronic disorder caused by abnormal lipid metabolism in the body, which can induce atherosclerosis and other associated cardiovascular diseases. With its age of onset trending younger and patient numbers continuously increasing, hyperlipidemia has emerged as one of the major global health challenges [1]. The etiology of hyperlipidemia is highly complex, and accumulating evidence indicates that cholesterol metabolic dysfunction may represent a critical driver underlying the “developmental origins of disease” paradigm [2]. Currently, traditional pharmacological agents (such as atorvastatin, fenofibrate, ezetimibe) for multietiology hyperlipidemia yield suboptimal therapeutic outcomes, as their mechanisms of action rely on single-target modulation [3]. Furthermore, long-term administration of these drugs is associated with varying degrees of multi-organ damage and adverse effects, including diarrhea, gastrointestinal disturbances, myalgia, and hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity [4, 5]. Therefore, improving the efficacy of conventional therapies while minimizing associated adverse effects remains a significant clinical challenge.

Lactobacillus rhamnosus is one of the most common types of probiotics in the Lactobacillus genus [6]. This probiotic has been shown to possess several beneficial properties, including acid and bile resistance, gastrointestinal tract colonization, regulation of intestinal flora balance, and cholesterol reduction [7, 8]. Both in vivo and in vitro studies have demonstrated that Lactobacillus rhamnosus exerts a significant cholesterol-lowering effect [9, 10]. However, due to the complex mechanism of probiotics in hyperlipidemia treatment and the influence of strain-specific characteristics, critical knowledge gaps and limitations exist in current research. For instance, probiotic effects are highly strain-dependent, yet most studies focus on known ones (e.g., Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG), with limited systematic characterization of novel isolates or their unique lipid-regulating mechanisms. Additionally, the key effector metabolites and core signaling pathways in the cholesterol-lowering effects of probiotics remain unclear, lacking a complete “strain-metabolite-pathway” regulatory chain. Therefore, investigating the mechanisms by which novel probiotics regulate cholesterol metabolism is extremely important for probiotic therapy.

Herein, we aimed to address these gaps by isolating and characterizing a novel Lactobacillus rhamnosus, named L. rB16 strain, from healthy human feces. Notably, we demonstrated that L. rB16 strains could regulate the cholesterol metabolic homeostasis in foam cells through activation of the LXRα signaling pathway. Furthermore, L. rB16 alleviates HFD-induced hyperlipidemia in mice by regulating lipid metabolic balance via dual mechanisms: inhibition of cholesterol anabolism and promotion of cholesterol catabolism. More importantly, acetic acid produced by L. rB16 was identified as a key mediator of these lipid-regulating effects. Collectively, our findings reveal a promising novel biological therapeutic strategy for ameliorating or slowing the progression of diseases associated with dysregulated lipid metabolism.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

L. rB16 was obtained by screening from 76 strains of cholesterol-degrading lactobacilli isolated from healthy human feces using MRS broth—Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe broth, a specialized culture medium for lactic acid bacteria (such as Lactobacillus) that provides nutrients like peptides, vitamins, and carbohydrates to support bacterial growth— containing quantified cholesterol. The strains were identified by API 50CH strips (BioMerieux) and ANI analysis (Biomarker). ANI analysis, short for Average Nucleotide Identity analysis, is a genomic-level measure of similarity between two genomes, calculated by comparing orthologous protein-coding genes to get a percentage-based identity value that reflects genomic similarity. L. rB16 strain (also named L. r CMU-pb-7) is kindly gifted by Guangdong Xinghai Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Cell culture

Human monocytic cell line THP-1 was purchased from the Procell Life Science&Technology Co.,Ltd. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 basal medium (Biological Industries, Cat. No. 01-100-1ACS) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Biological Industries, Cat. No. 04-001-1ACS) and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin; Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. 15140122). Culture conditions were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ in a humidified incubator. For differentiation the macrophage cells, THP-1 cells were stimulated with 100 nM phorbol 12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA, Sigma, Cat. No. P8139) at 37 ˚C for 24 h. Cells were exposed to 50 µg/mL ox-LDL (Yiyuan biotechnology, Cat. No. YB-001) for 48 h to generate foam cells.

Animals

Six-week-old adult male C57BL/6J mice (18–22 g) were obtained from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center and fed on normal diet for 1 week to adapt to the environment. The mice were housed in the Guangdong Medical University animal experiment center under standard conditions, with a strict 12 h light/dark cycle, humidity at 50 ± 15%, a temperature of 22 ± 2 ˚C, and ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups using a stratified randomization method based on body weight. All animal protocols were reviewed and approved by the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (approval no. GDY2004019).

Whole genome sequencing analysis

Strains were collected for whole genome sequencing. Sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were commissioned by Biomarker Technologies. The experimental process was based on the standard protocol provided by Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT), including sample quality testing, library construction, library quality testing, library sequencing, and biological information analysis (including gene prediction, gene annotation, and genome comparative analysis). In gene function annotation analysis, a total of 12 databases were used, including NR, GO, KEGG, eggNOG, Pfam, TrEMBL, CAZy, TCDB, CARD, PHI-base, and VFDB.

Label-free quantitative proteomics

Strains were collected for liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Sequencing and bioinformatics analysis commissioned by QLbio Technologies. RIGOL L-3000 HPLC Systems was used for LC-MS/MS detection (Rigol Technologies). The mass spectrometry (MS) raw data were processed using MaxQuant software. The data were processed against the UniProt database for Lactobacillus rhamnosus (https://www.uniprot.org/) for protein identification.

The colony and cell morphology of L. rB16

To observe colony morphology, a single colony of L. rB16 was inoculated onto a fresh MRS agar plate (Huankai Microbial, Cat. No. 027319) and cultured in an anaerobic incubator at 37 °C for 48 h; colony shape, surface texture, edge regularity, and color were recorded. For cellular morphology, a single colony was subjected to Gram staining (Solarbio, Cat. No. G1060) following standard protocols, and stained cells were observed under an oil immersion microscope (Nikon) at 1000× magnification to determine Gram reaction and cellular arrangement.

Detection the growth of L. rB16

MRS broth was used to activate L. rB16 strain, the strain cells were collected by centrifugation at 4000×g for 10 min, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended at 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL. The fresh suspension was inoculated into 50 mL MRS broth according to 2% (v/v) inoculation and cultured in 37 °C for 48 h. The OD600 value of MRS broth was detected by ultraviolet spectrophotometer at 2 h intervals [11]. Each time point is repeated 3 times, taking the average value. MRS broth was a blank control, and the growth curve was drawn with culture time as horizontal coordinate and absorbance as vertical coordinate.

Detection of biofilm forming ability

Preparation of fresh bacterial suspensions for biofilm formation experiments. The bacterial suspensions were inoculated into 15 mL MRS broth medium according to 2% (v/v) inoculation and mix well. L. rB16 and L. rB45 (2 mL) were plated in a 6-well plate tissue culture plate (Corning) with coverslips, and incubated for 72 h in an anaerobic environment at 37 °C. After incubation, the coverslips with biofilm stain with 1% crystal violet for 30 min. After the coverslips were naturally dried, the biofilm formation was observed under a 100 × oil immersion lens (Nikon). At the same time, semi-quantitative determination of biofilm formation ability of strain by microplate. The bacterial suspension (200 µL) was deposited in a 96-well plate (Corning) and incubated for 72 h. The OD630 value was determined by enzyme labeling instrument, recorded as A1, MRS broth was a blank control. Then the culture supernatant in the 96-well plate was discarded, 200 µL 1% crystal violet solution was added to each well for dyeing for 30 min. The OD630 value was measured by enzyme labeling instrument and recorded as A2. The adhesion rate represents the film-forming ability, recorded as B, B=(A2-A2C)/(A1 − A1C). A1c and A2C are the absorbance values of the control group when measuring A1 and A2 respectively. The adhesion rate B was divided into: B < 0.1 indicates non-adhesion, B ≥ 0.1 indicates adhesion biofilm formation, 0.1 < B < 1 indicates medium-level adhesion biofilm formation, and B ≥ 1 indicates strong-level biofilm formation [11].

Gastrointestinal fluid tolerance test

A total of 1 mL of fresh L. rB16 bacterial solution in the logarithmic growth phase was added into 9 mL artificial gastric juice (pH = 2.0, pH = 3.0) and intestinal juice (pH = 7.8) test tubes, and quickly mixed. The bacteria were cultured at 37 °C, and the colonies were counted at 0 h, 2 h, and 4 h, respectively. The survival rate%=(Nn/N0) × 100%, and N0 was the viable count (CFU/mL) of 0 h, and Nn was the viable count (CFU/mL) of 2–4 h after artificial gastric juice or intestinal juice treatment. The artificial gastric juice (pH = 2.0, 3.0) was prepared by dissolving 3 g/L pepsin (Sigma) in sterile normal saline, adjusting pH with 1 mol/L HCl, and filter-sterilizing (0.22 μm). Intestinal juice (pH = 7.8) by dissolving 1 g/L trypsin (Sigma) and 10 g/L bile salts (Solarbio) in sterile normal saline, adjusting pH with 1 mol/L NaOH, and filter-sterilizing (0.22 μm).

Analysis of cholesterol content

The cholesterol content in the supernatant of bacterial culture is determined using total cholesterol assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng). Total cholesterol (TC) content (mmol/L) = [(sample A-blank A)/(standard A-blank A)] × standard C. The formula of cholesterol degradation efficiency is: cholesterol degradation rate %=(C0-Cn)/C0 × 100%, A is the absorbance at 510 nm, C0 is the cholesterol content in cholesterol-enriched MRS broth before culture, Cn is the cholesterol content in the medium at different time points of the experimental strain; In the experiments of quantitative measurement of intracellular cholesterol: Intracellular total cholesterol content were measured using the tissue total cholesterol assay kit (Applygen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Preparation of heat-killed bacteria

L. rB16 was grown in MRS broth for 16 h at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm/min. Bacteria were centrifuged at 2000 g for 10 min and resuspended in sterile normal saline at a density of 1.0 × 109 CFU/mL. Thereafter, bacteria were heat-inactivated by incubation at 65 °C for 60 min, as previously described [12]. The resulting heat-inactivated strain is referred to as HI-L. rB16 (Heat-inactivated Lactobacillus rhamnosus B16).

Cell viability assay

In cell proliferation assay, foam cells were first induced. 100 µL HI-L. rB16 with different multiplicity of infection (MOI) (10− 3, 10− 1, 102, 105) were seeded into 96-well plates at 37 °C with 5% CO2. CCK-8 (Dojindo) was used to measure the cell proliferation. 10 µL CCK-8 solution was added at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h by following the manufacturer’s protocol. After 2 h of incubation, absorbance was measured at 450 nm. In bacteria proliferation assay, foam cells were first induced by PMA. 100 µL HI-L. rB16 with different MOI (10− 3, 10− 1, 102, 105) were seeded into 96-well plates at 37 °C with 5% CO2. XTT Bacteria Proliferation and Cytotoxicity Kit (KeyGEN BioTECH) was used to measure the bacteria proliferation. XTT solution was added at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h by following the XTT manufacturer’s protocol.

Assessment of foam cell formation by oil red O staining

THP-1 cells were induced with 100 nM PMA for 24 h in a 6-well plate (Corning) with coverslips. Cells were then incubated with 50 µg/mL ox-LDL for 72 h in the presence or absence of HI-L. rB16 with different MOI (10− 5,10− 3, 1, 103) in the same media. The cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, stained for 30 min in 1% Oil Red O (Solarbio). Followed by counterstaining with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Solarbio) for 1 min. The coverslips were mounted on glass slides and examined under light microscopy by trained observers blinded to the conditions. Oil droplets were observed and images were obtained using an inverted microscope (Nikon). Semi-quantitative analysis was carried out by foam cell area counting method. Cells were classified according to the area of cell lipid droplets. The area of cell lipid droplets less than that of nucleus was denoted as ' − ‘, and the area of cell lipid droplets equal to or greater than that of nucleus was denoted as ' + ‘, namely foam cells. Each piece of coverslips was counted for 100 cells, and the foam cells were expressed as the percentage of positive Oil Red O cells to total cells.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from cells using RIPA buffer (Solarbio) and quantified using a BCA kit (Beyotime). Then 50 µg of protein per sample were loaded onto 4–12% SDS-PAGE. After electrophoresis and transferring to PVDF membranes, the membranes were blocked in TBST (tris-buffered saline with Tween 20) containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 60 min at room temperature. After washing, membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody HMGCR, FXR, ABCA1, LXRα, ABCG1, SREBP1, CYP7A1, and GAPDH. Detailed information of all antibodies used (including vendor, catalog number, and working concentration/dilution) is provided in Supplementary Table 1. After incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. Immunoblots were imaged using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech). Optical density was assessed using the ImageJ analysis software.

Safety evaluation of L. rB16

Hemolysis test: the single colony of L. rB16 was inoculated on columbia blood agar plate and cultured under anaerobic condition at 37 °C for 48 h. After a single colony was grown in the plate, the formation of hemolytic ring around the colony was observed and photographed. The plates were observed for presence of clear zone, greenish brown, and no zone which indicates the complete (β hemolysis), partial (α hemolysis), and no hemolytic (γ) activity, respectively. Animal toxicity test: acute toxicity studies were performed in C57BL/6J (6 weeks) male mice. The animals were randomly allocated to three groups (Control group, HI-L. rB16 group, and L. rB16 group) using a stratified randomization method based on body weight and fat mass. The control group was treated with 200 µL normal saline, and the experimental group was treated with 200 µL bacterial suspension once a day for 30 consecutive days. Record the general signs of the mice daily, such as movement, hair, weight, death or not. On the 8th day, three randomly selected mice underwent cervical dislocation, and dissected mice to take their organs. Morphological changes of heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney in mice were observed and weighed. On the 31st day, the remaining 3 mice took the same operation as described on day 8. Calculate organ index = m1/m2 [m1 is organ weight (g), m2 was weight quality in mice (g)] [11].

High-fat diet model

After one week of adaptation to the normal diet, a hyperlipidemic mouse model was established using a HFD—a diet with ~ 60% of calories derived from fat (vs. ~10–15% in the normal diet), used to induce abnormal elevation of serum TC, triglycerides (TG), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) in mice, simulating human hyperlipidemic pathological features. A total of 24 six-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (initial weight 18–22 g) used in this study. To ensure balanced group assignment, mice were first stratified based on their initial body weight and then randomly assigned using a computer-generated random number sequence to one of three experimental groups (n = 8 per group), ensuring no significant differences in initial body weight between groups (p >0.05). The groups included the Normal diet (ND) group, which was fed a standard chow diet purchased from Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bioengineering Co., Ltd. (Cat. No. XTI01WC-008) with a composition of ~ 12% kcal from fat, ~ 20% kcal from protein, and ~ 68% kcal from carbohydrates (this diet was not isocaloric or isocholesterolemic with the HFD), the HFD Group fed a high-fat diet purchased from the same company (Cat. No. XTS-60HF) with a composition of ~ 60% kcal from fat (mainly lard), ~ 20% kcal from protein, and ~ 20% kcal from carbohydrates (this diet was not isocaloric or isocholesterolemic with the standard chow diet), and the HI-L. rB16 group fed the same HFD as the HFD group (Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bioengineering Co., Ltd., Cat. No. XTS-60HF). During the 9-week experimental period, mice in the ND group received daily oral gavage with 0.2 mL of sterile normal saline, the HFD group received the same volume of sterile normal saline via daily oral gavage, and the HI-L. rB16 group received daily oral gavage with 0.2 mL of HI-L. rB16 suspension (concentration: 1.0 × 10⁹ CFU/mL). Body weights were recorded weekly. All animals were maintained under standard conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C, ad libitum access to food and water) throughout the study. At the end of the 9-week period, all subjects underwent glucose tolerance testing after an overnight fast, followed by sacrifice for sample collection [13]. The specific experimental designs are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Histology and physiological assays in mice

The body weights of mice were recorded weekly. The formula for calculating weight gain percentage is weight gain percentage = (weight on day n - weight on day 0) / weight on day 0 × 100%. Glucose tolerance tests were performed on mice fasted overnight with free access to water. The mice were injected with either 2 g/kg glucose (Sigma) into the peritoneal cavity. Glucose levels were measured from tail blood before and 30, 60, 90, or 120 min after the injection with the blood glucose meter (Sinocare). After glucose tolerance tests, all mice were sacrificed to collect their serum, liver, and intestine tissue were collected for various analyses. While the ileum and the rest liver tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded, and cut into sections for H&E staining and inspection with light microscope. Ileum lesion scores were semiquantitatively evaluated according to Chiu’s described [14]. Liver lesion scores were semiquantitatively evaluated according to Kleiner’s described [15]. Randomly select 3 sections of each sample and 3 trained observers (blinded to the experimental groups) examined 10 random fields (magnification 400×) in each section. The average value for each part is calculated by the average level.

Results

Basic morphology and species identification of L. rB16 in vitro

L. rB16 was isolated from the feces of healthy individuals, designated and preserved in our laboratory. The API 50CH biochemical reaction system identified the strain as Lactobacillus rhamnosus with a 99.9% identification rate and a T value of 0.3 (Fig. 1A). In addition, average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis of the gene sequence against the NCBI database demonstrated 98.34% identity with Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (ATCC 53103). For the morphological characterization of the Lactobacillus rhamnosus strain, MRS agar plate and blood agar plate for culture. The colony morphology on these plates was characterized as round with a smooth surface, neat edges, creamy white color, and a creamy odor (Fig. 1B). Additionally, Gram staining indicated that the strain is Gram-positive, presenting as short rods arranged singly, in pairs, or occasionally in chains (Fig. 1B). Collectively, these analyses confirmed the successful isolation and identification of a novel Lactobacillus rhamnosus strain.

Whole genome and proteomic sequencing analysis of L. rB16

We then performed genome-wide sequencing to further characterize L. rB16 strain. The genomic structure of the L. rB16 chromosome was illustrated in (Fig. 2A). The complete genome of the L. rB16 strain had 2,961,420 base pairs (bp), with an average GC content of 46.81%. Annotated analysis using multiple databases showed 3,171 protein-coding regions (CDSs), 106 structural RNAs, and no plasmids. Genes associated with antibiotic resistance, such as OXA-45, lmrB, and poxtA, were identified, but without mobile elements, indicating a low risk of horizontal transfer (Supplementary Table 3). Subsequent consultation with the latest versions of the NCBI RefSeq, CARD, and EFSA antibiotic resistance gene databases confirmed that OXA-45, lmrB, and poxtA have not been previously reported in Lactobacillus rhamnosus. The presence of these genes in the strain L. rB16 is a strain-specific phenomenon rather than a common background resistance profile, thereby supporting the safety of this strain with respect to potential transmissible antibiotic resistances. KEGG database annotations are shown in (Fig. 2B). The top three enriched KEGG pathways were ABC transporters, phosphotransferase system, and biosynthesis of amino acids. Of interest, several KEGG pathways associated with lipid metabolism were identified, including fatty acid metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, glycerolipid metabolism, and glycerophospholipid metabolism. Notably, KEGG annotation revealed that L. rB16 harbors key genes involved in acetic acid biosynthesis, including pyruvate dehydrogenase (pdh) and acetate kinase (ackA), which are enriched in pyruvate metabolism pathway—providing a genetic basis for its metabolic function in short-chain fatty acid production. In parallel, proteome sequencing was also performed to analyze the strains. The Venn diagram shows that there are 908 identical proteins in L. rB16 supernatant (L. rB16-SN), HI-L. rB16, and L. rB16, accounting for 94.28%, 80.14% and 78.81% of the total protein, respectively. There are 18, 40 and 24 unique proteins, accounting for 1.8%, 3.4% and 2.1% of the total protein in L. rB16-SN, HI-L. rB16, and L. rB16, respectively (Fig. 2C). Additionally, the eggNOG functional class annotation of L. rB16 are shown in (Fig. 2D). The most prominent COG functional annotations in L. rB16 are related to translation, ribosomal structure, and biogenesis, encompassing 73 associated proteins. Interestingly, 21 proteins in the L. rB16 strain are involved in lipid transport and metabolism. Collectively, these analyses revealed that L. rB16 is involved in lipid metabolism-related processes.

Biological characteristics of L. rB16

The in vitro dynamic growth curve showed that L. rB16 strain entered the logarithmic phase between 4 h and 16 h, followed by the stationary phase, with no apparent death phase observed (Fig. 3A). The survival rate of L. rB16 exceeded 70% in gastric fluids at pH = 3 and intestinal fluids at pH = 7 (Fig. 3B). Next, semi-quantitative assessment of biofilm formation indicated that the biofilm formation rate of L. rB16 strain was greater than 0.6, classifying it as a medium-strength adherent biofilm, with a chain-like and aggregated morphology (Fig. 3C). These results indicate that L. rB16 exhibits superior gastrointestinal colonization capacity, attributed to its robust tolerance to gastrointestinal fluids, enabling it to survive and maintain activity under adverse gastrointestinal conditions. Additionally, the cholesterol degradation rate of L. rB16 reached 53.53% after 48 h culture at the concentration of 1 × 109 CFU/mL (Fig. 3D).

Safety evaluation of L. rB16

Next, a hemolysis test and an animal acute toxicity test were employed to evaluate the safety of L. rB16. The hemolysis test demonstrated the absence of hemolytic halos, characterizing L. rB16 as non-hemolytic (γ-hemolysis) (Fig. 4A). For in vivo evaluation, mice received L. rB16 by gavage (1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL) for 7 or 30 days. During both periods, the animals maintained normal physiological conditions, with shiny fur, regular feces, and active behavior. No morphological abnormalities or tissue lesions were observed in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney. The body weight, weight gain, major organ indices, and blood glucose levels of mice were not significantly different compared with the control group (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4B-I). These results indicate that the strain poses no infection risk and is therefore safe and reliable for both short-term and long-term intake.

L. rB16 promotes cholesterol efflux from foam cells

Given the cholesterol-lowering effect of L. rB16 in vitro, we further evaluated the effects of L. rB16-SN, HI-L. rB16, and L. rB16 on reducing cholesterol levels in foam cells. The results showed that L. rB16-SN, HI-L. rB16, and L. rB16 all exhibited cholesterol-lowering abilities compared with the ox-LDL group and L. rB45 group (Fig. 5A-C). Due to the advantages of easy storage and transportation, stable quality, and high safety associated with inactivated probiotics, we selected HI-L. rB16 for further study. Next, to exclude the factors secreted by L. rB16 that could affect THP1 cell activity, we used the CCK-8 assay to detect the proliferation activity of THP-1 cells. L. rB16 did not alter the proliferation activity of THP-1 proliferation after incubation with THP-1 at MOI of 10− 3, 10− 1, 102, and 105 (Fig. 5D). Meanwhile, to investigate whether THP-1 cells affect the proliferation of L. rB16—given that macrophages can phagocytose bacteria—the XTT method was utilized to detect the proliferation activity of L. rB16. As shown in (Fig. 5E), after incubation with L. rB16 at MOI of 10− 3, 10− 1,102, and 105, THP-1 cells did not significantly affect the strain’s proliferation. Taken together, there was no interference between L. rB16 and THP-1 cell proliferation activity.

To determine the MOI range for the cholesterol-lowering effect of HI-L. rB16, we tested various MOI values in co-cultures of HI-L. rB16 and THP-1 cells. Results indicated that cholesterol levels in foam cells decreased when the MOI between HI-L. rB16 and THP-1 macrophages was from 1 to 103 (Fig. 5F). Moreover, we used L. rB16 with different MOI (10− 5, 10− 3, 1, and 103) to determine its effect on foam cells formation. As expected, L. rB16 significantly inhibited foam cell formation at MOI of 1 and 103, as evidenced by fewer red lipid droplets in the cytoplasm and a reduced number of foam cells (Fig. 5G). Additionally, to explore the mechanism of cholesterol reduction in foam cells, we analyzed the expression of proteins associated with the cholesterol metabolism signaling pathway. The expression of LXRα —a nuclear receptor transcription factor that regulates cholesterol efflux by promoting the expression of downstream target genes (e.g., ABCA1, ABCG1)—ABCG1, ABCA1, and HMGCR was upregulated, and the expression of SREBP1(sterol regulatory element binding protein) was downregulated in foam cells treated with HI-L. rB16 compared with the ox-LDL group (Fig. 5H). Taken together, L. rB16 promoted cholesterol efflux in foam cells, decreased intracellular cholesterol content, and inhibited foam cell formation.

HI-L. rB16 strains alleviate hyperlipidemia in mice induced by high-fat diet

Subsequently, we utilized a HFD-induced hyperlipidemic mouse model to verify the cholesterol-lowering effect of HI-L. rB16. Mice treated with HI-L. rB16 exhibited a lower weight growth curve and weight gain rate than the HFD group (Fig. 6A and B). To determine whether HI-L. rB16 can improve blood glucose metabolism in hyperlipidemia mice, an intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test was performed. The blood glucose of mice treated with HI-L. rB16 was lower than that of the HFD group but higher than that of the ND group at 120 min, suggesting that HI-L. rB16 can alleviate impaired glucose tolerance in hyperlipidemic mice (Fig. 6C). To determine the effect of HI-L. rB16 on serum lipid metabolism in hyperlipidemic mice, we measured serum lipids-related markers. The levels of serum TC, TG, and LDL-C decreased, while high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C) levels increased in mice treated with HI-L. rB16 compared with the HFD group (Fig. 6D-G), suggesting that HI-L. rB16 can alleviate hyperlipidemia in mice. To evaluate the effect of L. rB16 on hepatic oxidative stress in hyperlipidemic mice, we measured markers of oxidative stress in liver tissue. The levels of total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), glutathione (GSH), and catalase (CAT) were increased, whereas MDA levels decreased after treatment with HI-L. rB16 compared with the HFD group (Fig. 6H-K, Supplementary Table 4), suggesting that HI-L. rB16 can increase antioxidant capacity and decrease the level of lipid peroxidation in the liver of mice.

Blood lipid metabolism disorder can induce different degrees of fatty liver [16]. Thus, we further observed the morphological and histological changes in liver tissue in mice. After treatment with HI-L. rB16, the livers of hyperlipidemic mice showed no obvious hypertrophy, with soft textures, smooth sections, and no apparent steatosis. In addition, the liver index of mice decreased after treatment with HI-L. rB16 (Fig. 6L). Meanwhile, H & E staining revealed that hepatocytes from HFD-induced mice displayed irregular cell morphology, rough and loose nuclei, disordered arrangement, numerous deposited fat droplets, and pronounced steatosis (Fig. 6M). Notably, all these HFD-induced pathological changes in hepatocytes were attenuated by HI-L. rB16 treatment, as evidenced by more regular cellular morphology, more compact nuclei, neater arrangement, reduced fat droplet deposits, and alleviated steatosis (Fig. 6M). According to Kleiner’s liver steatosis grading system, the hepatocyte steatosis score of mice treated with HI-L. rB16 was lower than that of the HFD group (Fig. 6M). Taken together, these findings indicate that HI-L. rB16 can reduce fat accumulation and alleviate the progression of fatty liver. The ileum is the primary site where most probiotics colonize the host [17]. Our results showed that the ileal mucosa of HFD-induced mice underwent obvious histological changes, including incomplete glands, disrupted and lost intestinal villi, widened intercellular gaps, and detached and necrotic epithelial cells (Fig. 6N). Conversely, treatment with HI-L. rB16 significantly alleviated all these pathological changes in the ileal mucosa of HFD-induced mice (Fig. 6N). According to Chiu’s ileal injury system, the ileal injury score of mice treated with HI-L. rB16 was significantly decreased (Fig. 6N). These results suggest that HI-L. rB16 can alleviate ileal tissue injury in HFD-induced mice.

HI-L. rB16 regulates liver lipid metabolism

We then determined whether HI-L. rB16 treatment affected the expression of key enzymes in the liver lipid metabolism pathway. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses showed that HI-L. rB16 treatment significantly decreased the positive expression rate of HMGCR in the livers of HFD-induced mice (Fig. 7A), which indicates that HI-L. rB16 inhibits cholesterol synthesis. Meanwhile, HI-L. rB16 treatment significantly increased the positive expression rate of FXR in the livers of HFD-induced mice (Fig. 7A), suggesting its potential to reduce hepatic triglyceride and cholesterol levels. Furthermore, HI-L. rB16 treatment significantly increased the positive expression rates of CYP7A1 and SREBP1 in the livers of HFD-induced mice (Fig. 7A), indicating that HI-L. rB16 promotes cholesterol catabolism/bile acid synthesis and triglyceride metabolism, respectively. The effects of HI-L. rB16 treatment on the protein expression of these key lipid metabolism-related enzymes in the livers of HFD-induced mice were further confirmed by western blot analysis (Fig. 7B and C). Collectively, HI-L. rB16 treatment alleviates HFD-induced hepatic lipid metabolism dysregulation by inhibiting the expression of cholesterol-synthesizing enzymes and promoting the expression of enzymes involved in cholesterol catabolism pathways.

Short chain fatty acids produced by L. rB16 reduce cholesterol level

To evaluate Escherichia coli (E. coli) as a negative control for cholesterol reduction, we analyzed the effect of E. coli on cholesterol levels in foam cell models. As shown in (Fig. 8A), cholesterol levels remained unchanged in E. coli-treated foam cells compared with the ox-LDL group. Subsequently, we determined whether L. rB16 exerts cholesterol-lowering effects through the production of diverse metabolites. The identification results of differential metabolites of Lactobacillus rhamnosus are shown in (Fig. 8B). Additionally, KEGG database analysis revealed metabolic pathways and association networks linked to these metabolites. Differential metabolites produced by Lactobacillus rhamnosus were enriched in cholesterol metabolism, butanoate metabolism, citrate cycle, carbon metabolism, and other related pathways (Fig. 8C and D), suggesting that Lactobacillus rhamnosus may regulate lipid metabolism via the SCFAs pathway. In vitro fermentation of Lactobacillus rhamnosus primarily produces acetic acid, with small amounts of propionic acid, butyric acid, isobutyric acid, and isovaleric acid (Fig. 8E-I). The OPLS-DA score plot demonstrated that acetate plays a pivotal role in biological processes (Fig. 8J). In parallel, Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) quantification showed that acetic acid was the major SCFAs produced by HI-L. rB16, compared with Lactobacillus rhamnosus and E. coli (Fig. 8K). Acetic acid (C00033) was enriched in KEGG pathway, which is involved in carbon metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, propanoate metabolism, and phosphonate/phosphinate metabolism (Fig. 8L). To corroborate these findings, we determined the effect of acetate on cholesterol levels in foam cells and observed that cholesterol levels were reduced in sodium acetate-treated foam cells compared with the ox-LDL group (Fig. 8M). Moreover, sodium acetate treatment decreased intracellular lipid droplet formation and inhibited foam cell formation compared with the ox-LDL group (Fig. 8N and O). Together, these data demonstrate that the small molecule acetic acid produced by L. rB16 is responsible for reducing cholesterol levels in foam cells. Discussion

In this study, a novel Lactobacillus rhamnosus strain, L. rB16, was isolated from the healthy human feces samples and characterized using genomic and proteomic analyses. Safety evaluation showed that L. rB16 exhibited safety and reliability in vitro and in vivo, supporting its potential application value. Functionally, L. rB16 was found to significantly decrease cholesterol levels in cholesterol-enriched MRS broth. Given this phenomenon, we hypothesize that L. rB16 may play an important role in cholesterol metabolism. Mechanistically, we further demonstrated that L. rB16 can promote cholesterol efflux from THP-1 macrophage-derived foam cells and alleviate HFD-induced hyperlipidemia in mice. This dual effect is achieved by regulating the expression of key enzymes involved in lipid synthesis and catabolism, a process that is facilitated by the production of acetic acid—a metabolite of L. rB16.

Genomic characterization of probiotics is crucial to determine whether L. rB16 can be used for probiotic development [18]. This method fully reveals the genetic information of lactic acid bacteria, including Lactobacillus rhamnosus strains, systematically explains their physiological functions and metabolic mechanisms. In this study, whole-genome sequencing results showed that the genome size of L. rB16 was 2,961,420 bp, which contained 2738 predicted coding genes and a GC content of 46.81%. KEGG functional annotation revealed that L. rB16 encodes genes involved lipid metabolism pathways. To bridge the gap between genomic potential and functional expression, we performed proteomic analysis. This approach aimed to identify actively expressed proteins related to lipid transport and metabolism, providing direct evidence of their involvement in lipid metabolism modulation. The proteomic results confirmed that L. rB16 expresses proteins associated with lipid transport and metabolism, including those potentially involved in cholesterol interaction and bile acid modification. Together, the genomic and proteomic data demonstrate that L. rB16 has the functional capacity to regulate lipid metabolism, offering strong evidence for its probiotic potential and supporting further investigation into its physiological characteristics.

In terms of the physiological characteristics of probiotics, the L. rB16 was characterized by its tolerance to gastrointestinal fluids and robust biofilm-forming capacity. Indeed, these biological characteristics suggest that this strain might be capable of tolerating the harsh gastrointestinal environment, enhancing colonization capacity, and strengthening probiotic effects in the intestine [9, 19]. In addition, we demonstrated that L. rB16 has obvious cholesterol-lowering ability in cholesterol-enriched MRS broth in vitro, with a cholesterol degradation rate of 53.53%.

By using foam cells as a valuable in vitro model to study the regulation of cholesterol metabolism, we demonstrate that three components of L. rB16, including supernatant, inactivated bacteria, and live bacteria, exert distinct effects on reducing intracellular cholesterol levels, suggesting that L. rB16 possesses potent cholesterol-lowering ability. Inactivated probiotics offer several advantages over live strains [20]. They are less susceptible to temperature variations, easier to store and transport, exhibit stable quality, and ensure safety and reliability [21]. More significantly, they display enhanced resistance to gastric juice, bile, and digestive enzymes [22]. Therefore, inactivated probiotics exert more pronounced beneficial effects on human health than live strains. We then explored the potential mechanisms by which HI-L. rB16 reduces intracellular cholesterol content and foam cell formation. On the one hand, our results showed that HI-L. rB16 significantly suppresses the expression of key enzymes, involved in cholesterol synthesis in foam cells. On the other hand, we found that HI-L. rB16 can upregulate the expression of LXRα, ABCA1, and ABCG1 proteins, suggesting that HI-L. rB16 promotes the expression of transporters involved in cholesterol efflux through activation of the LXRα signaling pathway. These results are consistent with findings from a previous study by Yoon et al. [23], who demonstrated that Lactobacillus rhamnosus BFE5264 reduces intracellular cholesterol content by increasing the protein expression of cholesterol efflux transporters. Nevertheless, the specific mechanisms by which HI-L. rB16 affects cholesterol synthesis and metabolism in foam cells warrant further investigation.

It is generally accepted that regulation of the two arms of cholesterol metabolism—synthesis and catabolism—constitutes one of the main mechanisms by which probiotics contribute to maintaining a balanced lipid metabolism [24, 25]. In this study, we showed that HI-L. rB16 treatment significantly decreased the expression level of a key enzyme involved in cholesterol synthesis (HMGCR) and concomitantly increased the expression level of two key enzymes involved in cholesterol catabolism (FXR and CYP7A1) in livers of HFD-induced mice, suggesting that HI-L. rB16 could regulate the lipid metabolic balance in mouse livers by downregulating the cholesterol synthesis pathway and upregulating the cholesterol catabolic pathway. Moreover, SREBP1 also plays an important role in regulating the lipid metabolic balance [26]. For instance, Zhang et al. showed that downregulation of SREBP1 expression in mouse liver tissue led to a decrease in serum cholesterol and triglyceride levels after simvastatin treatment in hyperlipidemic mice [13]. In contrast, Ye et al. reported that LXRα or RXR significantly upregulated the transcription of SREBP-1c, which subsequently facilitates the decomposition of triglycerides into fatty acids under cholesterol overload [27]. These findings suggest that SREBP1 plays a complex role in regulating lipid metabolism, while its own expression is regulated by multiple hormones and nutrients [28]. Herein, we found that HI-L. rB16 treatment upregulated the expression of SREBP1 protein in the mouse livers, suggesting that HI-L. rB16 may promote triglyceride catabolism by influencing LXRα/SREBP1 expression. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the mechanisms by which HI-L. rB16 reduces hyperlipidemia in HFD-induced mice.

Previous studies have indicated that probiotics regulate host lipid metabolism possibly by their production of various metabolites [29]. Among these metabolites, SCFAs—particularly acetic acid—have been identified as pivotal regulators of lipid metabolism [30, 31]. In the present study, in vitro fermentation assays demonstrated that L. rB16 produces SCFAs, with acetic acid as the dominant metabolite. Genomic analysis revealed that L. rB16 harbors key genes involved in acetic acid biosynthesis, including pyruvate dehydrogenase (pdh) and acetate kinase (ackA), which are enriched in KEGG pathways related to pyruvate metabolism—providing a genetic basis for its high acetic acid production. Metabolomic profiling further confirmed that acetic acid was significantly enriched in pathways such as pyruvate, propanoate, phosphonate, and phosphinate metabolisms, consistent with its role as a central metabolic intermediate. As a central metabolic intermediate, acetic acid can be converted intracellularly to acetyl-CoA, a key molecule in cholesterol and lipid metabolism [32]. Notably, LXRα functions as a cholesterol sensor capable of detecting changes in intracellular cholesterol levels [33]. Enhanced acetic acid metabolism may thus indirectly activate LXR signaling pathways by influencing cholesterol synthesis or the production of oxysterols [34–36]. This mechanism aligns with our observation that HI-L. rB16-derived acetic acid upregulates LXRα and its downstream targets (ABCA1/ABCG1) in foam cells, establishing a metabolic link between acetic acid flux and LXRα-mediated lipid regulation.

The regulation of lipid metabolism may be a process synergistically regulated by multiple metabolites and signaling pathways [37]. In this study, acetic acid is the primary SCFAs produced by L. rB16, but our metabolomic analysis also detected measurable amounts of propionate and butyrate in L. rB16 cultures, indicating these metabolites may also participate in lipid metabolism regulation. For instance, propionate has been reported to inhibit lipid accumulation by activating the GPR41/GPR43 signaling pathway, thereby ameliorating lipid metabolism disorders [38]. Similarly, studies have shown that fermented yogurt containing Lactobacillus rhamnosus HF01 enhances intestinal butyrate levels to activate the AMPK signaling pathway, which subsequently inhibits de novo lipogenesis and promotes the expression of fatty acid oxidation-related genes [39]. Therefore, L. rB16 may also exert lipid metabolism-modulating functions by activating other signaling pathways via these secondary metabolites. Future studies will be necessary to further dissect the potential roles of these secondary metabolite-associated signaling pathways in lipid metabolism regulation, which will help to clarify the comprehensive regulatory network of L. rB16 in lipid homeostasis.

In recent years, the application of Lactobacillus rhamnosus in clinical treatment has attracted more and more attention [40]. Consequently, the clinical translation potential of L. rB16 requires addressing several critical considerations based on our findings. Regarding dosage equivalence, our in vivo studies used 1 × 10⁹ CFU/day of HI-L. rB16 in mice, approximating a human equivalent dose of 5 × 1010–1 × 1011 CFU/day based on body surface area conversion, aligning with commercial probiotic dosages [41]. However, dose-escalation trials are needed to confirm optimal human dosages due to interspecies differences. Turning to formulation challenges, the primary focus centers on enhancing gastrointestinal stability and colonization [42]. Our data show L. rB16 exhibits moderate biofilm-forming ability (adhesion rate >0.6) and >70% survival in simulated gastric (pH = 3) and intestinal (pH = 7.8) fluids, indicating inherent tolerability. To further improve delivery efficiency, microencapsulation strategies may be explored to protect L. rB16 from gastric acid degradation and ensure targeted release in the ileum [43]. Importantly, the current study has limitations: validation was confined to a HFD-induced hyperlipidemia model in C57BL/6J mice, with no assessment conducted in models better recapitulating human disease complexity, and potential interactions with the diverse human gut microbiota, which could significantly influence efficacy, were not investigated. Furthermore, long-term safety, particularly concerning chromosomally encoded antibiotic resistance genes (OXA-45, lmrB, poxtA), requires extended clinical validation despite the absence of adverse effects in 30-day mouse toxicity studies. Therefore, addressing these interconnected factors—optimizing human dosage, overcoming formulation challenges (potentially via microencapsulation), rigorously evaluating long-term antibiotic resistance risks, and expanding validation beyond current limitations—is crucial for the successful therapeutic translation of HI-L. rB16.

Conclusions

In summary, our work reveals that L. rB16 has good biofilm-forming capacity, gastrointestinal fluid tolerance, and cholesterol-lowering ability in vitro. Furthermore, we establish L. rB16 can regulate cholesterol metabolism in foam cells through the LXRα signaling pathway. In addition, in HFD-induced hyperlipidemic mice, L. rB16 significantly ameliorates histopathological abnormalities by restoring lipid metabolic balance. Mechanistically, L. rB16 regulates the lipid metabolism balance in foam cells and alleviates hyperlipidemia in HFD mice through the production of its metabolite, acetic acid. These findings further support the important role of probiotics in regulating lipid metabolism and highlight the potential application of L. rB16 as a novel probiotic for the treatment of lipid metabolism disorders.
