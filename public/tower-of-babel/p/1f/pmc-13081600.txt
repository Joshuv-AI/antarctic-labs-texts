Varicella zoster virus–related myelitis: a case series and literature review
Authors: Zhijiao Song, Ruohao Li, Lantao Liang, Meiling Zhang, Hongjuan Wang, Hui Bu, Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionVaricella zoster virus (VZV) is a human neurotropic herpesvirus that remains latent in the dorsal root ganglia and can reactivate to cause herpes zoster. In immunocompromised patients, reactivation may lead to severe neurological complications such as encephalitis, meningitis, myelitis, and neuropathy. However, varicella zoster virus–related myelitis (VZVM) is relatively rare, particularly in immunocompetent adults. The pathogenesis may involve direct viral invasion of the spinal cord parenchyma during the acute phase or a postinfectious immune-mediated inflammatory response.Case presentationsThis report describes three patients with VZVM(one woman ages 49 years and two men age 60 and 56 years), none of whom presented with a typical rash. Two patients (cases 1 and 2) developed encephalomyelitis at disease onset, characterized by fever, impaired consciousness, and long-segment spinal cord lesions. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) detected VZV nucleic acid in both cases. In case 3, the patient initially developed VZVM complicated by cerebral venous sinus thrombosis. After an interval of approximately 40 days, delayed thoracic myelitis developed, and repeated CSF mNGS testing yielded negative results. All three patients received intravenous antiviral therapy; two additionally received low-dose corticosteroids combined with intravenous immunoglobulin, and one received intravenous methylprednisolone. During follow-up, one patient achieved full recovery of lower-limb motor function, whereas two patients remained paraplegic.ConclusionsVZVM presents with diverse clinical manifestations and may occur without the typical vesicular rash. It can develop either during the initial phase of VZV infection or as a delayed complication. Early magnetic resonance imaging and CSF molecular testing support timely diagnosis. Prompt and adequate antiviral therapy combined with immunomodulatory treatment may improve neurological outcomes.

Introduction

Varicella zoster virus (VZV) is a double-stranded DNA α-herpesvirus that is pathogenic to humans. Primary infection causes varicella (chickenpox), which typically occurs in children [1]. After the initial infection, the virus establishes latency in neurons of various ganglia, including the dorsal root ganglia, trigeminal ganglia, and neurons within the enteric nervous system [2, 3]. Years later, VZV may reactivate spontaneously or in response to specific triggers, leading to herpes zoster [4]. The incidence of herpes zoster ranges from 3 to 5 per 1,000 person-years in the global population [5]and from 3 to 10 per 1,000 person-years in Asia-Pacific countries [6–8], with an annual increase of 2.5% to 5.0% [6, 9]. Its incidence and severity substantially increase with advancing age, largely because of age-related declines in cell-mediated immunity to VZV. Reactivation can also be associated with immunodeficiency, including primary or secondary immune disorders [10]. Defects in T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells, as well as conditions such as malignancies, chemotherapy, organ transplantation, and use of immunosuppressive medications, can predispose individuals to VZV reactivation [4, 5, 11].

The reactivation of VZV can cause a range of neurological complications, most commonly meningitis, encephalitis, vasculitis, cranial nerve syndromes, and Guillain-Barré syndrome [4]. Varicella zoster virus–related myelitis (VZVM) is a rare complication of VZV infection [12, 13], with an incidence of 0.3% [12], but it carries a poor prognosis. VZVM typically manifests weeks to months after herpes zoster rash onset and occurs more frequently in immunocompromised individuals, although a small number of cases have been identified in immunocompetent patients [13–15]. Its pathogenesis remains uncertain. Proposed mechanisms include direct viral invasion of the spinal cord or an infection-induced autoimmune response [16–19]. VZVM often begins with a skin rash, followed by spinal cord dysfunction within days to weeks. In some cases, there is no typical rash, and spinal cord symptoms constitute the first manifestation. Clinical features include asymmetric paraparesis, sensory disturbances below the affected spinal cord level, and sphincter dysfunction [20]; lower-limb involvement is particularly common. Severe myelitis can cause partial Brown-Séquard syndrome or even transverse myelitis [21]. Atypical presentations [19] include the absence of herpes zoster before myelopathy onset, zoster sine herpete, the appearance of skin lesions after myelopathy onset, and a lack of anatomical correlation between myelopathy and rash distribution—patterns more frequently observed in immunocompromised patients.

Here, we describe three cases of VZVM. One patient had an underlying immunodeficiency; two patients exhibited concurrent encephalitis, and one presented with meningitis. None developed herpetic skin lesions, a pattern that is relatively uncommon in the literature. By analyzing the clinical characteristics, disease course, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) profiles, and treatments of each patient, we aim to provide practical references for the diagnosis and management of VZVM.

Case Presentations

Case 1

A 60-year-old man with a history of hypertension and diabetes was admitted for a 6-day interval of recurrent convulsions accompanied by bilateral lower-limb weakness. Six days before admission, he developed convulsions characterized by upper-limb flexion, fixed gaze, loss of consciousness, and frothing at the mouth, each episode lasting several tens of seconds before resolving. His consciousness remained impaired, and weakness in both lower limbs persisted. He subsequently developed fever, with a maximum temperature of 38.5 °C, and urinary retention that required placement of an indwelling catheter. He sought evaluation at a local hospital, where lumbar puncture showed CSF white blood cell (WBC) count of 1,120 × 106/L, predominantly mononuclear, and substantially elevated protein (5.75 g/L), the CSF glucose concentration was 6.52 mmol/L. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) testing of CSF revealed VZV positivity, no bacteria, mycobacterium tuberculosis, or fungi were detected. Autoimmune encephalitis and paraneoplastic antibody panels in both serum and CSF showed negative results. Viral encephalitis was suspected, and he received acyclovir (30 mg/kg/day, intravenous [IV]), intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (0.4 g/kg/day), and intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP) (1,000 mg/day) for 5 days. His level of consciousness improved, but bilateral lower-limb weakness persisted; thus, he was transferred to our hospital for further management. Neurologic examination showed reduced responsiveness and distal weakness (0/5 Medical Research Council [MRC] grade) in both lower limbs. Pinprick and temperature sensation were diminished bilaterally to the T12 level. Bilateral Babinski signs and nuchal rigidity were present. Laboratory and imaging studies revealed the following: contrast-enhanced brain MRI demonstrated patchy T1 high/low and T2/fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) high/low signal intensities in both temporal poles and the left cerebellar hemisphere, along with peripheral patchy FLAIR hyperintensity and mild heterogeneous ring enhancement (Fig. 1). These findings were suggestive of infection with associated hemorrhage. A filling defect in the left transverse sinus raised concern for cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST). Spinal MRI showed linear and patchy T2 fat-suppressed hyperintensities within the medulla oblongata, cervical spinal cord, and thoracic spinal cord from T2 to T10, most prominent at T9–T10 (Fig. 1). On the second day of admission, lumbar puncture showed an opening pressure of 155 mmH2O, CSF WBC count of 190 × 106/L (predominantly mononuclear), and CSF protein of 3.17 g/L, the ratio of CSF glucose to serum glucose was elevated at 0.98. Based on the clinical presentation and diagnostic findings, VZV infection causing encephalitis with associated myelitis was considered. During hospitalization, treatment included continued IV acyclovir, transition to low-dose corticosteroids (IVMP, 40 mg/day) for anti-inflammatory effect, and a second course of IVIG for immunomodulation. Additional management included measures to reduce intracranial pressure, valproate sodium for seizure control, and symptomatic supportive care. The lower-limb weakness slightly improved (1/5 MRC grade) compared with admission. At the 2-month follow-up, no further meaningful improvement in lower-limb strength was observed.

Case 2

A 56-year-old man with a history of myelofibrosis and chronic hepatitis B developed fever 10 days before admission, with a maximum temperature of 38.5 °C accompanied by chest and back pain. Four days before admission, he developed a persistent, progressively worsening severe headache. He was evaluated at a local hospital and received anti-infective therapy without meaningful improvement. One day before admission, he developed bilateral lower-limb weakness (0/5 MRC grade), inability to walk, and urinary dysfunction, prompting presentation to our emergency department. Thoracic spinal MRI showed strip-like T2 fat-suppressed hyperintensities within the thoracic cord from T3 to T11, suggestive of inflammation or demyelination (Fig. 2). Half a day before admission, he developed confusion, intermittent restlessness, auditory and visual hallucinations, and involuntary upper-limb movements, which led to urgent hospitalization. Neurologic examination showed impaired consciousness, restlessness, and dysarthria. Motor testing revealed complete paralysis (0/5 MRC grade) in both lower limbs. Pinprick, temperature, proprioception, and vibration sensations were diminished bilaterally to the T10 level. Nuchal rigidity was present. Laboratory and imaging studies revealed the following: contrast-enhanced brain MRI showed small patchy T1 isointense to hypointense and T2/FLAIR hyperintense signals in the bilateral frontotemporal lobes and right insular cortex, with patchy enhancement (Fig. 2). Mild thickening and enhancement of the adjacent meninges supported a diagnosis of meningoencephalitis. A diffusion-weighted imaging hyperintense lesion along the right margin of the pons suggested an acute or subacute infarct. Complete blood count and serum biochemistry findings were largely unremarkable. Serum interleukin-6 was 17.79 pg/mL (reference: 0–7 pg/mL), and C-reactive protein was 33.5 mg/L (reference: 0–8 mg/L). On the second day of admission, lumbar puncture revealed an opening pressure of 95 mmH2O. The CSF was pale yellow and clear, with a WBC count of 923 × 106/L (predominantly mononuclear) and substantially elevated protein (4.59 g/L), the ratio of CSF glucose to serum glucose was elevated at 0.92. mNGS detected VZV, no bacteria, mycobacterium tuberculosis, or fungi were detected. Based on the clinical presentation and laboratory findings, VZV infection causing meningitis with acute transverse myelitis was considered. After admission, the patient began receiving IV acyclovir (30 mg/kg/day), IVIG (0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days), and low-dose corticosteroids (IVMP, 60 mg/day), as well as additional therapy to improve circulation. On hospital day 11, repeat lumbar puncture revealed substantial reductions in CSF WBC count and protein. His condition showed mild improvement: body temperature normalized, and psychiatric symptoms lessened, with residual mild drowsiness and dysarthria. However, lower-limb strength did not improve. Half a month after discharge, his consciousness had largely returned to normal. At the 2-month follow-up, his symptoms showed no further clinically significant change. Repeat contrast-enhanced brain MRI demonstrated pronounced reduction or partial resolution of the previous lesions in the bilateral frontotemporal lobes and right insula, with considerable improvement in adjacent meningeal thickening and enhancement. Thoracic spinal MRI showed inflammatory or demyelinating changes from T3 to T8, with a slight reduction in lesion extent (Fig. 2).

Case 3

A 49-year-old woman with no significant medical history was admitted for evaluation of headache. Seven days prior to admission, she developed a severe, sharp, unbearable headache after an upper respiratory infection, accompanied by nausea and vomiting. She presented to our outpatient clinic, where contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance venography revealed a localized filling defect in the left transverse sinus suggestive of thrombosis (Fig. 3), prompting hospital admission. Neurologic examination showed no clinically significant abnormalities. Laboratory and imaging studies after admission showed the following: brain MRI demonstrated punctate T1 isointense and T2/FLAIR hyperintense lesions in the right frontal and temporal lobes, which were interpreted as nonspecific white matter changes. Magnetic resonance angiography revealed findings consistent with arteriosclerosis, and an aneurysm was identified in the C6 segment of the left internal carotid artery. Complete blood count, liver and kidney function tests, electrolytes, lipid profile, and coagulation studies were largely normal. Lumbar puncture on the day of admission showed an opening pressure of 210 mmH2O. The CSF was pale yellow and clear, with a WBC count of 330 × 106/L (predominantly mononuclear) and elevated protein (1.89 g/L), the ratio of CSF glucose to serum glucose was elevated at 0.58. mNGS detected VZV, no bacteria, mycobacterium tuberculosis, or fungi were detected. Based on the clinical presentation and diagnostic findings, VZV encephalitis with CVST was considered. Treatment was initiated with IV acyclovir (30 mg/kg/day) and subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin (5,000 IU/day) for anticoagulation. One week after admission, her headache had substantially improved, and she was discharged. Twenty days after discharge, follow-up brain MRI and contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance venography showed a substantial reduction in the filling defect within the left transverse sinus (Fig. 3). Repeat lumbar puncture demonstrated considerable decreases in CSF WBC and protein. Forty days after discharge, the patient developed bilateral lower-limb weakness accompanied by numbness in the waist, abdomen, and lower limbs, resulting in inability to walk independently, although bowel and bladder function remained intact. Physical examination revealed muscle strength of 4/5 (MRC grade) in both lower limbs. Pinprick and temperature sensation were decreased below the T10 level on the left; pinprick, temperature, proprioception, and vibration were decreased below the T6 level on the right. The heel-to-shin test was unsteady on the right, and the right Babinski sign was positive. Thoracolumbar spinal MRI showed a linear T2 hyperintensity within the spinal cord at the T3–T5 levels, with mild enhancement (Fig. 3). Lumbar puncture revealed an opening pressure of 150 mmH2O. The CSF was pale yellow and clear, with a WBC count of 55 × 106/L (predominantly mononuclear) and elevated protein (1.44 g/L), the ratio of CSF glucose to serum glucose was elevated at 0.73. mNGS findings were negative. CSF and serum tests for demyelinating antibodies and oligoclonal bands also showed negative results. Myelitis was considered. The patient received IVMP (1,000 mg/day) for 5 days. After 3 weeks, muscle strength in both lower limbs slightly improved to 5−/5 (MRC grade), and she was discharged. At the 2-month follow-up, muscle strength in all four limbs had fully recovered, and sensory function was normal.

Discussion

VZV infection involving the central nervous system (CNS) can lead to a variety of neurological disorders [22, 23], most commonly encephalitis and meningitis; presentations as myelitis are relatively rare, particularly in immunocompetent adults. VZV can remain latent in the spinal cord or dorsal root ganglia. Upon reactivation, it may spread outward to produce cutaneous herpes zoster or inward to cause myelitis. Clinical features, prognosis, and disease patterns vary among patients; severe cases may progress to transverse myelitis [21]. Since the first description of acute transverse myelitis by Ben-Amoret in 1876 [24], studies have shown that VZV-induced transverse myelitis occurs in fewer than 1% of VZV reactivation cases [19, 25, 26]. The typical presentation of VZV-induced transverse myelitis is one of rapidly progressive motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunction [27]. Given its aggressive clinical course—which may lead to paralysis in up to 80% of cases and can result in acute organ failure or death [28, 29]—prompt recognition and diagnosis are critical. Longitudinal extensive transverse myelitis [30], a rare form of acute transverse myelitis characterized by contiguous lesions spanning three or more vertebral segments, is notable for its acute onset, catastrophic progression, and poor prognosis. It predominantly occurs in immunocompromised patients [31], although rare cases have been documented in immunocompetent individuals [18, 24, 25, 32, 33]. Furthermore, distinct CNS complications of VZV reactivation can occur simultaneously. In a case series of 26 CNS VZV infections [34], two patients exhibited acute disseminated encephalomyelitis.

VZVM pathogenesis remains incompletely understood. Proposed mechanisms primarily involve two pathways: direct viral invasion of the spinal cord parenchyma, leading to inflammation and necrosis, or an immune-mediated inflammatory response triggered by viral reactivation. Autopsy findings demonstrating Cowdry A intranuclear inclusion bodies and positive VZV antigen immunostaining in spinal root ganglia support the direct viral invasion hypothesis [18, 19, 35]. Along with this mechanism, vascular [36]and autoimmune processes [37] have been suggested, although their contributions may be more limited. Potential postinfectious autoimmune responses may include molecular mimicry between infectious agents and CNS antigens or hypersensitivity-related inflammatory reactions [38]. Regarding the vascular hypothesis, some reports describe imaging features suggestive of vascular involvement [19, 39]. The direct viral mechanism often coincides with encephalitis and detectable CSF viral nucleic acid during the acute phase; the immune-mediated mechanism may manifest after CSF viral nucleic acid becomes undetectable. This distinction has implications for treatment: the former requires adequate antiviral dosing and duration, whereas the latter warrants additional immunosuppressive therapy. Laboratory diagnosis typically incorporates serum markers (VZV IgG and IgM) and CSF markers (VZV IgG and/or VZV DNA) [40, 41], combined with clinical presentation and imaging findings. Differential diagnoses include other viral myelitides and autoimmune demyelinating diseases such as neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, multiple sclerosis, and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody–associated disease.

In this case series, all three patients were diagnosed with VZVM via mNGS, highlighting the significant diagnostic utility of this approach for infectious diseases. However, this technique also has inherent limitations. mNGS represents a transformative, hypothesis-free approach for infectious disease diagnostics, enabling comprehensive detection and genomic characterization of bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites directly from diverse clinical samples. As a universal pathogen detection method, mNGS offers distinct advantages over conventional techniques by identifying novel, fastidious, and polymicrobial infections, as well as profiling antimicrobial resistance genes—proving particularly valuable in complex clinical scenarios such as immunocompromised hosts, sepsis, and culture-negative cases. Compared to the conventional CSF VZV-IgM antibody assay, mNGS demonstrates a significant diagnostic advantage. A recent head-to-head comparative study [42] revealed that among patients with suspected VZV CNS infections, the pathogen detection rate of CSF mNGS (51.6%) substantially exceeded that of CSF VZV-IgM testing (16.1%) and certain VZV-specific PCR protocols, indicating a statistically significant improvement in sensitivity. Nevertheless, serum anti-VZV IgM and IgG testing can provide supportive evidence and help assess the immune response phase. Serodiagnosis may be useful to confirm the diagnosis retrospectively. However, mNGS’ s integration into routine microbiology practice remains constrained by several challenges, including difficulties in distinguishing colonization from true infection, interference from extraneous nucleic acids, lack of standardized protocols, and complexities in data management, analysis, and interpretation. As mNGS assays become increasingly available in clinical and commercial laboratories, a clear understanding of both its capabilities and limitations is essential for optimizing its diagnostic utility [43, 44].

This study describes three cases of VZVM, each illustrating distinct clinical and mechanistic profiles. None presented with typical herpetic rash, reinforcing the notion that VZV CNS infection should remain on the differential diagnosis even in the absence of cutaneous manifestations. In two patients (cases 1 and 2), myelitis occurred concurrently with encephalitic features during the acute phase, and CSF mNGS readily detected VZV nucleic acids. Both exhibited longitudinally extensive spinal cord lesions on MRI, a pattern previously associated with VZV‑mediated direct viral invasion. The presence of high CSF protein and lymphocytic pleocytosis further supported active neuroparenchymal infection. These observations underscore the high sensitivity of mNGS during acute viral replication and its utility in establishing early etiological diagnosis. In contrast, case 3 developed delayed‑onset myelitis approximately 40 days after the initial VZV encephalitis, at which point CSF mNGS was negative. The temporal separation, the absence of detectable virus, and the lack of response to antivirals argue against ongoing viral replication. Instead, the clinical course aligns with a postinfectious, immune‑mediated mechanism, analogous to VZV vasculopathy or acute disseminated encephalomyelitis. Negative testing for demyelinating antibodies and oligoclonal bands further supports this hypothesis.These cases highlight a dual pathogenic spectrum of VZV myelitis: direct viral cytopathic injury during the acute phase and secondary autoimmune demyelination in the postinfectious setting. This distinction carries therapeutic implications—antiviral agents are essential in the former, whereas immunomodulatory therapy may be considered in the latter. Moreover, our experience emphasizes that CSF mNGS is a powerful tool for early diagnosis; a negative result does not definitively exclude VZV‑related etiology in late‑onset neurological complications.

The optimal therapeutic approach for VZVM has not been standardized. Most patients receive antiviral agents (e.g., acyclovir) along with corticosteroids [45], and outcomes are variable. Because corticosteroids have limited benefit in certain cases, some clinicians have used plasma exchange, particularly when an autoimmune mechanism is suspected [24]. Furthermore, acyclovir-resistant VZV strains have been identified in immunocompromised individuals (e.g., those with human immunodeficiency virus), which may explain the poor response to antiviral therapy in some patients [46]. All three patients in this report received antiviral therapy. In cases 1 and 2, patients were treated with low-dose corticosteroids combined with IVIG. The patient in case 3 received IVMP when delayed thoracic myelitis developed. The final outcomes differed, likely due to variations in pathogenesis and severity of spinal cord injury. Cases 1 and 2, which were considered to involve direct viral invasion with extensive longitudinal spinal cord involvement and more severe transverse injury than case 3, had poor outcomes: at the 2-month follow-up, lower-limb strength had not improved, and paraplegia persisted. The outcome in case 3, considered an instance of immune-mediated delayed myelitis with a limited lesion spanning only three segments and primarily involving gray matter, was far better. At the 2-month follow-up, the patient had essentially recovered fully, with normal muscle strength and intact sensory function.The distinction between direct viral and postinfectious immune‑mediated myelitis is not merely mechanistic—it carries direct therapeutic implications, as illustrated by the contrasting outcomes of our three cases. For acute VZV myelitis with confirmed viral replication (cases 1 and 2), timely and adequate antiviral therapy remains the absolute cornerstone. Adjunctive IVIG and low‑dose corticosteroids were also administered in an attempt to modulate excessive neuroinflammation. Despite this, both patients remained paraplegic at 2 month follow‑up. This observation might suggest that once extensive viral‑induced necrotising myelitis is established, immunomodulation alone cannot reverse structural cord damage. IVIG, in this context, did not compensate for the severity of the initial insult, nor should it be viewed as a substitute for early, high‑dose antiviral therapy.In contrast, for delayed immune-mediated VZVM (case 3), the clinical course was consistent with a postinfectious immune‑mediated process. No IVIG was administered; instead, the patient received IVMP followed by an oral taper. Her recovery was nearly complete at 2 month follow‑up. This favourable outcome supports the hypothesis that immune‑mediated VZVM is primarily responsive to immunosuppressive therapy. We acknowledge that these observations derive from a small number of cases and lack controlled comparison. Larger studies are needed to validate this mechanistic–therapeutic correlation. Nevertheless, the sharp contrast in outcomes between our patients offers a clinically actionable lesson: distinguishing the underlying pathogenesis at presentation can directly inform treatment selection and prognostic counselling.

We acknowledge the relatively short follow-up period as a primary limitation of this study. Longer-term observation is warranted to evaluate neurological function outcomes. Although this case series is limited in number and lacks uniform long-term follow-up, the findings indicate that VZV infection should be considered when evaluating acute or subacute spinal cord lesions, even in the absence of rash. Early lumbar puncture for viral nucleic acid testing and timely spinal MRI can facilitate prompt identification of the underlying cause. For cases of VZVM with different etiologies, careful antiviral and immunomodulatory therapy selection may help improve neurological outcomes.

Conclusions

VZVM demonstrates substantial clinical heterogeneity and can occur in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised adults. Some patients do not exhibit typical cutaneous manifestations; the disease may arise either during acute VZV infection or several weeks after onset. MRI frequently shows longitudinally extensive lesions involving the cervical or thoracic spinal cord. CSF molecular testing plays a crucial role in early diagnosis. Differences in pathogenesis—direct viral invasion versus immune-mediated mechanisms—are linked to distinct clinical courses and treatment responses, indicating that optimal management combines adequate antiviral therapy with appropriate immunomodulation. Early recognition and intervention may improve neurological outcomes, although some patients may continue to experience residual functional impairment.
