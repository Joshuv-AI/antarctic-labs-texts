SPRY1 suppresses microglial fatty acid oxidation by retaining FATP3 on the endoplasmic reticulum in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Yu-Ge Zhang, Shi-Yao Li, Yi-Zhen Hu, Yi Ding, Yu-Tong Zhang, Hui-Wen Zhang, Yong-Jie Zhang, Lu Xu, Zhi-Yuan Zhang, Lei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMicroglial metabolic dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a key driver of neuroinflammation in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). However, the specific metabolic regulators linking lipid metabolism to pathological activation remain poorly defined.MethodsWe performed integrative transcriptomic analysis on four human AD brain datasets to identify core metabolic hubs. Sprouty homolog 1 (SPRY1) expression was examined in human post-mortem tissues, and its functional role was validated in primary microglia, Drosophila, and APP/PS1-21 mice using genetic knockdown, behavioral assays, and histological analysis. Moreover, we employed co-immunoprecipitation, mass spectrometry, and metabolic flux assays to elucidate the underlying pathways.ResultsWe identified SPRY1 as a key hub gene upregulated in the hippocampus of AD patients and mouse models. SPRY1 was predominantly expressed in plaque-associated microglia, and specifically correlated with advancing age, Aβ load and Braak stage. Functionally, microglial SPRY1 knockdown enhanced Aβ phagocytosis, reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines, and protected neurons from Aβ-induced damage in vitro. In vivo, glial-specific knockdown of the SPRY1 homolog sty rescued cognitive and motor deficits in AD flies, while microglia-specific knockdown in mice reduced amyloidosis and neuroinflammation. Mechanistically, SPRY1 acted as a metabolic switch by retaining fatty acid transport protein 3 (FATP3) on the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). This interaction blocked FATP3 translocation to mitochondria, thereby reducing fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and promoting lipid droplet (LD) accumulation, which drives a microglial pro-inflammatory phenotype.ConclusionsThis study unveils a novel SPRY1-FATP3 retention mechanism that links impaired fatty acid metabolism to neuroinflammation. Targeting this axis represents a promising strategy to reprogram microglial metabolism and provide neuroprotection in AD.Graphical Abstract In AD microglia, elevated SPRY1 levels and enhanced SPRY1-FATP3 interaction reduce mitochondrial FATP3 localization, leading to decreased FAO, LD accumulation, and impaired phagocytic activity, while exacerbating inflammatory responses. SPRY1 knockdown restores FATP3 mitochondrial localization, FAO levels, and phagocytic activity while reducing inflammation, thereby improving Aβ pathology in APP/PS1-21 mice. Consistently, sty knockdown in Drosophila rescues behavioral deficits in an Aβ model. Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, characterized by progressive cognitive decline, the deposition of beta-amyloid (Aβ) plaques, neurofibrillary tangles, and chronic neuroinflammation [1, 2]. Despite decades of research, the complex pathological mechanisms underlying AD progression remain incompletely elucidated, and no curative therapies are currently available. Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of metabolic dysfunction in AD pathogenesis, particularly in microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS) that orchestrate neuroinflammation, Aβ clearance, and neuronal homeostasis [3, 4]. Microglial metabolic reprogramming, such as impaired energy metabolism, disrupted lipid homeostasis, and altered mitochondrial function, has been identified as a key driver of microglial overactivation, neuroinflammation, and neurodegeneration in AD [5]. However, the key metabolic regulators that mediate microglial dysfunction during AD progression remain to be fully characterized.

Metabolic genes serve as core regulators of cellular metabolic flux and functional states, making them promising candidates for identifying novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets in AD [5–7]. Previous studies have revealed that numerous metabolic genes are differentially expressed in AD brain tissues, and their dysregulation contributes to microglial metabolic dysfunction and pathological progression [8, 9]. For example, hexokinase 2 (HK2), a critical enzyme in glycolysis, has been shown to drive pro-inflammatory responses in microglia by enhancing glycolytic flux and lactate production, which subsequently promotes Aβ deposition and tau pathology [10]. Similarly, the PICALM AD risk variant induces harmful lipid droplet (LD) accumulation in microglia, thereby compromising their phagocytic function and contributing to disease pathogenesis [11]. Nevertheless, most studies have focused on a limited set of metabolic genes or single-dimensional analyses, lacking an integrative, systematic approach to identify core metabolic regulators with clinical relevance. Integrative transcriptomic analysis, which combines multiple independent datasets and multi-omics validation, has been widely used to uncover key genes in complex diseases by reducing batch effects and improving the reliability of findings [12, 13]. This approach may enable the systematic identification of metabolic genes with consistent expression changes in AD, as well as their functional roles and clinical correlations.

Sprouty homolog 1 (SPRY1) is a member of the Sprouty protein family, known primarily for negatively regulating the receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling pathway, a critical driver of development, angiogenesis, and tumorigenesis [14–16]. In peripheral tissues, SPRY1 has been shown to regulate adipocyte differentiation, insulin sensitivity, and mitochondrial function, indicating its potential involvement in metabolic processes [17–19]. Nevertheless, the expression and function of SPRY1 in CNS microglia during AD progression remain poorly understood.

In this study, we performed an integrative analysis of four human brain tissue transcriptome datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database to screen for key metabolic regulators in microglia during AD progression. We identified SPRY1 as an important regulator of microglial lipid metabolism and inflammatory response. We validated SPRY1 expression pattern, cell type specificity, and regulatory role of the candidate key gene in vitro using primary microglia, in vivo using Drosophila and APP/PS1-21 mouse models, and elucidated its underlying molecular mechanisms. Our findings identify SPRY1 as a pivotal metabolic regulator in AD microglia, offering new insights into the metabolic regulatory mechanisms underlying AD pathogenesis and highlighting a promising target for therapeutic intervention.

Results

SPRY1 is upregulated in AD and correlates with disease severity as a key metabolic gene

To explore the key metabolic regulators in microglia during AD progression, we first integrated four human brain tissue transcriptome datasets from the GEO database: GSE33000, GSE44772, GSE118553, and GSE125583 [20–23] (Fig. 1A). After batch effect correction, we performed differential expression analysis using a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 0.05 (Fig. S1A-S). By intersecting the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across the four datasets, we identified 946 commonly altered genes in AD brain tissues. We intersected these 946 genes with metabolic-related gene sets, yielding 461 genes with significant alterations in metabolic pathways (Fig. 1B). Functional enrichment analysis of these 461 genes revealed that they were predominantly enriched in biological processes such as the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, lipid metabolism, and glucose metabolism (Fig. 1C). To identify potential core regulators, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of the 461 metabolic DEGs. The network highlighted four major functional clusters associated with lipid metabolism, glucose metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and the TCA cycle (Fig. 1E). Subsequent clustering analysis within these modules identified 18 candidate hub genes, including SLC2A1, CD34, PECAM1, IGF2, FLT1, FGF9, SNAI1, ANGPT1, ITGB1, GATA2, FGFR1, PLCG1, CDK2, KRAS, PDGFRB, BDNF, SPRY1, and ATM (Fig. 1F). Notably, several of these genes are known to be involved in glucose transport and cellular metabolism.

Among the 18 genes analyzed, a subset of 12 genes was further evaluated. The combination of these 12 genes showed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.862 in receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis (Fig. 1G and H). To further prioritize the most clinically relevant gene, we performed correlation analysis with clinical parameters. Among these, SPRY1 demonstrated a significant positive correlation with both aging and Braak staging in AD patients within this gene set (Fig. 1I). Importantly, SPRY1 was consistently upregulated in all four independent GEO datasets (Fig. S2A-D). These findings suggest that SPRY1 may serve as a key metabolic regulator associated with disease severity and pathological stage in AD.

We next collected hippocampal tissue samples from non-AD controls and AD patients. Western blotting showed that SPRY1 protein level was significantly upregulated in AD hippocampus (Fig. 1J and K). We further examined the protein level of SPRY1 in AD transgenic mice. Compared with wild‑type mice, AD mice exhibited increased SPRY1 protein level in the hippocampus at 4, 7, and 10 months of age (Fig. 1L and M). Together, these results indicate that SPRY1 expression is elevated in both AD patients and model mice and may positively correlate with AD pathological severity.

SPRY1 is specifically upregulated in microglia surrounding Aβ plaques in AD

To elucidate the cell type specificity of SPRY1 in the brain, we initially examined publicly available transcriptome datasets from mice and humans. Among these species, Spry1 exhibits predominant expression in microglia (Fig. 2A). This finding was corroborated in primary cell cultures: compared to primary astrocytes and neurons, the SPRY1 protein level was highest in primary microglia (Fig. 2B). To further assess its expression alterations in microglia during AD progression, we analyzed the AD single-cell dataset GSE160936 [24](Fig. 2C). The volcano plot reveals that SPRY1 is one of the genes significantly differentially expressed in microglia (Fig. 2D), with its expression markedly elevated in the microglia of AD samples (Fig. 2E). Given that Aβ is the primary pathological factor in AD, we investigated whether oligomerized Aβ42 could change the expression of SPRY1 in microglia. As shown in Fig. 2F and G, treatment of primary microglial cells with varying concentrations of oligomerized Aβ42 resulted in a concentration-dependent increase in SPRY1 protein levels. Additionally, time-course experiments revealed that Aβ42 stimulation gradually upregulated Spry1 mRNA expression over time. These findings indicate that oligomerized Aβ42 can directly induce the upregulation of SPRY1 in microglia. To further assess its clinical relevance, we analyzed the correlation between microglial SPRY1 level and various clinicopathological parameters of AD. Significantly, results showed that the abundance of SPRY1 in microglia was significantly positively correlated with Aβ load, patient age, and Braak stage (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining of AD mouse brain sections revealed that SPRY1 protein level was significantly higher in microglia surrounding Aβ plaques than in those located away from plaques (Fig. 2I and J). These data indicate that SPRY1 is upregulated in plaque‑associated microglia in AD and may correlate with disease severity.

Knockdown of SPRY1 enhances microglial phagocytosis, attenuates pro-inflammatory response, and protects against neuronal injury

To investigate the role of SPRY1 in microglia during AD progression, we designed and transfected small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) to downregulate Spry1 expression; western blotting results indicated a significant reduction in the SPRY1 protein level in primary microglia (Fig. 3A and B). Firstly, the phagocytic ability of primary microglia towards Aβ in vitro was evaluated. Microglia were exposed to Aβ42 for specified durations, then HiLyte-Aβ42 was introduced and co-incubated at 37 °C for 60 min; a control group was maintained at 4 °C to mitigate nonspecific binding effects [25]. As shown in Fig. 3C-E, flow cytometry analysis revealed that Aβ exposure heightened the phagocytosis of HiLyte-Aβ42 by primary microglia, with a further augmentation observed following SPRY1 knockdown. Likewise, immunofluorescence assays corroborated these findings, illustrating that SPRY1 knockdown significantly enhanced the uptake of fluorescent microbeads by primary microglia (Fig. 3F and G). Furthermore, the knockdown of SPRY1 reduced the mRNA expression of inflammatory factors, such as Il6, Tnfa, and Il1b after Aβ treatment, which indicate that SPRY1 deficiency attenuates the neuroinflammatory response (Fig. 3H). In addition, to evaluate the indirect effects of soluble factors secreted by microglia on neurons, primary neurons were placed in the lower chamber, while control or SPRY1-knockdown microglia were placed in the upper inserts during Aβ exposure. MAP2 staining showed that SPRY1 knockdown in microglia significantly mitigated the Aβ-induced decrease in the number of neurite synapses and the reduction in dendritic complexity in neurons (Fig. 3I-L). CCK8 viability assay showed that Aβ treatment reduced neuronal viability, whereas co-culture with the SPRY1-knockdown microglia restored cellular viability (Fig. S3F). Co-staining results showed that the proportion of TUNEL⁺NeuN⁺ apoptotic neurons was significantly elevated in the Aβ + si-NC group and reduced after SPRY1 knockdown (Fig. S3A and B). Consistently, western blotting showed that Aβ decreased synaptophysin protein level, and this reduction was significantly restored by SPRY1 knockdown in microglia (Fig. S3C and D). Glial-specific knockdown of the drosophila SPRY1 homolog sty ameliorates AD-related phenotypes

To determine whether the knockdown of SPRY1 in microglia confers a protective effect in vivo, we employed a Drosophila model of AD that expresses human Aβ. The Drosophila genome contains a single homolog of SPRY1, known as sty (sprouty). Based on previous research [26], we first examined the specificity of the GMR56F03 promoter in glial cells of Drosophila (Fig. 4A), and further we employed GAL4 driver constructs to create a driver line designated as “nSyb-QF2w; GMR56F03” [26]. This driver was subsequently crossed with flies harboring both UAS-STYRNAi and QUAS-Aβ42 following recombination, thereby enabling the targeted inhibition of sty in the glial cells of AD model flies. Motor function was evaluated using a negative geotaxis climbing assay, which revealed that glial-specific sty knockdown significantly enhanced climbing ability in AD model flies (Fig. 4C and D). Cognitive function, assessed through a learning and memory paradigm, was also markedly improved by sty knockdown (Fig. 4E). Lifespan analysis indicated that glial-specific sty knockdown significantly extended both the median and maximum lifespan of AD model flies (Fig. 4F). Neuropathological changes were investigated using Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining of brain sections. AD model flies displayed pronounced brain vacuolization, a hallmark of neurodegeneration [27], while glial-specific sty knockdown dramatically reduced both the number and size of vacuoles (Fig. 4G and H). Collectively, these results suggest that the targeted knockdown of the SPRY1 homologous gene sty in glial cells can concurrently improve various AD-related phenotypes, including motor function, cognitive ability, survival, and neuropathology, indicating a conserved protective effect of cross-species glial cell SPRY1 knockdown.

Microglia-specific SPRY1 knockdown alleviates neuropathology in APP/PS1-21 mice

To assess the functional state of microglia after SPRY1 knockdown, we performed immunofluorescence co‑staining for IL‑1β (M1), Arg1 (M2), and the microglial marker Iba1 on hippocampal sections. SPRY1 knockdown significantly reduced the proportion of Iba1⁺IL‑1β⁺ cells and increased the proportion of Iba1⁺Arg1⁺ cells (Fig. S4E and F). Additionally, SPRY1 knockdown markedly increased the colocalization area of Iba1 and Aβ as well as the percentage of Aβ plaque area covered by Iba1⁺microglia, indicating enhanced microglial Aβ phagocytosis in vivo (Fig. 5E and F). The number, size, and total area of LDs in these cells were significantly reduced, further supporting the enhancement of metabolic phenotypes (Fig. 5G-J).

Next, the pathological burden of Aβ was assessed. Western blotting confirmed that microglia-specific SPRY1 knockdown significantly reduced Aβ peptide level in the hippocampus without altering APP level (Fig. 5K and L). Consistently, microglial SPRY1 knockdown also led to a significant decrease in both the number and area of Aβ plaques in the hippocampus, as indicated by the immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5Q-S). The ELISA results showed that microglial SPRY1 deficiency decreased the levels of both soluble and insoluble Aβ42 and Aβ40 in the hippocampus (Fig. 5M-P). Furthermore, the neuroinflammatory response and neuronal survival were analyzed. Western blotting of hippocampal lysates confirmed that SPRY1 knockdown significantly reduced the protein levels of iNOS, TNF-α, and IL-1β compared to the sh-NC group (Fig. 5T and U). Consistently, qRT-PCR analysis also showed a significant decrease in the mRNA expression of these pro-inflammatory factors in the hippocampus following SPRY1 knockdown (Fig. 5V). Nissl staining was performed to assess neuronal survival in the hippocampal DG, CA1, and CA3 subregions. Microglia-specific SPRY1 knockdown significantly increased the number of Nissl+ cells in all three regions compared to the sh-NC group (Fig. S4G and H).

Therefore, the knockdown of microglial SPRY1 inhibits neuroinflammation, alleviates Aβ deposition, and confers neuroprotective effects in AD transgenic mice.

SPRY1 deficiency promotes fatty acid oxidation and reduces LD accumulation in microglia

To investigate the mechanism by which SPRY1 knockdown inhibited microglial pro-inflammatory response, we conducted single-gene enrichment analysis on the single-cell dataset GSE160936. This analysis revealed significant enrichment of the fatty acid oxidation (FAO) pathway (Fig. 6A). As illustrated in previous studies, FAO plays an important role in regulating microglial activation and neuroinflammation [28, 29], we speculate that SPRY1 promotes microglial activation through the regulation of fatty acid metabolism, particularly FAO. Therefore, we first measured the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) to detect the mitochondrial function via using a Seahorse analyzer. Aβ stimulation significantly reduced basal, ATP-associated, and maximal respiration levels, while SPRY1 knockdown markedly reversed this inhibition and enhanced various respiratory parameters (Fig. 6B-E). These findings suggest that SPRY1 knockdown effectively restores the Aβ-induced defects in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, thereby improving the energy metabolic status of microglia. Notably, FAO activity assays further confirmed that SPRY1 knockdown rescued Aβ‑impaired fatty acid oxidation (Fig. 6F), and this effect was blocked by the CPT1 inhibitor etomoxir (Fig. S5A). Furthermore, FAO dysfunction frequently results in the abnormal accumulation of LDs [30]. A substantial increase in LDs within microglia in AD has been documented, correlating with inflammation and impaired phagocytic function [8, 31]. We assessed LD content using BODIPY staining. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the knockdown of SPRY1 significantly decreased the number, size, and total area of LDs in microglia treated with Aβ (Fig. 6G-J). To determine whether the reduction in LDs was accompanied by the redistribution of fatty acids to mitochondria, we analyzed the subcellular localization of the fluorescent fatty acid analog Red C12. Following Aβ stimulation, Red C12 exhibited enhanced co-localization with the LD marker BODIPY and diminished co-localization with the mitochondrial marker MitoTracker. Conversely, upon knockdown of SPRY1, Red C12 exhibited reduced co-localization with BODIPY and enhanced co-localization with MitoTracker (Fig. 6K-N). These findings suggest that the knockdown of SPRY1 mitigates Aβ-induced metabolic dysfunction in microglia by facilitating the redistribution of fatty acids from LDs to mitochondria, thereby enhancing fatty acid metabolism, particularly FAO.

SPRY1 retains FATP3 at the ER and limits mitochondrial fatty acid utilization

We further investigated the molecular mechanism by which SPRY1 knockdown enhances the transport of fatty acids to mitochondria. We constructed a Flag-SPRY1 overexpression plasmid, successfully transfected it into microglia (Fig. 7A), and analyzed the immunoprecipitation complex using mass spectrometry (MS). The results indicated that fatty acid transport protein 3 (FATP3) was specifically enriched in the Flag-SPRY1 group (Fig. 7B). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments further confirmed the interaction between SPRY1 and FATP3 (Fig. 7C). To further validate the endogenous interaction, reciprocal Co-IP was performed using an anti-FATP3 antibody, which pulled down SPRY1 under endogenous conditions (Fig. S6A and B). FATP3 catalyzes the conversion of long-chain and ultra-long-chain fatty acids into fatty acyl-CoA, a crucial step for fatty acids to enter the mitochondria for β-oxidation [32]. Thus, the interaction between SPRY1 and FATP3 suggests that SPRY1 may play a role in regulating FATP3-mediated fatty acid activation. Immunoprecipitation assays indeed showed that Aβ stimulation significantly increased the interaction between SPRY1 and FATP3 (Fig. 7D). Therefore, we investigated whether SPRY1 affects fatty acid utilization and the immune response of microglia in a FATP3-dependent manner. To test this, we co-expressed FATP3 and SPRY1 in microglia. Overexpression of SPRY1 alone led to LD accumulation and enhanced co-localization of Red C12 with BODIPY, indicating impaired fatty acid metabolism. In contrast, co-expression of FATP3 partially reversed these effects, reducing both LD buildup and Red C12/BODIPY co-localization (Fig. 7E-J). Furthermore, we evaluated the phagocytic activity and inflammatory responses of microglia. The overexpression of SPRY1 notably upregulated the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines Il1b, Tnfa, Il6, and Inos, as assessed through qRT-PCR. The simultaneous expression of FATP3 partially mitigated the SPRY1-triggered increase in the expression of these inflammatory cytokine transcripts (Fig. 7K). To further confirm that the metabolic and immune effects of SPRY1 knockdown are FATP3‑dependent, we designed and validated a specific siRNA targeting FATP3 (Fig. 7L). Primary microglia were co‑transfected with si-SPRY1 and si-FATP3. While si-SPRY1 alone reduced LD accumulation, decreased inflammatory cytokines (Il6, Tnfa, Il1b, Inos), and enhanced beads phagocytosis under Aβ treatment, co‑knockdown of FATP3 significantly attenuated these protective effects (Fig. 7M‑S). These results demonstrate that the functional benefits of SPRY1 knockdown require FATP3.

Finally, we explored the mechanism of SPRY1 regulating FATP3-mediated fatty acid utilization. Given that the proper anchoring of FATP3 to the mitochondrial membrane is essential for its function [33, 34], we hypothesized that SPRY1 might influence the subcellular localization of FATP3. Confocal imaging demonstrated that SPRY1 predominantly localized to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (Fig. 7T). Notably, compared to the control group, Aβ42 treatment redistributed FATP3 to the ER and reduced its mitochondrial colocalization, but knockdown of SPRY1 effectively reversed this mislocalization, restoring FATP3 distribution within the mitochondria (Fig. 7U-X). Immunofluorescence staining for FATP3 and the mitochondrial marker COX IV confirmed this redistribution, showing that Aβ reduced their colocalization while SPRY1 knockdown restored it (Fig. S6C and D). These findings indicate that the aberrant translocation of FATP3 from mitochondria to the ER, induced by Aβ, is critical to the disruption of fatty acid oxidation. Furthermore, the knockdown of SPRY1 restores fatty acid activation by preserving the mitochondrial localization of FATP3.

Discussion

In this study, we identify SPRY1 as a previously unrecognized metabolic regulator in microglia that links FAO to immune function in AD. Through integrative transcriptomic analysis of four human AD brain datasets, we found that SPRY1 is consistently upregulated in AD, correlates with Aβ plaque burden and Braak stage, and is specifically enriched in plaque‑associated microglia. Functional studies demonstrated that SPRY1 knockdown enhances microglial phagocytosis of Aβ, suppresses pro‑inflammatory cytokine expression, and protects neurons from Aβ‑induced injury. Mechanistically, we show that SPRY1 retains FATP3 on the ER, preventing its translocation to mitochondria, thereby impairing FAO, promoting LD accumulation, and driving metabolic and immune dysfunction. Notably, co‑expression of FATP3 partially rescues the LD accumulation and Red C12/BODIPY co‑localization induced by SPRY1 overexpression, indicating that these effects are FATP3‑dependent. Our findings establish the SPRY1-FATP3 axis as a critical node controlling microglial metabolic homeostasis and immune function in AD.

SPRY1 is well known as a negative feedback regulator of RTK signaling in development and cancer, but its role in microglia has remained unexplored. Our study reveals that in AD, Aβ directly induces SPRY1 expression in microglia in a concentration- and time‑dependent manner. SPRY1 is specifically elevated in plaque‑associated microglia, and its levels correlate positively with patient age, Braak stage, and Aβ load. SPRY1 expression increases with age in both human and mouse brains, suggesting its upregulation may partly reflect aging-related processes. However, Aβ directly induces SPRY1 in microglia, leading to its elevation in plaque-associated microglia. Importantly, SPRY1 knockdown ameliorates Aβ pathology in APP/PS1‑21 mice. Thus, while SPRY1 may also participate in aging‑related responses, multiple lines of evidence support its functional role in AD. This expression pattern is consistent with the emerging concept that metabolic genes are frequently dysregulated in AD microglia. For example, genes involved in lipid handling such as APOE and TREM2 have been shown to influence microglial metabolism and disease severity [6, 35, 36]. However, unlike APOE which primarily affects cholesterol transport, SPRY1 acts by interacting with and retaining a mitochondrial fatty acid transporter. Although genes such as KRAS, FGF9, and ITGB1 exhibited stronger correlations with Braak stage during our initial screening, they were not prioritized for further mechanistic investigation. This decision was based on their lack of significant correlation with age and the absence of established roles in microglial lipid metabolism or fatty acid oxidation in the current literature. Whether KRAS, FGF9, or ITGB1 contribute specifically to tau pathology requires future investigation in tauopathy models (e.g., P301S mice) or human brain tissue with well-characterized tau burden. Our study does not exclude the possibility that these genes may be more directly involved in tau-driven neurodegeneration. Our data suggest that SPRY1 acts as a pathological metabolic switch that shifts microglia from a homeostatic FAO‑dependent state to a dysfunctional LD‑accumulating state.

The mechanism we uncovered centers on the subcellular trafficking of FATP3. FATP3 is a member of the fatty acid transport protein family that converts long‑chain fatty acids into fatty acyl‑CoA, a prerequisite for mitochondrial β‑oxidation [33, 34]. Prior studies have largely focused on the expression level of FATP3, but the regulation of the subcellular localization has received little attention. Our study provides the first evidence that under physiological conditions, FATP3 localizes to mitochondria, whereas Aβ stimulation triggers its redistribution to the ER. This mislocalization is mediated by SPRY1, which interacts with FATP3 and retains it on the ER. Given that SPRY1 itself is predominantly localized to the ER and does not change its localization upon Aβ treatment, we propose a model in which Aβ‑induced upregulation of SPRY1 leads to increased SPRY1-FATP3 interaction, trapping FATP3 on the ER and preventing its proper anchoring to the mitochondrial membrane. Consequently, fatty acid delivery to mitochondria is blocked, resulting in impaired FAO and LD accumulation. This mechanism is distinct from other AD‑related metabolic defects, such as TREM2 mutations that impair mitochondrial metabolism more broadly [37] or APOE4 that promotes LD accumulation through altered lipid transport [38].

The functional consequences of SPRY1‑mediated metabolic disruption are directly linked to microglial immune responses. In our study, SPRY1 knockdown reduced LD number and size, restored FAO and mitochondrial respiration, and concurrently enhanced Aβ phagocytosis and reduced Il6, Tnfa, and Il1b expression. The rescue experiment with FATP3 co‑expression further supports a causal relationship, as FATP3 partially reversed the metabolic defects caused by SPRY1. These findings align with a growing body of literature showing that LD accumulation in microglia is associated with a pro‑inflammatory, dysfunctional phenotype [9, 39]. For instance, pharmacological or genetic inhibition of FAO has been shown to promote microglial inflammation and impair Aβ clearance [40]. Conversely, enhancing FAO promotes an anti‑inflammatory microglial state [41]. Our work adds to this framework by identifying SPRY1 as a direct upstream regulator that controls FAO through FATP3 retention. It is well documented that microglial functional states can affect neuronal health through various secreted factors. While we did not directly measure BDNF or inhibit TrkB/ERK signaling in the present study, we acknowledge that such pathways could contribute to the observed neuronal outcomes. Nevertheless, our complementary data on FAO, LDs, and FATP3 localization support a primary role for metabolic reprogramming in the microglial phenotype, which likely indirectly benefits neurons by reducing Aβ burden and inflammation. Future studies using BDNF neutralization or TrkB/ERK inhibitors will help dissect the relative contributions of these pathways.

The protective effects of SPRY1 knockdown were validated in two animal models. In Drosophila, glial-specific knockdown of the SPRY1 homolog sty improved motor function, cognition, and lifespan, and reduced neurodegeneration. In APP/PS1-21 mice, microglia-specific SPRY1 knockdown decreased Aβ deposition, reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines, and shifted microglia toward a protective, anti-inflammatory phenotype. These in vivo results are consistent with our in vitro findings and demonstrate that targeting SPRY1 in microglia is sufficient to alleviate AD-related pathology. Importantly, because SPRY1 is predominantly expressed in microglia and is specifically upregulated in plaque-associated cells, it represents a cell-type-specific target with potentially fewer off-target effects compared to broadly expressed metabolic regulators.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the SPRY1-FATP3 binding interface is unknown; structural studies are needed to identify potential drug-binding pockets. Second, mitochondrial-targeted FATP3 rescue has not been performed; future studies will test whether mitochondrial FATP3 is required for the benefits of SPRY1 knockdown. Third, our Drosophila experiments lacked driver-only and RNAi-only controls, which should be included to exclude off-target effects. Fourth, primary microglia from P3 pups cultured with serum may not fully recapitulate aged or AD-associated microglia; this should be considered when interpreting in vitro results. Fifth, only one shRNA was used for in vivo knockdown; future studies using two independent shRNAs or conditional microglial SPRY1 knockout would validate specificity. Sixth, our study primarily used male AD mice; given sexual dimorphism in AD, future studies should include both sexes to assess the SPRY1-FATP3 axis in male and female microglia.

In summary, our study identifies SPRY1 as a critical metabolic checkpoint in AD microglia that impairs FAO by retaining FATP3 on the ER, leading to LD accumulation, pro‑inflammatory activation, and impaired Aβ clearance. Microglia‑specific SPRY1 knockdown restores metabolic homeostasis, reduces neuroinflammation, and alleviates AD pathology. These findings position the SPRY1-FATP3 axis as a promising therapeutic target for AD, and strategies aimed at disrupting the SPRY1-FATP3 interaction or restoring FATP3 mitochondrial localization may offer new avenues for disease‑modifying treatment.

Experimental section/methods

Data Preprocessing

Whole-brain tissue gene expression data were retrieved from the GEO database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The mRNA datasets, including GSE33000, GSE44772, GSE118553, and GSE125583, were processed for batch effect removal and integration using the "sva" package implemented in R software.

Identification and cluster analysis of metabolism-related key genes in AD patients

Metabolism-related genes were searched using the GSEA website (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp), and previously documented genes were selected. Differential expression analysis was first performed independently within each dataset. The resulting DEG lists were then intersected across the four datasets to identify consistently altered genes. These common DEGs were then overlapped with metabolism-related gene sets to obtain the final set of metabolism-associated DEGs. Clustering of DEGs was conducted via the protein–protein interaction (PPI) platform (https://cn.string-db.org/), and the results were visualized with Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1). Among the DEGs, hub genes were further identified using the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) algorithm. All genes were subjected to cluster analysis using GO, Reactome, and KEGG pathway data within GSEA software (version 4.1.0). A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Construction of a disease severity-associated gene signature and clinical data analysis

Based on the core genes identified in AD patients, LASSO and logistic regression analyses were performed using the glmnet package. Twelve significant key genes were screened out, and a disease severity-associated gene signature was generated. The ROC function was applied to further assess the exploratory screening utility, with an AUC greater than 0.6 used as the cutoff value. Clinical data and mRNA from human brain tissues in the GSE185909 dataset were extracted, and Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was conducted using the Sangerbox platform (http://sangerbox.com). A P-value < 0.05 and a correlation coefficient > 0.3 were considered statistically significant.

Animals and human brain tissues

Wild C57BL/6 mice were obtained at Nanjing Medical University, and APP/PS1-21 mice by Prof. M. Jucker (University of Tuebingen, Germany). All experiments were conducted according to institutional guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-2604034) Nanjing Medical University, and were kept in 12-h light/dark cycle, free access to food and water. In order to exclude estrogen effects only male mice were used. APP/PS1-21 mice 4, 7, 10 months and age-matched wildtype control mice were used for experiments. Human brain tissue was approved by the Institute Ethical Committee Nanjing Medical University (approval No. 2022816). Detailed clinical and demographic information of human brain tissues is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Human Aβ42 was expressed in neurons using the nSyb-QF2w/QUAS system, while STY RNAi was driven in glia using the GMR56F03-GAL4/UAS system. The driver line was generated by combining the nSyb‑QF2w and GMR56F03 drivers (genotype: nSyb‑QF2w; GMR56F03). This driver was crossed with flies carrying QUAS‑Aβ42 (effector line) to generate Aβ‑expressing flies (nSyb‑QF2w; GMR56F03 > QUAS‑Aβ42). For glial‑specific knockdown of the SPRY1 homolog sty, flies carrying UAS‑STYRNAi were crossed into the same background to obtain nSyb‑QF2w; GMR56F03 > QUAS‑Aβ42/UAS‑STYRNAi. Flies were maintained on standard cornmeal‑agar medium at 25 °C with 50% relative humidity under a 12‑h light/dark cycle. Only female flies were used in this study. The following genotypes were used in this study: nSyb-QF2W; GMR56F03 > QUAS-Aβ42; nSyb-QF2W; GMR56F03 > UAS-STYRNAi; QUAS-Aβ42. Wild-type w1118 served as the control [26, 42].

Survival analysis

In the lifespan assay, collected flies were transferred to fresh vials and maintained for two days before sex separation. One hundred female flies were placed into each lifespan vial. Fresh food was provided every two days, and the numbers of dead and surviving flies were recorded. The flies were kept in an incubator at 25 °C. Survival curves were analysed using log-rank tests in GraphPad Prism 9 [43].

Climbing assay

The climbing assay was conducted following previously published protocols [44]. For each test, 15 flies were placed into an empty plastic tube and left to rest for a minimum of 30 min. The tube was gently tapped to bring the flies to the bottom, after which the flies were allowed to climb upward for 10 s to cross a line marked approximately 4 cm from the bottom of the tube. The number of flies that successfully crossed the line within the 10-s period was counted. This procedure was repeated three times per tube, and the average percentage of flies crossing the line was calculated and defined as the “climbing rate” for that tube.

T-maze

This study employed a classical olfactory aversive conditioning paradigm in Drosophila. Behavioral experiments were conducted using a custom-made organic glass T-maze apparatus. The T-maze consisted of a central chamber and two lateral arms (choice tubes), each of which could be connected to odor delivery tubes. The inner walls of the training tubes were lined with copper grids connected to an electric stimulator, which delivered electric shock punishment. A gentle airflow of approximately 2 L/min, maintained by an air pump, ensured uniform odor delivery throughout the chambers. Two odors with no innate bias for wild-type flies were selected as conditioned stimuli (CS): 4-methylcyclohexanol (MCH) and 3-octanol (OCT). Both odors were diluted in mineral oil to appropriate concentrations (MCH at 1:67, OCT at 1:100) to ensure no significant difference in naïve odor preference. The unconditioned stimulus (US) was a 90 V direct current shock delivered uniformly to the flies’ feet through the copper grids. All behavioral experiments were conducted in a temperature-controlled room at 25 °C with 70% relative humidity, and operations were carried out under dark red light to avoid visual interference. Learning tests (assessing short-term memory) were performed immediately after training. Flies were transferred to the central chamber of the T-maze, where both odors (MCH and OCT) were simultaneously delivered to the two lateral arms. Flies were allowed to freely choose either arm for 120 s [45]. After the test, flies from each lateral arm and the central chamber were collected and counted to calculate percentages.

Stereotaxic surgery and viral injection

To knockdown the gene, Brain Case synthesized a recombinant AAV targeting SPRY1. Surgical instruments were sterilized before use. APP/PS1-21 mice were anesthetized and secured in a stereotaxic frame (RWD Life Science, China). The skin on the skull was disinfected with iodophor, then the hair was removed, and the skull was cut open with scissors. Medical grade hydrogen peroxide was used to clean the skull and expose the bregma. 0.5 μL of the viral solution was injected into the bilateral hippocampus using a microsyringe at coordinates: for the anterior hippocampus, bregma ‑1.55 mm, ML ± 1 mm, DV ‑2 mm; for the posterior hippocampus, bregma ‑2.7 mm, ML ± 2 mm, DV ‑2 mm. After the injection, the microsyringe was left in place for 10 min and slowly removed. Finally, scalp incision was sutured, disinfected, and the mice were monitored for a week until the animals recovered normal vital signs. The siRNA sequence targeting mouse Spry1 (sense: 5’-GUGCAAAUGUGGAGAGUGU-3’) was obtained from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Based on this sequence, a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting mouse Spry1 was designed and cloned into the AAV vector pAAV-mIBA1-EGFP-5’miR-30a-shRNA-3’miR-30a-WPRE-4 × miR9T, where the shRNA insert (mspry1) corresponds to the siRNA sense strand. The control AAV (AAV-NC) was constructed using the same backbone but containing a scrambled shRNA (Scramble) sequence. Both AAV vectors were packaged in the AAV11 serotype and produced by Brain Case Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). The final viral titer was 1 × 1013 VG/mL. Viruses were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until use.

H&E and Nissl staining

Following the supplier’s protocols, brain tissues from Drosophila models of AD were subjected to H&E staining (G1076, Servicebio). Brain Sects. (15 μm) from APP/PS1-21 mice were stained with Nissl (G1036, Servicebio) following standard protocols.

Immunofluorescence

Mice were euthanized via pentobarbital sodium overdose perfusion with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were extracted and stored in 4% paraformaldehyde 24 h, dried in graded sucrose (10%, 20%, 30%). Brain tissues were sectioned at 15 μm and treated with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, then incubated in 1% bovine serum albumin for 1 h. The primary antibodies used were as follows: SPRY1 (rabbit, 1:200, CUSABIO), Aβ (mouse, 1:200; Abcam), Iba1 (rabbit,1:100, Abcam), Iba1 (mouse, 1:100, Abcam), MAP2 (rabbit, 1:1000, Abcam), PDI (mouse, 1:400, Proteintech), IL-1β (mouse, 1:250, CST), and Arg1 (rabbit, 1:250, CST). After that, brain sections were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies. Finally, the slices were washed six times with PBST, before capturing the images using fluorescence microscopy (Olympus). All image quantification was performed using ImageJ software under blinded conditions. After importing the images, background was subtracted using the “Subtract Background” function (rolling ball radius: 50 pixels). Positive signals were binarized using either the Otsu automatic threshold or a manually fixed threshold determined from negative control images. For colocalization analysis, Manders’ coefficients were calculated using the Coloc 2 plugin. Subsequently, target signals were segmented and quantified using the “Analyze Particles” function. Normalization strategies included: dividing integrated fluorescence intensity by the number of DAPI-positive cells or the analyzed area to obtain mean fluorescence intensity per cell or per unit area, and dividing colocalized area (e.g., Iba1⁺Aβ⁺) by total Iba1⁺ area to obtain the relative coverage proportion. For each experimental group, at least three animals (biological replicates) were analyzed, with 3–5 sections per animal and 3–5 representative fields of view per section. All image acquisition parameters were kept constant across experiments.

BODIPY staining for brain sections

For brain sections requiring both immunofluorescence and BODIPY staining, all sections were routinely treated with an autofluorescence quencher (G1221; Servicebio; China) prior to staining. Subsequently, LDs were labeled with BODIPY prior to DAPI counterstaining. BODIPY (0.25 μM, D3922, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used for staining. Briefly, 100 μL of the diluted BODIPY solution was directly applied onto each brain section [46]. The sections were then kept in a humidified chamber and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the sections were gently rinsed, counterstained with DAPI, and finally mounted.

ELISA

To measure soluble Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels, brain tissue samples were homogenized in ice-cold PBS supplemented with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 0.03% Tween-20, and a protease inhibitor cocktail. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected for subsequent analysis. For the determination of insoluble Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels, the pellets obtained from the above step were re-suspended in PBS containing 1% Triton X-100 and protease inhibitors, followed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C. The resulting pellets were then re-suspended in 70% formic acid (F112038, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) and subjected to another round of ultracentrifugation under the same conditions (100,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C). The supernatants were subsequently neutralized using a formic acid neutralization buffer composed of 1 mol/L Tris base, 0.5 mol/L Na₂HPO₄, and 0.05% NaN₃ prior to quantification [47, 48]. The concentrations of Aβ40 and Aβ42 were quantified using commercial ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s protocols, and the absorbance was read with a microplate reader. Detailed information of ELISA kits used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Isolation of primary microglia, astrocytes, and neurons

Primary microglia, astrocytes, and mature neurons were isolated from the brain tissues of postnatal day 3 (P3) mice. The neonatal mice were surface-sterilized, after which the brains were carefully removed and placed in serum-free DMEM. Following removal of the meninges, the brain tissue was cut into appropriately sized fragments and digested with 0.25% trypsin and 0.025% DNase I at 37 °C for 10 min. The digestion was halted by the addition of complete DMEM. The cell suspension was then centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 min, resuspended in pre-warmed culture medium, and filtered through an 80 μm mesh to remove debris. The filtered cells were subsequently seeded into culture flasks pre-coated with poly-L-lysine. For primary neuron culture, the cells were maintained in Neurobasal medium supplemented with B27, and 1 μM cytosine‑β‑D‑arabinofuranoside (AraC) was added to the medium. After 7–10 days of inoculation, the cultures formed distinct layers of astrocytes and microglia, with the culture medium being replaced every 48 h. Microglia were then detached from the mixed primary cultures by shaking the flasks at 100 rpm for 5 h and collected. Astrocytes were harvested by trypsinization. Cortical tissues from each litter (8–12 pups) were pooled to form one cell pool. For each independent experiment, cell pools derived from at least three different litters were used as biological replicates (n ≥ 3) [49]. The collected cells were finally cultured under standard conditions (5% CO₂, 37 °C) in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The above reagent information has been added to Supplementary Table S1.

Cell culture, drug treatments

Microglial cells were grown in DMEM containing 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. To analyze the subcellular localization of the fluorescent fatty acid analog Red-C12, primary microglia were pre-labeled with Red-C12 (D3835, Life Technologies) for 16 h to fluorescently tag fatty acids as described previously [46]. Subsequently, the cells were either co-incubated with BODIPY (2.5 μM, D3922, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), or treated with MitoTracker (100 nM, C1048, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) followed by further incubation at 37 °C for 15 min. Each combination was added directly to the culture medium for incubation. Fluorescent images were acquired using a microscope with excitation light for the respective dyes.

Cell transfection

For transfection, PRIMARY microglia were transfected with siRNA or plasmid using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (L3000001, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. At 12 h post-transfection, the culture medium was replaced to eliminate residual transfection complexes. The cells were harvested 48 h after transfection for subsequent analyses. The siRNA sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Transwell co-culture system

Primary neurons were seeded into the lower chamber of 12-well plates, and primary microglia were plated onto Transwell inserts with a pore size of 0.4 μm. In the experimental results, Aβ represents oligomerized Aβ42. Aβ was purchased from Qyaobio, and the cells were treated with different concentrations for 12 h according to our well-established methods [50]. After overnight attachment, siRNA transfection and Aβ treatment were performed on the microglia. Following the treatments, the medium inside the inserts was discarded, and the inserts were gently washed twice with PBS to remove residual Aβ and transfection reagents. The inserts were then transferred to wells without neurons, containing fresh serum-free medium, and allowed to equilibrate for 15–30 min. Meanwhile, the original medium in the neuron-containing plates was aspirated and replaced with fresh neuronal culture medium. The equilibrated inserts were then carefully placed into the wells containing neurons, ensuring that the bottom of the inserts was immersed in the medium without trapping any air bubbles. After 24 h of co-culture, the inserts were removed, and the neurons in the lower chamber were collected for subsequent experiments.

TUNEL assay

Apoptotic cells were identified through the TUNEL assay using the One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (A112-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, samples were incubated with the TUNEL mixture solution for 1 h at 37 °C and subsequently stained with DAPI. Apoptotic cells were observed and recorded using a fluorescence microscope.

Lipid droplets staining for microglia cells

For LD staining in microglia, the culture medium was first removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. The cells were then incubated with an appropriate volume of BODIPY solution (2.5 μM, D3922, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C for 15 min. Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and stained with Hoechst 33,342 (C1027, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for live-cell nuclear labeling [46]. The cells were subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed again with PBS, and mounted using a DAPI-containing mounting medium. Images were captured using an Olympus microscope and quantified with Image J software.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from a sample of RNA with FreeZol Reagent (R711-01, Vazyme) and reverse transcribed using QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (AG11705, Accurate Biology). qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green (AG11701, Accurate Biology). Gene expression was normalized to β-actin and analysed using 2–ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences for target mRNA are given in Supplementary Table S3.

Co-IP, western blotting and MS analysis

Cells or tissues were lysed in a lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (P0013; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). After centrifugation, the supernatants were incubated with specific primary antibodies and Protein A/G magnetic beads (HK-K0202-1; MCE, USA). The beads were washed five times, and the immunoprecipitated proteins were then eluted for western blotting. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Sparkjade Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Jinan, China). Protein samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following washes with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween-20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h. Immunoreactive bands were finally visualized using a Tanon imaging system. The primary antibodies used included the following: SPRY1 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), FATP3 (1:1000, Proteintech), TOM20 (1:5000, Proteintech), Calreticulin (1:1000, Proteintech), β-Actin (1:3000, Servicebio), GAPDH (1:50,000, HUABIO), Flag (1:5000, HUABIO), Aβ (1:1000, Abcam), APP (1:2000, Abcam), and Synaptophysin (1:20,000, Abcam).

For MS analysis, protein samples pulled down by Co-IP were resolved on an SDS-PAGE gel via electrophoresis. After electrophoresis, the gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue to reveal distinct protein bands. The gel pieces corresponding to the regions of interest were excised using a rubber scraper and then processed for further analysis. MS data acquisition was performed using a Thermo Q-Exactive liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) system. The raw MS data were analyzed with ProteomeDiscoverer 2.1 software, which embeds the SequestHT search engine. Protein sequence searching was conducted against the UniProt mouse proteome database.

Fatty acid oxidation (FAO) assay

FAO activity in primary microglia was measured using a fatty acid oxidation colorimetric assay kit (E-BC-K784-M, Elabscience, USA). The assay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, after treatment, microglia were collected and centrifuged, and the supernatant was retained. An aliquot of the supernatant was used for protein concentration determination, while another aliquot was used for the FAO activity assay. The optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader [51, 52]. FAO activity was calculated based on the standard curve, taking into account the dilution factor and protein concentration.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK 8) assay

Primary neurons were seeded in 96-well plates. After treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (C0038, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 2 h. Thereafter, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader [46].

Mitochondrial fractionation

Following the indicated treatments, primary microglia were harvested and processed for subcellular fractionation using a mitochondrial isolation kit (C3601; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer's protocol. In brief, cells were re-suspended in the provided mitochondria extraction reagent, homogenized with a micro-homogenizer, and then kept on ice for 15 min. The homogenate was centrifuged at 600 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was retained and subjected to a second centrifugation at 11,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The cytosolic fraction was recovered from the supernatant, whereas the mitochondrial fraction was obtained from the pellet. Western blotting was employed to confirm the purity and effectiveness of the fractionation.

ER fractionation

Following the manufacturer's instructions, a commercial kit (Cat. No. ER0100; Sigma) was used to isolate ER-enriched fractions, and the fractionation outcome was validated by western blotting.

The flow cytometry-based assay

Primary microglial cells were seeded into 24-well plates and cultured until they reached approximately 60% confluence. They were then pre-treated with unlabeled Aβ42 for 12 h to mimic AD exposure conditions. Following a gentle wash with PBS, 10 μL of HiLyte Fluor™ 488-labeled Aβ42 was added to each well and incubated for 1 h. Cells were then harvested by centrifugation, and the supernatant was carefully removed. The cell pellets were resuspended in a pre-prepared cocktail of flow cytometry antibodies and incubated for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed via centrifugation, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed using a flow cytometer. Data processing was performed using FlowJo software.

Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection and transcardially perfused with PBS to remove circulating blood. The hippocampus was dissected and minced in ice-cold Hanks' Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS). The minced tissue was homogenized in medium supplemented with 2% FBS, followed by enzymatic digestion with 0.5 mg/mL collagenase IV and 10 U/mL DNase I at 37 °C for 15–20 min. The resulting cell suspension was filtered and then layered onto 70% Percoll for density gradient centrifugation at 500 × g for 20 min. The intermediate cell layer, enriched for microglial cells, was collected and washed twice with PBS. The cells were then resuspended in PBS containing 2% FBS and stained with fluorescently conjugated antibodies and a fixable viability dye for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Finally, the stained cells were resuspended in staining buffer and immediately subjected to flow cytometric sorting [48].

Oligomeric Aβ42 preparation: 1 mg of amyloid peptide was dissolved in 1 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), followed by sonication to assist complete dissolution. The final concentration of the solution was 1 mg/mL.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, LLC, San Diego, California, USA). Two-group comparisons utilized unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, while multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Sample sizes were determined based on prior studies and are indicated in the figure legends. Randomization and blinding were implemented throughout the study. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.
