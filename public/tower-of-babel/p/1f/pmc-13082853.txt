Projected estimates of cancer in Canada in 2026
Authors: Darren R Brenner, Jennifer Gillis, Alain A Demers, Larry F Ellison, Carmina Ng, Shary Xinyu Zhang, Christian Finley, Natalie Fitzgerald, Nathalie Saint-Jacques, Lorraine Shack, Donna Turner, Robin Urquhart, Ryan R Woods
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Cancer is the leading cause of death and has major health and economic impacts on people in Canada. We sought to provide updated estimates of cancer incidence and mortality to highlight progress and areas of need for planning and awareness.Methods:We estimated cases, deaths, and age-standardized incidence (ASIR) and mortality rates (ASMR) in 2026, standardized to the 2021 Canadian standard population, by sex and province or territory. We used data from the Canadian Cancer Registry (until 2022) and the Canadian Vital Statistics Death Database (until 2023). We modelled incidence and mortality with the canproj projection package.Results:In Canada, an estimated 254 100 people will be diagnosed with cancer and 87 900 will die from cancer in 2026. Overall, the ASIR (591.4 per 100 000) and the ASMR (200.0 per 100 000) are projected to decrease from previous years. Lung, breast, prostate, and colorectal cancers are projected to account for 47% of all new cases. The ASIR for all cancers combined is anticipated to be 16% higher among males than females (642.2 v. 553.9 per 100 000), and the ASMR 36% higher (235.8 v. 172.8 per 100 000). Notable findings in cancer-specific rates by sex were observed.Interpretation:Age-standardized cancer incidence and mortality rates are projected to decline in Canada; however, the numbers of new cases and deaths are expected to remain at high levels, given the growing and aging population, with differential impacts expected by sex. These findings suggest that continued investment and diligence are needed to continue the major progress in cancer control in the face of changing population demographics.

Cancer surveillance reports focus on specific rates and population estimates, but these cannot capture the large impact that cancer has on Canadian society. Estimates from the Canadian Cancer Statistics Advisory Committee suggest that 42% of all people in Canada will receive a cancer diagnosis in their lifetime.1 The population of Canada grew by about 9.5% between 2020 and 2025, primarily because of immigration, and continues to age, with a record percentage (19.5%) of people aged 65 years or older in 2025.2 An aging and growing population has resulted in a greater number of people diagnosed with or dying from cancer year after year, and a major economic impact on society. A 2024 report from the Canadian Cancer Statistics Advisory Committee estimated that the total societal cost was $37.7 billion, with as much as 20% of costs falling on patients and caregivers.3

Given the considerable health, social, and economic impacts of cancer in Canada, awareness of up-to-date estimates of cancer incidence and mortality is essential for service planning. The collection of detailed, high-quality, population-level cancer data can lag by several years, given the time required to register a cancer diagnosis accurately and validate the data. To mitigate this delay, short-term projections — estimated by extrapolating previous trends into the future using statistical models — can be employed. Such projections provide a snapshot of the impact of cancer in Canada to frame where progress has been made and where additional efforts are needed.

The Canadian Cancer Statistics 2025 report provided detailed estimates of cancer incidence, mortality, and survival in Canada by sex assigned at birth, age, and province or territory, as well as time trends for 23 cancer types, up to reference year 2025.1 We sought to provide updated projections of the age-standardized rates and counts of new cancer cases and deaths expected in 2026, by sex and province or territory for all ages combined.

Methods

Study design

We projected estimates of cancer incidence and mortality using population-level cancer data. This study was developed by the Canadian Cancer Statistics Advisory Committee, a collaboration between Statistics Canada, the Public Health Agency of Canada, and the Canadian Cancer Society. The advisory committee includes representation from each of these organizations, the Canadian Partnership Against Cancer, and provincial and territorial cancer registries, as well as clinical and academic collaborators. The advisory committee makes analytical and methodological decisions collaboratively based on documented approaches.1 Unless otherwise stated, the data sources and methodological decisions used in this study are as described in detail in Canadian Cancer Statistics 2025.1

Data sources

We used national population-based databases, maintained at Statistics Canada to source data on cancer incidence (National Cancer Incidence Reporting System, 1984 to 1991, and the Canadian Cancer Registry Tabulation Master File, 1992 to 2022, released Jan. 31, 2025)4 and deaths (Canadian Vital Statistics Death Database, 1984 to 2023, released Feb. 19, 2025).5 These databases include data submissions from provincial and territorial cancer and vital statistics registries to Statistics Canada. We also obtained actual and projected population estimates from Statistics Canada.6,7 The Canadian Cancer Registry is a population-based registry that includes information about new cases of invasive primary cancer (excluding nonmelanoma skin cancer) and in situ urinary bladder cancer diagnosed among Canadian residents. Details on included cancer types can be found in Appendix 1, Table A1-1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.252152/tab-related-content. We omitted incidence data for 2020 from the analyses to not erroneously skew projections, given our previous report highlighting the potential impact of drops in the registration of multiple cancer types in this outlier year.1

Statistical analysis

We used the validated8 Canproj projection package to generate updated estimates of cancer incidence and mortality, projecting counts and rates to 2026.9 The Canproj package uses trends in historical data to select the best-fit model for subsequent years based on a decision algorithm comparing 6 models: Nordpred (age–period–cohort drift), age–cohort, hybrid age-specific trend, hybrid age-common trend, hybrid age-only, and 5-year average. Model selection is based on statistical tests, starting with the most complex (Nordpred) model, and specific rules, where a simpler model may be selected. Additional details on the Canproj modelling approach are provided in Appendix 1.

Decisions on the most appropriate models for each province or territory were guided by a set of principles and decision tree (Appendix 1) and made by consensus in consultation with that jurisdiction’s cancer registry. Projected estimates of incidence and mortality were rounded. Full details of the projection review process and rounding rules can be found in Canadian Cancer Statistics 2025.1

For cancer incidence projections, we used incidence data from 1997 to 2022 (with the 2020 data year excluded; Appendix 1) to project case counts and rates from 2023 to 2026, for all jurisdictions except Quebec and Nova Scotia. For cancer mortality projections, we used death data from 1999 to 2023 to project deaths and rates from 2024 to 2026, for all jurisdictions except Nova Scotia. For mortality projections, we used the same Canproj models selected for Canadian Cancer Statistics 2025.1

For Quebec, we generated incidence projections from 2018 to 2026, as cancer cases diagnosed after 2017 had yet to be submitted to the Canadian Cancer Registry at the time of analysis. As previously reported,10 we used locally estimated scatterplot smoothing for the 2006 to 2017 reference period for some age- and sex-specific rates in Quebec to account for an artifactual increase in some cancers caused by the transition from a cancer registry based exclusively on hospital discharge data to a more integrated central registry (details in Appendix 1).

For Nova Scotia, we generated incidence projections from 2020 to 2026, as cancer cases diagnosed after 2019 had yet to be submitted to the Canadian Cancer Registry when the analyses were performed. We generated mortality projections for Nova Scotia from 2023 to 2026, because we considered the data for 2023 to be incomplete for our purposes.

We generated projected estimates for 23 cancer types by sex assigned at birth (hereafter referred to as sex) and geographic region (province or territory). Definitions of cancer types are presented in Appendix 1, Table A1-1.11,12 We calculated projected estimates for Canada as sums of the projections for each individual province and territory. All incidence and mortality rates were age-standardized to the 2021 Canadian standard population using the direct method,13 whereas our previous publications employed the 2011 standard population. Given changes in the age structure of the Canadian population over these 10 years, age-standardized rates may differ between those reported herein and those reported previously.

Ethics approval

This study used publicly available anonymized data; therefore, consideration and approval by an ethics review board were not required.

Results

Incidence and mortality in 2026

In 2026, 254 100 new cancer cases are projected to be diagnosed in Canada (Table 1). Lung (n = 32 900), breast (n = 32 700), prostate (n = 29 300), and colorectal (n = 25 300) cancers are expected to remain the most commonly diagnosed cancers, representing 47% of all new cases diagnosed in 2026. Among males, the most commonly diagnosed cancers are expected to be prostate (23%), lung (12%), colorectal (11%), and bladder (8%) cancers; among females, breast (26%), lung (14%), colorectal (9%), and uterine (7%) cancers are expected to be the most common.

In 2026, 87 900 people in Canada are expected to die from cancer (Table 2). The 5 most common causes of cancer-related death are lung (n = 19 300), colorectal (n = 9200), pancreas (n = 6500), breast (n = 5400), and prostate (n = 5300), which are projected to account for more than half (52%) of all cancer deaths in Canada in 2026. On its own, lung cancer is expected to account for 1 in 5 cancer-related deaths in 2026, with the highest number and proportion of cancer deaths among both males (n = 9900, 21%) and females (n = 9300, 23%). Prostate (11%), colorectal (10%), pancreas (7%), and liver and intrahepatic bile duct (5%) are expected to be the next most common causes of cancer death among males, while the next leading causes among females are breast (13%), colorectal (10%), pancreas (8%), and ovarian (5%) cancers.

Incidence and mortality among males and females

Apart from sex-specific cancers, all cancers except breast and thyroid are more commonly diagnosed among males than females (Table 1). Overall, the ASIR for all cancers combined is expected to remain higher (almost 16% higher) among males than females (642.2 v. 553.9 per 100 000, respectively) in 2026 (Table 1). In 2026, the ASMR is expected to remain considerably higher (36% higher) among males than females (235.8 v. 172.8 per 100 000, respectively), apart from sex-specific cancers, with males expected to have higher mortality rates for all cancer types except for breast and thyroid (Table 2). Age-standardized rates for the most common cancer types have historically been higher among males than females (Figure 1A and Figure 2A). For both incidence and mortality, sex-specific rates of lung cancer have been converging over the last 40 years (Figure 1A and Figure 2A). This is because incidence and mortality rates have been declining for longer among males (peaks in the early and late 1980s) and only more recently among females (peaks in the late 2000s to mid-2010s). Colorectal cancer has been declining similarly among males and females since the early 2000s. The ASIR is expected to be approximately 30% lower than it was in the early 2000s for both males (69.8 per 100 000 in 2026 v. 102.9 per 100 000 in 2000, 32% lower) and females (48.9 per 100 000 in 2026 v. 68.6 per 100 000 in 2000, 29% lower).

Incidence rates of leukemia have been declining among both males and females for more than a decade, and more recently, similar sex-specific declines in mortality have now been observed (Figure 1B and Figure 2B). Bladder cancer mortality is now notably decreasing among both males and females (Figure 2B) and is expected to be 24% lower for males and 17% lower for females than a decade ago (9.2 per 100 000 in 2026 v. 12.1 per 100 000 in 2016 for males; 2.9 per 100 000 in 2026 v. 3.5 per 100 000 in 2016 for females). Since the early 2000s, the incidence of pancreatic cancer has been moderately increasing (Figure 1B) while no progress has been made in reducing mortality rates (Figure 2A). Pancreatic cancer is expected to remain the third leading cause of cancer-related death in 2026, with a similar number of males (n = 3400) and females (n = 3100) expected to die from the disease.

The incidence of uterine cancer continues to increase; it remains the fourth most commonly diagnosed cancer among females (Figure 1B and Table 1). Notably, uterine cancer mortality has been increasing since the mid-2000s (Figure 2B), and the mortality rate is expected to be 53% higher in 2026 than it was in 2005 (7.5 per 100 000 in 2026 v. 4.9 per 100 000 in 2005). After decades of decreasing incidence, cervical cancer rates have plateaued since the mid-2000s (Figure 1B and Figure 2B).

Interpretation

Our projections suggest that the total number of cancer cases and deaths are expected to remain at high levels, similar to recent years, but with overall reductions in age-standardized incidence and mortality rates from those reported for 2025.1 The expected reductions are, in large part, owing to improvements in prevention, screening, early detection, and treatment.14 Net survival among people with cancer has improved markedly over the past 20 to 25 years.15,16 The combination of a high number of people receiving a cancer diagnosis each year and improved survival contributes to a rapidly growing population of people living with and surviving cancer. A report from the Canadian Cancer Statistics Advisory Committee estimated that more than 1.5 million people in Canada were living with and beyond a cancer diagnosis as of Jan. 1, 2018.17

Five-year survival has improved for many cancers, even for late-stage cancers, through advances in surgical, radiation, and systemic therapies.18,19 Several cancers are now treated with curative intent, where only a decade ago, outcomes were poor. Increased availability of targeted therapies20,21 and immunotherapies22 has led to improved survival outcomes for several clinical populations, including those previously treated with palliative intent.23,24 Despite these advancements, cancer remains the leading cause of death in Canada, with 26% of all deaths in Canada in 2023 being due to cancer.25 Cancer is also the leading cause of premature death, particularly among females.1

The estimates provided here show that the decline in age-standardized incidence for several common cancers is continuing, including for colorectal and lung cancer, 2 of the most common cancers among both males and females in Canada. The decrease in incidence and mortality rates of colorectal cancer likely partially reflects screening efforts by provinces, territories, and health organizations. Since the implementation of endoscopic-based screening and, later, fecal-based index tests in the 2000s, both incidence and mortality rates have decreased by more than 30% owing to the removal of precancer lesions and earlier diagnoses. These reductions have occurred despite jurisdictions not achieving their screening targets.26 Options and outcomes for clinical management of colorectal cancer have also improved considerably with advancements in surgical, radiation, and systemic therapies.27

For lung cancer, tobacco cessation and avoidance appear to be making an impact. In 2026, however, more females are expected to be diagnosed with lung cancers than males. Additional efforts are needed to continue to lessen the impact of the deadliest cancer in Canada. A higher proportion of lung cancers among females than males are not explained by tobacco consumption, suggesting that additional exposures — including lifestyle factors, radon, and air pollution — may need to be addressed to further reduce incidence of lung cancer.28 Many provinces have now initiated screening programs for lung cancer, at various levels of implementation,29 that should enable earlier detection and, combined with advances in the clinical management of disease, should lead to reduced mortality. 30 To date, eligibility for screening across Canada is focused on individuals who have a history of smoking tobacco.31

Mortality associated with bladder cancer has begun to decline notably, with the Canadian Cancer Statistics 2025 report finding a statistically significant annual percent change decrease (from 2017 to 2022) of 3.6% in the ASMR among females.1 A similar trend had previously been observed among males.32 Tobacco use is a leading risk factor for bladder cancer,33,34 highlighting similar impacts of tobacco cessation and avoidance that have driven declining lung cancer rates. Globally, similar declines in mortality have been observed for bladder cancer, including in the United States, some countries in Central and South America, and Europe.34,35

Diagnoses of leukemia have been statistically significantly declining in Canada over the past decade.1 More recently, leukemia mortality has notably declined among both sexes, tripling the rate of decline previously observed.1 Although the factors behind the trends remain unclear and complex, research suggests reductions in environmental exposures and improvements in behavioural risk factors such as tobacco cessation may play a role, as well as parental factors (e.g., intake of folate before conception and during pregnancy) for trends in childhood leukemia specifically.36,37 The reductions in diagnoses of prostate cancer among men and thyroid cancer among both sexes reflect updates in diagnostic practices to reduce the number of clinically indolent cancers, which were reflected in spikes in incidence of prostate cancer in the 1990s19,38,39 and of thyroid cancer in the early 2010s.40,41

For additional information and empirical estimates of cancer-specific rate trends, our 2025 report includes the average annual percent changes in incidence and mortality for each cancer type.1

Our projections highlight concerning trends for several cancers. Increases in the number of cases are projected for cervical cancer, cancers of the head and neck, melanoma, pancreatic cancer, and uterine cancer. Cervical and head and neck cancers are strongly associated with human papillomavirus (HPV) infection. 42 After decades of meaningful declines, incidence rates of cervical cancer have plateaued well above the World Health Organization elimination target (less than 4 per 100 000 females) and have even increased among younger age groups.43 With several jurisdictions switching to HPV testing for cervical screening, maintaining and elevating HPV vaccination rates continues to be important for enhancing the effectiveness of screening programs for cervical cancer and preventing other HPV-related cancers, including some head and neck cancers.44 Increasing incidence rates of pancreatic cancer and uterine cancers (likely driven by increases in endometrial cancer) highlight the potential impact of excess body size and related biological mechanisms, including insulin resistance, on cancer development in Canada.45,46 A recent report from Statistics Canada47 suggests that excess body size is higher than ever before, with 49% of adults having a waist circumference that elevates their risk for these and several other cancers.45,46,48–50

Limitations

Despite highly accurate data on the diagnosis and outcomes of cancer in Canada, individual-level data on relevant socio-demographic elements (e.g., race, ethnicity, income, education) or risk factors (e.g., smoking commercial tobacco or anthropometric measures) are lacking.51 The burden of cancer prevalence is likely not felt equally across Canada.52 Individual-level measures of social determinants and other important factors are not systematically captured and included in provincial or territorial cancer registries. This is a noted limitation of this study and health data resources in Canada, as several analyses have highlighted the impact of socioeconomic status on cancer incidence, mortality, and other outcomes.53,54

Although the projections are based on the most up-to-date cancer data available nationally, they are subject to variability over time. The registration of cancer is complex and resource and time intensive, resulting in delays in data availability. The projected incidence estimates are based, in part, on data following the initial years of the COVID-19 pandemic (2021, 2022). We omitted the 2020 data year in analyses to not erroneously skew projections, given our previous report highlighting the potential impact of drops in registration.1 This is in line with suggested approaches from other cancer surveillance efforts.55 Data from Quebec (cancer incidence from 2018 to 2022) and Nova Scotia (cancer incidence from 2020 to 2022; cancer mortality in 2023) were not included in the projection models; estimates were projected for those years. Further, changes to coding of cancer types have been retroactively applied to all diagnosis years in alignment with changes in definitions for national cancer registries.

Conclusion

The projected estimates of cancer for 2026 underscore the substantial impact cancer will continue to pose in Canada, with differences by sex. Although advances in prevention, screening and early detection, treatment and care, and survivorship have contributed to reductions in incidence and mortality for some types of cancer, further innovation, investment, and sustained commitment are required to achieve equitable progress and generate measurable, lasting improvements in cancer outcomes across the country.
