Understanding helmet compliance through an environmental lens
Authors: Shreya Sengupta, S. Adithya Prasad
Published: 2026

Sir,

We read the article, “Physical environmental attributes and helmet use in an Indian metropolis: An ecological study” with great interest.[1] We would like to appreciate the authors for exploring a very under-researched yet relevant dimension of safety, which is, the potential influence of macro-level environmental factors on helmet use among two-wheeler riders in a metropolitan setting of India. Since there is a substantial burden of road traffic injuries in India, and across the globe and the already proven protective effect of the use of helmets, inquires like these are important and timely.[23]

This study benefits from a large cohort, uniform assessment of rider helmet use at various sites, and is an innovative attempt to correlate environmental variables with behavioral outcomes. It has demonstrated that there was no significant relationship with temperature, which may assist in dispelling the myth that warm temperatures prevent riders from wearing helmets, a common excuse for non-compliance and provides important implications for enforcement messaging along with risk communication. The finding that riders reported a positive relationship between humidity and helmet use, suggests that the relationship between environmental variables and rider behavior is complex and may be subject to multiple interpretations.

However, there are several limitations in the methodology of the study. As the authors themselves noted, the ecological design of the study means that it is not possible to make strong causal inferences based on the findings and is vulnerable to ecological fallacy since individual behavior may not reflect group-level associations.[4] Furthermore, the study is prone to the problem of ecological fallacy: The fallacy of inferring relationships between variables from group data that do not hold for individuals within those groups. The individual level variables that could potentially be confounding the relationship between the characteristics of helmets and their use include rider demographics, helmet quality and comfort, trip purpose, enforcement exposure, perception of risk, and so on. None of these variables were directly measured in the study and therefore it is difficult to know for certain how important the potential sources of bias were. Earlier studies have identified a variety of potential factors, including a range of behavioral, sensory, and policing-related influences.[5] Additionally, the reliance on secondary meteorological data from a single source may not be accurate and can lead to misclassification due to the presence of micro-environmental variations experienced by the riders at the point of travel.

From a community medicine perspective, our findings indicate that rider environmental discomfort alone is not a sufficient factor to explain non-compliance with cycling helmet wear requirements. We recommend a multilevel or mixed-methods approach that links individual rider behavior to precise and valid environmental data to facilitate better injury prevention practice. The authors’ suggestion for adapting the design of helmets to climate conditions is commendable and will require the collaborative efforts of many, including those from the public health community, road transport organizations, and manufacturers. The study is an important contribution to road safety literature in India by challenging common assumptions about weather and helmet use.
