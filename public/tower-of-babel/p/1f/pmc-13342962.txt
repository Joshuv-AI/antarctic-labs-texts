Fulminant acute hemorrhagic encephalomyelitis mimicking Guillain-Barré syndrome in a child: A fatal case report
Authors: Ghizlane EI Aidouni, Hamid Madani, Manal Merbouh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute hemorrhagic encephalomyelitis (AHE), or Weston Hurst disease, is vanishingly rare and almost always fatal. A fulminant variant of acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, it is defined by rapidly progressive demyelination, parenchymal necrosis, and hemorrhagic white matter destruction. Here we report a 4-year-old girl who arrived with ascending flaccid tetraparesis 1 week after a flu-like illness, a picture that, not unreasonably, pointed toward Guillain-Baré syndrome. Within 24 hours, the diagnosis collapsed: she developed impaired consciousness, bilateral Babinski signs, and dysphagia signs with no place in a peripheral neuropathy. Emergency brain and spinal MRI then showed the full picture: bilateral confluent T2/FLAIR white matter hyperintensities with susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI)-confirmed hemorrhagic petechiae and parenchymal necrosis, consistent with AHE. High-dose corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulins were started immediately. She died on day 2. The lesson is uncomfortable but clear: normal CSF and an ascending paralysis do not rule out a central catastrophe. When central signs emerge—any of them—emergency MRI with SWI/T2* is non-negotiable.Keywords: Child, Acute hemorrhagic encephalomyelitis, Weston Hurst disease, Guillain-Baré syndrome, ADEM, Case report

Introduction

Acute hemorrhagic encephalomyelitis (AHE), also referred to as Weston Hurst disease, is one of those diagnoses that clinicians hope never to face. Fewer than 100 cases have been published across all age groups, the majority in adults, and pediatric series remain essentially anecdotal [1]. It is the most aggressive form of acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM)—characterized histologically by necrotizing vasculitis of small vessels, extensive demyelination, microhemorrhages, and massive cerebral edema—and it has a way of killing patients before the diagnosis is even considered.

Clinically, AHE follows a viral infection or vaccination by days to weeks. The onset is sudden, the neurological deterioration is rapid, and the outcome is grim despite intensive management. Cerebral MRI shows multifocal white matter lesions broadly similar to ADEM, but with a critical addition: areas of necrosis and microhemorrhages, optimally detected on susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) or T2* gradient echo sequences. These sequences are not optional, they are what separates AHE from nonhemorrhagic ADEM radiologically, and missing them means missing the diagnosis. In children, the diagnostic challenge is compounded by the fact that early presentations can look exactly like Guillain-Baré syndrome (GBS), encephalitis, or vasculitis [2].

The IPMSSG criteria define ADEM as a first polysymptomatic event with encephalopathy and multifocal MRI lesions, but say nothing specific about the hemorrhagic variant [3]. That silence matters clinically.

We report a 4-year-old girl whose ascending tetra paresis was initially read as GBS—a reasonable call, as it turns out—but who deteriorated within hours to fulminant AHE confirmed by MRI and died despite aggressive treatment.

Case presentation

The patient was a 4-year and 3-month-old girl with no notable personal or family history, appropriately vaccinated according to the National Immunization Program. A week before admission, she developed a flu-like syndrome—fever, cough, rhinorrhea—managed at home with acetaminophen. That part seemed unremarkable. What followed was not (Table 1).

She rapidly lost the ability to stand or walk. At an initial emergency consultation, examination showed tetraparesis with present deep tendon reflexes (DTRs), no sphincter disturbance, SpO₂ 97% on room air. Initial labs showed CRP 22 mg/L, WBC 8120/mm³, procalcitonin 2.15 ng/mL, otherwise normal chemistry, and a chest radiograph consistent with bronchial syndrome. She was transferred to the PICU.

On PICU admission (H0), she was conscious—GCS 15/15—and hemodynamically stable (BP 100/50 mmHg, HR 90 bpm, SpO₂ 97%). But her DTRs were now absent. Flaccid tetraparesis. Urinary retention requiring catheterization. No meningeal syndrome, no demonstrable sensory level. The shift from present to absent DTRs within a few hours likely reflects rapid demyelinating progression rather than an inconsistency in examination.

Brain CT before lumbar puncture was normal (Fig. 1) CSF: 0 cells/mm³, protein 0.41 g/L, normal glucose—no albuminocytologic dissociation. ABG showed only mild metabolic acidosis (pH 7.36, HCO₃—20.8 mmol/L, PaCO₂ 36.5 mmHg, PaO₂ 87 mmHg). The working diagnosis was GBS a defensible call, given that albuminocytologic dissociation is frequently absent in the first week of GBS. Monitoring, empirical amoxicillin-clavulanate, analgesia, enteral feeding, and EEG surveillance were initiated.

Brain CT scan (prelumbar puncture): normal parenchyma without edema, hemorrhage, or space-occupying lesion.

Less than 24 hours later, everything changed. GCS fell to 10/15. Bilateral Babinski signs appeared. The tetraparesis until now flaccid and areflexic became spastic. That single finding is incompatible with a peripheral neuropathy. Dysphagia developed. The picture had shifted from peripheral to central, unmistakably. GBS was no longer on the table. Emergency MRI was the only logical next step. SpO₂ held at 97%-98%, but bulbar dysfunction and deteriorating consciousness mandated orotracheal intubation and protective ventilation.

Emergency brain and spinal MRI (Fig. 2) showed bilateral confluent T2/FLAIR hyperintensities of the supratentorial white matter. SWI/T2* sequences (Fig. 3) confirmed what conventional sequences alone could not have shown: punctate hypointense foci consistent with hemorrhagic petechiae, alongside areas of parenchymal necrosis. Spinal MRI: no cord lesions. MR angiography excluded vascular malformation. The diagnosis was AHE. Methylprednisolone 30 mg/kg/d IV was started immediately, combined with IVIg 2 g/kg over 2 days. Plasma exchange was considered but could not be safely initiated given the severity of neurological and hemodynamic deterioration.

Deterioration was relentless. Brainstem dysfunction and autonomic instability set in despite sedation, mechanical ventilation, and immunotherapy. Follow-up CT (Fig. 4) showed massive cerebral edema, transtentorial herniation, refractory intracranial hypertension. All ICP control measures were applied. The child died on day 2 of PICU admission.

Discussion

AHE is, in plain terms, a disease that kills. Regarded as the hyperacute, necrotizing-hemorrhagic variant of ADEM, it has been reported in fewer than 100 patients worldwide across all age groups—and pediatric cases are particularly sparse [1]. The presumed mechanism is a dysregulated postinfectious autoimmune cascade: molecular mimicry or bystander activation triggers necrotizing vasculitis of small cerebral vessels, perivenular demyelination, hemorrhagic extravasation, and rapidly progressive edema. Our patient’s flu-like prodrome 1 week before onset fits the most commonly described trigger [1,5]. Worth noting: recent literature has documented AHE following COVID-19, including SARS-CoV-2 Omicron infection in children, with radiological profiles and outcomes indistinguishable from classic postviral cases [6].

The initial GBS diagnosis here was not unreasonable. Ascending flaccid tetraparesis, absent DTRs, postinfectious context, normal CSF—these are the textbook criteria. The catch, of course, is that CSF can be entirely normal in the first week of GBS; albuminocytologic dissociation often lags behind the clinical onset by 7-10 days [2]. What is not part of GBS, under any circumstances, is impaired consciousness, bilateral Babinski signs, or a rapid switch from flaccid to spastic tetraparesis. When those appeared within 24 hours the diagnosis had to change. The key differentiating factor is that central neurological findings such as pyramidal signs or altered consciousness are anatomically incompatible with GBS; ADEM shares white matter lesions with AHE but lacks hemorrhagic petechiae; and AHE alone combines SWI-confirmed hemorrhage with necrosis and this speed of deterioration (Table 2).

The MRI protocol matters enormously here, perhaps more than in any other demyelinating emergency. Standard T2/FLAIR will show the white matter lesions. What it will not reliably show is the hemorrhagic component. For that, SWI or T2* gradient echo sequences are non-negotiable, and their absence from the protocol risks a misdiagnosis of nonhemorrhagic ADEM with potentially different management implications.

In our patient, the protocol included axial T2/FLAIR (bilateral confluent supratentorial hyperintensities), DWI/ADC (restricted diffusion in necrotic areas), SWI/T2* (punctate hypointense foci confirming hemorrhagic petechiae; the diagnostic hallmark), MR angiography (vascular malformation excluded), and spinal sequences (no cord lesions). Gadolinium enhancement was not documented; this may reflect early-stage disease or the timing of imaging relative to blood-brain barrier disruption. The EEG was planned but could not be formally interpreted before intubation—no clinical seizures were observed in the awake phase.

There are no randomized trials in AHE. Management is built from case series, expert opinion, and extrapolation from ADEM protocols. The standard approach combines: high-dose methylprednisolone (20-30 mg/kg/d for 3-5 days, then oral taper), intravenous immunoglobulins (2 g/kg total), and plasma exchange for cases with insufficient response or fulminant evolution [[1], [7]]. Aggressive ICP management—sedation, osmotherapy, controlled hyperventilation, and decompressive craniectomy in refractory cases runs in parallel.

Our patient received corticosteroids and IVIg promptly. Plasma exchange was discussed but was not initiated, given the speed and severity of hemodynamic and neurological deterioration. Whether earlier plasmapheresis would have changed the outcome is unknowable. The rapid development of refractory ICP and death within 48 hours is, unfortunately, entirely consistent with published mortality data for fulminant pediatric AHE.

Roughly half of patients with AHE die. That figure ∼50% has remained stubbornly stable across decades of case reports and is substantially worse than ADEM (<5%) or GBS (∼5%) [1]. Factors consistently associated with poor outcome include: rapid progression to coma (within 24-48 hours of onset) and diffuse MRI lesion burden, brainstem involvement, [[1], [8]]. Our patient had all of them.

The modifiable side of the equation is narrower but real. Early combined immunotherapy, aggressive ICP control, and critically getting the diagnosis right in the first place are the variables that appear to shift outcomes in the literature, even if the evidence base remains thin [5,9]. A non-negligible minority of pediatric cases with early diagnosis and aggressive treatment have achieved near-complete neurological recovery [5]. That possibility is what makes rapid recognition matter.

This case presents several important messages for clinical practice:

•Postinfectious ascending flaccid tetraparesis with normal CSF does not equal GBS. Any central sign—impaired consciousness, pyramidal findings, bulbar dysfunction—demands immediate neuroimaging, not a wait-and-see approach.

Conclusion

Weston Hurst disease can look like GBS for a matter of hours. Then it declares itself, violently, as something else entirely. The signal to watch for is the emergence of central signs—impaired consciousness, pyramidal features, dysphagia—in a child with postinfectious ascending paralysis. When those appear, emergency MRI with SWI/T2* sequences are the immediate priority, followed without delay by aggressive immunotherapy and neurocritical care. This case is a sobering illustration of how fast the window closes. Early diagnosis is not just clinically important—in AHE, it may be the only chance.
