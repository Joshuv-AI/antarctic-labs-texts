Age-Related Neurovascular Dysfunction in the Retina and Potential Pathogenic Mechanisms
Authors: Yanchi Wu, Qiuchen Cao, Qinghuai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the accelerating global demographic shift toward an aging population, retinal aging has emerged as a major contributor to visual decline in older individuals and represents a key risk factor for several age-associated ocular diseases. Age-associated changes in the retinal neurovascular system have been increasingly investigated as potential contributors to the structural and functional alterations observed during normal retinal aging. Neurons, glial cells, and vascular endothelial cells exhibit age-related structural and metabolic adaptations, which may influence vascular regulation and tissue homeostasis in the aging retina. Alterations in components of the neurovascular unit have been proposed to affect metabolic coupling, vascular regulation, and inflammatory signaling. This review systematically summarizes the alterations in neurovascular anatomy and changes in function during retinal aging, and discusses mechanistic insights and emerging strategies that may help to preserve retinal resilience during aging.

Population aging is a rapidly escalating global phenomenon. The proportion of individuals aged 60 and older has increased from 8.0% in 1950 to 13.5% in 2020, and is projected to reach 28.2% by 2100.1 However, the extension of lifespan does not necessarily coincide with an equivalent increase in healthspan.2 Visual impairments, including deficits in near and distance vision as well as decreased contrast sensitivity, affect more than 25% of the elderly population.3 Concurrently, aging is a major risk factor for several neurodegenerative and ocular disorders.4 Retinal degenerative diseases such as AMD, glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy and Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathophysiology in the retina contribute substantially to visual disability in older adults.5 These conditions collectively compromise visual health in older adults, leading to blurred vision and loss of visual field, as well as more complex visual dysfunctions such as decreased contrast sensitivity and impaired stereopsis.2 Microcirculatory alterations in the retina and choroid can be assessed in vivo using optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA).6,7 Alterations in components of the neurovascular unit (NVU) have been described in various ocular and neurological diseases.8 However, the extent to which comparable changes occur during normal retinal aging remains incompletely defined.

The NVU functions as an integrated entity composed of neurons, glial cells, and vascular components. Through coordinated communication, these components contribute to the regulation of retinal microenvironmental homeostasis.9 The structural and functional integrity of the NVU is essential for maintaining the blood–retina barrier (BRB), matching local blood flow to metabolic demand, and ensuring the effective clearance of metabolic waste products.10 During aging, this integrated system may undergo structural and functional adaptations that influence communication between neuronal and vascular components. Human imaging studies have identified age-related changes in retinal thickness and vascular parameters,11,12 whereas experimental models have reported vascular and cellular alterations with advancing age.13 Altered vascular regulation may influence oxygen and nutrient delivery, potentially interacting with mitochondrial function, cellular senescence pathways, and intercellular signaling processes.14 In models of ischemic retinal disease, senescent cells have been shown to secrete senescence-associated secretory phenotypes, which propagate senescence in neighboring cells via paracrine signaling, thereby accelerating pathological vascular proliferation.15 Collectively, these findings suggest that interactions among neuronal, glial, and vascular components during aging may modulate the trajectory of retinal aging, although the relationships among these processes remain to be clarified.

Structural Characteristics of the Neurovasculature in the Aging Retina

Aging is a multifactorial biological process associated with molecular and cellular alterations16 that affect multiple tissues, including the retina. Alterations in neurovascular structure and regulation are thought to be influenced by cellular aging14,17 (Fig. 1). At the anatomical level, retinal aging is associated with measurable changes in retinal layer thickness and alterations in the neuronal, glial, and vascular architecture. Neuronal–Glial Network in the Healthy Retina

The retina is a highly organized, multilayered structure whose visual signaling system relies on precise synaptic connectivity among neurons and coordinated homeostatic regulation by glial cells (Fig. 2A). In the context of visual transmission, signals are conveyed through a vertical pathway: photoreceptor cells, which initiate retinal signaling, convert light stimuli into electrical signals, and form synaptic connections with the dendrites of bipolar cells in the outer plexiform layer. The axons of bipolar cells then form synapses with the dendrites of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) in the inner plexiform layer, completing the primary relay of visual information to the brain.21 Meanwhile, horizontal cell processes located in the outer portion of the inner nuclear layer (INL) form synapses with cones, rods, and bipolar cells. By exerting lateral inhibition on photoreceptor output, horizontal cells modulate luminance and visual adaptation,34 establishing the fundamental architecture for image formation.

At the homeostatic level, retinal glial cells provide essential structural and metabolic support. Human retina harbors three major glial populations: microglia and two types of macroglia—astrocytes and Müller cells.35 Müller cells are the predominant macroglial population and extend radially across the entire thickness of the neural retina.36 They recycle neurotransmitters such as glutamate and γ-aminobutyric acid, regulate extracellular ion (e.g., K⁺) and water balance, and supply metabolic substrates to sustain neuronal activity.37 In the inner retina, Müller endfeet interface with retinal vessels and contribute to BRB maintenance through interactions with endothelial cells (ECs) and pericytes.38 Notably, Müller cells perform distinct structural, optical, and neurovascular regulatory functions within the foveal NVU.35 In the human fovea, Müller glia are organized into two specialized populations: foveolar Müller cells, which form the characteristic Müller cell cone, and z-shaped Müller cells in the foveal walls that extend obliquely through the Henle fiber layer.39,40 Within the foveola, Müller cells constitute the only macroglial type and dominate a largely avascular and astrocyte-free microenvironment.41 Structurally, foveolar Müller cells provide mechanical stabilization through their dense inner processes and glial fibrillary acidic protein–associated cytoskeletal support, helping to maintain the foveal architecture and resist mechanical stress.41,42 Functionally, they are specialized for optical and structural roles, including light guidance and macular pigment storage, rather than direct metabolic coupling to vessels or synapses.43 In contrast, Müller cells in the foveal walls contact retinal vessels and express glutamine synthetase and GLAST, supporting neuronal metabolism and contributing to neurovascular interactions.43 In contrast, astrocytes are primarily confined to the nerve fiber layer and ganglion cell layer (GCL), where they form a three-dimensional network closely associated with RGC axons and the superficial vascular plexus (SVP).35 Together with microglia, they support RGC survival, maintain metabolic homeostasis, and assist in angiogenesis and BRB integrity.44 Microglia, the resident immune cells of the retina, are primarily distributed in the GCL, inner plexiform layer, INL, and outer plexiform layer, with a perivascular localization.45 They express pattern recognition receptors, such as Toll-like and NOD-like receptors, which enable them to detect pathogen- and damage-associated molecular patterns. This process initiates phagocytic clearance and preserves immune homeostasis.46 In addition to immune regulation, microglia are key modulators of the neurovascular system: they regulate synaptogenesis and pruning to stabilize neuronal circuits and directly participate in vascular development and maintenance.47 The precise integration of neuronal circuitry and glial architecture is collectively responsible for normal retinal function, forming the structural basis on which age-related NVU dysfunction develops.

Alterations in the Neural and Glial Structure of the Aging Retina

With advancing age, the retinal neuronal–glial network undergoes marked structural alterations. At the neuronal level, progressive thinning occurs across multiple layers—including the GCL, outer nuclear layer, INL, inner plexiform layer, and outer plexiform layer. This reflects both neuronal loss and impaired synaptic connectivity.48–50 Such morphological changes are consistent with decreased efficiency in visual signal transmission. In humans, up to 25% of RGCs are lost in the macular and nasal retina of elderly individuals.12 In the retinas of aged mice, photoreceptors exhibit degenerative changes,51 and aberrant dendritic extensions of bipolar and horizontal cells into the outer nuclear layer disrupt normal synaptic contacts with photoreceptors.21 These cascading alterations compromise the structural integrity of the visual transmission pathway, leading to progressive functional decline.

In parallel, glial cells, which serve as the essential intermediaries between neurons and blood vessels, also undergo significant age-related changes. Structural abnormalities in glial cells indicate concurrent neuronal injury or loss9 and undermine glial support for other NVU components.52 Focusing first on Müller cells, the predominant glial population in the retina, although Müller glial cell numbers appear to be stable with age in single-cell datasets, transcriptomic analyses reveal strong inflammatory activation and extracellular matrix remodeling,22 consistent with ultrastructural evidence of progressively increased Müller cell gliosis in the aging human retina (minimal before ∼60 years, mild at 60–80 years, and severe at >80 years).23 In the macula, Müller cells remodeling is prominent around Henle fibers, where thin, lightly investing processes in younger eyes are replaced by thick, dense, intermediate filament–rich ensheathment with aging, frequently accompanied by extracellular and glial edematous changes.23 Aging is further associated with mitochondrial disorganisation, accumulation of lipids and autophagosomes, lipofuscin granules, and phagolysosomal inclusions containing phagocytosed debris and axonal remnants within inner processes.23,24 Müller cells maintain the integrity of NVUs through mechanical tension53 and dynamically regulate their volume to modulate the extracellular space and neuronal excitability.52 In this process, the transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) channel and aquaporin-4 (AQP4) play pivotal roles in maintaining ionic and osmotic equilibrium.54 Chronic activation of TRPV4, a mechanosensitive Ca²⁺ channel, induces RGC death.25 In mouse models of chronic ocular hypertension, aged animals exhibit decreased responsiveness to TRPV4 antagonists, indicating an age-dependent dysregulation of this channel.

In addition to changes in Müller cells, astrocytes also undergo significant age-related alterations. In humans, astrocytes show an age-dependent increase in number and exhibit reactive gliosis.22 Consistent with this, increased immunoreactivity for glial fibrillary acidic protein in the retinas of old mice indicates reactive proliferation and activation of astrocytes.21 Such astrogliosis may reshape the local microenvironment, with downstream consequences for neurovascular regulation. AQP4 is localized predominantly to the endfeet of astrocytes and Müller cells with perivascular polarization.55 During aging, reduced expression of AQP4 around retinal blood vessels has been reported in mice,56 which may impair the structural and functional integrity of the NVU by disrupting glial volume regulation. Specifically, microglia adopt an amoeboid morphology characterized by reduced branching and soma hypertrophy, along with diminished motility and surveillance capacity. The shift toward a proinflammatory phenotype further weakens immune homeostasis and tissue repair.26

Retinal Vascular System

In addition to neuronal degeneration and glial activation, the vascular system of the aging retina also undergoes marked structural deterioration, characterized by progressive decreases in vascular density, capillary network atrophy, and loss of vascular cells.27,28,57–59 These changes encompass extensive rarefaction and remodeling of the vascular plexuses across retinal layers,58,60 accompanied by abnormal extracellular deposits.27

Anatomy of the Normal Retinal Vasculature

The retina is one of the most metabolically demanding tissues in the human body.61 It possesses a highly organized dual blood supply: the inner two-thirds of the retina are nourished by the central retinal artery and its branches, forming the inner retinal vasculature. The outer one-third, including photoreceptors, is supplied by the choroidal circulation.62 The intraretinal vasculature of mammals comprises a complex, multilayered network composed of the SVP in the GCL, the intermediate vascular plexus within the INL, and the deep vascular plexus (DVP) at its basal side.63 In humans, an additional radial peripapillary capillary plexus radiates from the optic disc to ensure efficient perfusion of the nerve fiber layer. The venous network parallels the arterial system, together supporting the retina's exceptional energy demands and visual performance (Fig. 2A).

At the cellular level, ECs maintain vascular barrier integrity through tight, gap, and adherens junctions. Pericytes, which tightly ensheath capillaries, act as central regulators of microcirculatory flow and neurovascular coupling by expressing α-smooth muscle actin and responding rapidly to neuronal activity to adjust capillary diameter and local perfusion.64,65 Recent advances in the mouse retina have identified interpericyte tunneling nanotubes (IP-TNTs), which are tubular structures that connect pericytes across different capillary segments. These nanotubes facilitate organelle transfer (e.g., mitochondria) and mediate intercellular Ca²⁺ signaling, thereby synchronizing neurovascular coupling across capillary domains.66 IP-TNTs are widely distributed throughout all vascular plexuses of the retina and represent a fundamental ultrastructural basis for pericyte–pericyte communication and coordinated neurovascular regulation.

Anatomy of the Normal Choroidal Vasculature

The choroidal vascular bed serves as the principal blood supply to the outer retina. It is primarily supplied by the posterior ciliary arterial system (including the short and long posterior ciliary arteries) and drained by the vortex veins.67–70 Structurally, the choroid is organized into Haller's layer, Sattler's layer, and the choriocapillaris (CC) adjacent to Bruch's membrane (BM).71 In contrast with the traditional concept of a continuous, freely communicating vascular network, in vivo studies have demonstrated that choroidal circulation exhibits a segmental distribution, with the posterior ciliary arteries and their branches functioning as end arteries and forming watershed zones between vascular territories.72 The CC consists of lobular functional units supplied by terminal arterioles and is characterized by wide, fenestrated capillaries that support high-volume exchange, thereby providing oxygen and nutrients to the outer retina while facilitating metabolic waste clearance.73

Age-Related Alterations in the Retinal Vasculature

During aging, the retinal vascular system exhibits not only gross morphological changes such as decreased vascular density, but also progressive disruption of the microenvironmental homeostasis, including degeneration and loss of vascular cells as well as accumulation of perivascular deposits (Fig. 2B). OCTA-based studies have reported age-dependent declines in retinal vascular density,11 blood flow, and capillary functionality.57 Human lifespan OCTA data further show that retinal vessel density in the radial vascular network, SVP, and DVP decreases significantly with age, with the strongest inverse association observed in the DVP, suggesting that the deep retinal microvasculature may serve as a particularly sensitive imaging marker of age-related microvascular degeneration.58 Consistent with these OCTA findings, large-scale fundus imaging in human cohorts has confirmed an age-dependent decrease in branching points and terminal capillaries, resulting in a significant decrease in overall vascular network complexity.59 In animal models, the density of intermediate vascular plexus and DVP decreases by 14% to 32% with age in mice, whereas the SVP remains relatively preserved.60 In addition, microaneurysm formation is a typical phenotype of vascular degeneration during aging, in which focal dilation and structural damage of the vessel wall further compromise vascular integrity,33,74 although its underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

At the cellular level, these morphological alterations arise from degeneration and loss of multiple vascular cell types. In normally aging mice, acellular capillary formation increases progressively with age,13 whereas ultrastructural analyses in humans reveal ECs and pericyte degeneration or dropout.27,28 Degenerating ECs and pericytes exhibit a largely shared senescence signature, marked by organelle and cytoplasmic filament loss, basement membrane thickening, and accumulation of lipofuscin and autophagic vacuoles.27,28 These alterations are attributed to cell cycle arrest, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype.29 Chronic stress-induced overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) leads to DNA damage and mitochondrial impairment, which may contribute to cellular senescence. Simultaneously, the downregulation of antiaging molecules such as Klotho and FGF21, coupled with activation of prosenescent pathways like the angiotensin II–mammalian target of rapamycin–IGFBP3/5 axis, further accelerates EC deterioration.30 In addition to intrinsic vascular senescence, intercellular communication contributes to retinal vascular structural injury. In degenerative retinal diseases, aberrant intracellular Ca²⁺ influx disrupts IP-TNT integrity and weakens pericyte-mediated neurovascular coupling. However, the inhibition of pathological Ca²⁺ entry restores IP-TNT functionality, enhances pericyte communication, and promotes RGC survival and function.31 Furthermore, senescent ECs secrete elevated levels of matrix metalloproteinase 9 while reducing secretion of brain-derived neurotrophic factor, thereby exacerbating pericyte and RGC loss.75 Collectively, these intracellular and extracellular structural alterations converge to impair the structural and functional coupling within the NVU.

Age-Related Alterations in Choroidal Microvasculature

With aging, the CC shows concurrent structural and functional changes across human and nonhuman primate studies. In a human in vivo OCTA study, systematic CC quantification showed age-related increases in CC vessel thickness, together with decreases in vascular complexity, distribution uniformity, and perfusion.76,77 In a cynomolgus macaque spectral domain OCT study, CC thickness increased significantly with age across the nasal, foveal, and temporal regions.77 The same study further examined human postmortem specimens and found positive age associations for total CC thickness and lumen diameter, supporting age-related CC structural remodeling in humans.77 In addition, a human OCTA-based velocimetry study reported an age-related decline in CC red blood cell velocity, particularly in the central macula.78 Overall, available evidence indicates that CC aging is associated with measurable changes in thickness/morphology, perfusion-related metrics, and flow velocity in humans, with parallel structural trends observed in nonhuman primates.76–78

Vascular Extracellular Matrix Remodeling and Deposits

Age-related vascular alterations in the retina are accompanied by remodeling of the extracellular matrix and accumulation of various deposits. Although direct evidence of extracellular matrix remodeling was not assessed, retinal arteries and veins in older individuals are approximately 22% and 17% thicker, respectively, than those in younger adults, indirectly suggesting substantial extracellular matrix remodeling during aging.33 Meanwhile, multiple age-related deposits accumulate within the aging retina. One of the most common of these deposits is lipofuscin, which predominantly localizes within the RPE, although it is also detected within the ECs and pericytes of aged human retinas.27 These lipofuscin granules exhibit lower electron density than those in RPE cells and are often surrounded by an electron-dense rim.74 Excessive lipofuscin accumulation impairs cellular function, likely through photo-oxidative reactions that trigger apoptosis,79 although alternative mechanisms such as lysosomal aggregation–induced membrane permeabilization and atypical necroptosis have been proposed.80

Moreover, abnormal accumulation of proteins typically associated with neurodegenerative diseases, including tau, α-synuclein (α-syn), ubiquitin, and β-amyloid (Aβ), is frequently observed in the aging human retinas.81 Tau accumulation has been shown to induce BRB leakage and RGC loss82; however, its age dependency remains debated, with some studies reporting a positive correlation,81 whereas others found no significant association.83 α-syn, a central molecule in Parkinson's disease, correlates with reduced retinal vascular density.84 In elderly individuals, α-syn and ubiquitin aggregates colocalize within the INL, with inclusion body numbers increasing exponentially with age.81 Conversely, α-syn also plays physiological roles in regulating synaptic transmission, and its synaptic reduction during aging may compromise synaptic integrity.85 Importantly, Aβ, a peptide implicated in both neurodegeneration and BRB breakdown,86 is also present in the retinas of normal-aged donors.87 In addition to deposition in photoreceptor outer segments and the BM–RPE interface, Aβ preferentially accumulates around retinal vessels,56,88 suggesting a link between Aβ aggregation and NVU dysfunction during aging. Pericytes have emerged as critical cellular targets of Aβ toxicity.56,89,90 Aβ accumulation correlates with reduced pericyte coverage and downregulation of major facilitator superfamily domain-containing protein 2a.56 Evidence from in vitro and animal models suggests that Aβ disrupts pericyte function by inducing Ca²⁺ dysregulation, oxidative stress, and inflammatory activation. However, the precise mechanisms of Aβ involvement in physiological aging remain to be elucidated.

Neurovascular Function in the Aging Retina

Age-associated structural alterations of the NVU, together with metabolic and homeostatic dysregulation, act synergistically to impair multiple core functions of the retinal neurovascular system. These deficits affect essential processes such as neurovascular coupling,91 blood circulation,92 barrier integrity,93 and metabolic waste clearance94,95 (Fig. 3).

Fundamental Physiological Roles of the Retinal NVU

The coordinated structure and function of the retinal NVU provide a biological foundation for the orderly development of the vascular network and the activity-dependent regulation of blood flow. This neuron–glia–vascular coupling operates from development through maturation to ensure precise energy delivery to the metabolically active retina.

During development, retinal neurons actively shape vascular patterning through mechanical and chemical cues, including Piezo2-dependent guidance by perivascular Fam19a4/Nts-positive RGCs96 and temporally restricted dopaminergic signaling that modulates VEGF–DLL4–Jagged1–Notch-mediated vascular branching,97 while microglia98,99 and astrocytes100 coordinate angiogenesis and vessel maturation through soluble factors and VEGF-related support.100 In the mature retina, neuronal activity is translated into functional hyperemia through neuron–glia–vascular interactions, in which glutamate release and extracellular K⁺ changes evoke glial Ca²⁺ signaling that coordinates smooth muscle cells and ECs to increase local blood flow according to metabolic demand.91,101,102

In recent years, high spatiotemporal resolution retinal vascular imaging approaches (e.g., adaptive optics rolling slit ophthalmoscope imaging) have enabled dynamic in vivo characterization of flicker-evoked vasomotor responses in the living human eye, providing more direct imaging evidence for the in vivo assessment of retinal neurovascular coupling (rNVC) function.103 Building on this work, clinical retinal vascular analyses based on flicker responses have further shown that arterial flicker responses are influenced by factors including age, blood pressure, and baseline vessel caliber.92 Human retinal hemodynamic studies show age-related decreases in basal retinal blood volume, blood velocity, and total blood flow, with a concomitant trend toward reduced flicker-evoked arteriolar dilation, supporting an age-associated decline in retinal neurovascular responsiveness.92,104,105 Using functional OCTA in physiologically aging C57BL/6J mice, recent work demonstrated that flicker-evoked retinal neurovascular coupling responses across the trilaminar retinal vascular network (SVP, intermediate capillary plexus and deep capillary plexus) decline with age, with significant attenuation in older mice despite limited overt structural vascular changes, indicating that functional microvascular impairment may precede detectable morphological abnormalities.106 Mechanistically, these age-related hemodynamic changes likely reflect progressive disruption of retinal NVU coupling. With aging, mitochondrial structural disorganization, decreased oxidative phosphorylation efficiency, and increased ROS generation impair both RGC integrity18 and glial Ca²⁺ signaling dynamics, thereby weakening activity-dependent neurovascular signaling. Accordingly, neurovascular coupling may become attenuated, which could contribute to decreased basal perfusion and potentially increase retinal susceptibility to circulatory and metabolic dysfunction.91 Bidirectional feedback between RGCs and the microvasculature is central to this process, as RGC/axonal degeneration can secondarily drive vascular atrophy, whereas insufficient superficial capillary perfusion can aggravate ganglion cell hypoxia and RGC loss.107

Beyond the inner retinal NVU, the inner choroid/outer retina NVU describes coordinated coupling among the CC, the RPE, and photoreceptor outer segments to align outer retinal metabolic demand with choroidal blood supply and metabolite clearance.108 Unlike the largely autonomic innervation–free inner retinal NVU, regulation of choroidal perfusion is dominated by extrinsic neural control. This includes sympathetic noradrenaline/α1-adrenoceptor–mediated vasoconstriction and parasympathetic nitric oxide (NO)-dependent vasodilation, with additional trigeminal CGRP-containing sensory input implicated in light-evoked choroidal control.109–113 Although studies on age-related changes in choroidal neurovascular regulation remain limited, evidence from both human and animal models converges on a common theme that neural control of choroidal perfusion may diminish with age.114–116 In healthy human donor eyes, vasoactive intestinal polypeptide–positive perivascular (vasodilatory, parasympathetic) nerve fiber coverage in the submacular choroid declines with age and is positively associated with choroidal vessel caliber, suggesting that decreased vasodilatory innervation may accompany age-related narrowing of submacular choroid vessels.114 In pigeons, aging is associated with reduced basal choroidal blood flow and attenuated neurally evoked choroidal vasodilation to either Edinger–Westphal stimulation or retinal illumination, alongside a progressive loss of ciliary ganglion–derived intrachoroidal nerve fibers.115,116

Blood Circulation and Energy Metabolism

Under physiological conditions, the NVU dynamically matches blood flow and energy supply to neuronal activity, maintaining metabolic homeostasis in the retina. However, this regulatory mechanism progressively deteriorates with age, characterized by reduced vasodilatory capacity and perfusion efficiency during periods of increased neuronal demand.117 Clinical studies consistently demonstrate that both retinal microcirculation and central retinal artery blood flow decrease with age (by approximately 6%–11% per decade).57,118 Abdolahi et al.119 used a multimodal imaging system to quantitatively assess retinal oxygenation and hemodynamics in aged mice, reporting significant decreases in vascular oxygen content, oxygen delivery, blood flow, and velocity. These findings indicate that impaired perfusion constitutes a key limiting factor for neuronal metabolism in the aging retina. Further metabolomic analyses reveal widespread metabolic dysregulation in aged retinal tissue, particularly in mitochondrial pathways such as the tricarboxylic acid cycle, electron transport chain, and shuttle systems, as well as in glucose metabolism pathways such as glycolysis and gluconeogenesis.19 These metabolic deficits decrease energy availability for neuronal signaling and decouple neuronal activity from hemodynamic responses.

Outer retinal metabolism relies heavily on the choroidal circulation for the delivery of oxygen and glucose.120,121 Diffusion-based modeling derived from intraretinal oxygen microelectrode profiles indicates that sustaining the physiological oxygen demand of photoreceptor inner segments requires a high CC PO₂, low arteriovenous O₂ extraction per unit blood volume, and choroidal blood flow on the order of at least ∼500 mL/100 g·min.122 Both OCTA and laser speckle flow imaging have reported age-associated perfusion signal deficits in the CC.123,124 Age-related choroidal vascular sclerosis and atrophy may further decrease the efficiency of oxygen delivery to the outer retina, thereby increasing susceptibility to outer retinal hypoxia.125,126

BRB Integrity and Molecular Homeostasis

The decline in blood circulation may disrupt retinal homeostasis, and the BRB becomes increasingly vulnerable with aging (Fig. 3B). The BRB consists of the outer BRB (oBRB) and inner BRB (iBRB), which work together to maintain the retinal microenvironment. The oBRB, located between the choroid and photoreceptors, is composed of the RPE, BM, and the CC.127 The CC possesses fenestrated endothelia that facilitate efficient solute and nutrient exchange, ensuring metabolic support and waste clearance for the outer retina.128 However, with age, thinning of the CC and lipid-rich deposit accumulation within BM123 hinder solute, ion, and water transport across the oBRB, thereby potentially promoting waste retention and metabolic stress in the outer retina. Consistent with this possibility, immunofluorescence analyses of RPE tissue from aged mice have shown disrupted tight junction labeling in the oBRB.46

The iBRB is primarily formed by tight junctions between retinal microvascular ECs, which interact closely with astrocytes and pericytes.93 Notably, although age-related functional impairment of the retinal circulation has been described in humans, direct evidence for a marked, age-dependent breakdown of the iBRB in the healthy human retina (e.g., overt leakage on angiography) remains limited, and reported barrier leakage in clinical imaging is often confounded by vascular risk factors or comorbid disease rather than aging per se.129 In contrast, animal studies more directly support the age-associated vulnerability of the iBRB/NVU. In aged rodents, microvascular remodeling has been characterized by basement membrane thickening, altered pericyte cytoskeletal organization, and more widespread α-smooth muscle actin distribution, followed by capillary loss, aneurysm formation, and distorted vessels with increased leakiness.130,131 Fluorescein angiography and FITC–dextran leakage assays have demonstrated BRB disruption in aged mice.132 Beyond structural barrier changes, retinas from aged rats show an altered immune milieu, including the appearance of major histocompatibility complex class II–positive resident microglia, activated T cells, monocyte-like cells, and increased ED1-positive (phagocytic) microglia in the retinal parenchyma.131 Mechanistically, oxidative stress is a key pathway. ROS and reactive nitrogen species damage tight junction proteins and impair EC and RPE integrity.133 Additionally, deposits such as advanced glycation end-products and lipofuscin can induce oxidative stress and endothelial senescence, further compromising BRB function.32 This may facilitate increased extravascular exposure of plasma-derived proteins and other circulating factors, which can activate microglia and complement cascades, thereby disrupting retinal immune homeostasis.46

Waste Clearance and Metabolic Homeostasis

The maintenance of retinal proteostasis depends on multiple intracellular degradation systems, including autophagy, the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, the unfolded protein response, and molecular chaperones. These systems collaboratively degrade misfolded proteins, promote correct folding, and alleviate endoplasmic reticulum stress, thereby preventing toxic protein accumulation and ensuring cellular survival.134 Beyond intracellular processes, retinal homeostasis also relies on perivascular clearance pathways. Tracer studies have confirmed the presence of an ocular glymphatic system capable of removing metabolites such as Aβ and tau via perivascular spaces, draining through venous perivascular routes.135 Aging significantly impairs this multitiered clearance system, from intracellular degradation to perivascular drainage, leading to the progressive accumulation of insoluble protein aggregates, NVU dysfunction, and age-related retinal pathology.32

Generation and Degradation of Toxic Proteins

During aging, misfolded and aggregated proteins, including Aβ, α-syn, and tau, accumulate within neurons and the extracellular matrix, exerting neurotoxic effects. Proteolytic systems degrade these proteins to maintain proteostasis.136 As previously discussed, Aβ is a major toxic species in the aging retina, produced by amyloidogenic cleavage of amyloid precursor protein. However, in the physiologically aged neurosensory retina, direct quantitative evidence for age-dependent changes in β-site APP cleaving enzyme 1, neprilysin, or insulin-degrading enzyme remains limited, and the current literature more often reports related alterations in the RPE rather than in whole retina tissue.137–139 In murine studies of age-associated RPE dysfunction, neprilysin mRNA was reported to decrease, accompanied by an increase in β-site APP cleaving enzyme 1 activity, suggesting a shift toward decreased Aβ proteolysis and enhanced amyloidogenic processing at the level of the RPE.137,140 Nonetheless, some studies report no significant age-related change in Aβ metabolism.141,142 Thus, although enzymatic dysregulation may contribute to impaired clearance and NVU damage, whether such an imbalance universally occurs with aging remains under debate.

Phagocytosis and Autophagy

Glial-mediated phagocytosis and autophagy play pivotal roles in clearing toxic proteins. Microglia, the resident immune cells of the NVU, possess robust phagocytic activity that enables the recognition and removal of pathogens, protein aggregates, and cellular debris, thereby maintaining tissue homeostasis.143 Experimental evidence indicates that microglia clear neurotoxic Aβ through LC3-associated phagocytosis,144 and loss of autophagic function exacerbates tau propagation and deposition.145 Aging weakens both nonspecific and pathogen-specific phagocytic activity146; microglia isolated from aged mice exhibit a significantly decreased Aβ uptake,143 which impairs waste clearance and may reflect age-related deficits in endocytosis or activation. Moreover, aged retinal microglia display delayed responses to injury and prolonged retention at lesion sites, suggesting defective resolution from activated to quiescent states.147

Mitophagy serves as an intracellular counterpart to glial phagocytosis, maintaining mitochondrial integrity and cellular homeostasis.148 Although it is generally considered to decrease with age, recent studies reveal an unexpected compensatory increase in mitophagic activity within the aged retina. Jiménez-Loygorri et al.20 proposed that enhanced mitophagy represents an adaptive response to extensive mitochondrial damage, yet remains insufficient to suppress mitochondrial DNA–cGAS/STING–mediated inflammation, which perpetuates chronic retinal inflammation and NVU stress.

During aging of the outer retinal NVU, RPE cells, CC ECs, and pericytes can all develop intracellular and extracellular deposit accumulation secondary to impaired autophagic function149. Because these changes are closely implicated in the onset and progression of AMD, they are discussed in detail in the AMD section.

Axonal Transport in Neurons

The neuronal waste clearance system, particularly axonal transport, is essential for maintaining NVU integrity. Axonal transport within RGCs facilitates the bidirectional movement of materials along axons, delivering proteins and lipids to distal synapses while returning misfolded or aggregated proteins to the soma for degradation.150 Aging markedly decreased the efficiency of slow axonal transport in RGCs.151 Risner et al.152 reported that genetic enhancement of axonal resilience via Wlds expression failed to protect aged neurons as effectively as in young animals, suggesting that aging itself represents a critical barrier to axonal repair. Impaired axonal transport results in waste accumulation and nutrient insufficiency, which may further aggravate neuronal dysfunction and NVU impairment (Fig. 3C).

The Ocular Glymphatic Clearance System

In addition to cellular mechanisms, the retinal NVU also depends on an ocular glymphatic system for interstitial waste removal. The concept of an ocular glymphatic system was first proposed in 2015.153 Building on Iliff et al’s 2012 description of a perivascular transport pathway in the brain154—the “glymphatic system”—Alastair integrated adaptive optics–enabled ultrahigh-resolution retinal images showing perivascular spaces and suggested that a brain-like “ocular glymphatic system” might also exist.155 Wostyn et al.156 provided initial histological evidence for this hypothesis through a postmortem study: after the injection of India ink into the subarachnoid space of the human optic nerve, the ink was observed to diffuse along perivascular spaces, a distribution pattern closely resembling that of cerebral glymphatic pathways. This subsequent in vivo finding was later corroborated by a magnetic resonance imaging study in humans.157 Furthermore, rodent experiments by Mathieu et al.158 demonstrated that tracers from the cerebrospinal fluid could reach the optic nerve, indicating the existence of a direct glymphatic connection pathway between the brain and the optic nerve. In 2020, Wang et al.94 provided direct evidence in mice for a perivascular space–dependent glymphatic-like system in the eye using fluorescent protein tracers, including Aβ. Both anterograde (vitreous injection) and retrograde (cisterna magna injection) tracer studies reveal that ocular glymphatic clearance is significantly reduced in aged animals, concomitant with age-related decline in pupil light reflex and light-driven lymphatic outflow.95

AQP4 is of particular relevance to the ocular glymphatic system.94 After the ocular glymphatic system was proposed, studies reported a perivascular distribution pattern of AQP4 in the retina that is similar to the characteristic architecture of the brain glymphatic system,159 suggesting that AQP4 may likewise serve as a regulatory target of the ocular glymphatic pathway. At the molecular level, polarized distribution of AQP4 on astrocytic endfeet is essential for efficient glymphatic drainage.160–162 Age-related downregulation of perivascular AQP4 expression in the mouse retina56 may be associated with retinal glymphatic system dysfunction during aging (Fig. 3D). However, how AQP4 polarity in the human retina changes with age, the relative contributions of Müller cell vs. astrocytic endfoot AQP4 polarization to ocular glymphatic clearance, and potential differences in their age-dependent alterations all warrant more in-depth investigation.

AMD

The inner choroid/outer retina NVU meets photoreceptor-driven metabolic demands through coordinated processes of trophic supply and clearance. However, in AMD, both the structure and function of this unit undergo degeneration and injury.108,183 First, AMD is characterized by decreased choroidal thickness and decreased CC vascularity, which can compromise the delivery of oxygen and nutrients to the outer retina.184,185 Moreover, autophagy dysfunction in the RPE as well as in pericytes and ECs surrounding the CC promotes the extracellular accumulation of metabolic waste, such as lipofuscin, lipids, and misfolded proteins (e.g., Aβ), thereby jointly contributing to the formation and buildup of drusen.186–190 The deposition of these materials at the level of BM, leading to BM thickening and calcification, can further impede molecular exchange and waste clearance between the outer retina and the CC.191

Diabetic Retinopathy

Microvascular pathology is a hallmark of diabetic retinopathy, characterized by reduced vessel density,192 increased vascular tortuosity,193 neovascularization, and vascular leakage.194 However, increasing attention has been directed toward the mechanisms of neurodegeneration.195,196 Under hyperglycemic stress, Müller cells and RGCs exhibit stress responses and apoptosis,197 whereas microglia become abnormally activated.198 Flash stimulation studies in diabetic patients and rat models have demonstrated early impairments in neurovascular coupling during the diabetic course.199 This pathological change is largely driven by activated glial cells that upregulate inducible NO synthase, leading to excessive NO production that selectively disrupts glia–vascular signaling.10 Importantly, the pharmacological inhibition of inducible NO synthase can rapidly reverse neurovascular coupling deficits in diabetic models, highlighting NO dysregulation as a central molecular mechanism driving early microvascular dysfunction.200 In addition, dysfunction of the glymphatic system mediated by AQP4 polarity alteration may contribute to NVU impairment by promoting crosstalk between neuronal degeneration and microvascular damage.201 Targeting AQP4 has been shown to suppress gliosis, preserve RGC activity, and ameliorate NVU dysfunction in diabetic retinopathy.197

Glaucoma

Glaucoma typically leads to progressive visual field loss through degeneration of the retina, optic nerve, and visual pathways in the brain. The main focus of diagnosis and treatment remains IOP control. However, mounting evidence suggests that vascular dysfunction is an additional critical pathogenic factor beyond pressure-induced neurodegeneration.202 Dynamic vessel analysis in patients with POAG has shown chronic vascular constriction within the retina, limiting resting blood flow and potentially leading to neuronal metabolic impairment or death. Intriguingly, neurovascular coupling in response to flicker stimulation remains intact. This finding suggests that vascular dysregulation, rather than intrinsic neuronal degeneration, may serve as an initiating factor for vision loss.203 Further evidence from Flammer and colleagues demonstrated that IOP-induced decreases in capillary diameter and perfusion are associated with pericyte contraction.202 Damage or rupture of IP-TNTs disrupts Ca²⁺ signaling between pericytes, leading to neurovascular decoupling.204 Thus, stabilizing intracellular calcium homeostasis in pericytes may represent a key therapeutic approach to restore retinal NVU function.

AD Retinopathy

AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder, and the retina has emerged as a promising, noninvasive biomarker site owing to its accessibility and shared pathophysiology with the brain. OCT and OCTA can visualize functional abnormalities in the retinal microvasculature of AD patients.205 In AD mouse models, significant degeneration occurs in the inner retina of the dorsal quadrant and the outer retina of the temporal quadrant, accompanied by arterial narrowing and a decreased overall capillary density.206 These concurrent neuronal and vascular pathologies highlight a tightly coupled pattern of retinal NVU degeneration. Perivascular deposition of Aβ suggests that retinal vascular dysfunction in AD may result from AQP4 mislocalization, leading to impaired ocular glymphatic clearance and the accumulation of metabolic waste.135

Rejuvenation of the Retinal Neurovascular System

Interventional strategies targeting retinal aging and its associated neurodegenerative changes are expanding rapidly. Although most evidence still comes from preclinical models, several approaches have shown encouraging translational potential. Targeting Cells

Müller Cell Reprogramming

Neuronal apoptosis during retinal degeneration remains difficult to reverse, and enabling cellular regeneration with functional recovery is a major challenge. Müller glia provide a plausible endogenous source for retinal regeneration.207 In zebrafish and other lower vertebrates, Müller cells can be reprogrammed into (Müller glia–derived progenitor cells that re-enter the cell cycle and regenerate retinal neurons.208 However, although mammalian Müller cells also exhibit regenerative potential, their regenerative efficiency appears to be insufficient to meet the demands of retinal self-repair.209,210 Existing work suggests that limited regenerative competence in mammalian Müller glia may involve deficient induction of key proneurogenic factors, such as achaete-scute family bHLH transcription factor 1 and lin-28,211,212 epigenetic constraints including histone acetylation and DNA demethylation,213,214 and the antiproliferative influence of Prospero-related homeobox protein 1215; targeting these pathways may facilitate neuronal regeneration. Notably, manipulating these factors in uninjured mouse retina has not produced clear phenotypic improvement, whereas Müller cell reprogramming is more readily initiated after retinal injury.216 Accordingly, the contribution and feasibility of Müller cell reprogramming in age-associated retinal degeneration remain to be clarified.

Dual Modulation of Microglia

Microglia contribute to retinal immune homeostasis under physiological conditions,46 yet they can become activated in aging and diverse retinal degenerations, where they may act as drivers of complement-mediated synapse elimination and chronic inflammation.147 In this context, restraining age-associated microglial activation, proliferation, and migration has been considered a key therapeutic direction. Minocycline, a semisynthetic tetracycline derivative, has been reported to suppress microglial cyclo-oxygenase-2 expression and reduce the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, with structural and functional rescue observed across multiple mouse models of retinal degeneration.217–219 At the genetic level, insulin-like growth factor binding protein–like 1 and apolipoprotein E4 have also been reported to potentially modulate microglial activation and confer neuroprotection in the retina.220,221

At the same time, other evidence indicates that microglia may exert protective functions in aging by phagocytosing shed photoreceptor outer segments and lipids, thereby partially compensating for age-related limitations of RPE autophagy and helping to preserve visual function in aged mice.222 This apparent functional duality of microglia during aging introduces uncertainty regarding the optimal direction, timing, and context for microglia-targeted interventions.

Regulation of Pericyte Homeostasis

Pericytes, as key cellular components of the NVU, are emerging as novel therapeutic targets for neurodegenerative diseases (Fig. 4A). Yang et al. developed a multifunctional curcumin-based nanoprodrug (VLC@Cur-NPs) that selectively recognizes vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 on diseased pericytes and releases curcumin and very long chain peptides under high ROS conditions. This system effectively scavenges ROS, alleviates oxidative stress, inhibits proinflammatory cytokine expression, and reduces Aβ deposition, thus protecting pericyte function.223 ROS-mediated increases in intracellular calcium ([Ca²⁺]i) have been shown to contribute to microvascular flow disturbances. Korte et al.90 focused on L-type voltage-gated calcium channels, using nimodipine to improve cerebral blood flow, reduce leukocyte adhesion to pericyte bodies, and mitigate hypoxia. Mechanistically, moderate [Ca²⁺]i elevation activates the calcium-dependent anion channel TMEM16A, leading to chloride efflux, membrane depolarization, and secondary voltage-gated calcium channels activation. In stroke models, TMEM16A inhibition significantly delayed ischemia-induced [Ca²⁺]i elevation, capillary constriction, and pericyte death, while enhancing reperfusion.224 The application of the aforementioned approaches for maintaining cellular homeostasis in the context of retinal degeneration appears promising to some extent.

Targeting Energy Metabolism

Retinal neurons rely heavily on mitochondrial adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production. During aging, reduced efficiency of the mitochondrial electron transport chain may divert more electrons toward ROS generation rather than ATP synthesis, making mitochondrial restoration an important therapeutic direction.225 Strategies to augment nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) have attracted increasing attention (Fig. 4B). NAD⁺ is an essential redox cofactor for the tricarboxylic acid cycle and the electron transport chain,226–228 and retinal NAD⁺ levels have been reported to decrease with advancing age.229 NAD⁺ depletion may further aggravate mitochondrial injury: on the one hand, impaired electron transport can increase ROS production; on the other, disrupted mitophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis can compound metabolic instability.230–232

Supplementation with NAD⁺ precursors represents a relatively direct intervention strategy. Nicotinamide, nicotinamide mononucleotide, and nicotinamide riboside have been reported to decrease oxidative stress and attenuate apoptosis in mouse models of retinal degeneration.233–235 Further work suggests that sirtuins may mediate part of the mitochondrial-protective effects associated with NAD⁺ augmentation. In particular, SIRT1 activation has been linked to enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis.236,237 SIRT3, a major mitochondrial deacetylase, regulates oxidative phosphorylation complex activity and antioxidant defenses.238,239 SIRT6 has also been reported to protect RGCs against glaucomatous and age-related injury.240

Red-light–based therapies provide a novel, noninvasive approach to support energy metabolism in the aging retina. Near-infrared light has been proposed to protect the aging retina primarily by improving mitochondrial function and increasing ATP production.241,242 Cytochrome c oxidase, the terminal enzyme of the mitochondrial respiratory chain, has been described as a photoreceptor for near-infrared light photobiomodulation. Cytochrome c oxidase absorption of near-infrared light has been proposed to alter its redox state and trigger downstream effects, including increased ATP synthesis, reduced ROS generation, and lower inflammatory signaling, thereby improving overall mitochondrial performance.241

Systemic Intervention Strategies

Fecal Microbiota Transplantation

Age-related changes in the composition and function of the gut microbiome may exacerbate inflammatory aging phenotypes in the retina by disrupting homeostasis along the gut–retina axis (Fig. 4C). Under physiological conditions, the gut microbiome is important for immune system development and maintenance of the intestinal epithelial barrier.243 With aging, shifts in microbial community structure have been associated with impaired intestinal barrier integrity,244-247 increased permeability, and the emergence of inflammatory aging phenotypes.248,249 Because the retina is highly sensitive to metabolic and immune dysregulation,250 it may represent a vulnerable target tissue in the setting of systemic inflammation.

Parker et al.251 performed heterochronic fecal microbiota transplantation, exchanging microbiota between young and aged mice, and demonstrated potentially beneficial effects of microbiome modulation. Young recipients of aged-donor microbiota showed increased complement C3 deposition in the RPE layer, approaching levels observed in aged controls, along with a marked decrease in expression of the key visual cycle protein RPE65. In contrast, aged recipients of young-donor microbiota exhibited a reciprocal, more protective phenotype: C3 deposition at the RPE–BM interface was substantially reduced, and RPE65 expression was restored toward levels seen in young mice. Retinal cytokine profiling further suggested reductions in proinflammatory mediators (CCL11, CXCL11, and IL-1β) and an increase in the neuroprotective cytokine IL-13. Collectively, these findings support the possibility that microbiome-directed interventions may offer therapeutic benefit in mitigating chronic, age-associated retinal inflammation.

Plasma Rejuvenation

An increasing body of evidence suggests that circulating factors play systemic regulatory roles in NVU aging.252 Young plasma is rich in platelets and platelet-derived bioactive molecules that regulate chemotaxis, inflammation, and vascular tone.253 In aged mouse models, exogenous supplementation with platelet-derived factors alleviated neuroinflammation, enhanced synaptic plasticity, and improved cognitive function.254 Heterochronic parabiosis, which involves linking the circulatory systems of young and old mice, has been found to reverse retinal aging features by activating the AdipoR1–AMPK–mitochondrial axis, improving retinal metabolism and cellular homeostasis255 (Fig. 4C). Proteomic analyses have further revealed age-dependent heterogeneity in plasma composition, with enrichment of inflammatory and neurodegenerative markers in aged plasma. Targeting these molecules may offer systemic interventions to restore NVU function.256

Glymphatic Clearance System

Light stimulation has emerged as a promising noninvasive approach for modulating neural activity, cerebral blood flow, and energy metabolism in neurodegenerative disorders. Specifically, 40 Hz light flicker entrains gamma oscillations across multiple brain regions, promoting microglial morphological transformation and reducing cortical Aβ deposition.257 In addition, 40 Hz stimulation enhances glymphatic inflow and efflux through improved astrocytic AQP4 polarity and vascular motility.258 Similarly, 40 Hz transcranial magnetic stimulation can increase cortical perfusion and high-frequency oscillatory activity in AD patients.259 It is worth noting that direct interventional work aimed at therapeutically modulating an ocular glymphatic clearance system remains very limited. Therefore, whether neuromodulatory approaches analogous to the 40 Hz paradigms in the brain can be adapted to enhance ocular glymphatic clearance and confer retinal benefit remains an open and potentially important direction for future research.

Dietary and Exercise Interventions

Exercise and dietary modulation represent practical and complementary lifestyle strategies that show significant promise in mitigating retinal aging and degeneration.252,260,261 Exercise enhances NVU homeostasis by upregulating brain-derived neurotrophic factor, reducing oxidative stress,262 and improving endothelial function263 (Fig. 4D). At the microvascular level, isometric exercise–induced blood pressure fluctuations amplify retinal arteriolar reactivity,264 increase venular oxygen saturation, and augment peripheral blood flow,265 thereby improving oxygen–perfusion matching and possibly stimulating ocular glymphatic transport to enhance metabolic clearance. Consistent with these microvascular and metabolic benefits, a 24-week yoga intervention in healthy older adults was associated with improved retinal blood flow, capillary efficiency, and tissue perfusion, together with enhanced mitochondrial function,266 further supporting the broader concept that exercise-based interventions can help to preserve retinal neurovascular and metabolic function during aging.

Meanwhile, levels of very long chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in the retina decline with age. Deficiency in these PUFAs may drive retinal degeneration. In aging models, intraocular supplementation with very long chain-PUFAs or their precursors (e.g., 24:5n-3) replenished docosahexaenoic acid, restored contrast sensitivity and dark adaptation, and downregulated multiple aging-related genes.267 From a mechanistic perspective, the major ω-3 fatty acids—eicosapentaenoic acid and docosahexaenoic acid—activate large-conductance Ca²⁺-activated K⁺ channels, ATP-sensitive K⁺ channels, and Kv7 voltage-gated K⁺ channels, inducing smooth muscle cell hyperpolarization and relaxation,268 which promote vasodilation, reduce vascular resistance, and improve microcirculatory perfusion269 (Fig. 4E). Under other age-related retinal conditions, PUFA supplementation further enhances BRB integrity, restores immune balance, and facilitates metabolic waste clearance.270–272

In summary, exercise dynamically improves retinal perfusion and waste clearance, and PUFA supplementation supports lipid metabolism and ion channel homeostasis. Future research should focus on combined intervention strategies, dosage optimization, and molecular target identification to accelerate translational applications from mechanistic insights to clinical therapies.

Conclusions

Overall, current evidence suggests a potential regulatory involvement of neurovascular mechanisms in retinal aging and degenerative diseases. With advancing age, the NVU may undergoes structural and functional decline. This process includes reduced perfusion that could contribute to oxygen and energy deficits, alterations in BRB integrity, metabolic disturbances, and possible impairment of waste clearance pathways. These age-related alterations, in combination with direct cellular effects of aging on neurovascular components, may collectively contribute to increased retinal susceptibility to injury. Although progress has been made in understanding NVU aging, translating these mechanistic insights into effective interventions remains challenging. Future studies should aim to integrate the elucidation of the precise molecular pathways governing neurovascular coupling, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for strategies to delay retinal aging and preserve visual function in the aging population.
