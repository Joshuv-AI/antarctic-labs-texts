Cognitive‐behavioural treatment for neck pain
Authors: Sviatlana Kamarova, Nicholas Henschke, Toby Newton-John, Steven J Kamper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (intervention). The objectives are as follows:To assess the effects of cognitive‐behavioural therapy for the management of subacute and chronic NP in adults.

Background

Neck pain (NP) is a highly prevalent condition that leads to considerable pain, disability, and associated costs, experienced by both genders (1). NP is one of the most common musculoskeletal disorders, having an age‐standardised prevalence rate of 27.0 per 1000 population in 2019 (2). The global burden of NP in 204 countries increased with age until the age of 70 to 74 years, and was higher in females than males (3).

There are three categories of NP based on pain duration: acute, pain lasting for less than six weeks; subacute, pain that persists for at least six weeks but less than three months; and chronic, pain that endures over three months [4]. Chronic NP is a multifactorial disease. Psychological risk factors include stress, depression, anxiety, and insufficient social support, whilst the biological risks include diseases such as neuromusculoskeletal disorders and autoimmune diseases (2). Addressing the cognitive and behavioural factors for NP might reduce the clinical burden and the economic costs. Non‐pharmacological and non‐surgical treatments are commonly used for the treatment of chronic NP, whilst evidence for the effectiveness of cognitive‐behavioural therapy (CBT) in reducing chronic NP is inconsistent compared with other types of interventions (5). Ploutarchou and colleagues demonstrated (6) in their recent systematic review and meta‐analysis higher short‐term clinically significant pain reduction for CBT treatment conditions with or without additional intervention compared with no intervention.

Grounded in learning theory and adopting cognitive and behavioural strategies, CBT is a psychotherapeutic treatment that, through identification and modification of destructive patterns of thoughts, emotions, and behaviours, aims to improve coping, reduce psychological distress, and enhance functioning [7]. Based on third‐wave CBT, acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) combines acceptance and mindfulness strategies with commitment and behaviour change strategies to increase psychological flexibility through cognitive restructuring [8]. Finally, motivational interviewing (MI) is a collaborative, person‐centred counselling treatment designed to evoke and increase motivation for behaviour change, relying on such concepts as empathy and autonomy to resolve ambivalence and bring about self‐determination and commitment to change [9].

Regarding disparities in NP outcomes, there are inequalities in gender (women [10,11]), region (countries with lower developmental indices [10]), age (older adults [3, 11]), and socioeconomic status (lower [12]). These disparities were not entirely associated with biological differences, but related to modifiable avoidable factors such as maladaptive beliefs (e.g. fear‐avoidance, catastrophising), psychological stress, and poor coping strategies [11].

Description of the condition

NP is defined as pain perceived between the superior nuchal line and the spinous process of the first thoracic vertebra, which may be referred to as head, trunk, and upper limbs [13]. It is often associated with migraine and tension‐type headache (14) and muscle tension, stiffness and/or reduced range of motion, lower movement velocity, localised below the superior nuchal line and above the scapula line from the back, and below the superior nuchal line and the external occipital protuberance line and above the superior border of the clavicle and the suprasternal notch from the side (15, 16, 17). Spinal disease, osteoarthritis, muscle disorders, and neurologic diseases such as dystonia are possible causes of NP (14). Structures in the cervical region, including the spine or soft tissues involved in cervical motion, are likely origins of NP (2, 16). In cases of NP with traumatic onset (e.g. whiplash injury), additional psychological factors such as post‐traumatic stress reactions may contribute to symptom persistence and physical function or disability [18, 19], therefore studies involving traumatic NP should be interpreted with consideration of cognition and behaviour.

Recent systematic reviews have revealed that factors contributing to NP development are biological and psychological in nature, and include age, gender, genetics (i.e. a family history of NP), and comorbidity with other musculoskeletal problems (e.g. low back pain) (2), whilst prognosis appears to be influenced by factors including age, prior NP episodes, and poor psychological health (20). In an overview of systematic reviews, the only prognostic factors supported by moderate to strong evidence were history of other musculoskeletal disorders (other than NP) and work‐related factors associated with NP (21). Finally, psychological factors were consistently found to be a contributing factor to persistent NP (22, 21), whilst improvements in mental health from baseline at discharge and at six‐month follow‐up were independently associated with greater reductions in pain and disability following specific multidisciplinary, biopsychosocial rehabilitation (MBR) for chronic NP (23). Consistent with these findings, other studies of MBR programmes have demonstrated sustained improvements in pain and physical functioning for at least one year, even amongst participants who had not responded to prior interventions (24). Evidence links persistent NP to psychological factors such as depression (22), anxiety (25), and depressive symptoms (26). Indeed, psychological factors can drive the process of the chronicity of symptoms, contributing to poorer NP outcomes by increasing avoidance and catastrophising (27, 28), whilst education and change of beliefs and coping skills can alleviate symptoms (29).

Description of the intervention and how it might work

CBT is a scientifically informed psychological management strategy that seeks to directly manipulate dysfunctional patterns of thinking and behaviour in order to reduce physical and psychological suffering (30, 31). CBT has been shown to be efficacious in the treatment of anxiety and related disorders (32). Moreover, this type of intervention can also be used for treatment of subacute and chronic NP, given their link to these psychological factors, whether independently or together with other therapeutic modalities such as physical therapy. Due to the multifaceted nature of CBT for pain management, it is delivered by a variety of health professions and multidisciplinary teams.

A recent meta‐analytical evidence found that CBT, with or without additional interventions, may result in more short‐term pain reduction than no intervention (standardised mean difference (SMD) −0.73, 95% confidence interval (CI) −1.23 to −0.23) (6). An integrative review to determine the most efficacious dose and delivery methods of CBT to reduce chronic pain found that pain intensity was decreased in 43% of CBT‐based trials, whilst the efficacy of online and in‐person formats were comparable (33). Williams and colleagues found that CBT has very small to small beneficial effects for reducing pain, disability, and distress in chronic pain, based on moderate‐certainty evidence across 59 studies [34]. Research also suggests that the efficacy of CBT treatment varies across individuals, with matching treatments to participant characteristics an effective strategy to improve treatment outcomes (35). Turner and colleagues (2007) found that individuals with greater baseline somatisation, depressive symptoms, number of pain sites, rumination, catastrophising, and perceived stress may require more intensive CBT, where treatment of depression may be required before or in conjunction with CBT for pain [36]. However, trials often did not collect and report CBT intervention and participant characteristics, and did not incorporate sufficient tailoring to flexibly adapt to the nature of pain, individual differences and preferences (6).

The effects of the CBT‐based interventions delivered by trained professionals (i.e. psychologists or health professionals) are explained through modifying maladaptive cognitions and emotional responses, which in turn leads to behavioural changes that support recovery (35). Coping skills can be provided as a part of CBT to change the thinking strategy of pain and the related disability as a malleable condition that can be self‐managed and improved, rather than as a serious disease that requires ongoing intervention. By accepting their pain, becoming educated in the problem, and proactively coping with disruptive thoughts and mood alterations, individuals start to transfer their attention from dysfunctional thoughts (e.g. catastrophising, fears) to healthy ones. This enables engagement in physical activity rather than avoidance of it. Evidence suggests fear of pain following an acute pain episode activates the process of pain chronification, disability development, and reduced functionality (37). CBT attempts to reactivate the individual and improve compliance with the treatment and more active engagement in rehabilitation (38).

Drawing from the assumption that psychological and social factors substantially contribute to pain and associated disability, CBT suggests that a change of one’s beliefs and behaviour can be instrumental in pain alteration. Therefore, CBT interventions share a focus on changing maladaptive beliefs about the likelihood of anticipated harms by using various cognitive (e.g. cognitive restructuring, imagery, attention diversion, relaxation) and behavioural (e.g. operant treatment, pacing, graded exposure approaches) techniques to alleviate the impact of pain or its cessation and accelerate recovery process (39, 40, 41). Research shows preliminary evidence that CBT produces changes in the prefrontal cortex, which lead to the release of pain‐inhibiting neurotransmitters blocking pain impulse transmission from the spinal cord to the brain reflected in alterations of the perception of ‘pain’ signals (42). Therefore, CBT offers inhibitory mechanisms that reduce pain perception [41, 43].

A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis including 19 studies and 1640 participants found that CBT, especially in combination with other modalities as a multimodal intervention, produced clinically significant improvements in pain (SMD −0.73, 95% CI −1.23 to −0.23) and other outcomes (disability, and psychological indicators such as depression and anxiety) [6]. These effects were also found to be sustained over time.

CBT interventions can be delivered adopting various modes such as in‐person, online, and hybrid models. A recent Cochrane review examined the effectiveness of psychological therapies delivered remotely for chronic pain, recognising their distinct characteristics [44]. The review was conducted in parallel to an earlier review focused on in‐person, face‐to‐face traditional delivery mode [34].

Why it is important to do this review

Given the growing attention to CBT, the high number of recent randomised controlled trials on CBT for chronic NP, and updated Cochrane guidelines and standards (45), it is timely to revisit whether CBT delivery in individuals with subacute and chronic NP can produce clinically meaningful changes in pain, disability, and other outcomes through alteration of cognitive and behavioural barriers to recovery such as dysfunctional behaviours/habits and disruptive beliefs. Previous Cochrane reviews have found that CBT has limited analgetic effects on chronic pain [34]; however, pain remains a clinically relevant outcome. The current review will examine whether newer CBT approaches, including third‐wave interventions, digital delivery formats, and CBT combined with physical therapies, are associated with greater improvements in pain outcomes than those observed in previous reviews of chronic NP populations. Moreover, this is a condition‐specific (NP) review, which can uncover more suitable therapeutic responses compared to existing evidence through the inclusion of third‐wave CBT therapies, which were underrepresented in earlier reviews. Finally, unlike earlier reviews, we will use the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist for detailed intervention characteristics and flag problematic studies, followed by a sensitivity analysis, for more robust evidence.

Though chronic NP is recognised as a public health burden, subacute NP also requires attention given its potential contribution to exacerbation into chronicity. By definition, subacute pain indicates a window of opportunity for intervention [4]. Evidence suggests that early, targeted interventions during the period of three weeks to three months can prevent long‐term disability and health risks and reduce costs [46]. We will include subacute NP in this review, because it allows us to evaluate if CBT interventions are effective in this transitional phase, thereby contributing to public health strategies focused on prevention and early rehabilitation. The planned subgroup analysis dividing studies focused on subacute from chronic patients enables us to test whether CBT interventions are more promising if offered earlier in the disease course.

In addition to CBT interventions for the management of chronic pain, the literature suggests that third‐wave CBT approaches, such as ACT (47, 48) and MI (49, 50), may offer potential benefits for pain management. We will therefore use these methods as additional comparisons to provide a more complete picture regarding the effectiveness of CBT interventions on NP. Our review supersedes the Cochrane review published in 2015 [51], which is the subject of an expression of concern.

Objectives

To assess the effects of cognitive‐behavioural therapy for the management of subacute and chronic NP in adults.

Methods

We will conduct the review according to the methods described in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [45] and Methodological Expectations for Cochrane Intervention Reviews (MECIR) [52], and report the review following PRISMA [53, 54].

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We will include randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and exclude other study designs (e.g. quasi‐RCTs). Quasi‐RCTs are experimental studies where participants are prospectively allocated to groups using a non‐random method, such as by a systematic process that is not truly random.

Screening eligible studies for scientific integrity or trustworthiness

To minimise the chances of including problematic studies in the review, we will assess the trustworthiness of all trials eligible for inclusion using the Cochrane Pregnancy and Childbirth Trustworthiness Screening Tool (TST) [55], following the steps published in a previous Cochrane review [56]. The TST assesses criteria around research governance and trial registration, the plausibility of baseline participant characteristics, study feasibility, and the plausibility of results. All studies meeting our inclusion criteria will be evaluated by two review authors (SK, NH). Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion with a third review author (SJK), and final decisions will be reached by consensus amongst all three review authors. Trials satisfying all TST criteria will be automatically included in the review, and trials with concerns limited to trial registration will not be considered untrustworthy if no other domains raise concerns, and thus will also be included. However, if the outcome data seem implausible, the study will be flagged as problematic, irrespective of judgements in the other domains.

We will contact authors of trials flagged with potential concerns in at least one domain using an email template to request clarification of trial registration, ethics approval, and study results, including requesting individual participant data where applicable. We will allow three months for a response, sending a follow‐up email if no response is received. If the trial authors respond and satisfactorily address our concerns, the trial will be included in the review. If the response does not ameliorate the concerns or if the trial authors do not respond, we will categorise the study as awaiting classification.

Types of participants

We will include adults (aged over 18 and older) with a clinical diagnosis of subacute NP (i.e. a documented history of pain lasting for at least one month and not longer than three months) or chronic NP (i.e. a documented history of pain lasting for at least three months), irrespective of the presence of radiculopathy or whiplash injury.

When an RCT includes participants suffering from pain in different body regions, we will exclude the study if results for NP are not presented separately. When an RCT evaluates participants with both subacute and chronic NP, we will include the study only if data for participants with subacute and chronic NP are presented separately. We will include studies if 75% of their population is aged 18 or older, otherwise the study will be excluded. We will exclude serious spinal pathologies: malignancies, systemic inflammatory conditions, fractures, and spinal cord injuries.

Types of interventions

We will include RCTs (cluster and cross‐over) if they analyse one or more types of CBT (i.e. traditional CBT, ACT, and MI), since CBT encompasses a wide set of interventions, including cognitive reconditioning and behavioural modifications of specific activities with the aim of modifying or reducing the impact of pain and physical and psychosocial disability (31, 40). Only trials that specify the use of treatment based on cognitive‐behavioural principles will be considered eligible. Simple psychologically oriented pain management strategies will not be considered cognitive‐behavioural treatments. An existing Cochrane review by Williams and colleagues [34] investigated the effects of psychological therapies for chronic pain; the authors stated "typical treatment protocol for CBT… will involve 4 elements" (p.7), which will be used as inclusion criteria (any three out of four elements will qualify for inclusion):

"Methods of cognitive appraisal which directly assess, reality‐test and, where necessary, revise the self‐defeating beliefs about and repetitive thoughts associated with pain;Strategies of emotional regulation or coping, and exposure to reduce the anticipation, expectation and avoidance of unpleasant thoughts about predicted pain;Behavioural activation to promote engagement with rewarding activities; andSkills in problem‐solving, and motivation."

We expect high variability in the types of CBT provided (i.e. cognitive, respondent or operant treatments and varying modalities of administration), and we anticipate uncertainty about what was actually done as practical intervention. Doubts about the types and treatment characteristics of CBT will be resolved through discussion amongst review authors, or by finding a process paper associated with the study that provides further information. In terms of format of delivery, we will include any mode including individual, group, online, if they are structured and delivered by a trained professional. We will not apply a restriction on the duration or intensity of intervention manipulation. However, we will extract these characteristics to report them in subgroup or sensitivity analysis, if appropriate.

We will include multi‐arm RCTs if they contain at least one pair‐wise comparison that meets the eligibility criteria. Where necessary, we will extract data from relevant arms only or combine arms as appropriate to avoid unit of analysis errors. We will exclude interventions that lack description of the intervention content.

In line with MECIR C7 guidance [52] and the Cochrane Handbook [45], we will include sham interventions, attention controls, or conditions adopting minimal contact that do not include active psychological or physical treatment components as eligible placebo comparators, but will exclude comparators that contain any active therapeutic elements unless they are part of a predefined comparison group. Acceptable comparisons will include placebo, no treatment, waiting list, or active comparisons such as physiotherapy and education. We will include all relevant arms of multi‐arm RCTs. Co‐interventions will be allowed if they are applied equally across all groups.

Outcome measures

We will include all trials that meet the eligibility criteria for study design (i.e. RCT), population, and intervention, regardless of whether they report any of the specified outcomes. We will include trials measuring one or more of the outcomes listed.

We will prioritise pain intensity (critical), and then the other (important) outcomes described below. We will consider each outcome at three time points: short term (outcomes measured closest to four weeks follow‐up), intermediate term (outcomes measured closest to six months follow‐up), and long term (outcomes measured closest to one year follow‐up). Considering resource constraints, we will use the timeframe of six months as a prioritised timeframe for the summary of findings tables due to its relevance to clinical decision‐making.

Following the recommendations from the COMET Initiative (Core Outcome Measures in Effectiveness Trials) [57] and the INTEGRATE‐HTA project [58] to incorporate the standardised sets of measured and reported outcomes using Core Outcome Set (COS), we will prioritise chronic pain and transition from acute to chronic pain outcomes such as pain intensity and pain interference, physical function, quality of life, psychological function, work absence, and adverse events. This will prevent outcome reporting bias and facilitate meta‐analysis. Based on a Delphi consensus process involving multidisciplinary experts and people with lived experience [58], these selected outcomes will reflect stakeholder priorities, ensuring that the outcomes remain meaningful to patients, clinicians, and policymakers.

Critical outcomes

Our critical outcome is pain intensity, expressed by (with corresponding preference in the order) means of a visual analogue scale (VAS) or a numerical rating scale (NRS) (59), or other scales. The pain measures will be self‐reported, where low values refer to lower pain intensity.

The clinically important difference (CID) for pain intensity, the smallest change perceived as important, is defined as an improvement of at least 1.5 points on a 0‐to‐10 VAS/NRS based on the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) for musculoskeletal acute pain, including NP [60]. Baseline pain levels used in this definition ranged from moderate to severe (VAS > 4/10), which is consistent with the expected baseline characteristics of the studies we expect to include in the review.

We will follow Cochrane standards and guidelines [45].

A draft strategy for Ovid MEDLINE was written by an Information Specialist and will be translated using appropriate controlled vocabulary and syntax for other databases. The source of the filters will be stated in the search strategies. The MEDLINE strategy will be peer‐reviewed by an Information Specialist from, or assigned by, the Cochrane Central Editorial Service based on PRESS criteria [74, 75]. Searching other resources

We will screen the reference lists of all included studies and relevant systematic reviews.

After full‐text inclusion and prior to data extraction, we will search for retractions using the Retraction Watch Database; searches in bibliographic databases will also identify retractions and errata. If a study is retracted, we will document the reason and assess potential data usability. Inclusion decisions will be made through consensus amongst review authors and documented appropriately.

Where identified studies are published in languages other than English and translation is required, we will post requests for assistance on Cochrane Engage.

Data collection and analysis

Selection of studies

The selection process will be guided by the Criteria for considering studies for this review. We will use the RCT Classifier in Covidence, which is based on Cochrane's Screen4Me (S4M) [77, 78, 79, 80, 81] technology to remove non‐RCT references, and tag potential RCTs. Records retrieved by the systematic review search will be screened before activating the RCT Classifier. If the results of our search are in excess of 10,000, we may use Cochrane Crowd (Cochrane’s crowdsourcing platform) [80] to identify additional non‐RCT records. The results of the Covidence RCT Classifier will be reported in the review; if Cochrane Crowd is used, its results will also be documented in the review.

Two review authors (of SK, NH, SJK) will independently screen references identified by literature searches using Covidence [82]. Disagreements will be resolved by discussion or in consultation with a third review author (TNJ). Two review authors (of SK, NH, SJK) will independently assess the full texts of studies retained following title/abstract screening; disagreements will be resolved by discussion or in consultation with a third review author (TNJ). The reasons for exclusion of studies after full‐text review will be documented in the review.

We will include studies reported as conference abstracts if they include sufficient data for extraction and analysis. In cases where data are insufficient, we will attempt to contact study authors or identify the full report of the study. If the information is unobtainable, the abstract will be cited in studies awaiting classification. For fully reported studies, we will attempt to contact study authors for clarification or additional data if necessary. We will report our contact, or attempted contact, with study authors in the review.

We will cite any ongoing studies in the review.

We will record the selection process in sufficient detail to report it both narratively and in a PRISMA flow diagram [53, 54], and will report characteristics of included studies, excluded studies, studies awaiting classification, and ongoing studies in the review.

If we identify studies in languages not read by review authors, we will use an online translation service such as Google Translate. If the translation is sufficient, we will use it. If it is insufficient, we will attempt to identify an individual conversant in the language of the report to translate the study. If translation by one of these methods is insufficient, we will categorise the study as awaiting classification.

If we identify a study or studies conducted by the authors of this review, other authors will independently undertake selection for that study.

We will collate multiple reports of the same study so that each study, rather than each report, is the unit of interest in the review.

We will extract measures of effect and variability in the form of follow‐up (post‐intervention) measurements or change scores from baseline in all intervention and control groups. Where possible, follow‐up measures will be entered into the meta‐analyses [84].

Two review authors will assess the clinical relevance of each included trial. A list of five questions has been recommended to facilitate decisions about the applicability of results to other populations (84, 85, 86). A clinically important treatment effect (i.e. the smallest change in score of the construct that participants perceive as important) for our critical outcome (pain intensity) will be defined as an improvement of at least 1.5 points on a 0‐to‐10 VAS/NRS [87] and numeric pain rating scale (NPRS) [88]. A 25% relative improvement will be taken as a clinically important treatment effect for all important outcomes [88, 89, 90].

We will also collect data on adverse events, including types, rates, severity, and duration of harmful events.

Risk of bias assessment in included studies

Two review authors (of SK, NH, SJK) will independently assess the risk of bias for each outcome in the included RCTs using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool, following the guidance in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [45]. Any disagreements will be resolved in consultation with a third review author (TNJ). We will assess the effect of assignment to the intervention, regardless of whether the interventions are received as intended, for all outcomes in the summary of findings tables. The risk of bias domains will be modified for assessment of cluster and cross‐over RCTs.

We will assess each domain as being at low risk of bias, some concerns, or high risk of bias, and will report the ratings in the risk of bias table. We will not use RoB 2 to select trials for inclusion in the review. Each signalling question will be answered using the following response options: 'yes'; 'probably yes'; 'probably no'; 'no'; and 'no information'.

The review authors performing the risk of bias assessments cannot be blinded to study authors, institution, and journal because they are familiar with the literature. Review authors who are authors of included studies will be excluded from risk of bias assessment of their own studies.

We will produce a risk of bias table, graph, and summary figure to illustrate potential biases within each of the included studies.

Measures of treatment effect

We will analyse the data using RevMan [92]. We will calculate the treatment effects for dichotomous outcomes using the risk ratio (RR); for continuous outcomes, we will use the mean difference (MD), or the standardised mean difference (SMD) when the outcome was measured using different instruments, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Unit of analysis issues

We anticipate that most trials will be randomised at the participant level. However, if we identify a cluster RCT, we will include it and extract effect measures and standard errors from an analysis that takes clustering into account when possible. If this is not possible, we will extract the number of clusters and estimate the intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC).

For multi‐arm RCTs, we will either (i) combine relevant groups to create a single pair‐wise comparison or (ii) include only the relevant arms in the analysis. If combining is not appropriate due to clinical or methodological heterogeneity, we will keep the intervention arms separate but halve the size of the comparison arm to ensure the trial is not overweighted in the analysis [45].

If we identify any cross‐over RCTs, we will only include data from the first period to avoid carry‐over effects, as recommended by Cochrane guidelines [45]. This approach assumes that the first‐period data are not influenced by the second treatment phase, and that randomisation was properly conducted. We will document this decision and assess the implications for risk of bias and applicability.

Dealing with missing data

We will extract levels of and reasons for attrition in the included studies. For studies that report an MD but no standard deviation (SD), or other statistics that could be used to compute the SD via appropriate methods outlined in the Cochrane Handbook [45], we will first attempt to obtain the missing data from study authors. Where this is not possible, for each outcome, we will impute missing SDs as the pooled SD from all other trials in the same meta‐analysis by treatment group.

We will treat with caution the imputation of missing data, particularly for outcomes that are likely to be skewed (e.g. pain intensity). Where data are missing, we will consider the distribution of available data before imputation. If data are skewed, we will not apply mean‐based imputation, but exclude the data from quantitative synthesis, documenting the reasoning, or apply alternative imputation methods [45]. We will conduct sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of missing data for findings.

Reporting bias assessment

We will use funnel plots to explore the likelihood of reporting biases when at least 10 studies are included in a meta‐analysis; we will use a trim‐and‐fill method to impute the missing studies in our analysis (if applicable). First, we will assess funnel plot asymmetry visually, integrating visual inspection with the use of formal tests for funnel plot asymmetry. For continuous outcomes, we will use the test proposed by Egger and colleagues [93]; for dichotomous outcomes, we will use the test proposed by Harbord and colleagues [94]. When asymmetry is detected in any of these tests or is suggested by visual assessment, we will discuss possible explanations (such as publication bias, poor methodological quality, true heterogeneity, artefact, or chance) on the basis of available information [45] and perform sensitivity analyses to consider the implications. We will interpret funnel plots cautiously, as they can be misleading.

We will also check for inconsistencies between the information presented in clinical trial registries and that provided in published reports of trials. Review authors who are authors of trials will be excluded from decisions about their own studies.

Synthesis methods

When possible, we will combine the results from individual trials through meta‐analysis. We will not conduct analysis on studies not included in meta‐analyses. The main analysis will be performed irrespective of the presence/absence of participants with cervical radiculopathy or whiplash injury. Pooling of the data will be dependent on the level of clinical heterogeneity.

The comparisons in Objectives 1 and 2 (below) will be the primary objectives, and GRADE will be applied to meta‐analyses to assess the certainty of evidence.

1. To evaluate the benefits and harms of CBT versus placebo, no treatment, or waiting list for people with subacute or chronic NP.

2. To evaluate the benefits and harms of CBT in addition to another intervention (e.g. physiotherapy) versus the other intervention alone for people with subacute or chronic NP.

The comparisons in Objectives 3 to 6 (below) will be secondary objectives, and meta‐analyses will be reported without GRADE.

3. To evaluate the benefits and harms of ACT versus placebo, no treatment, or waiting list for people with subacute or chronic NP.

4. To evaluate the benefits and harms of ACT in addition to another intervention (e.g. physiotherapy) versus the other intervention alone for people with subacute or chronic NP.

5. To evaluate the benefits and harms of MI versus placebo, no treatment, or waiting list for people with subacute or chronic NP.

6. To evaluate the benefits and harms of MI in addition to another intervention (e.g. physiotherapy) versus the other intervention alone for people with subacute or chronic NP.

Investigation of heterogeneity and subgroup analysis

The primary analysis will include samples with subacute and chronic NP. We will perform subgroup analyses separating these studies if sufficient data are available.

We will assess between‐trial statistical heterogeneity using the I² statistic and the Chi2 test. For the meta‐analyses, we will use a random‐effects model regardless of statistical heterogeneity, and downgrade the certainty of evidence for inconsistency where I² > 75%. The same heterogeneity analysis will be performed for subgroup analyses.

Equity‐related assessment

We will evaluate equity‐related aspects of the included studies using the PROGRESS‐Plus framework [45, 83], which includes Place of residence, Race/ethnicity/culture/language, Occupation, Gender/sex, Religion, Education, Socioeconomic status, Social capital, and additional factors (Plus) such as age, disability, and sexual orientation. Therefore, we will extract and report relevant equity‐related characteristics of included study populations and settings, considering their influence on intervention effects, using a table adapted from earlier research [12] to support assessment applicability and generalisability of review findings. Where possible, we will conduct narrative/descriptive analysis, and, when appropriate, stratify reporting based on these characteristics (e.g. gender, education level). We will also use equity implementations to interpret the findings and discuss generalisability to diverse populations. We do not expect there to be sufficient data to allow a quantitative assessment of equity‐related concerns.

Sensitivity analysis

We will exclude studies with substantial missing data (> 20% of treated participants excluded from the final analysis) in a sensitivity analysis to allow investigation of any bias they could confer on the results.

Certainty of the evidence assessment

Regardless of whether available homogeneous data are sufficient to allow a quantitative summarisation of the data, we will assess the overall certainty of the evidence for our critical and important outcomes for the two main comparisons of this review: (i) CBT compared with placebo, no treatment, or waiting list controls, and ii) CBT in addition to another intervention (e.g. physiotherapy) versus the other intervention alone for people with subacute or chronic NP.

We will use the GRADE approach to assess the certainty of evidence, as recommended in the Cochrane Handbook [45] and adapted in the updated Cochrane Back Review Group method guidelines [84]. The certainty of the evidence for a specific outcome will be based on the performance of studies against five factors: study design and limitations, consistency of results, directness (generalisability), precision (sufficient data), and reporting of results across all studies that measured that particular outcome. RCTs start as high‐certainty evidence, and the certainty of evidence is downgraded by one level for each of the factors not met.

Two review authors (of SK, NH, SJK) will independently assess the certainty of evidence, with any inconsistences discussed and resolved by a third review author (TNJ), if required.

We will present the main results of the review in a summary of findings table, which provides key information concerning the certainty of evidence, the magnitude of effect of the RCTs examined, and the sum of available data on the outcomes with grading of the extracted evidence using the GRADE approach [45]. We will include only outcomes measuring pain and disability in the summary of findings tables.

We will include two summary of findings tables, each reporting the most important comparison for the two categories of subjects (subacute and chronic NP). We will use GRADEpro GDT [95] to prepare the summary of findings tables. We will assess the certainty of evidence as high, moderate, low, or very low, as follows.

Consumer involvement

We will not involve consumers in this review due to limited resources, although we will use core outcome sets for the review's outcomes, which have been developed with consumer involvement.
