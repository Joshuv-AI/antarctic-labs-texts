Pregnancy, Delivery, and Neonatal Outcomes in Women with Tourette Syndrome or Chronic Tic Disorder
Authors: Neda Razaz, Lorena Fernández de la Cruz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse pregnancy and neonatal outcomes in women with Tourette syndrome or chronic tic disorder (TS/CTD) have not been systematically studied. This Swedish population‐based study investigated associations between maternal TS/CTD and pregnancy, delivery, and neonatal outcomes.MethodsWe included all singleton births at ≥22 weeks between 2001 and 2021. Each pregnancy with TS/CTD was matched on mother's age and delivery year with up to 10 pregnancies without TS/CTD. Crude and adjusted relative risks were estimated using Poisson log‐linear regressions.ResultsWe compared 581 pregnancies in women with TS/CTD to 5777 matched unexposed pregnancies. Maternal TS/CTD was associated with significantly increased risks of gestational diabetes, and elective and emergency cesarean section. Neonates of women with TS/CTD showed no increased risks of adverse neonatal outcomes.ConclusionsMaternal TS/CTD is associated with increased risk of some adverse pregnancy and delivery outcomes, but not with adverse neonatal outcomes. Enhanced maternal care for women with TS/CTD is warranted. © 2025 The Author(s). Tourette syndrome (TS) and chronic tic disorder (CTD) are early‐onset movement disorders characterized by multiple motor and phonic tics in TS, and multiple motor or phonic tics in CTD, persisting for over 1 year. 1 TS/CTD often co‐occur with multiple psychiatric comorbidities and somatic conditions. 2 , 3 , 4 Given that they mostly affect males, 5 the literature on reproductive‐age women with TS/CTD is extremely limited. In a case series of 8 women with TS and 11 pregnancies, 6 TS was not associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes. Other previous reports focused on pregnancies and deliveries in women with TS/CTD have mainly examined changes in tic severity 7 and treatment tolerance during this period. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 However, whether women with TS/CTD face higher risks of pregnancy and perinatal complications, as seen in other neurological conditions (eg, epilepsy) 12 and related psychiatric disorders (eg, obsessive–compulsive disorder [OCD]), 13 remains unknown.

To fill this gap in the literature, this Swedish matched cohort study investigated pregnancy, delivery, and neonatal outcomes among births to women with TS/CTD, compared with matched women without TS/CTD.

Methods

The Swedish Ethical Review Authority approved the study, with informed consent waived due to the use of non‐identifiable data. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Data Sources

Data from Swedish population‐based registers were linked using unique personal identification numbers. The Medical Birth Register contains prospectively recorded information since 1973 on antenatal, obstetric, and neonatal care. 14 The National Patient Register (NPR) includes information on inpatient care for somatic and psychiatric disorders, including outpatient specialist services since 2001. 15 Diagnoses are based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD). ICD‐10 codes were implemented in 1997. The Prescribed Drug Register contains all dispensed drug prescriptions (classified according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical [ATC] system) since 1 July 2005. 16 The Total Population Register and the Education Register include sociodemographic information.

Study Population and Exposure

We included all singleton live births at ≥22 gestational weeks between 1 January 2001 and 31 December 2021. Mothers or offspring with missing IDs and offspring with missing sex were excluded. Women with an ICD‐10 diagnosis of TS/CTD (eTable 1), recorded between the age of 3 years and the index childbirth, were considered exposed, following a validated algorithm 17 used in previous studies. 4 , 18 Each of the pregnancies to women with TS/CTD was matched on maternal age and year of delivery with up to 10 pregnancies to women without TS/CTD.

Maternal Use of Antipsychotics

Exposure to antipsychotics (ATC N05A), the group of medications with the strongest evidence base for TS/CTD treatment, 19 was defined as the drug being dispensed from 30 days before conception to the index childbirth, between July 2005 and 2021.

Outcomes

Pregnancy and delivery outcomes included gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, induction of labor, mode of delivery (elective cesarean section, emergency cesarean section, instrumental delivery), and postpartum hemorrhage. Neonatal outcomes were severe neonatal morbidity, preterm birth, small for gestational age, low birth weight (<2500 g), low 5‐min Apgar score, neonatal hypoglycemia, neonatal jaundice, neonatal respiratory distress, neonatal infections, and congenital major and minor malformations (eTables 1, 2, and 3).

Covariates and Additional Variables

Maternal characteristics included age at delivery, parity, year of delivery, height, body mass index (BMI; kg/m2), birth country, cohabitation with a partner, education, smoking during pregnancy, and pre‐pregnancy diabetes and hypertension. We also studied maternal psychiatric comorbidity diagnosed at any time before childbirth, including neurodevelopmental disorders, bipolar and psychotic disorders, and depression and anxiety disorders. A composite variable including any such psychiatric comorbidity, diagnosed before childbirth, was also examined (eTables 1 and 3).

Data Analysis

We estimated relative risks (aRRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for TS/CTD pregnancies versus matched pregnancies without TS/CTD using Poisson log‐linear regression conditioned on the matching set. To account for heterogeneity between strata, we applied a cluster‐robust variance estimation. Adjustments were made for maternal BMI, birth country, cohabitation with a partner, education, and smoking during pregnancy. Four additional models also adjusted for (i) neurodevelopmental disorders, (ii) bipolar/psychotic disorders, (iii) depression/anxiety disorders, and (iv) any of the previously mentioned disorders.

In the sensitivity analyses, we first repeated the primary analysis in women with TS/CTD diagnosed within 10 years prior to childbirth to include those likely to have tics persisting into adulthood. Second, we excluded exposed and unexposed women treated with antipsychotics during pregnancy.

Data management and analysis were performed between 13 January and 30 April 2025 using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). All tests employed two‐tailed significance at P < 0.05.

Results

The final cohort included 581 pregnancies with TS/CTD and 5777 matched pregnancies without TS/CTD. The mean age at the first recorded TS/CTD diagnosis in the exposed women was 19.4 (SD 7.2) years. Women with TS/CTD were more likely to be born in a Nordic country, have a lower education level, live alone, be obese, smoke, and have more psychiatric comorbidity. A total of 424 (73.0%) mothers with TS/CTD had a diagnosis of any psychiatric disorder compared with 767 (13.3%) mothers without TS/CTD. Forty‐eight (8.3%) of the women with TS/CTD used antipsychotics during pregnancy (eTable 4).

Pregnancies with TS/CTD had higher rates of preeclampsia, induction of labor, and elective and emergency cesarean section (Table 1). In the adjusted analyses, maternal TS/CTD was associated with 79% increased risk of gestational diabetes (95% CI 1.04–3.10), 74% increased risk of elective cesarean section (95% CI 1.33–2.30), 70% increased risk of emergency cesarean section (95% CI 1.28–2.26), and 47% increased risk of preeclampsia, albeit this association was not statistically significant (95% CI 0.93–2.32) (Fig. 1). Neonates of women with TS/CTD had slightly higher rates of severe neonatal morbidity, preterm birth, and low Apgar score compared with those of women without TS/CTD (Table 1). After adjusting, no significant increase in any neonatal adverse outcomes was observed (Fig. 1). Results remained overall unchanged after additional adjustment for psychiatric comorbidities (eTable 5).

Discussion

In this matched cohort study, maternal TS/CTD was associated with a significantly increased risk of gestational diabetes and elective and emergency cesarean section, as well as a non‐significant increased risk of preeclampsia, compared with matched pregnancies without TS/CTD. Neonates of women with TS/CTD showed no increased risk of adverse neonatal outcomes, compared with the neonates of women without TS/CTD. Adjusting for psychiatric comorbidities and excluding women on antipsychotic medication from the exposed and unexposed groups did not substantially affect the results.

To our knowledge, this is the first population‐based study examining pregnancy, delivery, and neonatal complications in women with TS/CTD. Previous literature was limited to case reports and series and reported no adverse pregnancy 6 and delivery outcomes. 9 , 10 The two case reports by Gómez‐Ríos et al. 10 and Sener et al. 11 described deliveries by cesarean section, attributed to a significant worsening of the motor tics during delivery. Stern et al. 6 described 10 vaginal and 1 cesarean section births in the 11 pregnancies of 8 women with TS. They reported that the tics caused concern among attending obstetricians, although whether this concern influenced the delivery plans and medical decisions is unknown. 6 Consistent with our findings, other neurological conditions like epilepsy 12 and psychiatric disorders such as OCD 13 are also associated with increased risk of cesarean section and gestational diabetes. Our finding of increased risk of gestational diabetes in women with TS/CTD, even after adjusting for maternal BMI and accounting for antipsychotic use, raises the possibility of a shared biological or genetic vulnerability, aligning with evidence that pro‐inflammatory states in pregnancy are linked to neurodevelopmental disorders, including TS. 20

Taken together, our results suggest that collaboration between neurologists, psychiatrists, and obstetricians is needed to monitor the pregnancies and deliveries of women with TS/CTD.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths include the use of nationwide registers, providing comprehensive coverage and prospectively collected data, and the excellent validity of the ICD codes for TS/CTD. 17 Limitations include, first, the relatively small cohort size, which limited our ability to assess less common outcomes. There is also a potential risk for misclassification, as some TS/CTD cases may have been missed or diagnosed later, as reflected in our exposed cohort's mean age at first diagnosis of 19 years and the known later symptom onset, age of diagnosis, and longer time‐to‐diagnosis in females, compared with males. 21 Second, the NPR only includes specialist care diagnoses, limiting generalizability to milder cases. Third, medication data reflected dispensation, not adherence. Finally, we did not examine other variables that could play a role in the association between TS/CTD and perinatal and neonatal outcomes, such as additional medications used to treat TS/CTD (eg, alpha‐adrenergic agonists), 19 somatic conditions, or genetic and environmental variables shared within families. Future studies should explore potential mechanisms behind the described associations.

Conclusions

Maternal TS/CTD was associated with increased risk of some pregnancy and delivery complications, but not to adverse neonatal outcomes. Improved liaison between neurology, psychiatry, and obstetric services is warranted.
