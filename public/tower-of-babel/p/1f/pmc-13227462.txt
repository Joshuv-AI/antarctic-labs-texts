Investigating the Use of Antidepressants in Treating Major Depressive Disorder: Insights from a Retrospective Study in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Hassan A Alsaad, Wajd A Alfaraj, Maryam M Alrababi, Ali M Sonbaa, Dalal M AlBakr, Ali Saleh Abensaad, Fadhel A Alomar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:To investigate patterns of antidepressant prescribing and continuation rates among patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) in Saudi Arabia and to identify factors associated with treatment persistence.Method:A retrospective study was conducted at King Fahad Hospital of the University between May 2023 and February 2025. Data on patients' demographic characteristics, diagnostic information, and prescribed medications were collected. Initial antidepressant therapy and treatment continuation were evaluated, and potential influencing factors were analyzed.Results:Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) accounted for most first-line prescriptions (71%), followed by SSRI/atypical combinations (11%) and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) (5%). Of the 220 patients assessed for treatment continuation, only 38% maintained their initial therapy beyond three months. Continuation was highest among those treated with SNRIs, either alone or in combination with atypical agents, while SSRIs had the lowest persistence rate (35%). Older age and higher BMI were also significantly associated with enhanced treatment adherence.Conclusion:Although SSRIs remain the predominant first line treatment for MDD in Saudi Arabia, their limited continuation rates highlight the need for alternative approaches. The comparatively higher persistence observed with SNRIs highlights their potential for long term management. These findings advocate for personalized antidepressant selection and adherence enhancing interventions, thereby improving outcomes in MDD. Future studies should investigate biological markers, such as alterations in BDNF expression, and adjunctive non-pharmacological approaches to better elucidate the variability in treatment outcomes among patients with MDD.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) remains a significant global public health challenge, affecting an estimated 5% of the global adult population [1]. In Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of depression is reported to be high. Studies have identified multiple risk factors, including sex, socioeconomic status, educational attainment, chronic medical conditions, and social determinants such as marital status and family structure [23]. The MDD is linked to considerable disability, reduced productivity, increased healthcare utilization, and an elevated risk of suicide. The consequences of MDD place a substantial strain on individuals, families, and healthcare systems [45]. These burdens underscore the need for effective, evidence-based pharmacological interventions to enhance patient outcomes and mitigate the disorder's societal impact.

Although antidepressants are a cornerstone in the treatment of MDD, huge disparities exist in the prescribing practices across healthcare systems [67]. These differences are partly influenced by the expanding range of available antidepressants. Since the introduction of the first agents in the 1950s, pharmacological developments have produced numerous drugs with diverse mechanisms of action, efficacy profiles, and tolerability [8]. Furthermore, prescribing patterns are shaped by patient-related factors, such as demographic characteristics, comorbid medical or psychiatric conditions, and individual treatment histories [9]. Therapeutic response to antidepressants is not uniform, and approximately 30%–40% of patients fail to achieve adequate improvement with their initial medication, often necessitating a switch to an alternative agent [1011]. In addition, adverse effects are a major barrier to adherence, as a result of which some patients discontinue therapy prematurely or follow their regimen inconsistently [1213]. Addressing these tolerability issues is essential to sustain treatment engagement and reap optimal benefits from antidepressant therapy in MDD.

Examining the prescribing patterns of antidepressants offers beneficial insights for clinical practice and the broader understanding of MDD management. Such analysis provides information on real-world therapeutic effectiveness, aiding in the identification of optimal strategies for specific patient subgroups and in uncovering common barriers to successful outcomes [1415]. Familiarity with these patterns allows clinicians to make evidence-based prescribing decisions, minimize the reliance on trial-and-error approaches, and promote more individualized, patient-centered care.

Earlier studies on antidepressant prescribing patterns have contributed to the development of treatment algorithms designed to optimize clinical outcomes. These algorithms provide evidence-based guidance on strategies such as switching medications in cases of inadequate response or combining therapies to enhance efficacy and address complex patient needs [1617]. Nonetheless, real-world evidence on antidepressant prescribing patterns remains limited in Saudi Arabia. Hence, exploring the current prescribing trends in the country is crucial for assessing the efficacy of existing therapies and suggesting improvements in prescription practices. Such research has the potential to enhance the quality of psychiatric care and optimize clinical outcomes for patients living with MDD and other mental health conditions.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the patterns of prescribing antidepressants for treating MDD in Saudi Arabia. Moreover, this research intended to determine the factors influencing the utilization of antidepressants for patients with MDD. Gaining such knowledge is essential for advancing the pharmacological management of MDD as it can refine the treatment strategies and raise the overall standard of mental healthcare.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study was conducted at King Fahad Hospital of the University in Khobar, Saudi Arabia, from May 2023 to February 2025. This research was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University (IRB# 2024-05-690). Eligible participants were patients aged ≥16 years who had diagnostic codes for depression according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) criteria, as recorded in the hospital's electronic health records. Patients were excluded if they had not been prescribed antidepressant medications during the study period or if they were already receiving antidepressant therapy at enrollment. The reason was that prescriptions from this timeframe could not be reliably classified as first initiations or refills predating the available data (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of patient inclusion and exclusion criteria for the study cohort.

Patient data were extracted from the hospital's electronic medical record system (TrackCare) using ICD-10 codes corresponding to MDD. The dataset included demographic profile (age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities, and nationality), diagnostic information, and prescribed medications. For descriptive and analytical purposes, antidepressant agents were categorized by their pharmacological classes.

Treatment persistence was determined by assessing antidepressant discontinuation rates over a 6-month period following the initial prescription, providing an estimate of ongoing usage patterns within the cohort.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, IBM Corporation, Version 29). Descriptive statistics were reported as means, medians, counts, and percentages. Associations between categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test. In contrast, the relationships between continuous and categorical variables were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the independent-samples t-test. When the assumption of normality was not met, nonparametric alternatives (Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test) were applied. A 2-tailed p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Data were extracted for 515 patients, of whom 333 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis. The study sample was prescribed a range of antidepressants, which included selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as escitalopram, paroxetine, and sertraline; serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) such as venlafaxine; tricyclic antidepressants such as amitriptyline and clomipramine; and atypical antidepressants such as mirtazapine.

The mean age of the cohort was 43 ± 17 years. The majority of patients were women (65%) and Saudi Arabian citizens (90%). In addition to antidepressant drugs, 32% of the patients were taking medications for other psychiatric conditions, mostly antipsychotics. The SSRIs were the most prescribed first-line medications, representing 71% of all initial prescriptions. Other first-line treatments included SSRI combined with an atypical antidepressant (11%, n = 37), SNRI alone (5%, n = 18), SNRI combined with an atypical antidepressant (3%, n = 10), and other antidepressants or combinations (10%, n = 32). These distributions are depicted in Fig. 2.

Patterns of first-line antidepressant prescribing, showing the predominance of SSRIs compared with other antidepressant classes and combinations (N = 333).

The link between the antidepressants' pharmacological class and the patients' demographic characteristics was examined to identify factors influencing prescribing patterns. All 333 patients were included in these analyses; however, information on body mass index (BMI) was available for only 165 patients due to incomplete weight and height data.

A Kruskal–Wallis test and one-way ANOVA were applied to determine the correlation of age and BMI with the antidepressant class. A statistically significant difference was observed in age across prescribing patterns (X2(4, N = 333) = 32.388, p < 0.001). Those prescribed SSRIs had the lowest median age (38 years), and those prescribed an SSRI combined with an atypical antidepressant had the highest median age (55 years). Furthermore, BMI varied significantly by antidepressant class (F(4, 160) = 2.775, p = 0.029), and SSRI prescriptions were associated with the lowest mean BMI (27.3 ± 6.6 kg/m2).

Chi-square tests were performed to explore categorical demographic variables (sex, nationality, and concomitant use of psychotropic medications) in relation to prescribing patterns of antidepressants. However, none of these variables exhibited statistically significant associations (Table 2).

Of the 220 patients followed up for 6 months, 62% discontinued their therapy, defined as no prescription reorder during months 4-6. All patients prescribed SNRI/atypical antidepressants continued the treatment. Continuation rates were 58% for patients prescribed SNRIs, 35% for SSRIs, 32% for SSRI/atypical combinations, and 29% for other antidepressant drugs or combinations. These variations and continuation patterns across antidepressant classes are depicted in Fig. 3.

Continuation rates of initial antidepressant therapy by pharmacological class over a 6-month follow-up period (n = 220).

Additionally, age and BMI were directly associated with the continuation of therapy. Patients who continued treatment had a higher median age than those who discontinued it (47 vs. 38 years; Mann–Whitney U = 4320.5, p = 0.003). BMI analysis, which was limited to 99 patients for whom data were available, showed that those who continued therapy had a higher mean BMI than those who discontinued it (30.9 ± 6.5 vs. 27.0 ± 6.1 kg/m2; t(97) = 3.073, p = 0.003). In contrast, sex, nationality, and concomitant psychotropic use were not significantly linked to the continuation status (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

The treatment outcomes for MDD can be improved by increasing our understanding of antidepressant prescribing patterns and patient responses within specific populations. This insight can aid in developing tailored therapies that effectively meet the requirements of a population [18]. Hence, studying the prescribing patterns of antidepressants for Saudi Arabian patients is crucial. The findings from this study revealed that SSRIs were the predominant class of antidepressants prescribed initially. The prescribed class of antidepressants was significantly associated with age and BMI, whereas sex, concomitant psychotropic use, and nationality did not exert a significant influence. Furthermore, the results indicated that less than half of the patients continued their initial antidepressant therapy beyond 3 months, with continuation rates varying across drug classes. The continuation rates were higher among patients treated with SNRIs and SNRI/atypical drug combinations. Similarly, older age and higher BMI were linked to a greater likelihood of therapy adherence; in contrast, sex, nationality, and concomitant psychotropic use were not significantly associated.

In this study, most patients with MDD were women, which is consistent with existing literature. This sex-based disparity has been extensively reported and is considered to reflect a combination of biological, psychological, and sociocultural influences [2319]. Our study observed that a considerable proportion of the patients (32%) were prescribed drugs for mental health conditions other than MDD, such as antipsychotic drugs. This finding agrees with previous investigations and meta-analyses reporting comparable patterns of comorbidity among patients with psychiatric conditions [2021].

Investigation of antidepressant prescribing patterns revealed SSRIs to be the most frequently prescribed class of antidepressants. This finding aligns with prior studies that have consistently reported higher rates of SSRI use for MDD compared with other antidepressant classes. However, the proportion of patients initiated on SSRIs in our study (71%) was higher than the rates reported in earlier investigations (52%) [2223]. This variation may reflect differences in study populations and temporal alterations in prescribing practices, given that earlier reports were based on data collected around 2015. In contrast, our study examined patterns in 2023, which may reflect evolving prescribing trends over time.

In our study, patients receiving combination therapy were, on average, older than those treated with SSRIs or SNRIs alone. Antidepressant prescribing for elderly patients requires careful consideration, as they often exhibit altered pharmacokinetics, coexisting medical conditions, and an increased risk of side effects [2425]. Therefore, our results imply that age may influence clinical decision-making, particularly in guiding the selection of complex or cautious therapeutic strategies for older patients. Similarly, other patient characteristics may influence the prescribing decisions. For instance, BMI differed among patients when stratified by the prescribed antidepressant. As BMI data were incomplete for a proportion of participants, this analysis was performed with a smaller subsample. However, our results align with previous studies that have identified body weight as a key consideration in antidepressant selection. This factor is significant as certain agents have been associated with changes in body weight, a concern for many patients. Moreover, BMI itself has been shown to influence the therapeutic response [2627]. In addition to body weight, sex has been examined as a potential determinant of prescribing patterns. Nonetheless, our study failed to find a significant link between sex and the type of antidepressant prescribed. This finding is aligned with previous investigations reporting a lack of difference in antidepressant efficacy between the 2 sexes [15]. However, evidence from longer-term studies indicates that despite comparable therapeutic outcomes, the prescribing patterns may differ by sex, with women more often receiving combination treatments [16]. Extending this analysis to other clinical variables, our findings also found no significant variations in prescribing patterns for medications targeting other mental health conditions co-occurring with MDD. Literature has highlighted the complexities in managing patients with psychiatric comorbidities [28]. This discrepancy in findings regarding sex and the management of comorbid conditions may be explained by the fact that our analysis focused exclusively on initial prescriptions. In contrast, other studies included follow-up treatments too, capturing changes in therapy over time.

In our study, only about one third of the patients maintained their initial antidepressant therapy beyond 3 months. This rate is lower than that reported in a previous study, where 60% of the patients continued antidepressants, suggesting possible differences in patient populations, healthcare settings, or prescribing practices. Importantly, this research observed significant variations in continuation rates across antidepressant classes. In this study, SNRI/atypical antidepressant combinations demonstrated the highest continuation rates. This contrasts with earlier research indicating that the atypical antidepressant mirtazapine was associated with the greatest likelihood of discontinuation [2930]. Such discrepancies may reflect variations in study populations or characteristics of the healthcare system. However, these observations highlight the influence of medication class on treatment persistence and underscore the need for individualized plans for patients initiated on regimens associated with low continuation rates [31].

In this study, patients who continued therapy had a higher median age than those who discontinued it, a result consistent with previous studies reporting greater adherence to the initial pharmacotherapy among older patients [32]. Age appears to influence adherence, as older individuals often show higher adherence rates than younger patients, possibly due to greater health awareness and an increased recognition of the consequences of untreated depression [3334].

Body mass index is a significant clinical factor and has been documented to influence therapeutic outcomes in patients with MDD [2627]. The higher continuation rates observed among individuals with elevated BMI may reflect the persistence of depressive symptoms often associated with obesity. Excess adipose tissue and alterations in gut microbiota can promote chronic systemic inflammation, during which proinflammatory cytokines increase plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) production. Elevated PAI-1 impairs the conversion of proBDNF into mature brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), potentially disrupting neuroplasticity and contributing to sustained depressive symptoms [3536]. Additionally, individuals with lower BMI may be more concerned about potential weight gain—a well-recognized side effect of many antidepressants—which could lead to earlier discontinuation or reluctance to maintain medication use [30].

The impact of demographic factors, including sex, nationality, and concomitant psychotropic use, on treatment persistence shows inconsistency in findings across research studies [2937]. Such discrepancies could be attributed to variations in study populations, healthcare system characteristics, and sociocultural influences on treatment adherence. These disparities emphasize the complexity of factors influencing antidepressant continuation and highlight the need for further investigations involving larger, more diverse cohorts. It is also important to consider the specific reasons of antidepressant discontinuation, including adverse effects such as sexual dysfunction and weight gain—which often lead to premature treatment cessation—as well as patient-related perceptions, such as perceived lack of efficacy and fear of dependence [3038]. A more in-depth understanding of these variables is crucial for optimizing therapeutic strategies and enhancing patient outcomes.

This study has certain limitations. First, its retrospective design relied on electronic health records, which are prone to incomplete or inaccurate information, such as missing BMI data. However, this approach enabled the inclusion of a real-world cohort and reduced recall bias, offering practical insights into prescribing practices. Second, this study was conducted in a single tertiary hospital, limiting its generalizability to other healthcare settings. Nonetheless, this focus ensured consistency in data collection and clinical protocols, strengthening internal validity and providing a solid foundation for future research. Third, treatment persistence was evaluated only over a 6-month period, which may not capture longer-term adherence or outcomes. Yet, this period is the critical early phase of therapy, during which discontinuation is most common, making the findings clinically meaningful. Additionally, information regarding pregnancy status and the postpartum period was not available in the dataset, which may limit the interpretation of prescribing trends among women of reproductive age. Finally, this study did not assess clinical effectiveness or symptom improvement, which prevented a direct linkage between prescribing patterns and patient outcomes. Nonetheless, by furnishing information on prescribing behavior and persistence, this study provides baseline evidence and serves as a foundation for a prospective multicenter research.

In conclusion, this study offers valuable insights into antidepressant prescribing practices for MDD in Saudi Arabia. The SSRIs constituted the predominant first-line treatment, with age and BMI influencing the prescribing decisions. Therapeutic adherence rates were suboptimal, with less than half of the patients continuing the treatment beyond 3 months. Furthermore, significant variations were observed across drug classes, and higher persistence was linked to SNRIs and SNRI/atypical drug combinations as well as to older age and higher BMI. These findings underscore the importance of personalized prescribing approaches that consider patient characteristics and the tolerability of various agents. Improving the prescribing practices and supporting patients in continuing the therapy could help optimize the outcomes and strengthen the quality of mental healthcare for patients with MDD in Saudi Arabia. While this study focused on pharmacological treatment patterns, future investigations examining biological markers such as BDNF expression or adjunctive non-pharmacological strategies may provide a more comprehensive understanding of treatment outcomes in MDD.
