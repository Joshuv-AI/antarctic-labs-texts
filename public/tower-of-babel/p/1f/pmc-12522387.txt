Low-grade NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasm presenting as a colonic polyp and managed by polypectomy: a rare case report and literature review
Authors: Gertruda Evaristo, Namrata Setia, Peng Wang, Peter Pytel, Lindsay Alpert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasms constitute a novel, heterogeneous group of mesenchymal neoplasms originally described predominantly in soft tissue locations. They are commonly characterized by co-expression of S100 and CD34 immunostains and presence of NTRK fusions. While exceedingly rare, there are increasing reports of this lesion involving the gastrointestinal tract, presenting predominantly as large masses of the stomach, small bowel and colorectum.Case presentationWe present a case of a 37-year-old male who on colonoscopy was found to have a one cm polyp of the sigmoid colon which was removed by hot snare polypectomy. Histologic examination revealed haphazardly arranged bland spindle cells with diffuse CD34 and S100 co-expression. A targeted Next-Generation RNA Fusion Assay identified a TPR::NTRK1 fusion, confirming the diagnosis of low-grade NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasm. The mucosal and deep margins were free of tumor. In contrast to the previously reported cases, the patient was managed with polypectomy and active surveillance, and remained disease-free at 14 months follow up.ConclusionThis case contributes to the limited body of literature on gastrointestinal low-grade NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasms and raises the possibility of endoscopic treatment consideration for carefully selected patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13000-025-01713-3.

Introduction

Neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK)-rearranged spindle cell neoplasms constitute a relatively novel and heterogeneous group of mesenchymal neoplasms originally described in various soft tissue and osseous locations [1, 2]. Introduced in the fifth edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of soft tissue and bone tumors in 2020, this emerging entity comprises rare, molecularly-defined soft tissue tumors that are commonly characterized by co-expression of S100 and CD34 immunostains but lack SOX10 reactivity and have underlying NTRK fusions. NTRK1 is most often implicated, with fusion partners including LMNA, TPM3, TPR, and SPECC1L [2]. These tumors occur in both pediatric and adult patients and show no gender predilection. They are morphologically heterogeneous but exhibit certain overlapping characteristics. Macroscopically, they vary from small nodules to large (> 15 cm) masses. Histologically, these neoplasms are characterized by a haphazard proliferation of monomorphic spindled cells that may infiltrate the surrounding parenchyma and entrap native epithelial and stromal elements. Areas of fascicular growth and a storiform pattern can be seen as well. Distinctive band-like stromal and perivascular hyalinization is common, and scattered lymphocytes may be present within the lesion. Cytologically, the neoplastic cells are typically bland, with minimal nuclear atypia and a mitotic index of 1–5 mitoses per 10 HPFs. Rare cases with high-grade features, such as hypercellularity, an increased mitotic rate (> 10 mitoses per 10 HPFs) and marked nuclear atypia and pleomorphism, have also been described. These histologic features appear to predict the clinical course; while low-grade NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasms can be locally aggressive, metastases to date have been exclusively documented in cases exhibiting high-grade histologic features [1, 2].

Recently, the visceral involvement by these tumors has been increasingly recognized, and several reports of NTRK-rearranged mesenchymal tumors involving the gastrointestinal tract have been documented, outlining a heterogeneous group of neoplasms with a wide spectrum of morphologic features [2–9]. These lesions largely recapitulate their soft tissue counterparts, presenting predominantly as mural masses of varying size and exhibiting both low- and high-grade histologic features. However, while most of these lesions appear to grow as nodules and masses, little information is available regarding the prognosis and treatment of those that present as small gastrointestinal polyps. In this context, we present a unique case of a 37-year-old male patient who underwent a polypectomy of a sigmoid colon lesion, which was morphologically, immunohistochemically and molecularly confirmed to represent a small, submucosal, polypoid NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasm of colon. The polyp was completely removed, with no evidence of disease recurrence after 14-months of follow up. Comparing and contrasting this case to the previously described gastrointestinal NTRK-rearranged spindle cells neoplasms provides potential insight into the therapeutic and prognostic implications of these lesions in relation to their macroscopic and microscopic features.

Case report

Clinical history

A 37-year-old male patient presented with fatigue and loose bowel movements. He underwent a colonoscopy which revealed three sessile colonic polyps including a 1.0 cm polyp in the sigmoid colon, which was removed via hot snare polypectomy (Fig. 1). The remainder of the colon appeared normal. A concurrent esophagogastroduodenoscopy was unremarkable.

Pathologic examination

Histologic evaluation revealed normal mucosa in the terminal ileum and colon as well as two sessile serrated lesions in the proximal colon. Microscopic examination of the sigmoid polyp revealed haphazardly arranged bland spindle cells infiltrating around the colonic crypts and extending into the submucosa (Fig. 2A-B). Interspersed band-like collagen bundles and scattered lymphocytes were noted within the lesion (Fig. 2C-D). There was no evidence of increased cellularity, infiltrative/destructive growth, conspicuous mitotic activity (< 1 mitosis per 10 HPFs), significant cytologic atypia or necrosis. By immunohistochemistry, the tumor cells revealed strong and diffuse CD34 (QBEND/10, 1:50, Dako) and S100 (EP32, prediluted, Leica) co-expression (Fig. 2E-F), but showed no immunoreactivity with SOX10 (EP268, 1:200, Cell Marque), SMA (1AF, 1:200 VMS diluent, Dako), desmin (D33, 1:40 VMS diluent, Dako), EMA (E29, 1:400 Casein, Dako), CD117/KIT (polyclonal, 1:75, Dako) and DOG1 (K9, 1:300, Leica NCL), which were performed with appropriate controls. The mucosal and deep margins were free of tumor.

Molecular analysis

An in-house targeted Next-Generation RNA Fusion Assay was performed per methodology described in additional file 1 and detected a TPR::NTRK1 fusion with breakpoints within exon 14 of TPR (NM_003292) and connection with exon 11 of NTRK1 (NM_001007792). The chimeric transcript appeared to retain the proper reading frame at the junction point, confirming the diagnosis of low-grade NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasm (Fig. 3).

Follow up

Given the complete endoscopic resection and absence of high risk pathologic factors, the patient did not receive any tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) or undergo any further treatment and was managed with active surveillance. His follow up colonoscopy after 11 months showed no evidence of residual or recurrent disease. He remained free of disease at 14 months post diagnosis.

Discussion

Clinicopathologic characteristics of NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasms in the gastrointestinal tract

Following the original description of NTRK-rearranged spindle cell tumors of the soft tissue, Suurmeijer et al. [2] first reported a gastrointestinal counterpart in the stomach, presenting as a high-grade spindle cell neoplasm harboring an LMNA::NTRK1 fusion. Since then, three additional gastric cases have been described in the English literature, as well as five instances of small bowel involvement and eight instances of colorectal involvement, including the current case (Table 1). Similarly to their soft tissue counterparts, the gastrointestinal variants involve both pediatric and adult populations (age range of 2 months to 63 years; median of 27 years) and affect both genders (56% men). The lesions are predominantly described as masses, varying in size from 2 cm to 25 cm (median of 6.8 cm). In addition to the current case, three other colorectal lesions were clinically described as polyps, albeit all of larger size, varying from 2.5 cm to 4.5 cm (overall median of 3 cm). Microscopically, high risk features such as increased cellularity, increased mitotic index and marked nuclear atypia were reported in eight of seventeen cases – one gastric tumor (25% of gastric lesions), three small bowel tumors (60% of small bowel lesions) and 4 colorectal tumors (40% of colorectal lesions).

The spectrum of NTRK rearrangements in gastrointestinal NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasms

The molecular rearrangements reported in the gastrointestinal NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasms likewise closely mimic their soft tissue counterparts, with NTRK1 involved in 16 of 18 cases. The most common partners are LMNA (6) and TPM3 (6), followed by TPR (2). Two NTRK1-rearranged tumors involved an unknown partner. Two additional lesions harbored an NTRK2::STRN fusion and an NTRK3 rearrangement with an unknown partner. The identification of NTRK fusions was performed with a variety of ancillary techniques across studies, including Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization, and DNA and RNA-based sequencing, all of which can be used in clinical practice to confirm the diagnosis of this specific entity. Several pan-TRK antibodies are additionally available and can serve as a rapid screening tool in suspected cases, though the reported sensitivity (75–88%) and specificity (81–95%) limits their utility as a diagnostic test [10].

Prognosis and therapeutic considerations in management of gastrointestinal NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasms

Given the novelty of this entity, the prognostic significance of NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasms has yet to be firmly defined. Studies predominantly including the soft tissue neoplasms have outlined histologic grade as the main prognostic factor [1, 2]. This concept appears to apply to gastrointestinal NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasms as well, as all patients with available follow up information whose tumors did not exhibit any high risk histologic features, including the current case, had no evidence of residual or recurrent disease at up to 14 months of follow up (Table 1). Among the eight high-grade tumors, metastatic spread was reported in one patient with a small intestinal tumor who succumbed to the disease at 12 months follow up, as well as two patients with colorectal tumors living with disease at the time of last follow up (40 and 50 months). Tumor-related mortality was reported in one additional patient with a gastric tumor, while one patient was alive with the disease in absence of reported metastatic spread. Three patients with high risk histologic features showed no evidence of residual or recurrent disease at follow up ranging from 3.5 to 30 months (37.5% of the high-grade tumors).

Similarly, clinical guidelines for treatment of NTRK-rearranged neoplasms are not well established, albeit several reports have detailed the use of TRK-directed therapy for NTRK-rearranged mesenchymal neoplasms [11]. In the studied cohort, information on patient management was available for 15 of 18 cases. Thirteen patients were treated with surgical resection, including both patients with low- and high-grade tumors, and three of these patients, all with high-grade tumors, had additionally received TKI; one patient with high-grade tumor received TKI therapy without surgical excision. All but one patient treated with surgery alone had no evidence of residual or recurrent disease at follow up ranging from 3 to 30 months, and one patient was alive with metastatic disease at 40 months follow up. The outcome within the combined surgery-TKI group was variable, with one patient with no evidence of residual or recurrent disease at 3.5 months follow up, one patient alive with metastatic disease at 50 months follow up and one patient deceased from metastatic disease at 12 months follow up. The single patient treated with TKI alone was alive with disease at 25 months follow up. Among the four cases that clinically presented as a colorectal polyp, only the current case was treated with polypectomy and surveillance, while the remaining three cases underwent surgical resection. Bertolini et al. [5] specifically described a polypectomy followed by right hemicolectomy approach and confirmed no evidence of residual neoplasm, peritoneal disease or other abnormalities in the resection specimen. While further data will be needed to better understand the prognostication and management of these patients, the currently available literature appears to suggest that local excision and surveillance could represent an appropriate therapeutic approach to low-grade tumors. Additionally, extrapolating from the current case, it is conceivable that small, localized tumors presenting as gastrointestinal polyps may be amenable to endoscopic resection without the need for follow up surgery.

In summary, the current case and the literature review highlight multiple similarities among the gastrointestinal NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasms and their soft tissue counterparts, allowing for extrapolation of our current understanding of the clinicopathologic characteristics and the management of this novel entity. The current case additionally suggests that endoscopic excision with active surveillance could be a therapeutic consideration in small, low-grade NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasms presenting as gastrointestinal polyps. Further characterization of these rare gastrointestinal lesions will be required to establish definitive treatment algorithms and prognostication.
