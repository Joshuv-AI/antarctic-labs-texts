Support Cells to Stem Cells: The Promise of Enteric Glia in Treating Gut Neuropathies
Authors: Tim J. Hibberd, Nick J. Spencer
Published: 2026

Enteric glial cells (EGCs), once considered mere structural “glue” for the enteric nervous system (ENS), are increasingly recognized as dynamic and crucial players in gastrointestinal health and disease.1 Over the past decade, research has illuminated their active role in gut function,2,3 revealing that EGCs respond to neurotransmitters, engage in complex bidirectional communication with enteric neurons through purinergic and peptidergic signaling, and even interact directly via pannexin hemichannels.4, 5, 6, 7 Their influence extends beyond the nervous system, as they converse with immune cells, positioning them as critical intermediaries between the gut environment and the ENS.8,9

A key aspect of EGC biology that has garnered significant interest is their function as a resident neural stem cell population within the adult ENS. The concept of neural crest-derived stem cells persisting in the adult gut emerged in the 1990s, with studies demonstrating that cells isolated from the gut could form neurospheres in vitro.10,11 It is now understood that EGCs are the source of this neurogenic and proliferative potential. However, under normal physiological conditions, their proliferation and differentiation into new neurons are exceptionally low.12, 13, 14, 15 This quiescent state changes dramatically in the face of injury or inflammation, where EGCs can be activated to generate new neurons, highlighting the inherent plasticity of the ENS.12,16

The EGC population is not monolithic. Morphological classifications first established in the guinea pig ileum have been validated and expanded upon by modern genetic and single-cell RNA sequencing techniques.17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 These studies have revealed multiple EGC subpopulations, although a definitive map linking gene expression profiles to specific functions and locations remains to be developed. A growing consensus suggests that a specific EGC subset located within the myenteric plexus retains the primary stem cell function in the adult gut.24 This heterogeneity is a critical consideration for developing regenerative therapeutic strategies.

The regenerative capacity of EGCs offers profound therapeutic promise for treating gut motility disorders, such as Hirschsprung disease, which are characterized by a loss of enteric neurons. Recent breakthroughs have shown that EGCs can be isolated, expanded in vitro, and transplanted into an aganglionic gut, where they can generate new, functional neurons.25, 26, 27 This has opened a new frontier in regenerative medicine for the gut but also raised critical questions about the optimal methods for promoting differentiation and ensuring the functional integration of these new neurons.

Two recent studies in a previous issue of CMGH provide crucial insights into these challenges. Han et al28 focused on optimizing in vitro conditions, finding that a combination of glial-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) and retinoic acid synergistically enhances neurogenesis from both mouse and human EGCs.19 Complementing this, Van Mechelen et al29 provide vital in vivo proof-of-concept. Having also identified the neurogenic role of GDNF, they then transplanted GDNF-cultured EGCs into a mouse model of Hirschsprung disease and demonstrated, using optogenetics, that the resulting new neurons functionally integrated with the host tissue, eliciting physiological responses in surrounding smooth muscle.28 Together, these studies represent a significant leap forward, demonstrating not only how to efficiently generate enteric neurons but also confirming that these lab-grown cells can functionally repair the gut, paving the way for novel cell-based therapies.
