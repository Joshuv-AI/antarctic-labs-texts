Cognitive complaints and their associations with role functioning limitations among adolescent and young adult cancer survivors in Hong Kong
Authors: Panpan Xiao, Yan Kate Chow, Weishang Deng, Cheuk Him Liu, Winnie Wan-Yee Tso, Keary Rui Zhou, Chun-Kit Ngan, Herbert Ho-Fung Loong, Chi Kong Li, Yin Ting Cheung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCognitive problems may be associated with role functioning in adolescent and young adult (AYA) cancer survivors. This study aimed to characterize the prevalence and phenotypes of cognitive complaints and examine their associations with role functioning limitations in Chinese AYA cancer survivors.MethodsThis multicenter cross-sectional study included AYA cancer survivors (diagnosed at 15–39 years). The participants’ cognitive complaints were collected using the Childhood Cancer Survivor Study-Neurocognitive Questionnaire (CCSS-NCQ). Role functioning in family, leisure, and work/school was assessed using the Life Functioning Questionnaire. Patterns of cognitive complaints across the four CCSS-NCQ domains were determined using latent class analysis (LCA). Multivariable logistic regression was used to investigate the association between phenotypes of cognitive complaints and role functioning limitations, adjusting for clinically relevant factors.ResultsOverall, 421 AYA cancer survivors (female: 59.6%, mean age: 31.51 ± 8.35 years) were recruited. With reference to age- and sex-adjusted reference scores derived from community controls, survivors reported cognitive complaints in task efficiency (23.5%), emotional regulation (24.7%), organization (20.2%), and memory (18.1%). The LCA identified three phenotypes of cognitive complaints (Class 1: no/mild complaints; Class 2: complaints in higher-order thinking; Class 3: complaints in global cognition). Compared with the “no/mild complaints” class, the “complaints in global cognition” class had higher odds of role functioning limitations in duties at work/school (OR = 5.43; 95% CI: 1.87–15.83) and duties at home (OR = 7.81; 95% CI: 3.49–17.48).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that phenotyping cognitive complaints may help characterize patterns of role functioning limitations in AYA cancer survivors.Keywords: Cognitive complaints, Role functioning, Adolescence, Young adults, Cancer

Introduction

Adolescents and young adults (AYAs) with cancer are diagnosed between 15 and 39 years of age. The recent estimated 5-year survival rate of AYAs with cancer in the United States (US) was greater than 75%. However, these survivors face a high risk of developing late effects of cancer treatments.1 Besides physical late effects, the cancer survivorship literature has increasingly documented cognitive changes among AYAs.2 This is highly concerning to AYAs, as the diagnosis and treatment of cancer coincide with the development and peak of various cognitive abilities, making them vulnerable to cancer-related cognitive impairment.3 Cognitive assessment typically includes two main aspects: (1) cognitive symptoms reported by survivors (subjective) using multi-item questionnaires, such as the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Cognitive Function Instrument (FACT-Cog),4 the Childhood Cancer Survivor Study-Neurocognitive Questionnaire (CCSS-NCQ),5 and (2) cognitive performance evaluated by objective neuropsychological tests, such as traditional paper-and-pencil neuropsychological tests and computerized cognitive tests.6 Evidence suggests that these two types of measures may evaluate distinct constructs, yet both provide essential information about cognitive functioning of cancer patients.7 One US population-based study showed that compared to non-cancer population, AYA cancer survivors reported a higher prevalence of difficulties with remembering and concentrating more than 10 years after diagnosis (27.8% vs. 16.4%).8 Chan et al.6 utilized the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery and found that AYA cancer survivors performed worse than healthy controls in domains of memory, response speed, executive function, and attention. In addition, a scoping review reported that approximately one third of AYA cancer survivors reported cancer-related cognitive impairment.2 To highlight, only one study in this review was conducted in Asia (Singapore)4 – one-third of the Singaporean AYA cancer survivors (n = 31/91) diagnosed with solid tumors reported cognitive difficulties on the FACT-Cog. The majority of studies4,9, 10, 11, 12 on neurocognitive issues in Asian countries have focused on survivors of breast cancer and central nervous system (CNS) cancers and middle-aged and older survivors; studies targeting AYA cancer survivors are scarce. However, differences in cultural values (e.g., collectivist), educational practices that prioritize academic/career progress requiring higher-order executive functioning, and social environments (e.g., stigma of mental disease) within the Asian contexts may disproportionately influence cancer survivors’ perceived cognitive difficulties compared with their Western counterparts.13, 14, 15 Collectively, given the ethnocultural differences between regions and the critical developmental stage of AYAs, understanding the cognitive problems faced by Asian AYA cancer survivors is crucial.

Besides cognitive problems, AYA cancer survivors experience a myriad of physical and psychological symptoms related to cancer treatment and its late effects.16 Both symptom distress and cognitive problems pose considerable challenges to their role functioning at home, school, and workplace during long-term survivorship, including educational (e.g., higher grade retention rate) and vocational (e.g., work reallocation, decreased work ability) interruption,17,18 social self-isolation,19,20 relational difficulties,21 and prolonged time required to complete daily tasks.22 These are the common limitations faced by AYA cancer survivors, as outlined in the National Comprehensive Cancer Network Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology: Adolescent and Young Adult Oncology.23 However, results from Western countries may not be generalizable to Asian populations. For example, our previous review24 suggested that AYAs with cancer in Japan and Singapore are concerned about school reintegration and coping with family responsibilities, struggles that are highly relevant to Asian cultures. Furthermore, psychosocial studies in Asian AYA populations have focused more on work functioning and family relationships than peer relationships, likely due to sociocultural nuances when compared with Western societies.24 Notably, even among various Asian countries/regions, there are evident differences in concerns and perceived challenges in psychosocial functioning.24 However, there is a paucity of studies delineating the associations between cognitive complaints and role functioning limitations in everyday life in Asian AYA survivors. To date, most studies on the role functioning of AYA survivors in China have focused on the work context.25,26 Limited data are available on other aspects of AYAs’ role functioning crucial in traditional Chinese culture, such as family functioning.

In Hong Kong, approximately 1540 AYAs were newly diagnosed with cancer yearly from 2018 to 2022.27 Data on cognitive outcomes and their associations with role functioning in everyday life among Chinese AYA cancer survivors remain scarce. In addition, beyond the culturally shaped differences in cognitive manifestations, patient-specific and clinical factors may contribute to different cognitive phenotypes within and between individuals over the cancer care continuum.28 Latent class analysis (LCA) is a person-centered statistical method that enables the identification of distinct subgroups, with individuals within each latent class exhibiting similar patterns of responses.29 Two studies have utilized LCA to identify cognitive phenotypes in cancer survivors; one30 focused on adult survivors of childhood cancer, and the other31 included a cancer population with a wide age range. Thus, the prevalence and characteristics of cognitive complaints during Chinese AYAs’ cancer survivorship warrant further research.

To address the above gaps, the objectives of this study are (1) to identify the prevalence and associated factors of cognitive complaints, (2) to identify the phenotypic profiles of cognitive complaints using the LCA method, and (3) to assess the association between phenotypes of cognitive complaints and work/school, family, and social functioning in Chinese AYA cancer survivors.

Methods

Study design

This multicenter, cross-sectional study was conducted at the Prince of Wales Hospital and Hong Kong Children’s Hospital (HKCH) from September 2023 to December 2024. Approval was obtained from the Joint Chinese University of Hong Kong–New Territories East Cluster Clinical Research Ethics Committee (2021.244) and the Research Ethics Committee of the HKCH (HKCH-REC-2021-060). Potential participants were recruited via stratified sampling based on the distribution of the cancer diagnoses in Hong Kong.27 Stratification was conducted by age group to approximate the projected proportions (15–19 years: 15% to 25%; 20–29 years: 30% to 40%; 30–39 years: 40% to 50%). Participants were also sampled according to the proportional distribution of cancer diagnoses and survival rates reported by the Hong Kong Cancer Registry.27 For example, higher survival rates were observed in leukemia, Hodgkin’s lymphoma, and breast cancer. The projected proportions per stratum were as follows: hematological cancers (15% to 20%), breast cancer (15% to 20%), sarcoma (5% to 10%), gonadal tumors (8% to 15%), CNS tumors (3% to 8%), and other solid tumors (20% to 30%). Efforts were made to follow the projected distribution in each stratum, though logistical barriers and recruitment rates hindered full adherence to these targets. Written consent was obtained from adult participants (≥ 18 years old). For underage participants (< 18 years old), written consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians, and written assent from the participants.

Study population

AYA cancer survivors who were diagnosed with cancer between 15 and 39 years of age, were alive ≥ 3 years after diagnosis, understood Cantonese or Mandarin, and had available medical records were eligible. Those with pre-existing non-cancer-related conditions associated with cognitive dysfunction (e.g., Down syndrome, traumatic brain injury) were excluded.

Local community-dwelling individuals without cancer (hereafter, “community controls”) aged 15–42 years during the survey were recruited via electronic invitations on social media platforms. The upper age limit was extended to 42 years to ensure comparability with AYA cancer survivors who were diagnosed at the upper age limit (i.e., 39 years) and recruited or evaluated for psychosocial outcomes ≥ 3 years after diagnosis. The same exclusion criteria were applied to both survivors and controls. Data from community controls were used solely to derive age- and sex-standardized normative references for cognitive complaints (CCSS-NCQ) and role functioning limitations (Life Functioning Questionnaire, LFQ). This approach has been adopted by other studies in the literature where normative references or population norms are not available.32,33

Survey instruments

Primary outcomes

The participants’ cognitive complaints were self-reported using the CCSS-NCQ. CCSS-NCQ was originally developed by Krull et al.34 to measure four domains of neurocognitive concerns with 25 items in young adult cancer survivors: task efficiency (9 items), emotional regulation (8 items), organization (8 items), and memory (8 items). The participants were asked to report the degree to which they experienced the problem presented by each item over the past 6 months. For this study, we used a revised version of the CCSS-NCQ35 consisting of 33 items, which was validated for use in young adult cancer survivors in the US. Separately, a Japanese group has previously reported that a 32-item version of CCSS-NCQ is valid and reliable for use among AYA cancer survivors in Japan.36 After obtaining approval, we translated and culturally adapted the CCSS-NCQ into a Traditional Chinese version according to the guidelines stipulated by the Translation and Cultural Adaptation-Principles of Good Practice.37 The questionnaire was forward- and backward translated, reconciled by independent parties, and underwent cognitive pretesting with a representative and culturally homogeneous sample of 10 local patients to identify items that were offensive and/or structurally difficult to understand within the local context. No item content was modified during the whole process. The final Traditional Chinese version maintains all 33 original items, and each item is rated on a 3-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“never a problem”) to 3 (“often a problem”). Domain-specific total scores are calculated separately for the four dimensions, with higher scores indicating more cognitive complaints. In addition, validity and reliability analysis were conducted. The Cronbach’s α of the Traditional Chinese CCSS-NCQ was 0.84 for task efficiency, 0.90 for emotional regulation, 0.83 for organization, and 0.83 for memory. Corrected item-to-scale correlations ranged from 0.47 to 0.78 for all items in the CCSS-NCQ (Supplementary Table S1), and the factor loadings ranged from 0.60 to 0.84.

Role functioning limitations in everyday life were assessed using the Life Functioning Questionnaire. LFQ was developed by Altshuler et al.38 to measure participants’ role functioning in four domains: leisure time with friends, leisure time with family, duties at home, and duties at work/school. Each domain includes questions about the amount of time spent in activities, interpersonal conflict or friction, and level of enjoyment. The home and work/school domains include an additional self-assessment question about duty performance. Items are rated on a four-point scale (1 = “no problems”; 4 = “severe problems”). The duties at work domain includes a question on additional information regarding work, financial, and living status in the past 6 months. Higher scores indicate more severe limitations in role functioning. We translated and culturally adapted the LFQ into a Traditional Chinese version following the guidelines stipulated by the Translation and Cultural Adaptation-Principles of Good Practice as described above 37 and conducted the validity and reliability analysis. The Cronbach’s α of the Traditional Chinese LFQ subscales ranged from 0.83 to 0.88, suggesting favorable internal consistency. Corrected item-to-scale correlations ranged from 0.62 to 0.80 for items in the LFQ (Supplementary Table S2), and the factor loadings ranged from 0.59 to 0.85.

Covariates

Baseline demographic (current age, sex, education level, and marital status) and clinical (age at diagnosis, cancer diagnosis, treatment modalities, and comorbidity status) characteristics of survivors were retrieved from the hospital’s medical records.

The symptom burden was assessed using the Rotterdam Symptom Checklist (RSCL). The original 30-item RSCL was developed as a tool to measure psychological symptoms (8 items) and physical symptoms (22 items) reported by cancer patients participating in clinical research39 and was later validated for use in real-world settings among cancer patients.40,41 The participants were asked to rate the extent to which each symptom had bothered them in the past month. The response categories were “not at all,” “a little,” “quite a bit,” and “very much.” The raw scores were standardized by transforming into a 100-point scale, with higher scores indicating higher symptom burden. In this study, the 30-item RSCL was used, with Cronbach’s α of 0.85 for physical symptom distress and 0.89 for psychological symptom distress.

Sample size calculation

Community controls were included as the comparison group. Our preliminary analysis showed that 14.6% to 18.0% of the young adult survivors of cancer reported significant limitations in the LFQ role functioning domains (p1).42 No reported LFQ estimates of functional limitations are available for the general population aged 15–39 years in Hong Kong. Hence, the estimated proportions of non-cancer controls with functional limitations (p0) referred to the rates reported in the “Social Data Collected via the General Household Survey: Special Topics Report - Report No.63–Persons with Disabilities and Chronic Diseases” by the Hong Kong SAR Government Census and Statistics Department.43 According to the report, 2.3% to 3.7% of the surveyed population aged 15–39 years had chronic disabilities that led to functional limitations. The required sample size to achieve 80% power (β = 0.2) at α = 0.05 (assuming that p0 = 0.037 and p1 = 0.14), with a survivor-to-control ratio of 2:1, was at least 296 survivors and 148 controls (Kelsey formula with continuity correction).

Statistical analysis

The raw scores of each CCSS-NCQ and LFQ domain were transformed into age- and sex-standardized T-scores based on the community control scores, with a higher T-score indicating worse cognitive function and role functioning. To identify clinically meaningful outcomes, subjective complaints/limitations on these measures were defined as a T-score falling in the top 10th percentile of the community controls (i.e., T-score ≥ 63), a definition used by studies involving cancer survivors.44,45

Descriptive statistics, namely frequency distributions, means, and standard deviations (SDs), were calculated for demographic and clinical variables. In the univariate analyses, the χ2 test and independent samples t test were used to compare the proportion of cognitive complaints and T-score differences, respectively, between the groups. We did not adjust for multiple testing as this exploratory step was to help identify the variables to be included in the multivariable analyses. Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine the associated factors for cognitive complaints in each domain; notably, cancer diagnosis and treatment variables were examined in separate models to avoid multicollinearity due to their high correlation. For each independent variable, odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were reported.

Latent class analysis, which helps to explain population heterogeneity within observed data through the identification of underlying subgroups of individuals, was performed using the item-level responses from the CCSS-NCQ to identify the phenotypes of cognitive complaints among the survivors. Each CCSS-NCQ item is originally a three-category variable, which was dichotomized for the LCA: responses of 1 were coded as 0, and responses of 2 or 3 were coded as 1.46 A series of LCA models between one and five classes were tested to identify the number of classes that best represented patterns of cognitive complaint based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Entropy, Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT) and its P-value. A significant p-value indicates that the estimated model fits the data better than a model with one fewer class. After an optimal LCA model was chosen, the association between phenotypes of cognitive complaints and role functioning limitations was explored using multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for demographic and clinical factors. All data analyses were conducted using Stata (Version 15, StataCorp, College, TX). LCA was conducted using Mplus (Version 8.0, Muthén & Muthén, Los Angeles, CA).

Results

Participants’ characteristics

Overall, 421 eligible AYA cancer survivors (hereafter, “survivors”) with a mean age of 31.51 (SD = 8.35) years at the survey and a mean of 4.76 years since cancer diagnosis were included in the analysis (Table 1). Of these, 300 (71.3%) were diagnosed in young adulthood, and 251 (59.6%) were female. Among survivors aged over 25 years, 174 (56.5%) did not complete college education, and 134 (43.5%) were single. The majority were diagnosed with hematological cancer (n = 157, 37.3%). The reported treatment modalities were chemotherapy (81.0%), radiotherapy (39.8%), surgery (38.3%), targeted therapy (34.3%), and immunotherapy (7.3%). About one fifth of the survivors (n = 92, 21.9%) had one or more comorbidities.

Prevalence of cognitive complaints and their associated factors

Fig. 1 illustrates the prevalence of cognitive complaints across all domains of CCSS-NCQ and role functioning limitations across all domains of LFQ. Up to a quarter of survivors reported significant complaints in emotional regulation (24.7%), task efficiency (23.5%), organization (20.2%), and memory (18.1%) compared with the expected 10% threshold among the general normative reference (all P < 0.001; Fig. 1A). Also, with reference to the age- and sex-adjusted reference scores derived from community controls (T-score = 50), survivors had higher T-scores of all four domains of CCSS-NCQ (all P < 0.05; Supplementary Table S4).

We summarized the cognitive domain scores and proportions of complaints in each domain among survivors, with stratifications for clinically relevant variables (Supplementary Table S4). Survivors diagnosed in young adulthood were more likely to report complaints on emotional regulation than those diagnosed in adolescence (OR: 3.24, 95% CI: 1.11–9.50; Table 2). More CNS tumor survivors reported complaints in task efficiency, emotional regulation, and memory than non-CNS solid tumor survivors (P < 0.05). Regarding treatment factors, exposure to chemotherapy (OR: 2.81, 95% CI: 1.22–6.49), surgery (OR: 2.17, 95% CI: 1.13–4.19), and immunotherapy (OR: 3.28, 95% CI: 1.18–9.12) were associated with more complaints on task efficiency. Exposure to radiotherapy was associated with more complaints on emotional regulation (OR: 2.32, 95% CI: 1.21–4.43) and memory (OR: 2.05, 95% CI: 1.08–3.91). Survivors with comorbidities were more likely to report task efficiency (OR: 2.36, 95% CI: 1.29–4.30) and emotional regulation (OR: 2.17, 95% CI: 1.11–4.24) problems than survivors without comorbidities. For symptom burden, survivors having higher levels of psychological symptom distress were more likely to report cognitive complaints across all cognitive domains. A similar pattern was observed for physical symptom distress (P < 0.05), except for emotional regulation, where no significant association was found (P = 0.72).

Prevalence of role functioning limitations

Among the 421 survivors, some survivors reported a “nil” response to “leisure time with family” (n = 3), “leisure time with friends” (n = 8), “duties at home” (n = 36) and “duties at work/school” (n = 125) per the instructions that survivors who had not engaged in these activities in the past month were not required to provide responses to the corresponding LFQ subscales.

We compared the characteristics of survivors who responded to these items with those who did not – Survivors with a ‘nil’ response were classified as non-reporters, whereas those who provided a valid response were classified as reporters. Compared with non-reporters, reporters were more likely to have been diagnosed in adolescence, have a higher monthly household income, have a college degree, be employed, and not have received surgery and immunotherapy (Supplementary Table S5). Differences in other demographic or clinical characteristics between reporters and non-reporters were not identified. All community controls reported role functioning outcomes.

Among the reporters of LFQ, survivors reported significant limitations in “leisure time with friends” (n = 61/413, 14.8%), “leisure time with family” (n = 85/418, 20.3%), “duties at home” (n = 101/385, 26.2%), and “duties at work/school” (n = 44/296, 14.9%) (Fig. 1B).

Association between phenotypes of cognitive complaints and role functioning limitations

After adjusting for demographic and clinical factors (Table 3), cognitive complaints in all domains were strongly associated with the domains of LFQ (P < 0.05). The strongest effect sizes were observed for the association between task efficiency and duties at work/school (OR: 6.14, 95% CI: 2.51–15.03), and between organization and duties at work/school (OR: 4.19, 95% CI: 1.82–9.65). In addition, a sensitivity analysis was conducted in which non-reporters were classified as having limitations in the LFQ domains of duties at home (n = 36) and duties at work/school (n = 125). The associations between cognitive complaints and duties at home and duties at work/school were consistent with the primary results (Supplementary Table S6), supporting the robustness of the observed associations.

The LCA identified the 3-class model as the best fit for describing cognitive complaints among survivors (Supplementary Table S7). Although the AIC and adjusted BIC continued to decrease as the number of classes increased, the 3-class model demonstrated high entropy (0.92), indicating clear classification, and yielded a significant LMR-LRT (P = 0.01) compared with the 2-class model. Furthermore, the 3-class model showed a balanced distribution, with the smallest class accounting for 16.1% of the sample. The average posterior classification probabilities for this model were 0.97, 0.96, and 0.98, respectively. In contrast, the 4-class model did not show a significant LMR-LRT (P = 0.23), and its entropy declined, suggesting limited improvement in model quality. Considering these statistical indicators alongside theoretical considerations, the 3-class model was selected as the final solution. Class 3 survivors had the highest T-score (≥ 70 points) across all CCSS-NCQ domains, suggesting complaints in global cognition phenotype. Class 1 survivors had the lowest T-score across all CCSS-NCQ domains (40–50 points) below the age- and sex-adjusted reference scores derived from community controls (i.e., T-score < 50 points), suggesting no/mild complaints. Class 2 survivors had T-scores between those of Class 1 and Class 3 survivors, with problems concentrated in emotional regulation and organization, suggesting complaints in higher-order thinking profile.

After adjusting for demographic and clinical factors (Table 4), compared with Class 1 survivors (no/mild complaints), Class 2 survivors (complaints in higher-order thinking) were more likely to report role functioning limitations in leisure time with family and duties at home (P < 0.05); Class 3 survivors (complaints in global cognition) had the highest odds of reporting role functioning limitations in all four domains, as compared with Class 1 survivors (P < 0.05). A sensitivity analysis was also conducted in which non-reporters were classified as having role functioning limitations. The results were consistent with the primary results (Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

Main findings

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in Hong Kong, and likely in a Chinese population, that provides evidence for the prevalence of cognitive complaints among AYA cancer survivors and their associations with role functioning limitations. AYA cancer survivors in Hong Kong reported more severe problems in cognitive functioning and role functioning than community controls. Cognitive complaints were associated with survivors’ poorer performance of family and work/school responsibilities. These results may provide future directions for validating the results using objective cognitive assessments and developing effective interventions to alleviate cognitive complaints in this population. Our findings also support ongoing advocacy for inclusive working environments for survivors with impaired working abilities.

In our study, up to a quarter of the AYA survivors reported significant cognitive complaints, particularly in emotional regulation and task efficiency. This is similar to the estimated prevalence of cancer-related cognitive impairment (25.75%) among AYA non-CNS cancer survivors in a recent scoping review that included studies assessing cognitive outcomes with objective or self-reported assessments.2 Using the CCSS-NCQ, Wu et al.47 found a similar prevalence of cognitive complaints in young adult survivors who had received pediatric hematopoietic cell transplant in the US. However, variation in the assessment tools covering different domains of cognitive function might contribute to the observed differences in the reported prevalence of subjective cognitive complaints.2 Additionally, cultural and socioenvironmental factors may be associated with cognitive functioning due to the complex interplay between an individual’s environment, experiences, and cognitive development.48,49 For example, collectivistic cultural norms predominant in most Asian societies emphasize emotional suppression and avoidance of burdening family members.13 AYA survivors tend to underreport subtle cognitive difficulties such as memory decline and psychological stress, which often manifest as a bidirectional, reinforcing loop.

Cultural stigma toward mental health issues in Asian societies also discourages survivors from disclosing cognitive symptoms and seeking professional help.15 In family-centric Asian cultural contexts, parental overprotection often prevents adolescent survivors from becoming independent even after treatment, particularly when physical effects such as cognitive decline are present – this may reduce opportunities for survivors to practice independent daily planning, problem-solving, and social interaction.20 We acknowledge that our study findings are limited by the use of self-reported measures, though they are widely adopted by other cancer cohorts to assess survivors’ cognitive complaints in everyday activities.4,49 As this is the first study to examine the association between cognitive complaints and role functioning limitations in Chinese AYA survivors, the findings may serve as a basis to facilitate larger-scale research to validate the preliminary findings of this study. Future work should include objective neuropsychological tests to identify survivors with clinical impairment and to investigate the trajectory of cognitive impairment in Chinese AYA cancer survivors.

Many studies have investigated the relationships between certain cancer diagnoses, treatment modalities, and cognitive outcomes.50,51 Consistent with the literature,51,52 brain tumor survivors in our study cohort who received CNS-directed therapies had the worst cognitive complaints. Notably, we found that the presence of comorbidities and high symptom burden were strongly associated with cognitive complaints. The mechanism underlying the relationships between comorbidities, cancer-related symptoms, and cognitive function has not been well-elucidated but is expected to be multifaceted in nature. Possible mechanisms include the direct influence of comorbidities and symptoms on brain structure, chronic inflammation caused by comorbidities, and neurodegenerative changes.53,54 Given the high symptom burden reported by AYA survivors, there is an imperative need for routine surveillance to detect treatment-related toxicities early and ensure timely referrals for survivors with comorbidities. Routine monitoring and surveillance of psychological and cognitive symptoms in AYA survivors who report high symptom burden or have developed comorbidities can facilitate early identification of cognitive problems. These patients may benefit from early interventions before symptoms develop into complications that may be associated with poorer functional outcomes later in life.

We found that cognitive complaints were significantly associated with various aspects of role functioning, including home, school/work, and social functioning. A scoping review summarized evidence on the associations between cognitive problems and functional outcomes in AYA cancer survivors and found that most of the included studies focused on employment or education.2 Few studies have explored the relationship between cognitive outcomes and survivors’ role functioning limitations in everyday life. Importantly, we found that while survivors with complaints in global cognition profile (Class 3) had the highest odds of reporting role functioning limitations, those with complaints concentrated in higher-order thinking skills (Class 2) reported worse family functioning. This finding is consistent with previous findings that breast cancer survivors were unable to fulfill their familial duties because of cognitive deficits55 and that some cancer survivors with persistent cancer-related cognitive impairment experienced difficulties in parenting.21 At home, insufficient attention and memory capacity increases the likelihood of forgetting scheduled household arrangements and lowers the efficiency of daily family task completion.21 For AYA survivors transitioning into important roles in family (e.g., primary income earner), difficulties in higher-order thinking skills (i.e., emotional regulation and organization) may be associated with reduced frequency and quality of normal interactions or participation in activities with peers and family members. In Asian cultures that prioritize collectivism, especially traditional Chinese contexts, family cohesion is accorded significant importance.14 Decreased role functioning within family (e.g., childcare, filial obligations to aging parents) is often coupled with feelings of guilt and elevated psychological distress among survivors.52 Collectively, the evidence suggests that survivors demonstrating such cognitive profiles may benefit from restorative interventions to improve problem-solving-based cognitive rehabilitation, as well as family interventions and support groups to facilitate social reintegration.56 Additionally, early interventions should be emphasized before cognitive difficulties become widespread and substantial enough to interfere with AYA survivors’ daily functioning.

Regarding role functioning limitations, about 30% of our AYA cohort reported disruption or did not engage in any full-time work or school commitments, similar to the rate in other Western countries.57,58 In the CCSS-NCQ, the domains of emotional regulation and organization are primarily measures of executive function, while task efficiency and memory address attention, processing speed, and both working and long-term memory.34 Deficits in working and long-term memory are reported to be associated with challenges in knowledge learning, information retention, and task completion, further characterizing the difficulties observed in individuals’ work/school performance.22,59 Survivors with more severe cognitive complaints were likely to not engage in full-time school or work. This finding is consistent with the connotation of the term “biographical disruption,” a ripple effect across most areas of life after cancer diagnosis, including relationships with friends and family and educational and vocational trajectories.60,61 Compared with Western countries, Chinese culture often prioritizes academic excellence and career ambitions as a means to fulfill traditional Chinese values.14 These work/school-related stressors might be prevalent among local AYA survivors. To support reintegration into work/school, employers may offer accommodation policies with adequately paid sick leave and flexible hours for AYA survivors who are transitioning back to work. Schools may also provide school-based support, such as tutorial classes, to assist adolescents in maintaining academic progress.

Implications for nursing practice and research

This study investigated the prevalence and phenotypes of cognitive complaints among Chinese AYA cancer survivors and examined their associations with role functioning limitations. The findings reveal that approximately a quarter of AYA cancer survivors reported clinically significant cognitive complaints, which were positively associated with role functioning limitations in everyday life, particularly in family and work/school settings. The results suggest the potential clinical utility of phenotyping cognitive complaints to characterize the patterns of role functioning limitations in this population. Integrating cognitive assessments and tailored support into routine AYA survivorship care holds promise for supporting work/school reintegration and family functioning.

Limitations

Our study investigated the prevalence of cognitive complaints in Chinese AYA cancer survivors, providing insights into the association between cognitive complaints and role functioning limitations. However, this study has several limitations. First, it used a cross-sectional design and was conducted at only two hospitals in Hong Kong; caution should be exercised when generalizing our results. However, the treatment strategies adopted by hospitals in Hong Kong are based on international guidelines and are no different from treatment protocols captured in epidemiological studies of other developed countries. Second, only limited perspectives on role functioning were examined using certain tools. Although these tools have been validated and commonly used in cancer studies, they might not comprehensively capture the multifaceted aspects of functional outcomes. In addition, potential selection bias may have been introduced by non-reporters in the LFQ, as survivors who did not engage in specific activities were not required to respond to follow-up questions, resulting in missing responses and varying denominators across domains. Importantly, individuals experiencing severe role functioning limitations may be fully disengaged from these activities, potentially leading to an underestimation of role functioning limitations. Third, the community control group in this study was not population-based and exhibited a higher educational level than the AYA survivors; caution is warranted when interpreting the age- and sex-adjusted normative comparisons. However, literature has reported that individuals with higher educational attainment are generally more willing to participate voluntarily in research than individuals with lower education levels.62 Despite these limitations, this work serves as both a preliminary study and a model with which to facilitate larger-scale research in the Chinese AYA population. Future studies should conduct neuropsychological tests to assess the level of objective cognitive impairment in AYA cancer survivors and examine its association with role functioning limitations.

Conclusions

AYA cancer survivors in Hong Kong reported a higher prevalence of cognitive complaints and role functioning limitations compared with age- and sex-adjusted normative references derived from community controls. Cognitive complaints were associated with poorer performance of home duties and work/school duties. This study provides directions for the validation of cognitive outcomes with objective assessments and the development of multidisciplinary services and interventions for this population. The routine screening of psychosocial problems and functional limitations may facilitate the implementation of remedial measures, such as behavioral interventions, timely referral to special training or educational services, and counseling support at school or workplace. For young adults with high symptom burden, social workers may focus on providing support to address their occupational and employment challenges. Interventions and peer support groups that promote self-confidence and positive peer interactions can be implemented in the early survivorship phase when young adults transition back to their full-time work and family life.
