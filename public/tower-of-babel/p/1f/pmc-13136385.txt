Introduction

Micron-sized metal particles, such as those made of silver, copper, and aluminum, are used in pastes and adhesives for electrodes in small electrical parts1–5. Among the different shapes of metal particles, such as spheres, flakes, cubes, and dendrites, metal flakes are ideal for high conductivity and heat dissipation due to their large surface area and flat shape5–9. Silver flakes are commonly used as conductive fillers in solar cell electrodes and die-attach for chip-mounting due to their high chemical stability and conductivity6–9. Several studies have shown that the electrical and mechanical characteristics are enhanced by using silver flakes8–10. Therefore, the demand for silver flakes has increased, making it essential to mass-produce them.

Various methods have been developed to produce silver flakes11–16. Broadly categorized, these include build-up methods11–13, such as chemical reduction and thermal decomposition of Ag+ ions, and ball-milling methods14–16 of flattening silver particles. As ball mills, such as tumbling and vibration mills, are frequently used for grinding in mass-production17,18, ball milling is also considered a suitable method for mass producing silver flakes. The large-scale ball mills are utilized for the mass-production because they have a high capacity. However, trial products of silver flakes are made by using the small-scale ball mills to minimize the loss of expensive silver particles. Therefore, scaling up of the flattening process with minimal tests is necessary to ensure that the industrial production of silver flakes while maintaining the same quality as the trial products.

Limited studies have reported on scaling up of the ball mills for flattening. The complexity of the parameters controlling these mills presents a challenging process. These parameters include the number of balls and input particles, ball size, rotation or vibration speed, and mill size. Several experimental studies on these parameters for flattening metal particles have been reported14,19–23. Despite the numerous experiments conducted, the scale-up factor remains ambiguous.

The Discrete Element Method (DEM), proposed by Cundall and Strack24, provides substantial information, such as the motion of balls during milling25, the motion of balls and particles from a micro viewpoint26, breakage and deformation behavior27–31, the velocity of balls, and the specific impact energy32. Kano et al.32–34 discovered the correlation between grinding rate constant, calculated from reduction in particle size, and the specific impact energy, calculated from DEM simulations32–34. Additionally, Mio et al. proposed a scale-up method for planetary ball mills by confirming the correlation between the grinding rate constant and the specific impact energy at different scales35. Therefore, specific impact energy is a key factor in predicting grinding phenomena31, predicting mechanochemical reactions36, designing ball mills37, 38, and scaling them up35. The specific impact energy is a potential scale-up factor for flattening processes because flattening occurs when a particle is sandwiched between two balls or a ball and the mill wall.

Hence, the objective of this paper is to confirm the applicability of the specific impact energy calculated from the DEM simulation to the scale-up of flattening silver particles. This scale-up predicted the increase in the normalized specific surface area (SSA) at large scales by using the flattening rate constant obtained from flattening experiments at small scales and the specific impact energy calculated from the DEM simulation because the SSA increases as the metal particles become more flattened19. The scale-up method is organized as follows:

The flattening rate constant is obtained from flattening experiments using the small-scale vibration mill under various conditions. This constant is calculated from the increment of the normalized SSA over the flattening time.The specific impact energy at small scales is obtained through simulation. The motion of the balls is modeled by the DEM simulation under the same conditions and scales as the experiments. The specific impact energy is then calculated from ball-ball and ball-wall collisions.The prediction coefficient is calculated using the correlation between the flattening rate constant and the specific impact energy at small scales.The simulations calculate the specific impact energy at large scales under several conditions.The normalized SSA for the large-scale vibration mill is obtained from the prediction coefficient, the specific impact energy, and arbitrary flattening times.

To confirm the scale-up validity, flattening experiments were performed using the large-scale vibration mill, and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) was calculated to compare the experimental and predicted values of the normalized SSA. Furthermore, this paper presents a comparative analysis of prediction accuracy using various components of the specific impact energy—including normal, shear, and total—to investigate whether one or both components can serve as the scale-up factor.

Methods

Experiments on silver particle flattening

To calculate the flattening rate constant and confirm the accuracy of the scale-up, flattening experiments were performed under various vibration speeds and at different scales. To calculate the flattening rate constant, the increment of the normalized SSA from the initial stage to certain flattening times was obtained experimentally. Additionally, to confirm that the increase in normalized SSA was caused by flattening, the particle shape was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM).

Equipment

Figure 1 shows a schematic diagram of vibration mills. Table 1 shows the geometry and material composition of the vibration mills used. B-1 made by Chuo-Kakohki-Shoji Ltd. was used as a small-scale vibration mill. FVR-20 made by Chuo-Kakohki Ltd. was used as a large-scale vibration mill. The small- and large-scale mills had volumes of 3.2 L and 70 L, respectively. Stainless-steel balls with a diameter of 1.6 mm and a density of 7.81 g cm−3 were used as flattening media in all experiments. The large-scale vibration mill had a separation screen, to separate balls and silver particles, and a separated space as shown in Fig. 1b. The silver particles were not flattened in the separated space because balls did not exist in the space.

Parameters and conditions in DEM simulation

DEM simulations were performed using in-house source code. The simulations are necessary to model the motion of the balls in the presence of the silver particles because the balls flow with the particles during the flattening process. Hence, the simulation parameters were determined based on the methods previously reported that simulated the motion of the balls in the presence of the particles25.

Table 3 shows the simulation parameters used in this paper. As written in the Eqs. (1–10), the behavior of the balls is affected by DEM parameters such as Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, the frictional coefficient, and the rolling frictional coefficient in the DEM simulation. Young’s modulus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E$$\end{document} is commonly set to a lower value than the actual value to reduce the calculation time40. According to the previous reports, a three-digit decrease in Young’s modulus had little contribution to the motion, velocity distribution, and energy consumption of the particles and balls41–43. Thus, Young’s modulus was set to a value three orders of magnitude lower than the actual value. Regarding the frictional coefficient μ, Kano et al.25 reported that the frictional coefficient of the balls correlated with the angle of repose of the input particles for grinding. Figure S1 shows the correlation between the frictional coefficient and the angle of repose25. The angle of repose of silver particles was measured using the fixed funnel and free-standing cone methods, yielding a value of 50 degrees. Based on the correlation shown in Fig. S1 and this value, the frictional coefficient μ was estimated at 0.8. The balls used in the experiments are assumed to be spherical, and the rolling frictional coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mu}_{r}$$\end{document} is set to 1.885, which was used to represent spherical objects in the DEM simulation detailed in the previous report44.

To reduce the calculation load at large scales, the length of the pot is set to 10 DB, equivalent to 0.035 times the length used in the experiments shown in Table 1. This adjustment is based on the previous reports on this approach45,46, which indicated that the pot length was set to 4 to 16 times the ball diameter. Since reducing the pot length increases the ratio of collisions between the balls and the front and back walls, these collisions are necessary to be excluded from the calculation of the specific impact energy. The ratio of collisions between the balls and the front and back walls in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{w\_total}$$\end{document} is small at small scales (see Table S1) and considered even smaller at large scales. However, the specific impact energy may be inaccurate when using this approach alone because these collisions may also affect the motion of the balls. It is considered that the specific impact energy can be accurately calculated if the motion of the balls in the simulation can represent that in the presence of the silver particles observed experimentally. Therefore, at large scales, the motion was fitted between the experiment and simulation. The angle of slope after vibration stopped was evaluated as the index of the motion. As shown in Fig. 4b and c, the angle was measured at 1s after vibration stopped in the simulation. The flattening rate constant from the small-scale flattening experiments

Firstly, the changes of the particle shape during flattening experiments using the small-scale vibration mill are shown. SEM images show the particles under two vibration speeds during the flattening experiments. The number of flat particles and their size increase with flattening time. The higher vibration speed of 1200 min− 1 appears to produce more flat particles and reduce their thickness. Calculating the prediction coefficient

According to the previous report35, it was indicated that the specific impact energy could be useful as the scale-up factor for the grinding process by confirming that the correlation between the grinding rate constant and the specific impact energy at different scales is expressed by a straight line. If the flattening process also shows the comparable correlation between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{w}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{f}$$\end{document}, the slope of the correlation can be utilized to predict \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{{t}_{f}}/{S}_{0}$$\end{document} at large scales. The findings are consistent with previous experimental studies in that normal collisions affect flattening behavior. Fujita and Motozuka et al. reported that the plastic deformation of iron particles during ball milling was induced by uniaxial compression, as revealed by analyzing changes in the crystallographic texture of the particles49,50. Hence, it is also considered that the findings can relate the mechanisms of the flattening metal particles with the motion of the balls and the collisional properties in the ball mills.

This scale-up approach might have a wider range of applicability than the existing scale-up approaches, such as experimentally power-based scaling51. The collisional properties could be changed in scales, conditions, and mill types36, and it is challenging to analyze the collisional properties such as the collisional direction from only power consumption. In contrast, this approach might be also applied to the scale-up using other types of mills and design mills because it can analyze the collisional properties. Further investigation is necessary to validate the applicability of this approach to the scale-up using other mill types.

Conclusion

To confirm the applicability of the specific impact energy calculated from the DEM simulation to the scale-up of flattening silver particles, the normalized SSA during flattening at large scales was predicted from the specific impact energy and the prediction coefficient. The prediction coefficient was obtained from the correlation between the flattening rate constant and the specific impact energy. These were derived from experiments and simulations at small scales. To confirm the validity of the scale-up, MAPE values were calculated from the predicted and experimental normalized SSA at large scales. Furthermore, prediction accuracies using different components of the specific impact energy (normal, shear, and total) were compared to investigate whether one or both components could serve as the scale-up factor.

In the experiments, silver particles were subjected to flattening using dry vibration mills at different vibration speeds, and the SSA of the silver particles was measured. The flattening rate constant was calculated from the increment of the normalized SSA and the flattening time in the small-scale experiment. In the simulations, the motions of the balls within the vibration mill were modeled by DEM simulations under the same conditions as the experiments. The specific impact energy was calculated from the ball-ball and ball-wall collisions.

At small scales, the flattening rate constant and the specific impact energy increased with vibration speed. Strong correlations were found between them on linear coordinates, so the prediction coefficient could be determined. Although the specific impact energy increased with vibration speed at large scales, the proportion of the normal component was higher at small scales. Validation showed the lowest MAPE when using the normal component of specific impact energy for predictions.

Therefore, it can be concluded that the normal specific impact energy calculated by the DEM simulation can be utilized to the scale-up of the flattening process. This finding could be valuable for designing equipment to flatten metal particles.
