Regional Variations in Surgical Approach and Perioperative Outcomes in Adult Cervical Instrumentation Surgery: A Nationwide Registry Study from the JSIS-DB
Authors: Shunsuke Katsumi, Hideyuki Arima, Koji Yamada, Mitsuru Yagi, Ryo Mizukoshi, Kimiaki Yokosuka, Junya Katayanagi, Atsushi Tagami, Yusuke Hori, Yukihiro Matsuyama, Hiroshi Taneichi, Tokumi Kanemura, Akira Matsumura, Haruki Ueda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction Cervical spinal surgery is performed for various pathologies, including degenerative disease, deformity, trauma, tumor, and infection. However, the surgical approach (anterior vs. posterior) and perioperative safety may vary among regions due to differences in patient characteristics, surgeon experience, and healthcare infrastructure. This study aimed to clarify regional variations in approach selection and perioperative outcomes for cervical surgery in Japan using the nationwide registry of the Japanese Spinal Instrumentation Society Database (JSIS-DB). Methods This retrospective observational study included adult patients (≥18 years) registered in the JSIS-DB between February 2018 and March 2022 who underwent cervical anterior or posterior fusion surgery. Occipito–cervical and cervico–thoracic fusion procedures were excluded. Patients were classified into 6 geographic regions. Regional differences in patient demographics, surgical characteristics, perioperative outcomes, surgeon experience, and reoperation rates were analyzed using appropriate statistical methods. Results A total of 2,581 patients were analyzed. Significant regional variation was observed in patient characteristics, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification, surgical approach, operative time, estimated blood loss, surgical urgency, length of hospital stay, complication rates, and surgeon and assistant experience levels (all p<0.05). Anterior procedures were more frequently performed in the Kanto and Kyushu regions, whereas posterior approaches predominated in other regions. Importantly, no significant regional difference was observed in reoperation rates (p=0.254). Reoperations were infrequent nationwide (5.9% overall) and were primarily related to mechanical or decompressive issues rather than major complications. Conclusions This nationwide registry study demonstrated substantial regional variation in surgical strategies and perioperative complication rates in adult cervical surgery in Japan. Despite these differences, reoperation rates remained consistently low and comparable across regions. These findings indicate that while reoperation rates are uniform, perioperative complication profiles vary geographically. This study provides a comprehensive overview of contemporary cervical fusion practice and highlights the need for further research into the factors driving regional variations. Keywords: perioperative complication, registry study, nationwide survey, spinal instrumentation, cervical spine surgery

Introduction

Cervical spine surgery is widely performed to treat a broad range of spinal disorders, including degenerative disease, deformity, trauma, tumor, and infection. The selection of the surgical approach—anterior or posterior—is influenced by multiple factors, such as the underlying pathology, sagittal alignment, surgeon experience, and the availability of surgical technology1). However, the distribution of surgical approaches and their perioperative safety profiles may vary across regions owing to differences in patient demographics, clinical practice patterns, surgical expertise, and institutional resources2-4).

To address the need for comprehensive, real-world evaluation of spine surgery, the Japanese Spinal Instrumentation Society established a nationwide, web-based, multicenter registry in 2018, known as the Japanese Spinal Instrumentation Society Database (JSIS-DB). This database systematically collects standardized perioperative data, including detailed patient characteristics, surgical techniques, and short-term outcomes, thereby enabling large-scale epidemiologic analyses of spinal instrumentation surgery in Japan5). Although previous studies using the JSIS-DB and other nationwide databases have provided important insights into overall surgical trends and disease-specific outcomes, a comprehensive nationwide evaluation focusing on regional variation in surgical approach selection and perioperative safety has not yet been sufficiently addressed6-9).

Understanding such regional variation is essential for identifying potential disparities in clinical practice, optimizing the allocation of surgical resources, and ultimately improving the quality and safety of spine care. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective cohort study using nationwide data from the JSIS-DB. The primary objective of this study was to examine regional differences in the selection of anterior versus posterior surgical approaches and associated perioperative safety. Secondary objectives included regional comparisons of patient characteristics and surgical variables.

Materials and Methods

Study design and ethical considerations

This retrospective observational study included adult patients (≥18 years) registered in the JSIS-DB between February 2018 and March 2022. We extracted all cases undergoing cervical anterior or posterior fusion that met the standard JSIS-DB criteria for spine instrumentation surgery. We excluded cases involving occipito–cervical and cervico–thoracic fusion, tissue biopsy, stand-alone vertebral augmentation (e.g., vertebroplasty/balloon kyphoplasty), and laminoplasty without fusion.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent for participation in the registry was obtained from all patients or their legal representatives; for cases in which written consent could not be obtained, an opt-out process approved by the ethics committee was implemented. The use of JSIS-DB data for secondary research was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the JSIS (#14R).

To ensure data reliability, the JSIS-DB data cleansing team (comprising multiple certified spine surgeons and administrative staff) reviewed the data stored in the JSIS-DB. After checking the cases with questions, the JSIS-DB secretariat provided feedback to each facility and requested that registrants correct any discrepancies.

Data collection and surgical information

Data collected from the JSIS-DB included patient demographics and perioperative clinical variables. Patient-related variables comprised age, sex, height, weight, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, and the presence of Parkinson's disease (PD). We included PD as a co-variable because its associated postural instability and mechanical challenges significantly influence surgical planning and outcomes in cervical spine surgery. Surgical variables included the surgical approach (anterior or posterior), estimated intraoperative blood loss, operative time, surgical urgency (elective or emergency), and surgery type (primary surgery or revision surgery). Anterior procedures included both fusion surgery and artificial disc replacement, as categorized in the JSIS-DB.

Postoperative variables included length of hospital stay, intraoperative complications, and short-term postoperative complications (defined as those occurring within 14 days of the initial surgery). The need for reoperation during the study period was also recorded. For patients who underwent reoperation, the primary reason for reoperation was recorded. Surgeon-related factors were collected, including the postgraduate years of experience of the primary surgeon and first assistant, categorized into 5 groups (Categories A-E) according to years of experience (<5, 5-9, 10-14, ≥15 years, and special technical expertise).

Regional classification

Institutions were classified into 6 geographic regions according to their prefectural location: Hokkaido/Tohoku, Kanto, Chubu/Tokai, Kinki, Chugoku/Shikoku, and Kyushu. Each region comprised the following prefectures (Figure 1): Hokkaido/Tohoku (Hokkaido, Aomori, Iwate, Miyagi, Akita, Fukushima); Kanto (Tokyo, Kanagawa, Saitama, Chiba, Ibaraki, Tochigi, Gunma); Chubu/Tokai (Aichi, Gifu, Mie, Shizuoka, Niigata, Toyama, Ishikawa, Nagano, Yamanashi); Kinki (Osaka, Hyogo, Kyoto, Shiga, Nara, Wakayama); Chugoku/Shikoku (Tottori, Okayama, Hiroshima, Yamaguchi, Tokushima, Kagawa, Ehime, Kochi); and Kyushu (Fukuoka, Saga, Nagasaki, Oita, Miyazaki, Kagoshima).

Japan was divided into six geographic regions based on prefectural location: Hokkaido/Tohoku, Kanto, Chubu/Tokai, Kinki, Chugoku/Shikoku, and Kyushu.

For each region, the distribution of surgical approaches, surgical volume, and perioperative outcomes was evaluated. This regional classification was adopted to ensure sufficient case volume for meaningful statistical comparison. Yamagata, Fukui, Shimane, Kumamoto, and Okinawa prefectures were excluded because no institutions from these areas participated in the registry during the study period.

Statistical analyses

Categorical data were presented as numbers and percentages. Continuous data were presented as mean±standard deviation if normally distributed and as median and interquartile range if not normally distributed. For tests of 3 or more groups, the chi-square test was used for categorical data, one-way analysis of variance was used for normally distributed continuous data, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for non-normally distributed continuous data. Between-group comparisons for categorical data were made using the chi-square test, for normally distributed continuous data using the unpaired t test, and for non-normally distributed continuous data using the Mann–Whitney U test. Bonferroni correction was performed as necessary to avoid multiplicity of tests. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 29.0.2.0 for Windows (IBM Japan, Tokyo, Japan). A p-value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Age at the time of surgery differed significantly among regions (p<0.001). Patients in the Kyushu region tended to be younger, whereas those in the Chubu/Tokai and Kinki regions were slightly older. Sex distribution also varied significantly by region (p=0.002), with a higher proportion of male patients in the Kanto region compared with other regions.

Anthropometric measures showed significant regional variation. Height and body weight differed significantly among regions (both p<0.001), resulting in a modest but statistically significant difference in body mass index (BMI) (p=0.013). Patients in the Kanto region had the highest mean BMI, whereas those in the Kinki and Chugoku/Shikoku regions tended to have lower BMI values.

Baseline physical status, assessed using the ASA physical status classification, demonstrated significant interregional differences (p<0.001). Higher ASA classes were more frequently observed in the Kyushu and Chugoku/Shikoku regions, indicating a greater burden of systemic comorbidities in these populations.

The prevalence of PD was low overall; however, a significant regional difference was observed (p=0.019). The Chugoku/Shikoku and Kyushu regions showed a relatively higher proportion of patients with PD compared with other regions.

Regional differences in surgical characteristics

Regional differences in surgical characteristics are summarized in Table 2. Significant variation was observed across regions in surgical approach, perioperative management, and length of hospital stay. The choice of surgical procedure differed significantly among regions (p<0.001). The proportion of anterior procedures, including fusion surgery and artificial disc replacement, was higher in the Kanto and Kyushu regions, with anterior approaches predominating in Kyushu. In contrast, posterior procedures were more frequently performed in the Hokkaido/Tohoku, Chubu/Tokai, Kinki, and Chugoku/Shikoku regions. Combined anterior–posterior procedures were uncommon overall but were relatively more frequent in the Kinki and Chugoku/Shikoku regions.

Operative time and estimated intraoperative blood loss both showed significant regional variation (both p<0.001). Operative time tended to be longer in the Kanto and Kinki regions, whereas shorter operative times were observed in the Hokkaido/Tohoku and Kyushu regions. Estimated blood loss was lower in the Kanto and Kyushu regions and relatively higher in the Chubu/Tokai and Chugoku/Shikoku regions. The urgency of surgery also differed significantly among regions (p<0.001). Length of hospital stay varied significantly across regions (p<0.001). Patients in the Hokkaido/Tohoku and Kyushu regions tended to have longer hospital stays, whereas shorter hospitalization was observed in the Kanto and Chubu/Tokai regions.

Perioperative outcomes and surgeon-related factors

Regional differences in perioperative outcomes and surgeon-related factors are summarized in Table 3. The incidence of intraoperative and early postoperative complications differed significantly among regions (p<0.001). The complication rate was highest in the Kanto region, whereas lower rates were observed in the Hokkaido/Tohoku and Kyushu regions. The Chubu/Tokai, Kinki, and Chugoku/Shikoku regions demonstrated intermediate complication rates. Significant regional variation was also observed in surgeon experience categories (p<0.001). Across all regions, the majority of procedures were performed by surgeons with longer postgraduate experience; however, this trend was particularly pronounced in the Kinki and Chugoku/Shikoku regions. The distribution of experience levels among first assistants differed significantly across regions (p<0.001). The Kanto and Chubu/Tokai regions tended to involve assistants with fewer years of postgraduate experience, whereas surgeries in the Kinki and Chugoku/Shikoku regions more frequently involved assistants with greater experience. No significant regional difference was observed in the rate of reoperation (p=0.254). Reoperation rates were low across all regions, indicating comparable short-term reoperation risks nationwide.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide registry-based study to comprehensively evaluate regional variation in surgical approach selection and perioperative safety in adult cervical surgery in Japan. Using data from the JSIS-DB, we identified substantial interregional differences in patient characteristics, surgical strategies, perioperative management, and short-term outcomes, highlighting meaningful variation in clinical practice patterns and healthcare delivery despite standardized surgical indications within a national registry.

Using the same database, previous nationwide studies have clarified the overall epidemiology, temporal trends, and perioperative safety profile of spinal instrumentation surgery in Japan10,11). However, those investigations primarily focused on nationwide trends or disease-specific outcomes and did not specifically address regional differences in surgical approach selection or perioperative safety. The present study extends these prior findings by providing a focused regional analysis of adult cervical surgery, thereby offering new insights into geographic variation in contemporary clinical practice.

Baseline patient characteristics differed significantly across regions. Patients in the Kyushu region tended to be younger, whereas those in the Chubu/Tokai and Kinki regions were slightly older at the time of surgery. Differences in sex distribution and anthropometric measures were also observed, with a higher proportion of male patients and higher BMI in the Kanto region. These variations likely reflect regional demographic differences and population structures, which may influence disease presentation and surgical decision-making12).

Notably, the distribution of ASA physical status classes varied significantly among regions. Higher ASA classes were more frequently observed in the Kyushu and Chugoku/Shikoku regions, suggesting a greater burden of systemic comorbidities in these populations. This finding is clinically relevant, as baseline health status is known to influence perioperative risk and postoperative recovery. Although the overall PD prevalence was low, its uneven regional distribution further underscores differences in patient complexity across regions13).

Marked regional differences were observed in surgical approach selection. Anterior procedures, including artificial disc replacement, were more commonly performed in the Kanto and Kyushu regions, whereas posterior approaches predominated in the Hokkaido/Tohoku, Chubu/Tokai, Kinki, and Chugoku/Shikoku regions. These differences may reflect variations in surgeon training, institutional preferences, availability of technology, and regional referral patterns14). Notably, the prevalence of anterior procedures in the Kanto region (61.1%) could be attributed to its high concentration of academic tertiary referral centers. These high-volume institutions often serve as hubs for specialized techniques, such as anterior cervical discectomy and fusion and cervical arthroplasty, and may attract complex cases that warrant an anterior approach. In contrast, the lower prevalence of anterior procedures in regions like Hokkaido/Tohoku (16.0%) might reflect a different distribution of case complexity or a stronger reliance on established posterior techniques in community-based settings. Importantly, the relatively higher use of anterior approaches in certain regions did not translate into higher reoperation rates, suggesting that multiple surgical strategies can achieve acceptable short-term outcomes when appropriately selected.

Operative time and estimated blood loss also differed significantly among regions. Longer operative times were observed in the Kanto and Kinki regions, whereas shorter procedures were more common in the Hokkaido/Tohoku and Kyushu regions. Differences in case complexity, surgical approach, and institutional workflows may partially explain these findings. Similarly, variation in emergency versus elective surgery rates suggests regional differences in disease presentation or access to timely surgical care.

Length of hospital stay showed significant interregional variation, with longer hospitalization in the Hokkaido/Tohoku and Kyushu regions. This may be influenced by differences in perioperative care pathways, rehabilitation availability, and regional healthcare infrastructure, rather than surgical factors alone.

The incidence of intraoperative and early postoperative complications varied significantly among regions. Notably, the Kanto region demonstrated a higher complication rate (14.2%) compared with the Kyushu region (5.4%), despite Kyushu having a significantly higher proportion of anterior procedures. This discrepancy suggests that the variation is not solely attributable to the surgical approach. One potential explanation is the difference in institutional characteristics between urban and rural areas. The Kanto region, as a major metropolitan area, is characterized by a high density of academic tertiary centers that predominantly manage more complex, high-risk cases referred from other regions. These institutional differences in case mix and patient complexity, combined with potential variations in complication reporting thresholds in large-scale academic settings, may contribute to the observed regional disparity. Another potential factor is the regional distribution of surgeon experience. Our data showed that regions with more favorable safety profiles, such as Kinki and Chugoku/Shikoku, also had a higher proportion of experienced surgeons (Category D/E). This suggests that the geographical imbalance in expertise may be an important driver of regional disparities in outcomes. Importantly, despite these differences, the overall complication rate was comparable to those reported in previous large-scale nationwide studies14).

Analysis of surgeon-related factors revealed significant regional variation in surgeon and assistant experience levels. Regions such as Kinki and Chugoku/Shikoku more frequently involved highly experienced surgeons and assistants, which may have contributed to stable perioperative outcomes despite differences in patient complexity. Conversely, regions with a higher proportion of less-experienced first assistants still achieved acceptable short-term outcomes, likely reflecting effective supervision and structured training systems.

Most importantly, no significant regional difference was observed in reoperation rates. Although the follow-up period was short, reoperation rates reported in this database were infrequent nationwide and were predominantly attributable to mechanical or decompressive issues rather than major complications. These findings indicate that, despite substantial regional variation in surgical approach, perioperative management, and perioperative complication rates, the short-term reoperation risk remained consistently low and comparable across all regions.

These findings have important clinical and health policy implications. Regional variation in surgical practice does not necessarily indicate suboptimal care; rather, it may reflect differences in patient characteristics, surgeon experience, and institutional resources across regions. Clarifying these differences may help facilitate objective comparison of clinical practice patterns and inform future efforts to improve the quality and consistency of care.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design limits causal inference, and the JSIS-DB does not capture detailed radiographic parameters, surgeon-specific case volume, or long-term functional outcomes. Furthermore, complications were limited to intraoperative and early postoperative events. Therefore, postoperative infections and other intermediate- to long-term complications could not be adequately assessed and may have been underestimated. Additionally, we did not perform an adjusted analysis to correlate surgeon category with complication rates. Further research is needed to clarify the independent impact of surgeon experience on these regional variations. Finally, the regional grouping strategy employed to ensure sufficient case volume may have obscured more granular differences at the institutional level. Furthermore, several prefectures were excluded because no institutions in these areas participated in the registry. This exclusion may introduce selection bias, particularly if surgical resources or patient demographics in these regions differ from those in the included areas.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this nationwide analysis demonstrated significant regional variation in patient characteristics, surgical strategies, perioperative management, and perioperative complication rates in adult cervical surgery in Japan. Despite these variations, reoperation rates were consistently low and comparable across regions, indicating that short-term reoperation risk is uniform nationwide even though complication profiles differ. These findings provide a comprehensive overview of contemporary cervical fusion practice and underscore the importance of further investigating the factors contributing to regional differences in complication rates to optimize surgical care.
