Effects of fever temperatures on metabolic changes and gene expression in activated monocytic cells from mouse, chicken and rainbow trout sources
Authors: Intisar Hassan, Kenneth H Taylor, Piyasi Ghosh, Sofia Shulga, Satyajit Rath, Jeannine M Durdik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Fever enhances nitric oxide production in murine, avian and piscine macrophages.•Fever modulates cell metabolism differently in murine, avian and piscine macrophages.•Fever accelerates metabolic changes in activated murine macrophages.•Brief fever exposure radically alters murine macrophage transcriptome.Keywords: Macrophage activation, Fever temperature, Cellular metabolism, Murine, Avian, Piscine

Introduction

Inflammatory responses to infections are associated with and lead to enhanced body temperature, or fever. Microbial ligands for pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) of the innate immune system activate systemic mediators that in turn trigger fever responses. Inflammatory cytokines also induce positive feedback pathways to amplify these responses. In homeothermic organisms, these mediators collectively signal to the hypothalamus to reset metabolism in muscle and adipose tissues to enhance heat generation ([1,12,26]; reviewed in [18]). In poikilothermic organisms, there is a less well understood pathway that leads to heat-seeking behavioral changes ([5,57]; reviewed in [55]). In both situations, the resultant increase in body temperature is positively correlated with survival in infection morbidity.

However, the detailed mechanisms mediating this fever-mediated protection remain unclear. There is evidence that viral replication is reduced at fever temperatures, but the mechanistic pathways remain undefined as yet [65]. We and others have shown that activation and differentiation of adaptive immune cells such as T cells is modified by fever temperatures [19,36,39,68,72] but these alterations have not yet been mechanistically connected to fever-mediated increases in protection. Activation-induced products of cells of the innate immune system such as macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells and NK cells, including cytokines and free radicals, are effector mechanisms against infection. There is evidence that these effector innate immune functions are enhanced at fever temperatures [3,11,33,[49], [50], [51], [52],58,73].

Induction of gene expression upon macrophage activation is energy-dependent. Temperature changes are associated with alterations in energy metabolism ([25,42,53,60]; reviewed in [30]), and macrophages activated by microbial and inflammatory stimuli show changes in cellular energy metabolism ([31,47,62,63,71]; reviewed in [17]). However, the relationship, if any, between modulation of effector functions and of cellular energy metabolism by fever temperatures in activated macrophages has not been extensively examined, nor have the possible species differences between such modulations been explored to any great extent [38,70].

On this background, we have undertaken an analysis of fever temperature-mediated modulation of effector gene expression and cellular energy metabolism in activated macrophages from homeothermic and poikilothermic species. Our findings suggest that, while effector functions in activated macrophages were commonly enhanced by fever in macrophages of mouse, chicken and rainbow trout origin, cellular metabolic changes showed much more variability. These data suggest significant evolutionary complexities in the effects of fever sensing on effector versus metabolic pathways in macrophages.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

The murine macrophage cell line, RAW264.7 (TIB-71; American Type Culture Collection (ATCC)) was grown in RPMI 1640 medium (Corning, USA) completed with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS; Neuromics, USA) and l-glutamine (2 mM), penicillin (100 units/ml) and streptomycin (100 µg/ml) (Fisher scientific, USA) in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

The chicken monocytic cell line HTC [56] was grown in complete RPMI medium as defined above in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

The rainbow trout monocytic cell line RTS11 ([21]; reviewed in [4]) was grown in Leibovitz’s l-15 medium (Hyclone) with 15% FCS and penicillin and streptomycin as above at 16 °C in a cold cabinet.

Animals

Mice: C57BL/6 mice, male and female, 2 to 6 months old, were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME).

Chickens: Male broiler chickens, 3–10 weeks old, were obtained from the University of Arkansas Agricultural Farm.

Fish: Rainbow trout of both sexes, 8–16 inches long, were obtained from the Norfolk National fish hatchery (Mountain Home, AR).

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with institutional animal care recommendations and with approvals from the University of Arkansas Fayetteville IACUC [AUP numbers: 20,130, 2403,002,297, 20,061,17,029, 2512,009,186].

Primary cell cultures

Mouse cells: Bone marrow-derived macrophages were obtained and cultured as described [22,66]. Briefly, femoral bone marrow cells were cultured for six days in complete RPMI1640 medium as used above for RAW264.7 cells with macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF; 20 ng/ml; Enzo, Fujifilm) with growth factor renewal at three days. Cells were harvested and used for assays.

Chicken cells: Splenic macrophages were isolated as described [64]. Briefly, spleens were minced, single-cell suspensions were prepared, leukocytes isolated using centrifugation over Ficoll-Hypaque, and incubated in complete medium as above for 18 h, non-adherent cells removed and adherent cells were used for macrophage analyses.

Fish cells: Monocytic cells from rainbow trout head kidneys were isolated as described [2]. Briefly, head kidneys were minced, single-cell suspensions were prepared, leukocytes isolated using centrifugation over Ficoll-Hypaque, and incubated in Leibovitz’s l-15 medium (Hyclone) with 15% FCS and penicillin and streptomycin as above for 18 h, non-adherent cells removed and adherent cells were used for macrophage analyses.

Cell stimulation

Stimulants: Bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS [Salmonella enterica serotype Minnesota]; Sigma), was used as a microbial ligand. Mouse interferon-gamma (IFNg; Sigma, Peprotech) was used as a cytokine ligand to activate mouse cells, while chicken IFNg (Kingfisher) was used for chicken cells. Capsaicin (Sigma) was used as an agonist for the transient receptor potential vanilloid type 1 (TRPV1) where indicated. All stimuli were titrated for use.

Incubation temperatures

In all assays, cells were used at either normal or moderate fever temperatures appropriate for the species.

For mouse cells, normal temperature was 37 °C and fever temperature was 39 C. This is a moderate fever temperature, with physiological consequences in most mammalian species including humans and mice [68].

For chicken cells, normal temperature was 40–41 °C, and fever temperatures were 41.5–42 C. This fever temperature falls in the range of fever shown during natural bacterial infections in chickens [43,69], and is known to have functional consequences in such infections [15,67].

For rainbow trout cells, normal temperature was 16 °C, while fever temperature was 19 °C. For a poikilothermic species, a single ‘normal’ temperature is uncertain. Maintenance temperature for rainbow trout is held to be between 10–20 C with best growth at 16 C [45], and the RTS11 rainbow trout cell line grows optimally at 16 C [56]. Therefore, 16 C was treated as normal baseline temperature in these experiments. Behavioural fever has been shown over this range of temperature, and a 3 C increase has been shown to have functional consequences with modifications in inflammatory responses [23]. Therefore, 19 C was used as fever temperature for rainbow trout cells.

Detection of cellular protein synthesis

Protein synthesis in cells was quantified by using homopropargylglycine (HPG) followed by labeling with click chemistry according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermofisher). Briefly, cells were plated in 24-well plates in methionine-free medium (Thermofisher) with 10 μM HPG. After incubation for 4 h at different culture conditions and incubation temperatures, the cells were washed, fixed, washed, permeabilized and AlexaFluor488-labeled according to kit instructions. Additional stains with other fluorochromes (F4/80-Alexa647; Biolegend) were used where indicated.

Flow cytometry

Cells from various species and tissues were stained for flow cytometric analysis. For RAW264.7 cells, the mouse monocytic lineage marker F4/80-specific monoclonal antibody labelled with Alexa647 was used in staining buffer (phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)+1% fetal calf serum (FCS)+0.05%NaN3) at 4 C for 20 min followed by two washes with staining buffer. Protein synthesis was detected by the AlexaFluor488 tag as described above. Labelled, fixed and stained cells were analysed using a FACS Aria Fusion (BD Biosciences) instrument, and FlowJo software was used for analysis (BD Biosciences). Totals of 100,000 cells were routinely collected per sample.

Griess assay for nitric oxide

Inflammatory activation of macrophages induces the expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and allows the cells to make NO (reviewed in [40]. NO output was measured for cell lines and primary monocytic cells from all three species tested. For this, cells were plated into 96-well plates at a plating density of 5 × 10^5 per well in phenol red-free RPMI1640 (Thermofisher) and then treated with various stimulants at different incubation temperatures. The production of NO was determined using the Griess assay, detecting nitrite in the medium [46]. Supernatants (100 μl) of the cell cultures were incubated with the Griess reagent (50 μl; 0.1% sulfanilamide and 0.01% N-1-naphthylethylenediamine dihydrochloride in 2.5% phosphoric acid) as described [14,24,61], incubated in dark for 10 min at room temperature, and the absorbance measured at 570 nm, along with a sodium nitrite standard, at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Biotek).

Measurement of cellular glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation and ATP levels

Seahorse analysis systems (Seahorse XF Pro, XFe24, XFe96 and XF HS mini analyzers; Agilent, Santa Clara CA) were used for measurements of glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation and ATP generation, with manufacturer’s kits and protocols as described (Agilent). Briefly, cells were plated in triplicate or quadruplicate, allowed to seed overnight, and measurements done. Sensor cartridges were hydrated with XF Calibrant media overnight at 37 °C. For running the assays, medium was switched from complete RPMI to XF RPMI-based assay medium with HEPES and equilibrated in a non-CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 1 h. For ATP, three measurements were done at baseline, followed by three measurements after oligomycin (1.5 μM), and then three measurements after rotenone and antimycin A (0.5 μM each). The Seahorse analyzer was moved to a 4 °C environment chamber for running rainbow trout cells at 16 °C or 19 °C.

The kinetic assays were run using the glycolytic rate assay (induced assay) at different temperatures with a variance setting of ±0.4 °C. After initial equilibration, we ran 3 cycles of baseline (3 mix, 0 wait, and 3 measure), addition of stimulant through port A with measurements for 50 cycles (3 mix, 0 wait, and 3 measure) for mouse and chicken cell samples, and for rainbow trout cell samples, baseline (3 mix, 2 wait, and 3 measure) flowed by stimulation through port A followed by 59 cycles (3 mix, 5.30 wait, and 3 measure).

Energy metabolism profiles of cells were estimated for glycolysis as measured by the proton efflux rate (PER) and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation as measured by oxygen consumption rate (OCR). PER was calculated to provide an estimate of extracellular acidification. Data were exported and overlayed using GraphPad Prism11.

Gene expression

For mRNA detection, RNA was extracted from confluent tissue culture flasks (25cm2 [∼8 × 10^6 cells] for quantitative RT-PCR (qRTPCR) and 75cm2 [∼24×10^6 cells] for RNA-seq) with the addition of Trizol to cells according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Ambion, Life Technologies). RNA concentration of 2 μl of the 50 μl sample was determined (Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer, ThermoScientific), and a TapeStation (4150, Agilent) was used to check the RNA integrity number with all samples being greater than 8. For RNA-seq, samples sent to Novogene for sequencing. Synthesis of cDNA for qRTPCR was performed using AMV reverse transcriptase (Promega kits). QRTPCRs were run on a thermal cycler (Bio-Rad C1000) and expression of target genes were calculated by the delta/delta CT method. Relative expression values for target genes were calculated by normalization to expression of beta-actin. Triplicate cell cultures were analyzed for each condition tested.

Where primers were not available in the literature we used primer-blast (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) to build qRTPCR primers that cross exon junctions.

RNA-seq analysis

RAW 264.7 cells were grown to confluence at 37 °C in 10 cm culture plates for each of the four time points in triplicate for both baseline and fever temperature. Prior to stimulation, half of the plates were transferred to a 39 °C incubator signalling the start of fever. After 20 min, cells were collected and lysed for a set of basal samples and the LPS+IFNg added to the rest before return to their designated incubators. RNA was extracted for each time point based on values from a glycolytic rate assay that ran in tandem. RNA sample quality control was performed, followed by directional mRNA library preparation with polyA enrichment in triplicate using TruSeq stranded mRNA kits. Sequencing was performed for each sample at 50 million 150-bp paired end reads on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus. The abundance of transcripts was quantified using Fragments Per Kilobase per Million base pairs sequenced (FPKM) which was then used to determine correlation between samples. Differential gene expression (DGE) was analyzed by mapping reads to the GRCm39/mm39 mouse genome using Novomagic, Novogene’s analysis platform. Analysis included log fold change (logFC) of a gene, average expression level, and whether the p-value is significant. Venn diagrams were made in Novomagic using comparisons in gene expression quantity tables with a cutoff of 1 FPKM. Gene enrichment was performed using DGE lists filtered by significance (P-value ≤0.05) and protein coding description. Upregulated genes were identified by having a log2FC of >0.0 and downregulated genes with <0.0. Filtered gene lists were input into the Enrichr (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) program to generate Gene Ontologies. Gene-set libraries used were GO_Biological_Process_2023 and WikiPathways_2024_Mouse.

The datasets have been submitted to the NCBI database (BioProject accession number PPRJNA11149014).

Statistical analysis

Values are reported as mean ± the standard deviation of triplicates and represent experiments performed two or more times independently as indicated. Statistical significance was determined using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Student’s 't’ test at a critical value of p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Fever temperatures enhance macrophage activation outcomes

There is some evidence [33] that mouse macrophage activation responses are enhanced at fever temperatures. We therefore measured outcomes of mouse macrophage activation with a microbial ligand (LPS) at non-fever versus moderate fever temperatures, namely, 37 °C versus 39 °C [68]. Activated RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 1a) as well as primary mouse bone-marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) (Fig. 1b) showed consistently enhanced nitric oxide levels at fever temperature.

Triplicate cultures of cells (1 × 10^5 cells/well) were left unstimulated (grey bars) or stimulated with LPS (100 ng/ml; black bars) for 18 h at either normal or fever temperature as shown, and NO levels measured in culture supernatants. Cells used were RAW 264.7 cell line (a), primary BMDMs (b), chicken HTC cell line (c), chicken adherent spleen cells (d), rainbow trout RTS11 cell line (e), or rainbow trout adherent head kidney cells (f). *p < 0.05, ns, not significant. Data are representative of at least three independent experiments.

We next tested the activation outcomes of chicken monocytic cell line, HTC, and primary chicken spleen cells (chicken spleen adherent cells; CSCs) at non-fever versus fever temperatures. The temperatures chosen were 40–40.5 °C and 41.5–42 °C respectively. Again, both HTC cells and primary chicken macrophages, when stimulated with a microbial ligand, LPS, showed consistent enhancement of nitric oxide levels at the fever temperature (Fig. 1c and 1d).

We similarly tested the rainbow trout monocytic cell line RTS11, as well as primary rainbow trout macrophages (head kidney-adherent cells; HKCs), for NO levels induced upon activation with LPS. Rainbow trout survive from 4 °C to 20 °C with metabolism stopping at 24 °C. The non-fever and fever temperatures chosen were 16 °C (at which RTS11 optimally grows) and 19 °C respectively [23]. Again, both RTS11 cells and primary trout macrophages showed modestly but consistently enhanced nitric oxide levels upon activation at the fever temperature (Fig. 1e and 1f). It is to be noted that, while rainbow trout cells are known to be somewhat refractory to Escherichia coli LPS [41], RTS11 cells have been shown to be LPS-responsive [6].

We also tested iNOS mRNA induction with LPS at normal and fever temperatures in all three cell lines. Mouse and chicken cell lines showed induction of iNOS expression at normal temperature and its enhancement at fever temperature (Fig. S1). While the rainbow trout cell line did not show detectable iNOS induction at normal temperature, induced NO levels could nonetheless be seen (Fig. 1). Further, the fish cell line also showed enhanced iNOS gene expression at fever temperature (Fig. S1).

Together, these data showed that fever temperatures in macrophages of all three species induced higher levels of NO production as an activation outcome.

Metabolic consequences of fever temperatures and activation in mouse, chicken and rainbow trout macrophages: protein synthesis rates

We next initiated an examination of cellular metabolism in macrophages activated at normal versus fever temperatures. We began by examining the rates of global protein synthesis in cells at non-fever versus fever temperatures, with or without LPS activation. We used HPG incorporation detected by click chemistry followed by flow cytometry as a measure of global protein synthesis. Since all three cell lines showed broadly unimodal distributions of the flow cytometric curves as expected (Fig. S2), we examined comparative modal HPG fluorescence levels as indicative of relative levels of global protein synthesis.

The mouse cell line, RAW264.7, did not show any change in protein synthesis levels of unstimulated cells between normal versus fever temperature (Fig. S2). At normal temperature, LPS activation led to a modest increase in the protein synthesis level in the mouse cell line, and activation at fever temperature showed a further modest increase.

The chicken cell line HTC, showed a somewhat different pattern. It showed a small decrease in protein synthesis levels of unstimulated cells at fever versus normal temperature, and at normal temperature, it showed little change in protein synthesis levels upon LPS activation (Fig. S2). However, it showed increase in protein synthesis by activation at fever temperature (Fig. S2).

The rainbow trout macrophage cell line RTS11, again, increased its protein synthesis levels progressively as temperature increased (13,16,19, 21 °C), even without activation (Fig. S2). However, these cells showed little increase in protein synthesis with LPS activation (Figs. S2).

Together, these data suggested that, while fever temperatures enhanced macrophage effector immune activation in cells of all three species tested, the metabolic consequences of fever and activation might be distinct in them.

Metabolic profile alterations by fever temperatures and activation in mouse, chicken and rainbow trout macrophages

Therefore, we next tracked the OCR and the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) reflecting the PER of these various cell types at normal versus fever temperatures, with or without activation. All cells in culture without stimulation for 6–10 h in the Seahorse analyzer tended to show a decline in PER (and a less consistent decline in OCR) over these time scales, most probably due to culture conditions. The absolute values of OCR/PER signals varied substantially even by small variations in adherent cell numbers, and therefore we have focused on the broad differences in the shapes of the time-course curves ('metabolic trajectories') over the 6–10 h of assay time.

Unstimulated mouse macrophages, whether the RAW264.7 cell line or primary BMDMs, showed similar and relatively stable OCR and PER trajectories at normal versus fever temperatures (Figs. 2a and 3a). Similar results were found in unstimulated chicken macrophage cells, whether in the HTC cell line or in primary splenic adherent cells (Figs. 2b and 3b). However, unstimulated rainbow trout macrophages, whether the RTS11 cells or primary adherent head kidney cells, consistently showed relative increases in baseline metabolic activity at fever temperature compared to non-fever temperature (Figs. 2c and 3c). Additionally, RTS11 cells showed a change in baseline OCR activity between normal versus fever temperatures, though this was not as prominent in primary rainbow trout cells (Figs. 2c and 3c).

When mouse or human macrophages [71] are activated with microbial PRR ligands such as LPS, they have been reported to show a switch to glycolysis reflected in a reduction in OCR (indicating a reduction in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation activity) and an increase in PER (as an outcome of glycolysis). Mouse macrophages, both RAW264.7 cells and primary BMDMs, showed a reduction in OCR and an accompanying increase in PER starting at about 4–5 h of activation at normal temperature (Figs. 2a and 3a), as expected. However, while LPS-activated chicken macrophages showed some initial increase in OCR, only primary chicken spleen cells showed any increase in PER activity occurring after the early OCR increase returned to baseline (Figs. 2b and 3b). Rainbow trout macrophages showed no consistent metabolic responses to LPS activation (Figs. 2c and 3c) with increases in OCR and PER being mostly temperature driven.

When mouse macrophages were LPS-activated at fever temperatures, the activation-mediated metabolic switch was seen to be enhanced and accelerated so that the OCR reduction and PER increase began at least 0.5 h earlier than at non-fever temperature (Figs. 2a and 3a). There was a tendency in chicken macrophages to such an acceleration in the increase in OCR upon activation at fever temperatures (Figs. 2b and 3b). LPS-activated rainbow trout macrophages showed additional consistent metabolic changes at fever temperature (Figs. 2c and 3c).

Thus, LPS activation led to somewhat different metabolic trajectories in cell lines versus primary cells in chicken and rainbow trout cells so that HTC cells behaved like mouse cells upon LPS activation at both normal and fever temperatures, but chicken primary macrophages behaved differently upon LPS activation in that they showed no greater late OCR decrease, and showed an early and sustained PER increase which was 'accelerated' at fever temperature. Similarly, LPS-activated RTS11 cells showed an early and sustained PER increase, while primary rainbow trout cells did not show such a change.

Metabolic consequences of fever temperatures and activation in mouse, chicken and rainbow trout macrophages: ATP levels

The next metabolic measurement attempted was that of ATP levels in activated mouse/chicken/rainbow trout macrophages at normal versus fever temperatures. Data showed wide variability for many cells and activation situations. Consistent results could be obtained, as shown (Fig. 4), for cell lines of all three species. Since LPS-activation induced modest and variable changes in ATP levels, we have used LPS+IFNg for activation (except for the RTS11 fish cell line in the absence of fish IFNg).

ATP levels, either glycolytic (grey) or OXPHOS (black), are shown in top panels (a-d), while bottom panels (e-h) show percentages of glycolytic (grey) versus OXPHOS (black) contributions to the ATP levels. Data are shown for mouse RAW 264.7 cells (panels a and d), chicken HTC cells (panels b and e) and rainbow trout RTS11 cells (panels c and f). The most striking difference observed in cellular ATP levels was an increase at fever temperatures, without any activation, in mouse and rainbow trout cells but not in chicken cells (Fig. 4). This is a noteworthy complexity, since mouse cells did not show any increase in protein synthesis rates at fever temperatures without activation, while chicken and fish cells did. The other finding of note was that, rainbow trout macrophages were far more reliant on oxidative phosphorylation for ATP generation than either mouse or chicken macrophages were (Fig. 4). Thirdly, the relative contribution of oxidative phosphorylation to ATP levels in non-activated macrophages increased at fever temperatures in mouse and chicken cells, but decreased in rainbow trout cells (Fig. 4). Whether these differences are related to poikilothermy or are simply species-specific differences remains to be clarified.

While activation did bring about some increases in cellular ATP levels, the increases were mostly modest and variable. The relative contributions of aerobic glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation to ATP production in activated cells showed some interesting patterns. In mouse cells, there was an increase in the contribution of oxidative phosphorylation early upon activation at both normal though not at fever temperatures (Fig. 4d). However, mouse cells showed an increase in the contribution of aerobic glycolysis at the last tested time points post-activation, at both normal and fever temperatures (Fig. 5d). This is consistent with the kinetic data above on OCR/PER measurements, which showed the expected late switch aerobic to glycolysis reflected in a reduction in OCR and an increase in PER. Chicken and rainbow trout monocytic cells did not show any major modification in this analysis (Fig. 4).

Collectively, these data indicate that the metabolic effects of fever temperatures and microbial activation on macrophages show distinct species-specific differences, despite the fact that functional outcomes of microbial activation are comparable in them and show similar enhancement at fever temperatures. The genesis and consequences of these differential effects is thus of great interest for further investigation.

The specific metabolic reprogramming seen in mouse cells, especially of the late metabolic switch to aerobic glycolysis upon activation and its acceleration at fever temperature, was further studied by further analyses of RAW264.7 mouse monocytic cells under these conditions.

Activation-induced metabolic reprogramming over time in mouse macrophages is NO-dependent at least in part, and the effects of fever temperature are TRPV1-independent

In RAW264.7 cells, we confirmed that the metabolic modulation induced by LPS was seen when LPS+IFNg was used for activation. IFNg, when used by itself, showed a similar metabolic effect as that of LPS (Fig. 5a), with a reduction in OCR and an accompanying increase in PER starting at about 5 h of activation. When LPS and IFNg were used together the effect was significantly pronounced in both the OCR and the PER components (Fig. 5a). Further, the acceleration of these effects brought about in LPS-activated cells by fever temperature was also seen when they were activated with LPS+IFNg (Fig. 5b).

Since there are indications that the late switch to glycolysis in activated macrophages may be NO-dependent [20,29,34], we tested that possibility in LPS+IFNg-activated RAW264.7 cells. An iNOS inhibitor, 2-aminoguanidnine (2AG), was added to the cultures for this purpose. When 2-AG was present throughout, both the OCR reduction and the PER enhancement seen at about 5 h in activated cells were inhibited, and this effect was seen at both normal and fever temperatures (Fig. 5c). Interestingly, the inhibition was similar regardless of whether 2-AG was added either at an early (20 min) or a later (200 min) time point (Fig. 5c). Notably, as shown above, this NO-dependent effect was not seen in either chicken or rainbow trout monocytic cells, even though they all made NO upon activation.

It must be noted that the presence of NO alone may contribute to a decrease in the OCR signal [9], although the increase in glycolysis is unaffected by NO. OCR is also likely affected, of course, by the effects of NO on cytochrome oxidase and complex IV [16,20], along with IRG1 [32] and RET [44].

There are indications that cellular sensing of fever temperatures involves the vanilloid channel TRPV1 [27]. We have previously shown that while some effects of fever temperatures on mouse CD4 T cells are indeed TRPV1-dependent, that is not true of mouse dendritic cells [3,68]. We tested the ability of a TRPV1 agonist, capsaicin [7], to mimic the ability of fever temperature to accelerate the metabolic modifications in activated RAW264.7 cells, and confirmed that the effect was TRPV1-independent (Fig. 5d) [35].

Transcriptomic landscape of fever-mediated metabolic acceleration in activated mouse macrophages

RAW264.7 cells showed a complex temporal response to a combination of activation and fever as shown above. Specifically, strong activation by a microbial ligand and an inflammatory cytokine together resulted in an early increase in OCR followed by a late reduction, which in turn was accompanied by an increase in PER, and this shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis was accelerated at fever temperature. To begin to examine the transcriptional correlates of these complex changes, we undertook RNA-seq analyses of RAW264.7 cells at either normal or fever temperatures, with or without activation with LPS+IFNg. We sampled all four groups of cells at four time points. The first basal (’B’) time point was 20 min at 37 C or 39 C before activation. The second time point (‘A’) was at the first PER peak post-activation, at ∼55 min 37 C and 50 min at 39 C. the third time point (‘N’) was when the OCR began to show a late decline, at 220 min at 37 C and 180 min at 39 C. The fourth time point (‘G’) was at the point of peak late PER increase, at 340 min at 37 C and 285 min at 39 C (Fig. 6a). These time points were determined, in the actual experiment, by setting up replicate wells for metabolic analyses parallel to the bulk cultures. These replicate wells were run in the Seahorse analyzer 20 min in advance of the bulk cultures, and the real-time data from these runs were used to determine the B-A-N-G time points of RNA harvesting as shown. The RNA-seq data, thus, reflect these four time points of metabolic modulations within the actual experiments.

Quantification analysis showed that transcriptomes tended to cluster by early and late timepoints. Thus, points B and A corelated more closely with each other than N and G and vice versa (Fig. 6b). And 2-D principal component analysis (PCA) revealed similar results with these time points clustering in different quadrants of the plot (Fig. 6c).

Differential gene expression between baseline and fever temperature were compared in Venn diagrams with overlaps quantifying the number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) shared among the various groups. The most differential expression was seen between normal versus fever temperature prior to stimulation (Fig. 6d), with more DEGs than at any of the other post-stimulation time points. Thus, with 20 min at fever temperature without any activation, 759 protein coding (4173 total) genes were already upregulated and 2285 (2631 total) downregulated at fever temperature as shown in volcano plots (Fig. 6e). Investigations into specific gene ontology (GO) pathways affected were done by running all significantly differentiated genes through the enrichr program against a GO database.

GO analysis showed that the major pathways ‘Positive Regulation Of Transcription By RNA Polymerase II’, ‘Regulation Of Transcription By RNA Polymerase II’ and ‘Positive Regulation Of DNA-templated Transcription’, while the prominent down-regulated GO terms were ‘Translation’, ‘Macromolecule Biosynthetic Process’ and ‘Cytoplasmic Translation’ (Table S1). Thus, although the effects of prolonged exposure to fever temperature led to a modest change in protein synthesis levels, the early changes leading up to this were an increase in mRNA transcription and a reduction in mRNA translation.

When the early effects of strong activation on the RAW267.4 cell transcriptome at normal temperature were analysed, it was evident that 209 protein coding genes (258 total) were upregulated and 1158 (1621 total) downregulated (Fig. 7a). The major pathways upregulated by GO analysis were ‘Cholesterol Metabolism’, ‘Cholesterol Biosynthesis’ and ‘Elongation Of Very Long Chain Fatty Acids’, while among the downregulated pathways were ‘mRNA Processing’, ‘Mechanisms Associated With Pluripotency’ and ‘EGFR1 Signalling Pathway’ (Table S2).

Volcano plots showing the differential gene expression from one post-activation time point to the next; showing B to A (panels a and b), A to N (panels c and d), and N to G (panels e and f). Each transition showed greater levels of differential expression at 39 °C [right panels] compared to the 37 °C [left panels].

At fever temperature, activation led to upregulation of 100 protein coding genes (121 total) and downregulation of 904 protein coding genes (1184 total), with major pathways downregulated being ‘mRNA Processing’, ‘Mechanisms Associated With Pluripotency’, ‘Non Homologous End Joining’, ‘Regulation Of DNA Replication’, ‘Mitotic Sister Chromatid Cohesion’ and ‘Positive Regulation Of Cell Cycle Process’ (Fig. 7b, Table S3). No significant GO pathways could be identified in upregulated genes. Clearly, the pathways affected by activation at this early time point were substantially overlapping between normal and fever temperatures, and changes in lipid metabolism were prominent among them.

When the transcriptome change from the initial PER peak to the late OCR fall in activated RAW264.7 cells at normal temperature was examined (time point A to N), 1982 protein coding genes (2510 total) were upregulated and 2310 (2892 total) downregulated (Fig. 7c). The major pathways upregulated were ‘Type II Interferon Signalling’, ‘IL 17A Signalling and Apoptosis’, ‘Positive Regulation Of Cytokine Production’, ‘Response To Cytokine and Defense Response To Virus’, while among the downregulated pathways were ‘G1 To S Cell Cycle Control’, ‘Non Homologous End Joining’, ‘P53 Signalling, DNA Repair’, ‘DNA Metabolic Process’ and ‘DNA Damage Response’ (Table S4).

At fever temperature, the transition from time point A to N consisted of upregulation of 1918 protein coding genes (2292 total) and downregulation of 2417 protein coding genes (2835 total), with major pathways upregulated being ‘Type II Interferon Signalling’, ‘IL 17A Signalling and Chemokine Signalling Pathway’, ‘Positive Regulation Of Cytokine Production’, ‘Response To Cytokine and Cytokine-Mediated Signalling Pathway’, and downregulated pathways being ‘G1 To S Cell Cycle Control’, ‘Mismatch Repair’, ‘Non Homologous End Joining’, ‘DNA Repair’, ‘DNA Metabolic Process’ and ‘DNA Damage Response’ (Fig. 7d, Table S5). Again, the pathways affected by activation at this time point too were substantially overlapping between normal and fever temperatures, and changes in signal transduction and cytokine production pathways unsurprisingly prominent among them.

When the transcriptome change from the late OCR fall to the peak PER rise in activated RAW264.7 cells was examined (time point N to G), 289 protein coding genes (454 total) were upregulated and 339 (366 total) downregulated (Fig. 7e). The major pathways downregulated were ‘Apoptosis’, ‘MicroRNAs In Cardiomyocyte Hypertrophy’, ‘Fibrin Complement Receptor 3 Signalling Pathway’, ‘Ribonucleoprotein Complex Biogenesis’, ‘tRNA Modification and Ribosome Biogenesis’ (Table S6). No significant GO pathways could be identified in upregulated genes.

At fever temperature, the transition from time point N to G consisted of upregulation of 1545 protein coding genes (1920 total) and downregulation of 197 protein coding genes (238 total), with major pathways upregulated being ‘mRNA Processing’, ‘FAS Pathway And Stress Induction Of HSP Regulation’, ‘Mechanisms Associated With Pluripotency’, ‘DNA Damage Response’, ‘DNA Metabolic Process and DNA Repair’, and downregulated pathways being ‘Regulation Of Transcription By RNA Polymerase II’, ‘Positive Regulation Of DNA-templated Transcription’ and ‘Positive Regulation Of Transcription By RNA Polymerase II’ (Fig. 7f, Table S7). Once again, the pathways affected by activation at this time point were also substantially overlapping between normal and fever temperatures, and stress responses were prominent among them.

Thus, it is evident that macrophage activation at fever temperature started from a different basal transcriptome, and despite that, it shared many transcriptional changes with activation at normal temperature. However, the post-activation transcriptome changes also show some interesting differences between normal and fever temperature. A point of note is that, at fever temperature, activation led to greater fold-change values and levels of significance in the DEGs at the first, early time point (Fig. 7a), indicating that fever tended to increase overall transcription early post-activation. Such high transcriptomic activity early may plausibly expedite a downstream process like the described metabolic reprogramming.

Further, when GO pathways at normal versus fever temperature were examined at each time point post-activation (Tables S8-S10), the prominent pathways affected early post-activation at normal temperature were related to responses to LPS and inflammation. However, at fever temperature, translation and mitochondrial activity including respiration were predominately affected. At later time points post-activation, in addition to responses to LPS, increased cell signalling pathways were seen at 37 °C, whereas at fever temperature, pathways related to DNA replication and damage response and nucleic acid metabolism were seen.

We examined the strength of correlation to specific GO terms indicated by the fraction of differentially expressed genes out of the total of genes listed in the GO term. We looked at GO terms related to synthesis pathways, such as ‘Translation’, Macromolecule Biosynthesis Process’ and Protein Phosphorylation. At the first time point post-activation, these GO terms showed higher correlations at 39 °C compared to the levels at 37 °C, while at later post-activation time points, the correlations were similarly poor by and large (Fig. S3).

We similarly looked at post-activation changes in strengths of correlation in GO terms related to metabolism. Respiration-related pathways showed differences between normal and fever temperatures. For ‘Aerobic Electron Transport Chain’ and ‘Oxidative Phosphorylation’, activation at normal temperature did not yield enough significant DEGs resulting in their virtual absence post-activation. However, activation at fever temperature revealed high (∼0.9) correlation of these GO terms at the early time point, indicating that fever temperature is correlated with early increased regulation of respiration (Fig. S4).

Among other pathways, fever temperature caused early strong correlation for GO terms related to mitotic cytokinesis, glucose metabolism and cellular response to oxidative stress, whereas this occurred later at normal temperature (Fig. S4). GO pathways related to immunological responses showed a late rise in strength of correlation at normal temperature, but at fever temperature, their strengths of correlation were relatively high early activation onwards, although the fractions of genes affected in these GO terms even at fever temperature were relatively low (Fig. S5). At late time points, correlations were uniformly low.

Conclusion

Fever is known to be important for survival from humans [59] to fish (reviewed in [8] in responding to viral and bacterial infections. In such infections, macrophages differentiate and fine-tune their responses. Our previous work showed effects of fever temperature on mouse dendritic cell function to enhance Th1 responses from CD4 T cells. Fever has been reported to enhance outcomes of inflammatory stimulation [10,33,73]. There is evidence that the ability to reach fever temperature declines not only in humans but also in chickens as they age [43]. In the present work, we have therefore used fever temperatures to further explore and compare the effects of fever temperatures on activation-induced changes in the function and metabolism of macrophages from three different species: mouse, chicken and rainbow trout.

Macrophages from all three species were similar in that they made more iNOS/NO at fever temperatures in response to TLR stimulation. However, interspecies differences began to emerge when cellular metabolism was examined, with distinctions between mouse, chicken and rainbow trout cells. Cellular ATP levels went up at fever temperatures and modestly with activation in all three species. While global protein synthesis was enhanced in mouse cells at fever temperature only with LPS activation, it was enhanced in the absence of LPS activation in chicken and rainbow trout cells. These data may indicate that fever temperature alone has differential effects in cells from the three species, and those effects in turn may modulate the outcomes of activation at fever temperatures. Given our finding that twenty minutes of fever temperature led to global transcriptomic changes in mouse cells, investigations of such changes in chicken and rainbow trout cells, coupled to examination of their consequences for metabolic alterations, would be informative.

Metabolic profiles showed inter-species differences even at normal non-fever temperatures. The rainbow trout cells showed only modest metabolic perturbations upon activation at both normal and fever temperatures. Both mouse and chicken cells showed early induction of oxidative phosphorylation. However, mouse cells showed a distinct switch later to aerobic glycolysis, and this switch was dependent on the presence of NO. Further, mouse macrophages underwent these metabolic modulations more rapidly at fever temperature, and this ‘fever sensing’ was not phenocopied by a TRPV1 agonist. Molecular temperature sensors are known to involve transient receptor potential cation channels (TRPs) which, while pleiotropic in their activating ligands, are known to be temperature sensors, particularly the 'thermo-TRPs' that exhibit robust thermo-sensitivity (reviewed in [13]. Specifically, both the TRP vanilloid receptors (TRPVs), particularly TRPV1–4, and other related molecules such as TRPM8 have been shown to be thermo-sensors [54]. It remains to be clarified if any of these molecules function as fever sensors in mouse macrophages.

We further examined this fever-mediated acceleration of macrophage metabolic changes by analyzing the transcriptomic landscapes at the B-A-N-G time points, on the background of findings that fever range temperatures can change the expression timing and splicing patterns of mRNA [48]. While there are some transcriptomic data on activated human [37] or mouse [28] macrophages, including one reporting such changes at fever temperature [37], these studies report transcriptomes at time point much later than those reported here. Our data indicate that even twenty minutes of fever temperature, with no stimulation, bring about far-reaching changes in the mouse macrophage transcriptional landscape. Post-stimulation transcriptional changes are more specific and restricted and show substantial overlap between normal and fever temperatures. Thus, the subtle temperature change of 2 °C brings about a global adjustment in mouse macrophages that may be associated with a more rapid metabolic response and a higher functional output early upon activation. The pathways mediating these connections are clearly of major interest.

In this context, it must be noted that, while the focus of this work is on a comparative approach across three vertebrate species, the RNA-seq analyses reported have so far been carried out only in mouse macrophages, and not yet on chicken or rainbow trout cells. Clearly, it is plausible that transcriptomic differences may well accompany and even underlie the species-specific differences we report here. Given our findings that twenty minutes at fever temperature, without activation, brings about the most extensive global transcriptomic changes in the mouse cell line, and that post-activation changes are less comprehensively modified by fever temperatures, a potential hypothesis to test would be that non-activated baseline transcriptomes in chicken or rainbow trout cells may be less affected by fever temperatures. This in turn may be connected to the specific metabolic changes seen in activated mouse cells at fever temperatures. Further work on chicken and rainbow trout cell transcriptomes and investigations of the mechanistic connections between those changes and metabolic alterations is warranted for the future.
