Structural MRI assessment of hypothalamic alterations and cognitive implications in adolescents with rapid pubertal progression
Authors: Lulu Lin, Pingyi Zhu, Donghui Zhou, Shundan Zhao, Peilong Pan, Xiaozheng Liu, Zhihan Yan, Min Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRapid pubertal progression involves hormonal and hypothalamic adaptations, yet its neurocognitive implications remain unclear. Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), a key mediator of growth and neurodevelopment, is closely linked to pubertal processes. This study aims to investigate IGF-1-associated hypothalamic structural changes and their cognitive consequences in adolescents with rapid pubertal progression (Tanner stage advancement ≥ 1 within 3–6 months).MethodsIn this retrospective, cross-sectional cohort, 124 adolescents (8 to 13 years old) underwent a single session of structural MRI, cognitive assessments (The Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fourth Edition-Chinese Version), and gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) stimulation tests at baseline. The GnRH stimulation test was performed to assess hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis (HPG) axis activation, which was used to classify participants as HPG axis-activated (HPG+, n = 73) or non-activated (HPG−, n = 51) based on peak luteinizing hormone responses. Hypothalamic subunit volumes were quantified via high-resolution T1-weighted imaging. Stepwise forward regression identified structural predictors of HPG activation; mediation models tested IGF-1’s role in linking hypothalamic morphology to cognition. Covariates included sex, age, body mass index, and total intracranial volume.ResultsHPG+ adolescents exhibited elevated IGF-1 (p < 0.05), poorer cognitive performance (p < 0.05), and volumetric alterations in inferior tuberal and posterior hypothalamic subunits (p < 0.01). A model integrating right and total inferior tuberal volumes predicted HPG+ status with 83.9% accuracy. Mediation analysis demonstrated that total inferior tuberal enlargement fully mediated the inverse relationship between IGF-1 elevation and poorer cognitive performances.ConclusionsIGF-1-driven remodeling of the inferior tuberal hypothalamus contributes to cognitive deficits during rapid pubertal progression, revealing a neuroendocrine pathway linking hormonal activation, structural plasticity, and neurodevelopment. These findings position the hypothalamus as a biomarker candidate for early intervention in adolescents with atypical pubertal trajectories.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02255-w.Keywords: Puberty, Hypothalamus, Cognitive, IGF-1, Hypothalamic subunit volumes, Neurodevelopment

Introduction

Puberty represents a critical developmental phase characterized by dynamic neuroendocrine interactions, particularly within the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis. Central to this process is the pulsatile secretion of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), which is modulated by insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) [1]. IGF-1 directly stimulates GnRH release from hypothalamic neurons, thereby initiating puberty-related luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) surges in the anterior pituitary. These hormonal cascades drive gonadal steroid production (e.g., testosterone and estradiol), which not only accelerates physical maturation but also exerts profound influences on brain development, social cognition, and emotional regulation [2, 3]. However, the mechanisms linking hormonal changes to mental health outcomes, particularly the role of IGF-1 as an upstream regulator, remain unclear.

Interindividual variability in pubertal development encompasses two distinct characteristics: pubertal timing, which refers to the age at onset or completion of key pubertal milestones, and pubertal tempo, which is defined as the rate of within-person progression through multiple stages of maturation [4]. Both characteristics have been linked to adolescent cognitive development and mental health outcomes. Following a period of relative quiescence during childhood, the reactivation of the HPG axis represents a fundamental early milestone in this process, triggering a cascade of hormonal and physical changes. It therefore serves as a central focus in examining the relationship between pubertal development and cognitive outcomes.

Environmental factors, particularly early life stress (ELS), can significantly influence the trajectory of pubertal development and its associated mental health risks [5]. ELS has been associated with alterations in neuroendocrine axes, including the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis [6, 7]. These alterations may subsequently affect the timing and tempo of HPG axis maturation and sensitivity [8, 9]. Earlier or accelerated pubertal development may increase susceptibility to stress and sleep disturbances [10]. Longitudinal evidence indicates that ELS exposure predicts distinct developmental pathways of emotional problems and associated neural substrates across adolescence, such as reduced hippocampal volume [11]. Furthermore, ELS has been shown to disrupt the age-related refinement of functional brain network flexibility, a process associated with cognitive control (e.g., language, reasoning, working memory) and network reconfiguration efficiency during task performance [12]. Additionally, ELS has been found to moderate the relationships between sleep disturbances, structural integrity of the right cingulum bundle (CGC), and depressive symptoms. While CGC connectivity appears selectively involved in emotion-related disturbances, the uncinate fasciculus (UF) connectivity—which supports cognitive functions such as cognitive control, reward processing, and episodic memory—shows no consistent association with adolescent depression [13–15]. Critically, these pathways can be sex specific. For instance, sleep disturbances in early adolescence mediate the link between early life adversity and later depressive symptoms specifically in females [16]. This body of work underscores the importance of considering early life stress as a factor that may alter the neuroendocrine and neural context in which pubertal development unfolds, potentially modifying its cognitive and emotional outcomes.

A body of research has examined the associations of pubertal timing with cognitive and mental health outcomes. For instance, earlier pubertal timing has been associated with increased internalizing and externalizing problems, as well as risky sexual behaviors [17]. Neuroimaging studies have identified specific neural substrates underlying these puberty-mental health linkages. For example, pituitary gland volume was observed to mediate, in both sexes, the relationship between early puberty and depressive symptoms, with larger volumes predicting greater symptom severity [18]. Similarly, a smaller subgenual anterior cingulate cortex volume was found to mediate (in females only) the relationship between earlier pubertal timing and elevated psychological difficulties at follow-up [19]. Conversely, some studies have reported null effects of pubertal timing. Beltz et al. [20], for instance, found no association between pubertal timing and depression.

In contrast, research focusing specifically on pubertal tempo remains limited, and the available evidence comes predominantly from mental health studies rather than cognitive domains. The developmental readiness hypothesis [21, 22] posits that a mismatch between an adolescent’s physical maturation and the development of key cognitive networks, such as those supporting language processing (the auditory network and the mouth somatomotor network), may disrupt normative developmental trajectories. This imbalance can manifest as less efficient neural reconfiguration during cognitive tasks [23], which is associated with poorer behavioral performance in affected adolescents compared to their typically developing peers. For instance, the Pittsburgh longitudinal study of girls aged 10–13 years linked atypical pubertal rates (i.e., a faster or slower than normal tempo) to increased depressive symptoms [4]. While Deardorff et al. [24] observed in an Asian cohort that a more rapid rate of pubic hair development was inversely related to depressive symptoms. However, as existing neuroimaging studies have focused predominantly on pubertal timing, the neurobiological correlations of tempo remain poorly understood. Although recent work highlights white matter microstructure as a potential mediator, longitudinal data demonstrate that more advanced pubertal status (but not pubertal tempo) predicts cognitive-emotional dysregulation in 9-year-old girls [25]. These different patterns of results across tempo/timing studies may stem from methodological limitations in quantifying tempo/timing, cultural differences in pubertal experiences, or from unmeasured neurobiological mediators.

Among the potential neurobiological mediators of abnormal pubertal development, hypothalamic structural properties (e.g., regional volume) emerge as particularly significant candidates. It is important to note that executive function and cognitive performance rely on distributed neural circuits. These circuits extend beyond subcortical neuroendocrine hubs, such as the HPG axis, to include key limbic and prefrontal cortical regions. Recent large-scale genetic mapping indicates that hypothalamus is genetically correlated with structures like the hippocampus, accumbens, and thalamus, which together form integrated circuitry for arousal, motivation, and memory [26]. This highlights the hypothalamus as a node within broader networks crucial for cognitive-affective function. Nevertheless, research investigating hypothalamic mediation pathways connecting HPG axis activation-induced hormonal surges to cognitive consequences in adolescents exhibiting faster pubertal tempo is limited. This knowledge gap stems primarily from technical limitations associated with delineating hypothalamic subunits using conventional MRI protocols. However, recent methodological advances in neuroimaging analysis, particularly the development of automated segmentation protocols [27], now enable the precise volumetric measurement of five distinct hypothalamic subunits, namely the anterior-inferior (a-iHyp), anterior-superior (a-sHyp), posterior (posHyp), inferior tubular (infTub), and superior tubular hypothalamus (supTub). This now allows for framework to examine how the volumetric characteristics of specific hypothalamic subunits mediate hormonal effects on cognitive processing.

The current study aims to elucidate the tripartite relationship between IGF-1 levels, hypothalamic subunit volume changes, and cognitive performance in adolescents with rapid pubertal progression, addressing a critical gap in neurodevelopmental research.

Materials and methods

Participants

This retrospective study was based on 124 adolescents (53 males and 71 females) with rapid pubertal progression who were monitored for a 3–6-month period. This duration allowed sufficient time to assess whether the rate of progression from one developmental stage to the next was rapid or relatively slow [28]. Participants (8 to 13 years old) were recruited from the Child Healthcare Department of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University between March 2018 and February 2022. All evaluations were conducted prior to the GnRH stimulation test. Based on HPG axis activation, the participants were divided into two groups: HPG+ (73 participants; mean age = 9.84 ± 1.33 years; male-to-female ratio of 33:40) and HPG− (51 participants; mean age = 9.65 ± 1.50 years; male-to-female ratio of 20:31). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Protocol No. 2024385) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from the guardian(s) of each participant.

At the baseline visit, all participants underwent the following assessments in a standardized sequence: (1) Tanner stage evaluation by a senior pediatric endocrinologist via physical examination; (2) structural MRI scan; and (3) administration of the WISC-IV-Chinese test. These three baseline assessments were completed on the same day. To avoid potential confounding effects of hormonal changes induced by the GnRH stimulation test on MRI and cognitive measures, the GnRH test was scheduled 1 to 2 weeks after the MRI session. At the follow-up visit 3–6 months later, a second Tanner stage evaluation was performed.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) chronological age > 8 years for girls and > 9 years for boys (2) clinical referral for assessment of pubertal progression with documented advancement by at least one Tanner stage within a 3–6 month follow-up period; and (3) completion of all assessments (Tanner staging evaluation, MRI scan, and cognitive test), prior to the GnRH stimulation test.

Exclusion criteria were (1) an intelligence quotient < 70, as assessed using the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fourth Edition-Chinese Version (WISC-IV-Chinese); (2) a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders; (3) a history of head trauma; (4) use of medications affecting brain function or hormone levels; and (5) contraindications for MRI.

Clinical and biochemical data

Hormone levels were assessed using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay. A GnRH stimulation test (GnRH = 2.5 µg/kg, maximum dose = 100 µg) was performed on each patient between 8:00 and 8:30 a.m. after fasting, approximately one or two weeks after the MRI scan. This test serves as the clinical gold standard for assessing the functional activation of the HPG axis and was used to objectively classify participants into HPG+ (activated) and HPG- (non-activated) groups. Blood samples for LH and FSH were collected at 0, 30, and 60 min, and samples for estradiol were collected at 0 and 30 min. Hormone levels measured at 0 min were considered baseline. Prolactin, cortisol, testosterone, and IGF-1 levels were also measured at baseline. Complete physical examinations included anthropometric measurements of height, weight, and body mass index (BMI), while family income was also recorded.

The Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fourth Edition-Chinese Version (WISC-IV-Chinese) is an individually administered, norm-referenced intelligence assessment comprising 10 core subtests and 5 supplemental subtests. These subtests yield a Full-Scale Intelligence Quotient (FSIQ) ranging from 40 to 160 and four component scales. The Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI) is based on Vocabulary and Comprehension subtests; the Perceptual Reasoning Index (PRI) on Block Design, Picture Concepts, and Matrix Reasoning subtests; the Working Memory Index (WMI) on Digit Span and Letter-Number Sequencing subtests; the Processing Speed Index (PSI) on Coding and Symbol Search subtests. Testing was administered by an experienced examiner who was blinded to the participant groups.

Image acquisition and analysis

All participants underwent MRI scans 1 or 2 weeks before the GnRH stimulation test. MRI data were collected using a 3.0-T GE Healthcare Discovery scanner (General Electric, Milwaukee, WI, USA) and an eight-channel phased-array head coil. High-resolution three-dimensional T1-weighted images were obtained using a whole-brain spoiled gradient-echo sequence (repetition time = 7.7 ms, echo time = 3.4 ms, inversion time = 450 ms, flip angle = 12°, and field of view = 256 × 256 mm2), resulting in voxel sizes of 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 and 188 sagittal slices, with 1-mm thickness. The acquisition time for T1-weight imaging was 5 min and 27 s per participant.

All T1-weighted images were automatically preprocessed using an integrated “recon-all” workflow. This workflow systematically carried out the following tasks: (a) motion correction to address scanning artifacts, (b) skull stripping to isolate brain tissue, and (c) spatial normalization via Talairach transformation to the MNI305 template space. To automatically extract the volumes of the hypothalamus and its five subunits (a-iHyp, a-sHyp, infTub, supTub, and posHyp) (Fig. 1), we utilized an automated segmentation tool developed by Billot et al. [12] to process the preprocessed T1-weighted images. Both the preprocessing of the T1 images and the segmentation of the hypothalamic subunits were carried out using FreeSurfer (Version 7.2.0, Harvard University, Boston, MA, USA). As validated by its developers, this tool has been shown to exhibit superior inter-rater reliability and a lower error rate compared to conventional manual tracing. Total intracranial volume (TIV) was calculated using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12b software (Version 5953, University College London, UK) under Matlab R2016b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Pituitary volumes were obtained through semi-automatic segmentation using ITK-SNAP software (Version 3.4.0; University of Pennsylvania, USA), with intensity thresholds set between 1600 and 2200 for optimal tissue delineation.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 24.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the variables. Independent sample t-tests and Mann-Whitney U tests were conducted for normally distributed and skewed variables, respectively. Volumes of the whole hypothalamus and its five subregions were compared between groups using ANCOVA adjusting for sex, age, BMI, and TIV. A stepwise forward regression analysis was used to (i) identify hypothalamic subunit volumes most closely associated with HPG axis activation and (ii) develop a predictive model for distinguishing between the HPG − and HPG+ groups. Finally, mediation and Spearman’s partial correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationships among IGF-1 levels, hypothalamic subunit volumes, and cognitive performance. Sex, age, BMI, and TIV were included as covariates. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. TIV was included as a covariate in further analyses due to control for individual and sex variability in brain size [29]. Analyses on the five hypothalamic subunit volumes were Bonferroni-corrected, indicating significance at the p < 0.01 level.

Establishment of predictive model

A binary logistic regression analysis using stepwise forward regression was conducted to identify the hypothalamic subunit volumes most closely associated with HPG axis activation. The right and total infundibular tubule volumes were identified as significant targets of HPG axis activation (odds ratios [ORs]: 1.086 and 1.126; 95% confidence intervals [CIs]: 1.023–1.153 and 1.075–1.179, respectively; p < 0.001). The prediction model achieved an accuracy rate of 83.9% and had a Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test result of 0.889.

Relationships among IGF-1 levels, hypothalamic subunit volumes, and cognitive performance

Significant correlations were found after adjusting for sex, age, BMI, and TIV between hypothalamic subunit volumes and most cognitive indices. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, most recent studies of precocious puberty have focused on aspects of pubertal timing at early puberty. Research on the role of puberty progression, namely tempo is limited. To address this gap, this study used high-resolution T1-weighted imaging to measure hypothalamic nuclei volumes and investigate hormonally induced hypothalamic changes and their cognitive implications in adolescents with rapid pubertal progression. Our findings revealed significant differences in the level of IGF-1 and volumes of infTub and posHyp between HPG- and HPG+ groups (p < 0.01). Furthermore, our study introduced a predictive model for HPG activation with 83.9% accuracy. Additionally, mediation analysis demonstrated that total inferior tuberal enlargement fully mediated the inverse relationship between IGF-1 elevation and poorer cognitive performance. Our research therefore offers a novel insight to understanding how hypothalamic volumes change during puberty impact cognitive outcomes in this critical developmental stage.

Our findings indicate that HPG+ adolescents had higher levels of both IGF-1 and sex steroids (LH, FSH, and testosterone) compared to HPG- adolescents. These results are consistent with those of Cole et al. (2015). In their analysis of the Chard Growth study results, they showed that the increases in IGF-1, height, and sex steroids were synchronized with Tanner staging over time [30]. The elevated baseline levels of sex steroids in the HPG+ group reflect a pre-existing state of heightened neuroendocrine activity, which may represent an important preceding condition indicative of accelerated pubertal maturation. Additional research also shows that IGF-1 modulates GnRH neurons by acting on specific hypothalamic nuclei, such as the medial basal hypothalamus/arcuate nucleus (MBH/ARC), preoptic area (POA), and rostral hypothalamic area (RHA) [1, 31]. This action promotes the release of downstream sex steroids and leads to the simultaneous elevation of both IGF-1 and sex steroid levels. The observed inverse correlation between IGF-1 levels and cognitive performance is in line with findings in neurodevelopmental disorders, where elevated peripheral IGF-1 concentrations have been consistently reported in both major depressive disorder [32] and autism spectrum disorders [33] populations. However, this appears paradoxical considering current evidence suggesting that IGF-1 generally exerts neuroprotective effects within the central nervous system [34]. Thus, we hypothesize that the increased peripheral IGF-1 levels observed during rapid pubertal progression may represent a compensatory response to reduced cerebral IGF-1 bioavailability. This could potentially result from reduced sensitivity of central IGF-1 receptors under neuroinflammatory stress and may be associated with neural repair processes [35].

The observed reduction in cognitive performance among HPG+ adolescents aligns with contemporary neurodevelopmental models. These models suggest that deviations from typical maturational trajectories can disrupt the development of integrated brain networks essential for cognitive-emotional functioning. Specifically, normative adolescent development is characterized by increasing efficiency in network reconfiguration, such as in the reward system, which supports adaptive cognitive and affective processing [12]. However, this typical developmental progression can be attenuated by adverse experiences, highlighting the vulnerability of developing networks. Converging longitudinal research demonstrates specific sex-dependent pathways. In males, pubertal advancement is linked to the microstructural maturation of the fronto-accumbal white matter tract, which is linked to lower sensitivity to punishment [36]. Conversely, a declining trajectory of nucleus accumbens activation to reward, potentially indicative of blunted motivational salience, specifically predicts the later emergence of externalizing problems in adolescent boys [37]. These findings suggest that atypical development within subcortical reward and motivation circuits can alter reinforcement learning and value-based decision-making, processes that are foundational for adaptive cognition and behavior. In line with this, the coherence of the Executive Control Network (ECN), a neural substrate for cognitive control and emotion regulation, has been shown to play a protective role. Adolescents exhibiting higher ECN coherence appear buffered against stress-related increases in internalizing symptoms, even when experiencing early pubertal maturation [38]. However, evidence also indicates that more advanced pubertal staging is associated with reduced functional connectivity between brain regions involved in cognitive and emotional processing [39]. Our finding of an association between rapid pubertal tempo-related hypothalamic enlargement and poorer cognitive performance may reflect one specific pathway. Cognitive-emotional circuits may be affected through resource allocation trade-offs or altered network dynamics, which could contribute to increased vulnerability.

The observed volumetric increases in specific hypothalamic subunits (infTub, posHyp) and in the pituitary gland among HPG+ adolescents may reflect structural neuroendocrine adaptations to the demands of puberty. The enlargements of the infTub and supTub, which encompass key nuclei such as the arcuate (ARC), dorsomedial (DMN), ventromedial (VMN), and paraventricular (PVN) nuclei [27, 40], likely enhance kisspeptin (Kp) protein synthesis through IGF-1 receptor-mediated activation of the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway [41]. Kisspeptins are powerful stimulators of prepubertal GnRH, and an elevation in their levels can facilitate the progression of puberty [42]. Furthermore, these nuclei are also involved in the regulation of feeding and metabolism. The tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) neurons located in the arcuate nucleus (ARC) can enhance their projections to various nuclei within the hypothalamus and subsequently increase the release of orexigenic neuropeptides, thereby modulating feeding behavior [43, 44]. Although both human and animal studies consistently associate advanced pubertal status and early timing with an elevated risk of eating disorders [45], our data showed only a non-significant trend towards a higher BMI in the HPG+ group. Paradoxically, these structural modifications exhibited marked negative associations with cognitive performance metrics, suggesting potential neural resource competition between neuroendocrine optimization and higher-order cognitive processing. This concept is supported by large-scale genetic studies, which indicate that the hypothalamic structure is polygenic and pleiotropic, sharing genetic overlap with both cognitive functions (e.g., memory and learning) and social–emotional functions [26]. The genetic correlation between the hypothalamus and limbic/cognitive regions suggests an integrated circuitry in which structural adaptations prioritizing one set of functions (e.g., neuroendocrine activation) may come at the expense of others (e.g., cognitive processing efficiency), particularly under conditions of increased demand such as a rapid pubertal tempo.

Compared to HPG- adolescents, HPG+ adolescents exhibited a larger volume of the posHyp, a region that includes the mammillary bodies and the lateral-hypothalamic region. Although established literature suggests that enlargement in mammillary bodies could enhance memory and learning functions [46], we observed poorer cognitive performance in the HPG+ group. This discrepancy may stem from functional heterogeneity within posHyp subregions. While the medial mammillary bodies are involved in memory consolidation through inputs from the hippocampal fornix, lateral hypothalamic projections related to feeding-reward behaviors [47] may dominated during pubertal autonomic-endocrine changes. We propose that during rapid pubertal progression, structural adaptations in posHyp may prioritize neuroendocrine and metabolic functions over cognitive processes. Future neuroimaging studies should employ functional connectivity analyses to explore how these structural changes influenced network dynamics, particularly the interactions between neuroendocrine adaptation systems and cognitive neural circuits. While our findings elucidate a neuroendocrine pathway in adolescents with rapid progression, it remains an open question whether those with slower pubertal tempo would exhibit an opposite pattern. The relationship may be non-linear, with both faster and slower tempos potentially representing deviations from an optimal developmental window. Future longitudinal studies that recruit cohorts across the full pubertal tempo spectrum are needed to test these hypotheses and determine whether the structural changes we observed are specific to rapid pubertal progression or part of a broader plasticity mechanism.

Our findings further elucidate the interaction between IGF-1 signaling and alterations in the volume of hypothalamic subunits. The observed mediation effect, where elevated IGF-1 levels predicted an enlargement of the inTub subregion (Path A), and this enlargement subsequently correlated with poorer cognitive performance (Path B), indicates a structural-neuroendocrine pathway linking peripheral metabolic signals to cognitive outcomes. This aligns with preclinical evidence demonstrating the trophic effects of IGF-1 on hypothalamic neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity, particularly within the tuberal region that contains the arcuate nucleus (ARC), which is essential for GnRH regulation [1]. The seemingly contradictory association of IGF-1 elevation with both neuroendocrine activation and poorer cognitive outcomes may reflect a developmental trade-off. While IGF-1 promotes structural adaptations in nuclei that governs reproductive maturation (such as ARC-mediated GnRH pulsatility), excessive or inappropriate enlargement in these regions may disrupt extra-hypothalamic neuronal projections and functions that support higher cognitive processes [48]. Nevertheless, the hypothesized resource allocation mechanism also requires validation through longitudinal tracking of individual developmental trajectories or direct measurement of specific neurotransmitters.

This study suffers from several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limited our ability to further verify the hypothesized resource allocation mechanism. Longitudinal studies are urgently needed to elucidate how hypothalamic structural adaptations modulate network dynamics, particularly regarding competitive interactions between neuroendocrine adaptation systems and cognitive processing circuitry. Second, although sex-stratified analyses (male/female) would be methodologically ideal, the limited sample size in our research, particularly the low proportion of male participants, prevented us from conducting adequately powered subgroup analyses. Therefore, we carried out combined-sex analyses to maximize statistical power. Future studies with larger and sex-balanced cohorts should perform stratified analyses to explore potential neurodevelopmental dimorphisms, which could not be reliably evaluated in our dataset. Third, the control group consisted of participants without HPG axis activation. Due to the invasive nature of GnRH stimulation testing, we could not include typically developing adolescents of the same age. As a result, our findings may not be fully generalizable to a wider population. Fourth, our analysis mainly focused on the cognitive performance of adolescents with rapid pubertal progression. The cognitive deficits identified through the WISC-IV-Chinese need complementary behavioral assessments, emotional functioning assessments, and structured psychiatric evaluations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, IGF-1-driven remodeling of the inferior tuberal hypothalamus contributes to cognitive deficits during rapid pubertal progression, offering a novel insight into understanding how hypothalamic volume changes during puberty impact cognitive outcomes in this critical developmental stage.
