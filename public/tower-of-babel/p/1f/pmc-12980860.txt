Optimizing biomass production and antioxidant dynamics in Ceratophyllum demersum L. using multi-walled carbon nanotubes and machine learning models
Authors: Muhammad Tanveer Altaf, Muhammad Aasim, Seyid Amjad Ali, Ahmet Say, Sabeen Rehman Soomro, Salma Naimatullah Soomro, Meryem Demir, Waqas Liaqat, Farhan Aadil, Sabit Horoz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In vitro plant culture systems are widely used to investigate biomass production with physiological regulation, and can be benefitted by incorporating novel materials such as nanoparticles. Aquatic macrophytes are highly significant for sustainable biomass production, with optimization remains a major challenge. In this study, the combined impact of multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), sucrose (S), and Murashige and Skoog (MS) basal salt concentrations on plant biomass, pigmentation, and biochemical activities in Ceratophyllum demersum was evaluated. The experiment was designed using Box-Behnken Design (BBD) of response surface methodology (RSM). The optimization results were further predicted and validated using machine learning models, specifically the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) and Random Forest (RF) models, with six performance metrics, and the leave-one-out cross-validation technique. RSM analysis revealed a significant relationship between input parameters (MS basal salts, sucrose, and MWCNTs) and plant responses (growth and physiological traits). Among the testing ML models, the MLP model demonstrated superior predictive accuracy for several traits and varied for biochemical traits. Results illustrated the robust predictive capacity with R2 values of 0.99 for oxidative stress markers such as malondialdehyde and hydrogen peroxide. The integrated RSM-ML framework demonstrated robust predictive capacity of exidative markers and revealed distinct optimal conditions for growth and biochemical traits. The results provide valuable insights for studies conducted under in vitro conditions on how nanomaterials interact with nutritional and carbon sources for optimizing biomass production, physiological responses, and stress regulation in aquatic plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08441-w.

Introduction

Aquatic plants like coontail or hornwort (Ceratophyllum demersum) are highly significant for ecological and environmental implications due to oxygenation, carbon sequestration, and mitigating eutrophication. The plant also exhibits hyper-accumulative properties for a wide array of water-based pollutants, ranging from heavy metals to organic pollutants [1, 2]. Maintaining the general equilibrium and stability of aquatic environments by interacting with other organisms of the food web in freshwater ecosystems is highly significant for the resilience and dynamics of the ecosystem, and aquatic plants help in this regard. Other economic uses of plants include their use as a biological pesticide against stored grain pests [3], curing illness, diseases, and disorders [2, 4]. However, cleaner and safer production of plants is highly critical for their safer and sustainable uses.

In vitro propagation is the manipulation of different factors, ranging from plant to basal medium, carbon sources, and culture conditions [5]. The use of sucrose (S) in plant culture media is to provide carbon and nutrition [6] for developing favorable growth conditions [6, 7]. The transportation of these sugars (carbon) from the culture medium to plants causes the stimulation of the cellular differentiation process. However, supplementation of only carbohydrates in the culture medium is inadequate for achieving the necessary osmotic potential to enhance somatic embryos or organogenesis [8]; other nutritive additives are also significant for enhancing in vitro regeneration and biomass. The proper concentration of basal salts in the culture medium is also highly significant, especially when extra additives like nanoparticles (NPs) have also been incorporated in the culture medium, and it requires optimized concentrations for a targeted plant.

The NPs of different origins and characteristics are important additives in plant tissue culture, used generally for enhanced seed germination, sterilization, plant growth, yield, plant protection, and metabolite contents [9–11]. The other uses of NPs in the culture media include sterilization, an elicitor, or a precursor for enhancing secondary metabolites and inducing somaclonal variation [12]. Besides in vitro regeneration, NPs are also employed for genetic transformation and cryopreservation of elite plant cultivars [13]. However, to execute the real potential of NPs, there is a need to optimize the dose in a scientific way to boost the capacity for shoot multiplication, stress tolerance, plant biomass, or other specified targets [14]. The optimization of multiple input factors can be achieved by employing modern statistical tools like artificial intelligence (AI)-based machine learning (ML) models.

The Non-Linear Programming strategy provides superior levels of accuracy when compared to alternative methodologies, but this methodology has a laborious and complex problem-solving procedure [15]. Response surface methodology (RSM) is a statistical optimization and design technique of the design of experiment (DOE), used for investigating the relationship between two or more sets of variables [16] and also facilitates the optimization of multiple input factors [17] at the same time, with a minimum number of combinations. The central-composite experimental design with a full polynomial model yields an appropriate representation of most continuous response surfaces over a relatively broad factor domain [18]. On the contrary, Box-Behnken Design (BBD) is considered due to its ability to effectively handle both linear and non-linear interactions between both independent and dependent variables or responses [19]. In recent years, its use in plant and agricultural sciences has been employed for the production of intermediate metabolites, enzyme processes, and growth medium [20–22], predicting optimal conditions for in vitro regeneration and secondary metabolite production [22–24]. However, integrating RSM results with ML offers robust optimization and predictive accuracy of the input factors.

Integration of state-of-the-art AI and data analysis in the field of plant sciences is transforming the plant sciences, environmental sciences, phenomics, and plant genetics [24, 25]. These high-tech technologies enable the researchers to overcome the issue of multifactorial processes and noise in the data sets [25, 26]. Application of AI-driven data analysis in plant biotechnology, especially in the field of plant tissue culture, has been prevalent extensively in recent years [15, 26–28], especially in artificial neural networks (ANN) and decision tree-based models. The hybrid approach of RSM-ML modeling offers numerous advantages, such as optimization, and in this study, both approaches were employed for three different variables. Previous studies on multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) in plant systems primarily focused on a single factor, with a classical statistical approach for the interpretation of results [29]. Similarly, studies on in vitro culture with the use of NPs are also limited, especially when integrated with ML and modern statistical tools [30]. In this study, a novel approach of integrating nanotechnology, plant tissue culture, DOE, and AI-based ML modeling for investigating the impact on plant biomass and biochemical dynamics. The MWCNTs were prepared, followed by designing the experiment with DOE in combination with S and basal salts (MS). The data set attained after experiments was analyzed through RSM, followed by being subjected to ANN-based multilayer perceptron (MLP) and decision tree-based random forest (RF) models by six distinct performance indicators for validation and prediction. This combined approach will enable the researchers to investigate the trade-off between biomass and antioxidant response regulated by external additives. This study offers a more comprehensive and precise framework than single-variable or regression-based studies for nanoparticle-mediated plant tissue culture.

Synthesis of multi-walled carbon nanotubes

MWCNTs were synthesized by a chemical vapor deposition method using a horizontal tube furnace equipped with a quartz tube reactor. Iron (Fe) catalyst supported on alumina (Fe/Al2O3) was used as the catalyst, and 50 mg of the catalyst powder was placed in a quartz boat positioned at the center of the reactor. Before synthesis, the reactor was purged with argon (Ar) at a constant flow rate of 90 mL min−1 to remove residual air. The furnace temperature was then increased to 625 °C under continuous Ar flow. Once the temperature reached 625 °C, hydrogen (H2) was introduced at a flow rate of 15 mL min−1, while maintaining the Ar flow at 90 mL min−1, and the catalyst was reduced at 625 °C for 15 min. Following the reduction step, ethanol (C2H5OH) was introduced into the reactor as the carbon source using a bubbler system. The growth of MWCNTs was carried out at 625 °C under an Ar flow of 90 mL min−1 and a hydrogen flow of 15 mL min−1. After completion of the synthesis, the ethanol supply was stopped, and the reactor was cooled down to 200 °C under an Ar atmosphere to prevent oxidation. The system was subsequently cooled to room temperature under continuous Ar flow, and the synthesized MWCNTs were collected for further characterization.

Experimental setup

The experimental setup comprised three different factors, including S concentrations, basal salt concentrations (Murashige and Skoog—MS), and MWCNT concentrations. The concentrations of all three factors were generated by utilizing DOE via BBD of RSM, and a total of 20 different combinations were set as targets in this experiment (Table 1). The combinations of input factors were designed by providing a concentration ranging from zero to the maximum, based on prior in vitro aquatic plants regeneration studies and preliminary optimization. The selected minimum to maximum range was chosen to encompass optimal, sub-optimal, and supra-optimal physiological responses. The initial range of input factors to generate 20 combinations was 0 g/L to 5.5 g/L for MS, 1 g/L to 50 g/L S, and 0 g/L to 125 g/L MWCNT. Repeated central points (3.0 g/L MS, 25.50/L S, and 62.50 g/L MWCNT) suggested by the DOE were replicated multiple times to estimate pure experimental error, assess biological variability, and for reliable lack-of-fit testing. The graphical presentation of the RSM model is presented in Fig. 2. The liquid culture medium containing all three inputs, MS basal salt [31], S, and MWCNT were added before autoclaving, followed by automating the pH between 5.6 and 5.8. The in vitro regenerated C. demersum plants available in our lab were used as explant sources. These plants are available and are always maintained in a fresh culture to ensure a continuous supply of explants for experiments related to biomass production for phytoremediation studies [30] or other studies, such as biopesticides [3]. Nodal segments of 4–5 mm in length were used as explants with five explants per replication in the jars containing liquid culture medium. The jars were placed in a growth chamber regulated at a temperature of 24 ± 1 °C, relative humidity of 60 ± 5%, and an automated 16 h photoperiod utilizing white light emitting diodes (2000 Lux). The explants were cultured for 6 weeks, and data regarding plant biomass (fresh and dry weight), pigmentation [chlorophyll-a (chl-a), chlorophyll-b (chl-b), total chlorophyll (total chl), and carotenoid contents), and biochemical parameters, antioxidant and enzymes malondialdehyde (MDA), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide dismutase(SOD), catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD)]were computed.

Plant biomass

Fresh weight was determined by removing the plants from the culture medium, placing them on Whatman filter paper for 2 h to remove excess moisture, and then weighing them on a sensitive laboratory scale. The plantlets were then wrapped in aluminum foil and oven-dried at 70 °C for 72 h and weighed for dry weight. Both fresh and dry weights were calculated by replication due to extensive biomass growth.

Determination of chlorophyll and carotenoid content

The determination of the total chlorophyll content in plants and carotenoid content was performed according to the procedure, protocol, and equations documented earlier [32]. For this purpose, 200 mg of fresh leaf tissue was crushed in liquid nitrogen and then 5 ml of 80% acetone solution, pre-cooled at + 4 °C, and kept overnight at + 4 °C in the dark. Thereafter, the mixture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min with the temperature set at 10 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the absorbance at 663, 645, and 450 nm using a spectrophotometer was measured. Determination of hydrogen peroxide levels

For the quantification of H2O2, 0.5 g of plant tissue was homogenized in 5 ml of cold 0.1% trichloroacetic acid, followed by centrifugation at 10000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant (0.5 ml) was mixed with 0.5 ml of 10 mM KH2PO4 buffer (pH: 7.0) and 1 ml of 1 M KI. The absorbance value was measured at 390 nm, and the amount of H2O2 per gram was tabulated as μmolg−1.

Preparation of plant extracts

For determining the bioactivities from plant extracts, a modified procedure with some alterations was preferred [31]. The plant samples were powdered by using liquid nitrogen, followed by mixing 5 ml of a 70% ethanol/water mixture into 0.5 g of the sample. Samples were awaited overnight, followed by centrifuging at 10,000 g for 10 min. The mixture was homogenized in a shaking incubator at 25 °C, and the supernatant was shifted to the Eppendorf tubes for analysis.

Determination of antioxidant capacity with the phosphomolybdenum method

A modified phosphomolybdenum technique [32] was used to calculate the quantity of antioxidants. The reagent solution was initially produced up to 100 ml with distilled water after 28 mM sodium phosphate (28 ml), 4 mM ammonium molybdate (40 ml), and 0.6 M sulfuric acid (30 ml) were added. After combining a 0.4 ml plant sample with 4 ml of the reagent solution and vortexing, the mixture was incubated for 90 min at 95 °C in a water bath. Plant sample absorbance was measured at 695 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer. The ascorbic acid standard graph’s equation was used to compute the samples’ antioxidant activity, which was expressed as mg AAE/g of the sample.

Preparation of in vitro-grown Ceratophyllum plants extracts for measurement of antioxidant enzyme activities

The antioxidant enzyme activities of Peroxidase (SOD, POD, and CAT) were measured according to the previously reported protocols [33, 34] 0.5 g of plant samples were ground into a powder using liquid nitrogen, followed by adding 5 ml of cold homogenate buffer (0.1 M KH2PO4, pH:7.0 with 1 ml of ethylenediaminetetraacetic Acid (EDTA) and 1% PVP) and blended. The mixture was placed in a centrifuge tube and centrifuged for 15 min at 15,000 g and 4 °C. Next, the supernatant was cautiously moved to a different tube and kept at −20 °C, and utilized to assess the enzyme activities.

Determination of catalase enzyme activity

Using a spectrophotometer, the absorbance at 240 nm was monitored to determine the catalase activity during the breakdown of H2O2 into O2 and H2O [35]. The quantity of H2O2 that is reduced in the reaction will be ascertained using an H2O2 standard curve. The graph created using the acquired values was used as a reference, and its equation was used to calculate the concentration of H2O2 in µM. A combination of 40 mM H2O2 substrate solution, 25 µl enzyme extract, and 103 mM KH2PO4 buffer was produced for the analysis using 3 ml spectrophotometer quartz cuvettes. To record the results, the prepared solution was measured at 240 nm, and the absorbance decrease was then measured for three minutes at 0.5-min intervals against the control solution. The quantification of H2O2 (µM) was measured by recording the absorbance reduction per minute, followed by using a graph equation and averages. Thereafter, one enzyme unit (EU/g leaf) was defined as the enzyme value that reduced the absorbance by 1 µM in a minute [36].

Determination of peroxidase enzyme activity

The POD enzyme was quantified by adding guaiacol (5 mg) and H2O2 (5 ml) to 3 ml of a substrate solution having 0.1 M NaH2PO4 (pH 5.5). Thereafter, 10 µL enzyme extract was added and transferred to a quartz spectrophotometer cuvette. The absorbance was recorded at 470 nm [36] at one-minute intervals for five minutes. The amount of enzyme that increased absorbance by 0.01 in a minute was determined to be one enzyme unit [37].

Determination of superoxide dismutase enzyme activity

The reaction mixture of 50 mM KH2PO4 buffer (pH 7.8), 63 µM nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT), 13 mM methionine, and 0.1 mM EDTA was used for measuring the SOD activities. 2.58 ml of the mixture was transferred to a 3 ml cuvette and placed in front of a white light source to initiate the reaction. Thereafter, 30 µl of the sample was added to this combination, followed by adding 390 µl of a 13 µM riboflavin solution and mixing thoroughly. The SOD activity was measured at 560 nm, and enzyme units per sample (EU/g sample) were calculated as the quantity of enzyme that inhibited NBT reduction by 50% [38, 39].

Statistical analysis

The experiment was designed using the Minitab 21.0 statistical program by opting for RSM. The data generated was used for analysis by using response surface regression analysis. For optimization, results were also analyzed by Pareto charts, normal plots, and a response optimizer for the input–output relationship and optimizing appropriate conditions to produce enhanced biomass.

Model development, validation, and generalization

The original dataset generated by BBD of RSM, consisting of 20 different combinations with 3 replications (n = 60), was used for ML analysis. No synthetic data generation, resampling, or data augmentation techniques were employed. The input and output factors were the same as those used for response surface regression analysis (RSRA). The effectiveness of the employed algorithms was evaluated using the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV) method [42] due to a limited but structured dataset. In this technique, the dataset is split into two sections, and it uses a dataset with the same number of instances as folds. Every instance is individually put through the learning process, where the chosen instance is tested, and the remaining instances are used for training. Since each iteration uses more training samples, LOO-CV is better suited for small sample sizes and allows the model to understand the data more extensively. Hyperparameter optimization of both models was determined by utilizing a grid search to identify the ideal hyperparameters. The open-source Python programming language [43] and the Sklearn package [44] were used to assist with the coding process.

X-ray diffraction analysis of carbon nanotubes

The X-ray diffraction analysis of MWCNTs revealed three distinct peaks of 002, 100, and 004, and these peaks are highly significant for the crystal structures of nanotubes. These peaks represent the presence and characteristics of crystal planes and structures within the nanotube samples. The 002 peak is most significant, and it reflects the diameters and internal arrangements. The 100 peak reflects the fundamental structure of nanotubes, the core diameter, and the regularity of the graphene layers surrounding this core. The 004 peak controlled the longitudinally oriented structural features of the nanotubes, which express the length and arrangement of the nanotubes (Fig. 3a). Machine learning analysis

The predictive performance of the ML models was based on the data generated by BBD (n = 60). While high predictive accuracy was registered, the results are satisfactory and reliable due to the structured dataset. Therefore, a novel approach of LOO-CV allows us to interpret model performance within a structured DOE dataset rather than universal generalization. The results present a supportive and exploratory illustration under controlled in vitro conditions. ML models showed relatively higher R2 scores from the MLP model compared to the RF model for all output parameters (Table 3). Results of both models revealed the same R2 scores of 0.990 for both MDA and H2O2 contents. Similarly, relatively high R2 scores were recorded for plant biomass and plant pigments from the MLP model. On the contrary, higher SOD, CAT, and POD were recorded from the RF model. Discussion

Plant tissue culture is an important field of plant biotechnology for clonal propagation to attain plants with elite traits. It also allows researchers to investigate the impact of additives, other macromolecules, nanotubes, or NPs directly on plant germination, growth, and biochemical, physiological, and genomic processes, and even at the molecular level within cells [45]. These enzymes, pigments, and antioxidants are highly critical for plant growth [46, 47] in plant tissue culture, and act as a simple indication of stress, nutritional shortages, or external factors [48]. These outputs are generally analyzed by traditional statistical tools, but they often fail to optimize the input factors. Therefore, integrating ML models with modern computational models like DOE offers predictive modeling, pattern detection, and data-driven decision-making. This approach offers advantages over classical analysis by opting for multi-factorial optimization and predictive accuracy [49, 50].

Results on plant biomass exhibited a clear impact of sucrose concentration, and results revealed that Run 17 and Run 15 yielded more dry weight and fresh weight, respectively, indicating the variable impact of sucrose [49]. More dry weight by Run 17 was due to the provision of 2.0-fold more sucrose, which serves as a crucial energy source and signaling molecule in plants [50], stimulates photosynthesis, promoting the synthesis of carbohydrates and subsequently enhancing biomass production [51]. Whereas maximum and minimum chlorophyll contents were recorded from the Run 9 and Run 18, respectively, indicating the significance of MS and MWCNT concentrations that regulated the pigmentation under in vitro conditions [52]. The synergistic impact of MS basal salts, S, and MWCNT are likely to provide more nutrient availability and carbon skeleton supply required for pigment synthesis. Likewise, chlorophyll synthesis is also linked with enzymatic pathways and transcriptional regulation during growth and development [53]. Investigation on enzymatic activities exhibited the variable impact of media, with the highest from Run 1. The MDA and H2O2 contents were highest from the different media, but the minimum was from the same medium, indicating the impact of culture conditions [54].

The study highlighted the role of multiple additives on biomass accumulation, pigment contents, and antioxidant activity. Results revealed the significance of MS basal salts, S, and MWCNT on plant biomass due to carbon assimilation and nutrient availability. Whereas, divergence between fresh weight and dry weight revealed the differential water retention, which in turn regulated both biomass parameters. The pigment contents further confirmed the results with enhanced photosynthetic activity rather than stress-induced degradation. Conversely, elevated antioxidant enzymes and oxidative markers reflect the redox modulation rather than purely a growth response due to different combinations of additives. Results revealed that MWCNTs act as a mild elicitor at low concentrations, promoting antioxidant pathways. Whereas higher concentrations shifted the balance toward stress-induced oxidative regulation. The coordinated variations in pigmentation, antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, CAT), MDA, and H2O2 contents exhibited a dose-dependent modulation of oxidative metabolism [55]. Enhanced chlorophyll and carotenoids indicated enhanced photosynthetic efficiency, likely due to improved light harvesting and electron transport due to MWCNT. Whereas elevated antioxidant enzyme activities indicated the activation of the reactive oxygen speciesscavenging system. The rise of MDA and H2O2 contents at higher concentrations indicates that the buffering capacity of the antioxidant system of plants was surpassed, resulting in oxidative damage and lipid peroxidation rather than adaptive signaling [56]. Recent studies on carbon-based nanomaterials confirm the toxicity at higher levels by disrupting the membrane integrity and redox balance [57].

Regression analysis is an advantageous technique in plant in vitro investigations, providing significant benefits, particularly in determining the relevance of the overall performance of the model [58], especially for in vitro regeneration studies to determine the strength and direction of connections between variables [53]. The RSRA is a powerful tool and previously documented for optimizing multiple factors for in vitro plant growth and enzymatic characteristics. Results illustrated the significance of the output variable, as results showed that the MWCNT had no significant impact on fresh and dry weight. On the contrary, MS medium had a significant impact on fresh weight and dry weight. The impact of S and MWCNT revealed that biomass and enzyme are insignificant. The chl-a, chl-b, total chlorophyll, carotenoid, CAT, SOD, and POD, and biomass results revealed that S × MWCNT showed an insignificant impact, while a significant impact on MDA and H2O2. In general, the findings showed that several factors had varying effects, with statistically significant results for most of the parameters [48, 59].

The RSRA is a powerful statistical tool that is used to analyze the non-linear relationship between input parameters and offers multiple plots to analyze data in multi-dimensional directions. Results of R2-act. and R2-pred. exhibited relatively good reliability despite a significant lack-of-fit of parameters, such as carotenoids and SOD, showing weaker model performance. Whereas higher adequate precision scores confirmed the results with a reasonable signal for the RSM model, indicating a sufficient signal-to-noise ratio required for optimization. Results illustrated that the role of MS and MWCNT regulated antioxidant enzymes, while MS × S and S × MWCNT controlled the pigmentation. Results confirmed the impact of MWCNT on growth, pigmentation, and antioxidant activites unde in vitro conditions using MS basal salts [60, 61]. The normal plot analysis illustrates the relationship between factors by providing a visual representation of variance between the quantiles of a normal distribution and actual data by placing input factors on both sides of the line [32]. Results revealed the positive correlation between sucrose and dry weight, and vice versa for MS and MWCNT. Pigmentation patterns showed that AB combination maximizes pigmentation with concentration increase, while MWCNT concentration enhances it. A different pattern was recorded for antioxidant enzymes and BC concentration, which exhibited a positive impact. Whereas other output parameters were statistically insignificant, yet illustrated a positive relationship between input and output variables. It was concluded that the optimized combination by RSM can be beneficial for maintaining a nutrient-carbon balance for sustainable pigmentation and redox-regulated antioxidant responses [62]. In the next step, results were further confirmed by the heatmap-derived correlation analysis.

In statistics, correlation is used to assess the likelihood of a linear relationship between two continuous variables. It is quantified by using the correlation coefficient, a statistic that assesses the strength of the probable linear link between the two variables under investigation [63]. The correlation coefficient is a dimensionless number whose values are between −1 and + 1. Values close to 0 indicate that there does not exist any linear relationship between the two variables. The correlation closer to + 1 means a proportional and stronger relationship. Similarly, if the coefficient is negative, the variables have an inverse relationship, meaning that as one variable’s value increases, the other’s value tends to decrease. Heatmap analysis showed a clear correlation between input and output factors [55], indicating both positive and negative correlation.

ML plays an important role in problem-solving in the scientific field [56]. However, the success of precise prediction is based on choosing the right model, normalizing data, optimizing parameters, and validating models. ML is a subfield of AI and is redefining plant tissue culture by opting for regression-based models [28] compared to classification-based models. These algorithms can estimate the ideal nutrient combinations based on data analysis, encouraging cell development, minimizing contamination, and increasing tissue culture success rates [28, 29, 57]. Investigating the R2 scores and other metrics in plant tissue culture is highly significant for predicting the outcomes [26, 64]. In this study, high R2 scores with low scores of other performance metrics illustrated the improved reproducibility, viability, and overall performance under control conditions. Comparison of models reveals the better performance of the MLP model compared to the RF model. The better performance of the MLP model over the RF model might be due to the ability of the MLP model to capture complex, non-linear relationships and interactions through its deep learning architecture. Whereas the RF model might struggle with modeling intricate feature interactions due to noisy data, despite its robustness to handle complex data sets. Furthermore, MLP uses its multiple hidden layers and non-linear activation functions for data analysis and makes them effective for tasks involving physiological and biochemical traits prediction. The difference in their performance might be due to the limited data size, and MLP interpreted efficiently. However, both models are powerful, and enhanced biomass prediction through MLP and RF models can be used for maximizing productivity and efficient use of resources [65].

The integrated use of RSM and ML models offers a novel and balanced framework for interpretability, predictive power, validation, and optimization [32]. Although RSM facilitated the biological interpretation of interactions, it often fails to capture complex non-linear relationships due to predefined functional forms In contrast, ML models demonstrated higher predictive accuracy by learning a non-linear relationship directly from the data, especially by the MLP model Despite strong predictive performance, the study was framed with a limited dataset generated by DOE and performed under in vitro conditions. However, the use of LOO-CV reduced the risk of overfitting by using each dataset for training and testing [6]. Future work may extend model generalizability and biological interpretation under natural conditions, such as aquaculture, phytoremediation, or controlled bioreactors. The site-specific parameters can also be used to predict biomass yield and antioxidant responses based on a larger external dataset.

Summary and conclusion

This study integrates RSM and ML modeling to optimize in vitro culture conditions using MWCNTs, sucrose, and MS basal salts. This approach offered a predictive framework beyond the traditional single-variable approach and modeling. The predictive performance of the RSM model varied among biological parameters, reflecting the impact of all three variables on biomass production, pigmentation, and enzymatic activities of C. demersum. It was concluded that specific concentrations of these inputs led to a substantial increase in plant biomass (both fresh and dry biomass), and the most pronounced results were observed at higher MWCNT and sucrose levels. Pigmentation analysis showed elevated chlorophyll and carotenoid content, indicating improved photosynthetic efficiency. Enzymatic activity assays revealed heightened levels of antioxidants, enhancing stress tolerance. These observations highlight inherent limitations of the RSM model, where the second-order polynomial may not fully capture non-linear, threshold-dependent, or temporally regulated responses. The integration of AI-based models demonstrated high predictive accuracy in predicting biomass and enzymatic responses, complementing RSM by identifying optimal trade-offs between biomass production and oxidative stress responses. The developed framework can be deployed in other domains of aquatic plants for optimization, such as in vitro propagation, phytoremediation, aquaculture, and bioreactor systems for scalable biomass production. While the hybrid RSM-ML approach enhances predictive accuracy and multi-trait optimization, its effectiveness is limited by small sized dataset and validation under field conditions.
