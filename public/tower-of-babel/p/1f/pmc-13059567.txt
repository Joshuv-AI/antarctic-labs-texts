Multivariate analysis and machine learning prediction of Sorghum cultivar traits under nitrogen regulation
Authors: Muhammad Tanveer Altaf, Waqas Liaqat, Mehmet Bedir, Gönül Cömertpay, Seyid Amjad Ali, Muhammad Aasim, Muhammad Azhar Nadeem, Faheem Shehzad Baloch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenotypic differences in nitrogen use efficiency strongly influence sorghum growth and yield, highlighting the need for precise and reliable prediction of cultivar responses to nitrogen (N) availability. This study investigates the impact of two N treatments on sorghum cultivars, using artificial intelligence (AI) models for prediction.ResultsA randomized complete block design with two treatments: 0 kg N ha− 1 (0 N) and 238 kg N ha− 1 (238 N) was used. Six hybrid sorghum cultivars (Gustav, Estyphon, Foehn, Vegga, Aday1 and Beydarı) were evaluated for different traits. Statistical analysis included two-way ANOVA and factorial regression to assess treatment effects. Significant treatment effects were observed. Beydarı and Estyphon exhibited larger stem diameter and leaf area under 238 N, while Aday1 had the smallest values under 0 N. Gustav showed the highest panicle width, panicle weight, and grain yield under 238 N. Stomatal conductance showed an opposite trend, decreasing with N supplementation. Machine learning models, specifically Random Forest (RF) and Light Gradient-Boosting Machine (LightGBM), were used to model the interaction, achieving R2 values ranging from 0.759 to 0.966 for RF and 0.729 to 0.980 for LightGBM, indicating strong predictive accuracy.ConclusionLightGBM consistently achieved R2 values greater than 0.92 for key traits, such as stomatal conductance, panicle width, and grain yield, demonstrating its potential to optimize N management. Gustav performed best under high N, whereas cultivar responses to low N were genotype-specific, captured effectively by the machine learning models. These findings highlight the role of AI models in predicting cultivar performance and supporting sustainable agricultural decisions.Keywords: Chlorophyll fluorescence, SPAD, Stem diameter, Stomatal conductance, Light Gradient-Boosting Machine, Random Forest, Grain yield

Introduction

Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor L.) is an important cereal crop grown globally for food, fodder, and biofuel. It is particularly valued in arid and semi-arid regions due to its ability to tolerate drought and high temperatures [1, 2]. Globally, sorghum ranks as the fifth most widely grown cereal crop, with substantial production in regions such as Africa, Asia, and the Americas [3, 4]. It plays a crucial role in food security by providing essential energy and nutrients, particularly in areas with challenging climatic conditions [5, 6]. Nitrogen (N) is a key macronutrient governing plant growth and productivity through its role in photosynthesis, amino acid synthesis, and chlorophyll formation [7, 8]. In sorghum, adequate N supply enhances vegetative growth, biomass accumulation, and yield [9, 10], whereas insufficient N leads to stunted growth and reduced productivity. Conversely, excessive N application contributes to environmental pollution and reduces fertilizer-use efficiency [11, 12]. These challenges highlight the need for optimized N management strategies that balance productivity with environmental sustainability.

Improving nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) in sorghum is crucial for maximizing yield potential, especially under conditions of limited N availability. Sorghum genotypes have been evaluated for their ability to cope with low N supply and genetic diversity for NUE has been demonstrated [13, 14]. However, the mechanisms underlying NUE and the extent of variation across genotypes are still actively investigated [15]. N application significantly impacts various morpho-physiological traits, such as plant height, leaf area, and chlorophyll content [16, 17], which in turn affect assimilate partitioning and key yield components including panicle traits, grain weight, and total grain yield [7, 18, 19].

Conventional statistical approaches provide valuable insights into N-genotype interactions but may be limited in capturing complex, non-linear relationships among multiple traits. Response Surface Methodology (RSM), which integrates statistical and mathematical techniques, has been widely used to model treatment effects and identify optimal input levels [20–24]. However, recent advances in machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) offer powerful alternatives for modeling high-dimensional and non-linear biological data. However, recent advances in ML and AI offer powerful alternatives for modeling high-dimensional and non-linear biological data. In agricultural research, ML approaches have demonstrated strong predictive accuracy, robustness to complex interactions, and the ability to handle large datasets. These methods have been successfully applied to detect N deficiency, predict biomass and yield, classify genotypes, and estimate nutrient status across various crops, including sorghum, maize, canola, and cotton [25–32]. Such capabilities make ML particularly well suited for evaluating genotype-specific responses to N availability. This study aims to evaluate the responses of sorghum cultivars to nitrogen supply, focusing on key morpho-physiological traits and yield performance. It also aims to apply ML techniques to predict how N influences these traits.

Materials and methods

Site description and experimental design

The field trials were conducted at the Eastern Mediterranean Agricultural Research Institute, Adana-Türkiye on clay loam soil. The soil had an average organic matter content of 0.81%, a slightly alkaline pH of 7.84, an average lime content of 14.8%, and N content of 0.09%. The site represents the typical eastern Mediterranean climate, with hot and humid summers [33]. Six hybrid cultivars (Gustav, Estyphon, Foehn, Vegga, Aday1 and Beydarı) were used in this study. The experiment was conducted over two consecutive years (2022 and 2023), with sowing done on 10th of April in both years.

The experiment included two factors: nitrogen application at two levels (0 kg N ha− 1 (0 N) and 238 kg N ha− 1 (238 N) and six sorghum hybrids. The 238 kg N ha− 1 rate was selected based on local recommendations. The study was conducted using a randomized complete block design with a factorial arrangement of treatments and was replicated three times to account for spatial variability within the field. N was applied in two splits: half during seedbed preparation and the other half 40 days after sowing. Sowing was done manually, with 75 cm distance between rows and 10 cm between plants. Each subplot consisted of six rows, each 4 m long. Standard crop management practices (hoeing, insecticide application, and irrigation) were followed.

Data collection

Various agronomic and physiological traits were measured to assess the effect of 0 N and 238 N treatments. These included stem diameter, panicle width, panicle weight, grain yield, canopy temperature, stomatal conductance, chlorophyll fluorescence, and chlorophyll contents. Stem diameter (mm) was measured with a digital vernier caliper on ten tagged plants per subplot. Leaf area (cm2) was determined by measuring the largest length and width of the main panicle leaf in ten plants, using the formula: leaf area = 0.7 (L × W) [34]. Panicle width (cm) was measured at its widest section on ten selected plants, and the average was calculated. Panicle weight was recorded by weighing the un-threshed panicles from ten plants. Chlorophyll content was measured with a hand-held chlorophyll meter (Model: SPAD-502, Minolta Co., Japan) on the youngest fully expanded leaves of ten plants, with three measurements taken at 45, 65, and 85 days after sowing (DAS). Stomatal conductance was measured at 70 DAS between 11:00 and 14:00 under clear sky conditions using a portable leaf porometer (Model: AP4, Delta-T Devices Ltd, Cambridge, UK) on the youngest fully emerged, clean, dry leaves receiving sunlight. Canopy temperature (CT) was measured using an infrared thermometer (Model: IRTS-P, Apogee Instrument, Inc., Logan, UT, USA) between 11:00 and 14:00, ensuring dry and dew-free leaf surfaces. CT was measured three times: at 55 DAS (CT-1), 70 DAS (CT-2) and 85 DAS (CT-3). Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters including Y(II) (quantum yield of photosystem II) and Fv/Fm (quantum efficiency of photosystem II) were measured 50 days after sowing using a hand-held chlorophyll fluorescence meter (Model: OS1p, Opti-Sciences Inc., Hudson NH, USA) on the youngest fully expanded, leaves. Measurements were taken close to solar noon (11:00 h to 14:00 h). The leaves were consistently selected from the same part of the plant to reduce variation. For Fv/Fm measurement, leaf samples were dark-adapted for 30 min using clips before measurement to ensure proper adaptation of the photosystem II reaction centers. Grain yield was recorded as the weight of harvested grains per plot, expressed in kg ha− 1.

Statistical analysis

Minitab 20.4 statistical software was used to perform a two-way ANOVA on the input and output parameters. Tukey’s test was applied to determine the differences between means. Additionally, factorial regression analysis of all parameters was conducted using the same software. To optimize the results, a Pareto chart and response optimizer were used. Correlation and principal component analysis were computed using OriginPro 2024 software and Rstudio. Data from both years (2022 and 2023) were pooled for analysis, as the environmental conditions in both years were nearly identical. Given this, “Year” was not included as a factor in the statistical model.

Machine learning

The final dataset consisted of 72 experimental observations derived from six sorghum cultivars evaluated under two nitrogen levels (0 and 238 kg N ha− 1), with three replications over two growing seasons. Data from both years were pooled for analysis, as environmental conditions were comparable and year effects were not significant. Prior to model development, all numerical input variables were standardized to zero mean and unit variance to ensure comparable feature scaling. ML methods require hyperparameter tuning, either through grid or random search, to uncover the intricate and nonlinear behaviors of the predictive and predicted components. Grid search is computationally intensive and slow, as it exhaustively tests all possible combinations of parameters to find the optimal ones. In contrast, random search uses a fixed number of selected parameter alternatives to determine the best parameter combination. Although random search is faster than grid search, it is less likely to converge to the global optimum. Hyperparameter optimization was conducted using a grid search strategy embedded within the LOO-CV framework. For the Random Forest (RF) model, the number of trees, maximum tree depth, and minimum samples per leaf were tuned. For the Light Gradient-Boosting Machine (LightGBM), the number of boosting iterations, learning rate, maximum tree depth, and number of leaves were optimized. Optimal parameter combinations were selected by minimizing cross-validated root mean square error (RMSE). The same optimized parameter settings were consistently applied across all modeled traits to ensure reproducibility.

Additionally, the custom code utilized leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV) to make predictions and evaluate the model effectiveness [39]. Model evaluation was performed using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV). In this procedure, one observation was iteratively retained as the test set while the remaining observations were used for training, until all samples had been evaluated. LOO-CV was selected to maximize data usage and reduce estimation bias, which is particularly relevant for agronomic field experiments with limited sample sizes. Coefficient of determination

The R2 score, or coefficient of determination, is a critical statistical measure used in plant sciences to evaluate the predictive strength of regression models. A high R2 value indicates a strong correlation between the independent and dependent variables, aiding in the understanding of variability in plant-related data.

Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)

RMSE is a metric that quantifies the accuracy of a model’s predictions by calculating the difference between predicted and observed values. Lower RMSE values indicate better prediction performance.

Mean Absolute Error (MAE)

MAE is an important metric in plant sciences for evaluating predictive models by measuring the average magnitude of errors between predicted and observed values. It is commonly used in plant height estimation and disease monitoring.

Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE)

MAPE is a widely used metric in plant sciences for assessing forecasting accuracy, particularly for plant growth, yield, and environmental impact predictions.

Mean Least Square Error (MLSE)

MLSE is useful for optimizing plant-related models and improving forecasting precision, aiding in agricultural decision-making processes. It is particularly valuable for predicting plant responses to environmental factors, enhancing model reliability.

Median Absolute Error (MedAE)

MedAE is a robust metric for evaluating prediction accuracy, particularly in modeling plant responses to treatments and environmental conditions. It improves the reliability of predictive models and supports evidence-based decision-making in agriculture.

To evaluate the relative importance of the input parameters (i.e., N, genotype, and N x genotype), pareto charts were generated (Fig. 1, A-N). These charts indicate the level of significance of the factors on each trait. The central red line was recorded as 2.06, representing the threshold for significance. The Pareto analysis revealed that N had the most significant effects on the traits such as stem diameter, leaf area, SPAD-1, and SPAD-2. For panicle width, panicle weight, and grain yield, the order of significance was N > genotype > N x genotype, highlighting the dominant effect of N on these yield related traits. Meanwhile, the order of significance for SPAD-3 and CT-3 was N > N × genotype > genotype (A > AB > B). For stomatal conductance, CT-1, and Y(II), the order of significance was N > N × genotype > genotype (A > AB > B), which clearly emphasizes the role of N. Notably, the genotype × N was found to be the most significant factor after genotype for traits like stomatal conductance and CT-1. In contrast, for CT-2, genotype was the most significant factor, followed by N, with the order of significance being genotype > N × genotype > N (B > AB > A). Similarly, for Fv/m, genotype was the most influential factor, followed by N, with the order of significance genotype > N > N × genotype (B > A > AB).

Correlation and Principal component analysis (PCA)

Under N supplementation (238 N), the correlations matrix presented in Fig. 2 reveals strong positive relationships between key traits. Stem diameter showed a significant positive correlation with leaf area, and panicle width exhibited a strong positive correlation with both panicle weight and grain yield. Additionally, panicle weight was strongly correlated with grain yield, and grain yield showed a positive correlation with Y(II). These findings indicate that N supplementation promotes growth and improves photosynthetic efficiency, leading to increased yield potential. Conversely, under no N conditions (0 N), panicle weight showed a strong positive correlation with SPAD values (SPAD1, SPAD2, and SPAD3), indicating that chlorophyll content is closely linked to panicle development under N stress. Additionally, grain yield showed significant positive correlations with all SPAD values, reinforcing the idea that N availability influences both physiological and yield traits (Fig. 3).

PCA was conducted to assess the variation in sorghum genotypes under different N treatments. Under N conditions (238 N), the first three principal components (PC1, PC2, and PC3) accounted for 90.12% of the total variance, with PC1 explaining 39.9%, PC2 explaining 28.3%, and PC3 explaining 22% of the variation in the data (Fig. 4). The key discriminating traits contributing to PC1 were SPAD-2, SPAD-1, and SPAD-3, reflecting the chlorophyll content as a major factor in genotype differentiation under 238 N conditions. PC2 was influenced by Y(II), GY, panicle width, and panicle weight, suggesting that photosynthetic efficiency and yield-related traits are major contributors to the variation in sorghum genotypes under N supplementation. Under N-deficient conditions (0 N), the first three components explained 87.6% of the variance, with PC1 contributing 47.7%, PC2 contributing 26.4%, and PC3 contributing 13.5% (Fig. 5). PC1 was primarily influenced by panicle width, GY, SPAD-1, and panicle weight, suggesting that N deficiency has a significant impact on these traits.

Machine learning analysis

The results of two different ML models, i.e., RF and LightGBM are presented in Table 3. These models were evaluated using six performance metrics, providing a comprehensive assessment of model effectiveness across multiple traits. The R2 values showed close scores between the two models, indicating similar prediction accuracy for most traits. Specifically, stomatal conductance and Y(II) had nearly same R2 values for both models, suggesting strong predictive performance of these traits. However, slight differences were observed for certain traits. For example, LightGBM produced relatively high R2 scores for stem diameter, panicle width, and SPAD-2, while the RF model yielded higher R2 scores for leaf area and CT-2. These variations indicate the strengths of each model for specific traits, with LightGBM being more effective for leaf and stem-related traits, and RF providing better results for leaf area and CT measurements. Root mean square error results mirrored the pattern observed for R2. The LightGBM model exhibited lower RMSE values for stem diameter, panicle width, GY, SPAD-1, SPAD-2, and Y(II), suggesting that this model performed with greater accuracy for these traits. Conversely, the RF model demonstrated lower RMSE values for leaf area, panicle weight, SPAD-3, CT-1, CT-2, and CT-3 reflecting its superior prediction of these traits under varying N treatments.

Discussion

The increasing impact of climate change on agricultural productivity has led to the exploration of more resilient crops, such as sorghum, which offers significant potential in arid and semi-arid regions [40]. Recent studies emphasize the importance of genotype and N fertilization in optimizing sorghum yields [13, 41]. Nitrogen, a key component of essential biomolecules such as proteins, nucleic acids, and hormones, significantly influences plant growth and development [42]. However, imbalances in N application either insufficient or excessive, can lead to reduced crop yields and adverse environmental impacts, such as soil acidification and N2O emissions [43, 44]. This study investigates the role of N in sorghum cultivation, assessing the morpho-physiological, yield, and yield-related traits across different cultivars.

Nitrogen effects on morphological and yield traits

The present study indicates that N fertilization resulted in a significant increase in stem diameter. This is consistent with the findings of Almodares et al. [45] and Afzal et al. [46], who reported an increase in stem diameter with higher N application. Studies by Shehab et al. [47], Kathuli et al. [48], and Yönter et al. [49] also demonstrate that N enhances stem diameter in sorghum, likely due to the improved cell division and elongation facilitated by nitrogen. The higher N availability in this study may have promoted greater structural growth, enhancing stem support and contributing to improved nutrient and water transport, thereby supporting increased crop productivity. Leaf area, a key determinant of photosynthetic capacity, was significantly larger under 238 N conditions, especially in cultivars such as Beydarı and Estyphon. Nitrogen promotes cell division and protein synthesis, leading to expanded leaf area [50]. Previous studies observed that N application increased leaf area, thereby enhancing light interception and photosynthetic capacity [16, 17, 51–54]. Increased leaf area is often associated with improved growth and yield due to enhanced carbon assimilation and metabolic activity [55]. These results suggest that N application contributes significantly to vegetative growth by promoting leaf expansion.

Nitrogen application had a marked effect on grain yield, which increased from 2379.2 kg ha− 1 under 0 N to 3111.8 kg ha− 1 under 238 N. This increase aligns with the well-established relationship between N availability and grain yield. Studies by Amiri et al. [56] and Mousavi et al. [57] demonstrated that N fertilization enhances sorghum grain yield through improved photosynthetic activity and biomass accumulation. The significant yield improvement observed in our study corresponds with earlier research [10]. Recently, Thapa et al. [40] reported yield increases of 64.8%-81.6% with increasing N rates, while Ciftci et al. [58] reported higher grain yield and yield components with increasing N doses. The positive responses of stem diameter, leaf area, panicle traits, and grain yield to N application reflect nitrogen’s central role in enhancing photosynthetic capacity, assimilate production, and carbon allocation toward reproductive organs, thereby improving yield formation.

Nitrogen effects on physiological traits

Canopy temperature was highest under 0 N and lowest under 238 N consistent with the findings of Jiang et al. [59], who reported that N supplementation reduces CT by enhancing plant growth, especially leaf number and area. The increased leaf area facilitates better evaporative cooling, thus reducing CT under N-rich conditions. Lower CT is beneficial, as it can enhance photosynthetic efficiency and improve water use efficiency. SPAD values were significantly higher under 238 N indicating enhanced chlorophyll accumulation with increased N availability. Similar trends have been reported by Uchino et al. [60] and Wang et al. [61], confirming the positive role of N in chlorophyll synthesis and photosynthetic performance [62]. Increased photosynthesis generally requires more CO2 uptake, leading to higher stomatal conductance to facilitate gas exchange [63]. Studies have shown that N enhances stomatal conductance by regulating stomatal opening and ion balance [64]. However, the findings observed were not fully consistent with some previous studies [16, 65], suggesting that stomatal behavior under varying N conditions is highly context dependent. Such variability may arise from cultivar-specific stomatal regulation strategies, differences in N form and assimilation pathways, or environmental conditions during the measurement period (e.g., temperature and vapor pressure deficit), which can modulate stomatal behavior independently of leaf N status. Contradictory findings have also been reported across cereal species. For example, rice exhibits increased stomatal conductance under low inherent N levels [66], whereas wheat showed reduced stomatal conductance when ammonium is used as a source of nitrogen [67]. Similarly, Liu and Zhang [68] reported that high urea concentrations reduced stomatal conductance, transpiration rate, and net photosynthetic rate in woody plants. These observations underscore the complexity of nitrogen-stomatal interactions and highlight the importance of considering genotype x environment interactions when interpreting physiological indicators such as stomatal conductance.

Multivariate statistical analysis and factorial regression

The multivariate analysis in this study highlighted the complex interrelationships between morpho-physiological traits and grain yield. The strong positive correlations observed between stem diameter, leaf area, panicle weight, and grain yield under 238 N emphasize the direct influence of N on growth and yield potential. These findings align with the established understanding that N promotes vegetative growth by enhancing leaf expansion, which in turn improves photosynthetic capacity and nutrient assimilation [69, 70]. The significant correlation between grain yield and Y(II) further supports the role of N in enhancing photosynthetic efficiency and reproductive development. Furthermore, the PCA analysis revealed that under 238 N, SPAD was a major discriminating factor between genotypes, highlighting its importance in genotype differentiation under optimal N conditions. This highlights the crucial role of N in promoting photosynthetic pigment synthesis, which is essential for optimizing photosynthetic efficiency and growth. Conversely, under N-deficient conditions, panicle width, grain yield, and chlorophyll content contributed most to the variance, suggesting that N limitation disproportionately affects these traits. These PCA patterns corroborate the univariate results, indicating that yield variation under contrasting N conditions is associated with coordinated changes in key morpho-physiological traits rather than isolated responses.

Factorial Regression Analysis (FRA) is a statistical method that provides comprehensive insights, critical interaction detection, and flexibility in application across various fields. It offers multiple tools for understanding and optimizing independent variables for specific dependent variables. In this study, results based on P-values and R2 values revealed the significance of the model and input parameters. In general, individual inputs (linear) were highly significant for all studied traits. However, the interaction of N × G was insignificant for leaf area, SPAD-1, and SPAD-2. Results further revealed the maximum actual and predicted R2 scores with relatively low differences between them. Another advantage of FRA is the provision of different types of charts like the Pareto chart which allows the ranking of input parameters based on significance. The most significant parameters are placed on the top, while the least significant are at the bottom with a central line indicating the significance threshold. Results from the Pareto charts illustrated the significance of N on stem diameter, leaf area, panicle width, panicle weight, grain yield, SPAD-1, SPAD-2, SPAD-3, stomatal conductance, CT-1, CT-3, and Y(II), whereas genotype was a most significant factor for CT-2 and Fv/m. Pareto charts have been widely used in different studies [71–73].

Machine learning

ML models such as decision trees and neural networks, excel in understanding complex nonlinear interactions in real-world situations, often outperforming linear statistical models [72, 74]. A decision tree is a non-parametric, supervised learning technique that uses conditional control statements to model actions and their probable outcomes [37], making it particularly suitable for classification and regression applications [75]. The capacity of ML models to transform complex data into useful insights is crucial for scientific inquiry [76]. These models facilitate accurate predictions and reveal trends that may be overlooked by conventional analysis methods. Performance metrics like R2, RMSE, MAE, MAPE, MedAE, and MLSE are used to assess the precision, dependability, and resilience of these models [77], providing a consistent approach to evaluate and compare different models, ensuring the most suitable one is selected for a specific task. Researchers in plant sciences are using most of these metrics for making better predictions, and spur innovation by utilizing these cutting-edge analytical techniques [78–81] .

The study compares two different ML models across six performance metrics, revealing both notable differences and similarities in their predictive performance for different parameters. Their efficacy was demonstrated by the highly similar and consistent results that both models generated for most parameters. In general, LightGBM slightly outperformed the RF model in predicting leaf area and a few other traits, while LightGBM fared better for traits like stem diameter, panicle width, and measurements of SPAD values. The possible reasons for the performance might be due to using gradient boosting, building trees sequentially to correct errors in LightGBM, while RF uses bagging, building multiple trees independently, and smoothing predictions but not as effectively capturing complex patterns. LightGBM is a highly efficient and speedy training method that uses a histogram-based algorithm [37] for efficient data representation, reducing memory usage and speeding up the training process [37, 82] compared to RF model which enhanced the computation and memory requirements. The overall analysis demonstrated that each model has unique advantages, indicating that the best predicted accuracy in plant science research can be achieved by selecting a model that is customized to qualities.

The comparative performance of RF and LightGBM models can be partly explained by their underlying learning mechanisms. RF employs a bagging strategy in which multiple trees are trained independently and aggregated, providing robustness to noise and sampling variability but potentially limiting its ability to capture smooth, continuous response gradients. In contrast, LightGBM is based on gradient boosting, where trees are built sequentially to correct residual errors, enabling more efficient modeling of continuous physiological and yield-related traits. The slightly higher performance of LightGBM for traits such as stem diameter, SPAD indices, and grain yield is therefore likely attributable to its boosting framework rather than to fundamental differences in agronomic relevance. Importantly, the numerical differences between models were small and should not be interpreted as statistically or biologically decisive. Instead, the convergence of results across both algorithms supports the robustness of the underlying N response patterns identified through conventional statistical analyses. From a practical perspective, the developed ML models can serve as valuable decision-support tools for predicting cultivar performance under different N management strategies. By integrating genotype identity and N levels as input variables, these models enable rapid estimation of expected yield and physiological responses, eliminating the need for repeated field experimentation. This predictive capability can support cultivar recommendations, optimization of Napplication rates, and scenario-based evaluation of nitrogen efficiency, ultimately contributing to the development of sustainable, data-driven sorghum production systems.

Limitations of the study

This study was conducted with a limited number of sorghum cultivars and specific experimental conditions, which may not be fully representative of all agricultural systems. Despite the promising performance metrics obtained in this study, several important limitations must be recognized regarding the application of ML models. The final dataset comprised 72 observations derived from a controlled field experiment, which is relatively small for training flexible, nonparametric models such as RF and LightGBM. Although these algorithms are effective in capturing nonlinear relationships, their use with limited sample sizes increases the risk of optimistic performance estimates and reduces generalizability beyond the experimental domain. Model evaluation was performed using leave-one-out cross-validation to maximize data utilization, a common practice in agronomic studies with restricted replication. However, LOO-CV is known to exhibit high variance and may overestimate predictive accuracy in small datasets. Accordingly, the reported R2, RMSE, and related metrics should be interpreted as measures of internal consistency rather than as indicators of robust out-of-sample predictive capability. The ML models are therefore intended to complement, not replace, classical statistical analyses such as ANOVA and factorial regression, which form the primary basis for biological inference in this study. High coefficients of determination observed for certain traits largely reflect strong nitrogen-driven treatment effects already confirmed by conventional statistical methods. These values should not be construed as evidence of model applicability or universal predictive superiority. Future studies incorporating multi-location trials, additional nitrogen levels, and independent validation datasets are required to fully assess the transferability and robustness of the proposed models.

Conclusion

Nitrogen supplementation significantly improved yield and related traits in the sorghum cultivars, with Gustav and Beydarı showing the highest and lowest yields, respectively. The application of machine learning models, such as Random Forest and LightGBM, demonstrated strong predictive accuracy for both yield and morpho-physiological traits, showing promise as complementary tools for predicting agricultural outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of AI in optimizing nitrogen use and cultivar selection. However, while these models show promise, further research is needed to enhance their predictive accuracy and applicability across divers agricultural environment. Future work should consider incorporating additional variables, such as soil quality and climatic conditions, to improve model robustness and broaden their use in precision agriculture. By doing so, these models can help increase nitrogen use efficiency, reduce waste, and contribute to more sustainable farming practices. Ultimately, the integration of AI-driven tools in agriculture has the potential to improve resource management and foster a more resilient and ecologically sustainable agricultural future.
