Stroke in Patients Undergoing Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement: Embracing the Unpredictable
Authors: Ahmed Elkaryoni, Marwan Saad
Published: 2024

Post‐transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) stroke is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. 1 In a prior analysis, post‐TAVR stroke was associated with a 3‐fold increase in 30‐day mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 3.17 [95% CI, 2.87–3.49]), and the risk persisted in those who survived up to 5 years after TAVR (aHR, 1.18 [95% CI, 1.10–1.20]). 1 In addition, post‐TAVR stroke was associated with an increased risk of all‐cause readmission and recurrent stroke at 1 year. 1 The Valve Academic Research Consortium (VARC)‐3 2 has categorized post‐TAVR stroke into (1) periprocedural stroke: ≤30 days after the index procedure, which is subsequently classified into acute stroke (≤24 hours) and subacute stroke (>24 hours and ≤30 days); (2) early stroke: >30 days and ≤1 year after the index procedure; and (3) late stroke >1 year after the index procedure.

With the continuous efforts to improve outcomes with TAVR over the past 2 decades, multiple studies explored the temporal trends of post‐TAVR stroke. Results from the STS/ACC TVT (Society of Thoracic Surgeons/American College of Cardiology Transcatheter Valve Therapy) Registry between 2011 and 2019 showed a reduction in the incidence of in‐hospital stroke from 2.1% to 1.6% and 30‐day stroke from 2.75% to 2.3%. 3 Similarly, in a meta‐analysis of TAVR landmark clinical trials, the rate of periprocedural stroke declined with next‐generation versus first‐generation TAVR (2.09±2.93 versus 2.73±2.49%, P=0.01). 4

In the same vein, and in this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), van Nieuwkerk and colleagues examined the incidence, temporal trends, predictors, and outcomes of cerebrovascular events following transfemoral TAVR, using pooled patient‐level data from 10 clinical studies with a period spanning between 2007 and 2022. 5 The main findings were (1) 30‐day stroke after TAVR occurred in 2.2% of patients, half of which occurred in the first 24 hours, and almost 60% were fatal or disabling; (2) the incidence of 30‐day stroke after TAVR was similar across the study years and was not predicted by the patient's Society of Thoracic Surgeons‐Predicted Risk of Mortality risk category; and (3) 30‐day stroke after TAVR was associated with ≈3‐fold increase in 1‐year mortality (44% versus 15%) compared with patients without stroke at 30 days, and such high mortality rate did not change between 2007 and 2022.

Although the study results by van Nieuwkerk and colleagues align with prior reports of post‐TAVR stroke, it comes with important advantages including the large number of patients, being a real‐world cohort, and spanning a period of 15 years. Despite the observational nature of the study and the lack of adjudication for outcomes, the study provides important knowledge to physicians and patients.

The battle to mitigate the risk of stroke after TAVR is not an easy one to win, and the current study has proven this. After 2 decades, and despite significant improvement in TAVR procedures, including better transcatheter heart valve designs, cerebral embolic protection (CEP) devices, and operators' experience with adopting minimalistic transfemoral TAVR, the rate of post‐TAVR stroke remains largely unchanged. Although the incidence is low, around 2% to 3% in most studies, outcomes are poor. The main challenge in reducing the rate of stroke with TAVR lies in its diverse causes and lack of a precise tool to predict its risk. The timing of stroke after TAVR can provide some insight into the underlying mechanism of the neurological event. Periprocedural (acute and subacute) strokes after TAVR are thought to be embolic and directly related to the procedure, for example (1) disruption of atherosclerotic or calcific plaques, (2) interaction of transcatheter heart valve or balloon with calcified native or bioprosthetic aortic valve during crossing or valve deployment, or (3) thrombus formation related to subtherapeutic systemic anticoagulation or vascular/native valve endothelial injury. These mechanisms were suggested as histological examination of debris caught by CEP devices showed predominantly calcium, fibrin, and connective tissue materials. 6 Despite the promising hypothesis behind CEP devices, the Sentinel CEP device (Boston Scientific, USA) failed to achieve its primary endpoint of reducing all stroke with TAVR in the PROTECTED TAVR (Stroke Protection With Sentinel During Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement) trial but showed reduction in a secondary underpowered endpoint, disabling stroke. 7 Unfortunately, given its observational nature, the current study lacked important information about the use of CEP, which could have shed more light on the impact of CEP devices on stroke outcomes given the much larger cohort.

Strokes beyond the first few days of TAVR could be secondary to valve‐related (eg, valve thrombosis) or patient‐related causes (eg, atrial fibrillation, carotid artery disease, etc.). 2 Multiple studies have shown no significant role for anticoagulation or dual antiplatelet therapy in reducing clinical stroke and potentially increasing the risk of bleeding, compared with aspirin alone after TAVR. 8 , 9 Accordingly, the most recent American and European guidelines did not endorse the use of anticoagulants or dual antiplatelets after TAVR if not otherwise indicated (lifelong single antiplatelet is Class I in the European guidelines, and IIa in the American guidelines). 10 , 11 Furthermore, a prior analysis demonstrated comparable rate of late stroke (beyond 2 years) in patients who underwent TAVR versus age‐ and sex‐matched populations. 12 In the current analysis, history of cerebrovascular events was a predictor of post‐TAVR stroke beyond 24 hours, and CHA2DS2‐VASc score was significantly higher in patients who experienced 30‐day stroke after TAVR versus not. Strict risk factor control and ruling out other causes of stroke, such as atrial fibrillation, in those patients are of utmost importance.

Although “prevention is better than cure,” stroke with TAVR remains unpredictable and largely unavoidable, and identifying effective management strategies in those patients may be our best bet until better risk‐prediction models and prevention strategies are available. Although there is a lack of consensus about the optimal management of these patients, 13 the current advancement in the field of endovascular interventions to treat acute ischemic stroke 14 presents an attractive modality to manage this high‐risk group of patients. 15 , 16
