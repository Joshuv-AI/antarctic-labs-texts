Structural equation modeling analysis of factors influencing decisional conflict between dialysis modality among end-stage kidney disease patients in Wuhan
Authors: Shiyi Zhang, Jinrui Cui, Xiaoqin Liu, Xifei He, Yulin Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore the influencing factors and relationships associated with decisional conflict of dialysis modality in End-stage kidney disease (ESKD) patients.MethodsThis study was a survey-based cross-sectional investigation conducted on 150 ESKD patients in a third-class hospital in Wuhan. The general information questionnaire, decisional conflict scale, Montreal cognitive assessment, frail scale, perceived social support scale, and brief health literacy screen were used for investigation. SPSS 25.0 was used to compare the differences between the decisional and non-decisional conflict groups, and AMOS 23.0 was used to construct a structural equation model to explore the influencing factors.ResultsThe incidence of decisional conflict in 150 ESKD patients was 33.3% (50/150). Binary logistic regression analysis showed that the independent risk factors for decisional conflict of dialysis modality in ESKD patients included monthly household income (OR = 0.184), cognitive function (OR = 7.0), social support (OR = 0.891), health literacy (OR = 0.608), the level of eGFR (OR = 1.488), and the level of cTnI (OR = 9.558). The constructed path analysis model had a good fit (x2/df = 1.499, GFI = 0.957, AGFI = 0.911, NFI = 0.906, CFI = 0.967, RMSEA = 0.055). The path analysis showed that health literacy (0.577) had the greatest impact on the decisional conflict, with a direct effect of 0.480 and an indirect effect of 0.097 through cognitive function and monthly household income. Next was social support, with an effect value of 0.434.ConclusionsIn clinical practice, it is important to enhance the health literacy of patients and their families and to provide advanced education on dialysis plans. Additionally, in managing and planning chronic kidney disease progression and dialysis, it is recommended to regularly and systematically assess cognitive function, particularly before the patient’s cognitive impairment worsens or the severity of the disease progresses. Advanced care planning can be established through collaboration between healthcare professionals and patients to ensure appropriate decision-making and management.Implications for the profession and patient careThis paper finds that the factors that influence and relate to dialysis methods in end-stage renal disease patients help nurses exercise autonomy better, assist patients in reducing their decisional conflict, and improve clinical outcomes.Patient or public contributionPatients received a relevant questionnaire survey, and caregivers assisted in conducting the study.Keywords: Decisional conflict, End-stage kidney disease, Dialysis modality, Influencing factors, Path analysis, Nursing

Introduction

End-stage kidney disease (ESKD) is associated with significant mortality, morbidity, and impaired quality of life [1]. Globally, the incidence of ESKD continues to rise [2]. A national epidemiological study in China showed an increasing incidence of chronic kidney disease (CKD), with the prevalence of progression to end-stage renal disease reaching 442.13 per million [3]. The progression from chronic kidney disease to end-stage renal disease poses significant challenges for patients, most of whom approaching ESKD require renal replacement therapy to sustain life. Haemodialysis (HD) and peritoneal dialysis (PD) are the two primary renal replacement therapies for patients with ESKD [4]. According to the China Kidney Disease Data Network report, CKD patients are characterized by a “high prevalence and low awareness rate,” with 91% of ESKD patients choosing hemodialysis and only 9% undergoing peritoneal dialysis [5]. Although clinical practice guidelines advocate the use of shared decision-making (SDM) in patients with end-stage kidney disease, providing treatment modality information and assisting in clarifying their values and preferences aims to facilitate informed decision-making. However, it is concerning that 40% of patients continue to make choices without certainty regarding their long-term prognosis [6–9]. This suggests that patient goals, values, and preferences are not sufficiently considered during decision-making. Furthermore, medical personnel, often adhering to a paternalistic ideology, dominate decision-making communication, resulting in limited patient participation and a passive approach to decision-making [10, 11].

North American Nursing Association defines decisional conflict as uncertainty about actions to prevent risk, loss, or challenges to individual values [12]. Different renal replacement treatment modalities have their advantages and disadvantages. Survival comparisons between both modalities do not appreciably differ. The majority of ESKD patients are medically fit for either HD or PD. Peritoneal dialysis minimally influences patients’ lifestyles; however, it requires proficient operational skills and a suitable environment, as improper execution may result in complications such as peritonitis and catheter wound infection [13]. Conversely, hemodialysis, administered by healthcare professionals, offers a relatively secure procedure with a high dialysis clearance rate.

Nonetheless, it can restrict patients’ work and life schedules, diminishing their sense of life control and overall happiness. However, due to the lack of dialysis preparation and heavy treatment burden, patients face uncertainty in choosing a dialysis modality, leading to decision-making conflict. This conflict occurs during decision-making and continues until after treatment decisions are made. Inconsistent patient preferences and actual decisions lead to decreased patient treatment compliance, quality of life, and even unnecessary doctor-patient conflicts, ultimately negatively impacting medical outcomes [14, 15].

There is limited research on the determinants influencing decision conflict in dialysis choice among ESKD patients. Several socioeconomic elements, including neighborhood poverty, employment, insurance, and educational attainment, impact ESKD patients’ progression through decisional conflicts. A small sample report in the US indicated that individuals with lower decision self-efficacy, no predialysis education, lower dialysis knowledge, and perceived lower professional support reported higher decisional conflict when choosing a dialysis modality [16]. Other studies have linked male gender, age (45 or older), low self-efficacy, knowledge, social support, and emotional quality of life (QOL) to higher decisional conflict [17]. Singapore scholar Zack [18] found that the overall prevalence of severe decisional conflict was 27.5% (n = 190), with only the health literacy domains of Functional, Communicative, Critical (FCCHL), and Health Literacy Questionnaire (HLQ) Active Engagement remaining significant in the multivariable model.

Studies have shown that support from family, friends, or healthcare providers is crucial for patients in deciding on dialysis modality [19]. Additional research has demonstrated a positive correlation between CKD and an increased susceptibility to cognitive dysfunction, with the severity of kidney disease exacerbating this association [20]. Specifically, investigations have revealed that cognitive dysfunction among end-stage renal disease patients can reach levels as high as 27–67% [21]. In 2018, the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) conference proposed changing the term ‘adequate dialysis’ to ‘goal-directed dialysis.’ This term specifically refers to the use of shared decision-making between the patient and the care team to set realistic care goals, enabling the patient to achieve their life goals and the clinician to provide individualized, high-quality dialysis care. Depending on the patient’s cognitive function, health literacy, numeracy, socioeconomic status, and initial dialysis experience, goal-directed dialysis may need to be tailored. However, validating other associated factors, such as cognitive function, within the context of CKD remains unestablished. It is essential to acknowledge that these factors are inevitable and can potentially impact the decision-making process regarding the choice of dialysis modality in patients with ESKD.

Previous studies on decision conflict of dialysis modality in ESKD patients have mainly concentrated in Southeast Asian countries and the United States. However, due to differences in financial, cultural, educational, and health systems, the use of this modality varies considerably across countries [22]. Despite health literacy, cognition, frailty, and social support being associated with decisional conflict outcomes, their inter-relationships remain unclear, as prior work has only examined these factors in isolation. This study aimed to conduct a cross-sectional investigation into the prevalence of decisional conflict regarding dialysis modality in ESKD patients. According to the Ottawa Decision Support Framework, the factors influencing decision-making are determined by its foundation and structure, namely, decision-making needs. Decision-making needs include the following aspects: lack of necessary knowledge (health literacy), support and resources (social support, income), and personal/clinical needs. The Health Belief Model emphasizes that individuals’ perceptions and beliefs about health issues can affect their decisions to adopt health-related behaviors. Economic resources may affect an individual’s ability to access health information and medical services, known as health literacy, and individuals with high health literacy typically have more vital cognitive skills to process and understand health information more effectively, thereby reducing decision-making conflicts. Therefore, this study combines the Ottawa Decision Support Framework and the Health Belief Model to construct Structural Equation Model 1. We utilized path analysis modeling to analyze the factors influencing this conflict. The aim was to develop tailored decision-making assistance plans that can serve as a reference for improving these patients’ clinical decision-making challenges. Additionally, these plans aim to facilitate shared decision-making among medical staff, patients, and their family members, ultimately leading to improved patient health outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study recruited patients from the nephrology department in a tertiary-level general hospital in Wuhan. The study was approved by the hospital’s research ethics board (Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology). The results are reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Participants

The study population comprised patients admitted between May 2023 and August 2023. Inclusion criteria included: (1) diagnosis of Stage 4 or 5 CKD; (2) suitability for peritoneal dialysis and hemodialysis treatment; (3) having made a dialysis mode decision within the past six months, with either hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis selected; and (4) informed consent provided by each patient. Exclusion criteria were: (1) kidney transplant status or a history of kidney transplant; (2) presence of obvious mental illnesses, psychological illnesses, intellectual disabilities, preventing cooperation in completing the survey questionnaire; and (3) contraindications to peritoneal dialysis, such as colostomy, nephrostomy, abdominal trauma or surgery, intestinal obstruction, or contraindications to hemodialysis, such as severe shock, heart failure, and active bleeding.

Given that sixteen independent variables were included in the logistic regression analysis, accounting for five to ten times the number of independent variables (while also considering 20% potentially invalid data), the minimum sample size for the logistic regression analysis is 100 samples. According to the study conducted by Wolf [23], using structural equation modeling, the sample size is calculated based on the estimation of free parameters in the model, with a requirement of 5 to 10 observations per free parameter. Since we had 12 free parameters in the model, a sample size of 60–120 is needed. Hence, considering potential attrition, we increased the sample size to 157 individuals. After excluding incomplete patients, we included 150 patients in the final analysis.

Data collection and management

The study questionnaires included questions on demographics (age, gender, body mass index, marital status, educational level, work status, monthly household income, living site, alcohol consumption history, smoking history, and childbearing history) and laboratory assay values (estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate, blood creatinine, hemoglobin, Cardiac troponin I), as well as the Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS), the Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS), the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), the FRAIL Scale, and the Brief Health Literacy Screen. Laboratory assay values were abstracted from medical records. The electronic “Questionnaire Star” tool (https://www.wjx.cn/) was used to distribute questionnaires and collect participant data.

Instruments

Decisional conflict scale (DCS)

The DCS measured participants’ decisional conflict in choosing dialysis modalities. The DCS consists of 16 items across five subscales: uncertainty (e.g., ‘I am clear about the best choice for me’), feeling informed (e.g., ‘I know which options are available to me’), clarity related to personal values (e.g., ‘I am clear about which benefits matter most to me), feeling supported (e.g., ‘I have enough support from others to make a choice), and effective decision-making (e.g., ‘I feel I have made an informed choice). Participants responded on a 5-point Likert-type scale (0 = strongly agree to 4 = strongly disagree). Total DCS scores and subscale scores were calculated according to the DCS manual. Total scores (0–64) were converted to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating higher decisional conflict. Scores below 25 were associated with the ability to implement a decision. The established cut-off of 37.5 was used to identify severe decisional conflict [24]. The DCS has been validated in several languages, including Mandarin [25], a key consideration for this study, and showed good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.91).

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)

The MoCA is a brief cognitive screening tool for mild cognitive impairment. The maximum total score is 30 points, with a 1-point correction for individuals with 12 years of education or less. A score of 26 or above is considered normal, while a score lower than 26 is suggested as the optimal cut-off for diagnosing cognitive impairment. The MoCA test assesses a range of cognitive domains, including (a) Visuospatial/executive (5 points); (b) Naming (3 points); (c) Memory: Immediate (no points); (d) Attention (6 points); (e) Language (3 points); (f) Abstraction (2 points); (g) Memory: delayed recall (5 points); (h) Orientation (6 points). The MoCA has excellent test-retest reliability (correlation coefficient = 0.92, p < 0.001) and adequate internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α of 0.83 [26].

FRAIL scale

Frailty was assessed using the FRAIL scale, which includes five components: Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illness, and Weight Loss. The presence of each characteristic scores 1, and absence scores 0. The FRAIL score ranges from 0 to 5, with scores of 0 being robust, 1–2 pre-frail, and 3–5 frail [27].

Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS)

The Perceived Social Support Scale measures social support with 12 items across three dimensions: family support, friend support, and other support [28]. Each item is scored from 1 to 7 (7 levels from strongly agree to disagree strongly), with a score range of 12 to 84 points. Higher scores indicate higher levels of social support. This scale is widely used nationally and internationally and has good reliability.

Brief Health Literacy Screen (BHLS)

Developed by Chew in the United States, the Brief Health Literacy Screen (BHLS) contains only three questions, taking 1–2 min to complete. It has been confirmed in the United States, France, Germany, and other countries for quickly screening people with insufficient health literacy in clinical settings. Xue rendered the scale in Chinese, and empirical analysis demonstrated that the translated version exhibited robust reliability and validity. A BHLS score ≤ 9 suggests inadequate health literacy, while a score ≤ 11 indicates limited health literacy [29].

Statistical methods

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 25.0 (IBM SPSS, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive analyses were performed to present continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) and categorical variables as percentages. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test examined whether the continuous variables followed a normal distribution. The factors affecting decisional conflict were analyzed using binary logistic regression and path analysis to explore the internal relationships between variables. The model was analyzed using AMOS 23.0, and the path analysis diagram was drawn. The final path analysis model was obtained through continuous correction of the model fitting degree. The fit of the multivariate logistic regression models was assessed using Hosmer-Lemeshow tests, with a p-value > 0.05 indicating a good fit.

Results

Demographics and descriptive statistics

A total of 159 ESKD patients were initially enrolled in this study between May 2023 and August 2023. After processing and eliminating missing values, outliers, and invalid variables, 150 patients were included in the final analysis, comprising 50 patients with Decisional Conflict and 100 patients who did not have Decisional Conflict (Table 1). The total score of decisional conflict was 33.0 ± 17.3, which was at a middle level. Of the participants, 64 (42.7%) were aged 18–44 years, 51 (34%) were 45–59 years old, and 35 (23.3%) were older than 59 years. A total of 58.7% of the participants were male. About 24.7% had a bachelor’s degree or above; most were urban residents, with rural residents accounting for 45.3%. Only 22% of the participants had a monthly family income of more than 6000 yuan.

A binary logistic regression model was used to analyze the factors influencing decisional conflict, with the presence or absence of decisional conflict as the binary dependent variable (no decisional conflict was coded as 0, and decisional conflict as 1). Factors with p < 0.05 in the univariate analyses were included in the binary logistic regression model, as shown in Table 2. Monthly household income, MoCA score (OR = 7.0), PSSS score (OR = 0.891), the level of eGFR (OR = 1.488), and the level of cTnI (OR = 9.558) were all significant influencing factors for decisional conflict in ESKD patients (p < 0.05).

Discussion

With the increasing popularity of shared decision-making and protecting patients’ rights and interests, medical institutions encourage patients to participate and pay attention to patients’ attitudes and views on medical care decision-making. Decisional conflict is one of the problems in this process. In this study, the total score of decisional conflict was 33.0 ± 17.3, with 33.4% of patients in the high level, which is slightly higher than international results.

Many studies on decisional conflict in ESKD patients have shown that various factors affect decisional conflict, and different countries and regions within the same country have different influencing factors. According to the absolute value of the path coefficient, the factors affecting decisional conflict were ranked in descending order of influence: health literacy > social support > monthly family income > eGFR > cTnI > cognitive function. According to the SEM, health literacy can independently influence decisional conflict and indirectly affect it. The total effect coefficient of health literacy is 0.577, making it the most significant factor influencing decisional conflict. The World Health Organization defines health literacy as an individual’s ability to seek, understand, and use health-related information to promote and maintain health [30]. This literacy plays a pivotal role, both directly and indirectly, in reducing decisional conflict. The decision-making process is inherently tied to a patient’s perception of their illness and their level of health literacy [31]. Specific health literacy skills, such as evaluating information and interaction/communication, have shown significant correlations with clinical outcomes [32]. These skills are crucial for patient engagement in care and decision-making processes [33]. The Logistic regression analysis further corroborates that higher levels of health literacy are associated with a lower risk of decisional conflict (OR = 0.608, P < 0.05). This underscores the necessity to go beyond merely disseminating health knowledge and emphasize cultivating individuals’ health literacy, encompassing information acquisition, critical thinking, and decision-making abilities, to mitigate decisional conflicts and foster more informed health decisions among the public.

This study found that health literacy indirectly influences decisional conflict by affecting cognitive function. Patients with high health literacy are often more likely to have good work experience, health care awareness, etc., which are protective factors for cognitive function [34]. In controlled neuropsychological assessments, individuals with lower health literacy are more prone to experience negative psychological states, such as tension, which subsequently influence test outcomes and contribute to lower scores on the MoCA assessment. To address this issue, nursing personnel can motivate patients with limited educational backgrounds to engage in activities that enhance their cognitive reserve, such as reading, listening to music, and participating in physical exercise [35]. Additionally, it is crucial to provide comprehensive information to these patients prior to cognitive function evaluations to mitigate the impact of their emotional fluctuations on test results.

Additionally, cognition exerts a direct positive influence on decisional conflict. In this study, our results from the logistic binary regression model showed that the incidence of decisional conflict in ESKD patients with cognitive impairment was seven times higher than in those without cognitive impairment. Studies have demonstrated [36–38] that CKD increases the risk of cognitive dysfunction, and it is related to the severity of kidney disease. In particular, the overall prevalence of cognitive dysfunction in ESKD patients is as high as 27–67% [39]. Therefore, it is recommended that healthcare professionals regularly and systematically assess the cognitive function of the ESKD population, particularly before the deterioration of cognitive impairment or progression of disease severity, and tailor communication strategies based on their health literacy and cognitive abilities. Patients with cognitive impairment often cannot make choices for themselves. Family involvement in medical decision-making and symptom assessment should also be recognized. This suggests that we could mitigate decisional conflict by establishing proactive care planning through collaboration between healthcare professionals and patients, or by facilitating the decision-making process for surrogate decision-makers in cases of cognitive impairment.

The results of this study indicate that eGFR has a direct negative predictive effect on decisional conflict. The severity of kidney disease is primarily assessed based on the estimated eGFR derived from serum creatinine. Patients with higher eGFR may lack a comprehensive understanding of the severity of their disease, treatment options, and long-term implications. This cognitive insufficiency can lead to confusion and uncertainty when faced with multiple treatment options, thereby exacerbating decisional conflict [40]. Furthermore, cTnI has a direct positive predictive effect on decisional conflict and can indirectly influence it by affecting cognition. The cTnI is a highly sensitive and specific marker of myocardial injury and correlates with the severity of myocardial damage. Research has shown that there is a close connection between Cardiac dysfunction and cognitive function [41–43]. Patients with heart failure often suffer from cognitive impairment, which may be related to insufficient cerebral blood flow perfusion caused by reduced cardiac pumping function [44].

Further exploration is needed in the future to understand the link between cTnI and cognition. Overall, the levels of cardiac troponin and eGFR reflect the severity of the disease. ESKD patients combined with myocardial injury tend to experience more severe disease and higher decision-making conflict. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) Controversies Conference [45] recommended covering a transition care time window of at least 90 days before dialysis. Before initiating dialysis, there is a long window period to delay disease progression and adequately educate the patient. The transitional care unit (TCU) provides patient-centered medical education and psychosocial support for predialysis patients, leading to a more moderate, robust dialysis initiation process and more beneficial patient outcomes [46]. In conclusion, efforts should be made to develop transitional care in the Chinese culture to improve patient preparedness for the transition of treatment modalities and to reduce decision conflict.

This study demonstrates that social support among ESKD patients has a negative predictive effect on decisional conflict. There is a significant correlation between decisional conflict and social support, with patients experiencing higher levels of social support reporting lower levels of decisional conflict. Family support is a crucial factor influencing decisional conflict [47]. This study’s findings indicate a significant correlation between decision conflict and social support, with patients experiencing higher levels of social support reporting lower levels of decisional conflict. The presence of family and social support provides patients with additional resources and guidance during the decision-making process, ultimately reducing stress and mitigating decisional conflict. In China’s traditional social culture, family members are one of the primary sources of social support for patients, with elderly patients’ family members primarily being spouses and children. When ESKD patients are confronted with treatment recommendations from doctors, they often discuss them with their family members, seeking their opinions and attitudes to alleviate the burden and uncertainty of making decisions independently and mitigating decision-making conflicts. This implies that Chinese healthcare professionals should holistically consider factors pertaining to patients’ families, assess the level of familial support, actively harness support from families, and promote the involvement of patients and their families in decision-making discussions, thereby facilitating the optimization of the decision-making process for patients. Notably, Rini’s study highlights the bidirectional nature of family influence on patient decision-making, suggesting that negative discouragement from family members may also contribute to decisional conflict [48]. In this study, other forms of support such as other and friend support positively influence social support. Firstly, interactions with fellow patients can bring emotional support and empathy. Dialysis treatment often comes with long-term physical and emotional stress, and sharing experiences and mutual encouragement among patients can alleviate feelings of loneliness and anxiety, strengthening their confidence in treatment [49]. Secondly, close collaboration with healthcare professionals (HCPs) also enhances the level of social support. At dialysis centers, patients receive regular monitoring and evaluations by professional healthcare staff, allowing for timely adjustments to their treatment plans. Additionally, healthcare providers offer personalized psychological support and health education, assisting patients in better managing their illness and improving their self-care abilities. This professional support can mitigate decisional conflict, significantly impacting patients’ treatment outcomes and prognosis.

The results show that monthly household income directly negatively predicts decisional conflict. The logistic regression results also indicate that the higher the monthly household income, the lower the incidence of decisional conflict; these findings are consistent with other studies [50]. Studies have shown that economic level can affect the decision process, influencing decision preferences and impacting values and expectations [51]. The treatment cost for ESKD patients is high, and many are unable to return to society, leading to a heavy medical burden. Due to variations in individual characteristics, quantifying the risk associated with dialysis modalities becomes challenging, prompting patients to reconsider their treatment options upon realizing the considerable uncertainty surrounding treatment outcomes and economic implications. Patients with higher incomes bear a relatively lighter financial burden during disease treatment, leading to reduced adverse emotional experiences and a greater likelihood of favorable outcomes. Consequently, healthcare departments at all levels should work to enhance the medical insurance system, decrease healthcare expenses, and address the economic concerns of patients to improve their overall well-being.

This study analyzed the factors affecting decisional conflict in ESKD patients and provided a further theoretical basis for clinical intervention. However, it has certain limitations. First, it used a cross-sectional design for data collection, which brings into question the issue of causality. While path analysis facilitated causal hypothesis testing, it cannot definitively exclude reverse causality. Given the intricate interplay of factors in ESKD patients’ decision conflicts, we caution against definitive causal claims and advocate for longitudinal studies to substantiate our hypothesis. Second, the findings may not be generalizable as the study was conducted at a single site with a limited sample size. To enhance the representativeness and generalizability of the study, future research should consider conducting the study across multiple medical centers in order to expand the diversity and representativeness of the sample. Lastly, other clinical factors not included in this study might influence the results. To more accurately assess the causal relationships between factors and reduce the impact of confounding factors, future studies should further explore these unconsidered variables.

Conclusion

This study constructed and validated a structural equation model based on theoretical models and a literature review, representing an exploratory research endeavor. The situation of decisional conflict in ESKD patients is not optimistic, with issues such as decision-making delay and regret being prevalent. The less health literacy and social support a patient has, along with lower monthly family income, and the worse their cognitive function and kidney disease, the more severe the impairment of decisional conflict becomes. Additionally, through path analysis, the weight of each influencing factor on decisional conflict has been identified. This is conducive to constructing patient decision aids and interventions suitable for China, following the Ottawa Decision Support framework. Such aids and interventions could effectively reduce patients’ decisional conflicts.
