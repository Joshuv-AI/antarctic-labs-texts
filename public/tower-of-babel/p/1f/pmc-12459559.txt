Multi-omics integrated analysis identifies key biomarkers for hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Tao Xiong, Yumin Zhang, Xiangqiang Liu, Kaiming Leng, Luan Li, Caiyun Liu, Guangjun Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Due to the high malignancy of hepatocellular carcinoma and the fact that <20% of patients can be diagnosed in the early stages, the postoperative recurrence rate for advanced or metastatic liver cancer patients can reach up to 70%, with a 5-year survival rate of <10%. We attempt to explore new and effective biomarkers for hepatocellular carcinoma and analyze them in conjunction with multiple omics approaches. Therefore, in order to explore the key etiological factors of hepatocellular carcinoma and new treatment methods, we utilized various approaches, including transcriptome-wide association studies, summary data-based Mendelian randomization analysis, weighted correlation network analysis, machine learning, spatial transcriptomics, molecular docking, virtual screening, and phenome-wide association studies. We confirmed the heterogeneity of Cornichon Family Member 4 (CNIH4) in hepatocellular carcinoma and its relevance to the immune microenvironment, cancer-immunity cycle, and intratumoral infections. Additionally, we explored small-molecule compounds that can interact with CNIH4 and analyzed their potential side effects. We obtained the key biomarker of hepatocellular carcinoma-CNIH4 by combining conventional transcriptome analysis results with spatial transcriptome, intratumoral infection data, and immune microenvironment analysis. Although our results reveal novel insights into hepatocellular carcinoma pathogenesis, further validation with clinical samples is needed to strengthen the conclusions. We believe that CNIH4 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for hepatocellular carcinoma in the future.

1. Introduction

According to the latest global cancer burden data released by the World Health Organization/International Agency for Research on Cancer,[1] primary liver cancer has seen an increase of 906,000 new cases, ranking as the 6th most common malignancy. Additionally, there were 830,000 deaths, making it the 3rd leading cause of cancer-related deaths. And within China, there were 410,000 new cases of liver cancer, accounting for approximately 45.3% of the global total.[2] In May 2022, the National Cancer Center of China released Cancer incidence and mortality in China, 2006, which stated that there were 389,000 new cases of liver cancer annually in China, ranking as the 4th most common malignancy. Additionally, there were 336,000 new deaths, making liver cancer the 2nd leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the country.[3,4] Among liver cancer cases in mainland China, approximately 91.6% were hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The main causal factor for liver cancer is chronic hepatitis B virus infection. The distribution of cases according to the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer staging system was as follows: 3% were stage 0, 30% were stage A, 9% were stage B, 55% were stage C, and 2% were stage D. The overall survival period for liver cancer patients was 23 months, with a 5-year survival rate ranging from only 11.7% to 14.1%.[5,6] This poses a severe threat to the health of the Chinese people and places a heavy burden on Chinese society.

However, due to the asymptomatic nature of early-stage HCC, high-risk populations frequently overlook screening opportunities. Moreover, in the vast rural areas of China, factors such as the economy and education limit the awareness of liver cancer prevention and screening among the grassroots population. As a result, the screening rate, early diagnosis rate, and long-term survival rate for high-risk group of liver cancer in China remain relatively low. With the joint efforts of the government and healthcare workers, the diagnosis and treatment levels for liver cancer in China have significantly improved, leading to a downward trend in the incidence of liver cancer.[7] However, in the past 20 years, the liver cancer mortality rate has not decreased significantly, and the burden of the disease continues to rise. Furthermore, the burden of liver cancer is increasingly severe worldwide. IARC predicts that by 2040, the annual number of diagnosed liver cancer cases and deaths caused by liver cancer will increase by over 55%.[2] Therefore, effective screening and diagnosis of liver cancer are crucial at all levels, as there is still a long way to go in addressing this issue. The significant burden of HCC in China underscores the urgent global need to identify more effective early-warning biomarkers and therapeutic targets for this malignancy. The overarching objective of this study is to investigate broadly relevant causal molecular mediators implicated in the pathogenesis of HCC.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified thousands of genetic variations associated with many complex traits. However, their biological mechanisms are still largely unknown. Recently, transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) have emerged as valuable tools for investigating potential gene regulatory mechanisms associated with variant traits. In particular, TWAS integrates GWAS with expression profiling studies based on a set of joint variations, aiming to identify genes that are associated with the phenotype of interest.[8] TWAS has already been successfully applied in biomedical research for various diseases such as depression, stroke, rheumatoid arthritis, coronary artery disease, traumatic brain injury, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Alzheimer disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, and Crohn disease.[9–17] Summary data-based Mendelian randomization analysis (SMR) is based on the Mendelian randomization. It utilizes expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) mapping analysis and summary statistics from GWAS to identify disease susceptibility genes. With a large sample size, SMR achieves high statistical power, allowing for effective validation and supplementation of TWAS analysis results.[18] The comprehensive use of TWAS and SMR yields more comprehensive and reliable results. By integrating the results from TWAS, SMR, and weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) and employing various machine learning methods, we have identified indicators with excellent diagnostic value for liver cancer and predicted potential drugs that could target it. Here, we integrate multi-omics approaches (TWAS, SMR, and WGCNA) with machine learning to identify Cornichon Family Member 4 (CNIH4) as a key HCC biomarker linked to immune microenvironment and intratumoral infections, offering potential therapeutic targets.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Preliminary screening of HCC-related genes

TWAS adopts the functional summarybased imputation method. We performed an analysis on the HCC GWAS dataset ieu-b-4953 from the medical research council-integrative epidemiology unit GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/).[19] Using eQTL reference panels (liver tissue) obtained from GTEx v8. Additionally, we employed SMR and heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) methods, using the eQTL data from eQTLGen, to test for pleiotropic associations between gene expression levels and the complex traits of interest. To further identify genes specifically associated with HCC, we utilized the WGCNA method, clustering gene expression levels and calculating their correlations with phenotypes, to identify key gene modules. Finally, we took the intersection of genes obtained from these 3 methods.

2.2. Using machine learning to identify key gene

We employed 5 different machine learning methods: support vector machine (SVM),[20] least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO),[21] random forest (RF),[22] extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost),[23] and feature selection with the Boruta package[24] to further filter the 7 genes obtained from the initial screening. The dataset used for this analysis was obtained from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), which included 374 tumor samples and 50 normal samples. The results from the 5 machine learning algorithms were intersected using a Venn diagram to obtain the final result. Subsequently, we validated the differential expression levels of the CNIH4 in 6 datasets, which are GSE19665, GSE84402, GSE41804, GSE76427, GSE39791, and GSE62232. Furthermore, we utilized receiver operating characteristic curves to validate the diagnostic capability of CNIH4 for HCC in 3 cohorts, namely TCGA-LIHC, ICGC-LIRI, and GSE14520.

2.3. Heterogeneity verification of CNIH4

To validate the intratumoral heterogeneity of the CNIH4, we performed validation using both single-cell data and spatial transcriptomic data. The annotated single-cell data, including GSE166635, GSE146115, GSE140228, GSE98638, and GSE17975, were downloaded from the tumor immune single-cell hub.[25] The spatial transcriptomic data, including GSM5764424, GSE5764425, GSM5764426, GSM5764427, GSM5764428, and GSM6177612, were obtained from the gene expression omnibus database. All the data mentioned above were analyzed using the R package Seurat (New York Genome Center, New York). For the spatial transcriptomic data, standardization, selection of highly variable genes, and normalization were performed using the SCTransform function. The functions SpatialDimPlot and SpatialFeaturePlot were utilized for visual representation of the data.

2.4. Correlation analysis of CNIH4 with tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and intratumoral infection levels

We calculated tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion, immune score, estimate score, and stromal score for samples in the TCGA-LIHC cohort to assess the TIME. Additionally, we obtained the status of the cancer-immune cycle in the LIHC cohort from the TIP database (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/TIP/) and evaluated the immune infiltration of each sample using the ssGSEA method. The visualization was performed using the ComplexHeatmap package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=ComplexHeatmap).[26,27] To assess the presence of viral and bacterial infections in tumor tissues and their correlation with CNIH4, we downloaded the viral infection information of each sample in the TCGA-LIHC cohort from oncoDB (https://oncodb.org/)[28] and displayed the differential expression levels using box plots. We also obtained the bacterial infection information of each tumor sample in the LIHC cohort from bacterial infection in cancer (http://bic.jhlab.tw/),[29] integrated it with the expression levels of CNIH4, and displayed them using a heatmap.

2.5. Prediction of small-molecule compounds targeting CNIH4

We downloaded processed CMap data from BHKLAB (https://www.bhklab.ca/) and used the eXtreme Sum method to calculate scores for each small-molecule compound in CMap.[30] A lower score indicates a higher probability of the drug reversing the molecular features of the disease, theoretically making it more likely to have therapeutic potential for that disease. We downloaded PDB files of CNIH4 from UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/) and SDF files of small-molecule compounds from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). We then conducted further molecular docking analysis using CB-Dock2 (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/index.php).[31] Additionally, we performed virtual screening analysis on small-molecule compounds from the approved drug library, experimental drug library, and Traditional Chinese Medicine using DrugRep (http://cao.labshare.cn/drugrep/) (Sichuan University, Chengdu, China). The approved drug library and experimental drug library are obtained from the DrugBank database (version 5.1.7), while the Traditional Chinese Medicine library is from Topscience Company.[32]

2.6. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.2) (https://www.R-project.org/). Categorical variables were compared using Chi-square or Fisher exact tests, while continuous variables were assessed with Student t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Identification of key gene in HCC

First, we used the TWAS method to find 185 transcriptome associations. CNIH4 had the strongest association, followed by EPHA2, PPIL3, DNAJC21, and SPRN (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q77). Through the combined analysis of SMR and HEIDI, we identified a total of 684 genes, with 730 genes discovered through the SMR method and 46 genes excluded based on the inclusion criterion of P > .05 in the HEIDI analysis (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q77). We also conducted WGCNA analysis and identified 13 modules. Among them, the greenyellow, blue, black, and turquoise modules showed the most significant correlation with HCC, consisting of a total of 2798 genes. By intersecting the results from the above 3 groups, we identified 7 genes, namely CNIH4, ATRAID, F12, DCAF7, DDX42, MAN2B1, and PCMTD2 (Fig. 1A). Next, we performed feature selection on the aforementioned 7 genes using 5 machine learning methods, including SVM, LASSO, RF, XGBoost, and Boruta. By intersecting the results obtained, we ultimately identified CNIH4 as the final key gene (Fig. 1B).

3.2. Heterogeneity analysis of CNIH4

We conducted an analysis to determine whether there were differences in the expression of CNIH4 between HCC tissues and normal tissues. In the datasets GSE19665 (Fig. 2A), GSE84402 (Fig. 2B), GSE41804 (Fig. 2C), GSE76427 (Fig. 2D), GSE39791 (Fig. 2E), and GSE62232 (Fig. 2F), we consistently observed a significant upregulation of CNIH4 expression in HCC tissues. Moreover, CNIH4 showed excellent diagnostic value for HCC across multiple cohorts (Fig. 2G). To further investigate the heterogeneity of CNIH4, we performed single-cell analysis. The results revealed significant heterogeneity of CNIH4 expression in specific cells in 4 out of the 5 datasets (Fig. 3A–E). Similarly, in most spatial transcriptomics samples, we observed heterogeneous expression of CNIH4 in HCC slices (Fig. 3F–K), indicating the possibility of inter-tumoral and intratumoral heterogeneity of CNIH4.

3.3. Correlation between CNIH4 and TIME and intratumoral infection in HCC tissue

Firstly, we analyzed the correlation between CNIH4 and immune checkpoints in HCC. We found that CNIH4 showed the closest correlation with the expression of TNF receptor superfamily member 4. Additionally, we analyzed the immune infiltration in the tumor microenvironment of HCC tissues and found a negative correlation between CNIH4 and stromal score and estimate score. However, the correlation analysis between CNIH4 and immune score revealed a very weak and statistically insignificant correlation (r = −0.068, P = 1). In terms of immune cells in the microenvironment, we observed statistically significant correlations between CNIH4 and B cells, DC, and pDC. In the analysis of the correlation with the cancer-immune cycle, we found that CNIH4 was correlated with Step 1 (release of cancer antigens), Step 2 (cancer antigen presentation), Step 3 (T cell priming and activation), Step 4 (Th22 cell recruiting), Step 4 (Eosinophil recruiting), Step 4 (Th17 cell recruiting), Step 4 (B cell recruiting), Step 4 (MDSC recruiting), and Step 7 (killing of cancer cells). Except for Step 7, all other steps showed positive correlations with CNIH4 (Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q76). This suggests that potential therapeutic drugs targeting CNIH4 may achieve their therapeutic goals by modulating the immune microenvironment. Furthermore, we found a significant correlation between CNIH4 and the HBV infection levels in tumor tissues (Fig. 4A), while there was no correlation with hepatitis C virus (Fig. 4B), human papillomavirus (Fig. 4C), or human papillomavirus-16 infections (Fig. 4D). Regarding bacterial infections in tumor tissues, CNIH4 was found to be correlated with 72 different microbial species at different taxonomic levels (Fig. 5).

3.4. Drug prediction and potential side effect analysis

By using the eXtreme Sum method to analyze the CMap data, we obtained specific scores for each drugs (Fig. S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q76). The top 5 valuable drugs were identified as arachidonyltrifluoromethane, AH.6809, MS.275, X4.5.dianilinophthalimide, and PHA.00816795. For the top 3 drugs, we conducted molecular docking analysis with CNIH4 (vina scores were arachidonyltrifluoromethane −6.3, AH.6809 −7.3, MS.275 −7.8) (Fig. 6A). The results of the virtual screening analysis conducted in DrugRep can be found in Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q77. We also provided molecular docking displays for the top 3 results from each library (Fig. 6B–D).

4. Discussion

In summary, we used a combination of multiple complementary and validated methods to clarify that CNIH4 is a potent diagnostic biomarker for HCC, and its high expression has high specificity for HCC; meanwhile, its association with tumor heterogeneity and immune microenvironment supports its potential as a prognostic biomarker. Additionally, we predicted potential drugs targeting CNIH4 based on molecular docking and virtual screening. Furthermore, phenome-wide association studies was used to predict possible side effects of targeting CNIH4 in therapy.

Our study used integrative omics research methods (TWAS and SMR), and the WGCNA was used to combine the results from the previous step with bulk-RNA data. Consequently, we identified 7 genes, including CNIH4, ATRAID, F12, DCAF7, DDX42, MAN2B1, and PCMTD2. Subsequently, we employed a combination of various machine learning techniques, including SVM, LASSO, RF, XGBoost, and Boruta, to assess the importance of these genes. Ultimately, we prioritized CNIH4 as the main focus of our study. This decision was based on its confirmed correlation with HCC by TWAS, SMR, and WGCNA, as well as its high importance ranking across the 5 different machine learning methods.

Despite limited research on CNIH4, it has been confirmed to be closely associated with various pathological changes, such as cervical cancer, lower-grade glioma, gastric cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and colon cancer.[33–38] In addition to its impact on tumor cell proliferation, CNIH4 also plays an important role in the metastasis process of specific tumors. A recent study by Min et al proposed CNIH4 as an HCC prognostic marker, aligning with our findings. Our study provides novel insights into its spatial heterogeneity, immune microenvironment interactions, intratumoral infection associations, and therapeutic targeting potential.[39]

We assessed whether the expression of CNIH4 in HCC tissue differs from that in normal liver tissue. The results revealed significantly elevated expression of CNIH4 in multiple HCC datasets. The expression levels of CNIH4 were also different in immune microenvironment-infiltrating cells, as shown by single-cell analysis. Different single-cell datasets had slightly different levels of CNIH4 enrichment because the cells were not all labeled the same way. However, it was still clear that CNIH4 expression was different in different types of cells. Moreover, spatial transcriptome analysis also indicated a heterogeneous distribution of CNIH4 in HCC. Although the differences between different clusters were not significant, our results still indicated spatial heterogeneity of CNIH4 in cancer tissue. While CNIH4 exhibits spatial heterogeneity, its consistent overexpression across HCC tissues (vs normal) supports its diagnostic potential. Heterogeneity may reflect tumor subclones or microenvironmental interactions, necessitating single-cell resolution assays in clinical validation.[40]

Tumors are immunogenic diseases, and the regulation of local immune responses as a novel therapeutic strategy for tumors is being increasingly explored. In various types of tumors, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) have been shown to effectively restore the ability of tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes to eliminate malignant tumors. For responsive patients, ICI therapy has also demonstrated long-term potential for disease control, including metastatic and chemotherapy-resistant solid tumors.[41] ICI has also been applied in the treatment of HCC, but its effectiveness is <20%, indicating the complex immune microenvironment of HCC.[41,42] Therefore, finding new therapeutic targets or combining with existing ICIs is an urgent issue to be addressed in the field of HCC treatment. We evaluated the correlation of CNIH4 with immune checkpoints and discovered a close association between CNIH4 and TNF Receptor Superfamily Member 4. Among the infiltrating cells in the immune microenvironment, the association between pDC and CNIH4 was the strongest, suggesting that CNIH4 may be involved in functions such as antigen presentation.[43] Analysis of tumor-immune cycle indicated that CNIH4 is related to antigen presentation involving DC, which is consistent with the results of immune microenvironment-infiltrating cells analysis.

While alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) remains the standard serological biomarker for HCC, its limited sensitivity in early-stage disease and HBV-negative cases highlights the need for complementary approaches.[44] Unlike AFP, which is limited by sensitivity in early-stage HCC, CNIH4’s association with immune infiltration and heterogeneity may offer complementary diagnostic value. Future multicenter studies should evaluate combined CNIH4/AFP panels to determine whether their integration improves early detection rates or treatment selection.

In May 2020, researchers from the Weizmann Institute of Science in Israel conducted an analysis of over 1526 samples from 7 common solid tumors, including breast cancer, lung cancer, ovarian cancer, pancreatic cancer, melanoma, bone cancer, and brain tumors, they found that most solid tumors contain bacteria.[45] Given emerging evidence that intratumoral microbes modulate immunotherapy response, we hypothesized that CNIH4 may interact with viral/bacterial infections to influence HCC progression.[45] To assess the impact of CNIH4 on bacterial invasion in liver cancer, we downloaded data from the bacterial infection in cancer database. The results revealed that out of the thousands of bacterial species available in the database, CNIH4 is closely associated with 72 specific bacterial taxa, with the strongest association observed with Chromatiales. Regarding viral infections in liver cancer tissue, CNIH4 has been validated to be associated with HBV infection, while its association with other viruses lacks convincing evidence.

In order to predict potential therapeutic drugs targeting CNIH4, we conducted an analysis using the CMap database and 3 libraries from the Drugrep website. We performed molecular docking on the selected drugs to demonstrate their potential interaction with CNIH4. All the screened drugs showed binding energies <0, indicating their potential targeting of CNIH4. Although further experimental validation is required to determine the specific binding, our study has at least provided a preliminary direction. To further explore the reliability of CNIH4 as a therapeutic target for HCC, we also conducted a phenome-wide association studies analysis. The results revealed that the regulation of CNIH4 primarily leads to metabolic-related abnormalities. This indicates that in future research, we should focus on potential metabolic disturbances and strive to minimize side effects.

Although our study has demonstrated the heterogeneity of CNIH4 in HCC and validated its role and potential therapeutic approaches through multiple aspects, our research still has limitations. Firstly, we lack validation of our conclusions through HCC tissue samples collected during surgery. Secondly, it is important to validate the therapeutic efficacy of the small-molecule compounds predicted in our study, either in cells or in mouse models. These limitations highlight the areas that need further improvement in future research.

5. Conclusions

In summary, we have identified a key biomarker of HCC that is closely associated with intratumoral infection and immune microenvironment, and have screened for drugs that act on this gene.
