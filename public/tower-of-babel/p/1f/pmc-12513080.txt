Interdisciplinary approach in post-cardiac arrest anoxic brain injury with unconfirmed brain death
Authors: Ahmad Abdelrehim, Daniel Landau, Carolyn Gaukler, Hari Brundavanam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere anoxic brain injury after cardiac arrest can produce a clinical picture suggestive of brain death (BD). However, when ancillary testing, such as radionuclide cerebral perfusion scans (RCPS), shows preserved cerebral perfusion, the diagnosis of brain death cannot be confirmed, complicating end-of-life decision-making.Case summaryWe present a case of a post-cardiac arrest patient with profound neurological injury and absent brainstem reflexes. Although the clinical findings resembled brain death, two RCPS showed preserved cerebral perfusion, ruling out a formal brain death diagnosis. As a result, withdrawal of life-sustaining therapies was not permitted under existing protocols, despite a poor prognosis.Keywords: Unconfirmed brain death, Neurological assessment, Palliative care, End-of-Life Decision-Making, Radionuclide cerebral perfusion scan

Introduction

The Uniform Determination of Death Act (UDDA) defines death as the irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function, or all functions of the brain, including the brainstem [1]. The apnea test plays a key role in confirming BD by evaluating brainstem function, but it may be unsafe in unstable patients, necessitating ancillary testing. The Radionuclide cerebral perfusion scintigraphy (RCPS) is often used in such cases. The presence of preserved cerebral blood flow effectively rules out BD [2].

Cases in which brain death can not be formally pronounced raise challenges in prognosis and end-of-life care. The palliative care (PC) team helps in navigating the complex psychosocial dynamics and medical uncertainty surrounding a devastating neurologic injury and filling the gap between the medical team’s expectations for prognosis-driven decision-making and the family’s desire to “do everything” without fully grasping the neurologic implications [3].

We describe a case of severe anoxic brain injury following cardiac arrest in which the patient exhibited clinical findings suggestive of brain death, yet demonstrated preserved cerebral perfusion on ancillary testing. This case highlights the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration in navigating diagnostic uncertainty and facilitating appropriate goals-of-care discussions.

Case presentation

Medical history and initial presentation

A 63-year-old female with a history of asthma, hypertension, and diabetes was found pulseless by EMS 15 min after calling 911. She was resuscitated and intubated at the scene, achieving return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). However, she was arrested twice more after admission to the emergency department, requiring further resuscitation.

ICU course and neurological evaluation

On admission to the ICU, the patient underwent a pan-CT, which was unremarkable except for early signs of anoxic brain injury. A modified therapeutic temperature management (mTTM) protocol was initiated. By day 3, a neurological examination demonstrated the absence of all brainstem reflexes, including fixed and dilated pupils and absent corneal, oculocephalic, gag, and cough reflexes. Continuous video EEG showed suppression-burst patterns with no epileptiform activity. Repeated CT scans revealed extensive infarction involving the brainstem, cerebellum, and deep gray matter nuclei.

Challenges in brain death determination

In preparation for the apnea test, preoxygenation was initiated using 100% FiO₂ through the ventilator for 10 min, and the goal of PaO₂ > 200 mmHg was achieved before starting the test. The patient remained hemodynamically stable during preoxygenation, maintaining a mean arterial pressure (MAP) > 65 mmHg without the need for intravenous fluids or vasopressors. She was normothermic and had been off sedation and analgesia since hospital Day 3.

Following preoxygenation, the apnea test was attempted using continuous oxygen flow through a catheter placed at the carina during the apnea test. However, the procedure was aborted due to the onset of hemodynamic instability, marked by bradycardia and hypotension.

Given the inability to safely complete the apnea test, the ICU team proceeded with ancillary testing. A follow-up EEG showed worsening findings, with background suppression to less than 2 microvolts, consistent with severe cerebral dysfunction. Consequently, on Day 5, a radionuclide cerebral perfusion scan (RCPS) was performed. The scan demonstrated preserved perfusion in both the cerebral and cerebellar hemispheres, ruling out brain death (Fig. 1), further complicating the brain death diagnosis.

Despite these findings, the neurological examination consistently demonstrated absent brainstem reflexes, leading to continued discussions with the family regarding goals of care and code status. The nephrology team advised against initiating hemodialysis (HD), as it would not impact the overall prognosis.

Role of palliative medicine and ethical considerations

The ICU attending initiated a family meeting to address the patient’s critical neurologic condition, highlighting the unconfirmed diagnosis of brain death and the extremely low likelihood of meaningful neurologic recovery, regardless of ancillary test results. The family, expressing hope and uncertainty, requested that full code status be maintained and that more time be allowed for potential recovery.

In response, the interdisciplinary ICU team—including the intensivist, neurologist, palliative care physician, social worker, and case manager—adopted a collaborative and compassionate approach. A structured family meeting was held in which each team member contributed their expertise. The neurologist explained the concept of brain death, diagnostic barriers in this context, and the clinical signs suggesting poor prognosis. The palliative care physician guided the discussion around the patient’s wishes, focusing on goals of care and code status. The family shared that the patient valued dignity, comfort, and independence and would not want to live with prolonged suffering or full dependence.

The social worker provided emotional support and explored cultural considerations and family dynamics, while the case manager discussed potential transitions and resources for comfort-focused care. This multidisciplinary approach allowed the family to process complex information and values, ultimately leading to a care plan aligned with the patient’s goals.

This interdisciplinary collaboration allowed the team to shift the focus from confirming brain death to helping the family understand the prognosis of severe anoxic brain injury. The conversation emphasized that although brain death criteria were not met, the patient’s condition was irreversible, with no reasonable chance of meaningful recovery. By integrating medical facts with a values-based approach, the team supported ethical, patient-centered decision-making.

Following transparent and compassionate discussions, the family elected to pursue comfort measures only (CMO). A final family meeting was held before extubation, during which the chaplain provided emotional and spiritual support, helping the family honor the patient’s life and find peace in their shared faith. The patient was extubated on hospital day 13 and passed away peacefully within 30 min, surrounded by loved ones.

Discussion

This case illustrates the challenges of end-of-life decision-making in patients with severe post-cardiac arrest anoxic brain injury who exhibit clinical signs suggestive of brain death but do not meet formal diagnostic criteria. These patients may lack brainstem reflexes and show profound neurological impairment; however, preserved cerebral perfusion on ancillary imaging precludes a brain death diagnosis, complicating the trajectory of care.

The apnea test is an essential component of brain death determination. However, in this case, repeated attempts at apnea testing were discontinued due to hemodynamic instability—manifesting as bradycardia and hypotension—rendered the apnea test unsafe and inconclusive.

The American Academy of Neurology (AAN) recognizes three primary methods for assessing cerebral blood flow: conventional angiography, transcranial Doppler ultrasound, and 99mTc scintigraphy [4]. RCPS is a widely used ancillary test in situations where clinical brain death cannot be confirmed. However, in rare situations, residual flow may be observed even with critical elevations in intracranial pressure or those with decompressive craniectomies, ventriculostomies, or other anatomical factors that preserve flow despite non-functional brain tissue [5, 6].

To assess cerebral perfusion, radionuclide cerebral perfusion scans (RCPS) were conducted on days 5 and 10. Both scans demonstrated preserved perfusion in bilateral cerebral and cerebellar hemispheres. These findings were incompatible with brain death, despite the absence of brainstem reflexes and extensive radiographic evidence of brain injury.

In cases of severe anoxic brain injury that closely resemble brain death but do not fulfill formal diagnostic criteria, the clinical approach must extend beyond rigid diagnostic algorithms. Instead, the focus should pivot toward understanding prognosis, exploring quality-of-life implications, and guiding families through realistic, values-based expectations.

This case underscores the vital role of the palliative care (PC) team in navigating the intersection of complex psychosocial dynamics and profound medical uncertainty following catastrophic neurologic injury. The patient’s family, confronted with the sudden onset of her condition, was emotionally distressed—likely influenced by previous healthcare experiences, cultural values, and limited understanding of end-of-life frameworks. A significant gap emerged between the medical team’s prognosis-based recommendations and the family’s insistence on continuing full-code measures, driven by hope and an incomplete grasp of neurologic prognostication [7].

By employing trauma-informed care principles, the PC team created a safe, compassionate space for the family to express their fears, grief, and confusion. They validated the emotional weight of the situation while carefully clarifying the medical realities. This approach revealed previously unspoken concerns: fear of abandonment, cultural guilt associated with “giving up,” and a lack of understanding around the concept of brain death and its mimics [3, 8, 9].

Furthermore, the PC team facilitated values-based discussions, helping the family align their medical decisions with the patient’s known preferences and personal values. Through this integrative and multidisciplinary approach, the family was gradually able to move from a position of reactive insistence to one of informed, shared decision-making. The resulting care plan honored both the clinical realities and the patient’s dignity and autonomy [10].

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of a human-centered, interdisciplinary approach in navigating anoxic brain injury with unconfirmed brain death. Integrating perspectives from palliative care, critical care, and social work ensures that care remains grounded in empathy, ethical integrity, and the holistic needs of both patient and family—even amid diagnostic uncertainty.
