Effects of dietary paddy rice on the growth, serum biochemistry, intestinal development, microbiota, and metabolism of young laying ducks in a rice–duck–crayfish farming system
Authors: Xiaolong He, Zhenhua Liang, Zeheng Huang, Yan Wu, Jia Liu, Ming Fu, Jingbo Liu, Jinsong Pi, Hao Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of feeding paddy rice on the physiology, metabolism, and gut microbiota of ducks in a rice–duck–crayfish (RDC) system. A total of 540 ducks (20-days-old) were randomly divided into 3 groups with 3 replicates and 60 ducks per replicate. The 40-d experiment involved 3 diet treatments: a complete diet (CD), 50% paddy rice + 50% complete diet (RCD), and 100% paddy rice diet (RD). Results show that feeding paddy rice did not significantly affect duck growth, the final weight in the RD group was reduced by 5%, and the feed-to-gain ratio increased by 7% compared to the CD group. Additionally, compared with the CD group, the keel length, gizzard and proventriculus indices, and serum high-density lipoprotein levels increased (P < 0.05), and duodenal villus height and ileal crypt depth (P < 0.05) reduced in the RD group and ileal villus height decreased in the RCD group (P < 0.05). Compared with the CD group, the cecal abundance of Bacteroidota, Prevotellaceae_Ga6A1_group, and Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group decreased in the RD group (P < 0.05) while the abundance of Firmicutes, Megamonas and Faecalibacterium increased (P < 0.05). Metabolome analysis revealed that serum citric acid increased (P < 0.05) in the RCD and RD groups, whereas cytidine, cytosine, and 4-aminobutyric acid decreased (P < 0.05) in the RD group. In conclusion, these preliminary results suggest that paddy rice supplementation under an RDC system had no significant effect on duck growth, but it did cause changes in intestinal morphology, microbiota, and serum metabolic profiles. However, it is important to note that the limited overall number of replicates in this study contributed to a certain degree of high variance. While the growth differences among groups were not statistically significant, the full replacement of paddy rice still poses potential performance losses. In practical applications, this finding provides a reference for the RDC system, but further validation through larger-scale trials is required.Key words: rice–duck–crayfish farming, paddy rice, duck, metabolome, gut microbiota

INTRODUCTION

China is the largest producer and consumer of duck eggs, with a duck egg inventory of approximately 150 million, an annual duck egg production of more than 2.6 million tons, and a laying duck industry that accounts for more than 70% of the global total (Hou and Liu, 2023). Compared with laying hens, laying ducks are more resistant to pathogenic avian influenza viruses (Smith et al., 2015) and better at adapting to the environment. During duck brood rearing, intensive heating, ground levelling, and cage-based brood-rearing are typical practices (Chen et al., 2021). However, the stress and behavioral limitations caused by these intensive modes lead to deterioration in bone and feather quality (Nørgaard-Nielsen, 1990; Zheng et al., 2022). Fecal contamination from intensive farming can also cause waste production, deterioration of the environment, and high feed costs (Zeng et al., 2016).

A rice–duck–crayfish (RDC) coculture system is an efficient ecological cycle-based farming model that integrates the use of rice, ducks, and crayfish. In this system, ducks and crayfish live in different spatial environments in a paddy field, and the ecology of the paddy field is improved by ducks and crayfish foraging for weeds and insects (Yan et al., 2023). Duck feces function as fertilizer, provide food for the crayfish, and promote aquatic organisms in the paddy fields, which in turn provide additional food for the ducks and crayfish, forming a mutually beneficial ecological model for aquaculture. The RDC system not only harnesses natural duck behavior but also helps young ducks during brooding and rearing, thus serving as an ideal method for ensuring animal welfare. However, the weeds and organic matter in paddy fields cannot satisfy the growth requirements of ducks under this model, and supplemental diets are required to meet the developmental needs of young ducks (Long et al., 2013). Supplemental feeding of a complete diet is costly, but feed costs may be reduced by replacing part of the complete feed with local raw grain resources. Studies have shown that wheat can be substituted for complete feed in a rice–duck system to improve carcass quality with no adverse effect on growth (Hou et al., 2021). Paddy rice is a main grain crops; its price is low, and it is easily accessible and convenient in rice–growing regions. Previous studies showed that rice grain fed to broilers had no adverse effect on performance and could partially replace corn in the diet (Nanto et al., 2012; Sittiya et al., 2016). Adding a certain percentage of rice grain to the diet of 30-day-old or older ducklings had no significant effect on growth or digestion (LeMieux et al., 2021). The addition of 50% broken rice instead of wheat to the diets of laying ducks did not affect nutrient or laying performance (Naik et al., 2022). Studies have shown that the use of paddy rice as the main diet does not adversely affect the growth or development of young ducks in floor rearing (Zheng, 2014). The addition of 20% paddy rice to the diets of 1 to 35 d meat-type ducks did not affect the average daily feed intake (ADFI) but had adverse effects on the average daily gain (ADG) (Kang et al., 2013). These results suggest that the effects of paddy rice in poultry diets may vary. However, few studies have reported the effects paddy rice as a substitute for complete feed under an RDC system.

A healthy gut benefits the digestion, absorption, and use of nutrients by animals, and it has important immune functions. In poultry, the gut contains a large number and variety of symbiotic bacteria. Interactions between the host and gut microbiota are now understood to influence nutrition, health, and immunity (Bindari and Gerber, 2022). However, feed ingredients and the environment influence gut microbiota (Kers et al., 2018; Olson et al., 2022). The addition of paddy rice to the diet has been shown to affect the distribution of the intestinal flora in geese and increase the number of bacterial species in the duodenum and jejunum (Xu, 2011). Additionally, differences in rearing methods can affect the gut microbiota. Previous studies have shown that free-range chickens had more diverse and unique gut microbiota than caged chickens (Hou et al., 2020). In addition, gut microbiota act in a concerted manner to achieve metabolic communication with the host by influencing nutrient digestion and absorption, affecting metabolite levels, and participating in the regulation of host growth and development (Nicholson et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2020, 2023). Based on previous studies, we hypothesized that the use of paddy rice as a substitute for a complete feed was feasible under an RDC system and might influence the development of young ducks via changes in their intestinal microbiota and serum metabolites.

Therefore, Young Nonghu No. 2 ducks were used to investigate the feasibility of paddy rice supplementation under an RDC system. Changes in the growth and development, serum biochemistry, intestinal development, microbiota, and serum metabolomes of young ducks were measured to determine whether raw paddy rice material can be utilized in the diets of young ducks under an RDC system.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics Statement

All animal experiments were conducted according to the guidelines established by the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals (Order No.676, State Council, China, 2017.3.1).

Animals, Design, and Treatments

A total of 540 young (20-days-old) Nonghu. Two ducks were selected from the Hubei Academy of Agricultural Sciences Poultry Experimental Farm. The high-quality black rice variety “Huamo Fragrant Rice” was obtained from Shuangshui Shuanglv Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China. The area of the experimental paddy field was 45 mu (30,000 m²). The ducks were randomly divided into 3 treatment groups: a complete diet group (CD), a 50% paddy rice + 50% complete diet group (RCD), and a 100% paddy rice diet group (RD), with 3 replicates per treatment group and 60 birds per replicate. The feeding density was 12 birds/666.7 m2, and the RDC symbiosis period was 40 d. The experiment took place at the Shuangshui Shuanglv Production Base, Shishou City, Hubei Province, China (Supplementary Figure S1). The ducks in each group were fed by grazing-quantitative supplemental feeding at 7:00 and 17:00 h daily, and the amount of supplemental feed was adjusted for duck age. The amount of duckling feed was based on the standard of 47 g/only in 20 d, and thereafter, the amount of feed was increased by 2 g/d/only. The composition and nutrient level of diet are shown in Table 1. The paddy rice used in this study was obtained from the previous year's harvest at this farm (Shuangshui Shuanglv Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), and the complete diet was a commercial feed for laying ducks during the breeding period (Jingzhou Haida Feed Co., Ltd., Jingzhou, China). The ration of complete feed was reduced by 10%–20% on the basis of the daily feed intake for this breed at different ages to ensure that young ducks could be weeded and dewormed in the field.

Duck Management

The size of each experimental field was 3,333.35 m2, and there were 3 fields per experimental group, for a total of 9 fields. A ditch (2 m wide and 1 m deep) was dug from the field, and netting was set up around the field to prevent ducks and crayfish from escaping and the invasion of hostile organisms. A duck hut was set up for ducks to rest and feed. After the farmland was plowed, crayfish fry were placed in the ditches (20 kg/666.67 m2). Base fertilizer was applied before the rice seedlings were transplanted, and 20-day-old ducks were introduced to the field approximately 15 d after the seedlings were transplanted. The ducks were conditioned by musical signals for feeding management, and no pesticides, fertilizers, or herbicides were sprayed on the rice during the symbiosis stage. In the rice tasseling stage, the ducks were driven out of the field, which ended symbiosis. The symbiosis period was 40 d.

Growth and Body Development Measurements

The weight of ducks in each replicate was determined at the time of entry and exit from the field to calculate the ADG, and duck mortality during the trial was also monitored. At the end of the experiment, 6 ducks were selected from each group (n = 18). Body size indices including body oblique length, half-diving length, keel length, tibial length, and tibial girth were measured (using vernier calipers and a tape measure) after the ducks fasted for 12 h, and body size indices were determined according to the nomenclature and metrics calculation methods for poultry production performance (NY/T 823-2020) as follows: Body oblique length refers to the distance from the shoulder joint to the ipsilateral ischial tuberosity measured along the body surface with a tape measure. Keel length refers to the distance from the anterior end of the keel eminence to the end of the keel measured with a tape measure along the body surface. Tibial length refers to the distance from the superior joint to the third and fourth toes measured in a straight line with a Vernier calipers. Tibial girth refers to the circumference of the middle of the tibia measured with a tape measure. Half-diving length was measured with a tape measure from the tip of the rostrum to the midpoint of the line connecting the 2 acetabular tuberosities.

Organ Indices and Intestinal Morphology

The heart, liver, spleen, pancreas, gizzard, and proventriculus were weighed after the ducks were slaughtered, according to animal ethical protocols, to calculate organ indices. The duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were separated, and the intestinal segments of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were rinsed with saline and placed in 4% formaldehyde solution for fixation. Tissue sections were subjected to hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) staining, and each section was observed under a microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE E100, Tokyo, Japan). Six intact intestinal villi were selected for measurement with image processing software (ImageJ 1.8.0, National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD). The villus height was measured from the tip of the villi to the opening of the crypt. Crypt depth was measured from the muscularis to the crypt opening, and the villus height‒crypt depth ratio (VH:CD) was calculated. Organ index (g/kg) = organ weight (g) / body weight (kg).

Serum Biochemical Parameters

Blood samples from ducks were collected from wing veins. After collection, the blood was left for 2 h. The blood was then centrifuged at 3,000 r/min for 10 min, and the serum was collected, transferred to EP tubes, and stored at -80 °C until use. The total bilirubin (TBIL), glucose (GLU), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), immunoglobulin G (IgG), creatine kinase (CK), and cholinesterase (CHE) concentrations in the serum were measured using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (PUZS-300X, Beijing Plan New Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The kits were purchased from Chongqing Runkang Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Chongqing, China). The concentration of globulin (GLB) was calculated as the difference between total protein and albumin.

16S rRNA Sequencing

The ceca of the ducks were collected at the end of the experiment using sterile cryopreservation tubes, quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then transferred to a -80 °C freezer until use. Six samples of cecum contents from each group were used for high-throughput sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted from the samples via the CTAB method, and after testing for concentration and purity, the 16S RNA V3-V4 region was amplified via PCR using the DNA as a template. The sequences of the V3-V4 universal primers were 341F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3). The PCR products were purified and quantified after 2% agarose gel electrophoresis was used to ensure quality, and sequencing libraries were established. The libraries were quantified via Qubit and Q-PCR and then sequenced with a NovaSeq 6,000 for PE250 upsequencing. FLASH (version 1.2. 11) and fastp software (version 0.23. 1) were used for data splicing and quality control. The initial amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and feature lists were obtained via noise reduction using DADA2 or deblurring with QIIME2 software (version QIIME2-202202). The ASVs were further analyzed for species annotation using QIIME2 to obtain species abundances at different taxonomic levels and microbial ɑ diversity indices. The differences in β diversity and gut microbiota species classifications were subsequently analyzed using R software (4.0.3) and LEfSe software (1.1.01).

Metabolomics Analysis

Six serum samples from each group were used for nontargeted metabolome analysis via liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Briefly, 100 μL of each serum samples was added to 400 μL of 80% methanol in water, and incubated on ice for 5 min, and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Then, the supernatant was diluted with mass spectrometry-grade water to dilute the methanol content to 53% and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatant was collected and analyzed by LC‒MS/MS using a Vanquish UHPLC (Thermo Fisher, Suzhou, China) and a Q Exactive HF (Thermo Fisher). The downstream data were processed with Compound Discoverer 3.3 software for graphical processing and database searching for peak detection, peak extraction, peak comparison, and qualitative and quantitative characterization of each metabolite. The identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG, HMDB and LIPIDMaps database. The processed data were analyzed by principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) using metaX software to determine the VIP values of the metabolites. At test was used to calculate the statistical significance of each metabolite between groups (P value) and to calculate the fold change (FC) of the metabolites between groups. The following criteria were set to screen for differentially enriched metabolites between different groups: a FC > 1.2, a VIP > 1, and P < 0.05. Follow-up statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.0.3.

Statistical Analysis

Measurements were counted in Microsoft excel 2021 software, and one-way ANOVA with Tukey multiple comparisons were performed using SAS 9.4 software (Cary, NC). Polynomial contrasts for linear and quadratic responses were used to assess the effects of paddy rice supplementation in the diet. The data are expressed as the means and standard errors of the means. Statistical significance was set as P < 0.05. GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 software was used to plot the results. Spearman correlations were used to reveal the relationships between the gut flora and serum metabolites.

Zero mortality occurred during the trial. In addition, dietary paddy rice had no effect on the body oblique length, tibial length, tibial girth, tibial weight or half-diving length of the ducks (P > 0.05), but it did affect keel length. The keel length of the ducks in the RD group was greater than that of the ducks in the CD group (P < 0.05) (Table 3).

Effects of Dietary Paddy Rice on the Cecal Microbial Diversity of Young Laying Ducks in a Rice–Duck–Crayfish Farming System

To further evaluate the effects of dietary paddy rice on the cecal microorganisms of ducks in an RDC system, changes in cecal microorganisms in each group were analyzed via 16S rRNA sequencing. A total of 1,879,777 raw reads were obtained, and a total of 1,240,303 nt of valid sequences were obtained after splicing and filtering, with an average sequence length of 419 nt per sample (Supplementary Table S1). The total number of ASVs in each group was obtained via sequence clustering analysis, and the number of ASVs that were common to or specific to different rice-fed groups was determined using Venn diagrams. Total ASVs in the CD, RCD, and RD groups were 1,527, 1,318, and 899, respectively, which indicated that the addition of rice reduced the number of ASVs (Figure 2A). The sparse curve plateaued when the number of sequences reached 40,480, indicating that the sequencing data were sufficiently representative of all the ASVs in each sample, and only a very small number of ASVs were excluded from this study (Supplementary Figure S2). Species diversity was analyzed using the Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, and Observed_species indices, and dietary paddy rice had no effect on these indices, which indicated that being fed paddy rice did not alter the gut microbial community diversity or richness of the ducks (Figure 2B). In addition, the β diversity of the microbiota was compared using PCoA and NMDS based on weighted UniFrac differences, which revealed some separation in clustering between the different groups. Separate clustering of microflora occurred in the CD and RD groups and the RCD group placed between the CD and RD groups (Figures 2C and 2D). Effects of Dietary Paddy Rice on the Cecal Microbial Composition of Young Laying Ducks in a Rice–Duck–Crayfish Farming System

To identify changes in the microbial composition and abundance of cecal microorganisms in the groups fed paddy rice, we analyzed species information from the top 10 phyla, and the results revealed that Bacteroidota and Firmicutes dominated the CD, RCD, and RD groups, with Bacteroidota proportions of 62.71, 56.71, and 52.48%, respectively, and Firmicutes proportions of 19.53, 26.44, and 30.64%, respectively (Figure 3A). Feeding with paddy rice decreased the relative abundance of Bacteroidota (P < 0.05) and increased the relative abundance of Firmicutes (P < 0.05) in ducks (Figure 3B). At the genus level, the top 15 taxa in the 3 groups were Bacteroides, Fusobacterium, Brachyspira, Anaerobiospirillum, unidentified_Rikenellaceae, Mucispirillum, Phascolarctobacterium, Prevotella_7, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Prevotellaceae_Ga6A1_group, Enterococcus, Megamonas, Streptococcus, Fournierella, and Collinsella. Bacteroides was the dominant genus in the cecum and accounted for 41.91, 37.57, and 36.77% of bacteria in the CD, RCD, and RD groups, respectively (Figure 3C). The relative abundance of Prevotellaceae_Ga6A1_group was significantly lower in the 2 groups of ducks fed paddy rice than in the ducks fed a complete diet (P < 0.05), and the relative abundance of Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group decreased in the RD group (P < 0.05), while the relative abundance of Megamonas and Faecalibacterium increased than in the ducks fed a complete diet (P < 0.05) (Figure 3D).

Effects of Dietary Paddy Rice on the Serum Metabolites of Young Laying Ducks in a Rice–Duck–Crayfish Farming System

Metabolites were extracted from the serum of ducks in the 3 groups, and nontargeted metabolomic profiles were examined using LC‒MS/MS to identify metabolite variation among the groups. A total of 668 metabolites were identified in all the samples in this study. Principal component analysis (PCA) was first performed to obtain an overview of the clustering trends. The results demonstrated that there was no significant separation between the CD and RCD groups but partial separation between the CD and RD groups, although there was some degree of overlap (Figures 5A and 5B). The data were then analyzed using PLS-DA, which showed significant separation among the 3 groups (Figure 5C). These findings indicate that serum metabolic profiles changed after supplementation with paddy rice. Volcano diagrams revealed that 43 metabolites were significantly different between the CD and RCD groups, of which 25 were upregulated and 18 were downregulated; 59 metabolites were significantly different between the CD and RD groups, of which 41 were upregulated and 18 were downregulated; and 46 metabolites were significantly different abundant between the RCD and RD groups, of which 33 were upregulated and 13 were downregulated (Figure 6A). The upregulated and downregulated differentially abundant metabolites between the different groups are listed in Supplementary Tables S2, S3, and S4. Differentially abundant metabolites were categorized with pie charts, which showed that among the differentially abundant metabolites, lipids and lipid-like molecules accounted for 26.53%, organic acids and their derivatives accounted for 14.97%, and organoheterocyclic compounds accounted for 14.29% (Supplementary Figure S3).

The KEGG database was used to analyze the metabolic pathways associated with the differentially abundant metabolites, and bubble plots were generated to show the degree of enrichment of serum differentially abundant metabolites and the number of differentially abundant metabolites enriched in each pathway among the different groups. The pathways in which the differentially abundant metabolites were significantly enriched in the CD and RCD groups were related mainly to carbon metabolism and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism. Citric acid was the key differentially abundant metabolite in the metabolic pathways and was more abundant in the RCD group (P < 0.01) (Figures 6B and 6C). The differentially abundant metabolites were significantly enriched in alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, and pyrimidine metabolism in both the CD and RD groups. Among the key differentially abundant metabolites related to pyrimidine metabolism, cytidine and cytosine were significantly downregulated in the RD group (P < 0.01), and among the key differentially abundant metabolites involved in alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, citric acid was significantly upregulated in the RD group (P < 0.05), and 4-aminobutyric acid was significantly downregulated in the RD group (P < 0.05) (Figures 6B and 6C). The most significantly enriched pathway in the RCD and RD groups was purine metabolism, and its key differentially abundant metabolites hypoxanthine and uric acid were significantly upregulated in the RCD group (Figure 6B and Supplementary Figure S4).

DISCUSSION

The RDC system integrates agriculture and animal husbandry to improve resource utilization efficiency and improve animal welfare and product quality. The use of paddy rice as a potential food source for ducks in this system is attractive because of its on-site availability and possible cost effectiveness. Our experimental results demonstrated that dietary paddy rice did not significantly affect the growth or health of ducks but did induce changes in their intestinal morphology, intestinal microbiota, and serum metabolic profiles.

Growth and body size indices are used to assess the productivity of poultry and directly affect economic returns. This study showed that feeding a diet of paddy rice during RDC farming did not significantly affect the final body weight or ADG of the ducks, indicating that the use of a paddy rice-diet as an alternative to a complete diet did not stunt the ducks. This finding was similar to the previous finding that corn diets were replaced by different combinations of whole-grain paddy rice and whole-grain brown rice in different proportions and were shown to have no effect on the feed intake, body weight gain, or feed efficiency of broilers (Sittiya et al., 2016). Similarly, studies of ducks have shown that the addition of 10% rice to the diet before 0 to 14 d of age reduces the ADG and ADFI of ducklings, whereas the addition of 10% rice to the diet after ducks have been reared for 30 days does not affect the ADG, ADFI, or feed efficiency (LeMieux et al., 2021). The age of the ducks in this study was 20 days. In general, lowering dietary energy and protein levels affects the growth of poultry. Studies of ducks have shown that a reduction in dietary energy from 12.35 MJ/kg to 11.93 MJ/kg has an adverse effect on growth (Lim et al., 2024). Additionally, a reduction in dietary crude protein from 18% to 14% has been shown to negatively affect the body weight, ADG, and feed intake of ducks (El. Abdel-Hamid and Abdelfattah, 2020). However, in the present system, nutrient levels in the diets were reduced after the substitution of paddy rice for a complete diet, and the growth of the young laying ducks was not significantly affected. This difference may stem from the use of our RDC system relative to traditional captive and intensive farming conditions. Dietary nutrients are required in intensive farming modes because ducks are unable to supplement their diets with exogenous substances. In a crayfish‒paddy environment, the ducks have space to move around and freedom of behavior, which provides opportunities for additional nutrient acquisition. Similarly, there were no significant differences in final body weight, ADG, or F/G when free range chickens were fed at 50% of the daily requirements compared with deep litter production systems (Sogunle et al., 2012). The reduced nutrient levels in the RDC system may stimulate ducks to meet their growth requirements by supplementing their diets with exogenous substances. Although statistical analysis did not reveal significant differences in weight gain or F/G ratio due to the paddy rice treatment, the observed trends should not be overlooked. The limited number of replicates in this study may have influenced the statistical significance. Therefore, the use of full paddy rice substitution in the RDC system requires further investigation with more replicates to confirm its commercial feasibility.

As an important phenotypic trait, body size is an indicator of skeletal development and a marker of organismal health (Latshaw and Bishop, 2001). This study demonstrated that the keel length of ducks increased significantly after they were fed paddy rice, but other body size traits were not significantly affected. Similarly, previous findings showed that feeding whole brown rice or whole rice husk to goslings for 42 days led to better body size traits (relative to other crushed feeds) and contributed more to goose production (Li et al., 2010). Therefore, our results indicate that the replacement of a complete diet with paddy rice in RDC farming did not negatively affect the growth of ducks and that this farming model provides environmental conditions under which ducks can follow their natural habits, which may contribute to growth and body size.

Previous studies have shown that the grain type and the form of feed affect gastrointestinal tract development in poultry (González-Alvarado et al., 2008; Jiménez-Moreno et al., 2010). Our results revealed a significant increase in the gizzard and proventriculus indices of ducks after they were fed paddy rice, which indicated that the addition of paddy rice effectively promoted the development of the duck stomach. This result is in line with other studies on the effects of paddy rice on the digestive system of poultry (Sittiya and Yamauchi, 2014; Sittiya et al., 2016), which revealed a significant increase in the weight of the gizzard after chickens were fed diets with different proportions of whole-grain paddy rice. The gizzard and proventriculus weights of geese were greater after 600 g/k rice was added to their diets for 21 days (Lu et al., 2011). Paddy rice, which is rich in fiber, may promote the development of the digestive system in poultry through mechanical and chemical stimulation of the gizzard and proventriculus (Sittiya et al., 2011; Jiménez-Moreno et al., 2019). Similarly, the form of feed (pellet vs. crushed) can affect the gastrointestinal tract. Broiler breeder chickens fed grains had greater gizzard and visceral weights than did those fed ground rice (Rao et al., 2000). Compared with finely ground oat hulls, feeding coarsely ground oat hulls to broilers significantly improved the relative weights of the muscular stomach and pancreas, as did increasing the amount of oat hulls fed (Itani et al., 2024). In our study, the paddy rice used was unhusked rice, which may have contained more dietary fiber, and the hulls in the paddy rice diet were more difficult to grind than those in the corn-based diet, which increased retention time in the gizzard. The increase in the gizzard index may have resulted from the milling of whole rice grains, which require more activity to process (Sittiya and Yamauchi, 2014; Sittiya et al., 2016). Numerous studies have reported that coarse grain and fiber components in feed increase the degree of mechanical stimulation of the gizzard (González-Alvarado et al., 2008, 2010; Jiménez-Moreno et al., 2019). In addition, stimulation from grains causes strong contractions of the gizzard muscle and promotes stronger gizzard activity to ensure the complete grinding of grains, which may contribute to the secretion of digestive juices from the proventriculus, mixing of the chyme with gastric juices, and enhanced digestion.

Blood biochemical parameters are important indicators of nutrient metabolism in animals and are important references for assessing animal health. In the present study, feeding paddy rice had no significant effect on the serum TP, ALB, ALT, AST, or immunoglobulin levels, but there was a significant increase in the serum HDL level and a tendency toward an increase in TC fed paddy rice. Studies of other fiber sources have shown similar results; for example, higher serum TC levels occurred in broilers fed organic acid-essential oil plus 3% oat hulls, whereas other serum indices were not significantly affected (Adewole et al., 2021). A wheat bran diet supplemented with carbohydrase was reported to increase serum TC and LDL-C levels in chukar partridge; however, interestingly, both the serum TC and HDL levels decreased after feeding with rice hulls (Mohtashami et al., 2021). In contrast, rats fed insoluble fibers (rice bran and wheat bran) had higher serum or liver TC concentrations than did those fed corn bran (Anderson et al., 1994). The type and the form of feed as well species and experimental conditions may contribute to the diversity of the results, but in general, paddy rice had no significant effect on the liver or blood status of the ducks in the RDC farming system, and the body size indices and organ indices corroborated each other.

The intestinal tract is an important site for the digestion and absorption of nutrients, and the length of the intestinal villi and the depth of the crypts are the most important indicators for assessing intestinal tissue morphology (Pelicano et al., 2005). In this study, the duodenal and ileal villus heights were significantly reduced, and the ileal crypt depth was decreased after ducks were fed paddy rice. Previous studies have shown that the ileal crypt depth was significantly lower in broilers fed diets containing 50% whole-grain paddy rice (WPR) and 25% whole-grain brown rice (BR) than in those fed corn‒soybean meal-based diets and that the ileal VH:CD ratio was significantly greater in broilers in the 50% WPR + 50% BR group (Sittiya et al., 2016). Simultaneous feeding with 30 g/kg beet residue and rice grain and 7.5 or 15 g/kg rice husk significantly decreased the duodenal, jejunal, and ileal villus heights but increased the duodenal crypt depth and VH:CD ratio as well as the jejunal crypt depth in broilers (Sadeghi et al., 2015; Abazari, et al., 2016). This finding is similar to the results of the present study. However, the addition of 30 g/kg rice hulls to broiler diets reportedly increased the duodenal villus height and ileal crypt depth but had no effect on the VH:CD ratio (Sabour et al., 2019). Other studies have reported an increase in duodenal villus height after the addition of 2.5% rice hulls to broiler diets, whereas an addition of 5% had no significant effect (Sittiya et al., 2020). However, the results of numerous studies on the effects of dietary addition of fiber-rich ingredients on the morphology and structure of the animal intestinal tract are inconsistent. Decreased duodenal and jejunal villus height and crypt depth and increased ileal villus height and a VH:CD ratio with increasing dietary fiber proportions (4% to 8% fiber and soybean hulls) have been reported (Tejeda and Kim, 2021). Excessive dietary fiber may abrade the surface of the small-intestinal mucosa, thereby decreasing intestinal villus height (Montagne et al., 2003). The addition of high levels of sugar beet meal (75 g/kg) may adversely affect the structure of the jejunal mucosa; dietary fiber stimulates the development of the gastrointestinal tract in chicks, but excessive amounts may affect the intestinal mucosal structure (Jiménez-Moreno et al., 2013). The higher duodenal VH and VH:CD ratio in ducks in the RCD group relative to those in the RD group is similar to the results of other studies, where excessive fiber intake in the diet may be detrimental to intestinal mucosal structures. However, the low nutritional level of the paddy rice-fed group in this study was not reflected in growth. Previous studies revealed that when fortified with certain essential amino acids, lower dietary crude protein can maintain the production performance of laying hens (Zhou et al., 2021). Accordingly, we speculate that unlike artificial feeding in a captive mode, feeding in the RDC environment used in this study provides possibilities for ducks to forage for other nutrients, which in turn promotes the growth and development of young laying ducks.

The animal gut harbors many microbial species that participate in the digestion and absorption of nutrients as well as energy metabolism and the production of metabolites (Rinttilä and Apajalahti, 2013). To investigate the effect of a paddy rice diet on the intestinal flora of ducks under a RDC system, we analyzed changes in the cecal microflora of ducks via 16S rRNA sequencing. The diversity indices revealed that microbial abundance (Chao1) and diversity (Shannon and Simpson) were not altered by being fed paddy rice, which indicated that RDC farming with paddy rice supplementation had little effect on cecal microbiota species of the ducks. The composition of the microbiota plays an important role in the functioning of the gastrointestinal tract. In this study, Bacteroidota and Firmicutes were dominant at the phylum level and accounted for more than 80% of the gut microbiota, which is similar to previous findings (Qiu et al., 2022). Bacteroidota and Firmicutes are among the major fiber-catabolizing phyla in the intestine and have been shown to contain a range of cellulolytic species that promote the catabolism of dietary cellulose (Zhu et al., 2015; Gao et al., 2022). Firmicutes species promote polysaccharide degradation and are associated with increased nutrient uptake (Louis et al., 2014). Bacteroidota species play an important role in the degradation of carbohydrates, especially starch and pectin (He et al., 2023). In the present study, the relative abundance of Bacteroidota decreased significantly after with the ducks were fed paddy rice, whereas the relative abundance of Firmicutes increased significantly. Previous studies have shown that after consuming a daily dose of 60 g of whole-grain barley (WGB), BR, or an equal mixture of the 2 (BR+WGB) for 4 wk, the proportion of Firmicutes increased, whereas that of Bacteroidota decreased (Martínez et al., 2013). In vitro fermentation of millet by the intestinal flora has also been shown to increase the abundance of Firmicutes and decrease the abundance of Bacteroidota (Sori et al., 2023). An increased abundance of Firmicutes may enhance the ability of the cecum to ferment fiber. We found that the abundance of Bacteroidota, which play a role in carbohydrate metabolism, decreased after the ducks were fed paddy rice, whereas partial substitution did not have a significant effect. This was probably related to high fiber levels in the paddy rice diet, which increased the abundance of Firmicutes and decreased the abundance of Bacteroidota. Moreover, the results of this study revealed that Streptococcaceae, Erysipelotrichaceae, and Ruminococcaceae in Firmicutes increased in abundance after the ducks were fed paddy rice. Streptococcaceae and Erysipelotrichaceae, which are starch- and pectin-utilizing bacteria, are significantly more abundant in soybean hull-fed basal diets, and Erysipelotrichaceae species have polysaccharide degradation abilities that are positively correlated with the levels of short-chain fatty acids in feces (Zhang et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020). Members of Ruminococcaceae are important fiber-degrading bacteria that produce large amounts of cellulases and hemicellulases to degrade fibrous material that cannot be digested by the host and convert it to nutrients (Biddle et al., 2013). At the genus level, feeding ducks with paddy rice increased the relative abundances of Megamonas and Faecalibacterium, which is consistent with studies of broilers fed high-fiber diets and laying hens fed low-fiber diets, in which the abundance of Megamonas increased (Walugembe et al., 2015). Studies have also shown that among the in vitro fermentation methods involving oat arabinoxylan, at the genus level, Megamonas is dominant in the duck intestine (Tian et al., 2019). Faecalibacterium is an important genus that produces short-chain fatty acids in the gut, which contribute to the production of butyrate (Yang et al., 2018). In this study, the abundance of Faecalibacterium increased in ducks fed paddy rice-containing diets. However, Faecalibacterium abundance has been shown to increase in broilers fed low-fiber diets and decrease in those fed high-fiber diets while the opposite results occurred in laying hens (Walugembe et al., 2015). This suggests that changes in bacterial abundance may be influenced by diet type and amount and by species. Studies have shown that these microbial taxa play important roles in the coordination of polysaccharide degradation and utilization (Flint et al., 2012; Sergeant et al., 2014). However, in the present study, members of Prevotellaceae (Prevotellaceae_Ga6A1_group, and Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group) presented significantly lower abundances. Prevotellaceae species are able to utilize nutrients such as dietary fibers and degrade plant polysaccharides, and our opposing results may be related to the type of dietary fibers or the environmental conditions of feeding.

Serum metabolome analysis revealed changes in the serum metabolites after paddy rice feeding. The serum levels of citric acid, a key metabolite involved in metabolic pathways, increased in ducks fed paddy rice, whereas cytidine, cytosine and 4-aminobutyric acid significantly decreased in ducks fed paddy rice. Citric acid is a major intermediate product of the tricarboxylic acid cycle in organisms and is involved in the oxidative catabolic processes of sugars, lipids, and amino acids and in energy metabolism (Arnold et al., 2022). Previous studies showed that serum citric acid levels were significantly reduced in rats fed a high-fat diet and that citric acid levels in the serum became elevated after whole-grain oat intervention (Zhao et al., 2020). Supplementation with paddy rice in the present study may have promoted the TCA cycle to fulfill the energy requirements of the ducks. Various nutrient factors (NSPs, e.g., arabinoxylan) in paddy rice may limit nutrient digestion and absorption (Casas et al., 2019). An increased energy demand from the in vivo TCA cycle may be required to meet increased growth needs, as the ADG and end weight of the ducks were not affected by being fed paddy rice. Moreover, we observed a significant positive correlation between Megamonas and citric acid. Megamonas species can ferment plant polysaccharides to produce short-chain fatty acids (Zhou et al., 2022). Studies have shown that at the genus level, chicken and duck intestines are enriched with Megamonas, members of which can degrade complex polysaccharides from plants (Sergeant et al., 2014; Tian et al., 2019). This may improve nutrient utilization and benefit the host. Cytidine and cytosine are intermediates of pyrimidine metabolism, and their concentrations were reduced after ducks were fed paddy rice in this study. This suggests that being fed paddy rice affected substances involved in the pyrimidine metabolism pathway, which we hypothesize may be related to nucleotide perturbation in vivo. Nucleotides play important roles in biological processes, but the 2 biosynthetic pathways that increase the availability of nucleotides for cellular requirements have high energy and amino acid requirements (Zöllner, 1982; Carver, 1999), and the energy and amino acids from paddy rice may not have been sufficient to justify the high cost of their synthesis. On the other hand, nucleotides are absorbed mainly in the upper part of the small intestine, and the duodenum has a high capacity to absorb nucleotides and metabolites (Bronk and Hastewell, 1987). The morphological structure of the small intestinal villi was affected by paddy rice in this study, and the height of the duodenal, jejunal, and ileal villi decreased. Nucleotide concentrations in the cellular nucleotide metabolic pool play an important role in the development of intestinal villi and epithelial cells (Sato et al., 1999). This interaction may affect pyrimidine metabolism. 4-aminobutyric acid is a nonprotein amino acid that has been shown to improve animal health (Jeong et al., 2020). The level of 4-aminobutyric acid was reduced after the ducks were fed paddy rice, but the reason for this effect could not be determined. Studies have shown that in the gut microflora, Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium are the main taxa that produce 4-aminobutyric acid, especially Bifidobacterium (Yunes et al., 2016). However, our microbiological data revealed a significant decrease in the abundance of Bifidobacterium after paddy rice feeding, and a significant positive correlation was detected between Bifidobacterium and 4-aminobutyric acid. Therefore, the significant decrease in the abundance of this bacterial genus in the cecum may have negatively affected 4-aminobutyric acid production.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, while paddy rice substitution in the complete diet of young laying ducks within the RDC system did not result in statistically significant differences in growth performance, the body weight of ducks showed a downward trend and the ratio of feed to gain gradually increased with the increase of paddy rice addition, suggesting that paddy rice addition may cause potential loss of body weight gain. The limited number of replicates and the high variance among replicates may have contributed to the absence of statistically significant differences among groups. Therefore, in practical application, these findings provide a reference for ecological farming in the RDC system, but further large-scale trials are required for validation. Additionally, the feeding of whole paddy rice did affect the intestinal morphology, intestinal microbiota, and serum metabolism profiles of the ducks. Changes in the intestinal microbiota affect serum metabolites, especially lipid metabolites, by influencing the digestion and absorption of dietary nutrients. Differential lipid metabolites may be involved in the regulation of growth in young laying ducks, but further research is needed to investigate the relationship between paddy rice and lipid metabolism in vivo. The effects of the farming system were not considered in this study, and further exploration of the relationships between different farming systems and gut microbes is needed. This study provides preliminary insights into the use of paddy rice in duck diets in the RDC system. For future commercial applications, larger sample sizes and repeated trials are recommended to thoroughly evaluate the feasibility of paddy rice in duck diets in the RDC system.
