Selenium nanozymes effectively treat rheumatoid arthritis by specifically activating selenoproteins to block oxidative damage and cGAS–STING signaling pathway
Authors: Yang Yang, Yonglan Yang, Wenguan Huang, Xiaoyan Zhong, Shuyang Chen, Zhixiang Huang, Xinghuan Xie, Guanqun Yi, Tianwang Li, Xu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Redox imbalance has become an important factor in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) progression, previous studies showed selenium nanozymes (Se NPs) effectively treated RA by restoring redox. However, the process of Se NPs regulates RA, especially the mechanism of Se NPs activates selenoproteins in inhibiting inflammation remains unclear. Herein, the redox imbalance in RA patients was first analyzed, and selenoproteins associated with macrophage inflammation were identified via mRNA profiling. Subsequently, the synthesized Se NPs with glutathione peroxidase (GPX)-like activity as a supplement, and it was found that Se NPs specially upregulate selenoproteins in macrophages applied to in vitro and in vivo models, furthermore combined with GPX-like activity, Se NPs quickly eliminated reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), restored redox homeostasis in RA, and alleviated symptoms of RA by effectively inhibiting macrophage inflammation. The mechanistic study showed that Se NPs effectively prevent mitochondrial and nDNA damage by specifically activating selenoproteins, blocking the interaction between DNA and cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS), and restraining the activation of cGAS–stimulator of interferon genes (STING) and nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) pathway, thus effectively suppressing macrophage inflammation and treating RA. Therefore, this study reveals the mechanism by which Se NPs inhibit macrophage inflammation, providing a new potential therapeutic target for treating inflammatory diseases.

Introduction

RA is a common connective tissue disease characterized by chronic inflammation and extra-articular damage. The pathogenesis of RA is not yet clear, for a long time, T cell and B cell-mediated autoimmune responses have been considered to be the main factors in the development of RA1, 2, 3. Therefore, there are a series of drugs targeting these two cells for RA therapy and have achievedsignificant progress. However, with the continuous growth of RA patients and the complex immune mechanisms involved, nearly 20% of patients remain insensitive to traditional therapy4,5. Therefore, it is urgent to develop new therapeutic targets to improve the treatment outcomes for RA, especially refractory RA. In recent years, a large amount of data has shown that innate immune cells play an upstream signaling role in the persistent activation of inflammatory arthritis6,7. In the early stages of RA, the number of synovial macrophages in joints significantly increases and exhibits strong pro-inflammatory characteristics8. In 2020, a cohort study showed that specific subtypes of tissue macrophages expressing tyrosine protein kinase Mer receptor were significantly present in the synovial membranes of RA patients, these macrophages exhibited stronger inflammatory responses and immune memory functions when reactivated9. Further researches also found that synovial macrophages in RA patients exhibit enhanced glycolytic metabolism, which is closely related to the increased release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in macrophages10,11. These studies have preliminarily elucidated the important role and functional mechanisms of synovial macrophages in the development of RA. Although there are currently no drugs targeting macrophages for treating RA, the studies indicate that macrophages are a potential target for RA therapy.

Recent studies have found that glycolytic abnormalities in synovial macrophages in RA microenvironments can lead to severe redox imbalance, producing excessive RONS that not only directly cause oxidative damage to articular cartilage, but also act as amplifiers of the inflammatory response12. Fearon et al.13 further confirmed through arthroscopy that there is a significant redox imbalance in the synovial tissue of RA patients, and found that mitochondrial mutations induced by redox imbalance are positively correlated with the inflammatory response. Notably, the level of mitochondrial mutations significantly decreases in RA patients after treatment. Lee et al.14 further identified that peroxiredoxin 6 peroxide is an important factor affecting redox imbalance in the RA microenvironment; their results showed that the overexpression of peroxiredoxin 6 peroxide in macrophages exacerbated inflammatory responses, concomitant with JNK pathway activation, thereby amplifying synovial inflammation and accelerating RA pathogenesis. Recent advances have revealed that excessive RONS in the RA microenvironment rapidly induce mitochondrial and nDNA damage in macrophages. The damaged mtDNA and genomic DNA readily form autoantigen complexes with associated proteins, thereby exacerbating inflammatory responses15. Mechanistically, the DNA sensor cGAS specifically recognizes these damaged DNA fragments and catalyzes cyclic GMP–AMP synthesis, potently activating the cGAS–STING signaling pathway. This triggers downstream immune activation, amplifying the inflammatory cascade16,17. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that redox imbalance in the RA microenvironment critically drives synovitis and disease progression through DNA damage-mediated autoinflammation.

Based on the role of redox imbalance in RA, it has been hypothesized that modulating the redox imbalance in the RA microenvironment could ameliorate disease symptoms and halt RA progression. Xiao et al.18 and Guo et al.19 developed iron and platinum catalytic treatment systems, which effectively relieved the inflammatory response and RA symptoms after rapidly eliminating RONS in macrophages and inhibiting macrophage M1 polarization. Based on the important role of selenium (Se) as a necessary element in regulating redox balance and immune stability20, 21, 22, 23, our team was inspired by selenium-containing biological enzymes to develop a series of Se NPs with GPX-like activity. Through constructing Se NPs, previous studies have found that they not only showed good biocompatibility, but also could eliminate excess RONS in macrophages, alleviate mitochondrial inflammation-induced redox imbalance; furthermore, Se NPs could inhibit inflammatory damage and alleviate the progression of RA through blocking the amplification of RONS on the inflammatory response24, 25, 26, 27. However, the mechanism of Se NPs in modulating the macrophage inflammatory response in RA after inhibiting oxidative damage has not been clarified. Especially with the clear role of selenoproteins in osteoclast differentiation and regulation of bone-related diseases28, 29, 30, it is even more necessary to further reveal how Se NPs regulate the process and mechanism of inhibiting inflammatory responses and alleviating RA after internalization by synovial macrophages.

In this study, in order to further clarify the mechanism of Se NPs in regulating macrophage inflammatory response for RA treatment, clinical samples of RA patients were collected and used mRNA sequencing to investigate the relationship between expression imbalance of selenoproteins and inflammation in monocytes/macrophages, and the results of synovial tissue showed positive for free dsDNA from RA patients, thereby further confirming the relationship between redox imbalance–selenoproteins–inflammation in RA. Subsequently, according to previous reports, we synthesized Se NPs with GPX-like activity and applied them as selenoprotein supplements to inflammatory macrophages and CIA model mice. In those models, it is further verified and found that Se NPs could specifically activate the expression of selenoproteins in macrophages, combined with their GPX-like activity, effectively inhibited macrophage inflammatory responses, and alleviated symptoms of RA by restoring redox balance in the RA microenvironment. Mechanistic studies revealed that after Se NPs restored redox balance, Se NPs could prevent mitochondrial and nDNA damage, thereby blocking the interaction between free DNA and cGAS, and subsequently interrupting the activation of the cGAS–STING pathway and its cascade the NF-κB pathway, thus effectively inhibiting macrophage inflammatory responses and treating RA (Scheme 1). Materials and methods

Assessment of human samples

The human clinical samples used in this study have been approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial Second People's Hospital and conducted according to the provisions of the Helsinki Declaration (Approval No.: 2023-KY-KZ-256,2023-11-30); all the samples used in the study and all the sample providers gave their informed consent and agreed to the experiments. Each participant had thoroughly understood the research protocol and signed a written consent form. Additionally, the immunofluorescence was performed on synovial tissue sections separated from the knee joint of a patient with RA to verify the level of dsDNA. The detailed analysis process of mRNA sequencing can be found in the supporting materials.

Synthesis and character of Se NPs

The Se NPs were synthesized using the following method according to previous reports24: an appropriate volume of 20 mmol/L sodium selenite solution (5 mL) was prepared, 5 mL of which was fully mixed with 30 mg of polyvinylpyrrolidone in a beaker, and subsequently 5 mL of 40 mmol/L cysteine aqueous solution was slowly added into the mixture under stirring in an ice-water bath for 2 h. After the reaction, the product was centrifuged and washed with deionized water to obtain Se NPs. The following methods were used to characterize synthesized Se NPs. First, the distribution, morphology, and size of Se NPs were measured by using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM2100F, HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan). Then, the size of Se NPs was measured by using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Zetasizer Advance Pro, Malvern Panalytical, Malvern City, England). Finally, the electronic state of Se NPs was detected by using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, K-Alpha+, Thermo Fisher, Waltham City, MA, USA).

The cell counting kit-8 test

RAW264.7 cells (from ATCC of Department of Rheumatology and Immunology, Guangdong Second Provincial General Hospital) were inoculated into a 96-well plate, with the culture medium consisting of DMEM (Vivacell, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Vivacell, Shanghai, China) and 1 mL penicillin–streptomycin (GenXion, Guangzhou, China).Then the Se NPs were added to 96-well plate and adjusted its concentration to 1, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, and 500 μmol/L, respectively, and incubated at 37.5 °C, in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 h. After changing the medium, 10 μL Counting Kit-8 (GOONIE, Guangzhou, China) was added to 96-well plate and co-incubate for 1 h. Then the OD450 value was determined in the dark using an enzyme immunoassay reader (ELx800, BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA), and cell viability was calculated to screen the optimal concentration of Se NPs within 24 h.

Detection of Se NPs in cellular uptake and in vivo metabolism

To investigate the cellular uptake and distribution of Se NPs in RAW 264.7 cells, RAW264.7 cells were seeded onto the glass-bottom cell culture plates and treated with 100 μmol/L Cy3-labeled Se NPs for 0, 1, 2, and 4 h, respectively. After washing with PBS, the cells were stained for 20 min using the Cell Plasma Membrane Staining Kit with DiO (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), followed by another PBS wash. Subsequently, the cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 Staining Solution for Live Cells (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 10 min, washed again with PBS, and then incubated in serum-free medium. Similarly, Mito-Tracker Green staining was performed for 10 min to analyze the co-localization of Se NPs and mitochondria. Finally, the cells were observed under a confocal microscope (LSM800, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Additionally, the Cy3 absorption peaks of different groups were analyzed using a flow cytometer (FACSAriaII, Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lake, NJ, USA).

To investigate the metabolism of Se NPs in cells, the high performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC–MS) was used to analyze the changes of selenoamino acids in RAW 264.7 cells after treatment with Se NPs. Firstly, RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 10 × 105 cells per milliliter in 6-well plate (divided into control and treatment groups). The treatment group was supplemented with Se NPs at a final concentration of 20 μmol/L, and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 4 h. After removing the culture medium, the cells were washed three times with PBS, and the cell concentration was balanced using a cell counter. The cell pellet was collected by centrifugation and resuspended in 1 mL PBS. After sonication, enzymes (4 mg/mL trypsin and 4 mg/mL proteinase K) were added, followed by digestion at 37 °C for 6 h. Finally, the sample was centrifuged, and the supernatant was used for HPLC–MS system (LC/TQ-6495, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) analysis, with reference to the standards of selenocysteine and selenomethionine (Weiyel, Xinyang, China).

The products in the sample solution were separated by using an Agilent InfinityLab Poroshell 120 Aq-C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.7 μm, AgilentTechnologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) in reverse-phase chromatography (RP-HPLC); the column temperature was 40 °C, injection volume: 2 μL. Elution was achieved with a gradient of 0% to 98% (v/v) acetonitrile in 0.1% (v/v) formic acid at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min over 8 min.

Mass spectrometer conditions: mass spectra of were recorded using a LC–MS system (LC/TQ-6495, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) with electrospray positive ion source mode ionization (electrospray ionization, ESI+). To investigate the metabolism of Se NPs in vivo, mice were intraperitoneally injected with Se NPs labeled with Cy3 at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Blood samples were collected from the tail vein at different time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 18, and 24 h), and serum was separated to measure the absorbance of Cy3 (OD550) over different time points using the NanoPhotometer N60 (N60, Implen, Munich, Germany). Meanwhile, the metabolic distribution of Se NPs in mice was analyzed at different time points (0, 0.5, 1, 2, and 24 h) after administration using a small animal in vivo imaging system (In-vivo Xtreme II, Bruker, Billerica, Germany).

The detection of GPX activity

The Total Glutathione Peroxidase Assay Kit with DTNB (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to measure the GPX activity of Se NPs, with natural GPX as the control. The optimal concentration and time of Se NPs were screened on RAW264.7 cells by detecting GPX activity, and evaluated oxidative damage model in vitro. RAW264.7 cells (1 × 105) were inoculated into a 6-well plate. First, the Se NPs concentration was adjusted to 1, 10, 20, 50, and 100 μmol/L, respectively, and added to 6-well plate were incubated with cells in the incubator for 24 h to screen the optimal concentration. Then, cells were treated with the optimal concentration for 0, 1, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h to screen the optimal time. After determining the optimal pretreatment conditions for Se NPs, RAW 264.7 cells were stimulated with 1 μg/mL LPS (from Escherichia coli O55:B55, Sigma–Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) and (or) 100 μmol/L H2O2 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 24 h to establish an oxidative damage model, and GPX activity was detected to evaluate the intervention effect of Se NPs on the model.

RAW264.7 cells (1 × 105) were inoculated into a 6-well plate, then cells were equally divided into four groups (Control, LPS, LPS + H2O2, and LPS + H2O2 + Se NPs), first, the cells in group LPS + H2O2 + Se NPs were pretreated with 20 μmol/L of Se NPs for 12 h, followed by replacement with fresh medium. Subsequently, the LPS, LPS + H2O2, and LPS + H2O2 + Se NPs groups were stimulated with 1 μg/mL LPS and (or) 100 μmol/L H2O2 for 24 h. After stimulation, cells from each group after different treatments were lysed with Cell lysis buffer for Western and IP (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to prepare samples, which were then measured for protein concentration using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher, Waltham City, MA, USA). To detect GPX activity, glutathione peroxidase detection buffer, 10 mmol/L GSH solution, samples (or equal volumes of Milli-Q pure water) and 15 mmol/L peroxide reagent solution were mixed in accordance with the instructions and incubated for 10 min. Subsequently, DTNB solution was added and mixed thoroughly followed by another 10 min of incubation. Finally, the OD412 was measured using a NanoPhotometer N60 (Implen, Munich), and GPX activity was calculated based on the corresponding formula.

The detection of H2O2, NO and malondialdehyde (MDA)

The Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit, Nitric Oxide Assay Kit and Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit were purchased from Beyotime, Co., Ltd., China. The intracellular H2O2, NO and MDA were measured respectively to assess the levels of RONS and lipid peroxidation. The grouping was the same as in Section 2.5, and the LPS + H2O2 + Se NPs group was pretreated with Se NPs (20 μmol/L) for 12 h, subsequently, the LPS, LPS + H2O2, and LPS + H2O2 + Se NPs groups were stimulated with 1 μg/mL LPS and (or) 100 μmol/L H2O2 for 24 h, After stimulation, each group of cells was collected and lysed, the lysed samples were normalized based on protein concentration. To detect H2O2, samples were mixed with hydrogen peroxide detection reagent at a volume ratio of 1:2 (v/v), and the OD560 was determined. To detect NO, samples were blended with Griess Reagent I and Griess Reagent II at a volume ratio of 1:1:1 (v/v/v), followed by the measurement of OD540. To detect MDA, samples were mixed with MDA detection working solution at a volume ratio of 1:2 (v/v), heated at 100 °C for 15 min, cooled and centrifuged, and the OD532 was then measured. After all determinations, the contents of H2O2, NO and MDA were calculated according to the standard curves.

The detection of mitochondrial damage

The ROS Assay Kit, NO Assay Kit with DAF-FM DA, Mitochondrial Calcium Assay Kit with Rhod-2 AM, Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos, Mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit with JC-1 and Mitochondrial Permeability Transition Pore Assay Kit or mPTP Assay Kit were purchased from Beyotime, Co., Ltd., China. The mitochondrial damage was assessed by detecting changes in ROS, NO, mitochondrial calcium, mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial permeability transition pore on the phenotype. In addition, live cells were double-stained with Propidium Iodide (excitation wavelength 535 nm, emission wavelength 617 nm, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and Calcein AM after 24 h. Finally, TEM was used to observe the mitochondrial morphology of cells in each group. RAW 264.7 cells were inoculated into glass-bottom cell culture plates, the grouping was the same as in Section 2.5, and the LPS + H2O2 + Se NPs group was pretreated with Se NPs (20 μmol/L) for 12 h. Then the cells were stimulated with LPS (1 μg/mL) and (or) H2O2 (200 μmol/L) for 2 h, followed by subsequent detection.

To detect ROS, cells were incubated in a medium containing 10 μmol/L DCFH-DA for 20 min, washed, replaced with fresh medium, and observed by fluorescence microscopy (excitation wavelength of 488 nm, emission wavelength of 525 nm, Axio Imager A2, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). To detect NO, cells were incubated in a medium containing 5 μmol/L DAF-FM DA for 20 min, washed, replaced with new medium, and observed under fluorescence microscopy (excitation wavelength of 495 nm, emission wavelength of 515 nm, Zeiss). To detect mitochondrial calcium, cells were incubated in a medium containing 10 μmol/L Rhod-2 AM for 20 min, washed, replaced with new medium, and observed under fluorescence microscopy (excitation wavelength 552 nm, emission wavelength 581 nm, Zeiss). To detect mitochondrial membrane potential, cells were incubated in a medium containing 200 nmol/L Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos for 20 min, washed, replaced with new medium, and observed under fluorescence microscopy (excitation wavelength 579 nm, emission wavelength 599 nm, Zeiss). Additionally, the cells were treated with JC-1 staining buffer for 20 min. For detecting JC-1 monomers, the excitation wavelength was set at 490 nm and the emission wavelength at 530 nm; for detecting JC-1 aggregates, the excitation wavelength was set at 525 nm and the emission wavelength at 590 nm. To detect mitochondrial permeability transition, cells were incubated in a medium containing 1 × Calcein AM and CoCl2 for 30 min, washed, incubated again for 30 min with a new medium, and observed under fluorescence microscopy (excitation wavelength 494 nm, emission wavelength 517 nm, Zeiss).

The detection of nDNA damage

DNA Damage Comet Assay Kit/Comet Assay Kit, Comet Assay Slide and pH 7.5 Tris-HCl (Sterile, DNase free) were purchased from Beyotime, Co., Ltd., China. pH 8.0 RNase-free EDTA solution and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 99%) were purchased from Macklin, Co., Ltd., China. The cells were grouped and treated in the same way as in Section 2.7, collected and resuspended in PBS at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. According to the instructions, the preheated 1% normal melting point agarose was dropped into the circular wells of the comet assay slides, and after solidification, it was prepared as the first layer of gel. Next, the cell suspensions were mixed with 0.7% low-melting-point agarose at 37 °C evenly, then it was quickly and uniformly dropped onto the first layer of gel to solidify and form the second layer of gel. The slide was immersed into the pre-cooled lysis buffer, lysed at 4 °C for 1 h, then washed with PBS. After the lysis was completed, the slides were placed in a horizontal electrophoresis tank and soaked in the electrophoresis buffer (pure water containing 1 mmol/L EDTA pH 8.0, 200 mmol/L NaOH) for 20 min, followed by electrophoresis at 25 V for 30 min. After electrophoresis, the slides were immersed in a neutralization buffer (pure water containing 0.4 mol/L Tris-HCl pH 7.5) for neutralization. Finally, the gel was stained with propidium iodide in the dark for 20 min, washed and covered with cover slips, and observed under fluorescence microscope (excitation wavelength 535 nm, emission wavelength 617 nm, Zeiss). The CASP software was used to measure DNA intensity and tail moment.

The RT-qPCR analysis of mtDNA

RT-qPCR was used to detect mtDNA in the culture medium to assess the level of leakage. The cell Mitochondria DNA Isolation Kit (SaintBio, Shanghai, China) was used for the isolation and extraction of mtDNA. The grouping and treatment method of the cells was the same as in Section 1, 2.7 mL of medium was taken for later use. According to the instructions, the medium was mixed with Homogenization Buffer (1/1, v/v). Then the mixed medium was centrifuged at 4 °C and 1000 rpm for 10 min (TGL-1850, Shuke Instrument, Chengdu, China), and the supernatant was collected, and centrifuged at 4 °C and 14,000 rpm for 30 min (TGL-1850, Shuke Instrument, Chengdu, China). The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was retained. Next, the pellet was resuspended in 480 μL of High Salt Buffer. After 75 μL of Detergent Buffer was added, and it was incubated at 55 °C for 60 min. Then, 200 μL of Super High Salt Buffer was added andmixed well, and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C (TGL-1850, Shuke Instrument, Chengdu, China) to collect the supernatant containing mtDNA. The supernatant was mixed with 2 volumes of absolute ethanol (Gschemical, Guangzhou, China), centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min (TGL-1850, Shuke Instrument, Chengdu, China). The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet containing mtDNA was retained. Finally, the precipitate was mixed with 0.5 mL of Washing Buffer for repeated washing, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min (TGL-1850, Shuke Instrument, Chengdu, China), and the precipitate was resuspended in 50 μL of End Solution. The extracted mtDNA can then be used for RT-qPCR (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Finally, the precipitate was mixed with 0.5 mL of Washing Buffer for repeated washing, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min (TGL-1850, Shuke Instrument, Chengdu, China), and the mtDNA precipitate was resuspended in 50 μL of End Solution for RT-qPCR.

The fast Taq SYBR green qPCR mix (GOONIE, Guangzhou, China) was used for RT-qPCR reactions, the reaction process was as follows: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 10 s, extension at 30 s, repeated for 40 cycles, and the relative gene expression was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method. The primer sequence is listed in Supporting Information Table S1.

The ELISA of pro-inflammatory cytokines in vitro

ELISA is used to assess the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines secreted by cells. The mouse TNF-α, IL-6 and mouse IL-1β ELISA kits were purchased from ABclonal Co., China. RAW 264.7 cells were inoculated into a 6-well plate, the grouping was the same as in Section 2.5, and the LPS + H2O2 + Se NPs group was pretreated with Se NPs (20 μmol/L) for 12 h, then the cells were stimulated with LPS (1 μg/mL) and (or) H2O2 (100 μmol/L) for 24 h.

According to the instructions, first, the samples were added to the washed coated microplates and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h (the subsequent steps were the same), then the biotin-labeled antibodies were added for 1 h, followed by streptavidin-HRP incubation for 30 min, then TMB substrate incubation for 20 min, and finally the reaction was stopped by adding the stop solution. After the reaction was completed, the coated microplates were placed in an enzyme immunoassay reader (BioTek, Winooski) to measure OD450, and the concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines were calculated using the standard curve.

Animals and animal models

15 DBA/1 mice (male, 7–8 weeks) were purchased from Guangdong GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., China. Animal care was in accordance with the institutional guidelines and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangdong Second Provincial General Hospital (Approval number: 2023-DW-KZ-100-02,2024-04-11). Immunization grade bovine type II collagen (2 mg/mL) and complete Freund's adjuvant (1 mg/mL) were purchased from Chondrex Inc., USA. The collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model was established according to the instructions. The first immunization was defined as Day 1. Type II collagen was mixed with complete Freund's adjuvant at a 1:1 volume ratio (v/v) to prepare an emulsion, which was subsequently subcutaneously injected into the tails of 10 DBA/1 mice at a dose of 100 μL per mouse to complete the primary immunization. Then, after 21 days, the same amount of emulsified solution was subcutaneously injected into the tails of these mice again to complete the secondary immunization. The remaining 5 mice were used as the normal control group. From Day 1, the general condition of the mice was observed and body weight was recorded.

The therapy of CIA model mice

10 DBA/1 CIA mice were randomly divided into two groups (PBS and Se NPs groups), where the Se NPs group received daily intraperitoneal injection of Se NPs (1 mg/kg) daily starting from the first day after the second immunization (Day 22), while the PBS group was given an equal volume of PBS intraperitoneal injection daily. Starting from Day 22, the left ankle thickness of each mouse was measured daily, and at the same time, the arthritis scores of the mice's four limbs were assessed according to the standard. Standard evaluation arthritis scores: the joint was rated as 0 if there was no sign of erythema or swelling; 1 if the joint had any erythema or mild swelling; 2 if erythema and mild swelling extended from the ankle to the tarsus; 3 if erythema and moderate swelling extended from the ankle joint to the metatarsal joint; 4 if the joint had erythema and severe swelling including ankles, paws, and stiffness of fingers or limbs22.

The inflammation and micro-CT detection of CIA model mice

To assess the serum inflammatory levels in mice, ELISA was used to detect the main inflammatory cytokines in the serum. First, whole blood was collected via eyeball puncture. The serum samples were centrifuged at 6500 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected. The collected supernatant samples were immediately tested using the TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β and IL-18 ELISA kits, following the same procedure as in Section 2.12.

To assess the degree of bone and joint destruction in mice, limbs were stripped of skin and muscle and fixed in 10% formaldehyde solution (GenXion, Shanghai, China). After three days, the limbs were taken out and scanned using micro-CT (SkyScan1276, Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany). Subsequently, the ZKKS-Micro CT 4.1 workstation was used for image reconstruction to generate a three-dimensional (3D) image of the bone joint and measure the ratio of bone surface to bone volume (BS/TV), and calculate the bone mineral density (BMD) and tissue mineral density (TMD) values of the bone to assess bone mass loss.

The pathological analysis

On Day 60 of the experiment, after euthanizing the mice, pathological sections were made from the joints below the knee. These sections were stained with H&E, Masson's trichrome, Safranin O-Fast Green, and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) to assess histological changes in joint tissue. Additionally, immunohistochemistry (p-TBK1, p-NF-κB P65, p-STING, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) and immunofluorescence (dsDNA, GPX4, SELW, TFAM, BAX, and BCL-2) were performed to explore the biological mechanisms of the lesions and treatment in the mice's joints. The images were obtained by fluorescence microscopy (Oberkochen).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), based on at least three independent experiments. A P-value less than 0.05 is considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was conducted using Prism 8.0.1 software, and multiple group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

To assess the relationship between selenoproteins, redox imbalance and inflammation in RA, the differential genes of monocytes from healthy individuals and RA patients were analyzed through mRNA sequencing. GO enrichment analysis showed that the differentially expressed genes were enriched in three aspects: biological process (immune response and cell activation), cellular component (cytoplasm and cell periphery), and molecular function (protein binding and kinase binding) (Fig. 1B), and these genes are closely related to oxidative damage and inflammatory diseases (Supporting Information Figs. Selenoproteins as an important antioxidant enzyme, which not only can synergistically eliminate excessive RONS to protect tissues from oxidative damage, but also regulate osteoclast activity to protect cartilage tissue31, 32, 33. However, due to the continuous inflammatory response, the antioxidant enzyme system in the body is insufficient to resist the continuous redox imbalance and inflammatory damage, resulting in the leakage of inflammatory substances becoming new damaging molecules, which feedback and amplify the inflammatory response, ultimately forming a mutually reinforcing effect of inflammation and redox imbalance cycle.

Therefore, from the results of comprehensive sequencing, on one hand, monocytes/macrophages in RA patients may produce excessive RONS after redox imbalance, and induce the cascade reaction with high levels of inflammation and changes in antioxidant enzymes, leading to oxidant damage, which in turn increases cfDNA. On the other hand, the reduction of DNase further contributes to the increase in cfDNA levels. This excess cfDNA may be sensed by cGAS, thereby activating the cGAS–STING signaling pathway, which ultimately leads to the phosphorylation of NF-κB through a series of cascading reactions, increasing the transcription of inflammatory factors. Overall, oxidant damage may amplify RA inflammation, which requires further experimental verification.

Therefore, the mitochondrial membrane potential (red arrow, Fig. 5F), membrane permeability and morphology (red arrow, Fig. 5G) of RAW 264.7 cells remained largely intact, and the corresponding Ca2+ overload and mtDNA leakage in mitochondria were effectively inhibited. In general, the mitochondrial damage fully demonstrated that Se NPs restored the redox imbalance through their outstanding GPX activity, and mitochondria are their main target and regulatory site.

Se NPs resist nDNA damage in macrophage

The mitochondrial damage causing redox imbalance will lead to the leakage of RONS and other damage factors, which will enhance and amplify inflammation through a series of chain reaction pathways38. The nDNA damage caused by RONS is an important pathway among those, the damaged DNA, when released, is prone to form self-antigens and combine with cGAS to activate cGAS–STING pathway, thereby further enhancing the inflammatory response36,37. Therefore, to further verify the regulatory mechanism of Se NPs in selenoproteins–redox imbalance–inflammation relationship axis after restoring the redox imbalance, the activity of Se NPs in resisting nDNA damage were also explored. As shown in Fig. 6A, γ-H2AX as an important specific marker of DNA damage was firstly detected, the results showed that the macrophages were not damaged under normal conditions, due to without the generation of γ-H2AX, the green fluorescence of the γ-H2AX probe was hardly detectable. However, after stimulation with LPS or LPS combined with H2O2, the immunofluorescence of γ-H2AX was enhanced, especially under the combined LPS and H2O2, the fluorescence of γ-H2AX significantly increased, which indicated that there has been severe damage to the nDNA. Subsequently, it is found that the fluorescence significantly decreased after Se NPs treatment, indicating that nDNA damage was effectively prevented by inhibiting the production of γ-H2AX. In addition, the detection of 8-OhdG another important specific marker of DNA damage showed same trends with the detection of γ-H2AX. The results also clearly showed there was a large amount of 8-OHdG produced under the stimulation of LPS combined with H2O2, due to the intense redox imbalance. However, after treatment with Se NPs, restored the redox imbalance, the nDNA damage was prevented and the production of 8-OHdG was inhibited. Consequently, the fluorescence also rapidly weakened (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, the comet assay was used to further directly visualized the damaging effect of nDNA. As shown in Fig. 6C, the DNA fluorescence showed shorter comet tails without nDNA damage, but the comet tails will increase in length as the severity of nDNA damage increases39. In LPS group, the length of the comet tail was significantly longer than that in the control group. And with the combined effect of LPS and H2O2, nDNA damage increased, which led to an even longer comet tail. In addition, it also found that the length of the comet tail significantly shortened after treated with Se NPs, after quantification, compared to the combined LPS and H2O2 group, it showedthat after treatment with Se NPs, the tail DNA decreased by nearly 2-fold (Fig. 6D) and the olive tail moment decreased nearly 10-fold (Fig. 6E). Correspondingly, significant M1 polarization was observed in RAW 264.7 cells following 2 h of LPS stimulation, whereby the cells exhibited obvious morphological changes with increased pseudopodia alongside elevated CD86 expression. However, after Se NPs treatment, the morphological changes and CD86 expression were effectively inhibited, thereby reducing polarization (Supporting Information Fig. S13). Therefore, it can be concluded that the oxidative imbalance can directly induce nDNA damage and actively promote the M1 inflammatory polarization of macrophages. Se NPs regulate the selenoproteins–redox imbalance–inflammation relationship axis in macrophage via cGAS–STING signaling pathway

Treatment of CIA mice with Se NPs in vivo

CIA mice are a commonly used animal model for studying RA45. To construct the CIA model, as shown in Fig. 8A, the CIA models were successfully established after the second immunization (Day 22) by measuring left ankle thickness and arthritis scores. Subsequently, the CIA mice were randomly divided into two groups (PBS and Se NPs), and the healthy mice as control group. On the Day 60, as the symptoms of the CIA mice in Se NPs group significantly relieved, it was concluded the treatment. During treatment, the CIA mice in PBS group was worse than that in Se NPs group, manifested as more severe emaciation, messy hair, and appetite (Fig. 8B). In addition, with the treatment time, the symptoms of the mice in each group showed significant differences, the CIA mice compared to health mice, those with CIA mice exhibited significantly increased ankle joint thickness following RA induction, which progressively worsened with ongoing inflammation. However, in the Se NPs treated group, a turning point occurred on Day 44, whereby the swelling gradually receded. By the end of the treatment period, the ankle thickness in this group was approximately 3.0 mm which was about 1.2 mm less than that of the PBS group and close to the 2.5 mm in normal mice (Fig. 8C–E), indicating that Se NPs effectively alleviated RA symptoms. The clinical arthritis score provided further evidence of the therapeutic effect of Se NPs. At the endpoint of treatment, the average clinical score in the Se NPs treated group was around 3, in contrast to a score of 10 in the PBS group (Fig. 8D), demonstrated that Se NPs significantly mitigate joint inflammation and contribute to the amelioration of RA. Subsequently, the therapeutic effect in joint damage of Se NPs was further evaluated through gait analysis. As shown in Fig. 8F, the health mice in control group exhibited a regular walking trajectory, longer stride length, higher movement speed, and shorter duration time, reflecting unaffected joint function46. In contrast, PBS-treated mice, which received no therapy, showed significantly impaired locomotion, characterized by disorganized steps, reduced stride length, decreased movement speed (Fig. 8G), and prolonged duration time (Fig. 8H). Conclusions

In this study, to further clarify the regulatory mechanisms of Se NPs-which have shown outstanding efficacy in mitigating redox dysregulation in RA, both in vitro and in vivo research was conducted to systematically investigate the regulatory role of Se NPs in the selenoproteins–redox imbalance–inflammation relationship axis. After confirming a state of redox imbalance in clinical samples from RA patients and identifying selenoproteins associated with both redox dysregulation and inflammatory progression via mRNA sequencing, it was demonstrated that Se NPs specifically upregulate multiple selenoproteins, enhance antioxidant defense, suppress mitochondrial and nDNA damage in macrophages, and consequently inhibit DNA–cGAS interaction and the activation of the cGAS–STING and downstream NF-κB signaling pathways. Thus, a molecular mechanism was elucidated whereby Se NPs activate selenoproteins, restore redox homeostasis, prevent oxidative damage, and inhibit macrophage-driven inflammation. The CIA model further confirmed the role of Se NPs in treating RA through modulation of the selenoproteins–redox imbalance–inflammation relationship axis regulatory network. Additionally, Se NPs were found to exert protective effects on cartilage by upregulating specific selenoproteins. In summary, this study provides new insights into the pathogenesis and treatment of rheumatoid arthritis and offers novel strategic directions for RA therapy using Se-based nanomedicines.
