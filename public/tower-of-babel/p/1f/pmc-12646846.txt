Investigation of the nutritional applications of therapeutic diet for swallowing disorders at different IDDSI levels
Authors: Yi Cheng, Muxi Chen, Ke Li, Jiuming Yan, Wen Hu, Lei Shi, Yuan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the nutritional applicability of balanced therapeutic diets for elderly individuals (aged 65 and above) with dysphagia, using the International Dysphagia Diet Standardization Initiative (IDDSI) and the 2022 Chinese Dietary Guidelines as dual frameworks. Ingredient data were collected from “Meishichina”, a Chinese culinary database, and processed into dishes complying with IDDSI levels 1–7. Nutritional values were calculated using West China Hospital Nutrition Software, and statistical analyses, including ANOVA and Spearman correlation, were conducted to compare nutrients across IDDSI levels. Results showed significant variation in protein, fat, and carbohydrate content among food categories and IDDSI levels (p≤0.05), with meats and eggs contributing mainly to protein and fat, while fruits and grains contributed carbohydrates. Spearman analysis revealed positive correlations between IDDSI levels and energy, protein, and fat intake (p≤0.05). Only IDDSI level 7 met the elderly-specific target of 1,000 ‍kcal and 60 ‍g protein per meal. To address the nutritional gaps in other levels, supplementation with “Yi Quan Su” enteral nutrition powder was calculated based on the deficits. These findings highlight the nutritional limitations of texture-modified diets at lower IDDSI levels and emphasize the importance of targeted supplementation to meet the needs of older adults with dysphagia.

Introduction

Dysphagia, or swallowing difficulties, refers to abnormalities that occur when food or liquid moves from the mouth to the stomach during swallowing. These abnormalities may be due to neurological diseases, muscular dysfunctions, or anatomical irregularities. Additionally, swallowing functions tend to deteriorate with physiological aging and are often further impacted by prevalent chronic diseases among elderly people, such as diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and neurological disorders, leading to progressive functional impairments.(1) Studies indicate that the prevalence of dysphagia among Chinese elderly individuals reaches as high as 66.0%, varying with age: 21.0% among those aged 60–69, 28.0% among those aged 70–79, and 41.0% among those aged over 80. Furthermore, patients posttreatment for head and neck cancers and poststroke exhibit high dysphagia incidence rates of approximately 50–60% and 37–78%, respectively.(2,3) Dysphagia not only impairs nutritional intake but is also linked to several health complications, including malnutrition, dehydration, and aspiration pneumonia.(4) Research on stroke patients suggests that approximately 50% of stroke patients experience swallowing difficulties postonset, with many developing malnutrition due to inadequate nutritional intake.(5)

Nutrition plays a pivotal role in the management of dysphagia, where maintaining a good nutritional status is crucial for supporting patient health and enhancing recovery capabilities.(6) To address the challenges posed by dysphagia, the International Dysphagia Diet Standardization Initiative (IDDSI) was established. Initiated in 2013 by an international interdisciplinary team of healthcare professionals, scholars, industry representatives, and patient advocates, the IDDSI proposed a framework in 2016 for standardizing dysphagia diets globally through universally applicable, standardized operational procedures and sensory evaluations.(7) This framework enables medical professionals to more effectively match a patient’s swallowing capabilities with the provided diet, thus reducing the risks of swallowing difficulties and associated complications. Studies have shown that the adoption of IDDSI standards can significantly reduce the incidence of aspiration, thereby enhancing the overall nutritional status of patients.(8,9)

Despite these advances, research on the specific nutritional components of diets at different IDDSI levels and their capacity to meet particular nutritional needs remains limited. There is a shortage of systematic studies regarding whether diets at various IDDSI levels can fulfill specific nutritional requirements and how to maintain and optimize nutritional components within diets at different IDDSI levels, especially among high-risk groups such as elderly individuals.

With the increasing aging population, the nutritional needs of specific groups, particularly elderly individuals, have garnered extensive attention. This study aimed to explore the nutritional applications of the therapeutic diet at different IDDSI levels, with a special focus on how these diets can be adapted to meet the specific dietary and nutritional recommendations for elderly people as outlined in the “Chinese Dietary Guidelines (2022)”.(10) Acknowledging the variations in food texture and composition, this study analyzes the nutritional content per 100 ‍g of different food categories at each IDDSI level. It is important to note that analyzing food content by weight (100 ‍g) might differ significantly depending on the food type (e.g., 100 ‍g of carrot sticks compared to 100 ‍g of double cream) due to the variation in nutritional profiles between different foods. But this approach is intended to standardize comparisons. By analyzing the nutritional content per 100 ‍g of different food categories at each IDDSI level and integrating the dietary guidelines’ specific recommendations for elderly nutrition, this study calculates and optimizes the dietary nutrient intake for patients with dysphagia. This ensures that nutritional needs are met while maintaining the safety and palatability of foods. Through this approach, we provide more scientific and personalized nutritional support plans for patients.

Methods

This study did not involve experiments on humans or the use of human tissue samples.

Selection of study subjects

This study utilized the Python programming language and its web scraping library (Scrapy) to collect data from the “Meishichina” website (www.meishichina.com). Ingredient information was extracted from recommended recipes for elderly individuals, covering seven regions in China to ensure broad applicability of the study results. According to the website’s access policies and relevant laws and regulations, the recipe names and main ingredients were extracted to obtain the most representative data on the preferred ingredients for elderly people. The collected data were then cleaned and organized, duplicates and irrelevant content were removed, and only key information was retained for subsequent analysis. Finally, 26 of the most common and recommended ingredients for the elderly population were selected from the culinary websites of the seven regions in China. These ingredients included meats (pork, beef, chicken, and fish), eggs (eggs), grains (noodles, rice, and oats), tubers (potatoes, sweet potatoes, yams, and pumpkins), vegetables (tomatoes, eggplants, cabbages, carrots, greens, broccoli, and spinach), mixed beans (such as red beans, green beans), fruits (such as apples, pears, peaches, and bananas), and tofu. These foods were categorized according to IDDSI standards, ranging from liquids to normal solid foods, covering all IDDSI levels.

Food processing and preparation

According to the requirements of the IDDSI framework, selected food samples were processed to different degrees to ensure that their physical properties met the standards for the corresponding IDDSI levels. The natural ingredients used in this study were processed based on daily cooking habits, including washing, cutting into pieces, and steaming to ensure thorough cooking. The steamed ingredients were then mashed, and different proportions of water were added to achieve the required consistency and softness. For instance, pumpkin puree:water (1:2) indicates a ratio of 1:2 by weight for pumpkin:water.

Meat samples: Fresh meat was cut into 2 cm × 2 ‍cm pieces, minced, steamed for 30 ‍min, and mixed with different proportions of water to make different textures of meat puree, such as pork puree:water (1:0), meaning that the meat puree had a ratio of 1:0 with no water added. Eggs were boiled, and the yolks were mixed with water at different ratios to make different textures of egg yolk puree, such as yolk:water (1:0.5), meaning that the ratio of yolk to water was 1:0.5; steamed egg preparation:beaten eggs were mixed with different ratios of water to make steamed eggs, such as steamed egg-egg:water (1:2), meaning that the ratio of egg liquid to water was 1:2. Rice grains and tubers were soaked for 30 ‍min and then steamed with water, such as rice:water (1:4), meaning that the ratio of rice to water was 1:4; oat:steamed with water, such as oat:water (1:8), meaning that the ratio of oat to water was 1:8; and noodles were boiled for 8 ‍min and then minced and mixed with different proportions of water, such as noodles:water (1:1), meaning that the ratio of cooked noodles to water was 1:1. The vegetables were washed, cut into 2 cm × 2 ‍cm pieces, steamed for 30 ‍min, and then mashed with water, such as carrot puree:water (1:0.5), meaning that the ratio of carrot to water was 1:0.5. The beans were soaked for 12 ‍h, steamed for 30 ‍min, and then mashed with different proportions of water, such as red bean puree:water (1:2), meaning that the ratio of red beans to water was 1:2. Fruits: The fruits were washed, peeled, cored, cut into 2 cm × 2 ‍cm pieces, mashed with water or steamed for 30 ‍min and mashed with water, such as steamed apple puree:water (1:1), meaning that the ratio of steamed apple to water was 1:1. The standardized processing steps ensure uniform sample preparation.

Each food category in different processing states was prepared into three samples of 50 ‍g each. The 50 ‍g food samples were used for texture analysis to ensure consistency across different IDDSI levels. However, this does not imply that individuals will consume the same amount of food across different IDDSI levels. Actual food intake may vary based on the texture and type of food. Table 1 provides the recommended intake amounts, and the food intake per meal is adjusted accordingly.

IDDSI evaluation tools, including spoons, forks, and syringes, were used for IDDSI level assessment. To ensure each food sample met IDDSI standards, we utilized IDDSI evaluation tools (such as spoons, forks, and syringes) to perform texture testing. These tests helped confirm the consistency of food samples at each IDDSI level. While we did not specifically involve a speech and language therapist or other IDDSI-trained professional for validation, we followed the IDDSI guidelines and used these tools to ensure compliance. The syringe was filled with 10 ‍ml of liquid sample, and one person placed a finger on the nozzle to stop the flow, while another person timed it. After 10 ‍s, the nozzle was blocked again to record the remaining liquid in the syringe.

IDDSI levels were assessed as follows:

Level 0: All liquid passed through the syringe.

Level 1: 1 ‍ml to 4 ‍ml remaining.

Level 2: 4 ‍ml to 8 ‍ml remaining.

Level 3: 8 ‍ml to 10 ‍ml remaining.

Level 4: No fluid flow observed after 10 ‍s.

For samples that could not flow out of the syringe, a fork test was used. The standard metal fork has tines 4 ‍mm apart, with a total width of 1.5 ‍cm. The texture and cohesiveness of the food were assessed based on whether it could be mashed by the fork or hang on the fork. IDDSI levels were defined as:

Level 5: Food should easily separate and spill through the tines.

Level 6: Food should not spill through the tines.

Level 7: Food should be easily separated by the fork or spoon edge.

For IDDSI levels 6–7, a spoon test was added to evaluate whether the food could be easily picked up by the spoon and whether it retained its shape without flowing off or with finger pressure testing to assess its elasticity and firmness. Each processed food sample was subjected to the above IDDSI level testing procedures. Based on the test results, each food was classified into the appropriate IDDSI level. For foods at the level boundaries, adjustments were made according to practical situations and needs.

Nutritional component data

The professional nutritional analysis software “West China Hospital Nutrition Software”, which has a comprehensive food database and high-precision nutritional calculation capabilities, was used to evaluate and calculate the main nutritional components of each 100 ‍g food sample at different IDDSI levels. The software performed detailed nutritional calculations based on the type, weight, and IDDSI level of the food, providing information on energy, protein, fat, carbohydrates, and dietary fiber.

Data analysis

Nutritional component analysis at different IDDSI levels

Descriptive statistics and ANOVA (analysis of variance) were used to evaluate and compare the nutritional content of foods at different IDDSI levels on a per 100 ‍g basis. Descriptive statistics provided the mean and SD of the nutritional components of each food category. ANOVA was used to analyze the differences in energy, protein, fat, carbohydrate, dietary fiber, and water content among foods at different IDDSI levels. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (ver. 25.0) software, with the significance level for all tests set at p≤0.05.

Correlation analysis of nutritional components

This study explored the correlation between nutritional components and IDDSI levels by calculating the Spearman correlation coefficient (ρ). Correlation analysis helped determine the trends in nutritional components with changes in IDDSI levels, assessing whether these nutritional variables increased or decreased with the increase in food quality. The Spearman correlation coefficient is suitable for non-normally distributed or skewed data, making it appropriate for analyzing the rank correlation between nutritional components and IDDSI levels in this study.

Impact analysis of different IDDSI levels on the nutritional needs of elderly individuals according to the Chinese Dietary Guidelines (2022)

The portion sizes and nutritional data in the study were based on the Chinese Dietary Guidelines (2022), referenced against the balanced dietary pyramid for the elderly,(10) and the calculated raw-to-cooked conversion factors and the Chinese Food Composition Table.(11,12) These references provided the recommended intake, raw weight, cooked weight, and corresponding exchange portions and nutritional values (energy, protein) for each food category for a normal elderly diet (Table 1).

Based on the recommendations of the balanced diet pyramid for elderly Chinese individuals, the cooked weight, i.e., the actual intake amount for elderly individuals, ranges from 1,700 ‍g to 2,100 ‍g.(10) Due to the physiological decline in stomach capacity and digestive function with age, a dietary survey conducted by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention in 2018 across 15 provinces (autonomous regions, municipalities) revealed that the average dietary intake for elderly individuals was 1,500–1,800 ‍g/day.(13,14) Considering the physiological decline in digestive function with age, the per meal intake for elderly individuals (≥65 years) is relatively low. Therefore, this study chose 1,700 ‍ml as a fixed volume to correspond to the lower food volume needs of elderly people. By calculating the nutritional intake at a fixed volume of 1,700 ‍ml for each IDDSI level and supplementing with “Yi Quan Su” (Y) (nutritional content: 429 ‍kcal/100 ‍g, protein 18.5 ‍g/100 ‍g) as a supplementation strategy, this study provides practical dietary adjustment recommendations. Direct nutritional component calculations and estimated supplementation effects were used to provide practical dietary adjustment recommendations.

To assess whether diets at different IDDSI levels meet the nutritional needs recommended for elderly people in the Chinese Dietary Guidelines (2022), this study compared the nutritional components provided by each level with the recommended values in the Chinese Dietary Guidelines for elderly Chinese people (energy: 1,000 ‍kcal and protein: 60 ‍g).

The study revealed that the total nutritional content was greatest at IDDSI level 7, where eggs provided the most energy and fat, grains provided the most carbohydrates, and meats provided the most protein. At different IDDSI levels, meats and eggs significantly contributed to protein and fat content, carbohydrates were mainly provided by fruits and grains, and dietary fiber was mainly contributed by fruits and mixed beans.

There were statistically significant differences in energy, carbohydrates, protein, fat, and dietary fiber among the different food categories at various IDDSI levels (p≤0.05). Protein was mainly supplied by meats at IDDSI levels 0, 2, 3, 5, and 6 (p≤0.05); fats were mainly provided by eggs at IDDSI levels 0 and 6 (p≤0.05) and by meats at IDDSI levels 3 and 5 (p≤0.05); carbohydrates were mainly provided by grains at IDDSI levels 0, 1, 3, and 6 (p≤0.05) and by fruits at IDDSI levels 2, 4, and 5 (p≤0.05); and dietary fiber was mainly provided by fruits at IDDSI levels 1 and 2 (p≤0.05) and by mixed beans at IDDSI levels 3, 4, and 5 (p≤0.05).

However, at IDDSI level 1, vegetables were the main source of fat (p = 0.13>0.05), and fruits were the main source of dietary fiber (p = 0.22>0.05); at IDDSI level 2, eggs were the main source of energy (p = 0.12>0.05); at IDDSI level 4, eggs were the main source of energy (p = 0.18>0.05) and fat (p = 0.14>0.05); at IDDSI level 5, meats were the main source of energy (p = 0.07>0.05); at IDDSI level 6, mixed beans were the main source of energy and dietary fiber (p = 0.11>0.05), but the differences in the nutritional content provided by various food categories at this IDDSI level were not statistically significant. Correlation analysis of the nutritional components

Trends in nutritional components across IDDSI levels

As the IDDSI increases, the energy content of various foods shows an upward trend. However, the energy content of eggs decreases at IDDSI levels 2 and 5, while the energy content of meats fluctuates across IDDSI levels 1–7, decreasing at IDDSI levels 4 and 6. The carbohydrate content showed an overall upward trend in fruits, grains, tubers, and vegetables but decreased in fruits at IDDSI level 3 and in grains at IDDSI levels 4 and 6. Protein content generally increases across all food categories, with a temporary decrease in meats at IDDSI levels 4 and 6. Eggs and tofu do not contain dietary fiber, while other food categories show an overall increase in dietary fiber content. The water content of various food ingredients shows a decreasing trend. However, there is a temporary increase in the moisture content of egg-based ingredients at IDDSI levels 2, 5, and 6. Similarly, meat and grain foods exhibit a temporary increase in water content at IDDSI levels 4 and 6 (Fig. 2–7).

Correlations between nutritional components and IDDSI levels

The correlation between various nutritional components and IDDSI levels was explored using the Spearman correlation coefficient. The results indicated that energy, protein, fat, carbohydrates, and dietary fiber were significantly positively correlated with the IDDSI (p<0.05), while water content was significantly negatively correlated with the IDDSI (p<0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

Analysis of the impact of IDDSI levels on nutritional components supplied by different food categories

At different IDDSI levels, specific food categories exhibit significant differences in nutritional supply, particularly in terms of energy, carbohydrates, protein, fat, and dietary fiber. This information is crucial for formulating balanced diets that meet the needs of each level. At the same weight/volume, IDDSI level 7 foods provide the highest amounts of various nutrients: IDDSI level 7 mixed beans provide the most dietary fiber, meats provide the most protein, eggs provide the most energy and fat, and grains and tubers provide the most carbohydrates. Fruits at IDDSI level 5 provide the most carbohydrates, and tofu at this level provides the most protein and fat. This phenomenon is interesting because most fat comes from meat and eggs (and vegetables for IDDSI level 1), and not from typical fats such as oil or butter, or high-fat dairy products or snacks like cakes or biscuits. This could be attributed to dietary habits in certain areas of China, where fat intake tends to be lower, possibly due to higher levels of lactose intolerance, leading to lower dairy consumption. This stands in contrast to other countries where dairy products and high-fat snacks are more commonly consumed. It is important to note that the food types analyzed in this study, such as rice, tofu, yams, and noodles, are highly representative of the Chinese and East Asian populations. However, they may not be as widely consumed in many Western countries, limiting the generalizability of these findings. This limitation should be acknowledged, and future research should consider expanding the range of food types to better represent diverse dietary patterns, especially those in Western countries. Although this study focused primarily on macro-nutrients such as energy, protein, fat, carbohydrates, and dietary fiber, it is important to note that micro-nutrients, such as vitamin D, calcium, and iron, were not analyzed. Given that elderly individuals are prone to deficiencies in micro-nutrients, especially vitamin D, future studies should incorporate the assessment of these micro-nutrients. Analyzing the micro-nutrient intake of each IDDSI diet level would provide a more comprehensive understanding of how different food categories support overall nutrition. This could also help address the concerns regarding potential nutritional deficiencies in elderly populations with swallowing difficulties. Furthermore, the current study highlights the need for continuous evaluation of nutrient intakes in elderly patients, ensuring that both macro- and micro-nutrients are adequately provided to prevent deficiencies. Additionally, this study revealed that the same type of food may have different nutritional compositions at different IDDSI levels due to changes in the form of the food used to meet IDDSI standards or specific cooking preparation methods.(15)

As the IDDSI changed, the energy content of the eggs decreased at levels 2 and 5, while it increased at levels 0 and 4 and significantly increased at level 7. This is because the form of eggs at IDDSI levels 2 and 5 is steamed eggs made from egg liquid mixed with water, with water ratios of 3:1 and 1.5:1, respectively. At levels 0 and 4, the form is egg yolk diluted with different ratios of water, considering that the fat in eggs is mainly present in the yolk, which has high energy. The carbohydrate content of fruits decreases at IDDSI levels of 2–3, possibly due to the varying carbohydrate content of different fruits. At IDDSI level 2, the fruit food is banana puree:water (1:1), while at IDDSI level 3, the types of fruit puree are apple and peach, with bananas having relatively higher sugar and starch contents than apples and peaches. Additionally, the decrease in carbohydrate content in grains at IDDSI levels 4 and 6 is considered to be related to the food itself. At levels 4 and 6, the grain ingredient is rice, while at levels 3 and 5, it includes oats, which have a higher dietary fiber content but lower carbohydrate content.(15,16) Meats are the primary source of protein at almost all levels, especially at levels 5 and 7, where the protein content is significantly greater. However, the energy and protein content of meats fluctuates across IDDSI levels 1–7, with decreases at levels 4 and 6 due to the types of meat and water addition ratios. The types of meat at IDDSI levels 3 and 5 include pork, chicken, and fish, while at levels 4 and 6, it is beef. At level 3, pork puree:water (1:1) and chicken puree:water (1:1); at level 4, beef puree:water (1:3); and at level 6, beef puree:water (1:1); at level 5, pork puree:water (1:0), chicken puree:water (1:0), and fish puree:water (1:0).

Adjusting nutritional supplementation in IDDSI-level foods to meet nutritional needs in elderly individuals

The study results indicate that the contents of calories, protein, fat, carbohydrates, and dietary fiber are positively correlated with IDDSI levels, with calories and protein showing a greater correlation. This suggests the importance of nutritional supplementation when preparing food suitable for patients with swallowing difficulties. For individuals with lower IDDSI levels, high-energy and high-protein food choices and nutritional supplements should be prioritized. Additionally, the negative correlation with water content (ρ = −0.580) indicates that the IDDSI decreases with increasing water content, providing supporting data for understanding the suitability of liquid and semiliquid foods for patients with swallowing difficulties in clinical settings. Although fat and dietary fiber also showed positive correlations, their correlations were weaker (ρ<0.25), suggesting that adjustments to fat and dietary fiber in IDDSI food preparation are less critical than adjustments to energy and protein.

According to the Chinese Elderly Balanced Diet Pyramid, the recommended cooked weight of elderly diets is between 1,700 ‍g and 2,100 ‍g.(10) As digestive function physiologically declines with age, the single meal intake of elderly people (≥65 years) is relatively low, and for those with swallowing difficulties, it may be even lower.(13) This study takes a practical approach to explore the nutritional content of a fixed volume of swallowing disorder diets that corresponds to the lower range of the recommended intake, choosing 1,700 ‍ml to accommodate the lower food volume needs of elderly people, avoiding discomfort from excessive intake, ensuring a safe and comfortable dining experience, and more accurately reflecting the actual eating habits and needs of elderly people while providing enough food to reflect the nutritional content of foods with different IDDSI levels.(10,14)

The study results show that the energy and protein intake of balanced diets for elderly people at different IDDSI levels with a fixed volume of 1,700 ‍ml significantly differs from the recommendations in the “Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents (2022)”, especially at lower IDDSI levels.(10) This suggests that when providing IDDSI-level diets for elderly people with swallowing difficulties, merely changing the texture of natural foods is not enough to provide the necessary nutrients; nutritional supplements are needed to ensure adequate energy and protein intake; otherwise, malnutrition may occur, affecting the overall health and quality of life of elderly people.(17,18)

According to the recommended volume of 1,700 ‍ml of food (normal diet) in the diet pyramid, which provides approximately 1,000 ‍kcal of energy and 60 ‍g (57.22 ‍g) of protein. In contrast, IDDSI Level 0 foods only supply 363.55 ‍kcal and 20.40 grams of protein. As one transitions to higher levels of IDDSI foods, the energy and protein supply per equivalent volume increases. However, none of the foods from IDDSI Levels 0 to 4 reach an energy supply of 500 ‍kcal. In this study, IDDSI level 1 provided only 8.48 ‍g of protein, all of which are nonquality proteins, far below the recommended intake of 60 ‍g. This is because, in this experiment, IDDSI level 1 mainly included vegetables and fruits, which have low protein content and lack quality protein.(19) This finding emphasizes the need to consider the energy and protein types and amounts provided by food ingredients at appropriate IDDSI levels when preparing foods for elderly people with swallowing difficulties.

As the IDDSI increased, from IDDSI level 5 onward, the energy and protein provided by the food started to approach the recommended values. However, only IDDSI level 7 exceeded the daily recommended values for energy and protein, indicating that only this level does not require nutritional supplementation, while lower levels of IDDSI food do not provide sufficient energy and protein. Therefore, when using lower than IDDSI level 7 swallowing diets, nutritional supplements should be added to ensure adequate energy and protein intake, further enhancing the nutritional status and quality of life of elderly people. This study revealed that by adding appropriate amounts of enteral nutrition (100 ‍g each providing 429 ‍kcal of energy and 18.5 ‍g of protein), the nutritional deficiencies in lower IDDSI diets can be effectively supplemented.

By evaluating the energy and protein gap requirements, the study calculated the amount of “Yi Quan Su” enteral nutrition formula needed to ensure the nutritional needs of elderly individuals at different IDDSI levels are met. The results indicate that while meeting energy needs is a primary concern, achieving adequate protein intake often requires the addition of more “Yi Quan Su”.(20) The study further points out that if the patient’s intake capacity allows, increasing the intake of “Yi Quan Su” is recommended to meet higher protein requirements, thereby supporting the nutritional status and overall health of the elderly. Given that some guidelines, such as the UK’s, recommend 2,000 ‍kcal/day (for women) and 2,500 ‍kcal/day (for men), this study acknowledges that energy and protein needs may vary by country and population.(21) To address deficits in other IDDSI levels, specific quantities of “Yi Quan Su” enteral nutrition formula were recommended, calculated based on the difference between the prescribed energy/protein needs and the actual nutritional content of the diets.

This study was based on the assumption that the “Yi Quan Su” enteral nutrition formula would be used to supplement the nutritional needs of elderly individuals with swallowing difficulties at different IDDSI levels. However, it should be noted that individual nutritional needs may vary. While the formula provides a general estimate based on population averages, further adjustments may be necessary based on individual dietary intake, swallowing capacity, and specific nutritional deficits. Therefore, regular nutritional assessments and dietetic reviews are critical to ensure that the patient’s nutritional requirements are met accurately. This individualized approach will allow for more precise management of nutritional supplementation, addressing both energy and protein needs while preventing malnutrition.

Conclusion

This study explored the prevalence of dysphagia among elderly people and its impact on nutritional intake, particularly the differences in nutritional supply at different levels of the IDDSI. Through detailed nutritional analysis, the study revealed significant differences in the nutritional supply of specific food categories at different IDDSI levels within the IDDSI framework, which is crucial for formulating balanced diets that match the swallowing ability of elderly people. The results indicate that as IDDSI levels increase, the energy, protein, and fat content of the food also increase, while the water content decreases. This finding suggests that when preparing food for patients with dysphagia, particularly at lower IDDSI levels, it is necessary to add high-energy and high-protein food choices and nutritional supplements to prevent further health deterioration caused by malnutrition. Moreover, this study highlights potential differences in the ability of foods at different IDDSI levels to meet the nutritional needs of elderly people, as recommended in the “Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents (2022)”. At lower IDDSI levels, the energy and protein supplied by food often do not reach the recommended intake, indicating the need for appropriate nutritional interventions to improve this situation. For instance, adding nutrient-dense supplements such as “Yi Quan Su” can effectively increase the energy and protein content of lower-level foods to meet the daily nutritional needs of elderly people. However, it is important to acknowledge that these population estimates may not always be accurate for individual patients. The required amount of enteral nutrition may need to be further adjusted (increased or decreased) based on individual intake of the prescribed IDDSI diet and the degree of nutrient deficit. To mitigate this, regular nutritional and dietetic reviews, as well as personalized assessments, should be conducted to ensure that individual nutritional needs are met effectively.

This research provides data support for clinical nutrition management, particularly in preparing IDDSI-standardized foods, helping reduce the risk of aspiration in patients with dysphagia and ensuring that they receive the necessary nutritional support. The application of the IDDSI standards has significantly advanced the design of the therapeutic diet for patients with dysphagia. The findings reveal the specific distribution of nutritional components (such as protein, energy, etc.) in swallowing diets at different IDDSI levels, helping healthcare professionals better understand the impact of different textures of food on the nutritional status of patients and aiding in the prevention and management of malnutrition caused by swallowing difficulties, thereby improving patients’ quality of life. Additionally, this study provides practical guidance for clinical nutritionists in designing diets for elderly people, especially those with swallowing difficulties, who recommend more consideration of food nutrient density during food selection and preparation to ensure that the basic nutritional needs of elderly people are met even as the degree of food processing increases. While this study primarily focuses on energy and protein intake for elderly individuals with swallowing disorders, we acknowledge that deficiencies in micronutrients such as calcium, iron, and vitamin D are also common in this population. This issue will be explored in future studies as part of a more comprehensive approach to addressing the nutritional needs of elderly individuals.(21)

Furthermore, the study results emphasize that for patients with varying degrees of dysphagia, especially those who can choose only lower-level IDDSI therapeutic diets, other routes such as oral nutritional supplements or enteral nutrition are essential medical considerations. Future research should further explore the impact of different food processing techniques on nutrient retention to optimize food preparation methods and ensure adequate nutritional supply.
