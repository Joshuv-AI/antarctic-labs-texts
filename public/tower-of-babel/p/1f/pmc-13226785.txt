Striking corpus callosum lesions in immediate cerebral fat embolism: A rare clinical case
Authors: Hamdi Chaabouni, Rania Ammar, Mabrouk Bahloul, Basma Souissi, Chokri Ben Hamida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFat embolism syndrome (FES) is a known complication of long-bone fractures, typically occurring 24–48 h post-trauma and characterized by petechial rash, neurological symptoms, and pulmonary distress. Cerebral fat embolism (CFE), which can be fatal, can result from brain emboli. We report a case of a 17-year-old male who sustained bilateral closed femur fractures in a road traffic accident. The patient presented with immediate neurological distress, including coma, without respiratory symptoms. The brain's CT scan was normal initially. Still, an MRI later showed signs of FES, such as T2 and diffusion hyperintensities on the corpus callosum, a lipid peak on spectroscopy, and a microbleed. Delayed awakening and status epilepticus, treated with anticonvulsants, complicated the patient's course. This case highlights the diagnostic utility of MRI in detecting CFE. It emphasizes the need to consider FES in isolated neurological symptoms following long-bone fractures, even without respiratory distress. Early surgical fixation of fractures remains critical for prevention.Keywords: Cerebral fat embolism, Fat embolism syndrome, Neuroimaging, Trauma, Corpus callosum

Introduction

Fat embolism syndrome (FES) is more common following closed, long-bone fractures of the lower extremities, especially when there are several (≥3) sites involved [1]. Usually, it happens 24 to 48 h after the trauma [2]. The prevalence of FES and fat embolism varies greatly across the literature based on series design [2]. Retrospective long-term reviews show the lowest rates [2]. According to Muller, fat embolism in long bone fractures ranges from 0.9% to 2.2% of cases [3]. The incidence is often lower (0.5 to 0.8%) after intramedullary manipulation or instrumentation [3]. The three classic signs are petechial rash, neurological symptoms, and pulmonary distress [4]. For the positive diagnosis of this illness, the Guard criteria remain the most commonly utilized [5], [6]. Cerebral fat embolism (CFE), which can be fatal, can result from brain emboli [1]. Its frequency ranges from 0.9% to 2.2% [7], [8], [9]. Seizures, headaches, focal deficits, altered consciousness, and coma onset are the key characteristics of neurological symptoms. MRI may confirm the diagnosis, especially with spectroscopy [8]. The rate of CFE can be reduced primarily by supportive and preventative measures [9]. We present a case of CFE following the fracture of two closed femurs, which showed atypical changes on the MRI of the brain with severe corpus callosum damage due to a fat embolism that occurred less than 24 h after the accident.

Case report

A 17-year-old patient, with no past medical history, was involved in a road traffic accident, which resulted in the fracture of two closed femurs. He was in a coma immediately after the trauma. He was intubated on-site by the emergency medical team for neurological distress with an initial Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 8/15 while remaining hemodynamically and respiratory stable. A computed tomography (CT) body scan was performed and revealed a normal brain CT (Fig. 1(A)), pulmonary contusion, and displaced bilateral femoral fractures. Upon his arrival in our critical care unit, clinical examination showed a petechial rash on the upper chest and subconjunctival petechiae. A follow-up brain CT after 72 h (Fig. 1(B)) showed multiple nodular hypodense lesions of the semi-oval, capsule-lenticular-caudate bilateral centers and of the hemispheric bilateral subcortical white matter, which may be related to lesions caused by a fat embolism. Examination of the eye fundus showed superficial retinal hemorrhage in front of the superior temporal arch in both eyes. The biological analysis revealed thrombocytopenia, normocytic normochromic anemia, normal haptoglobin levels, and low total cholesterol at 2.5 mmol/L. The FES diagnosis was suspected. Echocardiography and bubble tests were performed ruling out a patent foramen ovale. MRI (Fig. 2) was performed on the 7th post-traumatic day, showing nodular and patchy signal abnormalities T2 and FLAIR (A) hyperintensities, diffusion hyperintensities with low ADC values (B), and punctate contrast enhancement after gadolinium injection (C) in the cerebellum, subcortical and periventricular white matter bilaterally and symmetrically, with a predilection for the semi-oval centers, basal ganglia, and corpus callosum. Spectroscopy (D) of these signal abnormalities presented a lipid peak at short TE (approximately 0.9 ppm), which was canceled at long TE without any detectable inversion of the peak, confirming the absence of lactate. Additionally, punctate signal abnormality in the left occipital subcortical region appears as T2* hypointensity, consistent with hemorrhagic petechiae. The MRI findings are consistent with supratentorial and infratentorial fat embolism, classified as type 2C. The patient underwent surgery for bilateral femoral fractures on the 17th day post-trauma. The clinical course was characterized by delayed awakening and status epilepticus confirmed by electroencephalogram, for which anticonvulsant medication was initiated. The patient was well-improved under symptomatic treatment. He was discharged 38 days following the ICU admission.

Discussion

We present an unusual case of immediate cerebral fat embolism with atypical changes on the MRI of the brain with severe corpus callosum damage due to fat embolism that occurred less than 24 h after the accident. Magnetic resonance imaging remains the best option to confirm the diagnosis and exclude differential diagnoses. In our case, it revealed significant damage to the corpus callosum, microbleeds in a characteristic starfield pattern, and a lipid peak on spectroscopy. Classically, the concept of a latent period is a consistent feature in diagnosing fat embolism, typically ranging from 24 to 48 h [2]. However, in our patient, the onset of neurological distress was immediate, leading to intubation. FES is generally characterized by respiratory distress, which typically appears first [6] associated or not with neurological distress. In contrast, in our case, it initially presented with isolated neurological distress, which is rare. The mechanisms of brain lesions in FES are multifactorial. Mechanical mechanisms by embolization of fat globules, which reach the right heart and subsequently the lungs. In cases of a patent foramen ovale or when the emboli are small enough to bypass the pulmonary capillaries, they can enter the systemic circulation and affect the brain, which may explain the initial isolated neurological distress in this case [4]. Biochemical mechanisms include the release of free fatty acids (FFAs), which are highly toxic to endothelial cells, induction of inflammatory response, and oxidative stress [4]. These processes collectively contribute to endothelial damage, increased capillary permeability, cerebral edema, and petechial hemorrhages. The differential diagnosis to consider in our patient was diffuse axonal injury. However, it was ruled out based on MRI findings [10]. Microbleeds in characteristic patterns resembling to starfield pattern, corpus callosum diffusion restriction with low ADC, and a lipid peak in spectroscopy are the best imaging markers to confirm the diagnosis and rule out differential diagnoses [11]. Damage to the corpus callosum is common in FES, but the severity of corpus callosum damage is exceptional [12].

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of considering cerebral fat embolism (CFE) as a differential diagnosis in patients presenting with immediate neurological symptoms following long bone fractures, even in the absence of respiratory distress. Early recognition and the use of advanced imaging techniques, particularly MRI with spectroscopy, are crucial for confirming the diagnosis and guiding management. The atypical presentation of this case, with severe corpus callosum damage and rapid onset of symptoms, underscores the variability in FES presentation and the need for heightened clinical awareness. Prompt supportive care and preventive measures remain pivotal in optimizing outcomes for patients with this rare but potentially fatal condition.
