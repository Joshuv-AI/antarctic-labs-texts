The distinguished predictive value of CFIm25 in schistosomal and non-schistosomal colorectal cancer
Authors: Shaojie Chai, Yandie Zhang, Xiaoping Zhang, Liang Liu, Hongyan Jing, Weixia Wang, Yanzi Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe impact of CFIm25 expression on the prognosis of colorectal cancer (CRC) has rarely been reported, and only limited studies have focused on CRC associated with Schistosoma japonicum infection. This study aimed to investigate the prognostic value of CFIm25 in CRC, with emphasis on its distinct prognostic role in schistosomal CRC (SCRC) and non-schistosomal CRC (NSCRC).MethodsA total of 286 CRC cases from Qingpu Branch of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University were retrospectively analyzed using tissue microarrays. CFIm25 expression was assessed by immunohistochemical staining, and its relationship with clinicopathological characteristics and clinical outcomes were evaluated.ResultsGender, tumor node metastasis (TNM) stage, lymph node positive, and CFIm25 expression were identified as independent prognostic factors associated with overall survival (OS) in the whole cohort. Patients were further stratified according to schistosomal infection status into NSCRC and SCRC subgroups. CFIm25 was a beneficial prognostic predictor in patients with NSCRC. CFIm25 expression had no significant effect on OS in patients with stage I–II tumors or in those without lymph node metastasis, whereas it was an independent prognostic factor in patients with stage III–IV tumors and in those with lymph node metastasis. Moreover, CFIm25 expression was negatively correlated with aggressive clinicopathological features, including advanced TNM stage, poor tumor differentiation, lymph node metastasis, and lympho-vascular invasion.ConclusionsCFIm25 was identified as a prognostic factor in the overall CRC cohort, but its prognostic significance varied across different subgroups. CFIm25 may serve as a useful biomarker for risk stratification, particularly in NSCRC and advanced disease.

Highlight box

Key findings

• CFIm25 could be used as a potential prognostic biomarker in colorectal cancer (CRC).

• The prognostic significance of CFIm25 differs significantly between CRC patients with and without schistosomiasis, as well as among patients at different clinical stages.

• These findings may facilitate improved risk stratification and personalized management of CRC patients, particularly those with a history of schistosomiasis or different tumor stages.

What is known and what is new?

• The prognostic value of CFIm25 expression in CRC remains rarely reported, and studies focusing on Schistosoma japonicum-associated CRC are particularly scarce.

• CFIm25 was identified as a prognostic factor in the overall CRC cohort, but its prognostic significance varied across different subgroups. In addition, the expression of CFIm25 was inversely associated with aggressive clinicopathological features, including advanced tumor node metastasis stage, poor differentiation, and lymph node metastasis.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• CFIm25 is a potential biomarker for prognosis in CRC.

• Clinical validation of CFIm25 and exploration of CFIm25-targeted therapies are needed.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks third in incidence and second in cancer-related mortality worldwide (1). Each year, it affects more than 900,000 individuals, and its incidence is rising in populations traditionally considered to be at low risk (2). Surgical resection remains the primary treatment for early-stage CRC. However, therapeutic options for advanced disease are limited, and chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of management (3). The invasive and metastatic potential of CRC cells is a major determinant of tumor recurrence and cancer-related death (4). Therefore, early diagnosis, timely intervention, and accurate prognostic assessment are essential for improving overall survival (OS) in patients with CRC.

Tumorigenesis has traditionally been attributed to the accumulation of genetic alterations that activate oncogenes and inactivate tumor suppressor genes (5). However, recent studies have shown that alternative polyadenylation (APA) can also promote oncogene activation in the absence of genetic mutations (6). APA is a post-transcriptional RNA-processing mechanism that generates distinct 3’ ends in messenger RNAs through alternative cleavage and polyadenylation of pre-messenger ribonucleic acid (pre-mRNA). As a result, transcripts with identical coding sequences but different 3’ untranslated regions (3’UTRs) are produced, which can differ in stability, localization, translation efficiency, and biological function (7).

CFIm25, encoded by the NUDT21 gene, is a key regulator of APA during pre-mRNA processing. CFIm25 generally suppresses proximal poly(A) site usage and promotes the generation of transcripts with longer 3’UTRs. Because the 3’UTR contains numerous microRNA-binding elements (8), APA-mediated 3’UTR shortening can facilitate escape from microRNA-dependent repression, thereby altering gene expression and influencing tumor progression and prognosis in multiple cancer types (9). Depending on the biological context, CFIm25 has been reported to function either as a tumor suppressor or as a pro-tumorigenic factor (10). On the one hand, CFIm25 can promote 3’UTR lengthening of target transcripts, thereby modulating mRNA stability and translational efficiency and suppressing cell proliferation and malignant transformation. In glioblastoma, reduced CFIm25 expression enhances tumorigenic potential and increases tumor growth, whereas CFIm25 inhibits proliferation, invasion, and metastasis (11). Similar tumor-suppressive effects have also been reported in hepatocellular carcinoma (6), lung cancer (12), bladder cancer (13), and testicular germ cell tumors (TGCTs) (14,15). On the other hand, CFIm25 has also been implicated in promoting proximal poly(A) site usage, thereby facilitating cell proliferation and transformation in certain settings (16). For example, Zhang et al. reported that CFIm25 downregulation in K562 cells inhibited proliferation and promoted apoptosis, and subsequent studies showed that CFIm25 may promote K562 cell growth through regulation of p-ERK signaling (17). CFIm25 regulates macrophage polarization by promoting AKT2 proximal polyadenylation, enhancing Akt2/NF-κB signaling, M1 phenotypes, and antitumor immune activity (18). FXR1 recruits CFIm25 and CFIm68 to regulate TRAF1 mRNA 3’ processing, promoting urothelial carcinoma proliferation, survival, and tumorigenesis (19). Lactate-induced CFIm25 lactylation promotes APA-mediated FDX1 3’ UTR lengthening, suppressing cuproptosis and driving therapeutic resistance in ESCC (20). One study showed that reduced CFIm25 expression is associated with aggressive CRC features and poor survival (21). Lin et al. reported that CFIm25 promotes CRC progression by regulating APA of oncogenes, enhancing tumor malignancy, stemness (22). CFIm25 drives CRC progression through APA-mediated CDK19 long 3’UTR isoforms, enhancing cholesterol biosynthesis, proliferation, and CD8+ T-cell immune evasion (23). However, the clinical significance of CFIm25 expression in CRC remains largely unclear. Therefore, we examined CFIm25 expression in CRC and evaluated its prognostic value.

Schistosome eggs were identified microscopically in a subset of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections from our cohort. Qingpu District was previously an endemic area for schistosomiasis, and most infected patients were likely exposed to Schistosoma japonicum. The relationship between schistosomiasis and CRC remains controversial. Studies from Egypt have generally not supported a clear association between schistosomiasis and CRC (24), whereas in Asia, S. japonicum infection has been regarded as a potential risk factor for CRC (25). After deposition in the intestinal wall, schistosome eggs release antigens that induce chronic inflammation and granuloma formation (26). Chronic inflammation is a well-established driver of tumor development (27-30), and infection-related inflammation is estimated to account for approximately one-quarter of cancer-causing factors (31). Inflammatory cells can generate genotoxic mediators, including reactive oxygen and nitrogen species and proinflammatory cytokines, which may damage cellular DNA and increase the risk of mutagenesis and malignant transformation (32,33). Taken together, schistosome infection may reshape the inflammatory tumor microenvironment and thereby affect gene expression and regulatory networks. We therefore hypothesized that the expression patterns of genes involved in gene regulation may differ between CRC patients with and without schistosomiasis.

In the present study, we investigated the prognostic significance of CFIm25 in CRC. CFIm25 expression was significantly associated with tumor node metastasis (TNM) stage, lymph node status, and patient prognosis. We also explored its relationship with schistosomiasis. Our findings suggest that CFIm25 may serve as a clinically relevant prognostic biomarker in CRC, particularly in patients with schistosomiasis. We present this article in accordance with the REMARK reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0893/rc).

Methods

Patients and sample collection

In this retrospective study, 500 unselected patients with CRC were initially screened. Among them, 351 patients met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria described previously (34). However, 65 tissue specimens contained abundant adipose tissue, which resulted in severe tissue detachment during immunohistochemical staining. After excluding these cases, a final cohort of 286 patients was included for the subsequent analyses. As shown in Table S1, there were no significant differences in clinicopathological characteristics between the final analytical cohort (n=286) and excluded patients (n=65). The specific contents are as follows. Enrolled subjects received radical primary resection at Qingpu Branch of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University between January 2008 and August 2016. Every surgical procedure was performed in line with standardized operative principles, including sufficient surgical margin resection, high ligation of the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) en bloc and systematic lymph node dissection, with negative circumferential resection margins achieved in all cases. We documented the total count of harvested lymph nodes as well as the quantity of metastatic lymph nodes. Clinical inpatient charts and pathological documentation were retrospectively collected, and follow-up information was acquired via telephone interviews. OS was calculated from the date of curative surgery until either the last follow-up contacts or death attributed to CRC.

All deaths resulting from causes unrelated to CRC were treated as censored observations. Additionally, patients who were lost to follow-up or remained alive at the last follow-up time point were also censored. The inclusion criteria were as follows (I) patients with primary CRC; (II) none of these patients had received any prior anti-tumor therapy before surgery. In addition, none of the enrolled patients underwent conventional postoperative adjuvant oncological treatments, including chemotherapy or radiotherapy. The only postoperative intervention received by the entire study population was traditional Chinese medicine treatment, and no other additional anti-tumor therapies were administered throughout the follow-up period; (III) patients were diagnosed as adenocarcinoma by pathology after resection of CRC. Exclusion criteria included (I) Tis tumours; (II) patients who lacked complete information; (III) patients with synchronous malignancy; (IV) patients with survival time less than 1 month.

H&E-stained slides were independently reviewed by two experienced pathologists to confirm the diagnosis and restage the tumors according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the medical ethics committee of Fudan University. Prior written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Detection of schistosome ova

Schistosomiasis was diagnosed by identifying schistosome eggs in any of the H&E-stained sections, as previously described (35). All original H&E-stained formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections for each case (typically 4–6 slides) were examined for schistosome ova under a conventional light microscope at ×10 and ×40 magnification by two pathologists blinded to the clinicopathological data.

Tissue microarrays (TMAs)

The most representative tumor region from a single paraffin block was selected for TMA construction (35). All samples included in this study were paraffin-embedded tissues and were preserved at room temperature in a dry, dark, sealed and stable environment. Briefly, viable, non-necrotic tumor cores with a diameter of 2.0 mm were carefully obtained from the most representative tumor region of each patient. During core screening and selection, experienced pathologists carefully excluded tissue areas with extensive necrosis, severe hemorrhage, prominent inflammatory infiltration, or dense fibrosis, as these features could interfere with the accuracy of protein expression evaluation. Cores containing more than 20% tumor cells were considered adequate for analysis.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining and scoring

Immunohistochemical staining was performed using a Leica DM2000 microscope (Leica, Germany) and a commercially available rabbit polyclonal anti-NUDT21/CFIm25 antibody (ab183660, Abcam). CFIm25 expression was independently evaluated by two pathologists who were blinded to the clinicopathological data. Expression levels were scored semi-quantitatively based on staining intensity and the proportion of positive cells. Staining intensity was graded as follows: 0, negative; 1, weak; 2, moderate; and 3, strong. The proportion of positive staining was scored as follows: 0, negative; 1, 1–10%; 2, 11–50%; and 3, 51–100%. X-tile analysis based on 5-year OS was performed to determine the optimal cut-off value for CFIm25 expression. X-tile plots for the whole cohort are shown in Figure S1. According to the optimal cut-off value, samples with a staining intensity score ≥2 were classified as the CFIm25-high group, whereas the remainder were classified as the CFIm25-low group.

Statistical analysis

The associations between CFIm25 expression status and clinicopathological characteristics were analyzed using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. X-tile software (version 3.6.1; Yale University, New Haven, CT, USA) (36) was used to determine the optimal cut-off value for CFIm25 expression in the whole cohort. OS was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to the date of death. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated and compared using the log-rank test to assess the prognostic significance of CFIm25 in the present cohort. The Kaplan-Meier Plotter was additionally used to validate the prognostic value of CFIm25 for OS, and survival results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding log-rank P values. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify independent prognostic factors. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 20.0 and GraphPad Prism version 7.0. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

The clinicopathological characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age at surgery was 69 years (range, 33–91 years), and most patients were male (61.19%). According to the AJCC Staging Manual (seventh edition), 222 tumors (77.62%) were well differentiated and 64 (22.38%) were poorly differentiated. Vascular and perineural invasion, regardless of extent, were observed in 105 (36.71%) and 27 (9.44%) cases, respectively. Lymph node metastasis was identified in 117 patients (40.91%). Of the 286 patients, 157 (54.90%) had AJCC stage I–II disease and 129 (45.10%) had stage III–IV disease. Tumors were located in the rectum (24.12%), left colon (33.91%), and right colon (41.97%) (Table 1).

IHC staining of CFIm25

CFIm25 expression was assessed by IHC in all 286 CRC cases. Representative staining images are shown in Figure 1, including weak staining (Figure 1A), moderate staining (Figure 1B), and strong staining (Figure 1C). Positive CFIm25 staining was predominantly localized to the nucleus. The optimal cut-off value for CFIm25 expression, based on staining intensity, was determined using the X-tile program (Figure S1). A staining intensity score ≥2 was defined as high CFIm25 expression, whereas a score <2 was defined as low CFIm25 expression. Based on this cut-off, 190 patients (66.4%) were classified into the CFIm25-high group and 96 (33.6%) into the CFIm25-low group. Among patients with lymph node metastasis (n=117), 48 (41.0%) were classified as CFIm25-high, whereas among those without lymph node metastasis (n=169), 48 (28.4%) were classified as CFIm25-high. Among patients with stage I–II disease (n=157), 45 (28.7%) were assigned to the CFIm25-high group, compared with 51 of 129 patients (39.5%) with stage III–IV disease.

Survival analyses of patients with CRC

The median follow-up time was 49.72 months (range, 1.0–134.4 months). During follow-up, 115 of 286 patients (40.2%) died. The mean and median OS times were 43.34 and 49.72 months, respectively. To assess the prognostic significance of CFIm25 expression, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed. Patients were stratified into CFIm25-high and CFIm25-low groups according to the predefined cut-off value. Log-rank testing showed that patients with high CFIm25 expression had significantly better OS than those with low expression (P=0.02; Figure 2A). To further validate this finding, we analyzed the prognostic value of CFIm25 in rectal adenocarcinoma (n=165) using the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database. Consistently, patients with high CFIm25 expression showed significantly prolonged OS in the external dataset (P=0.004; Figure 2B).

Univariate and multivariate regression analysis

Univariate Cox regression analysis identified several variables significantly associated with OS, including age (P=0.02), pathological T stage (P<0.001), lymph node metastasis (P<0.001), TNM stage (P<0.001), tumor differentiation (P=0.01), lymphovascular invasion (P=0.003), lymph node positivity (P<0.001), tumor budding (P=0.01), schistosomiasis (P=0.048), and CFIm25 expression (P=0.01) (Table 2). Variables that were significant in univariate analysis were subsequently entered into the multivariate Cox regression model. Gender (P=0.006), TNM stage (P<0.001), lymph node positivity (P<0.001), and CFIm25 expression (P=0.01) emerged as independent prognostic factors for OS (Table 2).

Further subgroup analyses were performed to assess the prognostic significance of CFIm25 expression in patients with different tumor stages and lymph node status (Table 2). In patients with stage III–IV disease and in those with lymph node metastasis, CFIm25 expression remained an independent predictor of OS after adjustment for covariates including gender, perineural invasion, and schistosomiasis [HR =0.738, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.552–0.987, P=0.041; HR =0.664, 95% CI: 0.479–0.920, P=0.01, respectively]. By contrast, CFIm25 expression was not significantly associated with OS in patients with stage I–II disease or in those without lymph node metastasis (Table 2). Taken together, these findings indicate that CFIm25 is an important prognostic factor in CRC, particularly in patients with stage III–IV disease and in those with lymph node metastasis.

CFIm25 expression and its correlation with clinicopathologic features

We further examined the association between CFIm25 expression and clinicopathological characteristics in the overall CRC cohort (n=286). As shown in Table 3, chi-square analysis demonstrated that CFIm25 expression was significantly associated with tumor differentiation (P<0.001), lymphovascular invasion (P<0.001), TNM stage (P=0.048), and lymph node metastasis (P=0.02). As noted above, CFIm25 expression was a significant prognostic factor in patients with stage III–IV disease (n=129) and in those with lymph node metastasis (n=117), but not in patients with stage I–II disease (n=157) or those without lymph node metastasis (n=169) (Table 2). We therefore further analyzed the relationship between CFIm25 expression and clinicopathological features in these clinically relevant subgroups (Table 3). Among patients with stage III–IV disease, CFIm25 expression was negatively associated with lymph node metastasis (P=0.02, Table 3). In addition, CFIm25 expression was significantly associated with histological type (P=0.03, Table 3).

Survival analyses based on schistosomiasis status

Interestingly, during review of the pathological and follow-up records, we found that 112 of 286 patients (39.2%) had evidence of schistosomiasis. Further analysis showed that schistosomiasis was associated with worse prognosis in the overall CRC cohort (Figure 4A and Table 2; P=0.03 and P=0.048, respectively). Patients were therefore stratified into two subgroups: schistosomal CRC (SCRC; n=112) and non-schistosomal CRC (NSCRC; n=174). During follow-up, 51 of 112 patients (45.5%) died in the SCRC subgroup, compared with 64 of 174 patients (36.8%) in the NSCRC subgroup. In the SCRC subgroup, the mean and median OS times were 40.86 and 48.00 months, respectively. In the NSCRC subgroup, the corresponding values were 45.02 and 51.57 months. The clinicopathological characteristics of the two subgroups are summarized in Table S2. No significant differences were observed between the two groups except for age (P<0.001, Table S2).

In the NSCRC subgroup, Kaplan-Meier analysis showed that low CFIm25 expression was significantly associated with poorer OS (Figure 4B, P=0.03). Univariate analysis identified pathological T stage (P=0.004), lymph node metastasis (P=0.01), TNM stage (P=0.002), tumor differentiation (P=0.02), lymphovascular invasion (P<0.001), lymph node positivity (P<0.001), tumor budding (P=0.007), and CFIm25 expression (P=0.02) as factors associated with OS (Table S3). In multivariate analysis, lymphovascular invasion (P=0.03), pathological T stage (P=0.044), lymph node positivity (P=0.005), and CFIm25 expression (P=0.03) remained independently associated with OS (Table S3).

In contrast, in the SCRC subgroup (n=112), Kaplan-Meier analysis showed no significant association between CFIm25 expression and OS (P=0.43, Figure 4C). Consistently, univariate Cox analysis showed that CFIm25 expression was not associated with OS (P=0.47, Table S3). Instead, lymph node metastasis, TNM stage, and lymph node positivity were significantly associated with postoperative outcome (all P<0.001, Table S3). In multivariate analysis, ulceration (P=0.040), TNM stage (P<0.001), and lymph node positivity (P=0.01) were identified as independent adverse prognostic factors (Table S3).

Discussion

In the present study, decreased CFIm25 expression was associated with shorter OS, whereas increased CFIm25 expression was associated with longer OS in patients with CRC. In addition, CFIm25 expression was significantly associated with OS in the NSCRC subgroup, but not in the SCRC subgroup. Moreover, CFIm25 expression was associated with prognosis in patients with stage III–IV disease. CFIm25 expression was inversely associated with aggressive clinicopathological features, including advanced TNM stage, poor differentiation, and lymph node metastasis. These findings are in line with previous reports in other malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma (6), osteosarcoma (37), and bladder cancer (13). Huang et al. reported that CFIm25 inhibition promoted proliferation of A549 lung adenocarcinoma cells, suggesting that CFIm25 may suppress tumor progression by regulating APA of oncogenes such as IGF1R and thereby limiting their protein expression (12).

Approximately 39% of CRC patients in our cohort showed evidence of schistosome infection. Schistosomiasis has been reported as a potential risk factor for CRC (38). In addition, Schistosoma infection can induce chronic inflammation and reshape the tissue immune microenvironment. Given these effects, we further examined the relationship among schistosome infection, CFIm25 expression, and CRC prognosis. Stratified analysis showed that higher CFIm25 expression was associated with longer OS in the NSCRC subgroup and CFIm25 remained an independent prognostic factor in multivariate analysis. In contrast, CFIm25 expression was not associated with OS in the SCRC subgroup. One possible explanation is that schistosome infection induces chronic inflammation, which may promote genetic and epigenetic alterations (39) through multiple pathogenic mechanisms. Genotoxic mediators generated during schistosomiasis may contribute to the pathogenesis of SCRC and potentially alter the biological and prognostic relevance of CFIm25 expression. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger, prospective, multicenter studies. Another plausible explanation is that the prominent age discrepancy between the NSCRC and SCRC cohorts may have masked the prognostic value of CFIm25 among elderly patients with SCRC. Specifically, as shown in Supplementary Table S2, patients with schistosomiasis-associated CRC were obviously older than those without schistosomiasis-associated CRC (P<0.001, median age: 73 vs. 64.5 years, respectively). Age itself is a strong prognostic factor. Although age was incorporated as a covariate in all multivariate Cox regression analyses to mitigate statistical confounding, statistical adjustment cannot completely eliminate intrinsic age-related biological differences, including aging-associated alterations in the tumor microenvironment, schistosome-induced mucosal fibrosis, immune homeostasis, and gene expression profiles. These biological disparities may have partially masked the prognostic value of CFIm25 in elderly patients with SCRC.

In addition, CFIm25 expression was an independent prognostic marker for OS in patients with stage III–IV disease and in those with lymph node metastasis, but not in patients with stage I–II disease or in those without lymph node metastasis. Several factors may account for these subgroup-specific findings. First, CFIm25 is a key regulator of APA during pre-mRNA processing (10) and may function as either a tumor suppressor or a pro-tumorigenic factor depending on the biological context. As tumors progress, the tumor microenvironment undergoes complex alterations that may disrupt the expression and function of multiple oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. Second, with tumor progression, cumulative molecular abnormalities may become more pronounced, and their biological effects may be more evident in advanced-stage tumors and in those with lymph node metastasis. Together, these factors may contribute to the differential prognostic significance of CFIm25 across clinical subgroups.

In the present study, we found that higher CFIm25 expression was associated with more favorable clinical outcomes in CRC. We also observed that the prognostic significance of CFIm25 differed according to schistosomiasis status and tumor stage. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, additional mechanistic studies are needed to clarify the relationship between CFIm25 and schistosomiasis and to determine how schistosome infection may influence CFIm25 expression. Second, because this was a single-center retrospective study, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Validation in larger, prospective, multicenter cohorts is therefore warranted. Third, we recognize the inherent limitation of the TMA approach in assessing biomarkers with potentially heterogeneous or focal expression patterns, which may increase the risk of false-negative results and affect the observed prognostic significance of CFIm25 in CRC. In order to minimize sampling bias and ensure tissue representativeness, tissue areas showing massive necrosis, severe hemorrhage, prominent inflammatory infiltration, or dense fibrosis were excluded by experienced pathologists. Subsequently, non-necrotic tumor cores with a diameter of 2.0 mm were harvested from the most representative tumor region of each patient. Fourth, preoperative and postoperative antitumor treatments are critical confounding factors that can substantially affect OS and may interfere with the detection and interpretation of target gene expression. To minimize treatment-related confounding bias, none of the included patients received any form of preoperative antitumor therapy. Furthermore, no patients underwent postoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy. All patients received only traditional Chinese medicine after surgery, and no other antitumor interventions were administered throughout the follow-up period. This highly uniform perioperative treatment strategy effectively reduced the confounding effects of heterogeneous antitumor treatments on survival outcomes and gene expression analysis, thereby improving the homogeneity and reliability of the study population. Nevertheless, individual differences in traditional Chinese medicine regimens may still have existed and should be acknowledged as a potential inherent limitation of this retrospective study. Lastly, in the univariate and multivariate regression analyses, multiple subgroup analyses were performed without corresponding correction for multiple comparisons, which may have increased the risk of type I error. Therefore, all subgroup findings should be regarded as exploratory and require further verification in an independent cohort.

In summary, our study showed that reduced CFIm25 expression is associated with poor prognosis in CRC, suggesting that CFIm25 may serve as a promising prognostic biomarker. These findings may also aid risk stratification and clinical management, particularly in CRC patients with schistosomiasis. Owing to limitations in research conditions and objective constraints, the present study should be regarded as a preliminary exploratory investigation and has several inherent limitations. Future multicenter, large-scale clinical studies with sufficient sample sizes are needed to validate our findings and further refine the relevant conclusions.
