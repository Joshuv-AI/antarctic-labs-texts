Gut microbial alterations and functional shifts in patients with hypertriglyceridemia: insights from a northwestern Chinese metagenomic study
Authors: Jing Lv, Ji-Han Wang, Yang-Yang Wang, Jing Huang, Fen-Rong Chen, Sa Fang, Xiao-Juan Wang, Zi-Tong Li, Yu-Peng Shi, Lei Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough hypertriglyceridemia (HTG) is a significant contributor to lipid-associated pathologies such as atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, its regulation by host‒microbiome interactions remain insufficiently characterized. While the gut microbiota (GM) is known to influence cholesterol metabolism, its specific role in systemic triglyceride (TG) homeostasis, particularly in non-Western populations, is poorly defined. This study aimed to identify preliminary robust GM signatures associated with HTG and to assess their translational potential using integrated multiomics and explainable machine learning approaches. In a cross-sectional investigation of 50 well-phenotyped adults from Northwest China, we combined 16S rRNA sequencing, shotgun metagenomics, and ensemble machine learning (LightGBM/XGBoost) to elucidate the associations between the GM and TGs. Microbial features were rigorously linked to serum lipid profiles through dual-algorithm validation and SHAP interpretability analysis, while functional potential was assessed via KEGG pathway mapping. Subjects with HTG exhibited a distinct gut microbial configuration, marked by consistent enrichment of Faecalibacterium and Bacteroides coprocola (positively correlated with serum TG levels) and depletion of Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum and Lactobacillus salivarius (inversely correlated). Machine learning converged on five exploratory consensus biomarker taxa, three of which were independently confirmed by LEfSe analysis (Faecalibacterium). Functional profiling further revealed the upregulation of microbial starch and sucrose metabolism pathways in the HTG cohort. Our findings establish a preliminary gut microbial signature for HTG patients and suggest context‑dependent associations of butyrate-producing taxa such as Faecalibacterium. By integrating multiomics with explainable artificial intelligence, this work addresses key challenges in reproducibility and mechanistic inference in microbiome research. Introduction

The global obesity epidemic has increased to unprecedented levels, with adult prevalence rates doubling and adolescent rates quadrupling since 1990 (Organization, 2025). By 2022, one in eight individuals worldwide met the criteria for obesity (Organization, 2025). This chronic, multifactorial disease state, resulting from prolonged positive energy balance, is characterized by pathological adipose tissue expansion and is fundamentally associated with significant disruptions in lipid homeostasis (Organization, 2025). These metabolic alterations are clinically evident through distinct changes in circulating and tissue lipid profiles, making comprehensive blood lipid analysis essential for elucidating obesity-related pathophysiological mechanisms. Lipid metabolism demonstrates substantial interindividual variability, shaped by intricate interactions between genetic determinants and modifiable environmental factors. Genome-wide association studies revealed more than 500 single-nucleotide polymorphisms across 150 loci that collectively explain approximately 40% of the observed total individual variation in lipid profiles (Garcia-Giustiniani and Stein, 2016). The remaining variance is attributable to dynamic lifestyle factors including dietary patterns, physical activity levels, substance use (alcohol and tobacco), psychological stress, and various clinical conditions and pharmacological treatments (Association et al., 2022; Management, 2023). These complex interactions give rise to diverse dyslipidemia phenotypes, clinically defined by abnormal concentrations of triglycerides (TGs), cholesterol fractions (total cholesterol [TC], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C]), or their associated lipoprotein particles. Of particular concern is the rapidly growing burden of dyslipidemia in China, where national epidemiological studies reported a prevalence of 35.6% among adults as of 2018, with more than half of the population exhibiting suboptimal lipid parameters (Gao et al., 2016; Management, 2023; Pan et al., 2016; Song et al., 2019). While genetic studies have provided important insight into lipid regulation, a substantial proportion (60%) of unexplained variance highlights the critical role of environmental modulators and emerging biological factors such as the host gut microbiota (GM) (Buck et al., 1997; Kokkinos and Fernhall, 1999; Matey-Hernandez et al., 2018; O’Doherty et al., 2017).

The human gut harbors a remarkably complex and dynamic ecosystem comprising more than 100 trillion microorganisms, including at least 1,000 distinct species. Collectively, these microbes encode millions of genes, exceeding the coding capacity of the human nuclear genome by 150-fold and endowing the host with expanded metabolic capabilities (Turnbaugh et al., 2007). This intricate microbial community, often termed the “forgotten organ”, exhibits marked interindividual variability shaped by host genetics (Kim et al., 2020b; Schloissnig et al., 2013; Yatsunenko et al., 2012), with early-life colonization establishing a foundational architecture that matures and stabilizes during adolescence (Dominguez-Bello et al., 2011). Nevertheless, its composition remains highly plastic throughout adulthood and is subject to modulation by diverse factors, including antibiotic use, pharmacotherapy, infections, long-term dietary habits, psychological stress, and other environmental exposures (Moszak et al., 2020). Emerging research has redefined the GM as a critical endocrine entity capable of orchestrating systemic physiological processes, particularly lipid homeostasis (Jasirwan et al., 2019; Oliphant and Allen-Vercoe, 2019; Rinninella et al., 2019). A growing body of evidence implicates GM dysbiosis as a central player in cardiometabolic disorders, in which it exerts dual effects on both adipogenesis and lipid metabolism regulation (Matey-Hernandez et al., 2018; Plaza-Diaz, 2020). The GM influences host lipid profiles through multiple mechanisms, including the production of metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids [SCFAs], secondary bile acids [BAs], and trimethylamine-N-oxide), epigenetic modulation of host metabolic pathways, and direct interactions with intestinal lipid absorption and transport systems (Bäckhed et al., 2007; Huang et al., 2025; Nogal et al., 2021; Vítek and Haluzík, 2016). Although a consistent GM signature distinguishing obese from lean phenotypes has been identified and dynamic microbial shifts during weight loss have been well documented (Seganfredo et al., 2017), methodological heterogeneity, particularly in dietary interventions, sequencing techniques, and study durations, has contributed to discrepancies across studies. Despite these challenges, the GM has increasingly been recognized as a crucial explanatory factor for the unexplained variance in human serum lipid profiles, a mechanistic contributor to dyslipidemia pathogenesis, and a promising therapeutic target for metabolic disorders (Fu et al., 2015).

While the cardiovascular (CV) consequences of cholesterol fractions, particularly LDL-C as the principal mediator of atherosclerotic pathogenesis, have long dominated dyslipidemia research (Borén et al., 2020; Ference et al., 2019; Fernández-Friera et al., 2017; Gorden et al., 2015; Meikle et al., 2014; Morales et al., 2022), accumulating evidence underscores the clinical significance of TG metabolism (Ingelsson et al., 2007; Lee et al., 2020). Notably, elevated TG levels are associated with subclinical atherosclerosis and vascular inflammation even in normocholesterolemic individuals (Raposeiras-Roubin et al., 2021), and large-scale epidemiological studies have consistently identified hypertriglyceridemia (HTG) as an independent predictor of adverse CV events (Esan and Wierzbicki, 2021; Klempfner et al., 2016). Despite these associations, current clinical guidelines reserve TG-lowering therapy (e.g., statins) only for high-risk populations with TG concentrations exceeding 200 mg/dL (Mach et al., 2020), reflecting persistent uncertainties about the underlying pathophysiology of HTG. This knowledge gap is further exacerbated by the inherent biological variability of TG levels (23%~40%) (2021, 2022), which complicates both mechanistic studies and clinical risk assessment. Intriguingly, while the interplay between the GM and cholesterol metabolism has been extensively characterized (Ference et al., 2019; Morales et al., 2022), investigations into GM‒TG cross-talk remain limited, despite the metabolic plasticity and heightened responsiveness of TG to microbial modulation. To bridge this critical gap, we conducted a comprehensive analysis combining 16S rRNA gene sequencing, metagenomic sequencing and machine learning-driven bioinformatics in a western Chinese cohort. Our study aims to systematically identify preliminary HTG-associated GM signatures, identify novel exploratory microbial biomarkers predictive of TG dysregulation, and elucidate potential mechanistic links between specific bacterial taxa/functions and TG metabolism. By shifting focus from the conventional cholesterol-centric paradigm, this investigation provides new insights into HTG pathogenesis while offering potential avenues for microbe-based risk stratification and therapeutic intervention.

Materials and methods

Study design and participant recruitment

This observational study enrolled 205 adults (aged ≥ 18 years) from Honghui Hospital, Xi’an Jiaotong University, China. To minimize confounding effects, participants were excluded on the basis of stringent predefined criteria (Guo et al., 2022; Marques et al., 2019): (1) inability to provide written informed consent; (2) pregnancy or lactation (due to hormonal and metabolic fluctuations); (3) chronic conditions, such as inflammatory diseases (e.g., irritable bowel syndrome and rheumatoid arthritis); active malignancies; a history of major gastrointestinal surgery (altered gut anatomy); (4) acute or major systemic dysfunctions, such as active infections (bacterial, viral, or fungal), or severe cardiopulmonary, neurological, or psychiatric disorders; (5) recent use of GM-modifying agents, such as probiotics/prebiotics within 6 weeks before fecal sampling and antimicrobial agents within 6 months; or (6) use of medications influencing GM or metabolic profiles, including anti-inflammatory drugs, immunosuppressants, acid suppressants, lipid-lowering agents, or glucose regulators.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Honghui Hospital, Xi’an Jiaotong University (Approval NO.: 201801022). All participants provided written informed consent, and the data were anonymized to ensure confidentiality. The study workflow is summarized in Fig. 1.

Clinical and biochemical data collection

Anthropometric and clinical measurements

At baseline, all participants underwent standardized clinical assessments conducted by trained personnel. Body weight (W) and height (H) were measured to the nearest 0.1 kg and 0.1 cm, respectively, with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Waist circumference (WC) was measured at the midpoint between the lower rib margin and the iliac crest using a nonstretchable measuring tape (± 0.1 cm precision), while participants stood in an upright position. Blood pressure (BP) measurements were obtained by trained physicians using a validated automated sphygmomanometer (OMRON HEM-7130 professional, OMRON, Dalian, China) after participants had rested in a seated position for at least 5 min. Three consecutive readings were taken at 1-min intervals on the left arm and maintained at heart level, with the mean value used for subsequent analysis. All measurement devices underwent calibration prior to study commencement to ensure accuracy.

Laboratory analyses

Fasting venous blood samples were collected following an overnight fast of at least 8 h. Serum TG, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels were quantified using an automated biochemical analyzer (Cobas c701, Roche, Mannheim, Germany). The analytical procedures followed standardized protocols, including regular calibration and stringent quality control measures to ensure measurement precision and reliability.

Diagnostic criteria

Dyslipidemia diagnosis was established in accordance with the Chinese Guidelines for Lipid Management (2023)(Management, 2023), with case definitions requiring the presence of untreated serum lipid abnormalities meeting at least one of the following diagnostic thresholds: fasting TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L, TC ≥ 5.2 mmol/L, LDL-C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L, or HDL-C < 1.0 mmol/L. To specifically investigate TG metabolism, the study participants were prospectively stratified into two distinct phenotypic groups: the HTG group characterized by an increase in isolated TG concentration ≥ 1.7 mmol/L with other lipid parameters within normal limits, and normolipidemic controls (CON), in which all measured lipid fractions (TG, TC, LDL-C) were within guideline-defined physiological ranges. This stratification approach enabled focused examination of TG-specific metabolic perturbations while controlling for confounding effects from other dyslipidemia subtypes.

Fecal sample collection, preservation, and DNA extraction

Fresh fecal specimens were collected by participants following standardized protocols (Franzosa et al., 2014; Lv et al., 2023), utilizing sterile collection kits containing insulated containers with frozen cold packs to ensure sample integrity. Immediately after collection, the samples were transported to the Clinical Laboratory of Honghui Hospital, Xi’an Jiaotong University, within a strict 6-h window to minimize alterations in the microbial composition. Upon arrival, the specimens were promptly stored at -80 °C in a dedicated cryogenic facility to preserve nucleic acid stability until further processing. For subsequent analyses, frozen samples were securely transported on dry ice to Novogene Co., Led. (Beijing, China), maintaining an unbroken cold chain throughout transit.

Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from precisely measured 200 mg fecal aliquots using a QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s optimized protocol. Extracted DNA underwent rigorous quality assessment through a three-part verification system: (1) UV spectrophotometric analysis (Nanodrop 2000; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) confirming optimal purity (OD260/280 ratio of 1.8 ~ 2.0) (2), electrophoretic separation on 1% agarose gels to verify DNA integrity and the absence of degradation, and (3) fluorometric quantification using a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer and dsDNA assay kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for precise concentration determination. Only samples meeting all predefined quality thresholds (> 1 µg total DNA yield, intact high-molecular-weight bands, and the absence of protein/chemical contaminants) were approved for downstream sequencing applications. Furthermore, all 50 subjects (19 HTG and 31 controls) underwent both 16S rRNA and metagenomic shotgun sequencing. In this study, 16S rRNA sequencing provided a broad taxonomic framework, while metagenomics was specifically employed to capture high-resolution functional metabolic profiles.

16S rRNA gene sequencing and microbiome profiling

The V3 ~ V4 hypervariable regions of bacterial 16S rRNA genes were amplified using barcoded universal primers (338F: 5’-ACT CCT ACG GGA GGC AGC AG-3’; 806R: 5’-GGA CTA CHV GGG TWT CTA AT-3’) with Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) under optimized cycling conditions. PCR products were pooled in equimolar ratios and subjected to gel electrophoresis purification using a GeneJET™ Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Purified amplicons were subsequently processed into sequencing libraries using an NEB Next® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), with library quality assessed through fluorometric quantification using a Qubit® 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The final libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE150) at Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Bioinformatic processing of the raw sequencing data was performed using a standardized pipeline. Sequence quality control and operational taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering were conducted in UPARSE (v7.0.1001) (Edgar, 2013), with high-quality reads clustered into OTUs at 97% nucleotide similarity. Taxonomic classification was performed using the RDP classifier (v2.2) (Cole et al., 2014). Microbial community analyses were conducted in QIIME1 (v1.7.0) (Cheng et al., 2020), including the generation of Venn diagrams (VennDiagram R package) to visualize shared and unique OTUs among groups, the construction of rank-abundance curves, and rarefaction analysis to evaluate sequencing depth adequacy. Alpha diversity metrics (Chao1, Ace, Shannon, and Simpson indices) were calculated to characterize microbial community complexity, and the taxonomic composition was analyzed across six hierarchical levels (phylum to species). For group comparisons, differentially abundant taxa were identified using Student’s t test and linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis.

Metagenomic sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

For comprehensive microbial community characterization, metagenomic sequencing was performed on qualified DNA samples (1 µg per sample) using an optimized library preparation protocol. Libraries were constructed with a NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) following a standardized workflow: (1) DNA fragmentation to ~ 350 bp fragments via focused ultrasonication (2), end-repair and A-tailing (3), adapter ligation with unique dual-indexed Illumina adapters, and (4) limited-cycle PCR amplification. The quality of the purified libraries (AMPure XP based purification) was rigorously assessed, including fluorometric quantification using a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), size distribution verification using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and qPCR-based molarity determination, to ensure optimal cluster generation. Only libraries meeting stringent criteria, such as an effective concentration > 3 nM, were pooled in equimolar ratios for high-throughput sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE250) at Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Raw sequencing data were processed through an established bioinformatics pipeline: (1) quality control using SOAP2 (v2.04) to remove low-quality reads and adapter sequences; (2) host DNA depletion via Bowtie2 (v2.2.4) alignment against the human reference genome; (3) de novo assembly using MetaGeneMark (v2.10) to generate high-quality scaftigs (≥ 500 bp); (4) gene prediction with MetaGeneMark (v2.10); (5) construction of a nonredundant gene catalog with CD-HIT (v4.5.8); and (6) gene abundance quantification via Bowtie2 (v2.2.4) alignment. Only genes with ≥ 2 mapped reads were retained for downstream analysis (Qin et al., 2010). Functional annotation was performed by aligning predicted genes against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database (KEGG Release 73.1, 2018) using DIAMOND (v0.9.9), and putative eukaryotic sequences were systematically excluded. Differential pathway analysis between the HTG and control groups revealed significant differences.

Machine learning-based classification and feature selection

To establish a robust preliminary GM signature for discriminating HTG patients from normolipidemic controls, we employed two advanced ensemble machine learning (ML) algorithms: light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM) and eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). LightGBM utilized a leafwise tree growth strategy, optimizing computational efficiency while maintaining high predictive performance, whereas XGBoost adopted a depthwise approach with integrated L1/L2 regularization to prevent overfitting, an essential consideration for high-dimensional microbial datasets (Chang et al., 2023; Comertpay and Gov, 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). Model hyperparameters, including the learning rate, maximum tree depth, subsampling ratio, and feature fraction, were systematically tuned via cross-validation to enhance generalizability and minimize bias.

To interpret model decisions and identify the most discriminative exploratory microbial biomarkers, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), a game theory-based method that quantifies the marginal contribution of each feature to model predictions (Novielli et al., 2024; Rynazal et al., 2023). Features were ranked by their mean absolute SHAP values, with the top 10 most influential taxa retained for biological interpretation. Additionally, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the “FactoMineR” and “factoextra” R packages to visualize sample clustering and intergroup variation on the basis of the GM compositional profiles. PCA incorporated scaled relative abundances of microbial taxa at the genus level, with principal components explaining the greatest variance used to assess structural differences between the HTG and control cohorts.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted using a comprehensive analytical framework incorporating SPSS (v23.0.0, IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL), R (v4.0.2, R Foundation, Vienna, Austria), and GraphPad Prism (v5.01, GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables were first evaluated for normality assumptions using the Shapiro‒Wilk test, and normally distributed data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). Between-group comparisons of clinical parameters were performed using independent Student’s t tests. The association between gut microbial features and serum TG levels was investigated through Spearman’s rank correlation analysis, implemented using the “cor.test” function in R (method = “spearman”), with the results visualized using the “ggcorrplot” package. All hypothesis tests were two-tailed.

Gut microbiota profiling: 16S rRNA sequencing and metagenomic functional analysis

Comprehensive 16S rRNA gene sequencing of fecal samples from all 50 participants (after stringent quality control) revealed preliminary distinct microbial signatures associated with serum TG levels. An evaluation of sequencing depth through species accumulation and rarefaction curves (Fig. 2A ~ B) confirmed adequate coverage of microbial diversity, while the results of the rank-abundance curves (Fig. 2C) revealed similar species richness and evenness patterns between the HTG and control groups. Taxonomic analysis revealed 1,247 OTUs, with controls harboring a greater number of unique OTUs than HTG individuals did (Fig. 2D). Alpha diversity (α-diversity) metrics (Shannon, Chao1, Simpson and Ace indices) showed no significant intergroup differences (Fig. S1), nor were meaningful correlations observed between these diversity measures and serum TG concentrations.

Taxonomic profiling across six classification levels (Fig. 3A, C, E, G, I, K) revealed Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes to be the dominant phyla, with Clostridia and Bacteroidia predominating at the class level. The microbial hierarchy further revealed Clostridiales, Bifidobacteriales, and Bacteroidales as the major orders; Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae, and Bifidobacteriaceae as the predominant families; Bifidobacterium, Faecalibacterium, Agathobacter, and Bacteroides as the most abundant genera; and Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum and Bifidobacterium adolescentis as the most abundant species. Notably, compared with controls, HTG group subjects presented significantly greater proportions of Proteobacteria (phylum, P < 0.05), Gammaproteobacteria (class, P < 0.1), Ruminococcaceae (family, P < 0.1), Faecalibacterium (genus, P < 0.01), and Bacteroides coprocola (species, P < 0.05). Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant positive associations between serum TG levels and the abundances of several microbial taxa, including unidentified Bacteria (phylum), Negativicutes (class), Selenomonadales (order), Burkholderiaceae (family), Faecalibacterium and Ralstonia (genus), and Ralstonia pickettii and Bacteroides coprocola (species) (all P < 0.05). Conversely, the abundances of Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum and Lactobacillus salivarius (species) were inversely correlated with the TG concentration (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3B, D, F, H, J, L). In addition, Ruminococcaceae (family) and Agathobacter (genus) were weakly positively correlated with serum TG levels, and Actinobacteria (phylum) was weakly negatively correlated (all P < 0.1). LEfSe analysis (Fig. 4) reinforced these findings, identifying Faecalibacterium (genus) and Bacteroides coprocola (species) as HTG-enriched biomarkers, whereas Peptostreptococcaceae (family), Intestinibacter (genus), and Lactobacillus salivarius (species) were characteristic of the controls.

ML-driven identification of gut microbial biomarkers for hypertriglyceridemia

Our ML analysis revealed preliminary robust gut microbial signatures capable of accurately differentiating HTG patients from normolipidemic controls. The LightGBM model identified 10 discriminatory taxa, prominently featuring Firmicutes (phylum), Gammaproteobacteria (class), and unidentified Gammaproteobacteria (order), along with the genera Agathobacter and Faecalibacterium and the species Ruminococcus bromii and Bifidobacterium adolescentis (Fig. 6A). The XGBoost algorithm independently selected a partially overlapping set of biomarkers, emphasizing Proteobacteria (phylum), Clostridia (class), Enterobacteriales and unidentified Gammaproteobacteria (order), and Prevotellaceae (family), while also highlighting Agathobacter and Faecalibacterium at the genus level (Fig. 6B). Notably, both algorithms converged on five exploratory consensus biomarkers, namely, the unidentified Gammaproteobacteria order and the Agathobacter and Faecalibacterium genera, and these findings were independently validated by LEfSe analysis, which revealed significant enrichment of Faecalibacterium in HTG group subjects (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

Dyslipidemia, a systemic metabolic disorder characterized by aberrant lipid homeostasis, contributes to the pathogenesis of multiple organ dysfunctions, including hepatic steatosis, vascular endothelial injury, and cardiovascular disease (CVD), through chronic lipid accumulation and subsequent lipotoxicity(Management, 2023). A growing body of evidence from translational and clinical studies highlights the GM as a key modulator of host lipid metabolism, exerting its influence through multifaceted mechanisms such as bile acid (BA) biotransformation, SCFA-mediated signaling, and immunometabolic regulation (Castaner et al., 2018b; Cotillard et al., 2013; Fu et al., 2015; Le Chatelier et al., 2013; Schoeler and Caesar, 2019). These microbial‒host interactions have been implicated in the development of dyslipidemia-associated comorbidities, including nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), atherosclerosis, and major adverse CV events. While previous investigations have reported inconsistent associations between specific microbial taxa and cholesterol metabolism (e.g., TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C) (Fu et al., 2015; Le Chatelier et al., 2013; Schoeler and Caesar, 2019), research has focused predominantly on cholesterol-centric pathways because of their well-documented role in atherogenesis. In contrast, the contribution of the GM to TG metabolism remains poorly characterized, despite emerging epidemiological and clinical data establishing HTG as an independent risk factor for CVD. The current literature lacks comprehensive, reproducible evidence defining GM‒TG interactions, a critical knowledge gap that our study addresses through an integrative approach combining 16S rRNA sequencing, metagenomic functional profiling, and ML-based biomarker discovery.

Serum TG regulation reflects a dynamic equilibrium between genetic determinants (~ 40% interindividual variability) and modifiable environmental factors, exhibiting characteristic fluctuations across demographic and temporal dimensions (Association et al., 2022; Garcia-Giustiniani and Stein, 2016; Management, 2023). Epidemiological patterns reveal distinct age- and sex-related variations: TG levels typically increase with age before plateauing in the sixth decade, whereas compared with women, men demonstrate greater seasonal variability (elevated winter levels potentially reflecting thermoregulatory metabolic adaptations) (Gordon et al., 1987). Lifestyle factors exert particularly potent synergistic effects on TG homeostasis: alcohol consumption drives dose-dependent hepatic VLDL overproduction, an effect amplified by dietary fat intake and polymorphisms in alcohol dehydrogenase genes (Taskinen et al., 1987), whereas smoking induces persistent HTG through nicotine-induced catecholamine release and subsequent adipose tissue lipolysis (Brischetto et al., 1983). The clinical spectrum of HTG extends well beyond primary dyslipidemia, encompassing diverse secondary causes including endocrine disorders (hypothyroidism and uncontrolled diabetes), organ dysfunction (hepatic steatosis and nephrotic syndrome), and medication effects (estrogens and immunosuppressants) (Management, 2023). This pathophysiological complexity explains why HTG persists as an independent CV risk predictor even in statin-treated patients who achieve optimal LDL-C targets (Nichols et al., 2018; Vergès, 2015; Zhao et al., 2019), promoting recent guideline updates to recognize borderline elevation (150 ~ 199 mg/dL) as a marker of metabolic dysfunction and levels ≥ 200 mg/dL as high-risk thresholds warranting intensified intervention (Jellinger et al., 2017). Current management follows a stepped therapeutic hierarchy: (1) facial lifestyle interventions: Mediterranean-style diets, structured aerobic exercise, and smoking cessation; [2] pharmacological strategies: Fibrates or high-dose omega-3 fatty acids (2 ~ 4 g/day) to treat severe HTG (TG > 500 mg/dL), or fibrates-statin combinations for HTG with high residual risk (TG ≥ 200 mg/dL and HDL-C < 40 mg/dL), as well as bile acid sequestrants cholesterol absorption inhibitors, PCSK9 inhibitors, and combination therapy; and [3] emerging approaches such as niacin derivatives and GM-targeted therapies (Management, 2023). While conventional pharmacotherapies demonstrate efficacy, their utility is constrained by adverse effects (myotoxicity, hepatotoxicity) and suboptimal long-term adherence (Fernandes et al., 2016; Law et al., 2003; Lim, 2016; Thompson et al., 2016), highlighting the need for innovative alternatives. Functional food supplements and nutraceuticals present distinct advantages, including multitarget activity, enhanced biocompatibility, and favorable safety profiles (Management, 2023). Our identification of preliminary robust HTG-associated microbial signatures provides a scientific foundation for developing microbiome-based interventions, including precision probiotics, phage-mediated modulation, and personalized dietary strategies targeting microbial metabolic pathways.

A growing body of evidence positions the GM as a central regulator of host lipid metabolism, with its composition dynamically influenced by intrinsic (age, sex, genetic background) and extrinsic (diet, medications, lifestyle) factors (Bezirtzoglou and Stavropoulou, 2011), many of which are known determinants of serum TG levels (Management, 2023). Preclinical studies provide compelling mechanistic insights: compared with their conventional counterparts, germ-free mice demonstrate significantly reduced adipocyte TG storage and enhanced systemic TG clearance, highlighting the pivotal role of the microbiota in lipid partitioning (Bäckhed et al., 2007; Kindt et al., 2018; Rabot et al., 2010; Velagapudi et al., 2010). Murine models further revealed that diet-GM crosstalk coordinately regulates circulating and hepatic lipid profiles through multiple interconnected pathways, including BA transformation, SCFA signaling, and epigenetic modulation of host metabolic genes (Schoeler and Caesar, 2019). In humans, the relationship between the GM and the TG is more complex but remains clinically relevant. Large-scale metagenomic studies consistently link reduced microbial gene richness (a key indicator of gut ecosystem health) with elevated serum TG levels, whereas dietary interventions that increase microbial diversity (e.g., high-fiber or Mediterranean diets) concurrently improve lipid profiles (Castaner et al., 2018a; Cotillard et al., 2013; Fu et al., 2015; Le Chatelier et al., 2013; Matey-Hernandez et al., 2018). Particularly noteworthy is the LifeLines-DEEP cohort study (n = 893), which quantified the independent contribution of GM to TG variance at 6%, with combined host-microbiome factors explaining 17.1% of interindividual TG variability (Fu et al., 2015). These findings directly informed our study design, driving our primary objective to identify robust, reproducible GM signatures associated with TG dysregulation and to advance beyond correlation toward clinically actionable biomarkers.

Through comprehensive multiomics analysis, we identified characteristic gut microbial alterations associated with HTG in a rigorously phenotyped Western Chinese cohort. Importantly, these preliminary microbial signatures emerged despite comparable baseline characteristics between HTG patients and normolipidemic controls, suggesting their potential as independent biomarkers of TG dysregulation. Our findings revealed three key microbial patterns in HTG subjects: (1) HTG-enriched taxa, namely, Ruminococcaceae, Faecalibacterium, and Bacteroides coprocola, which align with emerging evidence linking these taxa to enhanced dietary lipid absorption, altered BA metabolism, and proinflammatory mucosal responses; [2] protective microbial signatures, in which we identified inverse correlations between serum TG levels and Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum and Lactobacillus salivarius, which have been previously associated with improved gut barrier function and reduced endotoxemia; and [3] ML-validated biomarkers, in which both the LightGBM and XGBoost algorithms independently identified five discriminatory features, with three showing a consistent HTG association. The robust reproducibility of Faecalibacterium as an HTG marker across both the ML models and traditional LEfSe analysis is particularly noteworthy.

Specifically, our study identified Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum and Lactobacillus salivarius as key commensal bacteria that are inversely correlated with serum TG levels, with the latter being particularly enriched in normolipidemic controls. These observations are consistent with the well-documented metabolic benefits of the Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus genera, which appear to modulate lipid metabolism through several complementary mechanisms, such as BA deconjugation altering lipid absorption, SCFA production influencing hepatic lipid metabolism, and intestinal barrier enhancement reducing metabolic endotoxemia (Cano et al., 2013; Tomaro-Duchesneau et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2013). The lipid-modulating potential of these microbial taxa is strongly supported by preclinical evidence. In animal models, specific strains such as Lactobacillus curvatus and Lactobacillus plantarum have been shown to effectively reduce hepatic TG and cholesterol accumulation under high-fat diet conditions (Yoo et al., 2013). Similarly, the administration of various Bifidobacterium species has demonstrated consistent improvements in atherogenic lipid profiles, notably reducing TG and LDL-C while elevating HDL-C levels in obese rat models (An et al., 2011). These findings provide important mechanistic insights into how gut microbes may influence systemic lipid metabolism. However, translating these findings to human applications presents considerable complexity. Clinical studies reveal a nuanced picture in which microbial-lipid associations are significantly influenced by host factors. For instance, while Bifidobacterium species are generally associated with beneficial lipoprotein(a) reductions, other taxa, such as Agathobacter and Faecalibacterium, are positively correlated with serum TG and LDL-C levels (Xia et al., 2024). Moreover, the efficacy of probiotic interventions exhibits marked interindividual variation, with systematic analyses indicating that factors such as age, BMI, and dosage critically affect outcomes (TG, TC and LDL-C lowering effects) (Hendijani and Akbari, 2018). Compared with elderly recipients, younger individuals typically show more pronounced lipid improvements, likely due to age-related changes in microbial ecosystem plasticity (Hendijani and Akbari, 2018), with these responses further modulated by sex-specific differences in microbial composition and metabolic response (Xia et al., 2024). Clinical trials also highlight both the promise and challenges of microbiome-based interventions. While multistrain probiotics such as Probio-X significantly modulate levels of cholesterol fractions (TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C) in hyperlipidemic patients, their effects on TG levels remain limited (Wang et al., 2023). This pattern is corroborated by meta-analyses showing consistent cholesterol improvements but variable TG responses across different populations (Shimizu et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2017). Interestingly, certain microbial formulations exhibit selective lipid-modulating properties, with specific Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus strains showing preferential TG-lowering effects in healthy individuals, whereas Bifidobacterium-yeast extract combinations demonstrate comprehensive lipid-improving effects (Ruscica et al., 2019).

The field continues to evolve with innovative approaches. Promising strategies include Akkermansia muciniphila supplementation, which has shown potential in reducing hepatic TG synthesis and serum TG levels through the regulation of sterol regulatory element-binding proteins and the restoration of GM homeostasis in obesity models (Kim et al., 2020a). Additionally, plant-derived bioactive compounds such as perilla oil and soybean 7 S globulin-derived peptides appear to modulate TG metabolism through BA-mediated reshaping of gut microbial communities (Han et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2018). These findings collectively suggest that the functional equilibrium of microbial communities may be as crucial as their absolute abundance in maintaining metabolic homeostasis. This understanding underscores the potential of targeted microbial interventions, including precision probiotics and synbiotic formulations, for the management of dyslipidemia in high-risk CV populations (Beserra et al., 2015). Furthermore, the therapeutic efficacy of probiotic interventions demonstrates remarkable strain specificity, with each microbial species or strain combination exhibiting unique biological effects that are further modulated by critical factors including manufacturing protocols, formulation matrices, and individual variations in gut microenvironment conditions (Hendijani and Akbari, 2018; Ruscica et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023). This multidimensional variability explains the heterogeneous clinical outcomes observed across studies. While well-characterized strains such as Lactobacillus plantarum and Bifidobacterium lactis consistently demonstrate lipid-modulating capabilities in controlled clinical settings, their therapeutic effects show substantial interindividual variations depending on baseline microbiota architecture, host age, and metabolic status (Hendijani and Akbari, 2018; Ruscica et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023). In this context, our identification of Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum and Lactobacillus salivarius as novel TG level-lowering taxa represents a significant advancement, providing new candidates for targeted dyslipidemia interventions.

Our study enhances the current understanding by applying ML approaches to decipher complex host–microbiome interactions, revealing that Ruminococcaceae and Faecalibacterium are exploratory robust microbial biomarkers of TG metabolism despite inherent population heterogeneity. The Ruminococcaceae family, which is frequently associated with obesity (Riva et al., 2017), has complex relationships with lipid profiles, as exemplified by Ruminococcus gnavus—a microbial diversity marker that is positively correlated with serum TG levels (Fu et al., 2015; Le Chatelier et al., 2013; Vojinovic et al., 2019). Expended genomic analyses have identified 15 microbial taxa within this family (including Ruminococcus gnavus and various Ruminococcaceae subgroups) that correlate with lipid profiles (Vojinovic et al., 2019), potentially through their enzymatic activities in BA transformation (producing secondary BAs such as deoxycholic acid via 7a-dehydroxylase) and SCFA production (Kakiyama et al., 2013; Koh et al., 2016; Vital et al., 2017). These metabolic functions may underlie the observed associations between Ruminococcaceae composition and specific lipid classes, including positive correlations with polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA)-containing TGs and sphingomyelins and negative associations between Bifidobacterial diversity and plasma long-chain TG levels (Bondia-Pons et al., 2014), highlighting secondary BA-producing bacteria as promising intervention targets. The case of Faecalibacterium, a major butyrate producer, also illustrates the complexity of microbiome‒lipid relationships. While traditionally linked to metabolic health (Morales et al., 2022), our findings paradoxically associate it with elevated TG levels in HTG subjects, suggesting potential strain-specific effects, host-modifying factors, or ecological interactions within the microbial community. These contrasting observations emphasize the need to investigate strain-level genetic and metabolic determinants of lipid regulation beyond broad taxonomic classifications. Future research should focus on isolating and characterizing these microbial taxa to define their lipid-modifying mechanisms, followed by targeted evaluation in gnotobiotic models and clinical trials to assess their therapeutic potential.

Regarding study design and participant characteristics, it is noteworthy that the HTG group in our cohort presented significantly higher BMI and waist circumference compared to the control group. Given the well-established association between obesity and gut dysbiosis, it is possible that some of the microbial signatures identified in this study are influenced by body mass rather than by hypertriglyceridemia per se. Nevertheless, our findings regarding on the enrichment of Faecalibacterium and Bacteroides coprocola are consistent with their known roles in regulating lipid homeostasis and short-chain fatty acid production. Moreover, the functional enrichment in starch and sucrose metabolism pathways further supports a specific link to triglyceride dysregulation. Importantly, we emphasize that the identified microbial alterations and metabolic pathways should be interpreted as associative indicators rather than direct functional drivers of lipid metabolism at this stage. While our multi-omics and machine learning-based framework provides a robust foundation for biomarker discovery, the absence of in vivo validation remains a key limitation. Whether these microbial signatures play a causal role in the pathogenesis of HTG or represent a secondary response to systemic lipid dysregulation requires further investigation through gnotobiotic models and longitudinal clinical trials. Future studies utilizing BMI-matched cohorts or larger sample sizes with multivariate adjustment are warranted to further isolate the independent contribution of triglycerides to the gut microbiome.

Limitations

While our investigation provides valuable insights into the associations between the GM and TG levels, several limitations must be acknowledged. The primary constraint is our modest but well-phenotyped sample size (n = 50, with 19 HTG and 31 controls), which may reduce statistical power for detecting subtle microbial effects and precludes comprehensive subgroup analyses—particularly regarding sex-specific differences. This concern is especially relevant given established sexual dimorphism in both lipid metabolism and gut microbial composition: men typically demonstrate higher CV risk profiles with preferential free fatty oxidation, whereas women exhibit greater α-diversity and enhanced TG storage capacity (Bell et al., 2021; de la Cuesta-Zuluaga et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2022; Razavi et al., 2019; Uranga et al., 2005). These differences likely arise from sex chromosomes and hormone-mediated mechanisms (Huang et al., 2023), with recent work (Xia et al., 2024) confirming significant sex-based divergence in microbial community structure, underscoring the need for sex-stratified designs in future larger-scale studies. Furthermore, the risk of overfitting in machine learning models, even with cross-validation, remains present. Therefore, our findings should be considered exploratory and require validation in larger, independent cohorts. Additional limitations include incomplete assessments of lifestyle confounders such as alcohol consumption, sleep patterns, and psychological stress (Bishehsari et al., 2020; Cui et al., 2022; Song et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023)—all of which are known to significantly influence both GM and serum TG metabolism (Liu et al., 2018). For instance, alcohol disrupts TG homeostasis through multiple pathways, including impaired VLDL clearance and SREBP-1c-mediated lipogenesis (Bäckhed et al., 2004, 2007; Rodriguez and Delzenne, 2021; Turnbaugh et al., 2006). These unmeasured confounders may have contributed to the observed associations. Therefore, future studies should incorporate detailed lifestyle assessments to better isolate the microbial signature of HTG independent of these factors. To mitigate these limitations while maximizing data utility, we implemented robust ML approaches specifically designed to enhance result reliability. Our dual-algorithm pipeline (LightGBM/XGBoost) incorporated repeated cross-validation to identify microbial biomarkers resistant to unmeasured confounders, yielding more generalizable signatures than conventional statistical methods do. PCA demonstrated the dual advantages of our approach: it not only revealed improved group separability using ML-selected biomarkers compared with full microbiome profiles, but also revealed the critical dimensionality reduction capability of our ML framework. This methodology effectively transformed high-dimensional, sparse microbiome data into a compact yet biologically informative feature space while preserving the most discriminative preliminary microbial signatures for HTG classification. The enhanced clustering observed in reduced-dimensionality space illustrates how ML-driven feature selection can overcome the “curse of dimensionality” inherent to microbiome-wide association studies, where microbial features typically far exceed the sample size. Notably, our study cannot establish causality due to the cross‑sectional design. Whether the observed microbial alterations are a cause or a consequence of HTG remains to be determined in future longitudinal or interventional studies.

Conclusion

In this study, preliminary robust gut microbial signatures for HTG were established through integrated multiomics and ML approaches. The consistent identification of Faecalibacterium and Bacteroides coprocola as discriminative taxa across analytical methods highlights their potential as noninvasive biomarkers, advancing our understanding of gut–liver metabolic crosstalk. While these findings provide a foundation for microbiome-based diagnostics, translational applications require large-scale validation with standardized lifestyle monitoring to account for key confounders. Emerging gnotobiotic models support causal links between microbial transplantation and lipid modulation, suggesting promising therapeutic approaches such as precision probiotics and targeted dietary interventions (Org et al., 2015; Ridaura et al., 2013; Schoeler and Caesar, 2019; Turnbaugh et al., 2009). Our work connects microbial endocrinology and AI-driven biomarker discovery, offering a framework to address reproducibility challenges in microbiome research (Ghazalpour et al., 2016). Future efforts should focus on mechanistic validation through bacterial culturomics and proof-of-concept clinical trials to determine the therapeutic potential of microbiome modulation in metabolic disease management.
