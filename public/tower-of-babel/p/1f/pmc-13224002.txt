Abortion bans and maternal mortality rates in U.S. states, 2018-2021
Authors: Emily K Rippey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper seeks to understand the maternal health consequences of public policy decisions surrounding abortion care. To achieve this objective, this paper conducts an event study leveraging data from the Guttmacher Institute, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the U.S. Census Bureau. First, it analyzes sociodemographic and policy differences between states that did and did not pass abortion bans between 2018 and 2021.Then, using a quasi-experimental event study model, it explores the dynamic treatment effects of adopting an abortion ban. Following this analysis, the study conducts placebo tests for robustness. This study finds that states that enacted abortion bans faced distinct economic and social challenges, such as higher rates of poverty, lower levels of educational attainment, and higher rates of residents without health insurance. When controlling for state demographic data, the results from the event study analysis find a positive relationship between abortion bans and maternal mortality, though this finding is not robust to placebo tests. While the results of the present study are not indicative of a causal treatment effect of abortion bans on maternal mortality, it is important to continue monitoring this relationship as additional data become available.Keywords: Social determinants of health, Health inequities, Abortion, Maternal mortality, Racial health equity, Public policy, Demography

Introduction

Maternal mortality—defined as a death during pregnancy or within 42 days postpartum that is related to or aggravated by pregnancy and often expressed as a ratio of deaths per 100,000 live births—is a growing public health concern in the United States (Declercq & Zephyrin, 2020). From 1987 to 2021, the maternal mortality rate (MMR) in the United States quadrupled, increasing from 7.2 to 32.9 (Hoyert, 2023; Koonin et al., 1997). Recent years have demonstrated record-high numbers of maternal deaths. There was a 40% increase in the number of maternal deaths between 2020 and 2021, jumping from 861 to 1205 (Tanne, 2023). As the maternal mortality crisis plagues the United States, it is critical to understand the influence of public policy decisions on maternal health outcomes. The legislative landscape around maternal and reproductive health has become increasingly hostile and access to care has become more limited. As both anti-abortion legislation and MMRs continue to grow, this paper investigates the relationship between abortion bans and maternal mortality rates.

High rates of maternal mortality and limited access to abortion care disproportionately impacts Black and southern communities. The MMR for Black women in 2021 was 69.9, whereas the rate for white women was 26.6 (Hoyert, 2023). The ratio of Black to white maternal mortality has worsened since data collection on maternal mortality began in the U.S., rising from 1.8 in 1915 to 2.6 in 2021 (Declercq & Zephyrin, 2020; Hoyert, 2023). Data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) for 2018-2021 show that the eight states with the highest MMRs (Arkansas, Mississippi, Tennessee, Alabama, Louisiana, Kentucky, Georgia, and South Carolina) are all located in the U.S. South. Women in Arkansas have an MMR 4.3 times higher than women in California (43.5 and 10.1, respectively) (NCHS, n.d.). These states also have strict abortion policies, either banning abortion entirely or banning abortion after six weeks of pregnancy (State legislation tracker, 2024). In conjunction, these states also face challenges in maternity care, experiencing fewer maternity care providers, less access to prenatal care and postpartum care, increased maternity care deserts, or counties with no hospitals or clinics offering obstetrics and no providers per 10,000 births (Brigance et al., 2022; Kheyfets et al., 2023). Such factors influence quality and access to care and are key determinants of MMR (Frederiksen et al., 2023).

Only a small group of studies investigate how abortion restrictions impact maternal mortality rates. Addante et al. investigated the association of maternal mortality rates with the number of state-level abortion restrictions from 1995 to 2017 using the Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER) and Guttmacher. Using a time-lagged negative binomial regression, the authors found a strong association of maternal mortality rates with a greater number of abortion restrictions (Addante et al., 2021). Vilda et al. looked at specific policies (e.g., waiting periods, ultrasound requirements, etc.) and their association with maternal death for 2015-2018. They found that more restrictive state policies are associated with a greater risk of death while controlling for race and ethnicity (Vilda et al., 2021). Hawkins et al. investigated the relationship using a population-based quasi-experimental design for the years 2007-2015. The authors found that a 20% reduction in Planned Parenthood clinics was associated with an 8% increased maternal mortality rate (Hawkins et al., 2020). This study seeks to contribute to the existing literature by offering a more updated analysis than previously published through examination of data from years 2018 to 2021. Further, while other studies examined restrictive policies, none have examined the influence of near or total abortion bans enacted between 2018 and 2021.

This paper aims to explore the relationship between MMRs and state-level abortion bans. To examine this relationship, this study investigates trends in MMR from 2018 to 2021 in states with and without abortion bans. This study controls for relevant sociodemographic factors at the state level. Through understanding the relationship between passing abortion bans and maternal mortality rates, this study illuminates the social and political determinants that exacerbate geographic and racial disparities in maternal health outcomes. This is critical insight for policymakers and public health officials to understand and address the worsening maternal health crisis in the United States.

Material and methods

Maternal mortality data

This study linked natality and mortality datasets to calculate MMR by state and year. These data are collected by state registries, provided to the National Vital Statistics System (NVSS) and are publicly available through the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). To determine the number of total maternal deaths for each state and each year, this study used the 2018-2021 Underlying Cause of Death database (U.S. Health and Human Services, 2023) and filtered by appropriate International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10) codes. The World Health Organization classifies maternal deaths with ICD-10 codes A34, O00-O95, and O98-99. Data on maternal mortality are combined with information on births from CDC's natality records from 2018 to 2021. CDC data on natality and mortality is publicly disseminated by state and year as it becomes available. However, the CDC suppresses any data that falls below the determined cut-off value to protect personal privacy and prevent the publication of identifiable data. For the natality and mortality data used in this study, any value less than 10 is suppressed.

The analysis for this study begins in 2018 due to updates in the codification of maternal deaths that required states to implement a standardized pregnancy checkbox on death certificates between 2007 and 2018 (Hoyert & Miniño, 2020; National Center for Health Statistics, 2020, NCHS, nd). CDC's National Center for Health Statistics' National Vital Statistics System (NVSS) did not publish official U.S. maternal mortality rates between 2007 and 2018 due to changes in ICD-10 coding.

Abortion legislation data

The Guttmacher Institute provides comprehensive data on a range of reproductive health policies. The State Legislation Tracker contains information on every piece of abortion-related legislation that has been introduced, passed, enacted, or vetoed for each state, dating back to 2015.

Fifteen states enacted a near or total abortion ban between 2018 and 2021 (State legislation tracker, 2024). A near or total abortion ban is defined in three ways: (1) banned at any stage, except if the patient's life is endangered; (2) banned once a heartbeat is detected; (3) banned at eight weeks or less. Covariate data

Sociodemographic data on states, including median income, the unemployment rate, percentage of total civilian noninstitutionalized population without insurance, percent of individuals and families in poverty, educational attainment, and racial composition were captured from the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey 5-year estimates for 2018 to 2021. To account for differences in state-wide obesity rates, data was captured from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System. Finally, this study also included a binary variable indicating if the state expanded Medicaid. This data was collected from Kaiser Family Foundation's State Medicaid Expansion tracker.

Statistical analysis

This study examines the relationship between state-level abortion bans and MMRs between 2018 and 2021 using STATA Version 18. The results reflect a multistep analysis. First, this study conducted descriptive analyses to evaluate the distributions of study variables between states based on if the state passed an abortion ban between 2018 and 2021. These analyses yielded univariate frequencies of the dependent variable (maternal deaths per 100,000 live births) and the control variables (income, poverty, unemployment, educational attainment, insurance status, and race) for each state grouping across the full study period (2018-2021) as well as the absolute difference in the control variable values between the states that did ban and did not ban abortion between 2018 and 2021.

Second, the relationship between enacted abortion bans and state-level MMR was analyzed using an event study design that explores dynamic treatment effects over time. For the purposes of this analysis, each state-year was classified as 0 or 1 if there was a presence of an abortion ban. Then, the time to treatment was assigned for each state-year (e.g., Alabama in 2018 would have a value of −1, representing one year from legislation enactment). To capture dynamic treatment effects, event time binary variables were created for years prior to treatment and years after treatment. The year immediately preceding treatment was excluded to serve as a reference category.

Next, to account for the missing data in the outcome variable, maternal mortality rates were imputed. Given the missingness is not at random, but rather due to systematic oppression of data under 10, data was imputed at the midpoint (five deaths), at the upper bound (nine deaths), and at the lower bound (one death). Approximately 39% of the data were imputed, or 80 of the total 204 observations of maternal mortality. Analyses were performed for the original values and for the three imputed values.

Finally, this study conducted a placebo test to assess if there was an affect prior to the actual treatment happening. This was done by shifting the treatment back in time and re-running the event study using the alternative treatment years.

Results

Fig. 1 displays average maternal mortality rates over the study period based on legislative classification. These findings show that the average levels of MMR increased across 2018 to 2021 regardless of abortion bans. For banned states, the average MMR increased from 27.79 (95% CI: 21.07, 34.52) to 47.46 (95% CI: 38.68, 56.24) deaths per 100,000 births. In states that did not enact abortion bans, the average MMR increased from 13.07 (95% CI: 8.87, 17.27) to 29.42 (95% CI: 21.47, 37.36) deaths per 100,000 births.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between passing an abortion ban and corresponding MMRs. Overall, there are three key findings. First, MMRs increased between 2018 and 2021, regardless of abortion bans. This indicates a growing public health challenge of national concern. Second, this study finds that states with abortion bans have higher MMRs and more socioeconomic distress, including higher rates of poverty, lower median incomes, lower levels of educational attainment, and higher uninsured rates. Third, passing an abortion ban is associated with an increase in MMRs, though these results are not robust to placebo tests and should be interpreted with caution. While the results are not indicative of a causal treatment effect, it is important to continue monitoring this relationship as additional data become available.

The data from this study are from before Roe v. Wade was overturned by the United States Supreme Court in 2022. The legislative intent to ban abortions may be indicative of a broader political and social environment that is hostile towards sexual and reproductive health. For example, these states may be less likely to expand access to contraception. Of the 15 states that enacted abortion bans between 2018 and 2021, only five have passed bills allowing pharmacists to prescribe self-administered hormonal birth control (A Visual Representation of Pharmacist Prescribing, 2024). The top eight states for MMRs (Arkansas, Mississippi, Tennessee, Alabama, Louisiana, Kentucky, Georgia, and South Carolina) do not have legal protections for the right to contraception (Mabel Felix et al., 2024). All eight of those states stress abstinence-only sex education, if provided, but only Mississippi, Tennessee, Kentucky, Georgia, and South Carolina mandate all schools offer sex education (Sex and HIV Education, 2023). This suggests that states that prioritize banning abortions also enact other policies that demonstrate hostility towards sexual and reproductive health. Decreased access to contraception, sex education, and prenatal care may contribute to worse maternal health and mortality.

In the wake of the Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization access to reproductive and maternal care is increasingly sparse (Baden & Driver, 2023). From 2020 to 2022, there was a five percent increase in counties with low to no access to maternity care, affecting up to 6.9 million women across the U.S. (Brigance et al., 2022). Shortages in maternal care access will likely continue to worsen post-Dobbs. Sixty-three percent of OBGYNs said they would be unlikely to consider a position in a restrictive state and 58% of medical students said they are unlikely to apply to residency programs in a state with abortion restrictions (Jarvis, 2023; Traub et al., 2023). These shortages have devastating consequences. A study in Louisiana found that residing in a maternity care desert is associated with a 91% increase in the risk of a pregnancy-related death compared to parishes with greater access to maternity care (Wallace et al., 2021).

Additionally, COVID-19 may explain the increase in maternal mortality observed in 2020 and 2021. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) found that COVID-19 contributed to 25% of maternal deaths in 2020 and 2021. Of the 861 maternal deaths in 2020, GAO found that 102 were related to COVID-19, and of the 1178 maternal deaths in 2021, 401 were related to COVID-19. The COVID-19 epidemic, along with restrictions to abortion access, exacerbated the barriers to quality and consistent care (United States Government Accountability Office, 2022).

Study limitations

This study is limited by data availability and reliability. CDC suppresses data with less than ten observations. Spatial analysis shows how such data suppression disproportionately biases smaller, more rural populations and leads to underreporting (Tiwari et al., 2014). Data are less likely to be suppressed when populations are larger, when periods are longer, or when geographic locations are more encompassing. As such, analysis by state and by year is more difficult for smaller or less populated states like Rhode Island or Idaho and led to missing data in this analysis. To account for this limitation, this study imputed data on maternal mortality and analyzed the imputed data at its midpoint and its upper and lower bounds. Imputation allowed for all states to remain in the sample, though may limit the precision of the estimates.

Maternal mortality data is also limited by recent changes in coding and publication. The NVSS did not publish official U.S. MMRs between 2007 and 2018 because of a new coding method (Declercq & Zephyrin, 2020). In 2003, the pregnancy status checkbox was introduced on U.S. Standard Certificates of Death. The implementation of this change was incremental and was not adopted by all fifty states until 2018 (Hoyert & Miniño, 2020). The pregnancy checkbox has been the subject of scrutiny (Catalano et al., 2020; Joseph et al., 2024), however, this paper trusts the official reports from the CDC.

Maternal mortality data is further limited by the absence of a national collection system. National estimates are based on local and state reports and rely on the quality and accuracy of local collection. Localities with fewer resources are more subject to gaps in quality control and reporting gaps. These limitations challenge the ability to comprehensively understand the rural and urban disparity in maternal mortality (Declercq & Zephyrin, 2020).

The present study employed a study design that bifurcated states based on if there was an 8-week or less abortion ban. The reduction of state legislation to a binary variable does not capture varying levels of restrictions across states, which may lead current analyses to overlook important legislative nuances. It is important to consider that the relationship between abortion bans and MMRs may vary based on the level of restriction and future studies should employ a design that differentiates between gestational limits to delineate this relationship.

Additionally, this study controls for state level rates of obesity, but there are additional unobservable factors that may influence MMRs that are not accounted for in the present study due to the lack of availability of state-year data. Future studies should aim to capture potential confounders with state-level data on hospital infrastructure, adherence to evidence-based guidelines, rates of diabetes and heart disease among women of reproductive age, and other factors that may mediate this relationship. Controlling for such characteristics will enhance the reliability and robustness of future studies analyzing the relationship between abortion bans and MMRs. Finally, this study is limited by the sociodemographic disparities between the treatment and control group. States with abortion bans face significantly distinct economic and cultural challenges, including lower incomes, higher rates of poverty, uninsured status, and obesity, and lower levels of educational attainment. The differences between states with and without abortion bans may have influenced the estimation of the effects of abortion bans on MMRs and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Despite the limitations of present study, understanding the relationship between abortion bans and MMRs remains critical and merits continued attention and consideration. While the findings presented are not robust to placebo tests, this study offers a valuable foundation for future inquiry.

Conclusion

Maternal mortality poses a grave threat to birthing people in the United States and disproportionately impacts Black and southern communities. While this study does not provide causal inference between abortion bans and maternal mortality rates, it is important to continue exploring this relationship as more data becomes available post-Dobbs. Policy decisions can have downstream effects on population health. While proving this relationship is often difficult, policymakers should consider potential unintended consequences of policy decisions. Future studies should include qualitative analysis to obtain a more complete picture of how abortion bans may influence maternal mortality.
