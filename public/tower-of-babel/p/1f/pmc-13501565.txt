Adenosine 5'-monophosphate prevents sepsis-associated muscle wasting by activating AMPK and suppressing IL-1β inflammatory cytokines
Authors: Ikumi Yoshihara, Narumi Yoshihara, Kei Hanafusa, Koichiro Sueyoshi, Yuko Ono, Nao Umei, Shin Watanabe, Ken Okamoto, Hiroshi Tanaka, Yutaka Kondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis-associated muscle wasting (SAMW) causes long-term functional decline, even after recovery. Emerging evidence indicates that adenosine 5'-monophosphate (AMP) confers organ-protective effects in response to physiological stress or injury, potentially through the activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signalling pathways. In this study, we investigated the effects of AMP on SAMW to evaluate its efficacy as a therapeutic agent to alleviate SAMW.MethodsIn vivo, a mouse model of cecum ligation and puncture sepsis was established using male C57BL/6 mice, which received intraperitoneal AMP (0.5 mg/g) or saline as a control. In vitro, C2C12 myoblasts and RAW264.7 macrophages were cultured under standard conditions and treated with AMP. Forelimb grip strength, blood and muscle sampling, western blotting, AMP assays, RNA sequencing, ELISA, flow cytometry, real-time PCR, immunohistochemistry, histology, and computed tomography imaging were performed to assess molecular, cellular, and physiological responses. In addition, plasma samples from patients with sepsis were analysed to explore translational relevance.ResultsAMP suppressed sepsis-induced inflammatory cytokine production and improved muscle strength by attenuating mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 activation and modulating AMPK signaling, thereby contributing to the preservation of muscle mass and a reduction in systemic inflammation. In vitro, AMP suppressed LPS- induced IL-1β production in RAW264.7 macrophages and attenuated LPS- or IL-1β-induced myotube atrophy in C2C12 cells and shifted the cells towards a fast-twitch phenotype. Evaluation of clinical samples revealed elevated inflammatory cytokines in patients with sepsis exhibiting muscle wasting.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that AMP effectively mitigates SAMW by activating AMPK and suppressing IL-1β-mediated molecular pathways. These findings highlight the potential of AMP as a novel therapeutic agent for preserving skeletal muscle functionality and morphology in sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40560-026-00901-7.Keywords: Sepsis, Adenosine 5′-monophosphate, Muscle wasting, Inflammatory cytokine

Background

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterised by systemic inflammation induced by a dysregulated host immune response to infection. Globally, sepsis accounts for a higher mortality rate than cancer, with an estimated 48.9 million cases and 11 million deaths (22.5%) reported annually [1]. Although advances in medical care have contributed to a decline in sepsis-related mortality, the overall incidence continues to rise each year [2]. Sepsis-associated muscle wasting (SAMW), one of the prevalent complications following recovery from sepsis, is increasingly recognised as a critical factor influencing patient outcomes [3]. SAMW is closely linked to post-discharge functional recovery and reintegration into society; early identification and intervention are essential for improving patient recovery. Furthermore, with improvements in survival rates among patients with sepsis, the incidence of SAMW has increased, affecting 46–67% of patients and posing a growing clinical challenge [4, 5].

Increased expression of inflammatory cytokines during sepsis is one cause of SAMW. Interleukin 6 (IL-6) promotes skeletal muscle catabolism during sepsis [6, 7]. Inflammatory cytokines activate signalling pathways, such as nuclear factor-κB, toll-like receptor, and Janus kinase/signal transducers and activators of transcription-related pathways [8], and are implicated in muscle wasting. However, the underlying mechanism of SAMW is complex, involving additional contributors, such as oxidative stress, and protein metabolic imbalance [9–11].

Pharmacological activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) has recently gained attention as a potential therapeutic target for sepsis [12]. AMPK acts as a negative regulator of mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signalling by sensing cellular energy depletion. Under energy-deficient conditions such as sepsis, activation of AMPK suppresses mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signalling, which may reduce metabolic stress. Although AMPK does not directly activate mammalian target of rapamycin complex 2 (mTORC2), inhibition of mTORC1 can relieve mTORC1-dependent negative feedback on insulin/PI3K signalling, resulting in relative preservation of Akt–mTORC2 activity. This shift in mTOR signalling balance may promote cell survival and stress tolerance during sepsis [13]. The AMPK activators 5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide (AICAR) and metformin have been studied for their protective effects against sepsis [14]. AICAR has been shown to increase mitochondrial capacity in myotubes by promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and enhancing oxidative metabolisms [15].

One AMPK activator, adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP), has not been investigated for the treatment of sepsis. In hypoxic conditions, AMP administration reduces oxygen consumption and attenuates the progression of organ damage and mitochondrial dysfunction, although its effects on skeletal muscle have not been investigated [16]. Mitochondrial dysfunction is implicated in muscle atrophy via increased intracellular calcium levels and extracellular matrix degradation [17]. However, the relationship between mitochondrial dysfunction induced by AMP and skeletal muscle atrophy remains unclear.

The objective of this study was to elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying SAMW and determine the role of AMP in modulating this process through its effects on cytokine expression profiles, and AMPK activation. We used a murine sepsis model, cultured myoblast and macrophage cells, and human sepsis clinical information to evaluate the mechanistic basis and therapeutic potential of AMP for treating SAMW. We also examined the effects of AMP in sepsis on a genome-wide scale, and then investigated the key signalling pathways in greater depth.

Methods

Cell culture

C2C12 murine myoblast cells (CRL-1772; American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), Virginia, USA) were cultured in low-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; D6046; Sigma-Aldrich, Tokyo, Japan), supplemented with 10% (v/v) foetal bovine serum (FBS; 173012; Sigma-Aldrich, Tokyo, Japan), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (P0781; Sigma-Aldrich, Tokyo, Japan). The cells were incubated in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. Upon reaching 100% confluency, differentiation was induced by switching the culture medium to high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 2% horse serum (26050070; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin for 5 days [18].

RAW264.7 murine macrophage cells (TIB-71; ATCC, Virginia, USA) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C [19].

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Juntendo University (approval number: 2025005) and conducted in accordance with the guiding principles for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals established by the Physiological Society of Japan. Eight-to-ten-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from CLEA JAPAN, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). The mice were housed under a 12:12-h light–dark cycle in a controlled environment with access to food and water ad libitum. One week after arrival, the mice were randomly assigned to the following groups: sham, AMP, cecal ligation and puncture (CLP), and CLP + AMP. In a conventional CLP model [20], many mice died by day 5, and differences in disease severity were observed between the groups (Figure S1A, B). To match severity and increase the rate of survival to sepsis for evaluating muscle weakness, a less severe CLP model was established: the cecum was ligated 5 mm from the top of the cecum and punctured twice using small 27 G needles. Sham surgery was also performed as a control for CLP: the skin and peritoneum were opened and closed without CLP. AMP group was also performed sham surgery.

All mice were injected subcutaneously with buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg, Nisshin Pharmaceuticals, Yamagata, Japan) immediately after CLP or sham surgery. Mice were then closely monitored by the investigators and scored daily using a mouse sepsis score (maximum 28 points) [21]. The scoring system consisted of a five-point rating scale that assessed appearance, level of consciousness, activity, response to stimuli, eyes, respiratory rate, and quality of breathing. If the mouse sepsis score exceeded 15, the mouse was considered moribund and euthanised by cervical dislocation under general anaesthesia. These mice were counted as dead mice and their skeletal muscle was not used in the analyses. All experiments were performed in an unblinded manner. Mice in the AMP and CLP + AMP group received an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of AMP (0.5 mg/g body weight, equivalent to 14.4 μL/g body weight of a 100 mM AMP stock solution dissolved in normal saline; A2252, Sigma-Aldrich, Tokyo, Japan). The sham and CLP groups received an equivalent volume of normal saline. The same amount of AMP or normal saline was injected immediately after CLP and every 24 h until euthanasia based on a previous study [16]. Core body temperature was monitored using a rectal temperature probe (AD-1687; A&D Company, Tokyo, Japan).

Grip strength test

Forelimb grip strength was measured using a grip strength meter (MK-380Si; Muromachi Kikai, Tokyo, Japan). The apparatus consisted of a T-shaped metal bar connected to a force transducer. To assess forelimb grip strength, the experimenter gently held the tail of the mouse and allowed it to grasp the metal bar with its forelimb. Once the mouse grasped the bar, the experimenter slowly pulled the animal backwards by the tail until the grip was released. The device automatically recorded the peak force for each measurement in kilograms (kg) [22]. Forelimb grip strength was measured five times for each mouse. The values from the second to fourth measurements, ordered from greatest to least, were averaged and used for analysis.

Blood, ascites, and tissue sampling

Blood samples were collected 6 h after CLP treatment and lower limb skeletal muscles were collected 5-day post-treatment. Blood was collected by puncturing the heart using a heparin-coated 26 G needle syringe. Dissections were performed on all mouse groups under anaesthesia with 3% sevoflurane using a simple inhalation anaesthesia machine designed for animal experiments. Blood samples were centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and plasma was separated and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Ascites fluid was collected as follows: using a 22 G needle and syringe, 3 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin was gently injected into the abdominal cavity, and the fluid was subsequently withdrawn slowly to retrieve the ascites sample. The lower limb skeletal muscles, including the soleus (SOL), gastrocnemius (GAS), tibialis anterior (TA), and extensor digitorum longus (EDL), were collected. After collection, muscles were weighed, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. Muscle weight was expressed as a ratio to body weight, representing relative muscle weight.

Clinical data of patients with sepsis

This study was approved by the Juntendo University School of Medicine Ethics Committee for Medical Research (approval number: E22-0213). Written informed consent was obtained from either the patient or their legal representative. Participants were adult patients who presented to the intensive care unit (ICU) of Juntendo University Urayasu Hospital with a clinical diagnosis of sepsis or septic shock based on the Sepsis-3 criteria [23]. A flowchart of the study participants is shown in Figure S2. In total, 42 patients with sepsis or septic shock were admitted to the ICU during the study period and consented to the study. Cytokine evaluation was performed on all patients, and clinical characteristics are shown in Figure S2 and Table S1. In addition, 10 patients underwent repetitive computed tomography (CT) scans at the time of ICU admission and within 1 month of admission. When multiple follow-up CT scans were performed within 1 month of admission, the earliest scan was used for analysis. Patients with sepsis with a ≥ 10% reduction in cross-sectional area (CSA) of the psoas muscle were classified as the atrophy ( +) group, whereas those with a < 10% reduction were classified as the atrophy (−) group. Muscle atrophy was defined as a reduction of 10%, based on the threshold required to stratify the final cohort of 10 patients into two equal groups of five.

The relationship between the muscle atrophy rate and expression of inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, IFN-γ, and TNF-α) at admission was evaluated in these 10 patients, with a median age of 78.5 years (interquartile range: 61–84 years). Heparinised blood samples were immediately processed to separate plasma, which was then stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. The CSA of the psoas major muscle was measured on CT axial images at the level of the superior border of the L4 vertebral body. The muscle CSA was calculated by summing the mean values of the left and right muscles [24].

Western blot analyses

Muscle tissue was homogenised using a Biomasher III (893131; Nippi, Tokyo, Japan) in a cell lysate containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The homogenate was treated with RIPA Lysis Buffer (WSE-7420; ATTO, Tokyo, Japan) for 15 min at 4 °C. Afterwards, the sample was sonicated twice for 5 s each and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C [25]. The supernatant was collected and the protein concentration was determined using an advanced spectrophotometer (ND-ONE-W-CP24; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan). For western blot analysis, equal amounts of protein (30–50 μg) were separated on 4–20% polyacrylamide gels (XP04205BOX; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (LC2002; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan) using a wet transfer method [26]. The membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) non-fat dried milk and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the following primary antibodies: P-AMPKα (Thr172 30H9 Rabbit mAb; 1:500; #2535), AMPKα (1:1000; #2532), P-mammalian target of rapamycin (P-mTOR, Ser2448 D9C2 XP® Rabbit mAb; 1:1000; #5536), mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR, 7C10 Rabbit mAb) (1:1000; #2983), and β-actin (1:1000; #4967), all purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Tokyo, Japan). Phosphorylation of mTOR at Ser2448 is commonly used as a marker of mTORC1 activation [27]; therefore, P-mTOR (Ser2448) was used as a surrogate of mTORC1 activity throughout this study. The blots were washed three times in TBS-Tween 20 (0.05% vol/vol; 35624-15, Nacalai Tesque, Tokyo, Japan) and probed with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, #7074, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, US) or anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, #7076, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, US), and anti-mouse IgG (1:400, Fab-specific-peroxidase antibody; A2304, Sigma-Aldrich, Tokyo, Japan). Densitometric analysis of protein bands was performed using Clarity Western ECL Substrate (34580; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan) and the Amersham Imager 600 image analysis software (Cytiva, Global Life Sciences Technologies, Tokyo, Japan). Protein expression was quantified as the densitometric ratio of the target protein to β-actin using ImageJ 1.54f (National Institutes of Health) [28].

AMP assay

Samples used for the AMP assay included mouse plasma, ascites fluid, and muscle tissue (SOL, GAS, TA, and EDL). Owing to the rapid degradation in AMP quality, the assay was performed immediately after sample collection following the protocol outlined on the Promega K.K. website (https://www.promega.jp/products/cell-signaling/signaling-pathway-assays/amp_glo-assay/?catNum=V5011).

RNA sequencing

The GAS muscle samples from the lower limbs of mice were collected 5 days after treatment. The selection of the GAS muscle for RNA sequencing was justified by prior evidence demonstrating its vulnerability to SAMW and by the ability to collect an adequate sample volume [29]. Total RNA was isolated from each sample using the miRNeasy Micro Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA). RNA integrity and quantity were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and RNA 6000 Nano Kit (Agilent Technologies, Inc., CA, USA). Sequencing libraries were constructed from total RNA using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, MA, USA), along with the NEBNext Globin & rRNA Depletion Kit (Human/Mouse/Rat), according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Library quality was evaluated using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation High Sensitivity D1000 (Agilent Technologies, Inc., CA, USA). Pooled libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NextSeq 500 (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) in 76-base-pair single-end reads. Sequencing adaptors, low-quality reads, and bases were trimmed using the Trimmomatic-0.39 tool [30]. Sequence reads were aligned to the Mouse reference genome (mm10) using STAR 2.7.10b [31].

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Human plasma samples were analysed using the Human Inflammatory Cytokine Multiplex ELISA Kit (ARG80929; Arigo Biolaboratories, Hsinchu, Taiwan). For mouse skeletal muscle tissue and culture supernatant of RAW264.7 murine macrophages cells, the Mouse Proinflammatory Cytokine Multiplex ELISA Kit (ARG82842; Arigo Biolaboratories, Hsinchu, Taiwan) was used. Skeletal muscle lysates were prepared as described in the western blotting procedure. Absorbance was measured at 495 nm using a multi-plate reader (ARVO X4, PerkinElmer Japan, Kanagawa, Japan). Cytokine concentrations in muscle tissue were normalised to protein concentrations.

Flow cytometric analyses

To assess IL-1β and F4/80 expression levels on the plasma membrane of RAW264.7 macrophages, cells were first treated with PBS containing 1 mM EDTA, followed by treatment with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) containing trypsin and scraping from tissue culture dishes. Aliquots (80 µL) containing 1 × 105 RAW264.7 macrophages were transferred to Eppendorf tubes. The cells were then incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in a mixture containing phycoerythrin (PE)-labelled F4/80 (1:25; 12-4801-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan) and allophycocyanin (APC)-labelled IL-1β (1:25; 17-7114-80, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan). Isotype controls included APC-labelled Rat IgG1 kappa Isotype Control (eBRG1) (1:25; 17-4301-82, eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA) and PE-labelled Rat IgG2a kappa Isotype Control (eBR2a) (1:25; 17-4321-81, eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA). Subsequently, cells were exposed to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (1–100 μg/mL), AMP (1–10 mM), or their respective combinations, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 min. The drug additions are listed in Table 1. In the inhibitor groups, compound C (CompC: 20 μM; 171261, Sigma-Aldrich) [32, 33], an AMPK inhibitor, and rapamycin (Rapa: 100 nM; R8781, Sigma-Aldrich) [34], an mTORC1 inhibitor, were added and incubated in a warm bath at 37 °C for 1 min before the addition of AMP and LPS. Afterwards, the cells were incubated on ice for 10 min and centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was discarded. Cells were then fixed with 300 μL of CellFIX (340181, BD Biosciences, Tokyo, Japan). All samples were subjected to flow cytometric analysis (LSRFortessa X-20, BD Biosciences) and data were analysed using FlowJo v10 (BD Biosciences).

Immunohistochemistry

C2C12 cells (1 × 105 cells) were seeded onto poly-l-lysine-coated glass-bottom dishes (3910-035; IWAKI) and incubated at 37 °C in a differentiation medium for 96 h. The cells were transferred to a differentiation medium alone, with LPS (1 μg/mL), or with LPS and AMP (10 mM) for 48 h. Following incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde (163-20145; Wako, Tokyo, Japan), washed using cold PBS, and incubated in a blocking solution containing 2% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (018-15154; Wako), 5% (v/v) normal goat serum (555-76251; Wako), and 0.3% Triton X-100 (T9284; Sigma-Aldrich). The fixed cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in a blocking solution containing anti-MyHC antibody (1:500; 14-6503, Affymetrix, CA, USA). After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:400; A-11029; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan) and Monomeric Cyanine Nucleic Acid Stains (1:10000; T3605; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan) for 40 min [18]. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Histology

Muscle tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and allowed to infiltrate overnight. The samples were then embedded in paraffin using an automated tissue processor (TP1020, Leica) and a tissue embedding system (OSK 97LF202-CS, Ogawa Seiki, Tokyo, Japan). The embedded tissues were sectioned into 3-μm-thick slices using a medical microtome (Retratome REM-710, Daiwa Koki Industries, Tokyo, Japan) and mounted on silane-coated glass slides. Sections were deparaffinised, rehydrated, and stained with haematoxylin–eosin (HE) [35]. Fibre CSA was measured by tracing the outline of individual muscle fibres. The mean fibre type percentage and CSA were determined by analysing approximately 200 adjacent fibres located in the central region of each muscle [36].

Cell culture and in vitro cytotoxicity assay

To evaluate the potential cytotoxic effects of AMP, we assessed the proportion of cell death across a range of AMP concentrations. C2C12 myoblasts (1 × 105 cells) were seeded onto poly-l-lysine-coated culture dishes and cultured in growth medium for 2 days. The medium was then replaced with differentiation medium, and the cells were cultured for an additional 3 days to induce myotube formation. Subsequently, the cells were treated with differentiation medium containing AMP (0.01, 0.1, 1, 5, or 10 mM) for 2 days. After treatment, cells were harvested, and dead cells were quantified using a cell counter in combination with culture slides, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell viability was calculated based on the proportion of dead cells relative to the total cell number.

Statistical analysis

Because the magnitude of the effect of AMP was unknown, the effect size could not be estimated, and therefore, an a priori sample size calculation was not performed. We assessed the distribution of the data for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data were analysed using one- or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), except for body temperature, body weight, and grip strength. Body temperature, body weight, and grip strength were repeatedly measured over time in the same animals and were analysed using a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. Animals that died during the experiment were excluded from longitudinal analyses, and only animals with complete data were included. To control for multiple comparisons, p values were adjusted using Tukey’s honestly significant difference post hoc test, other than human data. An unpaired t test was used to analyse human data. Survival rate was analysed using the Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad software, San Diego, CA, USA). A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Regarding analyses of RNA sequence, downstream analyses were performed using StrandNGS 4.0 (Agilent Technologies, Inc., CA, USA). Gene and transcript expression (RefSeq Database 2018.02.25) was quantified using the Transcripts Per Million method [37, 38]. Statistical analysis between the ‘sample’ and ‘control’ groups was performed using a moderated t test [39]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with a p value of < 0.05 and fold change (FC) of > 1.5 were considered. To investigate gene expression differences and the relevant annotation among the three groups, genes with a ≥ 1.5-fold change were selected and Gene Ontology (GO) terms [40] and pathway analyses were conducted using mouse gene information data (Mm-Std_20060628.gdb) and mouse pathway data (Mm_Contributed_20070917) from the Gene Map Annotator and Pathway Profile (GenMAPP) and MAPPFinder (http://www.genmapp.org), respectively [41]. MAPPFinder analysis was performed by focusing on expression changes, with increased expression marked in red and decreased expression in green. Functional enrichment of DEGs (FC > 1.5) was performed using Metascape [42], emphasising GO biological processes and human gene-disease association datasets. Heatmap classification and K-means clustering were conducted using integrated differential expression and pathway analysis [43].

Results

RNA expression changes in gastrocnemius muscle during sepsis

A comprehensive gene expression analysis revealed differences in the altered genes among the CLP, CLP + AMP, and sham groups (Fig. 1A). In particular, prominent changes were observed in Cluster C when the genes were classified into four clusters (Cluster A [n = 283], Cluster B [n = 1059], Cluster C [n = 234], and Cluster D [n = 414]) using the K-means method (Fig. 1A). The genes in Cluster C were enriched in pathways associated with ‘response to cytokine’ and ‘response to bacterium’, highlighting cytokine-mediated signalling pathways as a key area of interest (Fig. 1B). Among these, IL-1 signalling, previously implicated in muscle atrophy [44], was elevated in septic muscle and substantially reduced with AMP treatment (Fig. 1C). GO analysis demonstrated a shift from dominant ‘inflammatory response’ pathways in the CLP group to enhanced ‘regulation of muscle adaptation’ in AMP-treated mice (Fig. 1D). These findings suggest that AMP exerts a regulatory effect on muscle tissue by suppressing inflammation-related gene expression changes at the transcriptional level. Confirmation of caspase gene expression revealed that caspase-1 and caspase-4, which are involved in pyroptosis, increased with CLP (Fig. 1E). Nfkb1, Nfkb2, and SOD1 were significantly upregulated in the CLP model; however, their expression levels were not further modulated by AMP administration (Fig. 1F, G). The expression levels of Rps6kb1 and Rps6kb2, which encode S6K, and Eif4ebp1, which encodes 4E-BP1, did not differ among the groups (data not shown).

AMP induces a hypometabolic state and improves muscle function and inflammation in septic mice

To determine the physiological effects of AMP in sepsis, we assessed whether AMP administration induces a hypometabolic state and improves sepsis-induced muscle wasting and systemic inflammation. In AMP-treated mice, core body temperature dropped rapidly, to 27.3 ± 1.7 °C (sham vs. CLP; p < 0.05, sham vs. CLP + AMP; p < 0.001, CLP vs. CLP + AMP; p < 0.05) 15 min after AMP injection (Fig. 2A), but gradually returned to baseline over 6 h. As reported previously [45], after their body temperature recovered, the mice became fully active and exhibited normal movement, feeding, and drinking behaviours, with no side effects observed. Furthermore, body temperature was measured every 24 h after AMP administration, but no significant difference was observed compared to the sham group on any of the 5 days (Fig. 2B). No significant differences in the body weight of the mice in the CLP and CLP + AMP groups were observed during the experimental period (Fig. 2C). We conducted a survival analysis and objective scoring of sepsis severity over time. Over the 5-day experimental period, neither the survival rate (CLP group: 17/19 [89.4%], CLP + AMP group: 18/19 [94.7%], p = 0.36) nor mean sepsis score (CLP group: 1.5 ± 0.9 points, CLP + AMP group: 0.7 ± 0.2 points, p = 0.83) was significantly affected by the addition of AMP (Figure S1C, D), demonstrating comparable survival rates and sepsis severity between groups. Because the two groups had similar backgrounds, we performed a grip strength test, which revealed that muscle strength declined from day 1 in the CLP group and continued to decrease through day 5. In contrast, the CLP + AMP group showed recovery beginning on day 3, reaching levels comparable to the sham group by day 5 (Fig. 2D). Histological analysis supported this functional improvement. The CLP group displayed reduced CSA and improved by the administration of AMP (Fig. 2E) and muscle weight per unit weight was decreased by CLP only (Fig. 2F) in the TA muscle. In line with earlier reports [11, 46], we also observed morphological and functional decline in muscle mass and strength following CLP induction. To investigate whether this decline was fibre-type specific, we examined differences among muscle groups. The reduction in CSA in fast-twitch-dominant TA, but not in slow-twitch-dominant SOL, supports fibre-type-specific susceptibility in SAMW, as no significant changes were observed in slow-twitch-dominant SOL muscle (Figure S3). Measurements of plasma cytokine levels revealed that IL-6 and IL-1β levels were significantly elevated in the CLP group but effectively reduced by AMP treatment (Fig. 2G–J).

AMP suppresses mTOR activation in skeletal muscle

At the protein level, sepsis-induced inflammation triggers the aberrant activation of mTOR, a key regulator of cell growth and metabolism [47]. In the present study, we observed an increase in phosphorylated mTOR (P-mTOR) in the skeletal muscle of CLP-treated mice, indicating inflammatory mTORC1 activation under septic conditions (Fig. 3A). AMP administration effectively suppressed mTORC1 activation in the skeletal muscle. Western blot analysis revealed that the suppressive effect of AMP was observed consistently across multiple muscle groups (Fig. 3B–E). These results suggest that AMP modulates metabolic signalling pathways by attenuating mTORC1 activation, which may contribute to the preservation of muscle mass and function during sepsis. Furthermore, when we measured the gene expression of NLRP3 and caspase-1 in the GAS and TA muscles, NLRP3 expression increased in CLP and was suppressed by AMP (Fig. 3F and G). Caspase-1 expression significantly increased in the GAS and TA muscles in CLP. AMP administration also suppressed caspase-1 expression (Fig. 3H and I).

Inflammatory cytokine levels are elevated in patients with sepsis accompanied by muscle wasting

In vivo results prompted us to investigate the associations among proinflammatory cytokine levels, sepsis severity, and SAMW in human patients. Multiplex ELISA revealed higher plasma levels of IL-6 (p < 0.05), IL-1β (p = 0.061), IFN-γ (p = 0.36), and TNF-α (p = 0.24) in the atrophy ( +) group than in the atrophy (−) group (Fig. 4A–D). These findings suggest an association between elevated systemic inflammation and muscle wasting in sepsis, aligning with our murine model findings.

Macrophages are a source of IL-1β and are regulated by AMP via AMPK activation

Inflammatory cytokine levels were elevated in the blood of CLP-treated septic mice; however, their expression was not detectable in the skeletal muscle tissue (Figure S4A–P), supporting the conclusion that skeletal muscle is not the primary source of these cytokines. Given the concurrent decrease in skeletal muscle weight and CSA, we hypothesised that the cytokines contributing to muscle atrophy might originate from cells outside the muscle tissue.

To test this hypothesis, we performed in vitro experiments using RAW264.7 murine macrophages. The RAW264.7 cells were labelled with IL-1β-APC and F4/80-PE antibodies, and F4/80 + cells were defined as macrophages. Subsequently, an isotype control antibody was used to confirm the absence of nonspecific binding. The proportion of F4/80 + /IL-1β + was calculated (Fig. 5A). IL-1β was upregulated in response to LPS in a concentration-dependent manner and dose-dependently suppressed by AMP (Fig. 5B).

To elucidate the underlying signalling pathway, the cells were co-treated with CompC, an AMPK inhibitor, and Rapa, an mTORC1 inhibitor. Both inhibitors reversed the AMP-induced suppression of IL-1β (Fig. 5C and D), indicating that AMPK activation is a key mechanism regulating the anti-inflammatory effect of AMP. These findings suggest that macrophages are a source of circulating IL-1β in sepsis and that AMP exerts anti-inflammatory effects.

AMP ameliorates LPS-induced myotube atrophy and alters fibre-type composition in C2C12 cells

LPS is a potent inducer of inflammatory cytokine expression in mouse C2C12 myoblasts and myotubes and directly promotes myotube atrophy independent of immune cell involvement [18]. To determine whether AMP mitigates LPS-induced muscle protein loss and atrophy, we evaluated its effects on mature myotubes exposed to LPS or IL-1β. Treatment with LPS (1 μg/mL) or IL-1β (1 ng/mL) for 48 h significantly reduced myotube diameter, area and number, as revealed by myosin heavy chain (MyHC) immunofluorescence (Fig. 6A–D). Co-treatment with AMP (10 mM) effectively reversed these atrophic changes, indicating that AMP suppresses inflammatory cytokine-induced myotube degradation.

To characterise the observed morphological improvements, we investigated the effect of AMP on muscle fibre-type distribution in C2C12 cells. As the baseline ratio of muscle fibre types in C2C12 cells has not been well-established, we quantified the proportions of slow-twitch (MyHC I) and fast-twitch (MyHC II) fibres under control conditions, which showed that the myotube comprised 30% MyHC I and 70% MyHC II. Conversely, AMP treatment for 48 h altered this distribution, reducing MyHC I to 16% and increasing MyHC II to 84% (Fig. 6E and F). These findings suggest that AMP promotes a shift towards a fast-twitch fibre phenotype.

AMP transiently activates AMPK, suppresses mTORC1, and suppresses the expression of muscle atrophy-related genes

To delineate the signalling dynamics triggered by AMP, we assessed the temporal activation of AMPK/mTOR following AMP administration in skeletal muscles. Western blot analysis revealed that AMPK activation was rapidly induced, peaking 1 min after AMP addition, and returning to baseline within 5 min (Fig. 7A and B). Conversely, mTORC1 activation increased transiently after AMPK deactivation, peaked at 5 min, likely as a compensatory response, and then returned to a suppressed state within 15 min (Fig. 7A and C).

To evaluate downstream effects on the expression of muscle atrophy-related genes, we measured the expression of Fbx32 mRNA 48 h after AMP treatment using real-time PCR. AMP significantly reduced Fbx32 expression (Fig. 7D), indicating that even transient AMPK activation suppresses genes involved in muscle atrophy.

Cytotoxicity assessment of AMP in differentiated C2C12 cells

Treatment with AMP at 0.01, 0.1, 1, 5, and 10 mM resulted in 2.51%, 1.45%, 7.37%, 10.71%, and 14.2% C2C12 cell death, respectively (Figure S5). Based on ISO 10993-5, materials that maintain cell viability at 70% or higher are considered non-cytotoxic. Accordingly, AMP was considered to exhibit low cytotoxicity [48].

Discussion

This study demonstrates that AMP treatment suppresses IL-1β expression in the plasma of CLP-treated mice and LPS-stimulated C2C12 myotubes and improves skeletal muscle wasting in in vivo and in vitro models. These findings indicate that IL-1β plays a key role in SAMW, contributing to the loss of muscle mass and strength. Furthermore, the suppression of IL-1β expression in macrophages during sepsis reduces skeletal muscle loss, suggesting a therapeutic strategy against skeletal muscle wasting caused by sepsis and subsequent systemic inflammation.

The pathogenesis of SAMW remains incompletely understood; however, this study demonstrated that IL-1β secreted by activated macrophages facilitates the progression of muscle wasting through paracrine inflammatory signalling, although it does not appear to directly translocate to or accumulate within myofibres. Moreover, circulating cytokines are not released from muscles and are of extra-muscular origin. This hypothesis is supported by the lack of IL-1β and other inflammatory cytokines in the skeletal muscle following CLP administration. Programmed cell death is thought to play a major role in this mechanism. Pyroptosis, a significant contributor to muscle atrophy, is triggered by the release of cytochrome c from mitochondria, which activates caspases [49]. In particular, pyroptosis is triggered in macrophages by pathogen- or damage-associated molecular patterns. NLRP3 inflammasome activation leads to the activation of caspase-1, cleavage of gasdermin D, and release of IL-1β through membrane pores, thereby amplifying systemic inflammation. This cytokine surge may promote pyroptosis in skeletal muscle through ATP depletion, initiated via pannexin-1 channels activated by caspase-1 [50]. In this study, we observed increased expression of NLRP3, caspase-1, and caspase-4 in muscle tissue following sepsis, suggesting the involvement of pyroptosis in sepsis-associated muscle pathology. In addition, pathway analysis using RNA sequencing revealed that AMP treatment suppressed caspase-1 and -4 expression and decreased apoptosis-related gene expression in fast-twitch fibre-dominant muscles, such as GAS and TA muscles. Although the present study did not demonstrate AMP-induced changes in NF-κB signalling or ROS-related pathways in sepsis, further investigations are warranted to clarify these mechanisms.

In this study, we focused specifically on IL-1β, because this cytokine is tightly regulated by inflammasome activation and caspase-1-dependent processing, mechanisms that were strongly upregulated in RNA sequence analyses. IL-1β directly reflects NLRP3–caspase-1 signaling and is a key upstream amplifier of pyroptosis-associated inflammation [51]. Thus, IL-1β was selected as the most mechanistically relevant cytokine to evaluate the macrophage-mediated inflammatory pathway targeted by AMP.

Inflammatory cytokines other than IL-1β are also implicated in muscle wasting. In sepsis, elevated levels of IL-6 promote skeletal muscle atrophy by activating the gp130/JAK2/STAT3 signalling pathway, thereby enhancing muscle protein degradation [7]. In addition, TNF-α and IFN-γ have been reported to promote muscle atrophy independently of, or in cooperation with, IL-6 through activation of NF-κB and STAT3 signalling pathways, leading to enhanced muscle protein degradation [52]. Although this study focused on IL-1β and clarified its mechanistic involvement in SAMW, the potential contributions of other cytokines remain to be explored in future studies. While IL-1β has been reported to be elevated in sarcopenia [53], its contribution to age-related muscle loss appears to be relatively limited. Unlike sepsis, which is characterised by acute and robust inflammatory responses, sarcopenia is associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, in which IL-1β is unlikely to serve as a primary mechanistic driver [54].

The AMP was administered to mice and induced hypothermia, creating a hypometabolic state. Since the hypothermia induced by AMP administration was transient, it is unlikely that the protective effects of AMP on skeletal muscle atrophy are mediated by temperature-dependent mechanisms. Daily body temperature measurements indicated that CLP and CLP + AMP mice showed similar recovery patterns throughout the experimental period, and the degree of hypothermia did not appear to correlate with changes in muscle fibre size or muscle strength. Furthermore, AMP-only control mice did not exhibit sustained hypothermia. These findings suggest that the beneficial effects of AMP on skeletal muscle may be mediated by mechanisms other than hypothermia. Although these findings suggest that mechanisms other than hypothermia contribute to the beneficial effects of AMP, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that AMP-induced hypothermia contributed, at least in part, to the observed protective effects. Under normal physiological conditions, the intracellular ATP level is 100-fold higher than the AMP level, reflecting a high-energy state [55]. However, during stress conditions, such as sepsis or energy deprivation, intracellular ATP levels decrease and AMP levels increase. This change triggers AMPK and other energy-sensing pathways [56, 57]. Beyond its intracellular role, AMP may act extracellularly by activating purinergic receptors, particularly the P2X and P2Y families, which modulate inflammatory responses and cell death pathways. In this study, we added extracellular AMP, which activated AMPK in muscle cells. In addition, mTORC1 activity was markedly elevated in skeletal muscle during sepsis, whereas AMP administration suppressed this activation. mTORC1 is known to be negatively regulated by AMPK activation, and excessive mTOR signalling has been implicated in organ dysfunction in sepsis. A previous report has shown that mTORC1 inhibition protects against sepsis-induced cognitive impairment [58]. Thus, the suppression of mTORC1 signalling observed in this study may represent an additional mechanism by which AMP preserves muscle integrity under septic conditions. Although AMP administration reduced mTORC1 activation, we did not detect corresponding changes in the downstream mTORC1 targets S6K and 4E-BP1. The activation of mTORC1 may have been transient or subject to feedback regulation.

Previous studies reported that sepsis preferentially induces suppression of protein synthesis and atrophy in fast-twitch muscle fibres [59, 60], which is consistent with our present findings. Muscle disuse atrophy is caused by muscle immobility and characterised by atrophied slow-twitch muscle fibres (MyHC I) [36]. In contrast, fast-twitch muscle fibres (MyHC II) were predominantly atrophied in this study. This pattern could be attributed to an increase in inflammatory cytokine levels in the blood, which promote increased immune cell infiltration into fast-twitch skeletal muscles, reduced muscle mass, and increased ectopic fat accumulation, impairing muscle strength and function [61]. The selective vulnerability of fast-twitch fibres may be explained by an imbalance between the supply and demand of ATP. Sepsis activates immune responses that elevate energy requirements and, simultaneously, impairs ATP production via oxidative phosphorylation owing to mitochondrial dysfunction [62–64], resulting in a compensatory increase in ATP production by the glycolytic system [65]. Thus, as fast-twitch muscle fibres primarily rely on glycolytic metabolism, they are more susceptible to energy deficits than slow-twitch muscle owing to selective glucose loss. Moreover, disuse-induced muscle atrophy involves distinct mechanisms from SAMW [66], with less involvement of inflammatory cytokines [67, 68]. Based on these mechanisms, we speculated that metabolic stress likely contributes to the preferential atrophy of fast-twitch fibres observed during sepsis. Therefore, the AMP-induced fast-twitch shift observed in SAMW may be protective rather than deleterious, although further studies are needed to fully elucidate its biological relevance.

Based on our findings, we propose two mechanisms by which AMP administration ameliorates muscle wasting (Fig. 8). First, AMP may promote morphological improvements in muscle in association with AMPK activation. AMPK activation was associated with reduced expression of muscle atrophy-related genes and improved muscle phenotypes, including greater muscle strength, larger muscle fibre size, and altered fibre-type composition. Second, AMP exerts anti-inflammatory effects due to the suppression of IL-1β release from macrophages during sepsis, thereby reducing skeletal muscle loss. However, although the anti-inflammatory effects of AMP may have a limited impact, our findings demonstrate that both mechanisms contribute to suppressing the progression of SAMW.

SAMW Mechanisms and Improvements with AMP. Based on the experimental findings of the present study, a potential mechanism underlying the suppression of sepsis-associated muscle wasting by AMP may involve intracellular activation of AMPK and attenuation of IL-1β release from macrophages. In this study, we demonstrated in C2C12 myoblasts that AMP activates AMPK, and reduces the expression of Fbx32. In RAW264.7 macrophages, we showed that AMP suppresses IL-1β release. In a sepsis animal model, AMP suppressed mTORC1 activity, reduced Fbx32 expression, increased muscle fiber area, and improved grip strength in skeletal muscle. However, we did not investigate whether AMP is taken up into muscle cells, nor whether the IL-1β responsible for inducing muscle atrophy is derived from macrophages. Based on these findings, we propose a potential mechanism underlying SAMW

This study has several limitations. First, the in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted using murine models; thus, whether these findings translate to humans remains uncertain. Second, although clinical data from patients with sepsis demonstrated a positive association between elevated inflammatory cytokine levels and the degree of muscle atrophy, the analysis was limited to the psoas muscle and the timing of CT imaging was not standardised. Moreover, the number of included human cases was limited, which reduces statistical power. In particular, IL-1β did not reach statistical significance due to the small sample size. Owing to this limited sample size, we could not perform multivariable analyses and correlation analyses; therefore, any clinical inferences based on these findings should be interpreted with caution. Accordingly, the human data presented in this study should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Further investigations in larger cohorts of patients with sepsis are warranted to validate these findings and clarify their clinical relevance. Third, the present findings are based primarily on experiments conducted in mice, and their direct applicability to humans is, therefore, limited. In particular, issues remain regarding the clinical feasibility and safety of inducing hypothermia in humans, and its translational applicability has not yet been established. Given the potential risks associated with hypothermia, careful evaluation of its safety, feasibility, and therapeutic benefit is required before clinical application. While some hibernating mammals are capable of entering a hypothermic state, humans, as non-hibernating mammals, are less susceptible to hypothermia [69]. Fourth, the present findings were derived from a low-severity CLP model. Given that muscle atrophy becomes more pronounced as disease severity increases, this approach was adopted to better delineate whether AMP ameliorates muscle atrophy indirectly via improvements in survival or exerts a direct protective effect on muscle wasting. Nevertheless, this study design limits the generalisability of the findings, and the efficacy of AMP in more severe forms of sepsis needs to be investigated in future studies. With regard to the severe-CLP data, the analyses were exploratory and limited by the small sample size, and should, therefore, be interpreted as supportive observations rather than definitive evidence, without generalisation to all severities of sepsis. Fifth, because skeletal muscle evaluation required tissue collection at the predetermined study endpoint, only animals that survived until that timepoint could be included in the analyses. This may have introduced selection bias and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, because AMP is a readily metabolized compound, the effects attributable to its metabolites remain unclear. Although AMP-induced hypothermia was first reported in 2006 [45], the contribution of its metabolites has remained unclear since that initial report and was also not elucidated in the present study.

Conclusions

We demonstrated that the administration of AMP effectively ameliorated muscle atrophy by suppressing IL-1β expression in macrophages. These findings highlight AMP as a therapeutic candidate for preserving skeletal muscle integrity in sepsis. Further studies are warranted to clarify the broader role of AMP in muscle atrophy, including its potential applicability to other muscle atrophic conditions, as well as to confirm its efficacy in more severe models of sepsis, given that the present study was restricted to a moderate sepsis model.
