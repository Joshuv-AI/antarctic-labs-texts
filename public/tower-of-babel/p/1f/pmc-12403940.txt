Baseline ocular biometrics in children with orthokeratology-induced axial length reduction or elongation
Authors: Xin Ai, Xuehui Zhang, Ruoxin Wang, Yanhong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo determine the baseline ocular biometrics in children with axial length reduction after orthokeratology for better myopia control.MethodsChanges in ocular parameters in 164 subjects who completed one year of Ortho-K treatment were retrospectively reviewed. The subjects were divided into AL reduction (n = 105, ≤ 0 mm/year axial growth) and AL elongation (n = 59, ≥ 0.4 mm/year axial growth) groups based on 1-year axial length changes.ResultsCompared with those in the AL elongation group, children in the AL reduction group were older (p < 0.001), had a higher degree of myopia(p < 0.001), larger pupil diameter (p < 0.001), deeper anterior chamber depth (p = 0.007), and lower corneal endothelial density (p < 0.001). In the AL reduction group, the reduction was negatively correlated with WTW and CCT changes (ρ=−0.346, p < 0.001). Endothelial cell loss in the elongation group was 1.95 times greater than that in the reduction group (44.239 ± 79.629 vs.22.646 ± 79.038 cells/mm²).ConclusionsBaseline age, degree of myopia, pupil diameter, and anterior chamber depth were associated with the efficacy of orthokeratology in controlling axial length. The ΔWTW/ΔCCT ratio may be useful for predicting AL reduction. Regular examination of the corneal endothelium in children with rapid axial length elongation is recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04356-6.Keywords: Myopia, Contact lenses, Orthokeratology, Axial length, Pupil diameter, Central corneal thickness

Introduction

In 2020, 2.6 billion people worldwide had myopia, a number projected to reach 4.7 billion by 2050 [1]. Complications such as glaucoma, myopic macular degeneration, and retinal detachment underscore its severity [2, 3]. By 2050, global myopia prevalence is estimated to hit 50%, with 20% categorized as high myopia [4]. This high burden has driven intense interest in strategies to slow myopia progression, among which orthokeratology (Ortho-K) has emerged as an effective option that induces peripheral myopic defocus [5].

Orthokeratology lenses, overnight-worn rigid gas-permeable contact lenses, reshape corneal morphology to correct refractive errors and slow pediatric myopia progression via the defocus theory [6]. Its myopia-controlling efficacy is most pronounced in the initial months of wear [7, 8]. Axial length is a core metric for ocular development and serves as a critical objective marker for myopia prediction and progression assessment [9, 10]. Previous studies have explored changes in the refractive, biometric, and corneal topographic parameters during Ortho-K. For example, Queirós et al. found that corneal parameters stabilized within one week of Ortho-K lens wear, while axial length (AL) changes were negligible in children over 11 years of age or with baseline myopia > 4.00 D. They also identified age and baseline myopia as key predictors of accelerated axial elongation in the study. However, most existing studies have focused on the overall changes in Ortho-K populations, with limited attention to subgroups with distinct AL trajectories. Additionally, data on corneal endothelial changes and their association with AL changes are scarce. A growth rate < 0.2 mm/year of axial length is the safety threshold for 6–12-year-olds [11], while ≥ 0.4 mm/year after 6 months of optical intervention defines excessive elongation [12]. Thus, this study aimed to identify baseline ocular biometric characteristics associated with AL reduction versus elongation after 1-year of Ortho-K treatment, explore dynamic changes in these parameters that may underlie the two AL trajectories, and clarify whether age, baseline myopia, and corneal parameters play consistent roles in AL progression. This study classified children into an AL reduction group (n = 105, ≤ 0 mm/year growth) and an AL elongation group (n = 59, ≥ 0.4 mm/year growth) based on 1-year axial length changes.

Methods

This retrospective study included 164 children (164 right eyes) who visited the Optometry and Vision Center of Xi’an First Hospital for orthokeratology lens fitting between January 2020 and January 2021, with a 1-year follow-up completed by January 2022. Subjects aged 8–17 years had a spherical refractive error of less than − 6.00 diopters(D), with-the-rule astigmatism of ≤ − 1.50 D (excluding other types of astigmatism), and a best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of ≥ 0.0 as measured by the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (log MAR) and had no influencing factors such as active ocular inflammation, corneal abnormalities, or systemic diseases. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xi’an First Hospital (for the Shaanxi Provincial Key R&D Program, Project No. 2023-YBSF-070) and followed the principles of the Helsinki Declaration.

All children’s baseline data before orthokeratology lens fitting were recorded, including age at baseline, sex, baseline refractive error (spherical equivalent, SE = sphere power + ½ cylinder power), lens power reduction (PR), corneal curvature (K1 and K2 representing horizontal and vertical corneal curvatures, respectively), axial length (AL), anterior chamber depth (ACD), central corneal thickness (CCT), white-to-white corneal diameter (WTW), pupil diameter (PD measured in a darkroom environment), tear break-up time (BUT), corneal endothelial density (ECD), and proportion of hexagonal cells (HCR).

At 6 months and 1 year after orthokeratology lens fitting, the ocular parameters were remeasured using the same measurement equipment and methods. All measurements were performed by the same trained technician following standard operating procedures, with each parameter measured three times, and the average values were recorded.

The children viewed a Snellen chart at 5 m for 10 min before comprehensive refractive and biometric assessments were performed. AL, ACD, and CCT were measured using a LENSTAR 900 optical biometer, and K1, K2, pupil diameter (PD), and WTW were obtained using a TOMEY TMS-4 N corneal topographer. Corneal endothelial density (ECD) and the proportion of hexagonal cells (HCR) were measured using a Topcon SP-3000P specular microscope. AL measurements used ten consecutive readings with > 0.02 mm deviations excluded. Cycloplegic refraction employed 0.5% tropicamide (three doses, 15-minute intervals), with adequacy confirmed by 6 mm pupil dilation. Euclid orthokeratology lenses were fitted according to the standard protocols. PR is determined by the power reduction of the trial lenses, additional spherical power on the lens, and the overcorrection amount. Measurements were performed using an autorefractor (Topcon RM-800). All orthokeratology (Ortho-K) participants were fitted with the same 4-zone design lenses (Euclid Systems Corporation, USA), which featured a spherical base curve (BC) with a standard optical zone diameter of 6.2 mm and were fabricated from gas-permeable materials. Lens selection and fitting adhered to the manufacturer’s protocols: initial trial lenses were chosen based on corneal topographic parameters, refraction, and horizontal visible iris diameter, with base curves specifically determined according to the K1/K2 values. The final fitting lens was confirmed by an optimal dynamic fluorescein pattern, ensuring proper centration, an adequate peripheral vault, and stable tear layer distribution. Follow-up measurements (6 months and 1 year) included AL, ACD, CCT, PD, WTW, BUT, ECD, and HCR values.

All participants were followed up at 6-month and 1-year post-fitting. Temporary disruptions to follow-up occurred in early 2020 because of initial public health restrictions. However, outpatient services and follow-up procedures were resumed in mid-2020. To ensure data completeness during this period, staggered in-person appointments and online care guidance were provided to the participants. Both 6-month and 1-year follow-up data were collected during the normalization phase of the study.

Statistics: SPSS 22.0 (right eye data). Normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the chi-square test was used to assess sex differences. Independent t-tests (or Welch’s test for heterogeneous variance) and Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare group baselines. Paired t-tests were used to evaluate pre- and post-changes, and repeated measures ANOVA was used to assess temporal parameter changes. P < 0.05 denoted significance.

Discussion

The change in axial length (AL) is a key parameter for assessing myopia progression. Previous 2-year studies have shown that AL changes were consistent during the study period in patients wearing orthokeratology (OK) lenses, with incremental increases of 0.09 ± 0.10 mm, 0.11 ± 0.09 mm, and 0.10 ± 0.08 mm in the average 6-month increases, respectively [13]. Zhao et al.’s 3-year study demonstrated that the annual mean AL growth after OK lens fitting was 0.20 ± 0.12 mm, with a 6-month AL change of 0.04 ± 0.12 mm, indicating that 6-month AL changes could predict axial elongation from 6 months to 3 years [14].

In recent years, an increasing number of studies have reported AL reduction after OK lens wear. In children who exhibit AL shortening within 1 month of OK lens fitting, AL gradually lengthens after the initial reduction. Discontinuing OK lens wear leads to AL rebound (significant lengthening), whereas resuming wear results in renewed AL reduction [15]. Earlier observations showed that AL decreased by 0.028 mm after 1 week of OK lens wear [16], consistent with trends reported in prior studies: Lau et al. documented a 0.026 mm AL reduction after 1 week of OK lens wear [17], and Gardener et al. observed a 0.04 mm reduction after 1 month of wear [18].

Collectively, these findings show that orthokeratology not only provides temporary refractive correction but also influences underlying ocular growth patterns [15].

Growing evidence suggests that choroidal thickness may contribute to axial length reduction [19, 20]. A prospective study showed that subfoveal choroidal thickness increased by 16 μm from baseline after 1 month of OK lens wear, with sustained thickening at 6 and 12 months [15, 21]. A meta-analysis including 239 myopic children demonstrated a significant increase in subfoveal choroidal thickness after 6 months of OK lens wear [22]. In addition to choroidal changes, lens thickness has also been studied: one study observed a 32-µm increase in lens thickness after 1 week of OK lens wear [17], while another 2-year study found a ~ 10-µm increase in lens thickness after 2 years of wear [23].

This study evaluated the baseline biometric parameter characteristics of myopic children with significant axial length reduction (AL reduction group) and rapid progression (AL elongation group) during overnight orthokeratology lens wear. The results showed that the AL reduction group had a significantly older baseline age, more severe myopia, longer axial length, deeper anterior chamber depth, larger pupil diameter, and lower endothelial cell density. Similar conclusions have been reported in the literature, such as lower baseline spherical equivalent myopia, higher parental myopia degree, and younger age at lens fitting being associated with faster axial elongation. Children with higher baseline myopia and older age showed relatively less AL growth after wearing orthokeratology lenses [24]. In children aged 8–11 years, those with a longer baseline axial length (≥ 24.5 mm) exhibited slower AL elongation during the first 2 years of orthokeratology treatment [25]. This may be because higher degrees of myopia lead to a greater reduction in the diopter in the central corneal treatment zone and a greater increase in peripheral corneal refraction, inducing more peripheral retinal myopic defocus. Additionally, our study found that a greater lens power reduction was associated with a smaller AL increment. Whether increasing the peripheral corneal refractive power can achieve more significant therapeutic effects requires further investigation is required. Regarding anterior chamber depth, a previous study with a small sample size found no significant difference in the baseline anterior chamber depth between the AL elongation and reduction groups [20], which may have limitations, possibly due to age differences. The sample age in his study was 19–29 years old.

Another study showed that anterior chamber depth was significantly positively correlated with axial length and negatively correlated with SE, meaning deeper anterior chamber depth in more severe myopia, which explains the deeper anterior chamber depth in the AL reduction group [26]. In terms of pupil diameter, we found that larger baseline pupil diameter was associated with better myopia control, consistent with Guo Y et al.’s research showing that a larger intersection area between the pupil and defocus ring induced more myopic defocus at 30–53° from the fovea, with a greater control effect on AL growth [27]. Age, SE, and pupil area of myopic children significantly influence myopia control outcomes with orthokeratology [28]. Jian et al. [29] observed a significant negative correlation between baseline pupil diameter and axial growth within 1 year, indicating that a larger pupil diameter enhances the effect of orthokeratology lenses in slowing AL growth. The pupils may influence the effective defocus of incident light. Pauné et al. [30] confirmed that the peripheral defocus effect of orthokeratology primarily depends on the spatial matching relationship between the plus power ring diameter (PPRD) and pupil diameter, rather than the absolute value of pupil diameter. Their key finding—that axial growth is slower when the PPRD aligns with the pupil (full-effect group)—emphasizes that “relative matching” (PPRD within the pupil range) is critical, rather than a static pupil diameter.

In the present study, despite the initial differences in pupil diameter, the convergence of values at the end of the year suggests that orthokeratology-induced PPRD likely remained matched with the pupil in both groups. The changes in pupil diameter may be adaptive. In the AL reduction group, an effective orthokeratology intervention would adjust the PPRD to adapt to changes in the pupil diameter, maintaining the peripheral defocus. The control efficacy was enhanced by optimizing the matching between the PPRD and pupil diameter.

This study also found that the AL reduction group had lower baseline endothelial cell density, which may be related to significantly reduced endothelial cell density, increased coefficient of variation, and longer AL in more myopic eyes [31].

After 1 year of follow-up, we found that endothelial cell density (ECD) decreased in both the AL reduction and elongation groups, with the AL elongation group showing an increase in anterior chamber depth and a decrease in corneal diameter. The endothelial cell loss in the elongation group was 1.95 times greater than that in the reduction group (44.239 ± 79.629cells/mm² vs.22.646 ± 79.038 cells/mm²). Chen XH et al. [32] also reported a decline in binocular ECD after 24 months of lens wear, which contradicts the findings of Guo Xi et al.’s [33] 7-year study, possibly due to differences in study design, measurement instruments, or the negative correlation between ECD and age [34]. Our findings of ECD reduction, particularly in eyes with axial elongation, suggest that corneal endothelial monitoring may be prudent in Ortho-K wearers, despite the stability of posterior corneal elevation reported by Queirós et al.[35] Endothelial responses may arise from factors such as fluctuations in intraocular pressure during rapid axial growth. The greater ECD loss in the AL elongation group aligns with their concurrent WTW reduction, indicating that corneal radial tension may transmit axial stress to the endothelium. The greater ECD loss in the AL elongation group, coinciding with rapid axial growth, may reflect indirect mechanical stress on the corneal endothelium, although the direct causal pathways remain unclear.

The ΔWTW/ΔCCT ratio (negative correlation between WTW reduction and CCT thickening, ρ=−0.346, p < 0.001) may be useful for predicting AL reduction. In the axial length (AL) reduction group, the more pronounced thinning of the central corneal thickness (CCT) may also be related to the degree of refractive correction and the design of orthokeratology lenses. For myopic children requiring orthokeratology, higher baseline refractive errors require a greater “power reduction” of the lenses to achieve the target correction effect. This stronger reshaping force is likely to cause more epithelial cells to shift from the central cornea to the peripheral region, resulting in more significant thinning of the central epithelium [36]. As central epithelial thinning becomes more prominent, the peripheral cornea may undergo slight thickening or structural rearrangement, which could limit the radial contraction of the corneal diameter. This pattern—more significant central thinning accompanied by relatively stable WTW—reflects adaptive epithelial remodeling under orthokeratology, where the cornea balances central refractive correction and peripheral structural stability.

As noted by Sung et al. [37], the structural linkage between anterior scleral remodeling, corneal parameters, and axial growth further explains the negative correlation between ΔWTW and ΔCCT in the AL reduction group. Their study demonstrated that in myopic eyes, anterior scleral thickness (AST) is closely associated with axial length (AL) and CCT, and axial elongation is correlated with AST thinning. In our AL reduction group, the concurrent occurrence of axial shortening and significant CCT thinning suggested that a thicker anterior sclera may reduce the tensile force transmitted to the cornea through the limbus, thereby limiting the radial expansion of the cornea. The stabilizing effect of a thicker anterior sclera reduces the need for corneal stromal thickness to counteract biomechanical stress, thus amplifying the inverse relationship between ΔWTW and ΔCCT. Sung et al. observed that corneal thickness (CCT) and anterior scleral stability are biomechanically interdependent. This interdependence is amplified in the AL reduction group, making ΔWTW and ΔCCT likely to respond to common scleral-driven forces, thereby strengthening their negative correlations.

The apex-periphery refractive power difference (ARPD) proposed by Lee et al. [38] is a key parameter after orthokeratology, reflecting the refractive power gradient between the central and peripheral cornea. Studies have found that an increase in ARPD after treatment is significantly correlated with slowed axial growth, and the formation of ARPD relies on a remodeling pattern of “central corneal flattening + relative steepening of the peripheral cornea.” In the AL reduction group of our study, to form sufficient ARPD to correct high refractive errors, the central cornea needed to thin significantly. Meanwhile, the peripheral cornea becomes bulkier due to epithelial accumulation, which may restrict radial expansion or even maintain relative stability, ultimately leading to a negative correlation between ΔWTW and ΔCCT. Further prospective validation is essential to determine the clinical utility of this ratio.

The baseline biometric characteristics of children who showed axial length reduction after wearing orthokeratology lenses were described in our study. Nonetheless, this study has a few limitations that are worth considering.

Critical variables that may independently influence AL changes, such as parental myopia status, daily outdoor exercise duration, and compliance with orthokeratology use, were not well assessed due to the retrospective nature of the study. The lack of data on treatment start time and initiation season precluded the analysis of their potential effects on axial growth in this study. Although all participants completed the 1-year follow-up, we could not evaluate whether the initiation season influenced short-term axial growth. Future studies should collect temporal data to further clarify these relationships. We were unable to quantitatively evaluate key corneal molding parameters such as the treatment zone diameter and eccentricity. We cannot rule out potential variations in corneal molding quality that may confound the axial length changes. Choroidal and lens thicknesses were not evaluated, precluding mechanistic insights into AL reduction/elongation pathways. A more comprehensive analysis of changes in the anterior and posterior segment biometric parameters following orthokeratology (OK) lens wear would enhance the mechanistic understanding of myopia control.

Although the AL elongation group in this study showed more endothelial loss, the ECD reduction may be due to a variety of factors (mechanical lens stimulation and age-related loss), requiring more corneal specular microscopy examinations.

The 1-year follow-up period was chosen primarily because most children using orthokeratology lenses have their lenses replaced shortly after the first year of wear. As a retrospective study, we could only analyze existing data with a limited understanding of long-term mechanisms, particularly beyond one to two years or longer. The long-term effects of axial reduction on anterior and posterior segment structures remain uncharacterized.
