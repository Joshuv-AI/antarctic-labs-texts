Metabolic Perspective on Atherosclerosis: Macrophage Reprogramming and Novel Therapeutic Targets
Authors: Zexun Wang, Ying Zhang, Lihua Li, Zhongqun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease driven by metabolic disorders, and macrophages play a central role in their occurrence and development. Macrophages are not static; their functional polarization and fate decisions are highly regulated by metabolic signals from the microenvironment, a process known as metabolic reprogramming. This review systematically reviews the latest progress in the metabolic reprogramming of glucose, lipids, and mitochondria in atherosclerosis, focusing on how key metabolites such as glycolysis, pentose phosphate pathway, cholesterol/sphingolipid metabolism, gut microbiota derivatives, and oxaloacetate dynamically regulate the inflammatory phenotype, foam cell formation, and immune response of macrophages. This review also delves into new concepts such as trained immunity and analyzes the therapeutic potential of targeting these metabolic pathways, aiming to comprehensively reveal the core role of metabolic‐immune cross‐talk in atherosclerosis and provide a theoretical basis for developing new strategies for diagnosing and treating atherosclerosis based on macrophage metabolic reprogramming.

Atherosclerosis is a progressive pathological process whose major clinical manifestations (cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events) are ultimately mediated by superimposed thrombosis at sites of vulnerable plaque. Its essence is a chronic inflammatory disease, and macrophages play a crucial role in its progression. Traditionally, macrophages have been simply classified into proinflammatory M1 and anti‐inflammatory M2 types. However, with the development of technologies such as single‐cell RNA sequencing, multiple macrophage subsets with distinct functional and metabolic characteristics have been identified in atherosclerotic plaques.

In terms of cell origin, macrophages within plaques have dual sources. Some are embryo‐derived resident‐like macrophages, such as Lyve1+(lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1 positive) resident‐like macrophage in the vascular adventitia. The other part consists of bone marrow‐derived monocytes, which are recruited to the intima and differentiate into various effector subsets under atherosclerosis, such as TREM2+ (triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2+) macrophages, inflammatory macrophages, proliferative macrophages, and interferon‐induced macrophages.

In terms of functional and metabolic heterogeneity, at least 5 conserved macrophage subsets have been identified to date. Inflammatory macrophages (interleukin‑1β positive (Il1b+)) localize predominantly to rupture‐prone regions such as the plaque shoulder and are characterized by enhanced glycolysis, pentose phosphate pathway activity, and 1‐carbon metabolism. TREM2+ macrophages accumulate in the necrotic core and adjacent calcified areas, exhibiting active cholesterol efflux and elevated oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). 1 Interferon‐inducible macrophages can be further divided into 2 functional subtypes: an inflammatory subset, M (interferon‑γ(IFN‐γ)i) and a phagocytic subset, M (IFN‐γ)p. The former displays robust glycolysis and a truncated tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, whereas the latter maintains a complete TCA cycle with predominant fatty acid oxidation and OXPHOS. 2 Resident‐like macrophages (Lyve1+, TLF+) reside in the adventitia under homeostatic conditions and can infiltrate the plaque during disease progression. Resident‐like macrophages (Lyve1+, TLF+) are located in the adventitia under steady‐state conditions and can infiltrate into plaques during pathological processes. Intermittent hyperlipidemia in the early stage can induce a metabolic shift from OXPHOS to glycolysis in these macrophages and accelerate their foam cell formation. 3 Proliferating macrophages are found in actively expanding regions of advanced plaques, although their metabolic profiles remain poorly defined. 4 The spatial distribution and metabolic features of these subsets are systematically summarized in Table 1. 1 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10

In addition, apart from the aforementioned macrophage subsets, vascular smooth muscle cells have emerged as a significant source of macrophage‐like cells within atherosclerotic plaques. During the disease progression, smooth muscle cells can undergo a phenotypic switch from a contractile state to a synthetic state and further transdifferentiate into cells that express macrophage markers (such as cluster of differentiation 68(CD68) and (MAC2)), acquiring the ability to phagocytize and accumulate lipids. Moreover, smooth muscle cell‐derived macrophage‐like cells exhibit distinct metabolic characteristics compared with monocyte‐derived macrophages, but the specific metabolic conditions still require further study. 11

It is worth noting that the functional state of macrophages is closely related to their metabolic patterns. When responding to microenvironmental signals (such as oxLDL [oxidized low‐density lipoprotein], cytokines, hypoxia), macrophages can undergo metabolic reprogramming. This process not only involves the switching of energy metabolism pathways, but also regulates the production of proinflammatory factors, epigenetic modifications, and cell signal transduction through metabolites (such as succinate, isocitrate, α‐ketoglutarate), thereby precisely regulating the immune response in atherosclerosis. 12 In recent years, the gut microbiota and its metabolites (such as short‐chain fatty acids [SCFAs], trimethylamine N‐oxide [TMAO], and bile acids) have also been found to affect the metabolic state and function of macrophages through the circulatory system, further expanding the connotation of the metabolism‐immunity regulatory network. Moreover, the trained immunity induced by continuous metabolic stimulation keeps macrophages in a long‐term proinflammatory state, providing a new mechanism for the accelerated progression of atherosclerosis in metabolic diseases. This review aimed to systematically summarize the mechanisms of lipid, glucose, amino acid, and mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming of macrophages in atherosclerosis, focusing on the roles of key enzymes, metabolites, and microbiota‐host interactions in regulating the inflammatory phenotype, lipid accumulation, and plaque stability of macrophages; at the same time, it looks forward to targeted metabolic reprogramming treatment strategies, with the aim of providing new ideas for the precise intervention of atherosclerosis.

LIPID METABOLIC REPROGRAMMING

Triacylglycerol and Ketone Body Metabolism

The ability of macrophages to maintain lipid homeostasis in atherosclerosis is crucial in determining the fate of the plaque, and the balance between triacylglycerol (TAG) synthesis and breakdown, as well as the signaling pathways mediated by ketone bodies, play a central role in this process.

TAG metabolic imbalance is one of the metabolic bases for macrophage foaming. The absence of macrophage MCT4 (monocarboxylate transporter 4) can enhance the lactylation modification of histone H3K18, significantly reduce lipid uptake, and inhibit its polarization toward the M1 type. 13 During the process of foaming, the ability of macrophages to clear excessive lipids through TAG hydrolysis gradually deteriorates, whereas docosahexaenoic acid can enhance TAG degradation and alleviate lipid accumulation. 14 Itaconate can guide the metabolic flow toward oxidative energy supply by upregulating solute carrier family 25 member 1(SLC25A1) and promoting fatty acid β‐oxidation. It not only reduces the substrate for TAG synthesis but also indirectly promotes its breakdown, thereby jointly alleviating lipid load. 15 Furthermore, cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) plays a crucial regulatory role in the lipid metabolism of macrophages. Similar to low‐density lipoprotein, when high‐density lipoprotein undergoes oxidative modification to form oxidized high‐density lipoprotein, it can induce the palmitoylation of CD36 on the surface of macrophages, enhancing their scavenger receptor function and thereby promoting lipid uptake. 16 Enhancing the sirtuin 6 activity of macrophages can enhance lipophagy through the Wnt1/β‐catenin pathway and improve plaque stability. 17 Low expression of the prostaglandin E2 receptor4 (EP4) in plaque macrophages accelerates oxLDL uptake and foam cell transformation by upregulating cell surface CD36. 18

Ketone bodies, as key metabolic signaling molecules, integrate immune and metabolic responses through multiple mechanisms. β‐hydroxybutyric acid (β‐HB), the most abundant ketone body in the body, can be produced through fatty acid β‐oxidation or by converting from acetoacetic acid. It inhibits M1 polarization by acting on the GPR109a (G protein‐coupled receptor 109a) receptor 19 and promotes M2 polarization by activating the JAK2 (Janus kinase 2)‐signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6) pathway. 20 Recent studies have shown that β‐HB can induce a novel histone modification in macrophages lysine Kbhb (β‐hydroxybutyrylation). This modification can directly inhibit the transcriptional activity of STAT1 (signal transducer and activator of transcription 1), thereby blocking M1 polarization. 21 Additionally, β‐HB promotes Kbhb modification of PHD2 (prolyl hydroxylase domain‐containing protein 2), stabilizing HIF‐1α (hypoxia‐inducible factor 1‐α) to enhance VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) transcription and participate in vascular repair responses in atherosclerosis. 22

In conclusion, appropriate supplementation of β‐HB shows significant potential in the prevention and treatment of atherosclerosis. It is worth noting that exercise intervention has been proven to effectively increase circulating β‐HB levels and slow down the progression of atherosclerosis, suggesting its important value as a nonpharmacological strategy. 23 In regard to the precursors such as acetyl‐coenzyme A produced during the TAG metabolic process, whether they themselves can affect the endogenous β‐HB level or Kbhb modification within macrophages, thereby forming a feedback loop, this can be further studied in the future.

Cholesterol and Bile Acid Metabolism

The imbalance of cholesterol homeostasis in macrophages is the core aspect of atherosclerosis, and the obstruction of cholesterol efflux is the key issue. In recent years, bile acid metabolism and its related signaling pathways have been found to play a significant role in regulating macrophage lipid metabolism and inflammatory phenotype, providing new targets for intervention.

The inefficient reverse cholesterol transport is one of the reasons for macrophage foaming. Various pathological factors can impair this process. For instance, in type 2 diabetes, the ability of macrophages to transport cholesterol via ABCA1 to small high‐density lipoprotein is specifically impaired. 24 As a key epigenetic regulator, miR‐33b blocks cholesterol efflux by suppressing ATP‑binding cassette transporter A1/ATP‑binding cassette transporter G1 (ABCA1/ABCG1) expression. 25 Furthermore, multiple key proteins have been confirmed to precisely regulate the reverse cholesterol transport pathway of macrophages: EEPD1endonuclease/exonuclease/phosphatase family domain containing 1, as a target gene of the LXR (liver X receptor), its reduced activity weakens the cholesterol transport mediated by LXR to high‐density lipoprotein and apo A1 (apolipoprotein A1), 26 whereas TNFAIP1 promotes lipid accumulation by interfering with the long non‑coding RNA enhancing endothelial nitric oxide synthase expression/forkhead box O1/ATP‑binding cassette transporter A1 (LEENE/FoxO1/ABCA1) axis. 27 The depletion of cholesterol synthesis intermediates (such as dehydrocholesterol) can activate the NLRP3 (NOD‐like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3) inflammasome by promoting glycolysis and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) production. 28 This suggests that cholesterol metabolism itself may directly regulate the inflammatory state of macrophages, thereby linking lipid accumulation more closely to the inflammatory response.

It is worth noting that the bile acid metabolism regulated by the gut microbiota is closely associated with atherosclerosis. Ursodeoxycholic acid and taurine‐deoxycholic acid can induce M2 polarization of macrophages and inhibit the secretion of proinflammatory factors. 29 Glycyl‐taurine deoxycholic acid can downregulate the expression of SR‐A1 (scavenger receptor class A member 1) in macrophages, reduce the uptake of oxLDL and inflammatory infiltration, and may exert beneficial effects by regulating the intestinal flora. This suggests its potential value as a potential alternative therapy for patients with statin intolerance. 30

Sphingolipid Metabolism

Sphingolipids, especially ceramides, are not only novel circulating biomarkers of atherosclerosis, but also key regulators of macrophage inflammatory response, polarization fate, and lipid homeostasis. The pathogenic effect of ceramides is closely related to their molecular types. Large‐scale clinical cohort studies have shown that specific ceramides and their ratios are powerful markers for predicting atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. 31 The Ceramide Test score, developed on this basis, assesses the risk of atherosclerosis by quantifying the ratio of specific plasma ceramides. Its predictive value has surpassed that of traditional risk factors and has been validated in multiple large‐scale, multicenter clinical cohort studies. 32

Hypoxia‐Inducible Factors Reprograms Sphingolipid Metabolism

The hypoxic microenvironment within atherosclerosis plaques directly regulates sphingolipid metabolism via hypoxia‐inducible transcription factors, influencing macrophage function. HIF‐1α can promote the hydrolysis of myelin sphingomyelin to generate ceramides through activating SMPD3 (sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase 3), thereby exacerbating atherosclerosis. Usage of HIF‐1α inhibitors (such as PX‐478 (a HIF‑1α inhibitor)) can reduce plaque burden. 33 In contrast, HIF‐2α promotes the breakdown of ceramides by upregulating Acer2 (alkaline ceramidase 2), and exerts an antiatherosclerosis effect in macrophages. Applying prolyl hydroxylase domain inhibitors (such as FG‐4592) to stabilize HIF‐2α, or upregulating its expression through cold exposure, can all achieve the goal of reducing plaque area through promoting ceramide catabolism, a process driven by HIF‐2α, which mitigates proinflammatory macrophage polarization. 34

Trans Fatty Acids Reprogram Sphingolipid Metabolism

Trans fatty acids (TFAs) are primarily categorized into 2 types: industrial TFAs and ruminant TFAs. Industrial TFAs feature trans double bonds in a linear configuration, endowing them with physical properties similar to those of saturated fatty acids. In contrast, ruminant TFAs possess trans double bonds at various positions; some even contain conjugated double bonds, resulting in a more diverse structural profile. Current research clearly identifies industrial TFAs as a major risk factor for promoting atherosclerosis, with their primary source being exogenous intake (eg, partially hydrogenated vegetable oils). The impact of ruminant TFAs on the cardiovascular system, however, is more complex and appears to be dependent on both the intake level and the specific structural characteristics of the fatty acids. 35

New research indicates that TFAs can serve as a preferred substrate for the key enzyme SPT (serine palmitoyltransferase), which is involved in the synthesis of sphingolipids. This promotes the production of very low‐density lipoproteins rich in ceramides in the liver. These atherogenic lipoprotein particles are then taken up by macrophages, which further increase their lipid load and inflammatory response. This provides a new explanation beyond cholesterol for the pathogenic mechanism of TFAs. 36 Saturated fatty acids also significantly increase the levels of various ceramides in macrophages, inducing the necrosis of macrophages and generating local inflammation. 37

Reprogramming Effects of Sphingolipid Derivatives

oxPAPC (oxidized 1‐palmitoyl‐2‐arachidonoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine), generated from PAPC (1‐palmitoyl‐2‐arachidonoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine) oxidation, is a product of local oxidative stress in atherosclerosis plaques. oxPAPC enhances the cell's ability to uptake glutamine by upregulating the expression of glutamine transporters, glycolysis, and OXPHOS. The α‐ketoglutarate produced by the breakdown of glutamine serves as a key intermediate product for the TCA cycle, maintaining mitochondrial respiratory activity and providing energy for the generation of proinflammatory factors. Additionally, oxPAPC causes the accumulation of oxaloacetate in the cytoplasm, thereby stabilizing HIF‐1α and ultimately amplifying the transcription and expression of IL‐1β (interleukin 1β), exacerbating atherosclerosis 38 (Figure 1).

GLUCOSE METABOLIC REPROGRAMMING

Glycolysis

Under the stimulation of the microenvironment of atherosclerosis plaques (such as oxLDL, IFN‐γ), macrophages undergo metabolic reprogramming from OXPHOS to aerobic glycolysis. 39 The hypoxic microenvironment of the plaque can enhance the glycolytic flux of macrophages through the hypoxia‑inducible factor 1‑alpha/6‑phosphofructo‑2‑kinase/fructose‑2,6‑bisphosphatase 3/NOD‑like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (HIF‐1α/PFKFB3/NLRP3) axis. 40 This transformation is a key mechanism by which macrophages actively acquire biosynthetic raw materials to meet the energy demands of proinflammatory signaling pathways, thereby driving and maintaining their M1 phenotype, and is closely related to the instability of atherosclerosis plaques. 41 The initiation of this reprogramming in macrophages is closely related to metabolic decision points such as the accumulation of succinate in the TCA cycle and the inhibition of IDH (isocitrate dehydrogenase) activity. 42

The activation of the HIF‐1α/PKM2 (pyruvate kinase 2) axis is crucial: atherosclerosis‐related signals such as oxLDL can upregulate PKM2 in macrophages and promote its nuclear translocation, working in synergy with HIF‐1α to enhance the transcription of glycolytic genes, forming a proinflammatory positive feedback loop. 43 The PDK/PDH (pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase/pyruvate dehydrogenase) axis controls the flow of pyruvate. The inhibitor dichloroacetate promotes the entry of pyruvate into the TCA cycle by activating PDH and inhibits the succinate‐NLRP3 inflammatory pathway, thereby alleviating the progression of atherosclerosis. 44 The mechanistic target of rapamycin/ribosomal protein S6 kinase beta‑1 (mTOR/S6K1) axis can be activated by oxLDL, which upregulates the expression of prosaposin to promote glycolysis in macrophages and M1 polarization. 45

Functionally, the glycolytic reprogramming within macrophages not only provides energy for proinflammatory signaling pathways such as JAK/STAT1, but also the lactate derived from it and the inhibition of OXPHOS can promote mtROS production, thereby stabilizing HIF‐1α, amplifying inflammatory signals, and accelerating the progression of atherosclerosis. 46 Furthermore, overexpression of sirtuin 1 or deletion of Pnpt1 (polyribonucleotide nucleotidyltransferase 1) can enhance macrophage glycolysis and exacerbate NLRP3 inflammasome activation and other inflammatory responses, which once again confirm the crucial role of the glycolysis pathway in atherosclerosis. 47 , 48

Pentose Phosphate Pathway

The pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) pathway, as the core hub that coordinates the redox balance and immune function of macrophages, consists of the oxidative branch and the nonoxidative branch, and is an important bypass in glucose metabolism. In activated macrophages, the activation of the PPP pathway goes far beyond merely providing biosynthetic precursors. It is instead a central metabolic hub that coordinates redox homeostasis and supports immune functions. 49 Therefore, the flux dynamics of PPP precisely reflect the immune functional state of macrophages.

A series of proinflammatory signals that trigger atherosclerosis can significantly reconfigure the PPP pathway in macrophages. For instance, after lipopolysaccharide activates macrophages through toll‑like receptor 4 (TLR4, it synergistically upregulates glycolysis and the PPP pathway to drive an efficient oxidative burst of NOX2 (NADPH oxidase 2). 50 Interestingly, this process forms a self‐amplifying positive feedback loop. NOX2 activation consumes nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH), thereby stimulating the PPP to regenerate the NADPH pool; at the same time, interferon‑beta (IFN‐β) induced by lipopolysaccharide can further enhance the expression of NOX2, continuously amplifying this signal. This indicates that the upregulation of PPP is a basic metabolic adaptation for macrophages to achieve their innate immune effector functions.

Long‐term hypoxia can induce macrophages to enter a primed preparatory state, characterized by inhibition of glycolysis and the TCA cycle, whereas the PPP is significantly enhanced. Even more intriguingly, the intermediate products of PPP (such as S7P [sedoheptulose‐7‐phosphate] and R5P [ribose‐5‐phosphate]) are first converted into glycolytic intermediate F6P (fructose‐6‐phosphate), which then cycles back to generate G6P (glucose‐6‐phosphate) and re‐enter PPP, forming an efficient NADPH generation cycle that maximizes NADPH reserves with the lowest glucose consumption. At the same time, macrophages also simultaneously enter a poised standby state (with only transcription of phagocytic function genes, no translation); when encountering apoptotic cells (initiating efferocytosis), the poised state quickly initiates translation, combined with the metabolic reserves of the primed state, efficiently synthesizing the proteins required for phagocytic function. 51 This mechanism reveals the crucial supporting role of PPP in the macrophage clearance function within the oxidative stress microenvironment of the plaque (Figure 2).

AMINO ACID METABOLIC REPROGRAMMING

Branched‐Chain Amino Acids

Branched‐chain amino acids (BCAAs; leucine, isoleucine, and valine) play a significant role in atherosclerosis. Among them, leucine, as a key signaling molecule, mainly drives macrophage metabolic disorders and inflammatory responses by activating the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) pathway. Its transporter, SLC7A5, promotes the entry of leucine into cells and acts in concert with proteins on the lysosomal membrane to further enhance mTORC1 signaling. Solute carrier family 7 member 5 (SLC7A5) can also bind to the mitochondrial protein phosphoglycerate mutase family member 5 (PGAM5), leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, upregulation of the glycolytic rate, and foam cell formation. 52 , 53 In addition, BCAAs can increase the levels of H2O2 in macrophage mitochondria and nuclei, induce the formation of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) disulfide bonds, and activate the TLR4/NF‐κB (nuclear factor κ‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells) pathway, promoting the development of atherosclerosis. 54

Although high levels of BCAAs are positively correlated with cardiovascular risk, 55 their metabolic outcomes are environment dependent. For example, excessive dietary protein intake can increase the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease by elevating plasma leucine levels, activating the macrophage mTORC1 signaling pathway. 56 Conversely, promoting the catabolism of BCAAs by regulating the gut microbiota can inhibit the mTORC1 pathway, thereby reducing inflammation and plaque formation. 57 However, in vascular smooth muscle cells, enhanced BCAAs catabolism has been observed to promote arteriosclerosis. 58 This highlights that BCAAs metabolism may play opposite roles in different cell types.

Tryptophan

Tryptophan metabolism mainly generates metabolites such as kynurenine through the IDO1/TDO (indoleamine 2,3‐dioxygenase 1/tryptophan 2,3‐dioxygenase) pathway. Studies have found that during efferocytosis, macrophages upregulate IDO1 and solute carrier family 36 member 4 (SLC36A4) on lysosomes, promote kynurenine production, activate the AhR (aryl hydrocarbon receptor), induce the expression of anti‐inflammatory factors such as IL‐10 (interleukin 10), and alleviate atherosclerosis. 59 Inhibiting TPH1 (tryptophan hydroxylase 1) to prevent the metabolism of tryptophan to 5‐HT (5‐hydroxytryptamine) or supplementing bacterial‐derived indole derivatives (IAA [indole‐3‐acetic acid], IPA [indole‐3‐propionic acid], tryptamine) can reduce the infiltration of monocyte‑macrophage antigen 2‑positive (MOMA2+) macrophages in atherosclerosis plaques and shrink plaque volume. 60 IPA, a tryptophan derivative metabolized by the gut microbiota, promotes plaque regression by inhibiting miR‐142‐5p and enhancing ABCA1‐mediated cholesterol efflux. 61

In addition, IDO1 and its isozyme TDO show obvious stage characteristics in the progression of atherosclerosis. They increase with the progression of the disease in the early stage and do not increase or even downregulate in the late stage. 62 This suggests that targeted therapy for atherosclerosis by targeting IDO1/TDO needs to be precisely implemented in combination with the stage of lesion development.

Methionine

Methionine metabolism plays a key epigenetic regulatory role in macrophages through its core metabolite SAM (S‐adenosylmethionine), significantly affecting plaque stability. Studies have found that MAT2A (methionine adenosyltransferase 2α) in macrophages promotes histone trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 4 (H3K4me3) modification by increasing SAM production, thereby promoting the vulnerability of atherosclerosis plaques. 63 Through efferocytosis, macrophages can convert methionine in apoptotic cells into SAM to methylate Dusp4 (dual specificity phosphatase 4), maintain the continuous activation of ERK (extracellular signal‐regulated kinase), promote the secretion of transforming growth factor beta 1(TGF‐β1), thicken the fibrous cap, and stabilize plaques. 64 During lipopolysaccharide‐induced inflammation, the SSP (serine synthesis pathway), PPP, and 1‐carbon metabolism work together to drive the activation of inflammatory macrophages and the expression of IL‐1β by regulating the production of SAM and histone trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 36 (H3K36me3). 65 In summary, the function of the methionine‐SAM axis is significantly context‐dependent and plays a double‐edged sword role in atherosclerosis.

Synergistic Effects of Other Amino Acids

In addition to BCAAs, tryptophan, and methionine, glycine, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and arginine also coordinately regulate macrophage functions through an interconnected metabolic network. Glycine and its analog DT‐109 (a glycine‑based compound) can promote the de novo synthesis of glutathione in macrophages, reduce superoxide production, and alleviate atherosclerosis independently of lipid‐lowering effects. 66 In an inflammatory state, the activation of the aspartate‐argininosuccinate bypass in macrophages causes the accumulation of fumarate in the cytoplasm, especially in the absence of fumarate hydratase, which further exacerbates mitochondrial stress, mitochondrial DNA release, and inflammatory responses. 67 The downregulation of GLS1 (glutaminase 1) expression but the upregulation of solute carrier family 7 member 7 (SLC7A7) expression in macrophages within plaques cause glutamate accumulation and diversion to the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway, thereby interfering with extracellular matrix homeostasis and weakening efferocytosis function. 68 During the repair process of atherosclerosis, macrophages metabolize arginine and ornithine produced by apoptotic cells into putrescine through Arg1 (arginase 1) and ODC (ornithine decarboxylase), and enhance efferocytosis, promoting plaque stability. 69 These pathways are intertwined and jointly coordinate macrophage inflammation, repair, and homeostasis maintenance in atherosclerosis. In addition, other amino acids such as cysteine and proline play more important regulatory roles in the metabolism of other cells involved in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis, such as endothelial cells and smooth muscle cells, so they will not be elaborated here.

MITOCHONDRIAL METABOLIC REPROGRAMMING

In addition to the fatty acid β‐oxidation discussed in the previous section, the reprogramming of the TCA cycle and OXPHOS is also crucial in regulating the function of macrophages. This section will review the metabolic remodeling of the TCA cycle and OXPHOS in atherosclerosis, and specifically explore the core regulatory roles of itaconate and its derivatives.

TCA Cycle Metabolites Drive Inflammatory Reprogramming

The reprogramming of the tricarboxylic acid cycle in macrophages significantly influences the progression of atherosclerosis. For example, in myocardial infarction, TREM2 signaling in macrophages downregulates the mitochondrial nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) transporter solute carrier family 25 member 53 (SLC25A53). This reduction inhibits mitochondrial NAD+ uptake and creates a break point in the TCA cycle, which subsequently increases the production of the metabolite isocitrate and itaconate. This process is associated with improved cardiac repair. 70 In a different context, after lipopolysaccharide activates macrophages, the accumulation of succinate leads to the generation of a large amount of mtROS through the induction of reverse electron transport, thereby activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and the release of IL‐1β, which is a core event of proinflammatory reprogramming. 71 , 72 This reflects the functional differences in the reprogramming of the TCA cycle in macrophages. Succinic acid can also predict the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and promote the generation of harmful lipids. 73 The accumulation of fumarate activates the NRF2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2) pathway through alkylating KEAP1 (Kelch‐like enoyl‑CoA hydratase (ECH)‐associated protein 1), upregulates the synthesis of antioxidant proteins, and maintains the redox balance of macrophages, 67 , 74 , 75 whereas IL‐33 (interleukin 33) promotes the reprogramming of macrophages into anti‐inflammatory M2 type through inducing mitochondrial uncoupling and upregulating GATA3 (GATA binding protein 3), it inhibits the progression of atherosclerosis. 76 These findings have established that the metabolites of the TCA cycle play a crucial role in regulating the fate transformation of macrophages.

OXPHOS Dysfunction and the Vicious Cycle of Metabolic Reprogramming

The dysfunction of OXPHOS is the core step in the metabolic reprogramming of macrophages. Various internal and external stimuli can regulate the progression of atherosclerosis by interfering with mitochondrial function.

Endogenous stressors, such as the absence of polyribonucleotide nucleotidyltransferase 1 (Pnpt1), induce significant accumulation of double‑stranded RNA (dsRNA) in macrophages, which subsequently triggers the MAVS (mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein)‐dependent type I interferon response. Moreover, Pnpt1 deficiency also upregulates glycolysis, as outlined in preceding sections (via HIF‐1α/GLUT1 (glucose transporter type 1) upregulation). Together, these 2 pathways converge to activate NLRP3 and drive the progression of atherosclerosis. 47 The deficiency of drp1 (dynamin‐related protein 1) leads to excessive mitochondrial fusion, an increase in mtROS production, and further promotes M1 type polarization. 77 In addition, the combination of oxLDL and CD36 can also inhibit the β‐oxidation of long‐chain fat acids, make them accumulate in mitochondria, force the metabolic state of macrophages to change from OXPHOS to glycolysis, and intensify the pathological process of atherosclerosis by activating the NF‐κB pathway. 78

Exogenous factors, like benzo[a]pyrene from tobacco smoke, upregulate VDAC2/3 (voltage‐dependent anion channel 2/3) on the macrophage mitochondrial membrane, promoting mitochondrial uptake of cholesterol and Ca2+, increasing mtROS production, and inducing M1 polarization. 79

To cope with the oxidative stress caused by OXPHOS imbalance and metabolic reprogramming, macrophages activate key self‐protection mechanisms. They clear lipid peroxides through the solute carrier family 7 member 11/cystine/glutathione/glutathione peroxidase 4 (SLC7A11/cystine/GSH/GPX4) axis, whereas the NRF2 pathway regulates ACSL4 (acyl‐coenzyme A synthetase long‐chain family member 4) and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) to reshape membrane lipids and reduce polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) content, jointly resisting ferroptosis. 80 , 81 Furthermore, the absence of TIPE2 (tumor necrosis factor‐α‐induced protein 8‐like 2) in macrophages leads to Ca2+ overload, activating dynamin‑related protein 1 (Drp1) and causing excessive mitochondrial division and dysfunction. Furthermore, the absence of TIPE2 in macrophages leads to Ca2+ overload, activates Drp1, causing excessive mitochondrial division and dysfunction. These dysfunctional mitochondrial fragments can be transmitted via tunneling nanotubes to adipose stem cells, inducing ferroptosis in the latter and connecting atherosclerosis with metabolic disorders such as total insulin resistance. 82

Regulatory Role of Mitochondrial Epigenetic Modifications

Epigenetic mechanisms mediate communication between mitochondria and the nucleus.

On the one hand, mitochondrial metabolites can regulate the epigenetic state within the nucleus and fix the functional phenotype of macrophages. For instance, γ‐aminobutyric acid taken in by macrophages activates histone demethylase LSD1 (lysine‐specific demethylase 1) and reduces mitochondrial protein succinylation, promoting OXPHOS and inhibiting inflammation, achieving anti‐inflammatory reprogramming and effectively alleviating atherosclerosis. 83 Exercise‐induced lactylation of MeCP2 (methyl‐CpG‐binding protein 2) occurs in macrophages, which directly drives their polarization toward the M2 type, contributing to plaque stability. This provides a molecular mechanism for exercise to improve atherosclerosis. 84

On the other hand, epigenetic modifications can directly target the mitochondria themselves and reprogram their functions. For instance, oxLDL can induce high expression of METTL4 (methyltransferase‐like 4) in macrophages, thereby mediating m6A (N6‐methyladenosine) modification of mitochondrial DNA, directly hindering OXPHOS, triggering compensatory glycolysis and inflammatory responses, and exacerbating atherosclerosis. 85 Under pathological conditions, the metabolites (such as propionic acid and methylmalonic acid) and their derivatives accumulated in the intestinal microbiota can cause methylmalonylation of mitochondrial proteins in macrophages, resulting in a significant reduction in mitochondrial DNA copy number and inhibition of the electron transport chain. This process is regulated by the demethylating enzyme sirtuin 5 (SIRT5). 86 This mechanism suggests that in future treatments targeting the metabolites of the gut microbiota, the dosage must be precisely controlled to avoid negative impacts on the mitochondrial function of macrophages.

Important Regulatory Role of Itaconate

Itaconate, a derivative of the TCA cycle abundant in the mitochondrial matrix of macrophages, is produced by the decarboxylation of the TCA intermediate cis‐aconitate catalyzed by ACOD1 (aconitate decarboxylase 1, also known as IRG1). 87 In recent years, its role has shifted from being merely a metabolic by‐product to becoming an important immune metabolic regulator. 88 Therefore, at the end of this section, we have focused particularly on summarizing how itaconate regulates the progression of atherosclerosis by reprogramming the mitochondrial metabolism of macrophages.

The itaconate within macrophages modifies the key cysteine of KEAP1 through its α, β‐unsaturated carboxylic acid structure, causing a conformational change that prevents the ubiquitination and degradation of NRF2. This results in the nuclear translocation of NRF2, triggering the expression of various antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory genes such as Hmox1 (heme oxygenase 1) and GSH, and inhibiting M1 polarization. 89 , 90

Macrophages undergo phenotypic transformation in the atherosclerosis microenvironment due to the accumulation of succinate in the TCA cycle and the inhibition of isocitrate dehydrogenase activity. These 2 key decision points determine this process. In the M1 polarization, the decrease in IDH activity and the increase in ACOD1 (IRG1) activity leads to a significant increase in the flow of isocitrate to the synthesis of oxaloacetic acid. 7 It is worth noting that isocitric acid, as a structural analogue of succinic acid, can competitively inhibit the activity of SDH (succinate dehydrogenase), leading to the accumulation of succinic acid. The accumulated succinic acid drives mtROS bursts through complex I reverse electron transport, promoting the stabilization of HIF‐1α and the expression of proinflammatory factors such as IL‐1β. 42 Furthermore, the production of itaconate in macrophages has an important cross‐talk with the NO signaling. Adequate NO can modify specific cysteine residues of IDH through nitration, thereby inhibiting its activity and promoting the metabolic flow toward succinate synthesis, thereby enhancing its anti‐inflammatory effect. 91 This reveals the bidirectional regulatory role of the NO‐itaconate axis in metabolic reprogramming.

The single‐cell RNA sequencing analysis revealed that ACOD1 was significantly upregulated in macrophages within human atherosclerosis plaques. This upregulation is considered an endogenous protective response. When ACOD1 is deficient, the production of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) increases, stimulating the excessive activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages and the release of IL‐1β, thereby accelerating the inflammation of atherosclerosis. 92 Furthermore, the absence of ACOD1 leads to a comprehensive disruption of the metabolism of glucose, lipids, and nucleotides in macrophages, promoting their transformation into foam cells and M1 type polarization, and inhibiting the expression of antioxidant genes. Eventually, this exacerbates atherosclerosis 93 (Figure 3).

CROSS‐TALK BETWEEN METABOLIC HUBS AND IMMUNE RESPONSES IN MACROPHAGES

G6P: The Decision Point for Metabolic Flux

G6P, as the first intermediate product of glucose metabolism, plays a crucial role in the decision‐making of macrophage fate. In the inflammatory environment of atherosclerosis, the glycogen synthesis of macrophages is abnormally active, and its key intermediate product, uridine diphosphate glucose, can act as a signaling molecule and induce M1 polarization through GPCR (G‐protein‐coupled receptor) signaling. 94 PPP1R3B (protein phosphatase 1 regulatory subunit 3B) combines and activates phosphorylated signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (p‐STAT3), causing it to translocate to the mitochondria. It activates the phosphorylated glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta/phosphorylated glycogen phosphorylase L/phosphorylated glycogen synthase 2 (p‐GSK‐3β/p‐PYGL/p‐GYS2) pathway, enhances glycogenolysis, and induces M2 type polarization. 95

The metabolic fate of G6P is also precisely regulated by the subcellular localization of HK1 (hexokinase 1). When HK1 binds to the outer mitochondrial membrane, G6P mainly enters glycolysis, whereas when it dissociates into the cytoplasm, it interacts with the S100A8/A9 (S100 calcium‐binding protein A8/A9) complex, which inhibits (glyceraldehyde‑3‑phosphate dehydrogenase)GAPDH activity and forces more G6P to enter the PPP pathway, enhancing the oxidative burst and inflammatory factor production of macrophages. The observed reduction in HK1 mitochondrial localization and increased inflammation in patients with type 2 diabetes reveal the crucial role of this mechanism in accelerating atherosclerosis in diabetes. 96

PKM2: A Central Node Coordinating Metabolic Reprogramming and Inflammatory Signaling

PKM2 is a key enzyme in the glycolysis process that catalyzes the conversion of phosphoenolpyruvate to pyruvate and produces ATP. In atherosclerosis, oxLDL upregulates PKM2 via PPARγ and promotes its Tyr105 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation. The nuclear PKM2 interacts with HIF‐1α, activating the expression of glycolytic genes, forming a proinflammatory positive feedback loop, and promoting M1 polarization. 97 More importantly, PKM2 directly links the disorders of sugar metabolism and lipid metabolism. It can upregulate CD36 and activate the SREBP‐1/FASN (sterol regulatory element‐binding protein 1/fatty acid synthase) axis, simultaneously promoting cholesterol uptake and lipid synthesis, and accelerating the formation of foam cells. On the contrary, myeloid‐specific knockout of PKM2 can enhance the phagocytic effect by upregulating LRP‐1 (low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 1), thereby alleviating atherosclerosis. 98 , 99 Furthermore, PKM2 can also combine with JAML (junctional adhesion molecule‐like protein) to form the phosphorylated pyruvate kinase M2/NF‑κB subunit p65 (p‐PKM2/p65) complex, which activates the NF‐κB pathway and the NLRP3 inflammatory body, thereby intensifying atherosclerosis from multiple aspects. 100

NRF2: The Central Transcriptional Hub Integrating Metabolic Stress and Antioxidant Defense

In the metabolic comprehensive network of atherosclerosis, the NRF2 pathway plays a central role in integration and execution. Its activity is precisely regulated by various metabolites in the plaque microenvironment; electrophilic metabolites, such as itaconate and fumarate, covalently modify the key cysteine residues of KEAP1, thereby preventing its ubiquitination and degradation, and promoting the nuclear translocation of NRF2, 74 whereas the metabolite TMAO produced by the microbiota inhibits this pathway, exacerbating oxidative damage. 101 The activated NRF2 then initiates a comprehensive cellular defense program; it enhances the antioxidant capacity by upregulating genes such as HO‐1 (heme oxygenase‐1), 90 reprograms glucose metabolism by directly promoting the expression of 6PDG (6‐phosphogluconate dehydrogenase), a key enzyme of the PPP, to regenerate NADPH, 102 and resists ferroptosis by regulating lipid metabolism enzymes such as ACSL4 to reduce the polyunsaturated fatty acid content of membrane phospholipids. 81 Therefore, as the central hub for sensing metabolic stress, NRF2 fundamentally determines the inflammatory phenotype and fate of macrophages in atherosclerosis through coordinating multiple effects such as antioxidant defense, metabolic reprogramming, and cell survival.

Intercellular Communication: Shaping the Plaque Metabolic Microenvironment

The immune‐metabolic microenvironment within atherosclerosis plaques is jointly shaped by various cells. Obesity, as a key driver of atherosclerosis, has high expression of 4‐1BBL (4‐1BB ligand) in its adipose tissue. This can activate the protein kinase B/mechanistic target of rapamycin (AKT/mTOR) signaling axis, reprogram the metabolism of macrophages, resulting in significantly enhanced glucose uptake, glycolysis, PPP, and fatty acid synthesis. This, in turn, drives their polarization toward the proinflammatory M1 type, exacerbating vascular inflammation. 103 Our previous research has shown that 4‐1BBL can also cause atherosclerosis by reprogramming endothelial cells and modulating mitochondrial metabolism. Therefore, 4‐1BBL has the potential to serve as a new therapeutic target for atherosclerosis, and further studies are warranted.

The interaction between T cells and macrophages is also crucial in the progression of atherosclerosis. The ligands expressed by T cells, such as versican and GRP94 (glucose‐regulated protein 94), can promote the formation of foam cells through the TLR1/2 (toll‐like receptor 1/2) signaling pathway of macrophages. In an inflammatory state, IFN‐γ inhibits the promoting effect of apolipoprotein E (APOE) derived from T cells on the cholesterol efflux of macrophages, exacerbating lipid accumulation. Moreover, the triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 high (TREM2hi) macrophages in the plaque can mediate the clonal expansion of cluster of differentiation 4‑positive (CD4+) T cells through direct adhesion, forming a positive feedback loop that continuously amplifies the inflammatory response and accelerating the progression of atherosclerosis. 104 The hyperglycemic plaque microenvironment enhances the formation of NETs. These NETs activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and glycolytic pathway in macrophages, driving their polarization toward a proinflammatory M1 phenotype and promoting atherosclerosis. 92 , 105 Moreover, EphA2 (ephrin type‐A receptor 2) emerges as a key modulator of intercellular communication in atherosclerosis. Endothelial EphA2 deletion significantly reduces firm adhesion of macrophages under flow conditions, whereas smooth muscle EphA2 regulates advanced plaque progression by modulating smooth muscle cell proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition. 106 Additionally, neutrophil‐derived extracellular vesicles deliver miR‐27a‐3p to macrophages, thereby enhancing endogenous itaconate accumulation and exerting an antiatherosclerotic effect. 107

Gut Microbiota

The gut microbiota and their metabolites are deeply involved in the pathological process of atherosclerosis via the gut–immune–vascular axis. 108 Beyond microbial translocation directly into plaques, microbiota metabolites have become a core regulatory link in atherosclerosis by modulating host immunity and metabolism (Figure 4).

SCFAs: Immunometabolic Regulators With Cardioprotective Potential

SCFAs (such as butyrate and propionate) are produced by the fermentation of dietary fibers by gut bacteria. The abundance and metabolic capacity of SCFA‐producing bacteria in the intestines of patients with atherosclerosis are generally decreased. 109 The SCFAs produced by the fermentation of probiotic fibers by the intestinal microbiota have protective effects on the cardiovascular system. The guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology also recommend high intake of dietary fiber to manage dyslipidemia. 110

In terms of mechanism, SCFAs precisely regulate the functions of immune cells through epigenetic and GPCR signaling pathways. Butyrate can activate GPR41/43 (G‐protein‐coupled receptor 41/43), inhibit HDACs (histone deacetylases), block the nuclear translocation of NF‐κB p65, inhibit the polarization of macrophages toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype, and simultaneously promote cholesterol efflux by upregulating ABCA1, thereby consolidating its anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype. 111 , 112 Butyrate, through the GPR109a‐TGF‐β signaling pathway in macrophages, induces the differentiation of Treg cells and inhibits the inflammatory response. 113 A fascinating new finding is that butyrate can induce the butyrylation of GAPDH in endothelial cells at the K263 (lysine 263) site and competitively inhibit lactoylation, thereby inhibiting valvular calcification driven by glycolysis. 114 Given that endothelial cells and macrophages coexist in the same plaque microenvironment and share similar metabolic reprogramming characteristics, it is worthy of in‐depth study whether this mechanism exists in macrophages and affects the progression of atherosclerosis.

TMAO: A Proatherogenic Danger Signal

In contrast to SCFAs, TMAO is widely recognized as a promoter of atherosclerosis. Its precursor, trimethylamine, is produced by gut microbial metabolism of dietary phosphatidylcholine and carnitine, and is subsequently oxidized in the liver primarily by FMO3 (flavin‐containing monooxygenase 3) to TMAO. TMAO promotes foam cell formation by upregulating macrophage CD36 and SR‐A1 and downregulating ABCA1, acting synergistically. 115 Furthermore, TMAO can also inhibit the NRF2/ARE (nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2/antioxidant response element) signaling pathway in macrophages, weakening the cells' antioxidant defense capabilities and further accelerating the process of foam cell formation. 101 TMAO also regulates macrophage polarization, not only inhibiting polarization toward the anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype, 116 but also promoting a shift toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype by inducing mtROS generation and enhancing proinflammatory cytokine secretion. 117

Emerging Metabolites and Prospects

In addition to the aforementioned classic metabolites, emerging metabolites such as imidazole propionate have demonstrated greater potential in predicting cardiovascular events than TMAO, indicating the broad prospects of microbial metabolomics in the early warning and intervention of atherosclerosis risk. Overall, targeting the regulation of the gut microbiota‐metabolite‐immune axis, through dietary intervention, prebiotics, or direct supplementation of beneficial metabolites, has opened up new strategies for the prevention and treatment of atherosclerosis. 118

Microbiota at Other Sites

A number of authoritative studies have revealed that the oral microbiota is closely related to the occurrence of atherosclerosis. It has been confirmed that bacteria from the mouth exist in the plaques. They are out of balance in oral diseases such as periodontitis and can migrate to the intestines and arterial plaques, thereby promoting local inflammatory responses. 119

The respiratory tract microbiota, the urogenital tract microbiota, the skin microbiota, and even the microbiota in breast milk has all been found to have the ability to regulate inflammation, and there is interaction and communication among the microbiota in different parts. 120 However, the specific mechanisms by which they reprogram macrophages to regulate atherosclerosis remain unclear and require further investigation.

Key Mechanisms of Microbiota–Macrophage Interaction

The microbiota and its components can regulate the lipid metabolism and inflammatory state of macrophages through specific signaling pathways. For instance, the absence of NOD1/2 (nucleotide‐binding oligomerization domain‐containing protein 1/2) can reduce the uptake of oxLDL by macrophages, and upregulate the expression of ABCA1/ABCG1, promoting cholesterol efflux, thereby inhibiting foam cell formation. 121 Furthermore, lipopolysaccharide derived from the microbiota can activate macrophages through the cluster of differentiation 14/toll‑like receptors/nuclear factor κB (CD14/TLRs/NF‐κB) pathway, leading to systemic inflammation and accelerating the progression of atherosclerosis. 122

Statins, as the cornerstone drugs of lipid‐lowering and antiatherosclerosis, mainly reduce cholesterol levels by inhibiting hydroxymethylglutaryl‑coenzyme A reductase. It is worth noting that EphA2, which is highly expressed in plaques, may become a potential target of statins. 123 Atorvastatin can upregulate the expression of EphA2 in macrophages through the KLF4/HDAC11 (Krüppel‐like factor 4/histone deacetylase 11) axis, and the combination of EphA2 inhibition can enhance plaque stability, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for combination therapy. 124 EphA2 is also involved in regulating the interaction between macrophages and other cells, influencing cell adhesion and plaque progression. This is a brief introduction for the purpose of providing ideas for subsequent research. The knockout of the endothelial EphA2 gene can significantly reduce the firm adhesion of macrophages in a flowing state. The expression of smooth muscle EphA2 can regulate the progression of advanced atherosclerosis by modulating smooth muscle proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition. 106

TRAINED IMMUNITY

Training immunity refers to an adaptive state where the innate immune cells (such as macrophages) undergo metabolic–epigenetic coordinated reprogramming within their cells after the initial stimulation, thereby generating a stronger response to subsequent stimuli. This long‐term functional memory drives a persistent inflammatory response in atherosclerosis. 125

Metabolic Reprogramming as the Foundation of Trained Immunity

The macrophages that have undergone trained immunity exhibit significant metabolic remodeling including increased fluxes in glycolysis, glutamine breakdown, and cholesterol synthesis, as well as increased lactate production. Hyperglycemia is a key factor in inducing trained immunity, which can explain the phenomenon that the risk of atherosclerosis remains high even after glycemic control in patients with type 2 diabetes. 126 The core mechanism lies in that the breakdown of glutamine replenishes the TCA cycle, leading to the accumulation of fumaric acid. Fumarate, on the one hand, competitively inhibits the histone KDM5 (demethylase lysine demethylase 5), increasing the H3K4me3 modification at proinflammatory gene loci (such as Il‐6 [interleukin 6]); on the other hand, it stabilizes HIF‐1α, thereby synergistically promoting the transcription of genes related to glycolysis and reinforcing the proinflammatory phenotype. 127 Furthermore, oxLDL in hyperlipidemia pre‐activates macrophages by activating the NLRP3 inflammatory body. This process relies on the synergy of fatty acid synthesis and fatty acid oxidation, as well as the amplification effect of the HIF‐1α pathway, and forms a vicious cycle of enhanced glycolysis and cholesterol accumulation. 128 These metabolic changes collectively form the basis of the epigenetic reprogramming of the immune system during training and the long‐term consolidation of proinflammatory phenotypes.

Hyperglycemic/Hyperlipidemic Environments Drive Macrophage Trained Immunity

Hyperglycemia upregulates the transcription factor Runx1 (runt‐related transcription factor 1), reprogramming the function of macrophages, inducing promoting the expression of M1‐related genes, enhancing glycolysis, inhibiting M2 genes, and enhancing their adhesion to activated endothelium and their ability to uptake oxLDL, accelerating the formation of foam cells. These effects can be blocked by glycolysis inhibitors (such as dichloroacetate), confirming the central role of metabolic reprogramming. 129

High blood sugar also stimulates bone marrow production, increasing circulating monocytes and neutrophils. The latter form NETs in the plaques. NETs activate the NLRP3 inflammatory body and glycolysis, inducing M1 polarization of macrophages. Using DNase1 (deoxyribonuclease 1) to degrade NETs can effectively alleviate atherosclerosis. 105 , 130 , 131

Early intermittent hyperlipidemia, as a premetabolic stress stimulus, can reprogram macrophages through multiple complex mechanisms, accelerate the progression of atherosclerosis, and at the same exposure level, this stimulus has a greater effect on promoting atherosclerosis than late persistent hyperlipidemia. 3 , 132

Pivotal Role of Lactate and Metabolic Memory

The training immune reprogramming of macrophages can be inherited by the metabolic memory of monocytes. Clinical studies have found that after revascularization, lactate accumulates continuously in the bodies of patients with atherosclerosis, and macrophages maintain a high glycolytic phenotype for as long as 1 month. 133 During this period, lactate not only replenishes the TCA cycle as a carbon source, but its derived lactylation modification plays a dominant role; it maintains the chromatin accessibility of key immune genes, enabling transcription factors such as NF‐κB to respond rapidly to secondary stimuli and amplify the inflammatory response. 134

THERAPEUTIC STRATEGIES TARGETING MACROPHAGE METABOLISM

Targeting Cholesterol and Bile Acid Metabolism

Intervening in the metabolic pathways of cholesterol and bile acids is a potential therapeutic strategy. Natural compounds such as proanthocyanidins have been proven to reduce the formation of foam cells through bidirectional regulation (downregulation of scavenger receptors and synthases, and upregulation of ABCA1/ABCG1). 135 , 136 What is even more innovative is that a biomimetic nanoparticle (macrophage membrane‑coated quercetin‑loaded nanoparticles (MP‐QT‐NP)) based on β‐cyclodextrin modification and encapsulating quercetin liposomes with macrophage membranes ingeniously integrates the anti‐inflammatory effect mediated by the NRF2 pathway and the cholesterol efflux‐promoting effect mediated by the LXR pathway. In preclinical studies, it has demonstrated great potential for synergistic plaque regression. 137 The synthetic bile acid‐lipophilic conjugate ursodeoxycholic acid lysophosphatidylethanolamine inhibits the release of proinflammatory cytokines and the infiltration of macrophages into the plaque by integrating into the lipid rafts of macrophage membranes. 138 Furthermore, the nanoplatform constructed with UDCA can precisely target the treatment of atherosclerosis, dissolve the intraplaque cholesterol crystals, upregulate ABCA1 and ABCG1, and accelerate the cholesterol efflux from macrophages. 139

Mitochondria‐Targeted Therapy

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a core aspect of macrophage metabolic reprogramming. Therefore, mitochondria have become an important target for therapeutic intervention. Small molecule compounds, such as mitochondria‐targeted esculetin, target and improve mitochondrial metabolism, promote the polarization of macrophages to the M2 type, and show potential in antiatherosclerosis in preclinical models. 140 Dietary intervention can also target mitochondrial function; consuming foods rich in omega‐3/omega‐6 polyunsaturated fatty acids can improve the metabolic state of macrophages by inhibiting the mTORC1 pathway, reducing the accumulation of tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates and oxidative stress. 141 In addition, proteolysis‐targeting chimera technology can reverse the harmful mitochondrial epigenetic reprogramming in macrophages by targeting and degrading METTL4, providing a new strategy for mitochondria‐targeted therapy. 85 Currently, all the above strategies are in the preclinical research stage, and their safety and effectiveness need further verification.

Microbiota‐Based Intervention Strategies

Microbiota‐host interaction has become a new frontier in the treatment of atherosclerosis. Intervention strategies encompass multidimensional approaches, including dietary modification, probiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation, and traditional Chinese medicine.

Dietary intervention is the most basic regulatory means. Supplementing type A proanthocyanidins can reshape the microbiota structure, promote the production of butyrate, and thus improve atherosclerosis indicators. In some animal models, its effect is even better than that of statins. 135

Probiotics and their derivatives show good application prospects. Membrane vesicles derived from Lactobacillus rhamnosus promote macrophage lipid efflux and induce M2 polarization by upregulating the NR1H3‐ABCA1 (nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group H member 3‐ATP‐binding cassette transporter A1) pathway, significantly improving atherosclerosis. 142 Supplementing probiotics with plaque‐stabilizing effects such as Bifidobacterium can also exert therapeutic effects by regulating the gut microbiota. 143

Fecal microbiota transplantation can delay the progression of atherosclerosis in animal models by reshaping the microbiota structure, repairing the intestinal barrier, and regulating the metabolism of short‐chain fatty acids and trimethylamine N‐oxide. 144 However, its clinical application still requires strict safety assessment and more evidence‐based medical evidence. It is worth noting that antibiotic treatment for atherosclerosis is not only ineffective but may also disrupt the microbiota homeostasis, suggesting that a nonspecific antibacterial strategy is not a viable therapeutic approach. 144

Traditional Chinese Medicine

The traditional Chinese medicines, wogonin and neferine, have unique advantages in targeting macrophage glycolysis for the treatment of atherosclerosis. The Wogonin extracted from Scutellaria baicalensis targets the peroxisome proliferator‑activated receptor alpha/Krüppel‑like factor 11/Yes‑associated protein 1 (PPARα/KLF11/YAP1) pathway, inhibiting the glycolysis of macrophages to promote M2 polarization and enhance cholesterol excretion. 145 The neferine present in the center of lotus seeds inhibits the glycolytic rate in macrophages by disrupting the assembly of the eukaryotic translation elongation factor 1 alpha 1/AT‑rich interactive domain‑containing protein 3A/protein kinase C delta (eEF1A1/ARID3A/PKC‐δ) complex, thereby exerting anti‐inflammatory and antiatherosclerosis effects. 40

Itaconate and Its Derivatives

Itaconate, a key metabolite derived from macrophage mitochondria, has been extensively validated for its antiatherosclerotic effects, establishing the targeting of this pathway as a promising therapeutic strategy.

Mechanistically, itaconate, owing to its electrophilic nature, directly modifies cysteine residues in nuclear factor of IκBζ (κ light polypeptide gene enhancer in B‐cells inhibitor ζ), inhibiting its synthesis and cooperatively ATF3 (activating transcription factor 3), thereby blocking the production of proinflammatory cytokines and reprogramming macrophage inflammatory responses. 146 The efflux of itaconate is mediated by the ABCG2 protein on the macrophage membrane, whereas its uptake is facilitated by SLC13A3 (solute carrier family 13 member 3) via a sodium gradient; modulating the activity of these 2 transporters can increase intracellular itaconate levels, thereby exerting antiatherosclerotic effects. 147

In terms of drug development, the direct application of natural itaconate is limited by its negative charge, which hinders cell membrane penetration. The synthetic derivative 4‐OI (4‐octyl itaconate), featuring a lipophilic octyl chain, readily penetrates the cell membrane and undergoes intracellular hydrolysis to release active itaconate, effectively circumventing this limitation. Furthermore, 4‐OI induces 2,3‐dicarboxypropylation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages, reducing IL‐1β secretion. 148 The extracellular vesicles produced by neutrophil‑derived extracellular vesicles (NDEVs) can deliver miR‐27a‐3p to macrophages, inhibiting the expression of succinate‑CoA ligase GDP/ADP‑forming subunit alpha (Suclg1) in macrophages, promoting the accumulation of endogenous itaconate and exerting anti‐inflammatory effects (Figure 5; Table 2).* 1

SUMMARY AND PERSPECTIVES

In summary, this review systematically summarizes the glucose, lipid, and mitochondrial metabolic remodeling occurring in macrophages under the stimulation of the atherosclerosis plaque microenvironment. It details how key metabolites and pathways, including glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, cholesterol metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, microbiota‐host interactions, and itaconate, precisely regulate macrophage polarization, inflammatory activity, and foam cell formation, thereby influencing plaque stability.

However, there are still numerous challenges and knowledge gaps in this field. First, most research on the ACOD1‐itaconate axis relies on gene knockout models. In the future, multiple validations should be performed by combining genetics and pharmacological interventions. Meanwhile, extending the anti‐inflammatory effect of itaconate from in vitro macrophages to in situ tissues of human atherosclerotic plaques and establishing the clinical association between its expression profile and plaque phenotypes are important prerequisites for promoting the translation of this target. Additionally, metabolomics analysis needs to be extended from bone marrow‐derived macrophages to plaque‐resident macrophages to more accurately restore the characteristics of metabolic remodeling in the disease microenvironment.

Second, the traditional M1/M2 binary classification model is no longer sufficient to cover the continuous spectrum characteristics of polarization states in vivo. Future research should shift toward unsupervised clustering and trajectory inference based on multi‐omics data to systematically analyze the metabolic characteristics, functional localization of intermediate‐state macrophages, and the dynamic conversion rules in plaque stability and rupture. Currently, most multiomics studies are based on static sampling at discrete time points, making it difficult to capture the temporal causal relationship between metabolite accumulation and histone modification remodeling. In the future, it is necessary to integrate single‐cell spatial metabolomics, in situ metabolic imaging, and real‐time flux analysis techniques to map the metabolic evolution trajectories of macrophage subsets in different stages of atherosclerosis and different plaque regions at the single‐cell and sub‐cellular resolution.

Third, the existing in vitro restimulation models have revealed the potential mechanism of metabolic regulation of epigenetics. However, whether tissue‐resident macrophages and infiltrating monocytes in the plaque microenvironment have the same trained immunity characteristics and how these characteristics affect foam cell formation and inflammation resolution still need to be further confirmed by lineage tracing and in‐vivo intervention experiments. apolipoprotein E‑deficient (ApoE−/−) and low‑density lipoprotein receptor‑deficient (Ldlr−/− ) mice have inherent limitations in simulating spontaneous rupture of advanced human plaques, thrombus formation, and complex calcified lesions. Developing humanized myeloid chimeric models, plaque organ‐on‐a‐chip, and organoid systems based on human induced pluripotent stem cells will be the key path to bridging the model gap and improving the efficiency of translational research.

Fourth, although the association between specific microbial metabolites and atherosclerosis has been widely reported, the complete molecular chain of metabolic enzymes from specific bacterial strains, host recognition receptors, and downstream macrophage metabolic reprogramming pathways has not been elucidated. In the future, techniques such as synthetic microbiota, germ‐free colonization, and metabolite tracing should be combined to move from correlation to causal mechanism verification.

Furthermore, the trans‐differentiation of smooth muscle cells into macrophage‐like cells and the accompanying metabolic reprogramming are also worthy of in‐depth exploration. Currently, the metabolic characteristics of smooth muscle cell‐derived macrophages mainly come from a few studies on in vitro induction differentiation models and lineage tracing. Their actual metabolic status in the human plaque microenvironment, the functional interaction with monocyte‐derived macrophages, and their specific contributions to plaque stability remain to be clarified. In the future, combined with lineage tracing, single‐cell metabolomics, and spatial transcriptomics techniques, the metabolic evolution pathway of smooth muscle cell‐derived macrophages and their role in the occurrence and development of atherosclerosis should be analyzed.

We believe that interdisciplinary exploration of the mysteries of macrophage metabolic reprogramming will undoubtedly open new avenues for the diagnosis, prevention, and treatment of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases.
