Rapid Enlargement of Vulvar Glomus Tumor After Needle Aspiration and Tumor Base Ligation
Authors: Yukari Nagao, Satoshi Tamauchi, Akira Yokoi, Nobuhisa Yoshikawa, Kaoru Niimi, Hiroaki Kajiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVulvar glomus tumors are rare and typically benign, slow‐growing tumors. Rapid enlargement is uncommon and may suggest malignancy or other atypical conditions. A 58‐year‐old postmenopausal woman noticed a small vulvar nodule. After needle aspiration at another hospital, significant bleeding occurred, requiring tumor base ligation. Although hemostasis was achieved, the mass subsequently continued to enlarge rapidly, prompting referral to our hospital. The lesion continued to enlarge gradually, so surgical excision was therefore performed. Histopathological findings confirmed a glomus tumor with atypical features, including a marked edematous stroma. The rapid enlargement was not attributed to malignancy, a hematoma, or an infection, but was considered to represent apparent growth associated with increased edema secondary to vascular congestion within the tumor. The present case highlights that the rapid enlargement of glomus tumor‐related lesions does not necessarily indicate malignancy.Keywords: glomangiomyoma, glomus tumor, ligation, needle aspiration, vulva

Introduction

Glomus tumors are mesenchymal neoplasms arising from glomus bodies, specialized arteriovenous structures involved in thermoregulation, and account for < 2% of soft tissue tumors [1]. Most glomus tumors develop in areas rich in glomus bodies, such as the subungual regions of the fingers and the deep dermis of the palm, wrist, forearm, and foot [1]. However, they rarely occur anywhere in the skin and soft tissues, including the face [2], lung [3], gastrointestinal tract [4], bladder [5], and even the female genital tract [6, 7, 8]. Glomus tumors are typically small and benign; however, a minority may be classified as malignant based on histological features including deep location, size of > 2 cm, nuclear atypia, necrosis, and increased mitotic activity [9]. In this context, we report a case of a vulvar glomus tumor that rapidly enlarged after needle aspiration and tumor base ligation.

Case

A 58‐year‐old married nulliparous postmenopausal woman with a history of hyperlipidemia and menopausal syndrome presented to our hospital. She had undergone menopause at 55 years old and had no other notable medical history or allergies.

She initially noticed a small vulvar papule and visited a nearby gynecological clinic, where the lesion was suspected to be acne and was therefore observed conservatively. Two months later, she was referred to another hospital for evaluation of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2. At that time, the vulvar lesion persisted, and a fine‐needle aspiration was performed. However, persistent bleeding occurred after the procedure and required ligation of the tumor base under local anesthesia. Thereafter, the lesion enlarged relatively rapidly. One month later, she visited a vascular surgery clinic because a vulvar varicose vein was suspected; however, no evidence of a varicose vein was identified, and she was referred to our hospital.

On examination, an approximately 2‐cm mass was identified in the midline of the vulva (Figure 1a,b). A biopsy was not performed because of the previous bleeding episode following the fine‐needle aspiration. She complained of vulvar discomfort but no severe pain.

Laboratory tests revealed a white blood cell count of 9300/μL, a hemoglobin level of 13.4 g/dL, a platelet count of 322 × 103/μL, a C‐reactive protein level of 0.24 mg/dL, and a lactate dehydrogenase level of 189 U/L. Coagulation tests demonstrated no abnormal findings, and tumor markers were not tested.

Computed tomography revealed a 17‐mm nodule with a low‐density area in the midline of the vulva. The margins of the lesion showed contrast enhancement, whereas the central region demonstrated poor enhancement (Figure 1c). The left vulvar vein adjacent to the lesion was mildly dilated, suggesting a potential connection to the tumor (Figure 1d). No other abnormal findings were identified.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a well‐circumscribed superficial vulvar nodule measuring approximately 2 cm without invasion into the surrounding tissues. The lesion was hypointense on T1‐weighted imaging (WI) and heterogeneously hyperintense on T2WI, with high signal intensity on the ADC map (Figure 1e–g). Marked contrast enhancement was observed on contrast‐enhanced T1WI (Figure 1h).

Approximately 1.5 months after her first visit to our hospital, she underwent surgical excision under spinal subarachnoid anesthesia because the tumor had rapidly enlarged, the possibility of malignancy could not be ruled out clinically, and the lesion caused discomfort.

The tumor, which had enlarged to > 3 cm, was located slightly left of the midline, with stretched normal skin forming a stalk‐like structure (Figure 2a,b). A circumferential incision was made in the normal skin to expose the stalk containing the feeding vessels, and the tumor was removed by ligating and dissecting the stalk. The normal skin was then closed with single‐layer sutures (Figure 2c).

Legend: Intraoperative and postoperative vulvar findings (a, b) The tumor located slightly left of the vulvar midline, enlarged to > 3 cm, with stretched normal skin forming a stalk‐like structure. (c) Vulvar findings at completion of surgery.

The surgery was completed in 20 min with minimal blood loss and without notable complications. The patient was discharged on the day after surgery.

The macroscopic appearance is shown in Figure 3a. The mass was a well‐circumscribed, solid, ovoid nodule. Histopathological findings are shown in Figure 3b–e. Oval to short spindle‐shaped tumor cells were aggregated around vascular channels. The tumor exhibited an abundant edematous stroma with focal myxoid‐like changes. No necrosis or increased mitotic activity was observed. Immunohistochemical staining results were as follows: cytokeratin AE1/AE3, negative; S‐100, negative; CD34, negative; CD31, negative; alpha‐smooth muscle actin, positive (Figure 3f); desmin, negative; estrogen receptor, negative; and progesterone receptor, negative. Based on the above histopathological findings, the tumor was diagnosed as a glomus tumor, specifically glomangiomyoma. The surgical margins were negative.

The patient was followed up at approximately 3 weeks and 3 months postoperatively, with no evidence of wound complications or recurrence.

Discussion

Glomus tumors are small benign mesenchymal neoplasms that most commonly arise in the extremities [1]. They are subclassified into solid glomus tumors (75%), glomangiomas with prominent vascular components (20%), and glomangiomyomas with prominent vascular and smooth muscle components (5%) [1]. The present case was diagnosed as a glomangiomyoma.

Although glomus tumors may theoretically occur anywhere in the skin or soft tissues, they are rarely reported in the vulva [10, 11, 12, 13], including cases in the clitoral [7, 14, 15] or periurethral areas [14, 16, 17]. To our knowledge, no vulvar glomus tumor showing rapid enlargement, as observed in the present case, has been previously reported.

A previous report described a benign glomus tumor of the wrist that enlarged several months after a biopsy; however, the underlying mechanism was not discussed [18]. In the present case, the rapid enlargement raised concern for malignancy, a hematoma, an infection, or specific histological subtype. Histopathological findings demonstrated benign features with low proliferative activity and no evidence of necrosis. No definitive findings suggestive of a hematoma or infection‐related inflammation were identified. However, a prominent edematous stroma with focal myxoid‐like changes was observed.

In this case, uncontrollable bleeding occurred after a fine‐needle aspiration of a small vulvar lesion, and ligation of the tumor base was subsequently performed for hemostasis. Thereafter, the lesion enlarged rapidly and continued to increase gradually over the following months. We speculate that this unusual clinical course may have been associated with hemodynamic changes within the tumor.

Glomus tumors are highly vascular neoplasms. Compared with arteries, veins constitute a low‐pressure, high‐compliance system because arteries are thicker, more muscular, and less distensible [19, 20, 21]. This structural difference makes veins more prone to collapse or outflow obstruction. Therefore, in the present case, tumor base ligation may have impaired venous outflow while partially preserving arterial inflow. Consequently, congestion within the intratumoral vessels likely increased intravascular pressure, resulting in plasma leakage into the stroma and subsequent edema formation [22]. Thus, although the proliferation of tumor cells was not particularly rapid, the lesion may have shown an apparent rapid enlargement because of stromal edema rather than true tumor growth.

Additionally, it is possible that a hematoma formation following the fine‐needle aspiration contributed to the enlargement but was subsequently absorbed before surgery, and it was therefore not identified histopathologically [23].

In conclusion, the rapid enlargement observed after tumor manipulation in this case was likely associated with stromal edema caused by intratumoral vascular congestion rather than true tumor proliferation. This case highlights that the rapid enlargement of glomus tumor‐related lesions does not necessarily indicate malignancy and may instead reflect hemodynamic changes within the tumor.
