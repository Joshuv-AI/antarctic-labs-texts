Beyond Traditional Risk Factors: The Role of Mental Health and Intersectionality in Cardiovascular Disease Risk Prediction Models
Authors: George Collett, Ajay K Gupta
Published: 2026

Since the early 2000s, various guidelines encouraged the use of cardiovascular risk prediction calculators (eg, Framingham Risk Score, QRESEARCH Risk Estimator 3 [QRISK3], Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation [SCORE]) to help identify individuals at high risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and inform clinical decision‐making. Most traditional cardiovascular risk calculators incorporate established risk factors such as age, cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, and lifestyle behaviors including smoking. More recently, models such as the American Heart Association's Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease Events (PREVENT) calculator have evolved to include additional determinants, notably kidney function and glycemic control. 1 Despite methodological differences, these calculators are pivotal guidance tools for implementing preventive interventions to reduce CVD risk, the leading cause of death worldwide. 2

Beyond conventional risk factors, there is growing recognition of the role of mental health in influencing the risk of CVD. Although the causal role of mental disorders on CVD is not yet fully accepted, 3 common mental health disorders, such as depression and anxiety, are consistently associated an increased risk of incident CVD. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Similar findings, although less frequently, have been reported for severe mental disorders such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. 8 Several overlapping mechanisms have been proposed to underpin the mental health and CVD link, encompassing both biological and behavioral pathways. The physiological factors influencing the relationship includes the presence of a dysregulated hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, systemic inflammation, autonomic dysfunction, and consequent vascular dysfunction. The influential lifestyle behavior factors that may mediate the mental health–CVD link include smoking, worsened diet, and sedentary lifestyle. 9 Furthermore, individuals living with mental illness often encounter systemic barriers to effective CVD prevention and management, including reduced engagement with screening, poorer treatment adherence, reduced self‐care, and social isolation, 10 all of which may lead to excess CVD mortality.

Despite this, information on mental health is largely absent from most CVD risk calculators, 10 , 11 except for QRISK3, which includes severe mental illness as a broad category. As such, CVD risk may be underestimated among those with mental disorders. 12 However, an important question remains: Given that many existing models include factors such as smoking status, cholesterol ratio, systolic blood pressure, and body mass index, which are all correlated with mental disorders and collectively explain a large proportion of CVD risk, does the inclusion of mental health condition improve model prediction?

Previous reviews on the efficacy and usefulness of currently available CVD risk prediction models are informative, but mental health has received relatively little attention. One review made no mention of mental health, 13 whereas another focused specifically on the application to those with psychotic disorders. 14 In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Siddiqi and colleagues address this important gap by conducting a comprehensive systematic review of all cohort studies detailing model development, validation, or recalibration of CVD risk prediction models that include mental disorders as prognostic factors. 15 Their search is broad, extending to vascular cognitive impairment and vascular dementia, and without limiting to specific mental health conditions.

Across 31 unique models, depression and/or anxiety were most commonly included as covariates, with fewer models including schizophrenia, bipolar, psychosis, or other mood disorders, or severe mental illness as a broad entity. Some, but few, models incorporated pharmacological treatment for mental disorders and substance/tobacco use disorder as covariates.

Unsurprisingly, mental health indicators were often significantly associated with an increased risk of CVD outcomes in these models. Although improvement in the performance metrics across most of these models was generally moderate, several exhibited enhanced predictive accuracy, but some reported inconsistencies. Notably, in almost all of these models, the predicted outcome was an iteration of a composite CVD outcome, with comparatively few evaluating cognitive outcomes such as vascular dementia and poststroke cognitive impairment. This represents an important gap, particularly given the well‐established links between adverse mental health and cognitive outcomes even in the absence of overt cerebrovascular events such as stroke. 16 , 17 CVD and cognitive impairment share similar vascular pathways, and large cohort studies, such as the Framingham Heart Study, the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) study, and the ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities) study incorporate cognitive outcomes reflecting that, but their integration into CVD risk prediction models remains limited. This raises the question of whether risk prediction models that consider mental health would be improved by expanding their focus to include both cardiovascular and cognitive end points, thus providing a more comprehensive assessment of vascular risk.

In addition to mental health indicators, Siddiqi and colleagues explore whether these models consider sociodemographic/intersectional identities. Although characteristics such as age, sex, and ethnicity are individually associated with CVD risk, intersectionality describes how combinations of these characteristics shape unique risk profiles for CVD. 18 For example, in the United States, Black women have a disproportionately higher burden of CVD compared with women of other racial and ethnic groups, 19 and there is also evidence of racial differences in cardiometabolic risk among sexual minority women. 20 Despite this, only around a third of the included studies incorporated any intersectional analysis, and those that did commonly examined intersections of sex with ethnicity, education, or deprivation.

Several important directions for future research are borne out of this study. First, the limited inclusion of mental health variables in risk prediction models suggests that the risk of CVD together with poststroke cognitive impairment and vascular dementia may be underestimated, especially among those with mental disorders. Future studies should consider a broad range of mental health indicators (given the predominance of depression and/or anxiety as covariates) to identify the incident risk across both CVD and cognitive outcomes. Second, intersectional factors were generally limited to sex‐ or gender‐based interactions. None evaluated race or ethnicity as effect modifier variables, and other important determinants, such as socioeconomic status, deprivation index, and rural/urban residence, were not often considered despite their known influence on CVD risk. 21 Importantly, these factors do not operate in a vacuum, and individuals often have multiple and overlapping intersectional identities that may interact to shape CVD risk in complex ways. Although challenging, future studies may wish to explore the impact of compounded intersectional effects, rather than isolated intersections (eg, sex–race), on CVD risk. In that respect, the increasing use of machine‐learning algorithms and artificial intelligence models may help overcome these complexities. Third, intersectionality is not a fixed risk across different countries and cultural contexts. Most model studies were conducted in the United States, followed by Europe, and the elevated risk associated with sex and ethnicity in the United States may not translate directly to other regions where patterns of inequality and access to care and medical insurance likely differ. As such, greater emphasis on external validation across diverse populations will be highly valuable to assess the generalizability of these models.

In summary, the work of Siddiqi and colleagues provides a timely and important step toward advancing cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk prediction. Although incorporating mental health indicators and intersectionality offers promise, key questions remain on the predictive usefulness of mental disorders in a broad sense and the generalizability of these models across settings. Addressing these gaps is essential to ultimately improving accuracy and risk stratification, and avoiding systemic risk underestimation. The ongoing challenge for researchers is now to find evidence to better inform prevention and treatment strategies for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease.
