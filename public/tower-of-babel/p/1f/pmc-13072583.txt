The effect of fatigue on peak strain of hamstring muscles in dominant and non-dominant sides during sprinting: a pre-post intervention study
Authors: Yuduo Liu, Qiujie Li, Chen Yang, Xianglin Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough fatigue is widely recognized as a risk factor for hamstring strain injuries, the mechanisms by which fatigue influences these injuries remain unclear. It is also uncertain whether fatigue differentially affects the risk factors for hamstring strain between lower limbs.MethodsNineteen male track-and-field athletes underwent a fatigue intervention, with optimal muscle length assessed pre- and post-fatigue. Key biomechanical parameters during sprinting were also analyzed before and after fatigue.ResultsPost-fatigue, the non-dominant limb showed significant reductions in optimal lengths across all three biarticular hamstring muscles (p < 0.01; Cohen’s d = 0.95–1.07), with greater changes than the dominant limb (p < 0.05; = 0.16–0.24). No significant differences were observed in maximal hip–knee angles, peak muscle lengths, or the timing of peak muscle strain (p > 0.05). However, the non-dominant limb exhibited increased peak strain in the long head of the biceps femoris and semimembranosus (p < 0.05; Cohen’s d = 0.46–0.52), but not in the semitendinosus (p = 0.06; Cohen’s d = 0.90). No significant inter-limb differences were observed in the strain changes of the hamstring biarticular muscles (p > 0.05).ConclusionsFatigue was not associated with changes in sprint-phase peak lengths of the non-dominant biarticular hamstring muscles; however, it was accompanied by a reduction in optimal length, which may have increased peak strain. Clinically, monitoring fatigue-related changes in hamstring functional properties and addressing potential inter-limb asymmetries may be relevant for injury prevention and rehabilitation. The same fatigue protocol was not associated with increased peak strain on the dominant side, indicating a potential limb-specific susceptibility to fatigue-related strain increases.Keywords: Strain injury, Risk factors, Optimal length

Background

Hamstring strain injuries (HSIs) are the most common injuries among sports involving rapid running, jumping, and kicking, such as track and field, soccer, and rugby, accounting for 12%-26% of all sports-related injuries [1]. Although the majority of hamstring injuries do not require surgical intervention, the resulting time loss and increased recurrence rates can have a significant impact on athletes. During the sports season, HSIs account for the greatest number of missed days, representing 25% of all injury-related absences [2]. After returning to competition, athletes face a high risk of re-injury, with recurrence rates ranging from 22% to 34% [3]. Therefore, effectively preventing HSIs is crucial for enhancing athletic performance.

Identifying the injury mechanisms and risk factors is a prerequisite for preventing HSIs and enhancing post-injury rehabilitation outcomes. HSIs are considered multifactorial, with both modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors, including muscle flexibility, strength imbalance, previous injury, and fatigue [4]. Among these, fatigue has been recognized as an important modifiable risk factor for HSIs [5], but the relationship between fatigue and the mechanisms of HSIs remains unclear. An epidemiological study showed that HSIs increased toward the latter stages of each half during professional football matches [6], and evidence from another study indicated that more than a quarter (26%) occurred in the last 15 min of the match [7]. This higher incidence of injury may be related to fatigue-induced alterations in neuromuscular control, including changes in muscle activation timing, such as earlier onset and prolonged duration of hamstring EMG activity under fatigue [8], as well as reductions in force-generating capacity [9].

The direct injury mechanism of hamstring strain is excessive muscle strain. Animal experiments have demonstrated that strains primarily occur when the muscle is in a lengthened state, and the injury is associated with muscle strain, rather than muscle contraction force and velocity [10, 11]. One study showed that after hamstring fatigue, the knee flexion angle at maximal knee flexion torque increased, indicating a potential reduction in the optimal muscle length [12]. Since muscle strain is defined as the ratio of muscle deformation to its optimal length, the peak strain of the hamstring muscles during sprinting may increase due to fatigue, thereby increasing the risk of strain. However, one previous study [13] revealed that the hamstring muscle length, at the time of maximal knee flexion torque, differed from its optimal length. Therefore, whether fatigue affects the optimal length of the hamstring muscles remains unclear. On the other hand, fatigue may alter the absolute length of the hamstring muscles during movement, thereby influencing the peak strain. One study has indicated that during sprinting following fatigue, the maximum sum of hip flexion angle and knee extension angle decreased, and stride length was reduced [14], implying a potential reduction in the peak absolute length of the hamstring muscles. However, other studies showed decreased hip flexion angles [8] and increased maximal pelvic anterior tilt angles during fatigued sprinting [14], indicating that the absolute length may increase. These indirect kinematic pieces of evidence and conflicting results leave the question of how fatigue affects the absolute length of the hamstring muscles during sprinting unresolved.

Limb dominance may influence the incidence of HSIs, though the exact mechanisms remain unclear. A 7-year epidemiological study of Australian Football League athletes reported that 52.6% of hamstring strains occurred in the dominant limb (DL, the preferred kicking leg) and 47.4% in the non-dominant limb (NDL) [15]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis investigating the relationship between limb dominance and football-related injuries demonstrated that hamstring strains are more likely to occur in the DL [16]. Notably, these injuries are not limited to kicking maneuvers where the DL is preferentially engaged, but can also manifest during running activities [16]. It can be inferred from this phenomenon that beyond differences in movement frequency, variations in movement patterns and physiological structural characteristics between the limbs may contribute to injury risk disparities. However, it remains unclear whether peak hamstring strain differs between limbs in response to fatigue.

In summary, although fatigue is widely recognized as a risk factor for HSIs, the mechanisms by which fatigue influences HSIs remain unclear. It is also uncertain whether fatigue differentially affects the risk factors for hamstring strain between the dominant and non-dominant limbs. Consequently, the primary aims of this study were to (a) determine the effect of fatigue on the peak strain of the hamstring muscles, and (b) compare bilateral differences in fatigue-induced changes between the lower limbs. The following hypotheses were proposed in this study: Hypothesis 1: Following fatigue, the optimal length of the hamstring biarticular muscles would decrease, with a greater reduction in the DL than in the NDL. Hypothesis 2: After fatigue, the peak length of the hamstring biarticular muscles during sprinting would increase, with a larger increase in the DL. Hypothesis 3: Fatigue would increase peak strain of the hamstring biarticular muscles during sprinting, and the increase would be more pronounced in the DL.

Methods

Participants

Nineteen male athletes (18–25 years old) participated in this study. All participants had professional track and field training experience, defined as athletes who had competed at a high national level, with a personal best 100-m sprint time of 11.74 s or faster. All participants had no history of HSIs in the past 2 years and had no significant lower limb or foot injuries within the preceding 6 months. The participants’ anthropometric measurements were as follows: height 1.78 ± 0.04 m, body mass 71.4 ± 6.8 kg, and age 19.2 ± 0.9 years. The preferred leg for ball striking was defined as the dominant leg [17]. All participants provided informed consent. This study was approved by the Sports Science Experiment Ethics Committee of Beijing Sport University (No.2023102 H) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Procedures

One week prior to formal testing, VO2max was assessed to establish baseline values for prescribing the target exercise intensities (55% and 95% VO2max) used in the fatigue protocol. All other tests were completed during a single laboratory visit. The experimental session lasted approximately 2.5 h and included warm-up, instrumentation, implementation of the fatigue protocol, and pre- and post-fatigue assessments.

VO2max test

VO2max and the running speed associated with VO2max were determined using a two-phase incremental treadmill test. Phase 1 consisted of a 12-min submaximal incremental treadmill test that started at 2.33 m/s (8.4 km/h) at 0% grade; speed was increased by 0.67 m/s (2.4 km/h) every 3 min until 4.33 m/s (15.6 km/h), and rating of perceived exertion (RPE) was recorded at each stage. After 10 min of seated recovery, Phase 2 consisted of a graded incline protocol performed at a constant speed corresponding to an RPE of 13 in Phase 1; treadmill grade was set at 0% and was increased by 3% every 2 min until volitional exhaustion. This graded incline protocol was used to elicit VO2max without requiring excessively high running speeds. The VO2max value obtained in Phase 2 was combined with the VO₂–speed relationship derived from Phase 1 to estimate each participant’s individualized VO2max-associated running speed. Target speeds for the modified Loughborough Intermittent Shuttle Test (LIST) were then calculated as 55% and 95% of this speed for each participant.

Sprint test

Participants wore standardized compression garments with 21 retroreflective markers affixed to anatomical landmarks, including bilateral lower extremities: anterior superior iliac spine, iliac crest apex, anterior thigh, lateral/medial femoral condyles, tibial tuberosity, lateral/medial malleoli, the middle of the 2nd and the 3rd metatarsals, and calcaneus. An additional marker was attached at the junction of the 4th and 5th lumbar spine vertebra (L4 and L5).

The 40-meter sprint protocol required participants to assume a standing start position 20 m anterior to the force platform array. Upon auditory cue, participants sprinted maximally through the capture volume. A trial was considered valid if the participant maintained a natural gait without stride adjustments and landed the test foot on the force platform. The order of limb testing (DL vs. NDL) was randomized (the first tested limb was counterbalanced across participants) to minimize potential order effects. Each leg completed 3 valid trials with 60-second rest intervals between attempts. All participants completed 3 valid trials for each leg, resulting in a total of 6 trials for each participant. For each trial, participants were instructed to contact the force plate with the tested limb. Trials were repeated if the tested limb did not fully contact the force plate or otherwise failed to meet the validity criteria, until three valid trials were obtained for each leg. Three-dimensional kinematic data and ground reaction forces were synchronously collected during each trial.

Isokinetic strength assessment

Following sprint testing, a static standing calibration was performed to collect three-dimensional coordinates of all markers, then the lumbar marker was removed. The specific settings of the isokinetic dynamometer have been described in detail in a previous study [18]. Participants were seated on an isokinetic dynamometer with the hip flexed at 90°, and concentric knee flexion torque was measured at a slow angular velocity of 10°/s [13]. Synchronized data collection included isokinetic concentric knee flexion torque and retroreflective marker coordinates. Prior to the maximal voluntary contractions (MVCs), participants performed a standardized warm-up consisting of 2 submaximal contractions (approximately 70% perceived effort). Subsequently, three MVCs were performed for each limb. The highest MVC value was used for analysis. The order of limb testing was randomized for each participant to minimize potential order effects.

Fatigue protocol

The fatigue protocol was based on a modified LIST on a level surface (0% grade) [19]. Prior to testing, participants completed standardized warm-up procedures [19]. After the warm-up, participants completed two phases separated by a 3-min intermission: Part A (50 min) and Part B (~ 10 min). Part A comprised cyclic patterns: 3 × 20 m walking at 1.67 m/s, 1 × 20 m sprint, 4-second recovery, 3 × 20 m running at 55% VO2max speed, 3 × 20 m running at 95% VO2max speed. Each 15-minute exercise block repeated this sequence 10 times, separated by 3-minute intervals. Part B required alternating 20 m runs at 55% and 95% VO2max speeds until participants failed to maintain target speeds in 2 consecutive trials. Data collection

VO2max test

Gas exchange data were collected using a portable metabolic system (Cosmed K4b², Italy) during both the submaximal incremental running tests and graded incline running trials.

Sprint test

An 8-camera infrared motion capture system (Qualisys Arqus A12, Sweden; sampling frequency 200 Hz) and force platforms (Kistler 9287CA, Switzerland; sampling frequency 1000 Hz) were synchronously applied to collect retroreflective marker coordinates and ground reaction forces during sprint trials. Running velocities during the fatigue protocol were monitored using a timing gate system (SmartSpeed, Australia).

Isokinetic strength assessment and lower extremity coordinates of retroreflective markers data collection

An isokinetic dynamometer (Isomed 2000, Germany; sampling frequency 100 Hz) was used to acquire isokinetic concentric knee flexion torque at 10°/s. A 14-camera infrared motion capture system (Qualisys Oqus 400, Sweden; sampling frequency 200 Hz) was synchronously employed to collect coordinates of retroreflective markers.

Data reduction

Calculation of exercise intensity

The mean oxygen uptake (VO2) during the final 1-minute period preceding volitional exhaustion in graded incline running trials was calculated as VO2max. A linear regression between average VO2 and running speed at each stage of the submaximal incremental running tests was established, whereby the regression equation predicted relative exercise intensities (55% VO2max and 95% VO2max) during fatigue protocols [20].

Computation of optimal lengths in biarticular hamstrings

Three-dimensional coordinates of all surface markers were smoothed using a Butterworth low-pass filter with a 13.3 Hz cutoff frequency [21]. Marker trajectories were optimized via inverse kinematics [22] to minimize measurement errors. Based on the marker coordinates, segment coordinate systems were defined for the pelvis, thigh, shank, and foot of each lower limb.

Instantaneous muscle force was calculated from instantaneous knee flexion torque, muscle physiological cross-sectional area, and moment arms, with computational details described in prior research [18]. Muscle length was defined as the distance between origin and insertion points, normalized to ipsilateral thigh length (hip-to-knee joint center distance). Maximal hamstring strength was determined as the peak value from three maximal trials, with optimal muscle length (L0) corresponding to peak force production.

Peak strain calculation of biarticular hamstrings during sprinting

A running gait cycle was defined as the period from the initial contact of one foot on the force platform to the subsequent contact of the same foot. The initial contact moment was determined when the vertical ground reaction force recorded by the force platform first exceeded 20 N. The length (L) of each biarticular hamstring muscle during sprinting was calculated using the same procedure as that used to determine the L0, as described above. Based on the pre-determined L0, the muscle strain (ε) during sprinting was computed as ε = (L- L0)/ L0, and the maximal strain (εmax) was derived from this calculation. The timing of peak hamstring muscle strain during sprinting was normalized to the running gait cycle (0–100%).

Hip-knee angle computation during sprinting

Euler angles method was used to calculate the hip flexion and knee flexion angles [23]. The maximal hip-knee angle during sprinting was defined as the peak value of the sum of hip flexion and supplementary angles of knee flexion within a gait cycle.

Measurement validity and reliability

The reliability of modelling-derived hamstring optimal muscle length and peak muscle strain has been reported previously using between-participant coefficients of multiple correlation (CMC) and within-participant relative error. Using a comparable methodology, Wan et al. reported high waveform similarity (CMC = 0.84–0.90 for estimated optimal musculotendinous lengths and 0.96–0.99 for peak musculotendinous strains) and low within-participant relative errors (≤ 1.76% and ≤ 1.03%, respectively) [24].

A systematic review of marker-based 3D kinematic analyses reported that typical measurement error estimates for sagittal-plane lower-limb joint angles were generally < 5° [25]. Additionally, test–retest reliability of conventional gait models for registering hip, knee, and ankle joint angles has been reported as good in healthy participants [26]. These previously reported metrics were provided to support the reliability of the modelling outputs used in the present study.

Statistical analyses

Paired-sample t-tests were used to compare pre-fatigue and post-fatigue conditions within the DL and NDL, as well as to examine differences in maximal hamstring strength between the DL and NDL under both pre-fatigue and post-fatigue conditions. P values were adjusted using the Bonferroni method to control for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes for paired comparisons were quantified using Cohen’s d, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively [27].

For between-group comparisons, a 2 (side: DL, NDL) × 2 (time: baseline, post-fatigue) repeated measures ANCOVA was performed on the trial outcomes, using baseline hamstring strength as a covariate. Partial eta squared () was used to determine the effect sizes for the ANCOVA, with values of 0.02, 0.06, and 0.14 corresponding to small, medium, and large effects. All analyses were conducted using SPSS 21.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and statistical significance was defined as a Type I error probability of α ≤ 0.05.

Discussion

Despite extensive work on sprint-related hamstring biomechanics, evidence remained limited regarding how acute fatigue modified the behavior of the biarticular hamstrings during sprinting and whether these responses differed between the dominant and non-dominant limbs. This information was needed to better interpret fatigue-related biomechanical alterations that might contribute to injury risk. Therefore, this study aimed to quantify the effects of fatigue on hamstring optimal muscle length, peak muscle length, and peak strain during sprinting, and to compare fatigue-induced changes between limbs. In the present study, fatigue did not affect peak length of the non-dominant biarticular hamstring muscles during sprinting; however, it reduced optimal muscle length, which in turn increased peak strain.

Fatigue is a multifactorial phenomenon defined as the acute post-exercise loss of force-generating capacity [28]. In the present study, maximal hamstring strength decreased following the fatigue protocol, indicating that the protocol successfully induced fatigue.

The results of this study showed that the optimal lengths of the biceps femoris long head, semimembranosus, and semitendinosus in the NDL were significantly reduced post-fatigue, whereas no statistically significant changes were observed in the corresponding muscles of the dominant limb DL. The magnitude of post-fatigue changes in the NDL biarticular hamstrings was greater than that in the DL. These findings partially support Hypothesis 1: the predicted post-fatigue reductions in optimal length were observed in the NDL, but not in the DL, and the predicted larger reductions in the DL were not supported. Although neuromuscular activity was not directly assessed in the present study, previous literature suggests that fatigue may induce central nervous system adaptations, alterations in cross-bridge kinetics, and changes in muscle activation patterns, which may help explain the observed biomechanical responses [29, 30]. Muscle fatigue involves adaptive mechanisms where the central nervous system increases motor unit recruitment and elevates muscle activation to compensate for force loss [29]. Previous research indicates that muscle contraction can induce strain in series compliant elements (e.g., tendons) as muscle fibers shorten [30]. This strain may disrupt actin-myosin cross-bridge formation in new overlap zones, reducing cross-bridge binding probability—termed “strain-induced inhibition of cross-bridge binding”. Such strain dependence may limit cross-bridge formation during force redevelopment after shortening, a phenomenon referred to as “force depression”. Increased muscle activation may exacerbate strain-induced inhibition of cross-bridge binding, impairing force production at pre-fatigue optimal lengths and thereby reducing the optimal working length. Collectively, these mechanisms suggest that fatigue may alter optimal length by modulating muscle activation. Consistent with this interpretation, our results demonstrated post-fatigue reductions in the NDL hamstring optimal lengths. From a biomechanical perspective, muscle strain is defined as the ratio of muscle length deformation to optimal muscle length [31]. Therefore, a shorter optimal length would result in greater strain for a given deformation. In this context, fatigue-induced reductions in optimal length of the NDL biarticular hamstrings may increase mechanical strain during sprinting and potentially elevate the risk of hamstring strain injury. Dominant and non-dominant limbs may exhibit differential fatigue responses. Previous research has shown faster declines in the median frequency of EMG signals in the NDL during fatiguing tasks [32], suggesting that the NDL may fatigue more rapidly. In addition, the DL has been reported to have a higher proportion of type I muscle fibers [33], which generally exhibit greater fatigue tolerance than type II fibers [34]. Differences in fiber-type composition between limbs may therefore contribute to differential responses to fatiguing tasks [35], potentially explaining the observed interlimb discrepancy in the magnitude of post-fatigue changes in hamstring optimal length.

This study found no statistically significant pre- to post-fatigue differences in maximal hip-knee angles or peak lengths of the biarticular hamstrings during sprinting, nor any interlimb differences in these parameters. Therefore, Hypothesis 2 was not supported. The primary mechanical basis of hamstring strain injury involves excessive muscle strain [10] commonly defined as the change in muscle length relative to its optimal length. Accordingly, when optimal length remains unchanged, an increase in absolute muscle length would result in greater muscle strain [31], with potential implications for injury risk. The hip-knee angle, defined as the sum of hip flexion and the supplementary angle of knee flexion, captures lower-limb kinematics that may modulate hamstring length. Increases in hip flexion and/or knee extension could mechanically elongate the hamstrings, thereby increasing strain. However, the literature reports mixed findings regarding fatigue-related changes in hip-knee angle: Pinniger et al. [8] reported increased post-fatigue hip-knee angles associated with hamstring elongation, whereas Small et al. [14] observed decreased angles, and Wilmes et al. [36] found no significant changes. Our results were consistent with those of Wilmes et al. [36], showing preserved peak hamstring lengths and hip-knee angles during fatigued sprinting. This kinematic consistency may be attributed to minimal alterations in movement patterns and joint ranges of motion post-fatigue. Notably, trained runners have been reported to maintain more stable movement mechanics under fatigue than novice runners [37]. Given that our participants were well-trained track-and-field sprinters with extensive competitive experience, this characteristic may partly explain the absence of significant kinematic changes despite fatigue exposure.

The present results showed no statistically significant pre- to post-fatigue changes in peak strain of the biarticular hamstrings in the DL. Following fatigue, peak strain of the biceps femoris long head and semimembranosus in the NDL increased. Although the change in semitendinosus peak strain in the NDL did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.060), the effect size was large (Cohen’s d = 0.898), suggesting a potentially meaningful increase. These findings partially support Hypothesis 3: fatigue-related increases in peak strain were observed in the NDL, whereas the predicted increase in the DL and the predicted larger changes in the DL were not supported. Integrating these results, fatigue was associated with a reduction in optimal length of the NDL biarticular hamstrings while peak absolute length remained unchanged, which likely contributed to the observed increase in peak strain. In contrast, neither optimal length nor peak absolute length changed in the DL, and peak strain remained stable. Because muscle strain magnitude is considered a primary mechanical determinant of strain-injury risk [10], the post-fatigue increase in NDL peak strain may imply an elevated mechanical risk profile.

Notably, baseline muscle strength appeared to account for part of the between-limb difference in fatigue responses. In the unadjusted analysis, peak strain increased in the NDL after fatigue but did not change in the DL; however, when baseline strength was included as a covariate, the between-limb difference in strain change was no longer statistically significant. This suggests that the larger post-fatigue strain response in the NDL may be related to its lower baseline strength, which could impose a higher relative physiological load during the fatigue task. Consistent with this interpretation, baseline strength was significantly greater in the DL than in the NDL, which may have enabled the DL to perform the protocol at a lower relative intensity and thereby attenuate fatigue-related alterations [38]. Conversely, the weaker baseline strength of the NDL may have necessitated higher relative loading, contributing to a more pronounced post-fatigue increase in strain. These findings highlight that, in rehabilitation and conditioning, addressing interlimb strength asymmetry may be particularly important. Future studies should directly quantify relative loading during fatigue protocols to further test this mechanism.

This study also revealed that the peak strain of the biarticular hamstring muscles during fatigued sprinting consistently occurred at the late swing phase (between 85 and 95% of the gait cycle) [39], consistent with previous findings under non-fatigued conditions [40]. During late swing, the hamstrings reach peak strain and undergo eccentric action prior to foot strike, a phase associated with the greatest risk of hamstring strain injury. Notably, fatigue did not alter the timing of peak strain during sprinting, suggesting that participants maintained relatively stable sprint kinematics under fatigue.

This study has several limitations. First, the fatigue protocol used in this study may not fully reflect fatigue experienced during real-world training or match play. Although fatigue was experimentally simulated, differences in fatigue accumulation, intensity patterns, and exposure duration in real settings could influence hamstring strain responses. Second, our analysis focused primarily on biomechanical changes at the muscle-tendon unit level, without concurrently assessing neuromuscular mechanisms or fascicle-level behavior. Future studies could incorporate ultrasonography to provide a more detailed physiological explanation for the observed strain responses. Third, the sample size was relatively small, which may have limited statistical power to detect subtle between-condition differences (estimated post-hoc power ≤ 0.64). Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Finally, only male participants were included, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to females. Given that the incidence of hamstring strain injuries varies by sex Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting these findings. Finally, the study included only male participants, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to females. Given that the incidence of hamstring strain injuries differs between sexes [41], future research should include both male and female participants to examine potential sex-based differences in fatigue-related changes in hamstring strain and injury risk.

Conclusions

Fatigue was not associated with changes in sprint-phase peak lengths of the non-dominant biarticular hamstring muscles; however, it was accompanied by a reduction in optimal length, which may have increased peak strain. These findings suggest that fatigue-related increases in hamstring strain during sprinting may occur through shifts in the muscle’s operating length-strain behavior rather than through changes in peak muscle length alone. Clinically, monitoring fatigue-related changes in hamstring functional properties and addressing potential inter-limb asymmetries may be relevant for injury prevention and rehabilitation. Notably, the same fatigue protocol was not associated with increased peak strain on the dominant side, indicating a potential limb-specific susceptibility to fatigue-related strain increases.
