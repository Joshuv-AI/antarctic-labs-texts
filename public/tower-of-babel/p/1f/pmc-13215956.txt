Cepharanthine hydrochloride alleviates herpesvirus encephalitis by inhibiting Nrf2 degradation
Authors: Qiongzhen Zeng, Weixiangmin Zou, Jiaqi Wu, Menghe Li, Zexu Wang, Caiwenjie La, Cuifang Ye, Zhe Ren, Yifei Wang, Kai Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHerpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) infection can induce herpes simplex encephalitis (HSE), a life-threatening neurological disorder characterized by active viral replication within the central nervous system accompanied by excessive neuroinflammatory responses. Cepharanthine hydrochloride (CH) is a natural bisbenzylisoquinoline alkaloid with diverse pharmacological activities. CH was evaluated for its antiviral and anti-inflammatory activities against HSV-1 infection. In microglia, CH markedly inhibited viral replication and suppressed STING-NF-κB signaling, thereby reducing HSV-1-associated neuroinflammation. During HSV-1 infection, CH binds to Nrf2, disrupting Keap1-Nrf2 interaction and subsequent ubiquitination. This hindered Nrf2 degradation and attenuated STING activation. In an HSE mouse model, CH significantly reduced viral loads in brain tissue, improved survival rates, prevented weight loss, and alleviated neurological symptoms. Furthermore, CH promoted the accumulation of Nrf2 and inhibited the STING-NF-κB signaling pathway in vivo. Collectively, these results indicate that CH represents a potential antiviral strategy for HSE.Keywords: Herpes simplex encephalitis (HSE), Neuroinflammation, Cepharanthine hydrochloride (CH), Nrf2, STING-NF-κB pathway

Introduction

Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), a neurotropic double-stranded DNA virus, can access the central nervous system (CNS) through peripheral nerves and cause herpes simplex encephalitis (HSE) (Piret and Boivin, 2020; Zhang and Casanova, 2024). HSE is associated with high mortality and considerable morbidity, particularly in immunocompromised individuals, and is frequently accompanied by severe neuroinflammation and neurological impairment (Marcocci et al., 2020; Piret and Boivin, 2020). Current antiviral therapies, including acyclovir (ACV) and ganciclovir, primarily suppress viral replication but fail to effectively alleviate HSV-1-induced neuroinflammatory responses (Bradshaw and Venkatesan, 2016). Furthermore, most HSE survivors experience long-term neurological sequelae. Consequently, the development of novel therapeutic approaches aimed at modulating neuroinflammation is urgently required.

Microglia serve as the resident innate immune sentinels of the CNS and are essential for protecting neural tissue from infections caused by neurotropic viruses (Conrady et al., 2013; Reinert et al., 2016; Katzilieris-Petras et al., 2022). In the context of HSV-1 infection, microglial stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling recognizes cytoplasmic double-stranded DNA and activates innate immune defense mechanisms (Paul et al., 2021). Cyclic guanosine monophosphate–adenosine monophosphate (cGAMP), synthesized by cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), serves as a key mediator that activates STING (Motwani et al., 2019). Activation of the cGAS-cGAMP axis initiates TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) signaling, which subsequently drives IRF3 and NF-κB activation and promotes the production of type I interferons (IFNs) and inflammatory cytokines (Liu et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2022). However, persistent STING activation during advanced HSV-1 infection or viral reactivation exacerbates neuroinflammation and causes neuronal damage. Therefore, modulation of STING-driven neuroinflammation may represent a promising therapeutic approach for HSE.

Nrf2 acts as a central transcriptional regulator of oxidative stress responses and participates in antiviral immunity, inflammatory homeostasis, and immune clearance (Benarroch, 2017; Daskou et al., 2023). Accumulating evidence indicates that Nrf2 can influence viral pathogenesis by modulating the expression and responsiveness of STING (Olagnier et al., 2018). Nrf2 is tightly controlled by Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) via the ubiquitin (Ub)–proteasome degradation pathway (Lo et al., 2006; Bellezza et al., 2018). Under basal conditions, Nrf2 remains at low levels because Keap1 promotes its ubiquitination and degradation. Under conditions of cellular stress, Nrf2 separates from Keap1 and translocates to the nucleus, where it promotes transcription of cytoprotective genes such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), thereby enhancing cellular resistance to stress. Recent evidence indicates that activation of Nrf2 can attenuate HSV-1-induced neuroinflammation, partly through suppression of the NF-κB axis (Kim et al., 2024). However, the specific role of Nrf2 in STING-mediated neuroinflammatory responses during HSV-1 infection remains unclear.

Cepharanthine hydrochloride (CH) (Fig. 1A) is a salt form of cepharanthine, a bisbenzylisoquinoline alkaloid isolated from Stephania japonica. Increasing evidence highlights its diverse pharmacological properties, including anti-tumor and anti-inflammatory activities (Zhou et al., 2012; Chen et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2025). Furthermore, in vivo experiments revealed the presence of cepharanthine in the cerebral cortex of mice, confirming that this compound can alleviate neuroinflammation caused by hyperuricaemia (Guo et al., 2025). These results suggest that cepharanthine can cross the blood-brain barrier. Although CH has been reported to suppress viral replication, its potential role in alleviating neuroinflammation during HSV-1 encephalitis remains unclear. CH inhibited HSV-1 replication in a dose-dependent fashion. In infected microglia, CH disrupted the Nrf2-Keap1 interaction, stabilizing Nrf2 by reducing its ubiquitination and degradation. Stabilization of Nrf2 reduced the phosphorylation and aggregation of STING, leading to suppression of the STING-NF-κB signaling and a marked decrease in inflammatory responses. CH treatment markedly improved survival and reduced disease severity in mice infected with HSV-1. These findings indicate that CH alleviates neuroinflammation through Nrf2 stabilization and inhibition of STING activation, supporting its therapeutic potential for HSE.

Results

CH inhibits HSV-1 replication in microglia

CH exhibits inhibitory activity against DNA virus and protects neuronal cells (Zhou et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2019). To investigate whether CH affects neurotropic HSV-1 infection, we performed cytotoxicity assays in BV2 and HMC3 microglial cells. CH exhibited a 50% cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of 12.76 μM in BV2 and 18.34 μM in HMC3 cells (Fig. 1B). To further evaluate its antiviral activity, we treated microglial cells with CH or ACV and infected with HSV-1. RT-qPCR revealed that CH decreased viral DNA levels in a dose-dependent manner, with inhibitory effects comparable to ACV (Fig. 1C and D). Consistently, plaque assays showed that CH reduced the plaque number in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1E and F), indicating strong suppression of HSV-1 replication in microglia. Similarly, CH can inhibit the number of plaques in ACV-resistant virus strains (153-HSV-1 and Blue-HSV-1) in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1G and H). To identify the affected stage of infection, we performed plaque assays and found that CH had no effect on viral attachment, penetration, or direct virucidal activity (Supplementary Fig. S1A–C). RT-qPCR demonstrated that CH did not enhance the transcription of Ifnb1 and Isg15 (Supplementary Fig. S2A), indicating that its antiviral activity is independent of interferon signaling. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CH effectively suppresses HSV-1 replication in microglial cells.

CH mitigates the inflammatory response by inhibiting the STING-NF-κB signaling pathway

HSV-1 infection activates microglia and promotes the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, contributing to neuroinflammation in the CNS (Huang et al., 2024; Wang, E. et al., 2023). RT-qPCR demonstrated that CH reduced HSV-1-induced expression of Il-6, Tnf-α and Il-1β in microglial cells (Fig. 2A). We also assessed reactive oxygen species (ROS), key mediators of inflammation. Consistent findings from immunofluorescence in BV2 and HMC3 cells (Supplementary Fig. S2B and Fig. 2B) and flow cytometry in BV2 (Fig. 2C) revealed that CH significantly reduced ROS generation following HSV-1 infection. Furthermore, CH can inhibit the nuclear entry of p65 induced by HSV-1 (Fig. 2D). Given the established involvement of the STING-NF-κB pathway in HSV-1-related neuroinflammation (Huang et al., 2024), we next evaluated whether this pathway contributes to the anti-inflammatory action of CH. Western blot analysis revealed that CH markedly reduced HSV-1-induced phosphorylation of STING and its downstream regulatory factors p65, IKKα/β, and IKBα (Fig. 2E), indicating inhibition of NF-κB activation. To confirm the involvement of STING, siRNA-mediated knockdown was performed. Knockdown of STING markedly attenuated Il-6, Tnf-α, and Il-1β transcription in microglial cells following HSV-1 infection and diminished the inhibitory effect of CH on these inflammatory factors (Fig. 2F and G). However, the overexpression of STING can significantly reduce the number of HSV-1 DNA copies and weaken CH’s inhibitory effect on HSV-1 infection (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Since AP-1 is a key transcription factor involved in the regulation of inflammatory gene expression, we selected several well-known AP-1 downstream targets, including Cox-2, Mmp-9, Vcam-1, and Icam-1, to evaluate whether CH affects AP-1-mediated transcriptional activation in HSV-1-infected microglia. The RT-qPCR results showed that CH had no effect on their transcription (Supplementary Fig. S2D). Collectively, the results suggest that CH alleviates HSV-1-induced inflammatory response through inhibition of the STING-NF-κB signaling.

CH stabilizes Nrf2 to inhibit the STING-NF-κB signaling pathway

Previous studies have demonstrated that Nrf2 participates in antiviral defense and regulates STING-associated neuroinflammatory signaling (Sun et al., 2020, Wang et al., 2023, Wu et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2023). To determine how CH regulates the STING-NF-κB pathway during HSV-1 infection, we examined the role of Nrf2. We observed the temporal dynamics of Nrf2 degradation and STING activation at various time points following HSV-1 infection, and found that Nrf2 degradation coincided with an increase in STING (S365) phosphorylation (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S3A). This suggests that Nrf2 is negatively associated with STING phosphorylation. In contrast, CH treatment restored and stabilized Nrf2 expression during later stages of infection (Fig. 3B). Notably, in the absence of viral infection, CH had little effect on Nrf2 or STING protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S3B), indicating that CH primarily acts under HSV-1-induced stress conditions. CH treatment dose-dependently decreased the viral protein ICP0 while enhancing Nrf2 expression. A slight increase in the downstream antioxidant enzyme HO-1 was also observed (Fig. 3C and D). However, siRNA-mediated HO-1 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S3C) did not affect HSV-1 plaque formation, and CH still significantly reduced plaque numbers (Fig. 3E), suggesting that HO-1 is not required for CH’s antiviral effect. To assess the contribution of Nrf2 to CH-mediated inhibition of STING-NF-κB signaling, Nrf2 was silenced using siRNA (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Nrf2 depletion enhanced HSV-1-induced phosphorylation of STING and p65 (Fig. 3F), and markedly reduced CH's inhibitory effect on these signaling molecules. Together, the data indicate that CH stabilizes Nrf2 to restrain STING-NF-κB signaling in HSV-1-infected microglia.

CH interacts with Nrf2 to suppress STING activation in HSV-1 infection

To clarify the mechanism underlying Nrf2 stabilization by CH during HSV-1 infection, drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assays were performed. The results indicated that CH significantly inhibited Nrf2 degradation but did not affect STING expression (Fig. 4A). In line with the results of the DARTS experiment, the cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) results revealed that CH enhanced the thermal stability of Nrf2, but not STING (Fig. 4B). To determine whether Nrf2 interacts with STING and identify the functional domains involved, we generated STING mutants. Immunoprecipitation results showed that none of the truncations lacking HA-STING-CBD could associate with FLAG-tagged Nrf2, suggesting that the CBD domain is required for the interaction between STING and Nrf2 (Fig. 4C). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) further indicated that CH enhanced the interaction between Nrf2 and STING during infection with HSV-1 or the ACV-resistant strain Blue-HSV-1 (Fig. 4D and E). As STING oligomerization is a key step in its activation during neuroinflammation, we assessed the effect of CH on this process. Immunofluorescence and native PAGE demonstrated that CH significantly attenuated HSV-1-induced STING oligomerization (Fig. 4F and G). Phosphorylation of STING at serine 365 (S365) stabilizes its active conformation and promotes downstream signaling, including activation of IRF3 and NF-κB, leading to type I interferon and inflammatory cytokine production (Yamashiro et al., 2020; Kou et al., 2025). To evaluate the role of S365 phosphorylation in CH’s effect, we used an STING S365A mutant that cannot be phosphorylated. Compared to wild-type STING, the S365A mutant further increased the expression of Il-6 and Tnf-α, but had no effect on Cxcl10 or Ifnb1. Importantly, CH still suppressed inflammation in the presence of the S365A mutant (Fig. 4H). Taken together, these results indicate that CH may suppress STING activation by targeting and inhibiting Nrf2 degradation and by facilitating the interaction between Nrf2 and the CBD domain of STING during HSV-1 infection, thereby inhibiting the STING-NF-κB signaling pathway.

CH prevents Nrf2 degradation by inhibiting Keap1-mediated ubiquitination

Nrf2 stability is primarily regulated by Keap1, a substrate adaptor protein in the Cul3-dependent E3 ubiquitin ligase complex that promotes Nrf2 degradation through ubiquitination (Crisman et al., 2023, Itoh et al., 1999). Under stressful conditions, Keap1 undergoes conformational changes that weaken its interaction with Nrf2. This prevents ubiquitination and allows Nrf2 to translocate to the nucleus. There, Nrf2 regulates the transcription of various downstream target genes, including those involved in negatively regulating inflammatory factors (Crisman et al., 2023, Itoh et al., 1999). To determine whether CH stabilizes Nrf2 by inhibiting its ubiquitination during HSV-1 infection, we treated cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG-132 or the lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine. Western blot analysis showed that MG-132 prevented HSV-1-induced Nrf2 degradation, whereas chloroquine had no effect (Fig. 5A). However, following pretreatment with chloroquine, CH significantly upregulated Nrf2 in HSV-1-infected microglia (Fig. 5B) suggesting that Nrf2 is primarily degraded via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Furthermore, Co-IP assay indicated that HSV-1 infection promoted Nrf2 ubiquitination, whereas CH treatment reduced it (Fig. 5C). Activation of the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway suppresses the inflammatory response mediated by the Nrf2/NF-κB axis (Zou et al., 2024). Immunofluorescence assay further revealed that HSV-1 infection reduced Nrf2 expression and attenuated the binding between Keap1 and Nrf2 (Fig. 5D). To clarify whether CH regulates Nrf2 stability by affecting its interaction with Keap1, we pretreated cells with MG-132. Co-immunoprecipitation analysis demonstrated enhanced Nrf2-Keap1 interaction following HSV-1 infection, which was markedly diminished by CH treatment (Fig. 5E). In addition, DARTS analysis indicated that CH did not directly affect Keap1 stability. These findings suggest that CH primarily targets Nrf2 during HSV-1 infection, thereby disrupting the Nrf2-Keap1 interaction and preventing Keap1-mediated ubiquitination-dependent degradation of Nrf2 (Fig. 5F). Collectively, CH stabilizes Nrf2 and consequently suppresses STING activation.

CH alleviates HSE and neuroinflammation in vivo

STING-NF-κB signaling activation has been recognized as an important contributor to HSE pathogenesis (Huang et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2024). An HSE model was induced by intranasal delivery of HSV-1 (2 × 106 PFU per mouse). CH (5 or 10 mg/kg) was given intraperitoneally on days 1, 3, and 5 after viral infection (Fig. 6A), and body weight, survival rate, and neurological symptoms were monitored throughout the experiment. CH or ACV administration markedly attenuated body weight loss compared with HSV-1-infected mice (Fig. 6B). Simultaneously, they effectively mitigated the neurological symptoms associated with HSE, including eye swelling, periorbital alopecia, and hydrocephalus (Supplementary Fig. S4A and B). Furthermore, CH was found to significantly reduce the mortality of HSV-1-infected mice (Fig. 6C). Analysis of brain tissues further demonstrated that CH effectively reduced neuroinflammatory responses. RT–qPCR demonstrated that CH significantly suppressed Il-1β, Il-6, and Tnf-α expression (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining revealed that CH markedly attenuated HSV-1-induced cytoplasmic vacuolation and inflammatory cell infiltration in the brain (Fig. 6E). Notably, CH did not damage tissues, including those in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs and kidneys of mice (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Consistent with these findings, RT–qPCR analysis of viral genes (UL54, UL52, and UL27) indicated that CH significantly suppressed viral replication in brain tissues (Fig. 6F). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that CH increased Nrf2 expression and more effectively reduced the phosphorylation of STING, p65 and IKBα (Fig. 6G). In summary, CH effectively ameliorated the neuroinflammatory response triggered by HSV-1 infection in vivo.

Discussion

Previous research has primarily highlighted the anti-tumor activity of CH. More recently, CH has also been reported to possess antiviral activity against diverse pathogens, including hepatitis B virus and Japanese encephalitis virus (Yin et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2025; Rogosnitzky and Danks, 2011; Zhou et al., 2012). Nevertheless, its role in HSV-1 infection and HSE has remained largely unexplored. In the present study, CH treatment markedly limited HSV-1 replication in microglial cells and suppresses the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. In the mouse model of HSV-1-induced HSE, CH administration also improved survival and mitigated disease severity. Collectively, the data identify CH as a potential therapeutic approach for HSV-1-associated disease and HSE. It is worth noting that this study only investigated the effect of CH on HSE in male mice. Although choosing a single male gender can to some extent reduce experimental variables and improve the consistency of the results, it limits the general applicability of the conclusion that CH has an anti-HSE effect in terms of gender differences in the current research design, which is a limitation of the current study design. Future research should include both male and female animals to determine whether the neuroprotective effect of berberine hydrochloride observed in male mice is consistent in females, and to explore whether there are any gender-based differences in the underlying mechanisms.

Appropriate activation of STING in microglia is crucial for balancing antiviral defense and inflammatory responses during HSV-1 infection (Huang et al., 2024; Reinert et al., 2016; Reinert et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2022). However, prolonged STING-mediated NF-κB activation at later stages of infection can drive excessive cytokine production, leading to severe neuroinflammation (Huang et al., 2024). Targeting the STING-NF-κB axis may therefore offer a promising strategy for mitigating HSV-1-induced neuroinflammation. However, current antiviral drugs such as ACV and ganciclovir only inhibit virus replication. Despite their antiviral activity, nucleoside analogues fail to effectively control HSV-1-associated neuroinflammation (Huang et al., 2024). CH alleviated HSV-1-associated neuroinflammation. While HSV-1 promoted Nrf2 degradation, CH stabilized Nrf2. Nrf2 is a key regulator of antioxidant and anti-inflammatory responses, including the expression of HO-1. However, our results showed that HO-1 knockdown did not affect CH’s anti-inflammatory effects, suggesting that CH acts independently of the HO-1 pathway. Nrf2 negatively regulates STING signaling, and the Nrf2/STING-NF-κB axis has been linked to microglial activation and neuroinflammatory processes in retinal ischemia-reperfusion injury (Guo et al., 2023; Olagnier et al., 2018). While Nrf2 has been linked to herpesvirus-associated encephalitis, its role in STING-NF-κB signaling in HSV-1-infected microglia remains poorly understood (Xu et al., 2023). The present findings demonstrate that stabilization of Nrf2 is essential for suppressing HSV-1-induced activation of the STING-NF-κB pathway (Sun et al., 2020). Importantly, our Co-IP experiments further demonstrate that Nrf2 interacts with the CBD domain of STING, which is essential for STING activation by cyclic dinucleotides such as cGAMP (Yin et al., 2012). This interaction may interfere with the binding of STING to its endogenous ligands or block the conformational changes required for downstream signaling. Further studies, such as GST pull-down or surface plasmon resonance analysis, are required to determine whether this interaction is direct. These results suggest a novel molecular mechanism by which Nrf2 negatively regulates STING activity, potentially preventing excessive neuroinflammation during HSV-1 infection. CH directly targets Nrf2, enhances its protein stability, and consequently restrains STING activation and downstream NF-κB signaling. Consistent with the in vitro observations, CH treatment in vivo markedly increased Nrf2 expression, inhibited STING-NF-κB activation, and alleviated neuroinflammation and brain injury in HSV-1-infected mice. Overall, CH exhibited both antiviral and anti-inflammatory activities by modulating the Nrf2-STING signaling, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic approach for HSV-1-induced neuroinflammation.

Notably, the Keap1-Nrf2 regulatory axis is essential for maintaining cellular redox balance (Uruno and Yamamoto, 2023). Abnormal regulation of this axis has been widely reported in various neurodegenerative diseases and inflammatory responses associated with viral infections (Xu et al., 2023). HSV-1-induced Keap1-dependent ubiquitination drives Nrf2 degradation, thereby enhancing STING signaling and promoting neuroinflammatory responses. These results further clarify the molecular basis of virus-induced neuroinflammation and highlight potential targets for antiviral therapy. Previous studies have shown that modification of the Cys434 residue within the Kelch domain of Keap1 can disrupt the interaction between Keap1 and Nrf2, thereby preventing Nrf2 degradation (Li et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2025). However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which this site regulates protein–protein interactions remain incompletely understood. In the present study, DARTS analysis indicated that CH primarily targets Nrf2 rather than Keap1. By binding to Nrf2, CH interferes with the Nrf2-Keap1 interaction, thereby reducing Keap1-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of Nrf2 (Kim et al., 2024). One possible explanation is that CH competes with the Keap1 Kelch domain for Nrf2 binding or induces conformational changes in Nrf2 that weaken its association with Keap1. Nevertheless, further structural and biochemical studies are required to clarify this mechanism. In addition, the present findings suggest that CH selectively modulates the STING-NF-κB pathway through stabilization of Nrf2. Importantly, under basal conditions without viral infection, CH did not significantly alter Nrf2 expression or STING activity. This context-dependent regulatory effect indicates that CH may exert its antiviral and anti-inflammatory actions primarily under pathological conditions, highlighting its therapeutic potential for HSV-1-associated neuroinflammation.

Conclusions

Overall, CH targets Nrf2 and disrupts its interaction with Keap1. This interference limits Keap1-dependent ubiquitination and degradation of Nrf2, thereby suppressing STING activation and downstream NF-κB signaling. Consequently, CH alleviates HSE-associated neuroinflammation and improves survival rates in vivo. Our findings establish CH as a potential therapeutic agent for HSE and other neuroinflammatory disorders.

Cells and virus

Mouse microglial BV2 cells (3111C0001CCC000063) were provided by the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resource (Shanghai, China). Human microglial HMC3 cells (CRL-3304), Vero cells derived from African green monkey kidney epithelium (CCL-81), and HEK 293T (CRL-11268) were sourced from the ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). All cells were maintained in DMEM (8118305, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) enriched with fetal bovine serum (FBS; FND500, ExCell Bio, Shanghai, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (15140163, Gibco), and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. HSV-1 strain F (VR-733) and ACV-resistant strains (HSV-1/153 and HSV-1/Blue) strains were prepared as described previously (Wang, Z. et al., 2025).

Animal experiments

Five-week-old BALB/c mice were supplied by the Experimental Animal Center of Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). Prior to experimentation, animals were allowed to acclimatize for one week. Male BALB/c mice were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 10 each): control (Ctrl), HSE, HSE + CH (5 mg/kg), HSE + CH (10 mg/kg), and HSE + ACV (10 mg/kg). The HSE model was established following a previously described protocol (Huang et al., 2024). Mice were subjected to intranasal inoculation with HSV-1 at a dosage of 2 × 106 plaque forming units (PFU) per mouse. Body weight and neurological symptoms associated with HSE were recorded daily.

Cytotoxicity assay

Cell viability was evaluated with a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; 96992, Sigma-Aldrich). BV2 and HMC3 cells were plated in 96-well plates (1.5 × 104 cells per well) and exposed to different concentrations of cepharanthine hydrochloride (0–64 μM). After 24 h exposure, CCK-8 reagent was introduced and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1–2 h before measuring optical density at 450 nm. The half-maximal cytotoxic value (CC50) was determined using GraphPad Prism 7.

Viral DNA purification and quantification

Cells underwent three freeze-thaw cycles at −80 °C to generate viral lysates. Viral DNA was purified with the TIANamp Virus DNA/RNA Kit (Transgene, Beijing, China; #ER201-01) and quantified by real-time PCR using β-actin or EGFP as internal references. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

BV2 cells were harvested and lysed to obtain total proteins. Lysates were exposed to increasing temperatures (37–72 °C) for 3 min, followed by three freeze-thaw cycles at −80 °C. Insoluble proteins were removed by centrifugation (20,000 rpm, 20 min, 4 °C). Supernatants were analyzed by western blotting.

Plasmid construction and transfection

Full-length wild-type mouse STING (NM_028261.1) and STING-ΔCTT (deficiency of the amino acid sequence from 339 to 378), STING-ΔCTT-CBD (deficiency of the amino acid sequence from 152 to 378), and mutant STING (with the serine at position 365 mutated to alanine) were cloned into the pcDNA3.1-N-HA vector. Full-length wild-type FLAG-Nrf2 (NM_010902.5) were synthesized and purified by Sangon Biotch (Shanghai, China). BV2 cultured in 6-well plates were added to the corresponding plasmids (2 μg) via using jetPEI reagents (Polyplus Transfection, IIIkirch, France, PT-114-15) for 6 h, and then were changed DMEM containing 10% FBS for 72 h before being utilized for subsequent experiments. All Nrf2 siRNAs, STING siRNAs, and HO-1 siRNAs were designed and synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). Transfection of BV2 cells was performed using INTERFERin reagent (PT-409–10, Polyplus Transfection, Illkirch, France). The corresponding siRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analysis

All data processing and statistical evaluations were carried out with GraphPad Prism 7. Significance was set at ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, and ∗∗∗P < 0.001. The specific statistical tests used are indicated in the respective figure legends.
