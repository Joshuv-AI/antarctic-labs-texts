Safety of sedation-free endoscopic submucosal dissection of colon lesions: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Tzong-yun Tsai, Shu-Huan Huang, Kun-Yu Tsai, Yueh-Chen Lin, Wen-Sy Tsai, Hsin-Yuan Hung, Jeng-Fu You
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) has emerged as an effective technique for the en bloc and curative removal of sizable colorectal tumors. Despite ESD’s advantages over traditional surgery, its primary associated complications of perforation and hemorrhage pose significant challenges. Sedation-free ESD allows for real-time reporting of symptoms by patients, improving the identification and management of complications. This study aimed to evaluate the safety of sedation-free ESD.MethodsA retrospective cohort study included patients undergoing sedation-free ESD for colorectal lesions at a tertiary center in Taiwan from 2018 to 2023. Patient demographics, lesion characteristics, procedure time, and clinical outcomes were collected. The primary outcome was safety as assessed via intraoperative complications (e.g., perforation or hemorrhage) and overall complication rates. Additional analyses included factors associated with perforation and management strategies (i.e., endoclips or surgical intervention) using univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses.ResultsA total of 134 patients were included in the study. The mean patient age was 65.3 years, with an average lesion size of 3.6 ± 1.5 cm. ESD-associated perforation or hemorrhage occurred in 6.7% and 10.4% of cases, respectively, most of which were successfully managed with endoclips. Patients with hemorrhage—compared with those without—had significantly larger lesions (median: 4.7 cm versus 3 cm, p = 0.004). Multivariable analysis revealed no statistically significant associations between lesion characteristics and perforation. No delayed perforations were reported.ConclusionsSedation-free ESD is a safe alternative to traditional sedated ESD for colorectal lesions. Real-time symptom reporting by patients enhances management of complications. In addition, precision application of endoclips ensures optimal perforation and hemorrhage control, improving patient outcomes and safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10151-025-03224-1.Keywords: Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), Colorectal tumors, Sedation-free, Endoclips, Perforation, Safety

Introduction

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) stands as a cutting-edge and highly efficient technique designed for the en bloc removal of colorectal tumors. This innovative approach has garnered significant success, particularly in the treatment of early-stage colorectal tumors exceeding 2 cm in size, intramucosal tumors with a nonlifting sign, and rectal carcinoid tumors. With acceptable risk profiles and procedure times even for large colorectal tumors, ESD presents a compelling minimally invasive alternative to traditional surgical interventions [1, 2].

The recognized primary complications associated with ESD are perforation and hemorrhage, which can occur either during the procedure (intraprocedural) or post-surgery, each necessitating distinct management approaches [3].

Owing to the thinness of the colon wall and its intricate folds and flexures, colonic ESD is widely recognized as a challenging procedure and iatrogenic perforation can occur. Although ESD-induced perforation represents a severe adverse event, it can often be effectively managed without immediate surgery. Notably, endoscopic closure techniques, such as the use of endoclips, have shown success in sealing perforations of various sizes, promoting efficient healing [4, 5]. Subsequent intensive conservative treatments, such as fasting, broad-spectrum antibiotics, intravenous fluids, and vigilant vital signs monitoring, further contribute to positive patient outcomes [6, 7].

Hemorrhage during ESD is caused by direct cutting of vessels during the procedure. It is often self-limited and can typically be managed by coagulation using hemostatic forceps. In cases of thick arterial bleeding, the application of endoclips is a valuable tool for effectively controlling bleeding [8]. Clipping can also be a useful method to prevent delayed bleeding [7]. This comprehensive approach to managing perforation and hemorrhage during ESD underscores the success of conservative measures and endoscopic interventions in ensuring patient safety and achieving favorable outcomes.

Colorectal ESD procedures typically involve the use of sedative medications. Sedative agents including midazolam, propofol, and dexmedetomidine are commonly employed in many institutions to minimize patient discomfort during these procedures. However, these procedures can also be performed without sedation. At our institution, we have adopted a sedation-free approach, which is considered to have at least three distinct advantages. Firstly, it enhances the ability to manage patient positioning during the procedure, ensuring smoother execution. Secondly, it allows for the immediate identification of potential perforations through real-time feedback from patients. Thirdly, a sedation-free protocol is deemed cost-effective and reduces the risks associated with sedatives, such as cardiopulmonary complications such as arrhythmia or aspiration pneumonia, thus improving both patient safety and procedural efficiency [9].

This study aimed to evaluate patient complications and outcomes associated with sedation-free ESD at our institution, a tertiary medical center in Taiwan. The primary objective was to provide valuable insights into the safety and effectiveness of performing ESD without sedation. The findings from this analysis have the potential to guide future clinical practices and improve the overall quality of patient care.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study involved a chart review of consecutive patients from the tertiary medical center within the Division of Colon and Rectal Surgery, Department of Surgery, at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (CGMH) in Linkou, Taiwan, between 2018 and 2023. Consecutive patients who underwent sedation-free ESD for colorectal lesions were included in the study, with no additional criteria applied. Sedation-free ESD is the routine practice at our center; all patients who met the indications were offered this protocol.

Data collection

The following variables were collected: age, sex, comorbidities, lesion location, polyp morphology, lesion size, procedure time, histology (benign or malignant), type of ESD (hybrid or full ESD), and endoscopists’ experience. Postoperative length of stay and procedure duration were also recorded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the present analysis was safety as evaluated by intraoperative events during ESD, including perforation and hemorrhage, along with overall complication rates.

Sedation-free ESD procedure

Patients were positioned in the lateral decubitus position. A single 20 mg intramuscular injection of hyoscine butylbromide (Buscopan) was administered to induce smooth muscle relaxation and to reduce spasm in the colon. At the index colonoscopy, lesions were judged ESD-appropriate purely on gross morphology (size > 20 mm, nonlifting sign, or laterally spreading tumor characteristics) in line with Japan Gastroenterological Endoscopy Society (JGES) guidelines [7]. During surgery, intravenous morphine was typically administered only when required. This approach guarantees customized pain management in which clinicians can determine the precise moment when morphine is needed to ensure optimal patient comfort and fitness.

The ESD procedure involved several steps: initially, a hyaluronate solution (SH) was administered by submucosal injection to raise the lesion above the muscular layer. This fluid contained a small amount of dye to enhance visualization of the submucosal layer. Subsequently, the mucosal tissue was pre-cut around the lesion to define the margin. Finally, the connective tissue within the submucosal layer beneath the lesion was dissected using various knives according to each surgeon’s preference. In addition, hybrid ESD was defined as completing the resection with a standard snare.

All patients were scheduled for a follow-up visit 1 week after the procedure to assess recovery and review pathological reports, with instructions to seek immediate medical attention for any adverse symptoms.

Definition of complications

Complications of ESD in this study were graded using the Clavien–Dindo classification system [10]. These were assessed during the hospital stay and at a 1-week follow-up visit, focusing on clinical symptoms and intervention needs. All grading was based on the standard criteria outlined in the referenced system.

Management of perforation and hemorrhage using endoclips

Perforation

In cases of perforation during ESD, perforations were promptly closed using endoclips. Subsequently, additional imaging studies, such as supine X-rays, chest X-rays, or computed tomography, were performed as needed on the basis of the patient’s clinical condition. Patients were closely monitored post-ESD for clinical symptoms and vital signs. Data collected included fasting duration, antibiotic use, vital signs, blood tests (complete blood count [CBC], white blood cell counts [WBC], c-reactive protein [CRP], electrolytes, and biochemistry), and length of hospital stays. In cases of evident peritonitis or significant changes in vital signs, emergency surgery was performed. Patients managed with endoclips alone were compared with those requiring further surgical intervention.

Hemorrhage

If a hemorrhage occurred during ESD, initial hemostasis was attempted using an ESD knife. If bleeding could not be controlled with the knife, endoclips were applied. In cases where endoclips failed to achieve hemostasis, emergency surgery was performed. Post-procedural hemorrhage events were recorded.

Use of endoclips for wound closure after ESD was not common and was on the basis of the surgeon’s discretion.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data were summarized as numbers and percentages and were tested between groups through chi-squared tests, while continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), subjected to independent sample t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. Multiple logistic regression for perforation during ESD was performed to identify related factors. The criteria for variable inclusion in the multivariable model include variables exhibiting a p-value below 0.2 in the univariate analyses. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS software, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). A p-value of less than 0.05 by the two-sided test was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of patients undergoing sedation-free ESD for colorectal lesions

The study included 134 patients, with an average age of 65.3 years and about 48% of patients were female (Table 1). The most common locations for a lesion were ascending (A)-colon (22.4%) and cecum (20.1%), and the most prevalent polyp morphology was of laterally spreading tumors of granular type (LST-G) mixed nodular (36.6%). The majority of tumors were benign (88.1%). The highest percentages of surgery were of the ESD throughout type (82.1%) and the mean operative time for ESD procedures was 62.0 ± 39.9 min. Of the 16 patients where a malignancy was found, 8 had radical operations.

Safety outcomes of sedation-free ESD

Among the entire cohort, nine patients (6.7%) experienced perforation during ESD (Table 2). In eight cases, perforations were successfully treated using endoclips alone, while one patient required an immediate emergency surgery. In addition, 14 patients (10.4%) experienced hemorrhage that could not be controlled with ESD knives. Of these, 13 cases were effectively managed with endoclips, while 1 required surgical intervention.

Factors associated with perforation

No significant differences in clinicopathologic features were seen between patients with or without a perforation (Table 3). However, larger lesion sizes were observed in patients who experienced a perforation compared with those who did not (median 4.2 versus 3 cm, p = 0.061). Discussion

This study evaluated the safety and clinical outcomes of sedation-free ESD for colorectal lesions in 134 patients at a tertiary medical center in Taiwan. Perforation during sedation-free ESD occurred in 6.7% of cases and hemorrhage was observed in 10.4% of cases. Most complications were effectively managed with endoclips, with only two patients requiring surgical intervention. While no statistically significant factors were associated with perforation, the data suggests that larger lesion size, malignant histology, and polypoid morphology may increase the perforation risk. Hemorrhage was more common in patients with larger lesions and longer operative times. Notably, even when adverse events such as perforation or hemorrhage occurred, they were effectively resolved using conservative methods, such as endoclips. These findings highlight the feasibility, safety, and cost-effectiveness of performing ESD without sedation. Overall, a sedation-free approach, combined with meticulous colonoscopy control, may represent a safer and efficient alternative for colorectal ESD. The results also highlight the importance of skilled techniques and individualized management to optimize outcomes.

Owing to the extended procedural duration typically associated with ESD, sedation has traditionally been considered necessary. For example, Saito et al. [1] conducted a review encompassing 900 ESD procedures in 2014, with findings indicating a median operative time of 60 min and a mean of 100 min. A more recent study in 2022 by Kobayashi et al. [2] reported a mean procedure time of 80.6 min. Nevertheless, ESD procedures can be performed without sedation. Although sedation is commonly employed and generally safe, it can lead to complications such as arrhythmia, hypotension, and aspiration pneumonia—issues observed in standard colonoscopy practices [11, 12]. In contrast, sedation-free procedures, such as those evaluated in this study, may provide better communication between the endoscopist and the patient [13, 14].

In our cohort, we adopted an alternative approach by conducting all ESD procedures without sedation. This resulted in a notably shorter median operative time compared with the sedated procedures reported in previous studies. We hypothesize that this improvement in efficiency is not solely due to the absence of sedation but also to enhanced communication between the patient and physician. This facilitated swift positional adjustments by the patient, contributing to fewer interruptions during the procedure.

However, as mentioned previously, perforation is a significant concern in ESD. A comprehensive meta-analysis of 88 studies revealed an overall perforation rate of approximately 4.5% in Asian countries and 8.6% in non-Asian countries [15]. Another meta-analysis conducted by Juliana et al. identified endoscopist experience, large lesion size (exceeding 30 mm), right-sided colon lesions, and submucosal fibrosis as risk factors for perforation [16]. In our study, although lesion size did not correlate with the risk of perforation, protruding or polypoid lesions were associated with an elevated perforation risk. Despite the relationship between LST-NG and a heightened rate of multifocal submucosal invasion [17], the thickness of polypoid lesions emerged as a potential obstacle during submucosal dissection. The weight of the lesion causes traction of the mucosa, leading to excessive depth in dissection and contributing to the risk of perforation. Therefore, both lesion size and thickness should be considered in future perforation-related studies.

Various closure methods, such as endoclip closure, endoloop, clip fixation methods, the Overstitch Sx device, or surgical repair, are tailored to the size of the perforation hole. In this study, the majority of perforations were less than 10 mm and were effectively managed using endoclips, as shown in Fig. 1. Another benefit of sedation-free ESD is that patients can quickly report symptoms such as abdominal discomfort, enabling immediate intervention when a perforation occurs. This prompt response helps to prevent excessive dissection and enlargement of the perforation. This finding bolsters our main argument that sedation-free ESD not only increases procedural efficiency but also promotes the swift detection and effective management of complications such as perforations. Consistently, our study observed no delayed perforations. In addition, any abdominal discomfort that patients experienced during the procedure was effectively controlled with intramuscular morphine injections.

Strength and limitations

This study has several strengths. It focuses on a unique sedation-free approach for colorectal ESD, providing valuable insights into its safety and feasibility. The study also includes a relatively large cohort of patients undergoing sedation-free ESD at a tertiary medical center, which strengthens the generalizability of the findings. The detailed data collection on complications, intraoperative events, and postoperative outcomes allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the approach. Lastly, the study highlights the potential benefits of a sedation-free protocol, such as cost-effectiveness, reduced risks associated with sedation, and improved procedural efficiency, which could guide future clinical practices.

This study has several limitations. The conditions during ESD were documented on the basis of endoscopic images or surgeon interviews, which may introduce reporting bias. Patient satisfaction and pain scores were not assessed, leaving an important dimension of the sedation-free approach unexplored. The study lacked a sedated control group because sedation-free ESD is the only protocol at our institution. While this preserved cohort uniformity, it limited the ability to make direct comparisons between sedation-free and sedated procedures. The retrospective design of the study may introduce selection bias and limit the ability to establish causality. In addition, the study was conducted at a single medical center, which may affect the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Further well-designed, prospective studies are needed to address these limitations and validate the findings.

Conclusions

The administration of sedation-free ESD exhibited a favorable safety profile, with complication rates similar to those reported for sedated ESD in the literature. In this study, the rates of complications, such as perforation and hemorrhage, were moderate, and the majority were effectively managed using endoclips and conservative medical treatments. The absence of delayed perforations highlights the benefits of real-time symptom feedback during sedation-free procedures, enabling timely interventions and minimizing adverse outcomes. In conclusion, this study confirms that sedation-free ESD is safe, offering a viable option for colorectal tumor resection while reducing anesthesia-related risks. The ability to promptly address complications through endoscopic techniques also underscores sedation-free ESD as a potential method to improve procedural efficiency and patient safety. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and explore patient satisfaction and long-term outcomes.
