Validation of the art education in medical education questionnaire (AEMEQ): integrating medical sciences with the humanities
Authors: Fatemeh Salmani, Mohammad Javad Yaghoobi, Yahya Mohammadi, Farshid Abedi, Khadijeh Farrokhfall
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArt education is essential for developing creative abilities and improving sensory perception, as it integrates non-biomedical elements in medicine. Survey methodology gathers much of the medical education data relevant to these non-observable behaviors. The main objective of this study is to validate a questionnaire for an artistic education-based medicine curriculum by collecting opinions from expert faculty and students.MethodsThe AEMEQ was evaluated by its reliability and validity measurement. Content validity was determined by expert opinion (n = 20). To determine the internal structure of the AEMEQ and construct validity, an exploratory factor analysis was conducted, followed by a confirmatory factor analysis to assess the good fit using responses from 552 medical students who completed the AEMEQ. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and test-retest methods.ResultsCVI > 0.72 for relevance, clarity, and simplicity resulted in 23 items for final analysis. 21 items with high reliability (ICC = 0.92) were obtained following construct validity. Exploratory factor analysis revealed five components (KMO = 0.93) associated with significant results from the Bartlett test. All components with AVE and CR values ​​>0.7 are considered acceptable. Confirmatory factor analysis indicated significant fit indices such as Chisq/df = 2.25 and comparative fit index, CFI = 0.93. The final version of AEMEQ with 20 items showed acceptable reliability in most dimensions with potent relationships among components.Discussion and ConclusionsThis study precisely validates a 20-item AEMEQ for assessing arts education in medicine, exhibiting strong content/construct validity and satisfactory reliability between dimensions. Findings indicated significant positive correlations between different dimensions of the AEMEQ, highlighting the necessity of integrating artistic education into medical education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07815-2.

Background

The ultimate objective in medical education is to achieve the highest level of learning, social responsibility, with the goals of sustainable development, which has a multilayered construct from knowledge to skills, attitude, and interests/convictions, integrated with one’s personality [1]. This goal is achieved through critical thinking [2]. It is well understood that art education plays a crucial role in the educational system, primarily focusing on developing creativity and critical thinking skills. Effective teaching is a skillful blend of artistic and scientific elements, as studies have shown that students who engage in artistic activities tend to have better learning outcomes [3]. The more involved students are in the arts, the higher their levels of arts application, academic achievement, creativity, critical thinking skills, and higher-order thinking. Recent studies have shown that most medical schools incorporate the arts into their curricula [4]. To foster this competency, teaching non-biomedical sciences—an interdisciplinary learning approach—must be strengthened. This study integrates the humanities with medicine through art to facilitate this interdisciplinary learning. Art employs various learning methods, including cognitive and social perspectives [5]. Given the focus of this study, the learning approach closely relates to Piaget’s constructivist philosophy [6]. By considering dimensions such as agency (for whom), time, place, usefulness (for what), and levels of learning (micro or macro) [7]. Limiting oneself to only established sociological or psychological theories would hinder the precise expression of this training. Therefore, this study combines constructivist theory, fundamental to artistic education, with pragmatism, which is a key aspect of integrated learning. Together, these form the conceptual framework of the pragmatic-constructivist theory that guides this research.

Curriculum evaluation is a practical and necessary guide for educational planners, and one of the dimensions of this evaluation is student surveys. Survey-based research is vital in education, providing insights into human behaviors and gathering feedback from students and patients [8]. The prevalence of such studies in medical education has increased by 33% over the last decade [9]. Therefore, medical educators engaged in academic work must understand best practices in selecting, developing, administering, and reporting on surveys used in research and curriculum development projects. Then, a valid questionnaire development in this field is crucial. Validation involves a critical quality control system that ensures that educational institutions meet the desired standards [10, 11]. Validation involves groups of survey items from the same questionnaire designed to assess similar underlying constructs [12]. Previous studies have evaluated other art-based learning questionnaires [11, 13–15]. The processes of questionnaire design/validation differ and don’t follow consistent, rigorous standards [16]. Poor survey design leads to unreliable and inaccurate measurements. The main purpose of this study is to validate a questionnaire for an artistic education-based medicine curriculum using feedback from expert faculty and medical students.

Methods

A literature review was performed to clarify art education as a tool of medical humanities integration into medical education. a literature search was conducted in databases such as Google Scholar, PubMed, Iran Medex, Elsevier, Springer, Embase, Scopus, PsycINFO, and ERIC using keywords medical humanities/humanities, medical education, teaching, integration, art, learning, medicine, and clinical skills. After determining the dimensions and items of the instrument, the interview and expert focus group were conducted to match the participants’/respondents’ conceptualization of the construct. Finally, the initial instrument of AEME was developed with 53 items and scored on a five-point Likert response. The corresponding author completed the item generation as part of the NASRME project No. 972,475. Certain steps should be taken to evaluate the questionnaire’s validity. This study used the AMEE Guide for designing high-quality questionnaires. Addressing each step systematically will improve the probability that the survey designed will accurately measure what it intends to measure [16]. According to the AMEE guide 87, the survey scale design process has 7 steps. After item development, which includes the first four steps, the next three steps are expert validation, cognitive interviews, and pilot testing, as expressed below.

Participants

This study was conducted on medical students at Birjand University of Medical Sciences. Based on Krejcie and Morgan’s table from 923 studying medical students in different stages, the sample size was determined to be 555. Then, three incomplete questionnaires were excluded. The sample size determination based on Krejcie and Morgan’s Table [17] was deemed sufficient for the psychometry of the tool according to previous studies [18]. Samples were allocated based on the number of students in basic medical sciences (n = 350), clinical preparations (n = 146), and internships (n = 427). The questionnaire was prepared in electronic format. At the beginning of the questionnaire, students were asked to provide their consent to participate, and upon informed acceptance, they were presented with the electronic form. Participants were informally invited to take part in the study through convenience sampling, and the questionnaire link was shared with university students (https://survey.porsline.ir/s/MIG2vGxJ).

Expert validation-face and content validity

Face validity is the lowest level of content validity, which is used after the tool preparation. Without considering specific statistical analysis, it quickly determines whether the tool is visually suitable for the desired variable [19].

Content validity is related to an aspect of the questionnaire that indicates whether the stated questions cover all important and necessary domains of the variable of interest. Usually, the more items in the questionnaire, the better. Content validity better reflects the concept domain of the variable [20]. The criteria for an expert are often based on experience or knowledge of the survey field and, practically, dependent on the individual’s willingness and availability [21]. In this way, the 20 faculty members informed of artistic education with various specialists (medical education, biostatistics, and professors of basic and clinical sciences) were included. The criterion for the item acceptability should be determined in advance. The content validity ratio (CVR) and the content validity index (CVI) are common metrics used in decision-making for individual items. In addition to collecting quantitative data, the expert’s free-text comments were considered to elucidate existing items in which its construct is not well-represented. The Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was employed to quantify the perceived essentiality of individual items, as originally proposed by Lawshe [22]. Experts independently rated each item as “essential,” “useful but not essential,” or “not necessary.” CVR values were then calculated to reflect the proportion of experts identifying an item as essential. Items exceeding the critical threshold determined by the number of raters were considered to demonstrate acceptable content validity. To complement this assessment, the Content Validity Index (CVI) was calculated to evaluate the overall clarity and relevance of the items.

Cognitive interview

The interview (7 medical students) was performed with immediate retrospective verbal probe questions designed to elicit specific information [23]. The common verbal probes were (1) Restate the item in your language, and (2) What do you think this item means? (3) Could you provide an example?

Information gathered from the cognitive interview was incorporated into the overall questionnaire, and each item was refined. The student’s responses were analyzed thematically and continued until data saturation was reached (no new codes emerged).

Pilot testing

The questionnaire pilot test collected valid evidence (members of the target population, namely medical students, n = 552) based on two main approaches: internal structure (factor analysis) and reliability analysis (internal consistency by Cronbach’s alpha coefficient). The details are described in Sect. 2.5-7.

Exploratory factor analysis

To validate the factor structure identified in the exploratory factor analysis (EFA), the remaining half of the dataset (n = 276) was used for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). This method, using principal component analysis with Varimax rotation, considering eigenvalues above 1, and the scree plot, was employed. The quality of the dataset was evaluated using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. The KMO value exceeded 0.67, suggesting that the sample size was sufficient and that multicollinearity among items was within an acceptable range (values above 0.50–0.60 are generally considered adequate). Bartlett’s test produced a statistically significant result (p < 0.05), indicating that the correlation matrix was not an identity matrix and that meaningful shared variance existed among the items. Together, these results supported the suitability of the dataset for factor analysis and affirmed the robustness of the underlying factor structure [24, 25].

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

CFA was performed using AMOS software, employing the maximum likelihood (ML) estimation method. This technique is widely adopted in cases where the data approximately satisfy the assumption of multivariate normality. The assumption of multivariate normality was examined using skewness and kurtosis statistics. Various statistics are available for assessing goodness-of-fit. Hu & Bentler [26] recommended multiple fit indices and their corresponding cut-off values for evaluating data: Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI) with a cut-off of TLI ≥ 0.90, Comparative Fit Index (CFI) with a cut-off of CFI ≥ 0.90, Root Mean Squared Error of Approximation (RMSEA) with a cut-off of less than 0.08 [27]. The parsimonious Normed Fit Index (PNFI) with a cut-off value of PNFI ≥ 0.5. Furthermore, some other indices, such as Goodness-of-Fit-Index (GFI) ≥ 0.9 and Parsimonious Comparative Fit Index (PCFI) ≥ 0.6, were suggested [28].

Discriminant analysis validity was assessed using the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) index. The average variance extracted indicates the proportion of variance in the indicators explained by the latent constructs, providing a more cautious assessment of the measurement model’s validity. Typically, values above 0.5 for Average Variance Extracted (AVE) are accepted [29]. Also, acceptable CR values indicate that the measurement items exhibit high levels of internal reliability. CR values greater than 0.7 are required for confirmation [29]. Another approach to evaluating discriminant validity is by using the Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) correlation ratio [30]. A value approaching 1 suggests insufficient discriminant validity. To assess this using the HTMT method, its value is compared against a set cut point. If the HTMT exceeds that cut point, it implies that discriminant validity may not be present. Gold et al. challenged this approach and recommended setting the threshold at 0.90 [31].

Reliability assessment

The internal consistency method using Cronbach’s alpha was utilized to assess reliability. A value greater than 0.70 indicates good reliability [32, 33]. Additionally, the test-retest method is employed to evaluate reliability over time, with a threshold of 0.75 considered acceptable [32].

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were described using central tendency and dispersion criteria, while qualitative variables were described using counts and percentages. Given the normality of the quantitative variables, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was applied to assess the relationships among them.

Exploratory factor analysis was conducted using SPSS 26 software, while confirmatory factor analysis was performed using AMOS software 4.5.0, with the semTools package for McDonald’s Omega was used.

Confirmatory factor analysis (n = 276)

The structure of the CFA model was shaped by the results of the prior exploratory factor analysis (EFA). The number of factors, item allocations, and conceptual domains identified in the EFA provided the framework for specifying the CFA model. Items were assigned based on their strongest loadings in the EFA, and cross-loadings were minimized to maintain clarity and interpretability. By building on the EFA findings, the CFA allowed for examination of the model’s stability and conceptual alignment. To reduce the risk of overfitting, the CFA was performed using a separate subsample, offering a more robust validation of the proposed measurement structure. Half of the data from the confirmatory factor analysis was selected randomly and used to validate the discovered model from the previous stage. This sample size was acceptable based on the number of questions and the minimum acceptable sample size based on the study [34]. The goodness-of-fit test for the adjusted model was determined to be significant, and the goodness-of-fit indices supported the model based on the cutoff points established in validated studies. Goodness-of-fit indices for the model were as follows: Chisq/df = 2.25, NFI = 0.87, TLI = 0.91, CFI = 0.93, RMSE = 0.068 and PNFI = 0.78.

Discussion

This study approved the validity and reliability of the AEME questionnaire. Previous studies showed that art in medical education can enhance observation and empathy skills [15]. These findings help us recognize the importance of art integration into medical education and confirm its positive effects on student learning. Furthermore, the other study examined meta-motivational strategies in medical students. It demonstrated that several approaches are available to maintain and increase motivation throughout their studies [14]. This point can emphasize validating our questionnaire and its impact on student learning. Finally, continuous and accurate accreditation of evaluation tools used in medical education can help improve the learning process [11]. Therefore, a valid artistic education questionnaire in medical sciences not only aids in improving education quality but can also lead to key skill development in students.

In this study, our goal was to validate the artistic education questionnaire in medical education based on the integration of medical sciences with the humanities. The viewpoints of professors and students were used to validate the questionnaire. The research results showed that the average age of the individuals was 22.7 years, and 46.6% had a background in artistic activities. This questionnaire includes five dimensions: Arts-based curriculum goals, Arts-based curriculum content, Art-based teaching strategies, Art-based evaluation methods, and Art-based learning environment. Based on the results, the questionnaire possessed face validity. Face validity may be considered a preliminary step toward establishing content validity; if an item does not appear appropriate or relevant at a surface level, it is unlikely to demonstrate validity through more rigorous evaluation. The content validity (CVI and CVR) of the designed questionnaire with 53 questions was examined, and out of 20 evaluators, 23 questions advanced to the next validation stage. Two items were removed at the reliability stage before construct validity, and one item was eliminated during exploratory factor analysis (EFA). The reliability of the AEME instrument was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and ICC, both of which were desirable in most domains. Although the reliability coefficients for the Art-based learning environment dimension fall below commonly accepted thresholds, a factor may account for this outcome. The limited number of cases, as well as the different experiences of students in different courses and educational settings in this dimension, could contribute to the lower reliability estimates. Given the exploratory nature of this study and considering that other dimensions demonstrated satisfactory reliability, the observed values in Dimension 5 may be interpreted cautiously.

According to the KMO in the EFA, the sample size was adequate. Throughout the EFA process, maintain the study’s conceptual framework. A total of 20 questions were classified into five dimensions within the proposed model. In one component, the AVE index did not meet the required adequacy, which is sometimes observed in exploratory studies despite model confirmation [35]. Several fit indices were used for the model dimensions check, including Chi-Square (χ2), GFI, TLI, CFI, PNFI, PCFI, and RMSEA. The cutoff values for model fit indices were within acceptable ranges. RMSEA, one of the most popular fitness indices, also fell within the acceptable range. Based on CFA analysis results, the items demonstrated a good fit for the scale and were adequately validated by the evaluated indices. Finally, the AEME questionnaire possesses reasonable psychometric properties, demonstrating sufficient internal reliability and strong evidence of structural validity. To our best knowledge, the current study is the first research conducted to design a valid and reliable tool for the human sciences in medical education. Furthermore, the step-by-step procedure was carried out precisely according to AMEE guide No. 87.

The validation of the AEME questionnaire can have educational, practical, and academic implications. In terms of educational impacts, it ensures that the medical curriculum covers non-biomedical knowledge skills (empathy, creativity, and humanistic) in future physicians. Its academic use can support the development of future research and inform policy decisions related to curriculum in medical education. However, its practical applications focus on curriculum development and professional growth. In this way, it can help integrate the humanities more effectively into the medical curriculum. Additionally, it can assist medical educators in achieving professional excellence. Therefore, this study benefits various groups, including students, researchers, medical educators, and educational planners in specific contexts such as curriculum committees or institutions.

Finally, although the results show that the AEME questionnaire is consistent with theoretical assumptions and can be used to assess arts/humanities incorporation in medical education, this study suffers from the following limitations: A limitation was that the study was conducted in only one medical school in Iran, and its implementation in other medical students in Iran and other countries with different cultural norms could turn it into a global scale in this field. Also, in the “art-based teaching strategy and educational environment” dimensions, the number of questions was limited to two due to some items’ elimination in the psychometric stages. Future studies are expected to cover a wider range of concepts by including more questions in two dimensions. Questionnaire implementation in future studies results in more reliable evidence. This questionnaire does not measure learning on a wide and comprehensive scale but focuses on the individual excellence of medical students.

Conclusion

The study properly and exactly validated the AEME questionnaire to evaluate artistic education in medical curricula, demonstrating strong content, construct validity, and reliability in most dimensions. The AEME questionnaire can be used to assess the extent to which art is incorporated into medical curricula.
