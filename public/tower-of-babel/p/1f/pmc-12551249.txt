Nonlinear association between serum iron levels and moderate-to-severe uremic pruritus in peritoneal dialysis patients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wenjie Yu, Jing He, Zhiyao Bao, Zhaoting Li, Chunyan Zhang, Jingyuan Xie, Hong Ren, Xiaomin Huang, Tian Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the occurrence of uremic pruritus among peritoneal dialysis (PD) patients and explore its correlation with serum iron levels.MethodsA cross-sectional study was carried out on 189 PD patients at a single center in China. The severity of pruritus was assessed through the validated 5-D Itch Scale questionnaire. The relationship between clinical factors and pruritus was examined using multivariate logistic regression, restricted cubic spline analysis, piecewise regression, and cluster analysis of iron metabolism patterns.ResultsModerate/severe pruritus affected 27.5% of participants, mainly on lower limbs (52.9%) and back (46.6%). Serum iron was significantly higher in the moderate/severe pruritus group (18.43 ± 8.50 vs. 15.84 ± 6.09 µmol/L, p = 0.029). After adjustments, each standard deviation increase in serum iron corresponded to higher odds of moderate/severe pruritus (odds ratio (OR) = 1.45, 95% confidence interval (CI):1.03–2.08, p = 0.037). Analysis revealed a threshold association(p = 0.043) with an inflection point at 13.7µmol/L, above which each unit increase in serum iron raised pruritus odds by 10% (OR = 1.10, 95%CI:1.03–1.18, p = 0.006). Cluster analysis identified that patients with high transferrin saturation (TSAT), moderate ferritin, and high serum iron had the highest pruritus prevalence (52.8%).ConclusionIn PD patients, we identified a threshold effect of serum iron on uremic pruritus. Iron levels above 13.7 µmol/L independently worsened pruritus, especially in patients with high TSAT, moderate ferritin, and high serum iron, where prevalence reached 52.8%. These findings highlight the importance of assessing iron status and personalized management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04514-4.Keywords: CKD-associated pruritus, 5-D itch scale, Boruta algorithm, Nonlinear association

Introduction

Uremic pruritus, a distressing itch in advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD), impacts both dialysis and non-dialysis patients and is now termed CKD-associated pruritus (CKDaP). It significantly impairs quality of life, disrupts sleep and mood, and, in severe cases, increases mortality risk [1–3]. Uremic pruritus is influenced by multiple factors, including xerosis, peripheral nerve dysfunction, altered opioid receptor activity, and systemic inflammation, all of which contribute to its persistence in affected patients [1, 4–7].

Recent studies provide conflicting evidence regarding whether peritoneal dialysis (PD) or hemodialysis (HD) patients are more susceptible to CKDaP [8, 9], with reported prevalence ranging widely from 20% to 70% for both modalities [4]. Notably, PD uses a continuous and comparatively gentle toxin clearance process, which may better preserve residual renal function [10] relative to HD. In addition, fluid management strategies differ substantially between PD and HD, and prolonged exposure to peritoneal dialysate in PD patients is thought to contribute to a distinct systemic inflammatory profile [11]. Collectively, these differences underscore the importance of research specifically tailored to the PD population.

In HD patients, risk factors such as older age and low serum albumin are consistently linked to CKDaP, as reported by the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (DOPPS) [12]. However, the relationship between pruritus and factors such as disordered mineral metabolism (such as abnormalities in serum parathyroid hormone (PTH), calcium, or phosphorus) or dialysis adequacy is still debated [2, 12]. In contrast, research on CKDaP in PD patients is limited. Li et al. [13] reported that pruritus in Chinese PD patients was associated with dialysis vintage and elevated PTH levels. Min et al. [14] identified a significant association between Kt/V levels and the severity of uremic pruritus in patients undergoing PD, a relationship that was not observed in patients receiving HD.

Emerging evidence suggests that iron metabolic disorder contributes to pruritus [15]. There is nearly a consensus regarding the relationship between iron deficiency anemia and pruritus, with research demonstrating that iron deficiency can cause pruritus, while iron supplementation can alleviate it [15]. However, within the context of CKD, especially among PD patients, the specific characteristics of how serum iron levels influence pruritus remain poorly understood.

To fill this research gap, we undertook a cross-sectional study on PD patients at our center, utilizing a validated questionnaire to assess pruritus and analyzing clinical and laboratory data. Our purpose was to evaluate the prevalence of uremic pruritus and identify associated factors, focusing on serum iron levels, to guide clinical management in PD patients.

Methods

Study participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Peritoneal Dialysis Center within the Department of Nephrology at Ruijin Hospital in Shanghai, China, employing a convenience sampling approach. The sample size was determined by the number of prevalent PD patients available at our center during the study period. Of the 435 prevalent PD patients under active follow-up at our center, the pruritus assessment was conducted from November 2023 using the 5-D Itch Scale (5D-IS) [16, 17] administered as an electronic questionnaire via the Wenjuanxing APP (a widely-used online survey platform in China). We employed a dual distribution approach: (1) during routine outpatient follow-up visits, patients were invited to complete the questionnaire on-site by scanning QR codes, and (2) remotely via WeChat social media distribution of QR codes. Ultimately, 218 patients (50.1%) completed the electronic questionnaire in full. The remaining 217 patients did not participate, with their reasons for non-participation not systematically recorded. Among the 218 respondents, 10 patients definitely reported having no pruritus at all and were assigned a 5D-IS score of 0. After excluding 29 individuals (2 under 18 years old and 27 with less than 3 months of PD), a total of 189 subjects remained for final analysis. These participants were divided based on pruritus severity into a no/mild pruritus group (n = 137) and a moderate/severe pruritus group (n = 52). Written consent was obtained from all patients, and the study protocol received approval from the Ruijin Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No.2022 − 264). Pruritus assessment

Pruritus severity was assessed utilizing the validated 5D-IS questionnaire, which evaluates the degree, duration, direction, disability, and distribution of itch. This self-administered questionnaire by patients generates a total score spanning 0 to 25. Based on the 5D-IS total score, pruritus severity was classified into four categories: no pruritus (≤ 5), mild (6–10), moderate (11–20), and severe pruritus (21–25). For further analyses, patients were also stratified using a dichotomous classification: no/mild pruritus (score ≤ 10) versus moderate/severe pruritus (score ≥ 11), reflecting clinically significant thresholds for symptom management. In the analysis of pruritus distribution, the 16 original anatomical regions were consolidated into six areas: head/face, back, chest/ abdomen, lower limbs, upper limbs, and clothing contact areas, with the proportion affected in each area being calculated. Pruritus duration was dichotomized into < 6 h or ≥ 6 h daily, and the proportions of patients in each category were analyzed.

Data collection

A wide range of clinical and laboratory data was systematically collected, including demographic details and baseline clinical information such as patient age, gender, dialysis vintage, and prevalent comorbidities like hypertension, diabetes, or hepatic impairment. Laboratory evaluations encompassed serum iron, ferritin, transferrin saturation (TSAT), hemoglobin, albumin, C-reactive protein (CRP), various markers of mineral and bone metabolism (including blood calcium, phosphorus, PTH, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D(25(OH)D)), as well as creatinine, Kt/V, and creatinine clearance (CCr). Systemic inflammation markers, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), along with eosinophil percentage (EO), were also assessed. Dialysis-specific parameters included 24-hour urinary output, ultrafiltration (UF), and dialysis doses. Furthermore, lipid spectrum (triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C)), blood glucose, uric acid (UA), and carbon dioxide binding capacity (CO2CP) were documented. Iron therapy status was recorded for all participants, with most receiving standardized oral ferrous succinate and one patient treated with intravenous iron isomaltoside. However, individual iron dosages were not available. Similarly, the use of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) and hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitors (HIF-PHIs) was recorded, but data on specific dosing and duration were not collected. Blood samples were collected during routine examinations following standardized protocols, and patient information was extracted from electronic health records.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using Shapiro-Wilk test and skewness [18] evaluation, and expressed as mean ± standard deviation(SD) or median[interquartile range(IQR)] accordingly. Categorical variables are shown as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Before regression analysis, we assessed missing data patterns. The majority of variables exhibited less than 10% missing values. We used the missForest R package [19] for imputation to handle missing values (Supplementary Fig. 1).

After imputation, we started with univariate logistic regression to explore variables linked to pruritus. Serum iron, diabetes, EO, TG, and 25OH(D) met our inclusion criteria (p < 0.2). Next, we applied mutual information analysis [20] and the Boruta algorithm [21]—a random forest-based feature selection method that determines feature importance by comparing real features with permuted shadow features—to identify key factors. Serum iron was the leading variable, showing the highest mutual information score and confirmed as significant by the Boruta algorithm. Although ferritin and TSAT ranked among the top 10 based on mutual information, Boruta did not verify their significance. Serum iron, TSAT, and ferritin are closely linked indices of iron metabolism. Our correlation analysis showed moderate relationships between serum iron and TSAT, and between TSAT and ferritin, indicating a potential multicollinearity risk if included in multivariable models. Therefore, we chose serum iron as the primary exposure variable for the following regression analyses (Fig. 2).

To evaluate the association between serum iron (per standard deviation (SD) increase) and pruritus, three logistic regression models with progressively inclusive adjustment strategies were constructed: Model 1 included serum iron alone; Model 2 additionally adjusted for age and gender; and Model 3 further controlled for dialysis vintage, iron therapy, Kt/V, PTH, and CRP. Covariate selection was primarily based on clinical relevance, supplemented by feature selection results, while also considering the events-per-variable (EPV) principle [22] to ensure adequate sample size for stable parameter estimation and minimize model overfitting.

A restricted cubic spline analysis explored non-linear relationships between serum iron levels and pruritus. If we identify a threshold or inflection point effect, we will conduct a piecewise regression analysis to assess the differential effects of serum iron levels on pruritus.

To examine the relationship between other iron metabolism markers and moderate/severe pruritus, we initially employed restricted cubic spline curves to assess potential non-linear associations for ferritin and TSAT separately. To identify different iron metabolism patterns, we selected serum iron, TSAT, and ferritin as the primary indicators for subsequent K-means clustering analysis. Since K-means clustering has stringent requirements for data normality, ferritin was natural log-transformed before analysis. The optimal number of clusters was determined via the elbow method. We calculated the proportion of patients experiencing moderate/severe pruritus for each identified cluster and assessed inter-cluster differences using chi-square tests with Bonferroni correction. The results were visualized using principal component analysis (PCA) biplot and 95% confidence ellipses to identify specific iron metabolism patterns.

Subgroup analyses assessed the association between serum iron levels (per SD increase) and pruritus across various patient characteristics. Patients were categorized based on age (< 60 vs. ≥60 years), gender (male vs. female), urine output (< 400 vs. ≥400 mL/day), PTH levels (< 300 vs. ≥300 pg/mL), and phosphate levels (< 1.78 vs. ≥1.78 mmol/L). A sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding patients with missing serum iron values (n = 21) and re-running the regression analyses on the remaining cohort (n = 168).

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2), with two-sided tests and a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics and prevalence of pruritus

The study enrolled 189 patients undergoing PD. The average 5D-IS score among participants was 9.11 ± 3.48. Participants had a mean age of 51.37 ± 14.88 years. The moderate/severe pruritus group was slightly older (53.27 ± 15.66 years) compared to others (50.65 ± 14.57 years), but this age difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.281). The study population was balanced in gender, with 48.1% female patients overall. The median dialysis vintage was 2.50 [1.20, 5.09] years, showing no significant variation across pruritus severity groups (p = 0.439). However, serum iron levels were notably elevated in the moderate/severe pruritus group (18.43 ± 8.50 µmol/L) compared to the no/mild group (15.84 ± 6.09 µmol/L, p = 0.029). There were no significant differences between the two groups in clinical and laboratory parameters such as dialysis adequacy measures (Kt/V, CCr), urine output, UF, calcium, phosphorus, PTH, 25(OH)D, albumin, hemoglobin, TSAT, inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, TNF-α), lipid profiles, iron therapy status, and the use of ESAs and HIF-PHIs (Table 1).

Association of serum iron with pruritus severity

Multivariate regression analyses indicated a significant association between elevated serum iron levels and an increased odds of moderate to severe pruritus in peritoneal dialysis patients. Each standard deviation increase in serum iron corresponded to higher odds of moderate to severe pruritus, with odds ratios of 1.42 (95% confidence interval(CI) 1.04–1.98, p = 0.029) in the unadjusted model, 1.50 (95% CI 1.08–2.13, p = 0.017) after controlling for age and gender, and 1.45 (95% CI 1.03–2.08, p = 0.037) in the fully adjusted model incorporating dialysis vintage, iron therapy, Kt/V, PTH, and CRP as potential confounders. When analyzing 5D-IS scores as a continuous variable, a positive trend was observed between serum iron levels and pruritus severity (β = 0.44, 95% CI -0.06 to 0.94, p = 0.081 in the crude model; β = 0.53, 95% CI 0.01 to 1.04, p = 0.045 after demographic adjustment; β = 0.49, 95% CI -0.05 to 1.03, p = 0.077 in the fully adjusted model), although statistical significance was not consistently maintained across all models (Table 2).

Nonlinear association between serum iron and pruritus

Analysis using restricted cubic splines demonstrated a nonlinear association between serum iron and the odds of moderate/severe pruritus (p for overall = 0.015; p for non-linear = 0.045), with an inflection point at 13.7 µmol/L. Two-piecewise regression revealed that serum iron levels below this threshold were not significantly linked to pruritus risk (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.74–1.06, p = 0.180). In contrast, levels above 13.7 µmol/L were independently correlated with higher odds of moderate/severe pruritus (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.03–1.18, p = 0.006). The two-piecewise model provided a significantly better fit than the single-slope model (χ² = 3.99, p = 0.046) (Fig. 4).

Cluster analysis of iron metabolism patterns in pruritus development

To investigate the correlation between additional parameters of iron metabolism and moderate/severe pruritus, our analysis employing restricted cubic splines revealed no substantial relationship for either ferritin (p for overall = 0.414; p for non-linear = 0.998) or TSAT (p for overall = 0.271; p for non-linear = 0.343) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Based on K-means clustering, three distinct patterns of iron metabolism were identified: Cluster 1 (moderate TSAT, high ferritin, moderate serum iron: TSAT 30.05 ± 8.44%, ferritin 228.7 ng/mL [150.1–304.9], serum iron 16.38 ± 4.24 µmol/L), Cluster 2 (high TSAT, moderate ferritin, high serum iron: TSAT 46.71 ± 14.83%, ferritin 127.8 ng/mL [73.3–185.9], serum iron 24.91 ± 7.45 µmol/L), and Cluster 3 (low TSAT, low ferritin, low serum iron: TSAT 24.51 ± 8.14%, ferritin 54.5 ng/mL [30.1–78.5], serum iron 12.41 ± 3.86 µmol/L). Patients in Cluster 2 exhibited the highest prevalence of moderate/severe pruritus (52.8%), followed by Cluster 3 (27.3%), and Cluster 1 (17.2%) showed the lowest prevalence. The difference in moderate/severe pruritus prevalence between Clusters 2 and 1 was statistically significant after Bonferroni correction (p < 0.001), while the difference between Clusters 2 and 3 approached significance (p = 0.057). No significant difference was observed between Clusters 1 and 3 (p = 0.585). All p-values were adjusted using Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (Fig. 5).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses evaluated whether the relationship between serum iron and moderate/severe pruritus varied based on patient characteristics (Fig. 6). Although the association seemed numerically stronger in patients with PTH ≥ 300 pg/mL (OR = 1.62, p = 0.065) and in males (OR = 1.52, p = 0.096), these differences were not statistically significant. No significant interactions were identified among any subgroups (all p for interaction > 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis

Excluding patients with missing serum iron data (n = 168) did not alter the primary results (Table 3). The significant association between serum iron levels (per SD increase) and moderate/severe pruritus persisted in Model 1 (OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.03–2.03, p = 0.035), Model 2 (OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.07–2.19, p = 0.022), and Model 3 (OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.05–2.23, p = 0.029). Complete-case sensitivity analyses showed consistent effect directions and patterns, though some analyses lost statistical significance due to reduced statistical power from smaller sample sizes (Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3–4).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of 189 peritoneal dialysis (PD) patients, we found that uremic pruritus was highly prevalent, affecting more than a quarter of the cohort at moderate/severe levels. While the overall burden is in line with that in previous reports among PD and HD populations [4], our investigation provides new insights into the unique characteristics of PD patients and, crucially, identifies a nonlinear association between serum iron levels and pruritus severity.

The mean 5D-IS score (9.11 ± 3.48) indicated that pruritus remains a significant quality-of-life issue in this population, with 40.4% of our moderate/severe pruritus PD patients experiencing prolonged itching episodes (≥ 6 h), comparable to the 40.3% of hemodialysis patients who reported daily pruritus duration of 6–12 h in Ersoy’s study [23]. With the lower limbs (52.9%) and back (46.6%) being the most commonly affected regions, this distribution pattern is similar to Saori Minato’s study of PD patients using visual analog scale (VAS) assessment, which also found pruritus predominantly on the back (70%), followed by lower limbs (67%) [24]. In contrast, Ersoy’s study [23] reported different distribution patterns, with pruritus mainly affecting the back, upper arms, chest, and abdomen. Despite these inter-study variations in distribution, it is notable that the back consistently appears as a primary site for uremic pruritus across both dialysis modalities.

Our regression analysis demonstrated a positive association between serum iron levels and pruritus severity in PD patients, consistent with Śnit et al. [5], who identified serum iron as an independent predictor of pruritus in dialysis patients. Additional indirect evidence comes from occasional reports [25, 26]of pruritus relief after phlebotomy in hemochromatosis patients with high serum iron levels. Further analysis using restricted cubic splines and two-piecewise regression revealed this relationship demonstrated a threshold effect (p for overall = 0.015, p for non-linear = 0.043), with an inflection point at 13.7 µmol/L. Below this threshold, pruritus risk showed a non-significant downward trend (OR = 0.89, p = 0.183), while levels above 13.7 µmol/L demonstrated a significant positive association (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.03–1.17, p = 0.006). This threshold effect suggests that iron-mediated pruritus attains clinical significance exclusively at elevated concentrations, a threshold that has not been previously quantified in patients undergoing dialysis.

Serum iron reflects circulating iron levels, ferritin indicates iron storage capacity [27], and TSAT represents the proportion of transferrin binding sites occupied by iron, serving as an indicator of iron availability for tissue delivery [28]. Accurately assessing iron status in dialysis patients requires distinguishing between absolute iron deficiency, functional iron deficiency, and iron overload states [29]. This complexity may contribute to the inconsistent associations observed between different iron parameters and uremic pruritus across studies. Our restricted cubic splines analysis revealed distinct patterns for different iron parameters: elevated serum iron levels were linked to increased pruritus severity, whereas ferritin and TSAT showed no significant associations. The lack of association between TSAT and pruritus aligns with Virga et al. [30], who found no significant differences in TSAT or ferritin levels between hemodialysis patients with different pruritus intensities. However, regarding serum iron specifically, our findings contrast with their results, as they also found no significant association between serum iron and pruritus severity. Ramakrishnan et al. [31] similarly reported no significant correlation between ferritin and pruritus severity in hemodialysis patients, consistent with our findings. However, the DOPPS study by Pisoni et al. [32] reported that elevated serum ferritin levels were linked to decreased risk of moderate to severe pruritus in hemodialysis patients. These differing results in individual iron parameters imply that their collective impact on pruritus might provide more insight than separate evaluations, highlighting the need for further investigation.

Using K-means clustering, we identified three distinct iron metabolism phenotypes with significantly different pruritus prevalence patterns. Notably, Cluster 2 (high TSAT/moderate ferritin/high serum iron: TSAT 46.71 ± 14.83%, ferritin 127.8 ng/mL [73.3–185.9], serum iron 24.91 ± 7.45 µmol/L) showed the highest prevalence of moderate/severe pruritus (52.8%). This phenotype represents a state of iron overload with excessive circulating iron that may promote oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, potentially contributing to pruritus through enhanced skin inflammation and nerve sensitization [33, 34]. The elevated TSAT in this cluster, coupled with high serum iron levels, indicates near-saturation of transferrin, resulting in increased non-transferrin-bound iron (NTBI) formation that catalyzes free radical generation and tissue damage [35]. The relatively moderate ferritin levels despite high serum iron suggest impaired iron sequestration capacity, potentially reflecting dysregulation of the hepcidin-ferroportin regulatory axis that may occur in uremic patients [36]. This compromised iron homeostasis leads to inadequate cellular iron storage despite elevated circulating iron, consequently exacerbating NTBI accumulation and subsequently oxidative tissue damage. While our study cannot confirm causative mechanisms, this pathway is hypothetical and based on the literature. Validation with direct NTBI and oxidative stress markers in future studies is needed.

Conversely, Cluster 3 (low TSAT/low ferritin/low serum iron: TSAT 24.51 ± 8.14%, ferritin 54.5 ng/mL [30.1–78.5], serum iron 12.41 ± 3.86 µmol/L) demonstrated intermediate pruritus prevalence (27.3%), representing an iron-deficient metabolic state. Previous studies have primarily associated iron deficiency anemia with pruritus and suggest that iron supplementation may effectively relieve symptoms [15, 37, 38]. A small-scale study [39] in dialysis patients with similar characteristics provides some verification for this relationship.

In contrast, Cluster 1 (moderate TSAT/high ferritin/moderate serum iron: TSAT 30.05 ± 8.44%, ferritin 228.7 ng/mL [150.1–304.9], serum iron 16.38 ± 4.24 µmol/L) demonstrated the lowest pruritus rate (17.2%, p < 0.001 vs. Cluster 2), representing a balanced iron metabolism profile. The high ferritin levels in this cluster may reflect adequate iron storage capacity while maintaining controlled circulating iron levels. This optimal iron homeostasis may explain the significantly reduced pruritus prevalence observed in this phenotype. All these findings indicate that both iron excess and deficiency can contribute to pruritus through distinct pathophysiological pathways.

Serum iron measurements are rarely used independently as clinical indicators, primarily serving as a numerator in the TSAT calculation(TSAT = serum iron/TIBC (total iron binding capacity)) [27].However, Kalantar-Zadeh et al. [40] demonstrated that serum iron is a better predictor than TSAT, independently associated with increased mortality in maintenance hemodialysis patients. Multiple observations have documented serum iron as an independent risk factor in various cancers [41, 42].These findings collectively suggest that serum iron level beyond its role in TSAT calculation, may serve as a valuable standalone clinical indicator in various disease states. Furthermore, with advances in NTBI detection technology, elevated serum iron levels coupled with high TSAT may indicate NTBI formation, which directly mediates iron-related tissue damage through free radical generation and oxidative stress [43].

The differing effects of ESAs and HIF-PHIs on iron metabolism may be relevant to our findings. While ESAs can cause functional iron deficiency, HIF-PHIs not only stimulate erythropoietin but also modulate iron metabolism by suppressing hepcidin and increasing iron availability [44]. Although higher serum iron was linked to increased pruritus odds in our study, we found no significant association between either ESA or HIF-PHI use and pruritus severity. This suggests the relationship is complex and warrants further investigation in larger studies.

We also need to consider reverse causality: pruritus itself may influence iron metabolism. Persistent itching and scratching can increase inflammation and cytokines like hepcidin, while changes in diet, sleep, or use of antipruritic medications may also affect iron balance. In our study, inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, TNF-α) were similar between groups, suggesting that acute inflammation is unlikely to explain these findings, though subtle, ongoing inflammation cannot be ruled out.

Several limitations constrain this study. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. The use of convenience sampling without a formal a priori sample size calculation, together with the modest sample size and single-center setting, may limit the generalizability of our findings. Our response rate was 50.1%, and reasons for non-participation were not systematically collected, which may introduce some selection bias. Factors such as technology access, healthcare engagement, and symptom severity could have influenced participation. Despite adjusting for multiple variables, potential unknown confounders could impact the outcomes. We did not collect data on peritoneal transport status, which may influence inflammation and toxin clearance in PD patients. We recorded iron therapy only as a binary variable and did not quantify cumulative iron exposure. We did not collect Charlson Comorbidity Index data, which limits our ability to adjust for overall comorbidity burden in our analyses. The absence of evaluating novel iron status indicators [28] like hepcidin, soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR), and NTBI restrict a complete understanding of the identified iron metabolism trends. Furthermore, reliance on self-reported pruritus assessments introduces a potential reporting bias.

Conclusion

Our study found a significant nonlinear association between higher serum iron (above 13.7 µmol/L) and increased pruritus in PD patients, with certain iron metabolism profiles showing the highest prevalence. While these findings suggest a possible link, further studies—especially those with interventional or mechanistic data—are needed to confirm and clarify these associations.
