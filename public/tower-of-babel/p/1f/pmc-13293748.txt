Phenotyping seasonal photosynthetic energy-partitioning states in temperate evergreen conifers using hyperspectral imaging
Authors: Seok-Won Jeong, Myoung-Goo Choi, Young-Won Kim, Jaewook Kim, Vaughan Hurry, Alexander G. Ivanov, Wah Soon Chow, Youn-Il Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Evergreen conifers retain needles and photosynthetic capacity during winter despite prolonged exposure to freezing temperatures and high irradiance, yet it remains unclear which aspects of this seasonal photosynthetic regulation can be reliably detected using passive optical sensing. Here, we phenotype seasonal photosynthetic energy-partitioning states in two temperate evergreen conifers, Pinus densiflora and Pinus koraiensis, by integrating chlorophyll (Chl) fluorescence imaging, pigment analyses, photosynthetic oxygen evolution, and hyperspectral reflectance imaging. Across three years, Chl fluorescence parameters (e.g., Fv/Fm, YPSII, YNPQ, and YNR) indicated reduced PSII photochemical efficiency and increased energy dissipation during winter, accompanied by changes in carotenoid-to-chlorophyll (Car/Chl) ratio, xanthophyll cycle de-epoxidation (DEPS), and photosynthetic oxygen evolution. Despite reduced PSII photochemical efficiency, photosynthetic oxygen evolution and pigment pools remained comparatively stable, indicating that reductions in PSII photochemistry were not accompanied by proportional declines in whole-leaf photosynthetic capacity. These seasonal physiological states were consistently reflected in hyperspectral vegetation indices. Photochemical Reflectance Index (PRI) showed correlations with both regulated (r = 0.57) and non-regulated light energy dissipation (r = −0.67), consistent with its sensitivity to Car/Chl ratio (r = −0.87) and DEPS (r = −0.85). Indices including Fluorescence Curvature Index (FCI) and Ratio Analysis of Reflectance Spectra chlorophyll b (RARSb) showed consistent seasonal correlations with photosynthetic oxygen evolution (r = −0.68 and −0.68), while also reflecting variation in pigment composition (e.g., Chl content; r = 0.73 and 0.78). Drone-based hyperspectral imaging further indicated that these needle-level physiological patterns were detected at the canopy level under the conditions examined. Indices such as FCI exhibited clear seasonal profiles, with separation between summer and winter values. By framing seasonal photosynthetic regulation as a phenotyping problem rather than a mechanistic one, this study provides a data-supported proof-of-concept framework for non-invasive, scalable monitoring of evergreen forests using hyperspectral sensing.

Introduction

Conifers are the dominant tree species in the circumpolar boreal forest biome, accounting for 29% of the global forest area. They also dominate high-altitude forests in the temperate zones of mountain areas in North America and Eurasia [1,2]. Evergreen conifers such as Pinus densiflora (Japanese red pine) and Pinus koraiensis (Korean pine) endure significant winter stress, including low temperatures and high solar radiation, conditions that are known to promote reductions in photosynthetic efficiency in evergreen conifers [3]. Although these temperate species are phylogenetically related to boreal conifers such as Pinus sylvestris and Picea abies [4], they experience distinct winter environmental conditions that may influence their photoprotective responses. In this study, the term “PSII photoinhibition” is used in an operational sense to describe a reduction in PSII photochemical efficiency; however, it is important to distinguish between mechanistically distinct processes. A decrease in PSII activity may arise from reversible downregulation associated with non-photochemical quenching (NPQ), including sustained energy dissipation in winter needles [5], or from photodamage to PSII reaction centers, which involves impairment of the D1 protein and requires repair through de novo protein synthesis [6]. Under low-temperature conditions, both photodamage and repair processes are affected, and their relative contributions to PSII activity remain difficult to resolve. Previous studies have shown that winter needles can exhibit partial recovery of PSII activity under favorable conditions, indicating the coexistence of regulatory and damage-related processes [5,6].

In boreal forest regions, evergreen conifers typically experience freezing temperatures for six to eight months per year, often accompanied by persistent snow cover that limits both irradiance and temperature variability during winter [7]. Under these prolonged and relatively stable winter conditions, boreal conifers employ multiple photoprotective strategies to dissipate excess excitation energy. These include NPQ mechanisms, including LHCII and zeaxanthin-dependent excess energy quenching [8,9] as well as PsbS-dependent energy-dependent quenching [10]. Additional pathways have also been proposed, such as cyclic electron flow around PSI [11,12], PTOX-mediated oxygen reduction [13], and alternative electron transport routes involving flavodiiron proteins [14,15]. Energy dissipation may further involve processes within PSII reaction centers [[16], [17], [18], [19]] and interaction between photosystems [20,21]. Together, these processes are thought to contribute to the regulation of excitation energy under winter stress, although their relative importance and coordination remain incompletely resolved.

At our study site, midwinter conditions are characterized by variable temperature and irradiance, in contrast to the more stable winter conditions often reported for snow-covered boreal forests [7]. While winter photoprotection in boreal conifers has been extensively studied [[22], [23], [24], [25], [26]], it remains unclear to what extent similar processes operate in temperate conifers such as P. densiflora and P. koraiensis, particularly under more variable environmental conditions. This raises the question of how seasonal photosynthetic regulation is expressed in temperate conifers and whether it can be detected using optical approaches.

Chl fluorescence parameters such as Fv/Fm, YPSII, YNPQ, and YNR [27] were used to probe PSII energy partitioning. These parameters provide estimates of photochemical efficiency and non-photochemical energy dissipation, although they do not represent direct or absolute measurements of the underlying physiological processes [28,29]. To provide a complete accounting of absorbed excitation energy, energy-partitioning models that resolve PSII energy flux into three mutually exclusive fates: photochemistry (YPSII), regulated non-photochemical quenching (YNPQ), and non-regulated energy dissipation (YNO) were introduced [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. This framework provides a useful basis for interpreting PSII energy allocation under stress conditions, by distinguishing photoprotective, inducible heat dissipation from baseline energy losses. However, in practice, the term YNO is sometimes used inconsistently to include energy lost through photoinactivated, non-functional PSII centers (YNF). To avoid this ambiguity, we report the combined yield of YNO and YNF as YNR, representing the total fraction of energy lost through non-regulated dissipation pathways.

While Chl fluorescence imaging is a powerful tool for probing PSII energy partitioning, it requires controlled illumination, dark adaptation, and specialized instrumentation, which limits its practicality for large-scale or remote field applications. In such contexts, hyperspectral reflectance imaging (HSI) provides a valuable complement: it is fully passive, non-invasive, and capable of spatially extensive data acquisition under natural light conditions [34,35]. Integrating HSI with fluorescence-based approaches thus may contribute to a more comprehensive assessment of photosynthetic performance and photoprotective light energy dissipation across both temporal and spatial scales.

HSI captures detailed spectral information across hundreds of narrow, contiguous wavelength bands spanning the visible to near-infrared region, providing precise characterization of plant biochemical and physiological traits [36]. Vegetation indices derived from hyperspectral data—such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) [37] and the Photochemical Reflectance Index (PRI) [38]—are widely used to assess photosynthetic efficiency, pigment dynamics, and photoprotective responses [39]. NDVI primarily reflects canopy greenness and biomass via red/NIR reflectance differences and typically declines under severe stress. PRI, by contrast, is sensitive to the Car pool size and xanthophyll cycle activity and is often interpreted as an indicator of light-use efficiency, largely through its sensitivity to Car dynamics [38]. However, in evergreen conifers retaining photosynthetically inactive or photoinhibited foliage during winter, PRI may reflect sustained energy dissipation rather than photosynthetic efficiency per se, complicating its interpretation as a proxy for carbon assimilation.

Despite extensive knowledge of winter photoprotection mechanisms in evergreen conifers, it remains unclear to what extent seasonal photosynthetic regulation can be detected using passive optical sensing across spatial scales. While Chl fluorescence techniques provide detailed insight into PSII energy partitioning, their application is largely limited to controlled measurements. HSI offers a scalable alternative, but the extent to which reflectance-derived indices capture seasonal variation in photosynthetic regulation in evergreen conifers under natural seasonal conditions remains unresolved.

The global distribution of conifer-containing forest biomes, including boreal and temperate regions relevant to this study, is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. The primary objective of this study is not to identify new photoprotective mechanisms, but to evaluate the phenotypic detectability of seasonal photosynthetic regulation in temperate evergreen conifers using hyperspectral imaging. Specifically, we address three questions: (i) which physiological traits associated with winter photosynthetic downregulation and photoprotection can be robustly detected using hyperspectral vegetation indices; (ii) how effectively reflectance-based indices track the seasonal dynamics of PSII energy partitioning, including non-regulated energy dissipation that has been largely overlooked in remote sensing studies; and (iii) whether needle-level physiological signatures scale to canopy level patterns observable with drone-based HSI in heterogeneous forest environments. To address these questions, Chl fluorescence imaging, pigment analyses, and photosynthetic oxygen evolution are used as physiological reference data to validate hyperspectral signals across seasons in P. densiflora and P. koraiensis.

Materials and methods

Study site and plant material

This study was conducted on Japanese red pine (P. densiflora Sieb. & Zucc.) and Korean pine (P. koraiensis Sieb. & Zucc.) growing in an urban forest site in Yuseong, Daejeon, South Korea (36.33°N, 127.31°E; Fig. 1a and b). Needles were sampled monthly from 20 to 25-year-old trees between Jun 2021 and Oct 2024. Both first-year (newly emerged) and second-year (mature) needles were collected. For each sampling date, needles were collected from four trees and pooled to obtain approximately 30 g (fresh weight) of material per species. The pooled sample was divided into two subsamples (∼15 g each). One subsample was used for physiological measurements, while the other was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −20 °C in complete darkness for subsequent pigment analysis and protein quantification. Subsamples taken from the pooled material were used for Chl fluorescence and oxygen evolution measurements and represent technical replicates. This design prioritizes temporal replication across years rather than within-site biological replications among individual trees. Measurements were repeated across three or four independent years, depending on data availability, and these independent yearly observations constitute the biological replication for statistical inference in this study (n = 3–4). Data shown in figures represent mean ± standard error (SE). Environmental data collection

Monthly climate data were compiled for Daejeon, South Korea (36.35°N, 127.38°E) from June 2021 to May 2024. Variables included 2-m air temperature (°C), surface downward shortwave radiation (W m−2), and day length (h). Data were obtained from the NASA POWER (Prediction Of Worldwide Energy Resources) database, which provides agro-climatological and meteorological reanalysis products [40]. Original daily values were averaged to monthly resolution for seasonal visualization (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Pigment extraction and HPLC analysis

For total Chl extraction, 20 mg of needles were placed in 2-ml microcentrifuge tubes with a 5-mm stainless steel beads and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Samples were homogenized by bead beating at 30 Hz for 2 min using a TissuLyser II (Qiagen, Germany). Pigments were extracted overnight in darkness using 1 ml of N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF). After collecting the supernatant, Chl a and Chl b contents were determined as described by Porra [41].

To quantify xanthophyll cycle activity, Car were analyzed by HPLC following Thayer and Björkman [42]. Freeze-dried needles (300 mg) were extracted in ethanol containing 0.1% ascorbic acid at 85 °C for 5 min, saponified with 80% KOH, and cooled on ice. β-Apo-8′-carotenal (25 ppm) served as an internal standard. Pigments were extracted twice with hexane, the combined extracts evaporated under nitrogen, and residues re-dissolved in methanol:dichloromethane (1:1, v/v). Separation was performed on an Agilent 1100 HPLC system equipped with a photodiode array detector (450 nm) and a YMC Car column (250 × 4.6 mm, 3 μm) using a methanol/ammonium acetate–methyl tert-butyl ether gradient at 1 ml min−1 and 40 °C. Peak areas for violaxanthin (V), antheraxanthin (A), and zeaxanthin (Z) were integrated, and DEPS was calculated as (Z + 0.5A)/(V + A + Z) [23].

Chl fluorescence measurements

Needle sampling was conducted in the morning (approximately 09:00–10:00 h), and samples were transported to the laboratory within ∼30 min in sealed plastic bags to maintain humidity. Prior to measurements, samples were kept under dim laboratory light conditions. For Chl fluorescence measurements, needles were dark-adapted for 15 min at room temperature. Chl fluorescence measurements were performed using a Chl fluorescence imaging system (CF Imager, Technologica, UK). Pine needles were arranged flat on the image stage, which was covered with moist Miracloth to minimize desiccation during measurements. Room temperature minimal fluorescence (Fo) and maximal fluorescence in the dark (Fm) were determined using saturating pulses, and the maximum quantum efficiency of PSII photochemistry (Fv/Fm, where Fv = Fm − Fo) was calculated following Genty et al. [27].

In winter conifer needles, short-term dark adaptation at room temperature primarily allows relaxation of NPQ, whereas sustained quenching mechanisms may not fully relax under these conditions. In addition, recovery from photodamaged PSII reaction centers is expected to occur on longer timescales in darkness, and may not be fully achieved under these conditions. Consequently, functional, but quenched, and photoinactivated PSII centers are expected to coexist during measurements. Under these conditions, the dark-adapted fluorescence parameters Fo and Fm reflect contributions from multiple de-excitation pathways, including photochemistry (kp), fluorescence emission (kf), constitutive thermal dissipation (kD), and dissipation associated with nonfunctional PSII centers (kNF), such that:Fo = kf / (kf + kp + kD + kNF)Fm = kf / (kf + 0 + kD + kNF)

Accordingly, the Fm values reported here represent a mixed population of functional and photoinactivated PSII centers and correspond to (Fm)pᵢ as defined by Kornyeyev and Hendrickson [33]. As the contribution of PSII centers with reduced photochemical activity increases during winter (i.e., increasing kNF), Fm declines, resulting in a concomitant reduction in Fv/Fm. Under these conditions, Fv/Fm should therefore be interpreted as an indicator of overall PSII functional capacity rather than maximal efficiency of fully active reaction centers. Reduced PSII activity may reflect a combination of sustained NPQ and potential damage, which cannot be fully distinguished under the present experimental conditions.

For light-response measurements, actinic light was applied stepwise at 10, 30, 70, 140, 250, 500, and 750 μmol photons m−2 s−1 (Supplementary Fig. S3). At each light level, needles were exposed for 4.5 min until a steady state was reached, after which steady-state fluorescence (Fs) was recorded, and a saturating pulse was applied to determine light-adapted maximal fluorescence (Fm′). Light-adapted minimal fluorescence (Fo′) was also determined.

Fluorescence parameters were calculated following Genty et al. [27], Kramer et al. [31], and Kornyeyev and Hendrickson [33] using the PSII energy-partitioning model. The effective quantum yield of PSII photochemistry (YPSII) was calculated as:YPSII = 1 − Fs / Fm′

The parameter (1 – qP), defined as:1 – qP = 1 − (Fm′ − Fs) / (Fm′ − F0′)and was used as an index related to reduction state of PSII electron acceptors. The minimal fluorescence in the light-adapted state (F0′) was not measured directly but was estimated using the following equation based on dark-adapted fluorescence parameters [43]:F0′ = F0 / [(Fv/Fm) + (F0/Fm′)].

This approach is widely used but may introduce uncertainty under conditions of photoinhibition [28].

The quantum yield of regulated non-photochemical quenching (YNPQ)—largely zeaxanthin- and PsbS-dependent photoprotective heat dissipation—was calculated as:YNPQ = Fs/Fm′ − Fs / FmFinally, the quantum yield of non-regulated energy dissipation (YNR) was calculated as:YNR = Fs / Fm

Because Fm corresponds to a mixed population of functional and nonfunctional PSII centers, YNR represents the combined contribution of constitutive basal losses (YNO) and energy dissipation associated with photoinactivated PSII centers (YNF) and therefore does not represent a directly measurable physiological flux but an operational fluorescence-derived estimate. Partitioning of YNR into YNO and YNF was not possible because Fm values corresponding to fully functional PSII were unavailable. By definition, the three yields satisfy the relationship:YPSII + YNPQ + YNR = 1

Fluorescence parameter images were generated by the CF Imager software, and region-of-interest (ROI) values were extracted for quantitative analysis.

Hyperspectral data collection

Hyperspectral images of detached needles were acquired using a high-performance line-scan imaging spectrograph, SPECIM IQ (Specim Ltd.), which was mounted on a tripod stand (Manfrotto 155). The sensor captured reflectance across the 400–1000 nm spectral range at a spectral resolution of 2.9 nm [44], as previously described [45]. Radiometric calibration was performed using Specim IQ Studio, and the resulting hyperspectral image data were imported for analysis. ROIs were delineated to exclude the 99% barium sulfate white reference panel used for reflectance standardization. The cropped hyperspectral images were subsequently processed for vegetation index calculation (Supplementary Table S1), statistical analysis, and figure generation using Python.

Field-based and drone-mounted hyperspectral reflectance data were collected using a push-broom line-scan imaging spectrometer (microHSI™ 410 SHARK; Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA), which measures reflected light across the 400–1000 nm spectral range. The sensor provides a spectral resolution of approximately 2–5 nm with a detector array of 704 spatial × 155 spectral pixels and a field of view of 29.5°, yielding a ground sampling resolution of ∼9–10 cm per pixel when mounted on a DJI Matrice 300 RTK drone (DJI, Shenzhen, China) flown at ∼100 m above ground level. HSI acquisition was performed on four occasions from Jun 2021 to Feb 2022, encompassing seasonal transitions from spring to winter. Each mission was divided into 10–12 overlapping flight strips to ensure complete spatial coverage and consistent illumination. All flights were conducted under clear-sky conditions between 10:00 and 14:00 local time to minimize directional shadow effects and solar zenith variability.

Each hyperspectral mosaic contained 4755 × 4542 pixels stored in ENVI BSQ (uint16) format with 150 spectral bands across the visible–near infrared (VNIR) range. Raw data were subjected to radiometric calibration, denoising, and geometric correction using GPS/IMU information, ground control points, and proprietary Corning software supplemented with custom Python scripts. ROIs corresponding to well-characterized coniferous and broadleaf tree canopies—including P. densiflora, P. koraiensis, Quercus spp., and other evergreen or deciduous shrubs—were identified using GPS-referenced RGB imagery from Google Earth and field validation. These ROIs were subsequently used for vegetation index computation (Supplementary Table S1) and statistical analyses to assess canopy-scale variation in photosynthetic and structural traits within the Hanbat Arboretum (36.366° N, 127.388° E; Seogu, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) (Fig. 1c). A total of 55 hyperspectral vegetation indices (Supplementary Table S1) were selected based on prior literature, representing the majority of commonly used and well-established VIS–NIR indices reported for vegetation monitoring and photosynthesis-related applications were calculated using respective equations.

Isolation of thylakoid membranes and Western blotting

Thylakoid membranes were freshly isolated from frozen needles sampled in February, May, August, and November over three consecutive years (2022–2024) following the procedures of Ottander et al. [46] and Ebbert et al. [47], with minor modifications. Briefly, needles (5 g fresh weight) were homogenized in ice-cold grinding buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 0.3 M sorbitol, 2 mM EDTA, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.25% PVPP, 2.5% PEG 6000, 10 mM NaF, 5 mM ascorbate, and 0.05% BSA). After filtration through Miracloth and sequential centrifugations (200 x g, 90 s; 4600 x g, 6 min; 4 °C), the pellet was resuspended in shock buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, and 10 mM NaF) for 10 min and then washed and stored in storage buffer (10 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 100 mM sorbitol, 10 mM MgCl2, and 10 mM NaF). Final pellets were resuspended in 2 ml storage buffer and used immediately for subsequent procedure.

Thylakoid proteins normalized to equal Chl content were mixed with SDS loading buffer, denatured (95 °C, 5 min), and separated by SDS–PAGE. Chl content was determined as described in the pigment extraction and carotenoid HPLC analysis section. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes in Tris–glycine buffer (100 V, 1 h, 4 °C). Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) milk in TBS-T, incubated with primary antibodies (Agrisera, Sweden) for 1 h, washed, and probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, USA). Signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (Bio-Rad, USA) with a ChemiDoc imaging system. Relative protein abundance was estimated by densitometric quantification of band intensities and expressed relative to the corresponding Feb samples. Densitometric protein abundance data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD test (p < 0.05).

77K fluorescence emission spectra

Low temperature (77K) fluorescence emission spectra of isolated thylakoids were measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer LS55 (PerkinElmer, USA). Aliquots of 150 μL containing Chl at a concentration of 20 μg mL−1 (equivalent to 3 μg total Chl) were placed in Pyrex tubes, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and dark-adapted for 30 min prior to measurement. Chl fluorescence was excited at 435 nm (Chl a) and 475 nm (Chl b) while the sample containing Pyrex-tubes remained immersed in liquid nitrogen. The fluorescence spectra were recorded over the 500–800 nm range with excitation and emission slit widths set to 5 nm. Background emission from the medium was subtracted from all fluorescence emission spectra, which were subsequently normalized to the PSI peak centered at 730 nm.

Photosynthetic O2 evolution

Net photosynthesis of fresh needles (20 mg) was measured polarographically as CO2-saturated O2 evolution at room temperature (∼22–25 °C) using a Clark-type Oxylab oxygen electrode (OXYL1; Hansatech, King's Lynn, United Kingdom). Samples were dark-adapted for 12 min prior to measurements. Irradiance-response curves of oxygen evolution were determined at light intensities of 0, 70, 250, 750 and 1200 μmol photons m−2 s−1, with each light level applied for 5 min. O2 evolution at each light intensity were calculated per total Chl weight of the samples, and gross O2 evolution were obtained after subtracting the dark respiration rate. The maximum rate of net photosynthesis (Pmax) was obtained at saturating light intensity of 750 μmol photons m−2 s−1 PFD.

Statistical analysis

Seasonal variation in physiological and spectral parameters was evaluated using two-way ANOVA with species and seasonal phase as fixed factors. Seasonal phases were defined as early growth (Jun–Sep, year 1), winter acclimation (Oct–Dec, year 1), midwinter (Jan–Mar, year 2), recovery (Apr–Jun, year 2), and summer maturity to senescence (Jul–Oct, year 2). When significant main effects or interactions were detected, post hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey's HSD test within the relevant factor levels (p < 0.05). When no significant species × phase interaction was observed (p > 0.05), data were pooled across species and analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD. Species differences within phases were evaluated using Welch's t-tests, and effect sizes were estimated using Hedges' g. Statistical significance was annotated as follows: species effect (∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001) and seasonal phase effect (different letters indicate Tukey groupings at p < 0.05). Analyses were performed in Python (statsmodels, scipy). Correlation analyses between hyperspectral vegetation indices, photosynthetic parameters, and pigment concentrations were conducted using Pearson's correlation coefficient (r). Monthly measurements aggregated across three years are provided in Supplementary Table S2. These data were used for all statistical analyses and correlation assessments reported in the manuscript.

Results

This study examined seasonal photosynthetic responses in two temperate evergreen conifers, P. densiflora and P. koraiensis, growing in Daejeon, South Korea (Fig. 1). The study site experiences pronounced seasonal and diurnal variation in temperature and irradiance (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. S2), providing a natural context to assess winter photosynthetic regulation. Seasonal measurements were grouped into five physiologically defined phases spanning early growth, winter acclimation, midwinter, recovery, and summer maturity to senescence.

Photosynthetic oxygen evolution and pigment contents remain stable during winter in two pine species

Seasonal analysis including morphological and color changes (Fig. 2a) revealed that whole-leaf photosynthetic capacity, as assessed by oxygen evolution from intact needle tissues at room temperature (∼22–25 °C) under both limiting (25 μmol photons m−2 s−1; QYO2, mmol O2 g−1 Chl/μmol photons m−2) and light-saturating conditions (Pmax; 750 μmol photons m−2 s−1) (Fig. 2b), peaked during early needle development and declined thereafter to a lower but relatively stable level that persisted through winter. By contrast, total Chl and Car contents increased steadily during needle maturation and reached their highest levels by late autumn, remaining stable through winter (Fig. 2c). Both pigment groups showed only a slight decline in the following spring, indicating that pigment retention rather than degradation predominates during winter. Chl a/b ratio also showed seasonal variation, declining during midwinter and recovering in spring (Supplementary Fig. S4). The Car/Chl ratio increased sharply during midwinter (Jan–Mar) and then returned to summer levels in spring (Fig. 2d), indicating a transient enhancement of Car accumulation relative to Chl under winter stress. Seasonal shifts in PSII energy partitioning define distinct photosynthetic phenotypic states

Seasonal Chl fluorescence imaging revealed pronounced changes in the distribution of absorbed excitation energy within PSII in needles of P. densiflora and P. koraiensis (Fig. 3). The effective quantum yield of PSII photochemistry (YPSII) declined progressively from summer to winter, reaching minimal values during midwinter (Jan–Mar), indicating a strong seasonal reduction in photochemical energy use (Fig. 3a–c). The parameter (1 – qP), representing an index related to the reduction state of PSII electron acceptors, showed marked seasonal decrease in winter (Fig. 3a–d). Energy partitioning analysis further revealed a seasonal redistribution among photochemical, regulated non-photochemical, and non-regulated dissipation pathways. During summer and early winter, YNPQ increased whereas during midwinter YNPQ declined (Fig. 3a–e). By contrast, YNR exhibited the opposite seasonal pattern, reaching peak values during midwinter, reflecting a composite of unresolved processes including basal energy dissipation and contributions from partially inactivated PSII centers (Fig. 3a–f). It should be noted that fluorescence parameters were measured following dark adaptation under controlled light conditions at room temperature, and therefore reflect the response of PSII to standardized illumination rather than the instantaneous in situ physiological state. In particular, seasonal differences in YNPQ and YNR represent differences in the capacity for further energy dissipation upon illumination, rather than absolute levels of dissipation in situ. Discussion

Winter photosynthetic regulation in temperate conifers reflects coordinated and gradual changes in multiple photoprotective processes

This study demonstrates that seasonal photosynthetic regulation in temperate evergreen conifers arises from the combined and progressively changing contributions of multiple photoprotective and photoinhibitory processes, rather than from a single dominant mechanism. By integrating Chl fluorescence, pigment composition, photosynthetic oxygen evolution, and hyperspectral reflectance, we show that winter resilience in P. densiflora and P. koraiensis is characterized by gradual shifts in PSII energy partitioning.

In this study, we distinguish between PSII downregulation, referring to reversible reductions in photochemical efficiency associated with sustained non-photochemical quenching, and PSII inactivation, referring to loss of PSII function associated with photodamage. These changes are consistent with the accumulation of Car and increased PsbS abundance, supporting a role for sustained and regulated NPQ, as well as a reduction in D1 protein that may indicate partial damage [11,13,17,48]. In addition, changes in pigment composition and antenna organization likely contribute to reduced light absorption by PSII [13,17]. Together these processes appear to operate concurrently and vary in relative contribution across seasons, resulting in a continuum of photoprotective responses rather than discrete physiological states. Within this framework, hyperspectral indices do not resolve individual molecular mechanisms, but instead provide an integrated optical signal that reflects these combined physiological changes.

Winter photoprotection in evergreen conifers has been extensively studied in boreal species, where resilience has often been associated with sustained, zeaxanthin- and PsbS-dependent regulated non-photochemical quenching [7,11,25,49]. While similar pigment- and protein-level adjustments were observed in the present study, our data suggest that, under the conditions examined, temperate conifers may exhibit differences in the relative contributions of energy dissipation pathways during winter. During midwinter, YNR exceeded YNPQ and represented a large fraction of fluorescence-derived energy partitioning signal, coinciding with a strong decline in PSII photochemical efficiency and in parameters related to the reduction state of PSII electron acceptors. However, the observed changes do not appear to have substantially affected photosynthetic oxygen evolution which remained relatively stable across seasons, suggesting that whole-leaf photosynthetic capacity was relatively maintained. Previous studies suggest that a subset of functional PSII centers remains sufficient to sustain electron transport despite reduced bulk PSII efficiency [50]. In any case, the increase in YNR likely reflects a composite of unresolved processes, including basal energy losses and contributions from partially inactivated PSII centers. In addition, regulated NPQ may be underestimated in winter needles due to pre-existing quenching prior to measurement [5,6].

Consistent with the observed decline in PSII photochemical efficiency, dark-adapted Fv/Fm decreasing to ∼0.25 in midwinter, indicating reduced PSII functional capacity under the measurement conditions. Despite this, photosynthetic oxygen evolution and total pigment pools remained comparatively stable. This apparent divergence does not imply a complete decoupling between PSII activity and downstream electron transport, but rather reflects differential sensitivity of fluorescence parameters and whole-leaf photosynthetic measurements. A fraction of PSII reaction centers likely remains functional and sufficient to sustain electron transport [51,52], while sustained NPQ and structural adjustments, including changes in antenna organization and pigment composition, will limit PSII photochemical efficiency as detected by fluorescence. The reproducibility of these patterns across years suggests that they may reflect recurring seasonal adjustments in PSII function under the environmental conditions examined, although their magnitude may vary with interannual differences in temperature and irradiance.

At the structural level, 77 K fluorescence emission spectra revealed a reduction in the F685/F730 ratio in winter thylakoids, indicating reduced PSII-associated excitation relative to PSI [[53], [54], [55], [56]], consistent with reduced PsbA abundance and changes in light-harvesting and antenna organization during winter. However, the seasonal decline in the Chl a/b ratio (Supplementary Fig. S4) does not directly parallel the expected response of LHCII abundance. Chl a/b likely reflects integrated changes across multiple pigment-protein complexes and seasonal acclimatory processes. For example, in addition to LHCII protein abundance, strong seasonal dynamics were also observed in PSI-related LHCI (Lhca1, Lhca2, Lhca3 and Lhca4) proteins of Lodgepole pine [12]. Such structural and pigment-level adjustments are consistent with enhanced energy dissipation during winter. Importantly, these changes should not be interpreted as evidence of a distinct photoprotective strategy relative to boreal conifers. Similar seasonal adjustments in PSII organization, sustained quenching, and reduced photochemical efficiency have been widely reported in boreal species [7,11,18,25,49]. The observed increase in YNR during winter therefore likely reflects the combined effects of sustained NPQ, structural adjustments, and potential photoinhibitory processes, with their relative contributions modulated by environmental conditions rather than representing a fundamentally different mechanism.

Taken together, our data support a conceptual framework in which PSII energy partitioning in temperate evergreen conifers varies along a seasonal continuum with characteristic phase, rather than discrete states. During summer, energy partitioning is dominated by photochemistry. During early/late winter, increased Car content and PsbS abundance are associated with enhanced regulated NPQ. During midwinter, non-regulated energy dissipation increases relative to photochemical utilization, coinciding with reduced PSII efficiency and structural adjustments of the photosynthetic apparatus (Fig. 8). This framework emphasizes that winter resilience arises from the combined and dynamically changing contributions of multiple processes, including sustained NPQ, structural reorganization, and potential photoinhibitory effects. Utility and interpretation of vegetation indices for phenotyping photosynthetic states in temperate conifer forests

The vegetation indices examined in this study—including PRI, FCI, and RARSb—should be interpreted within the broader context of remote-sensing approaches applied to boreal and temperate forest ecosystems. Greenness-based indices such as NDVI and the Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) have long been used to track seasonal canopy development and biomass dynamics [[57], [58], [59]]. However, because evergreen conifers retain foliage year-round and maintain relatively stable Chl pools, these indices show limited sensitivity to seasonal regulation in photosynthetic function, as also observed in the present dataset.

PRI has been widely used as an indicator related to light-use efficiency and xanthophyll cycle activity in evergreen species, including P. sylvestris and Picea abies, particularly under high light and low temperature conditions [60,61]. It has also been shown to correlate with radiation-use efficiency at ecosystem scales [62]. In the present study, PRI showed strong seasonal variation and was correlated with fluorescence-derived energy-partitioning parameters. PRI was positively correlated with YNPQ and negatively correlated with YNR. These relationships may reflect the strong associations of PRI with Car/Chl ratio and DEPS observed in the present dataset, as xanthophyll cycle activity and Car dynamics are closely linked to seasonal changes in PSII energy dissipation. These opposing correlations likely reflect concurrent seasonal changes in multiple physiological processes influencing fluorescence-derived energy partitioning, rather than a single mechanistic pathway. Thus, PRI appears to respond to multiple components associated with energy dissipation processes, and should not be interpreted as a direct proxy for photosynthetic carbon assimilation.

Indices such as FCI and RARSb showed correlations with photosynthetic oxygen evolution across seasons. However, these relationships should be interpreted with caution, as many physiological parameters covary across seasonal transitions. For example, Chl content, Car composition, and photosynthetic capacity were themselves interrelated in the present dataset, and thus correlations between spectral indices and individual parameters may reflect shared seasonal trends rather than direct functional relationship. Similarly, indices such as ChlDela and GNDVI did not show strong or consistent sensitivity to Chl content in the present dataset, highlighting the context dependency of these relationships. Overall, these results indicate that hyperspectral vegetation indices capture integrated optical response to seasonal physiological variation, rather than providing direct measurements of specific processes. From a phenotyping perspective, combining multiple indices may therefore be more informative than relying on any single index to represent specific physiological states.

Finally, while hyperspectral indices provide useful tools for monitoring seasonal variation in canopy optical properties, translating these optical signals into estimates of carbon assimilation and primary productivity will require direct validation against CO2 exchange measurements. Establishing robust links between vegetation indices and ecosystem-scale carbon fluxes remains an important next step, particularly for evergreen forest systems where winter productivity is difficult to assess.

Scaling needle-level physiology to forest canopies using drone-based hyperspectral imaging

Drone-based hyperspectral imaging indicated that seasonal patterns observed at the needle-level were detectable at the canopy scale in the studied urban forest. Within evergreen canopies, NDVI remained relatively stable across seasons, reflecting persistent foliage and biomass, whereas PRI and FCI exhibited pronounced seasonal modulation. The winter decline in PRI and FCI values corresponded with needle-level observations of reduced PSII photochemical efficiency and changes in energy dissipation. However, these relationships should be interpreted cautiously, as vegetation indices do not directly measure specific physiological processes but rather reflect integrated optical responses associated with variation in pigment composition and photosynthetic state.

These observations suggest that hyperspectral indices can capture aspects of seasonal variation in canopy optical properties linked to underlying physiological changes under the conditions examined here. However, the present results are based on two conifer species and a single site, and thus do not establish general applicability across species or ecosystems. Rather than providing direct measurements of photosynthetic processes, hyperspectral imaging offers a non-invasive approach to detect seasonal variation in canopy reflectance that may be associated with underlying physiological changes. Further validation across species, environmental conditions, and independent measurements (e.g., CO2 flux) will be required to assess its broader applicability.

Conclusion

This study examined seasonal variation in photosynthetic energy partitioning in two temperate evergreen conifers using Chl fluorescence, pigment analysis, oxygen evolution measurements, and hyperspectral reflectance imaging. The results show clear seasonal changes in PSII-related parameters, including reduced photochemical efficiency and increased energy dissipation during winter. Hyperspectral vegetation indices exhibited seasonal variation corresponding to these physiological changes, with PRI showing sensitivity to changes in energy dissipation and indices such as FCI and RARSb showing correlations with photosynthetic parameters within the present dataset. However, these relationships largely reflect coordinated seasonal variation among multiple physiological traits and should be interpreted as correlative rather than mechanistic. Drone-based hyperspectral imaging further indicated that seasonal differences observed at the needle level were detectable at the canopy scale under the conditions examined here. This should be considered a proof of concept rather than a generalized framework, as canopy structure, illumination conditions, and species diversity may influence the generality of these patterns. Overall, these findings support the use of hyperspectral reflectance as a non-invasive approach for monitoring seasonal physiological variation in evergreen conifers. Future work incorporating additional species, environmental conditions, and direct measurements of carbon assimilation will be necessary to further evaluate the robustness and general applicability of these relationships.
