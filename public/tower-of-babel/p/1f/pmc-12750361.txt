Rational engineering of degradation tail-driven CELMoD–antibody conjugates for precision malignancy therapy
Authors: Yu Guo, Yi Song, Hanlin Wang, Yang Lu, Jingyu Zhang, Zheyuan Shen, Weijuan Kan, Yuxian Wang, Haiting Duan, Shuangshuang Geng, Bo Wang, Shaoting Li, Bizhi Li, Xi Chen, Shanshan Pei, Luo Fang, Jia Li, Yubo Zhou, Jinxin Che, Xiaowu Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Degrader–antibody conjugates (DACs) represent a promising drug modality for targeting hematological malignancy, but still lack rational design frameworks. Here, we show the strategies of reasonable antibody-degrader compatibility and degradation tail-derived conjugatability through a systematic exploration. Inspired by the success of IKZF1/3 degraders, we sought to explore the potential of cereblon E3 ligase modulators (CELMoDs) in constructing novel conjugates. By combining a modular library with neo-substrate screening and further conjugatable derivation, I034 was identified, a potent CELMoD payload with picomolar degradation activity and antiproliferative effects. Through linker chemistry, I034-based DACs were constructed and demonstrated superior efficacy and safety compared to auristatin-based conjugates both in vitro and in vivo, with the CD38-targeting Dara-VA-I034 achieving complete tumor eradication at low doses. Mechanistic insights revealed distinct positive feedback regulation of CD38 conjugates, highlighting the need for compatibility between payloads and antigens. These results demonstrate that the approach could provide a framework for discovering CELMoD payloads and advancing DACs for treating multiple myeloma and other malignancies.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematological malignancy involving plasma cells and represents the second most common hematological malignancy with a rising global incidence, classified as an orphan disease by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)1, 2, 3. Currently, proteasome inhibitors (PIs) and immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs) are widely used as primary first-line therapies for MM4,5. Notably, the second-generation IMiD lenalidomide has been established as one of the most effective treatment options across various stages of MM management6,7. In this process, IKZF1 (Ikaros) and IKZF3 (Aiolos) have been identified as main targets of IMiDs that induce substrate ubiquitination and degradation after binding to cereblon (CRBN)8. Therefore, to address resistance, more potent Cereblon E3 ligase modulators (CELMoDs) targeting IKZF1/3, such as CC-220 and CC-92480, have been developed as next-generation “IMiDs” and are under clinical evaluation9, 10, 11. Despite advances in overall survival, significant unmet needs persist for refractory and relapsed patients, prompting the exploration of novel therapies12. Among these, antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) represent a promising therapeutic strategy, offering targeted delivery and potent efficacy13,14. Belantamab mafodotin (Blenrep), which initially received FDA accelerated approval for MM treatment but was withdrawn in 2022 after failing to meet endpoints in its confirmatory Phase III trial15, 16, 17, achieved the primary endpoint of progression-free survival (PFS) in combination with bortezomib and dexamethasone in recent DREAMM-7 trial, raising hopes for its potential reapproval18,19. However, the clinical trial discontinuation of MEDI2228, AMG-224, ABBV-838, and IMGN901 due to suboptimal efficacy or adverse effects20, 21, 22, 23 highlights the unmet medical need arising from the genetic complexity and high heterogeneity of MM24,25, underscoring the importance of pursuing innovative therapeutic approaches.

Degrader–antibody conjugates (DACs) represent a novel class of targeted therapeutics that employ protein degraders as payloads, rather than cytotoxic or other biologically active molecules26,27, benefiting from degraders’ catalytic mechanism and target expandability28, 29, 30. Among these, Proteolysis Targeting Chimeras (PROTACs) as payloads have been extensively reported in the literature in recent years31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37. More recently, DACs utilizing degradative molecular glues (MGs) as payloads have emerged, exemplified by ORM-5029, the first DAC to enter clinical trials, and ORM-6151, which demonstrated remarkable therapeutic potential in acute myeloid leukemia (AML)38,39. Both DACs use SMol006, a highly efficient GSPT1-directed CELMoD, as their payload40,41. Besides, Yu et al.42 recently reported a conjugate, TE-1146, which combines lenalidomide drug bundles with an anti-CD38 fusion protein, demonstrating impressive anti-myeloma efficacy in vivo. A recent retrospective review suggests that MG payloads featuring a cereblon-binding glutaramide motif offer unique opportunities for developing innovative ADC technologies, likely due to their favorable physicochemical properties and straightforward synthetic accessibility43, demonstrating the potential of CELMoDs as next-generation payloads. Therefore, given the clinical potential of DACs and the widespread application of IKZF1/3 degraders in MM treatment, the above evidence inspires us to explore the potential of combining IKZF1/3-directed CELMoDs with conjugation strategies to develop CELMoD–antibody conjugates (CELMoDACs) for treating MM and other hematological malignancies. However, unlike toxin-based payloads, matching expression of target antigen with degrader payload cell biology lacks established references and requires reevaluation. Furthermore, balancing chemical conjugatability and degradation activity poses additional challenges for the rational discovery of CELMoD payloads.

In this study, we aimed to identify novel IKZF1/3-directed CELMoDs as payloads and systematically explored their conjugation potential through the development of degradation-tail strategies and rational selection of antigen target. Firstly, by integrating our in-house modular CELMoD library with a high-throughput zinc finger degradation screening platform, we identified six degrader payloads containing secondary amine tails through dual filtering based on conjugatable chemical structures and biological activity. Further derivatization with alcohol or thiol tails led to the discovery of CELMoD payloads exhibiting nanomolar to picomolar degradation activity and potent antiproliferative effects against MM cell lines. Remarkably, these novel degraders demonstrated significantly improved safety profiles compared to microtubule inhibitors and topoisomerase I inhibitors. Initial proof-of-concept was achieved by constructing non-cleavable conjugates, where these CELMoDACs exhibited anti-MM activity comparable to auristatin-conjugates. Utilizing further linker modifications, we developed a series of cleavable anti-CD38 and anti-BCMA conjugates based on the most potent payload I034, which displayed superior antitumor efficacy both in vitro and in vivo. Notably, among these, the CD38-targeting CELMoDAC, Dara-VA-I034, exhibited superior efficacy, achieving a 100% complete response rate with a single dose of 3 mg/kg. Further validation revealed that I034 and its conjugates induce upregulation of CD38 expression, suggesting that CD38-targeting CELMoDACs benefit from this unique positive feedback regulatory mechanism. Overall, our results demonstrate that coupling degradation-tail strategies with high-throughput screening platforms can serve as a paradigm for discovering cereblon-binding molecular glue payloads, thereby advancing novel DAC technologies. The high-efficiency CD38-targeting CELMoDACs featuring the payload I034 represent a promising therapeutic approach for combating MM.

Results and discussion

Discovery of conjugatable CELMoD payloads through degradation-tail strategies

Since thalidomide was found to be a CRBN ligand in 2010, CELMoDs have undergone over a decade of development from serendipity to rational discovery44. Throughout this process, the structure–activity relationship (SAR) of CELMoDs with a glutaramide motif as cereblon binders has been extensively discussed45,46. By analyzing several CELMoDs in the clinical stage (Supporting Information Fig. S1), we identified a clear pharmacophore model and used it to construct an in-house CELMoD library composed of molecules modularized into conserved glutaramide, CRBN-binding fragments and linkage-tail fragments (Fig. 1A). More specifically, in the library, the glutaramide ring, crucial for CRBN binding, is conserved. For the CRBN-binding blocks connected to glutaramide, we selected monocyclic, bicyclic, and tricyclic fragments to expand the diversity of CRBN binders (Supporting Information Fig. S2). Furthermore, the linkage fragments, typically incorporating aliphatic, phenyl, or heteroaromatic rings, connect to the CRBN binder via carbon or heteroatoms, while the tail fragment building blocks primarily consist of substituted or unsubstituted piperidine or piperazine rings (Fig. S2). Using previously reported methods47,48, we established a degradation imaging screening platform targeting ZF degrons and tested the CELMoD library (Fig. 1B). Analysis of the IKZF degron degradation data therein revealed the following SARs: monocyclic CRBN-binding fragments exhibited poorer degradation activity compared to bicyclic and tricyclic fragments; for bicyclic CRBN binders, linkage attachment at the 4-position requires a spacer of at least one atom, whereas the 5-position favors direct attachment; and aromatic linkages are superior to aliphatic ones. While a recent study proposed an approach using amides for conjugate construction49, considering that the imide in glutaramide is essential for maintaining interaction with CRBN, along with the unvalidated release efficiency of amide prodrugs and their plasma stability, we limited the range of conjugatable groups to amino, hydroxyl, and thiol groups. Furthermore, appreciable degradation activity against IKZF1/3 at nmol/L concentrations is also necessary for a degrader payload. Applying dual filters based on chemical structure and biological activity, we identified six amine-tailed molecules from the library (Fig. 1A and Supporting Information Fig. S3). Interestingly, the structure tendency of these hits was clear: isointholinone linked to glutaramide was essential, with the linkage extending from the 4-position of isointholinone to an aromatic group via a two-atom connection, and the tails were aromatic rings substituted with piperidine or piperazine. Further cross-validation of degradation activity for the six payload hits using immunoblotting revealed that I030 and I040 demonstrated superior degradation capabilities (Fig. 1C), aligning well with our in-house screening results. Further evaluation of their anti-proliferative activity in IKZF1/3 degradation-sensitive NCI-H929 cells revealed that I030 exhibited slightly better potency, with an IC50 value of 0.16 nmol/L (Supporting Information Table S1), making it suitable as a payload for novel conjugates. These results indicated that the distinct advantage of tricyclic over bicyclic isointholinone cores, with pyrazole or benzene as preferred linkage between the core and secondary amine tails.

Building on the high activity and synthetic accessibility of I030, the conjugatable derivatization was conducted to obtain CELMoD payloads with diverse tails (Fig. 1C). The thiol/alcohol acid reacted with piperidine’s secondary amine through a simple one-step amide condensation reaction to obtain CELMoDs with thiol/alcohol tails for further screening. The immunoblot results showed that I034 exhibited degradation activity comparable to CFT7455 at different concentrations (Fig. 1C). Several hydroxy-tailed CELMoDs achieved over 90% degradation of IKZF1 and IKZF3 at 10 nmol/L and maintained considerable activity at lower concentrations of 1 nmol/L (Fig. 1C and Supporting Information Fig. S4). This was particularly exciting, as it suggested that introducing lipophilic cycloalkyl groups on the hydroxy acid tails significantly enhanced degradation efficiency. More importantly, in the evaluation of proliferation inhibition activity against NCI-H929, I034 demonstrated an IC50 in the single-digit pmol/L range (Table S1), supporting its potential as a CELMoD payload for further evaluation and conjugate drug development.

CELMoD payloads demonstrate potent IKZF1/3 degradation and antiproliferative activity

Considering the diversity of conjugation resulting from different degradation tails, we selected I030 with an amino group tail and I034 with a hydroxyl group tail for further evaluation. Although thiol-based conjugation methods have shown clinical potential in recent years, the biological activity of CELMoDs with thiol tails obtained during the derivation process has been less than satisfactory. Therefore, we decided not to pursue further evaluation of these variants.

First, we assessed the degradation kinetics of I030 and I034. By measuring the protein levels of IKZF1 and IKZF3 in MM cell lines 4 h after treatment with the two payloads at different concentrations, we found that both I030 and I034 significantly degraded the target proteins at sub-nanomolar to picomolar concentrations (Fig. 2A and Supporting Information Fig. S5), with DC50 values of 0.256 and 0.092 nmol/L, respectively. Next, we conducted cytotoxicity tests on I030, I034, and several clinically approved ADC payloads using NCI-H929 and human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). The results showed that, in NCI-H929 cells, I034 exhibited significantly better activity than the other payloads, with an IC50 of 7 pmol/L (Fig. 2C and Supporting Information Table S2). The cytotoxic activity of I030 was comparable to that of traditional payloads, including MMAE, MMAF, DM1, DXd, and SN38, with IC50 values in the nanomolar to sub-nanomolar range (Fig. 2C and Table S2), outperforming lenalidomide. Notably, compared to tubulin inhibitor and topoisomerase I inhibitor payloads, CELMoD payloads demonstrated less toxicity on PBMCs (Fig. 2D and E).

As previously mentioned, CRBN-based molecular glues face unforeseen off-target issues due to neosubstrates48,50. Although subsequent conjugation strategies may aim to minimize off-tumor adverse effects, we still conducted concentration-dependent degradation profiling for the two CELMoD payload molecules. The results showed no significant degradation of several widely reported neosubstrates (Fig. 2F), including SALL4, ZFP91, GSPT1, and CK1α, at concentrations up to 1 μmol/L, indicating the selective advantage of the tricyclic CRBN binder for IKZF1/3. As a highly selective and efficient CELMoD payload, we further evaluated the proliferation inhibition activity of I034 in various tumor cell lines. I034 was found to be sensitive to several MM and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) cell lines, including NCI-H929, MM.1S, Daudi, and OCI-LY10 (Fig. 2G). Its proliferative inhibition activity and sensitive cell lines corresponded with previously reported CELMoDs, supporting its potential as a novel payload for further degrader–antibody conjugate construction.

Construction of CD38-and BCMA-targeting CELMoDACs with non-cleavable linkers exhibits comparable efficacy to auristatin-based conjugates

Given the remarkable in vitro activity of I034 and the recent success of belantamab mafodotin utilizing a non-cleavable linker, our initial conjugate exploration employed a simple linker-payload design where I034 was connected to 6-maleimidocaproic acid via an amide bond (Fig. 3A). In essence, we constructed a non-cleavable linker-CELMoD combination differing from mafodotin (MC-MMAF) only in the payload. By utilizing a maleimide-linker-payload reaction with NAC, we synthesized NAC-MC-MMAF and NAC-MC-I034 to simulate the release of non-cleavable linker-payloads (Fig. 3B). When assessing the proliferation inhibition activity of NAC conjugates on NCI-H929 cells, we observed that while NAC-MC-I034 displayed slightly weaker proliferation inhibition compared to NAC-MC-MMAF, it still maintained nmol/L-level IC50 values, supporting its further development into conjugates (Fig. 3C). To confirm the CELMoD mode of action of NAC-MC-I034, we employed AlphaFold3 to predict the structure of the ternary complex CRBN: Ikaros ZF1-2-3: NAC-MC-I034 (Fig. 3D and Supporting Information Table S3). As expected, NAC-MC-I034 effectively induced ternary complex formation, with its warhead binding to a shallow hydrophobic pocket formed by three tryptophan residues (Trp380, Trp386, Trp400) on the CRBN surface (Supporting Information Fig. S6A and S6B). The glutaramide ring formed hydrogen bonds with His378 and Trp380 (CRBN), while the isointholinone ring was exposed on the CRBN: I034 complex surface, creating a novel interface to recruit the ZF2 domain of Ikaros as a substrate. This mechanism of action closely mirrors the cryo-EM structure of CC-92480 (mezigdomide)/CRBN/DDB1/Ikaros ZF1-2-3 (PDB 8D7Z). These findings suggest that the activity of the non-cleavable payload arises from the NAC-MC tail extending into the solvent-exposed region, not interfering with CRBN–substrate interactions. Subsequently, we constructed non-cleavable CELMoDACs targeting CD38 and B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA), both of which are widely expressed antigens in MM. Notably, αBCMA-MC-I034 induced significant target protein degradation after 48 h of treatment on NCI-H929 cells (Fig. 3E). In proliferation inhibition assays against MM cell lines, we conducted a head-to-head comparison between non-cleavable CELMoDACs and Blenrep biosimilar. All conjugates exhibited sub-nanomolar IC50 values (Fig. 3F and H); however, both CD38-and BCMA-targeting CELMoDACs showed slightly weaker cytotoxicity than their MC-MMAF counterparts, consistent with the differences in the activity of their released payloads. Notably, after co-incubation with PBMCs for 4 days, all CELMoDACs demonstrated superior safety profiles (Fig. 3G). Overall, targeting both CD38 and BCMA is a reasonable choice for constructing conjugates, encouraging further exploration of cleavable linkers to enhance the therapeutic potential of CELMoDACs.

High-DAR cleavable CELMoDACs constructed via linker chemistry achieve satisfactory stability and responsiveness

Given the activity and conjugability of I030 and I034, we further performed linker modification to construct cleavable conjugates based on them. Valine-alanine (VA) has been shown in several studies to outperform valine-citrulline (VC) and glycine-glycine-phenylalanine-glycine (GGFG) as a next-generation protease cleavage linker sequence51, 52, 53. Therefore, we chose VA as the cleavage fragment to build the linker-payload. For I030 with an amine tail, the classic paraaminobenzyloxycarbonyl (PABC) spacer was selected as the self-immolative fragment, while for I034 with a hydroxyl group tail, the stable aminomethylene (AM) spacer was used (Fig. 4A and Supporting Information Fig. S7). To prevent nonspecific enzymatic cleavage of the Val-Ala-PABC fragment, we inserted an aspartic acid as an acidic amino acid at the N-terminal of valine. CD38 is highly expressed in MM and serves as an ideal target for macromolecular-targeted therapies. Given the extensive application of daratumumab (Dara) in MM treatment and its clinically promising synergy with IMiDs, we selected it as the carrier for cleavable conjugate construction. To obtain αCD38 CELMoDACs, maleimide-linker-payloads were conjugated to reduced daratumumab through inter-chain cysteines using a previously reported bioconjugation protocol (Supporting Information Fig. S8)51. Based on MC-DVA-(PABC)-I030 and MC-VA-(AM)-I034, we constructed four conjugates with drug-to-antibody ratios (DAR) of approximately 4 and 8, respectively (Fig. 4B). The DAR and homogeneity of the conjugates were confirmed through analytical characterization using reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC, Fig. 4C) and hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC, Fig. 4D). Considering the potential effect of hydrophobic tricyclic payloads on antibody stability, we tested the potential size variants of the conjugates. Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC, Fig. 4E) analysis revealed that conjugates with lower DAR values showed no significant aggregation (HMWconjugates < 1%). However, Dara-DVA-PABC-I030 (DAR 7.7) exhibited noticeable aggregation (Fig. 4E), which we hypothesized could be due to the rigidity and hydrophobicity of PABC and the tricyclic CRBN ligand, while the introduced hydrophilic amino acids did not substantially improve this issue. Fortunately, Dara-VA-AM-I034 (DAR 7.8) maintained a considerable monomeric rate, preserving the potential for exploration of high DAR CELMoDACs in subsequent research and development.

To demonstrate that the affinity and specificity of daratumumab were not significantly affected by the linker-payload conjugation, CD38-based ELISA assays were performed to determine the ADCs’ binding affinity. The results revealed that each ADC bound to CD38 with a Kd similar to that of unconjugated daratumumab (Kd Dara = 0.72 nmol/L, Fig. 4F). Subsequently, we evaluated the release efficiency of the linker-payloads using liver S9 of two species based on previously reported methods. Human liver S9 exhibited higher cleavage efficiency than rat liver S9 (Fig. 4G). After 24 h of incubation, DVA-(PABC)-I030 showed a higher release rate than VA-(AM)-I034, consistent with our previous observations. Furthermore, the stability of the NAC-conjugates in human and mouse plasma was evaluated (Fig. 4G). After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, NAC-VA-(AM)-I034 showed limited free payload release in both human and mouse plasma (<2%), indicating satisfactory stability. In contrast, NAC-DVA-(PABC)-I030 exhibited more noticeable payload release, particularly in mouse plasma. However, the payload release rates of all tested linker-payloads in human and mouse plasma were acceptable (within 10% after 24 h of incubation), suggesting that the conjugates had considerable plasma stability for further evaluation.

CELMoDACs display potent in vitro anti-MM activity with superior safety profiles

Next, we evaluated the in vitro activity of the characterized CELMoDACs. Cell lines with different CD38 expression levels (NCI-H929, MM.1S, and PBMC) were exposed to gradient concentrations of the compounds to determine their sensitivity to CD38-targeting conjugates. After 5 days of incubation, relative cell viability was measured. As expected, the proliferation of CD38-positive MM cells was significantly inhibited by the αCD38 CELMoDACs, with IC50 values less than 1 nmol/L (Fig. 5A, B, and D). All cell lines displayed no sensitivity to the isotype control CELMoDAC (IgG1) and daratumumab at concentrations of 10 nmol/L. Among them, Dara-VA-I034 (DAR8) demonstrated the most potent proliferation inhibition activity on both NCI-H929 and MM.1S cells, with IC50 values in the single-digit pmol/L range (Fig. 5D), further indicating the therapeutic potential of high-DAR homogeneous conjugates. Dara-VA-I034 (DAR4) was more active than Dara-DVA-I030 (DAR4), likely due to differences in the activity of the CELMoD payloads. Compared to two control ADCs conjugated with auristatin derivatives, the conjugate of I034 exhibited comparable activity to that of MC-MMAF and slightly superior activity to that of MC-VC-MMAE at equivalent DARs, while exhibiting better safety on PBMCs (Fig. 5C and D). Given the promising therapeutic potential of Dara-VA-I034 (DAR8), we further investigated its degradation kinetics on target proteins. At a concentration of 1 nmol/L, Dara-VA-I034 efficiently degraded Ikaros and Aiolos in a time-dependent manner. In the NCI-H929 cell line (Fig. 5E), the time to reach Dmax was within 12 h, which was later in MM.1S (Fig. 5F), and the Dmax in MM.1S was slightly lower, which was even more obvious for DAR4 conjugates (Supporting Information Fig. S10A and S10B). We speculate this is due to the considerably lower expression of CD38 in MM.1S compared to NCI-H929, which is also reflected in the immunoblotting and flow cytometry analysis of CD38 (Supporting Information Fig. S11), indicating that the effects of CELMoDACs on target cells are also antigen expression-dependent. Additionally, we performed preliminary validation of the mechanism of action of CELMoDACs, finding that the degradation effect of Dara-VA-I034 on substrates was affected by competition from daratumumab and neddylation inhibition by MLN4924, indicating that its activity depends on both antibody-antigen binding and the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway (Fig. 5G). To further investigate the antigen-dependent target protein degradation by Dara-VA-I034 and its degradation window in normal cells, we evaluated its dose-dependent IKZF1 degradation activity in NCI-H929 and PBMCs. The results showed a significant dose-response relationship for Dara-VA-I034 in NCI-H929 cells, with a DC50 of 0.075 nmol/L (Fig. 5H). While I034 maintained potent degradation activity in PBMCs (DC50 < 0.05 nmol/L, Fig. 5I), the conjugate, benefiting from its target selectivity, exhibited a DC50 of 13.8 nmol/L in these cells (Fig. 5J). Despite the partial expression of CD38 on PBMCs, this represents a 184-fold improvement in selectivity compared to CD38-positive NCI-H929 cells, indicating a satisfactory degradation window.

Given that non-cleavable BCMA-targeting conjugates demonstrated promising in vitro activity, we also developed cleavable CELMoDACs targeting BCMA, a tumor necrosis factor superfamily member predominantly expressed in malignant plasma cells and recognized as an ideal MM target. Based on the reported sequence of belantamab (Belan), we expressed the anti-BCMA IgG1 in mammalian cells and carried out the construction and related evaluation of CELMoDACs (Supporting Information Table S4, Supporting Information Fig. S12). Overall, the in vitro SAR of Belan conjugates was similar to that of Dara conjugates (Supporting Information Fig. S13A), exhibiting superior or comparable potency to auristatin conjugates and control CELMoD conjugates (Fig. S13B). Furthermore, Belan-VA-I034 also demonstrated an excellent IKZF1 degradation window between NCI-H929 cells and PBMCs (DC50: 0.06 vs. 13.06 nmol/L, Supporting Information Fig. S14A and S14B), thereby confirming the molecular basis of its activity and its antigen dependence (Fig. S14C). Given the satisfactory and similar in vitro activity and stability of the VA-I034 DAR8 conjugates targeting CD38 and BCMA, both of them were selected as the candidate CELMoDACs for further in vivo studies.

In vivo efficacy of CD38-targeting CELMoDACs achieves complete tumor eradication

Based on the above findings, we evaluated Dara-VA-I034 (8) and Belan-VA-I034 (8) for in vivo treatment efficacy in NCI-H929 cell-driven xenograft models. Given the good plasma stability and safety of both ADCs, we selected single doses of 6 and 3 mg/kg, using an in-house Blenrep biosimilar as a positive control. Treatment was initiated when the average tumor volume reached approximately 160 mm3 (Fig. 6A). The tumor growth in the vehicle group was aggressive, reaching the euthanasia endpoint within two weeks post-administration. The combination of daratumumab (6 mg/kg) and I034 (calculated as DAR = 8) showed only a slight inhibitory effect on tumor growth but also reached the endpoint by Day 32 (Fig. 6B). However, to our excitement, all treatment groups of CELMoDACs exhibited significant tumor regression or stability, and none of the treatment groups showed significant weight loss, providing preliminary evidence of their safety (Fig. 6B and C and Supporting Information Table S5). Compared to Belan-MMAF, Belan-VA-I034 showed significantly superior efficacy at the 6 mg/kg dose, with 2 out of 5 mice achieving complete remission, which was also reflected in the tumor weights and xenograft collection at the experimental endpoint (Fig. 6D and E). A single dose of Belan-VA-I034 3 mg/kg has comparable anti-tumor efficacy to a single dose of Belan-MMAF 6 mg/kg, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of CELMoDACs. Encouragingly, the Dara-VA-I034 showed effective and persistent tumor clearance at both single doses of 6 mg/kg and 3 mg/kg, with no signs of tumor recurrence observed in any treated mice until the end of the study (Fig. 6B and D), with the complete remission rate of 100%. The tumor growth inhibition rate was calculated based on the tumor volume on Day 25. Furthermore, we investigated the in vivo degradation activity of Dara-VA-I034, the CELMoDAC with the most optimal in vivo anti-tumor activity. NCI-H929 tumor-bearing mice were given a single 6 mg/kg injection of Dara-VA-I034. At 24-, 48-, and 72-h post-administration, three mice were randomly euthanized at each time point, and tumor tissues were isolated, preserved, and subjected to Western blot analysis (Fig. 6F). The results showed that 24 h post-administration, IKZF1 and IKZF3 were already significantly degraded, although maximal degradation had not yet been reached. In tumor tissues at 48- and 72-h post-administration, we observed sustained and significant degradation of IKZF1 and IKZF3, indicating good pharmacodynamic (PD) properties of Dara-VA-I034. Interestingly, the abundance of CD38 in tumor tissues after 48 h post-administration was completely different from that at 24 h, showing a significant upregulation compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 6F). This increased expression of the target antigen is expected to enhance conjugate targeting and efficacy, and it also explains why CD38-targeting CELMoDACs exhibit significantly superior in vivo efficacy compared to their BCMA-targeting variants sharing the same linker-payload and DAR.

A previous study indicated that Ikaros and Aiolos can inhibit CD38 expression through their interaction with the nucleosome remodeling and deacetylation complex (NuRD)55. Under this premise, the CELMoD-induced degradation of Ikaros and Aiolos could lead to the upregulation of CD38 on the surface of myeloma cells, thereby enhancing daratumumab-induced therapeutic effect. Therefore, we speculate that after initial internalization, αCD38 CELMoDACs release their CELMoD payloads to induce the degradation of Ikaros and Aiolos, mediating the upregulation of CD38 expression, thus forming a positive feedback loop of endocytosis-degradation-endocytosis. Subsequently, we validated this through flow cytometry, showing that NCI-H929 cells treated with I034 exhibited significant upregulation of CD38, but not BCMA (Supporting Information Fig. S17A and S17B). This result was further validated in NCI-H929 and MM.1S cells treated with different concentrations of I034 (Fig. 6G and Supporting Information Fig. S18). qPCR results showed that both I034 and its conjugates significantly upregulated CD38 mRNA levels after incubation with NCI-H929 cells for various durations (Fig. 6H and Supporting Information Fig. S19A), while this phenomenon was not observed in the groups treated with daratumumab or Blenrep. Moreover, the upregulation of CD38 mRNA by I034 and Dara-VA-I034 (8) exhibited a dose-dependent effect (Fig. S19B), further supporting our hypothesis. This finding partially explains the in vivo superiority of Dara-CELMoDACs, as the accumulation of ADCs and effective payload release at the target site is more critical in vivo than in vitro. However, it is worth noting that target antigen expression is not the only one factor influencing ADC distribution and efficacy. Beyond this, antigen internalization efficiency, turnover rate, and the activity of linker-cleaving enzymes are also key determinants of conjugate activity. Although there are currently no studies systematically comparing the internalization kinetics of CD38 and BCMA, it is foreseeable that higher target expression likely increases CELMoDAC internalization rates. Our in vivo degradation data show that surface CD38 levels rebound above baseline following IKZF1/3 degradation, but whether this originates from new expression or recycling is not yet known. In summary, these factors are still crucial for elucidating the mechanisms of CELMoDACs, and this also suggests that the future design and construction of non-toxin payload conjugates should consider multiple dimensions when matching target antigens with payload cell biology.

Multi-omics analysis confirms CELMoDAC degrades IKZF1/3 and upregulates interferon-stimulated genes

Transcriptomic profiling of NCI-H929 cells treated with Dara-VA-I034 revealed key molecular mechanisms underlying its anti-tumor activity. Analysis identified prominent downregulation of IRF4 (Fig. 7A), a critical survival factor in multiple myeloma and a known downstream target of IKZF1/3 degradation56,57. Since IRF4 directly regulates MYC transcription56, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)58 predictably showed significant downregulation of the MYC_TARGETS_V1 pathway (Fig. 7B). Additionally, the transcriptomic data also demonstrated downregulation of numerous cell cycle-related genes in Dara-VA-I034-treated cells (Fig. 7A), with significant negative enrichment of the G2M_CHECKPOINT pathway in GSEA (Fig. 7B), indicating the induction of cell cycle arrest. This finding aligns with established pharmacological effects of immunomodulatory drugs that target IKZF1/355,57. GSEA revealed that INTERFERON_GAMMA_RESPONSE and INTERFERON_ALPHA_RESPONSE were among the most significantly enriched upregulated pathways (Fig. 7B). Consistent with our transcriptomic findings, GSEA of the proteomic data corroborated the significant downregulation of the MYC_TARGETS_V1 pathway following IKZF1/3 degradation (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, the cell cycle suppression observed at the mRNA level was mirrored in the proteome, with significant negative enrichment of E2F_TARGETS and G2M_CHECKPOINT pathways (Fig. 7D), reinforcing the compound’s ability to induce cell cycle arrest. Discussion

In the past five years, the rapid development of ADC drugs has led to increasing attention from researchers towards the diversity of payloads. While unprecedented success has been achieved with payloads represented by MMAE and DX-8951 derivatives, researchers are beginning to contemplate the next generation of payloads. Targeted protein degraders, exemplified by PROTACs and MGs, have entered the spotlight due to their broad target accessibility and catalytic degradation properties. The exploration of PROTACs as payloads can be traced back to 2020, when scientists from Genentech conducted a systematic investigation of BRD4 DACs. In recent years, while there seemed to be some stagnation in PROTAC DACs, conjugates utilizing MG payloads have made significant advancements, primarily employing GSPT1 degraders as payloads. However, challenges remain in payload diversity, rational design of conjugatable degraders, and reasonable antibody-degrader compatibility. Inspired by this progress, we take another clinically validated class of CELMoD, specifically IKZF1/3 degraders, as the entry point and reference for the research.

The first challenge was to balance the degradation activity and conjugatability of degrader payloads, which differ significantly from traditional MG discovery. By constructing a degradation tail-derived library combined with a zinc finger protein degradation high-throughput screening platform, we identified conjugatable CELMoD payloads that possess both IKZF1/3 degradation activity and anti-multiple myeloma cell proliferation activity. Among them, I034 demonstrated potent target protein degradation at picomolar concentrations without affecting other frequently discussed neosubstrates. It also exhibited exceptional antiproliferative activity against MM cells, with an IC50 of 0.007 nmol/L against NCI-H929 cells. These data strongly support I034 as a novel payload for ADC development.

Then comes the second challenge was how to choose a proper targeting antigen for constructing DAC with high in vitro and in vivo activities. In this work, we constructed conjugates targeting CD38 and BCMA using I034 as the payload for anti-MM therapy. In both in vitro and in vivo efficacy evaluations, I034-based CELMoDACs outperformed existing auristatin-derived conjugates in terms of anti-MM activity and safety. Notably, in the NCI-H929 xenograft model, the CD38-targeting CELMoDAC, Dara-VA-I034 (8), achieved complete tumor clearance with a single dose of as low as 3 mg/kg (TGI >99%). The significant efficacy of the CD38 rather than BCMA-targeting CELMoDAC sparked our further interest. Dramatically, preliminary mechanistic exploration revealed that I034-induced IKZF1/3 degradation led to distinct fates for the two target antigens: CD38 was upregulated, while BCMA was slightly downregulated. This provides a critical insight: while degraders expand the therapeutic target space of payloads, the compatibility between the antigen and payload’s chemical biology must be carefully considered, a factor often overlooked in previous conjugates using toxin payloads. Drawing from previous DAC research and the findings in this study, compatibility can be considered from at least two aspects: First, the combination of antigen targeting and target protein degradation should synergistically exploit therapeutic vulnerabilities of specific indications. A typical example is ORM-6151, which targets CD33, widely overexpressed in AML, and utilizes a GSPT1 degrader already proven effective in AML. Second, target protein degradation can positively regulate the target antigen’s molecular biology, including the upregulation of surface antigen expression after protein of interest (POI) degradation, as discussed in this study, as well as enhancing antigen internalization even turnover, providing potential strategies for future DAC development.

Furthermore, the catalytic mechanism of CELMoD payloads and their modulation of immune-related protein homeostasis highlights the importance of considering their immune-related side effects. While preliminary safety assessments of Dara-VA-I034 showed no overt toxicity, careful monitoring during translation study remains crucial due to inter-species CRBN homology differences. CELMoD-mediated degradation of neosubstrates like IKZF1/3 is known to impact immune cell development and function. Although therapeutically promising for hematological malignancies and autoimmune diseases, this activity also carries risks of unintended immune dysregulation in other contexts. Consequently, further preclinical safety evaluations for CELMoDACs should encompass potential immune-related toxicities, particularly under repeat-dosing regimens.

Conclusions

Overall, our research provides a systematic approach for discovering conjugatable CELMoD payloads and constructing corresponding conjugates, which can serve as a paradigm for discovering CELMoDACs targeting other proteins, thus holding promise as a novel approach to advance degrader–antibody conjugate therapy toward clinical translation. CELMoD payloads containing I034 and their conjugates discovered based on this method could serve as a powerful supplement for conjugate drugs targeting multiple myeloma and plasma cell disorders.

All procedures performed on animals were in accordance with regulations and established guidelines, reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Shanghai Institute of Materia Medica, Chinese Academy of Sciences (ID: 2024-01-LJ-165). Zinc finger substrate screening model

Construction of ZF Fusion Protein-U2OS Stable Expression Cell Line: Day 1: Digest HEK293T cells and plate them in a 6 cm dish. Day 2: Virus Packaging: In an Eppendorf tube, sequentially add 300 μL of jet buffer (Polyplus), 1.2 μg of pMD2.G plasmid, 2.4 μg of psPAX2 plasmid, and 2.4 μg of the target plasmid. Discard the original culture medium from the dish and add 4 mL of complete culture medium. Add 12 μL of jet PRIME (Polyplus) to the Eppendorf tube, mix gently, incubate at room temperature for 10 min, and then evenly disperse the mixture into the culture medium, swirling gently. Day 3: Plate U2OS cells in a 6-well plate to achieve 20%–40% confluence. Day 4: After 48 h of virus production (during which fluorescence is monitored using a fluorescence microscope), aspirate the medium from the dish, filter the supernatant through a 0.45 μm filter, and collect the viral solution for later use. Discard the original U2OS culture medium, add a certain amount of viral solution (0.5/1/1.5 mL), and add 4% of the transfection reagent HitransG P to each well, mixing gently and incubating at 37 °C. Day 6: After another 48 h, change the medium and observe the culture daily until the cells grow; perform puromycin selection for 3-5 days.

Screening: Day 1: Once IKZF-U2OS cells in a T75 flask are fully confluent, digest, centrifuge at 1000 rpm (Cence, TG16-WS, Changsha, China) for 5 min, and count the cells. Plate 10,000 cells per well in a 96-well plate. Day 2: Prepare drug solutions at concentrations of 1000 and 10 nmol/L. Remove the original culture medium from the 96-well plate and add the drug solutions, incubating for 24 h. Day 3: Use a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX83) to capture images of the wells in the 96-well plate. Set light intensity to 30%, gain to 1 × , and camera resolution to 1920 × 1200 (3 CMOS). The exposure time for green fluorescence is 50 ms, and for red fluorescence, it is 100 ms. Analyze fluorescence intensity using Image J software, normalizing the data to the DMSO group with the highest fluorescence intensity and calculating the percentage of degradation.

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at 5000–10000 cells/well for different cell lines. After overnight incubation, serially diluted samples were added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 72 h (payloads) or 120 h (ADCs). Then, 10 μL of the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) or Cell Titer-Glo Luminescent (CTG) was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After gently agitating the plate, the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a BioTek Synergy microplate reader. EC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism 10.1 software. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Conjugation

For DAR-8 conjugates, a solution of daratumumab or belantamab [5 mg/mL in PBS (pH 7.4) containing 2 mmol/L EDTA] was treated with 7 molar equivalents of TCEP and shaken (600 rpm, Ruicheng Instrument, Thermo-Mixer Heat/Cool TCS10, Hangzhou, China) at 37 °C for 2 h. Then, 12 molar equivalents of maleimide linker-payload were added to the entirely reduced antibody solution with a total DMSO concentration <7.5% (v/v). The solution was incubated (600 rpm, Ruicheng Instrument, Thermo-Mixer Heat/Cool TCS10, Hangzhou, China) for another 2 h at room temperature. For DAR∼4 conjugates, the molar equivalents of TCEP and linker-payloads are 2.5 and 4.5, respectively. Post-reaction, the conjugates were purified and buffer-exchanged with PBS (pH 7.4) using an Amicon Ultra 50K centrifugal filters device and were sterile-filtered (0.22 μm PES filters).

Chromatographic characterization

Reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) for antibodies and conjugates was performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II (LC03) instrument equipped with a reversed-phase column (Agilent PLRP-S 1000 Å, 2.1 mm × 150 mm, 8 μm). The column temperature was 80 °C, water (0.1% trifluoroacetic acid) was used as phase A, acetonitrile (0.1% trifluoroacetic acid) was used as phase B, and the elution time was 35 min with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The gradient was 25%–50% A from 3 to 28 min, 50%–95% A from 28 to 30 min, and then 95%–25% A from 30 to 32 min. UV detection was monitored at 280 nm. Hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC) for antibodies and conjugates was performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II (LC03) instrument equipped with a HIC column (TOSOH TSKgel Butyl-NPR, 4.6 cm × 10 cm). The column temperature was 25 °C, 2 mol/L (NH4)2SO4 (containing 25 mmol/L Na2HPO4) was used as phase A, 25 mmol/L Na2HPO4 (pH 7.01) was used as phase B, isopropanol (IPA) was used as phase C, and the elution time was 35 min with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The gradient was 75% A and 25% B to 85% B and 15% C from 0 to 25 min, 85% B and 15% C to 75% A and 25% B from 25 to 30 min, and then kept 75% A and 25% B to 35 min. UV detection was monitored at 280 nm. Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) for antibodies and conjugates was performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II (LC03) instrument equipped with a SEC column (TOSOH TSKgel G3000SWxl, 7.8 cm × 30 cm). The column temperature was 25 °C, 1 × PBS was used as elution buffer, and the elution time was 35 min with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. UV detection was monitored at 280 nm.

Western blot

In the Western blotting procedure, cells were first treated with the desired concentration of the test drug for varying durations. Following treatment, the cells were collected by centrifugation at 4 °C and 500×g to pellet the cells, and the pellet was washed once with pre-cooled 1 × PBS to remove any residual medium. Total protein extraction was then performed using the Bestbio Total Protein Extraction Kit, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific), and protein samples were normalized to ensure consistent concentration across samples. For sample preparation, 60 μL of each normalized protein sample was mixed with 15 μL of 5 × SDS loading buffer, followed by heating at 100 °C for 5 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 20,000×g for 10 min. A total of 5 μL of PageRuler Prestained Protein Ladder (Thermo Scientific) and 15 μL of each prepared sample were loaded onto an ACE 12% FuturePAGE gel, and electrophoresis was conducted in Bis-Tris running buffer at 160 V for 45 min to achieve molecular weight-based protein separation. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane at 100 V for 75 min. After transfer, the membrane was blocked at room temperature for 1 h using 5% skim milk in 1 × TBST. The membrane was then washed three times with 1 × TBST for 10 min each at room temperature. Next, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody (1:1000 dilution using Western blot primary antibody dilution buffer). The next day, the membrane was washed three times in 1 × TBST, with each wash lasting 10 min at room temperature. It was then incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000 dilution using 1 × TBST) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by another round of three 10-min washes in 1 × TBST. Protein bands were visualized using ECL luminescence reagent (abs920) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The image of the membrane was taken by the imaging system Azure 600.

ELISA assay

A 96-well ELISA plate was coated with recombinant human CD38 protein (0.1 μg 100/μL per well) for 16 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, wells were washed with PBST buffer (PBS with 0.1% Tween-20), and blocked with a blocking buffer (PBS with 1% bovine serum albumin) for 30 min. All samples were diluted in the blocking buffer and added to each well and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Following three washes with PBST, the cells were incubated with goat anti-human IgG-HRP conjugate at room temperature for 1 h. After washing, TMB single-component substrate solution was added and incubated in the dark. 2N-H2SO4 was added for reaction termination after 30 min, and then the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a SPARK multimode microplate reader (TECAN). All assays were performed in triplicate.

Liver S9-mediated cleavage assay

NAC-LPs were prepared by co-incubation of MC-linker-payloads and 5 molar equivalents of NAC under 37 °C for 2 h. Next, 50 μL of NAC-LPs (200 μmol/L) and 100 μL human liver S9 (20 mg/mL) or rat liver S9 (20 mg/mL) were added to 350 μL CTSB activity buffer (50 mmol/L sodium acetate, 100 mmol/L NaCl, 8 mmol/L l-cysteine, and 1 mmol/L EDTA, pH 5.0). Aliquots (100 μL) were taken at 24 h. Then, 400 μL methanol containing 50 μmol/L E64 was added, and centrifugation was performed for 10 min for protein sedimentation. Released payloads were analyzed on HPLC and LC–MS and quantified using the standard curve method. All assays were performed in duplicates.

Plasma stability test

NAC-LPs (200 μmol/L) was dispensed into undiluted BALB/c murine or human plasma, resulting in a final concentration of 2 μmol/L and incubated at 37 °C. Subsequent aliquots of 50 μL were systematically extracted at successive intervals (precisely at 0, 12, and 24 h) and conserved at −80 °C pending utilization. Released payloads were analyzed on HPLC and LC–MS, with the quantification derived through the standard curve method. All tests were performed in duplicates.

In vivo xenograft mouse model of NCI-H929

A suspension of 1 × 107 NCI-H929 cells in 100 μL of 1: 1 PBS/Matrigel solution was injected subcutaneously into the armpit of female CB-17 SCID mice aged 5 to 6 weeks (Day 0). On Day 11, the tumors reached an average volume of ∼160 mm3, mice were randomized to each group, and each drug or PBS was given to the mice at a single dose of 6 or 3 mg/kg through injection via the tail vein, with a volume of 10 mL/kg. The size of the tumor and body weight were monitored twice a week. The tumor volume was estimated according to Eq. (1):(1)Tumorvolume=1/2×Length×Width2

In circumstances wherein the calculated tumor volume exceeded the threshold of 2000 mm3, the tumor’s diameter surpassed 20 mm, or when the mice showed obvious signs of severe distress, humane euthanasia was promptly carried out.

Complete blood count and blood chemistry tests

After mice were treated with PBS or Dara-VA-I034 30 mg/kg for 7 days, approximately 0.5 mL of blood was drawn from each mouse (n = 3) for complete blood count (CBC) and blood biochemical tests (ALT, AST, TP, ALB, GLOB, UREA, and CREA). Complete blood count parameters were determined using a Sysmex hematology analyzer (XN-1000V, Japan) with supporting reagents. Serum biochemistry parameters were determined using a Biochemical analyzer (Cobas C501 Roche, Switzerland) with supporting reagents.

In vivo degradation test on NCI-H929 xenograft model

A suspension of 1 × 107 NCI-H929 cells in 100 μL of 1: 1 PBS/Matrigel solution was injected subcutaneously into the armpit of female CB-17 SCID mice aged 5 to 6 weeks. When the tumors reached an average volume of ∼160 mm3, mice were treated with PBS or Dara-VA-I034 (6 mg/kg). At 24, 48, and 72 h after administration, mice were then euthanized and the tumors were obtained for Western blot to detect the regulation of Dara-VA-I034 on the protein levels of IKZF1, IZKF3 and CD38.

Flow cytometry detection of CD38 expression

CD38 Monoclonal Antibody (eBioscience, 25-0389-42) was used to monitor the CD38 expression of cells by FACS. Briefly, cells with or without drug treatment were harvested and stained with CD38 Monoclonal Antibody-PE-Cyanine7 for 15 min, and then analyzed by using flow cytometry (CytoFLEX flow cytometer, Beckman Coulter, Inc.) (Excitation/emission spectrum: 569/780 nm) with analysis carried out using the FlowJo software.

RT-qPCR

Cell lines were exposed to drug or DMSO, then RNA was extracted from cell lines. After reverse-engineering the RNA of the sample into cDNA, the primers of the target gene (CD38, ACTB) were designed to perform PCR amplification reaction on the sample. Then fluorescent chemical materials (AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix, Q121-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) were added to the reaction system so that the fluorescent signal emitted by each cycle product could be monitored in real-time. A fluorescent signal amplification curve was obtained with the accumulation of reaction products to quantify the template. The expression of genes was normalized to the geometric mean of the housekeeping gene ACTB to control the variability in expression levels and was analyzed using the 2–ΔΔCt method. All samples within an experiment were reverse-transcribed at the same time. n = 3 biologically independent samples.

Primer sequence:

CD38-F: 5′-CAACTCTGTCTTGGCGTCAGT-3′

CD38-R: 5′-CCCATACACTTTGGCAGTCTACA-3′

ACTB-F: 5′-CATGTACGTTGCTATCCAGGC-3′

ACTB-R: 5′-CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGAT-3′

RNA-seq analysis and gene set enrichment

Samples were prepared after 48 h of 40 nmol/L drug treatment on NCI-H929 cells. Sequencing reads were aligned using HISAT2 to the human reference sequence (UCSC hg38 assembly) after removal of adaptor contamination. Samples were used to compress and sort the aligned files with Samtools, and gene expression values were quantitated with featureCounts. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq2 package in R software. Genes with an absolute log2(fold-change) ≥ 1 and an adjusted P-value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered DEGs. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA software (v4.1.0; Broad Institute). GSEA was used to identify enriched Hallmark gene sets (MSigDB) within the transcriptome. Gene sets were ranked based on differential gene expression between the vehicle and treatment groups. Significance was determined using a weighted Kolmogorov-Smirnov-like statistic with a two-sided permutation test, and P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Proteomic analysis

Samples were prepared after 48 h of 40 nmol/L drug treatment on NCI-H929 cells. The protein of the sample was processed through extraction, digestion, peptide desalting and quantification, data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry (Vanquish Neo coupled with Compass HyStar, Bruker, Germany), and protein identification to obtain the original proteomic data. P-values and Fold change (FC) for the proteins between the two groups were calculated using R package “+-test”. The thresholds of fold change (>1.2 or < 0.83) and P-value <0.05 were used to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). Bioinformatic analysis of proteomic data was performed with the Majorbio Cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was done by Excel and GraphPad Prism 10.1. Comparisons were made using a two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and all the results are expressed as a mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise mentioned.
