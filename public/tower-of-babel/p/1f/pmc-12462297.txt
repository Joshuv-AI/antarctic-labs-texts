Dissecting the genetic architecture of circadian rhythms in human tissues using a quantitative circadian deviation score
Authors: Zheyu Li, Liang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCircadian rhythms influence various physiological and behavioral processes, including sleep, metabolism, and immune response. Although key regulatory factors of biological clocks have been identified and genome-wide association studies have pinpointed some genetic variants linked to sleep traits, the genetic architecture underlying circadian rhythms remains incompletely understood.ResultsHere, we introduce the circadian deviation score, a novel quantitative trait that measures circadian disruption at the molecular level. Derived from gene expression levels of thousands of circadian genes across Human tissues, this score helps identify tissue-specific genetic influences on circadian rhythms. Our analysis reveals 654 SNPs, which we named Circ-SNPs, associated with global circadian disruption at the expression level. These include previously known SNPs Linked to insomnia, chronotype, and circadian rhythm, as well as new SNPs that enhance understanding of circadian regulation. Most Circ-SNPs exhibit significant associations with the circadian deviation score in the small intestine and adrenal gland, with about 19.4% situated on the X chromosome, highlighting sex-specific differences in circadian disorders. Circ-SNPs often reside near the 3′ end of transcripts, indicating their potential regulatory roles, particularly in post-transcriptional processes. The genes harboring Circ-SNPs, which we named Circ-regulators, are enriched for known circadian traits. DrugBank analysis shows 18 of 122 protein-coding Circ-regulators are targetable by 163 existing drugs, including six approved for sleep disorders.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the potential for repurposing existing drugs to treat circadian-related disorders and provide a deeper understanding of the genetic components of circadian rhythms and sleep disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-025-03767-4.

Background

Circadian rhythms, intrinsic 24-h tempos, underlie a wide range of daily cycles in physiological and behavioral processes, including activity levels, sleep–wake cycles, and eating-fasting cycles [1]. These rhythmic patterns are orchestrated by gene networks controlled by clock genes such as CLOCK, PER, and CRY, among others [2]. These core clock genes collectively influence nearly half of all mammalian genes [3], exhibiting tissue-specific coordination through feedback loops [4]. This orchestration results in the oscillation of gene expression levels in sync with the circadian rhythm, impacting various physiological and behavioral processes, such as the regulation of sleep, metabolism, and immune response [2].

Given the pivotal role of circadian rhythms in the regulation of daily bodily functions, several genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have sought to identify genetic variants associated with circadian rhythms [5–9]. Since the endogenous circadian rhythms align with external cues like light patterns and food intake [10], many of these GWAS studies rely on sleep-related traits reflecting daily light–dark cycles, such as sleep duration, sleep quality, and chronotype measurements. However, the limited suitable traits capable of accurately characterizing circadian rhythms present a challenge to these studies. A more direct approach would involve analyzing molecular-level traits obtained from gene expression data, particularly time-series gene expression levels exhibiting ~ 24-h rhythms. Nonetheless, obtaining rhythmic expression data from humans is challenging, and the variability in the daily rhythms of core clock genes across different tissues introduces additional complexity [11].

To overcome this challenge, we developed a circadian deviation score to quantify disruptions at the molecular level for individuals. The circadian deviation score incorporates the expression of thousands of circadian genes to present a tissue-specific phenotype, leveraging data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Project [12]. We then identified genetic variants associated with the circadian deviation score, which we named Circ-SNPs. This strategy not only circumvents the statistical power limitations for identifying trans-acting eQTLs associated with individual circadian genes but also provides a more comprehensive and robust understanding of the genetic architecture governing gene regulation in the context of circadian rhythms.

We investigated genetic variants linked to circadian deviation traits to uncover the molecular mechanisms behind circadian rhythms and identify new Master regulators. Through our research, we identified 654 significant Circ-SNPs. A substantial portion of them is in close proximity to established variants associated with circadian traits, including “insomnia,” “chronotype measurement,” and “circadian rhythm,” validating our approach. Our drug repurposing analysis revealed that Circ-SNP genes are more likely to be targeted by drugs approved for mental health, neurological, and gastrointestinal diseases, conditions frequently intertwined with circadian disruptions, compared to all drug targets recorded in the DrugBank database [13]. These existing drugs represent potential candidates for remediating circadian disruptions. Our comprehensive exploration of circadian regulators advances our understanding of the molecular components underlying circadian disruptions and paves the way for more targeted and effective investigations into the genetics of circadian regulation.

Results

Circadian variation at the gene expression level

We introduced a circadian deviation score for each tissue of an individual, reflecting the extent of the difference from the population median at the circadian gene expression levels. The larger the difference, the higher the deviation score. An extraordinarily large deviation score can indicate abnormal circadian regulation. We first calculated the expression deviation of each circadian gene expression from the population. The final circadian deviation score is a weighted sum over hundreds or thousands of circadian genes for a specific tissue (details in Methods).

Applying the circadian deviation score, we analyzed circadian gene expression patterns across various normal human tissues in GTEx [12]. The circadian gene list for each tissue was derived through homolog mapping with the circadian genes of the olive baboon, as identified via tissue-dependent time-series expression profiling [11]. Out of the 54 tissues in GTEx, we focused on 23 that had a sufficient number of samples (≥ 70) and had circadian gene lists available. The distributions of circadian deviation scores in each tissue, illustrated in Additional file 1: Fig. S1, exhibit a right skewness, indicating the presence of individuals with severely disrupted circadian gene expression. This aligns with the observation that circadian disruption is common within human populations [14]. The unusually large circadian deviation scores observed in certain individuals enabled genome-wide mapping of genetic variants associated with aberrant circadian rhythms at the molecular level, as these outlier profiles provided the necessary signal for detecting associations in the GWAS analysis.

Genome-wide mapping of SNPs associated with circadian disruptions across multiple tissues

We examined over 10 million SNPs for associations with the circadian deviation score in 23 tissues (details in the “Methods” section), identifying a total of 654 significant SNPs (named Circ-SNPs) across 16 tissues (Additional file 2: Table S1, p-value ≤ 5 × 10−8, corresponding FDR < 0.02). The Q-Q plot and Manhattan plot for each tested tissue are presented in Additional file 1: Figs. S2–S24, with the corresponding genomic inflation factors summarized in Additional file 2: Table S2. As shown in Fig. 1a, the small intestine—terminal ileum and adrenal gland—harbor the majority of these Circ-SNP associations (59.6% and 20.2%), with other tissues showing moderate quantities. Notably, the number of associations in specific tissues is not influenced by either sample size (Fig. 1b) or the number of circadian genes (Fig. 1c).

Tissue-specific analysis of 16 tissues with mapped Circ-SNPs. a Number of Circ-SNP associations identified in each tissue. b Count of available donors for calculating deviation scores within each tissue. c Number of circadian genes included in the calculation of deviation scores for each tissue

Analyzing the summary statistics of the discovered Circ-SNPs, we consistently observed a positive coefficient for the genotype variable when the alternative allele is the minor allele or a negative coefficient when the reference allele is the minor allele. Because we coded the genotype as the number of alternative alleles, this is consistent with the notion that the association study identifies deviation from the population mean and that minor alleles are more likely to be risk alleles [15]. As such, more minor alleles increase the risk of circadian disruption for an individual. This pattern contrasts with the result using randomly selected non-significant SNPs (Fig. 2), suggesting that the identified Circ-SNPs may be under selective pressure or linked to functional variants.

Minor alleles of Circ-SNPs correspond to higher deviation scores. Specifically, all minor alleles of Circ-SNPs result in higher deviation scores. In contrast, for approximately 50% of randomly selected SNPs, their minor alleles lead to higher deviation scores

We further investigated whether the major allele is the ancestral allele in the identified Circ-SNPs. Ancestral alleles were retrieved from the Ensembl database (https://useast.ensembl.org/index.html) using biomaRt [16], which utilizes phylogenetic modeling of multispecies alignments to ascertain ancestral alleles. Among the 654 Circ-SNPs, ancestral allele information was available for 624. Of these, 548 (87.2%) had the ancestral allele as their major allele (Additional file 2: Table S3). In contrast, when we randomly selected 200,000 non-significant SNPs, 74.2% had the ancestral allele as the major allele. Therefore, the major alleles of Circ-SNPs are enriched for the ancestral alleles compared to random SNPs (p-value = 6.8 × 10−3, proportion test). The enrichment of the ancestral allele in Circ-SNPs further suggests that these loci may be subject to selective pressures or linked to nearby variants under selection, in contrast to randomly selected SNPs.

We found that Circ-SNPs are distributed throughout the genome, as illustrated in the Manhattan plot in Fig. 3a. Some of them, such as LCT, PRKN, and DPP6, have been associated with circadian-related phenotypes such as chronotype measurements, insomnia, and circadian-associated mental health conditions, as reported by the GWAS Catalog [17] or the GeneHancer [18].

Circ-SNPs exhibit a clustering tendency, forming SNP clusters (spanning 1 M bp with at least two Circ-SNPs), as depicted by the 102 colored bands in Fig. 3a. These SNP clusters are likely due to linkage disequilibrium (LD), where nearby variants are inherited together [19]. Clusters exclusively comprising Circ-SNPs found in the same tissue are marked by blue bands (84 clusters in total), whereas clusters containing Circ-SNPs from different tissues are denoted by red bands (18 clusters). The upset plot in Additional file 1: Fig. S25 illustrates the distribution of these SNP clusters among tissues. Interestingly, the prostate shares Circ-SNP clusters with the brain (substantia nigra), the heart (atrial appendage), the adrenal gland, and the small intestine (terminal ileum). SNP clusters shared by multiple tissues may regulate circadian rhythm synchronously or coordinately across different tissues.

Out of the 654 discovered Circ-SNPs, 127 reside on the X chromosome (19.4%), despite only 3.3% of the tested SNPs being on the X chromosome (p-value = 8.6 × 10−59, hypergeometric test). In Fig. 3b, we illustrate the X-linked genes containing these Circ-SNPs. Among them, DMD, SMARCA1, SUPT20HL2, and IL1RAPL1 are associated with mental or behavioral disorders like depression, schizophrenia, and anxiety, as documented in the GWAS Catalog [17, 20–24]. IL1RAPL1 is also linked to chronotype measurements and circadian rhythm [25, 26]. Noteworthy, among the 8 Circ-SNPs identified in the brain substantia nigra, 7 are situated on the X chromosome. Among these Circ-SNPs, 3 are linked to genes associated with X chromosome inactivation, namely TSIX, FTX, and JPX [27, 28]. Circadian rhythms may vary between males and females due to hormonal differences and other biological factors [29–31]. Sex differences in the prevalence of certain circadian-related disorders, such as insomnia, have been observed [32, 33]. Our reported Circ-SNPs on the X chromosome provide valuable insights into understanding these sex-specific differences.

We examined the genomic locations of the identified Circ-SNPs. As illustrated in Additional file 1: Fig. S26, the Majority of Circ-SNPs, Like all SNPs we examined, were located in non-coding regions. Interestingly, Circ-SNPs tend to be enriched near the 3′ end of transcripts. In Fig. 4, the red dots show the distance distribution of Circ-SNPs from the transcript boundaries, while the red curves represent their Lowess smoothed density. The blue shaded areas indicate the smoothed density curves of randomly selected SNPs, Matched in number to the Circ-SNPs, over 1000 sampling iterations. Remarkably, the red curve for Circ-SNPs consistently surpasses those of the randomly selected SNPs at the 3′ end. This trend suggests that Circ-SNPs may have regulatory roles, possibly in post-transcriptional processes.

Upon further examination, we found that a noteworthy portion (27.7%) of these Circ-SNPs overlaps with cis-quantitative trait loci (cis-QTLs) reported by GTEx, with QTL type classifications (eQTL, sQTL, edQTL) directly derived from the GTEx dataset. Specifically, out of 654 Circ-SNPs, 164 are expression QTLs (eQTLs), with 26 being tissue-specific; 48 are alternative splicing QTLs (sQTLs), with one being tissue-specific; and 11 are editing QTLs (edQTLs). Moreover, certain Circ-SNPs exhibit multiple regulatory functions, with 36 falling into two types of QTLs and 3 encompassing all three types of QTLs (refer to Additional file 2: Table S4). We found no overlap between the identified Circ-SNPs and the trans-eQTLs and trans-sQTLs reported by GTEx, primarily due to the limited number of trans-QTLs identified by GTEx, stemming from limitations of GTEx and inherent challenges in detecting trans-QTLs [34]. Our findings collectively suggest that Circ-SNPs likely exert their regulatory functions through transcriptional, post-transcriptional mechanisms, or a combination of both.

Circ-SNPs are closely linked to known circadian-related traits

We compared our Circ-SNPs to genetic variants documented in the GWAS catalog [17], revealing that the Majority of these Circ-SNPs are novel, with only 5 having been reported in previous studies. To expand the signals, we designated the existing SNPs in the GWAS catalog within 1 Mb of our Circ-SNPs as proxies for further comparisons. In total, our Circ-SNPs were Linked to 34,262 proxy SNPs in the GWAS catalog.

Out of the 10,039 traits reported in the GWAS catalog, we selected 34 traits, including “circadian rhythm,” “chronotype measurement,” “sleep time,” and “insomnia,” identified through a keyword search for “sleep” (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/search?query=sleep). We observed that 24 of the 34 “circadian” traits (Additional file 2: Table S5) were among the 3551 traits associated with the proxy SNPs. This suggests an enrichment of associations with circadian traits among the proxy SNPs (24/34 = 0.71 vs. 3551/10,039 = 0.35, P = 3.0 × 10−5, hypergeometric test).

We employed ezQTL [35], a web-based tool for integrative QTL colocalization with GWAS data, to conduct colocalization analysis in proximity to the positions of the discovered Circ-SNPs. For this analysis, we used eQTL data from GTEx [12] and chose a study [36] investigating sleep duration as the phenotype. We obtained LD information from the European population in 1000 Genomes [37] and set the cis-QTL distance to 100 Kb. With eCAVIAR [38], a colocalization analysis method integrated within ezQTL, we identified 27 SNPs colocalizing between eQTLs and sleep duration SNPs across 25 Circ-SNPs regions (details in Additional file 2: Table S6). These SNPs correspond to 14 lead SNPs that regulate 22 genes, making them strong candidates for causal variants (Fig. 5). Among these genes, UVSSA, PLOD2, and RPL4 have been annotated by the GWAS Catalog [17] and GeneHancer [18] for association with insomnia, sleep duration, or circadian rhythm. Therefore, the discovered Circ-SNPs can aid in pinpointing causal SNPs in the sleep duration GWAS study. Furthermore, these findings reinforce the link between our Circ-SNPs and circadian rhythms.

Genes containing Circ-SNPs are associated with known circadian-related traits

In addition to the SNP-level analyses, we conducted analyses at the gene level. Our initial examination of genes harboring highly significant Circ-SNPs (labeled in Fig. 3a) and X-chromosome Circ-SNPs (labeled in Fig. 3b) sheds light on their involvement in circadian traits. To delve deeper, we probed all genes harboring the Circ-SNPs, which we designated as Circ-regulators, aiming to unveil their associations with reported circadian traits. The GWAS catalog [17] annotated 4236 genes containing SNPs associated with circadian traits. Our Circ-SNPs were Mapped to 199 Circ-regulators, with 48 of them overlapping with the 4236 GWAS circadian regulators. This indicates a significant enrichment of GWAS circadian regulators within our Circ-regulators (Additional file 2: Tables S7 and S8; 48/199 = 0.241 vs. 4236/69,222 = 0.061, p-value = 1.6 × 10−16, hypergeometric test). Interestingly, while 62.7% (2,654 out of 4236) of the GWAS-identified circadian regulators show circadian rhythmicity in their expression levels, only 36.7% (73 out of 199) of our identified Circ-regulators exhibit this rhythmicity (p-value = 3.3 × 10−13, proportion test).

As illustrated in Fig. 6, there is a notable enrichment of our Circ-regulators in circadian traits in comparison to all GWAS genes. Specifically, we examined the percentage distribution of our Circ-regulators associated with each circadian trait, comparing them to all GWAS genes. Out of 34 circadian traits documented in the GWAS catalog, 19 are associated with at least one Circ-regulator containing Circ-SNPs. Among them, 11 traits showed a higher enrichment ratio among Circ-regulators (Additional file 2: Table S9).

Repurposing existing drugs for genes containing Circ-SNPs

To assess the druggability of proteins coded by genes harboring Circ-SNPs (referred to as Circ-regulators) and repurpose existing drugs for the potential treatment of circadian-related disorders, we utilized the DrugBank database [13]. Among the 122 protein-coding Circ-regulators, 18 can be targeted by 163 existing drugs (Additional file 2: Table S10). Notably, six of these drugs—Doxepin, Desipramine, Amitriptyline, Doxylamine, Trimipramine, and Propiomazine—were approved for the treatment of sleep disorders and disturbances, including insomnia.

According to DrugBank, these 163 drugs targeting the Circ-regulators are associated with nearly 500 health conditions. We categorized these health conditions into 18 groups based on their symptoms and corresponding anatomical systems (Additional file 2: Table S11). As shown in Fig. 7a, mental health conditions predominate among these categories, accounting for 19.55% of all involved health conditions. Compared to the background distribution for all drugs (Fig. 7b, Additional file 2: Table S12), drugs targeting mental health conditions (8.61%, p-value = 1.1 × 10−15, proportion test, Additional file 2: Table S13) are significantly enriched for our identified Circ-regulators. This enrichment aligns with the comorbidity of individuals with circadian disruptions, including major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, anxiety, and schizophrenia [39]. It indicates potential shared mechanisms between mental disorders and circadian-related disorders. Drugs formulated for these mental conditions may be repurposed for circadian-related disorders, as their targeted genes align with the identified Circ-regulators associated with circadian expression disruption scores.

Less prominent categories (less than 5%) were consolidated into “Other Conditions.” Enriched condition categories are marked: *** P = 1.1 × 10−15; ** P = 5.7 × 10−4; * P = 8.0 × 10.−3

Gene targets of drugs used to treat gastrointestinal conditions are also significantly enriched among the identified Circ-regulators (p-value = 5.7 × 10−4, proportion test). The biological activities of the gastrointestinal tract, including cell proliferation, motility, hormone and gastric acid production, nutrient absorption, and microbiome structure, follow daily diurnal fluctuations. These fluctuations are primarily influenced by circadian rhythms, which play a crucial role in hormone production, sleep/wake cycles, and food intake [40].

Moreover, neurological conditions also emerge as an enriched category (p-value = 8.0 × 10−3, proportion test). Some neurological conditions are closely related to altered circadian rhythms. For example, disruptions in the circadian timing system and homeostatic sleep–wake cycles may influence susceptibility to seizures [41]. All these results highlight the potential of repurposing existing drugs targeting Circ-regulators for the treatment of circadian-related disorders, particularly those affecting mental health, gastrointestinal function, and neurological conditions.

Discussion

In this study, we proposed a deviation score based on circadian gene expression levels to capture circadian disruptions Manifested at the molecular level in individuals from the natural Human population in GTEx. Utilizing this deviation score as an intermediate phenotype, we discovered 654 significant SNPs. Despite the limitation of unavailable sampling times in GTEx, we validated our approach by linking novel SNP associations to circadian rhythms from multiple angles, such as their proximity to known circadian-related SNPs and localization within genes associated with circadian traits. Additionally, we contributed new biological insights, supporting the sex-specific nature of circadian rhythms and highlighting opportunities for drug repurposing.

Notably, in calculating the proposed deviation scores, the circadian gene sets were adopted from the olive baboon, and gene-specific weights were derived based on their rhythmicity in this primate. While this dataset is a close proxy to humans, it may not perfectly represent human circadian biology. Nevertheless, the validity of our approach is supported by multiple consistent findings throughout this study.

The robustness of our method is confirmed by the consistent pattern where individuals harboring minor alleles always exhibit higher deviation scores for each of our detected Circ-SNPs. In addition, the enrichment of Circ-SNPs for the major allele being the ancestral allele suggests that these variants are more likely to be preserved and functionally important compared to randomly selected SNPs [42, 43].

A noteworthy observation is that the vast majority (523 out of 654) of the discovered Circ-SNPs were found in the small intestine. terminal ileum and adrenal gland, despite the limited number of samples in these tissues. Furthermore, the significant presence of Circ-SNPs on the sex chromosome raises intriguing possibilities regarding the implications of sex on circadian rhythms. Although most Circ-SNPs are not reported by the GWAS Catalog [17], they exhibit strong colocalization with established GWAS SNPs responsible for circadian-related traits. Furthermore, our Circ-SNPs often reside in genes with functional consequences related to circadian traits such as “insomnia,” “chronotype measurement,” and “circadian rhythm.” Notably, many proteins encoded by genes harboring Circ-SNPs serve as targets for drugs treating diseases and conditions closely linked to circadian rhythms. All these connections validate the reliability of our proposed deviation score method in detecting meaningful SNPs associated with circadian disruptions.

Conclusions

In summary, our study not only unveils novel insights into circadian disruptions at the molecular level but also establishes the robustness and applicability of our deviation score method. The identification of Circ-SNPs, their colocalization with reported GWAS variants, and their association with drug-targeted proteins underscore the relevance of our findings to circadian-related traits and potential therapeutic avenues. Our study will help us better understand the genetic landscape underlying circadian rhythms.

Methods

Assembly of tissue-specific human circadian genes

Circadian rhythms extend beyond the core clock components and their immediate targets, encompassing a vast majority of genes with rhythmic transcription in mammals [2, 11]. Nonetheless, a comprehensive repertoire of human circadian genes is still lacking. In a study by Mure et al. [11], numerous circadian genes in the olive baboon were identified by analyzing the periodicity and rhythmicity in a set of time-series gene expression data. Based on the one-to-one homology provided by Ensembl (Ensembl Genes 106) [44], we acquired a set of human orthologs of these baboon circadian genes to be human circadian genes. Mure et al. examined a total of 64 distinct tissues, each contributing to an independent human circadian gene list.

Pre-processing of genotype-tissue expression data

To explore the disruption of circadian gene expression in normal human tissue samples, we utilized the GTEx data [11, 12]. Tissues that had available circadian gene Lists and consisted of more than 70 samples in GTEx were considered in our analysis (23 tissues). We selected all genes with expression greater than 0.1 transcripts per million (TPM) (and with at least six reads) in at least 20% of the samples in the specific tissue of interest. Subsequently, we normalized gene expression values across samples using the Trimmed Mean of M values (TMM) implemented in the R package edgeR [45]. Finally, expression values were further transformed into Z values across samples using an inverse normal transform [46]. The Z-transformed expression values of circadian genes were used in the calculation of the circadian deviation score, as outlined below.

Mapping of genetic variants associated with circadian deviation scores

We scanned over 10 million SNPs, whose genotype calls were provided by GTEx [12], to test for associations with circadian deviation scores. We removed rare variants with the minor allele frequency ≤ 0.01. SNPs with more than 5% missing genotypes were also discarded. For the remaining SNPs, missing genotypes were imputed based on reference and alternative allele frequencies. For SNPs on autosomes, their genotypes were coded as the number of alternative alleles. SNPs located on sex chromosomes were handled differently for females and Males. Females underwent the same coding procedure as autosomal SNPs, while Males, possessing only one X chromosome, were coded with either 0 (reference allele) or 2 (alternative allele). Finally, we retained SNPs with ≥ 5 individuals in at least two genotype groups (a total of nonredundant 10,826,972 SNPs across 23 considered tissues). With the retained SNPs, we performed 231,818,722 Linear regressions to detect associations in the 23 tissues.

We Mapped associations between SNPs and circadian deviations for different tissues separately. A multiple Linear regression model was fit to examine the relationship between circadian deviation scores in a specific tissue and the genotypes of a tested SNP, along with additional covariates obtained from GTEx, including the top 5 genotyping principal components, a set of Probabilistic Estimation of Expression Residuals (PEER) factors, sequencing platform (Illumina HiSeq 2000 or HiSeq X) and protocol (PCR-based or PCR-free), and sex. SNPs with a p-value ≤ 5 × 10−8 were considered significant and designated as Circ-SNPs (Circadian-related SNPs).

Investigation of potential confounding from tissue sampling time

Since circadian genes exhibit rhythmicity in expression, if samples with a specific genotype were collected from the donors around the same time during the day (i.e., more synchronized), such a genotype group may exhibit higher or lower expression compared to other genotype groups. This could potentially lead to false positives in our discoveries. Nonetheless, information on tissue sampling time is absent in GTEx. To address the possibility of different degrees of synchronization across genotype groups confounding our Circ-SNP discovery, we took the following steps.

For each identified Circ-SNP, we assessed whether individuals sharing the same genotype exhibited greater synchronization in circadian gene expression compared to those with different genotypes. Specifically, within each tissue, we calculated the Spearman correlation coefficient of all circadian gene expression for every pair of individuals. These pairwise correlations were then grouped based on whether the individual pairs shared the same genotype or had different genotypes at the given SNP. Wilcoxon tests were applied to compare the two groups. For the majority (95.1%) of the Circ-SNPs we identified, the within-genotype correlations were not higher than the between-group correlations (p-values ≤0.05, Wilcoxon tests). Therefore, sampling time does not appear to be a significant confounding factor in our search for Circ-SNPs.

Data Availability Statement

The following original data were obtained from online resources: olive baboon circadian gene sets [11] ; gene expression levels and genotype data from the GTEx project [12]; Gencode annotation file (release 45) [47]; and SNP information included in dbSNP [48]. Our source code (under an OSI-compliant open source license) and the generated summary statistics are available at [https://github.com/lee-zheyu/Circadian-rhythm-GWAS-by-Circ-deviation-score] (https://github.com/lee-zheyu/Circadian-rhythm-GWAS-by-Circ-deviation-score) [49] or [10.5281/zenodo.16824305] (10.5281/zenodo.16824305) [50].
