Protocol for the economic evaluation of integrated longitudinal public health interventions in a cluster randomised controlled trial setting: the healthy life trajectories initiative
Authors: Vivek U Padvetnaya, Ruguoser Liegise, Riddhi Sharma, Manohar Prasad Prabhu, TK Nagabharana, Kumar Gavali Suryanarayana, Ghattu V Krishnaveni, Stephen G Matthews, Kalyanaraman Kumaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn 2021, globally, cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) accounted for 35% of the 1.73 billion disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) attributed to non-communicable diseases. The Healthy Life Trajectories Initiative (HeLTI), a four-country randomised controlled trial based on the developmental origins of health and disease(DOHaD) concept, evaluates whether integrated longitudinal public health interventions started prepregnancy reduce the risk of childhood adiposity and, consequently, the development of CMDs in the offspring. This paper presents the protocol for the economic evaluation of the interventions in the Indian arm of HeLTI. The aim is to facilitate the translation of effective and affordable preventive strategies that mitigate the health, social and economic burden of CMDs.Methods and analysisA within-trial economic evaluation will be conducted from provider, health system and societal perspectives. Costs will be identified using the activity-based costing method mapped onto a theory-of-change framework that attributes interventions to health outcomes. Costs will include both direct and indirect costs as well as capital and recurring costs. Cost data will be collected using project accounts, time-use tools, surveys and key informant interviews. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) will be estimated for the fat mass index, a measure of adiposity, the primary health outcome, at the fifth year of offspring. Cost-utility and cost-consequence results will be presented for health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and other secondary health outcomes, respectively, in mothers and offsprings. Sensitivity of the results to assumptions will be analysed and uncertainty intervals will be presented. Equity impact of the outcomes will be assessed using the social determinants of health framework.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the ethics committees of the lead local institutions, CSI Holdsworth Memorial Hospital (Ref CSIHMH/ERU2019/1) and Swami Vivekananda Youth Movement (Ref IRB 004/2020-21). Findings will be disseminated through scholarly articles, policy briefs, opinion pieces, conferences, interactive sessions with stakeholders and the HeLTI website.Trial registration numberISRCTN20161479.Keywords: Cardiovascular Disease, Pregnancy, HEALTH ECONOMICS, Health Care Costs

Introduction

Background

In 2021, globally, non-communicable diseases (NCDs) with their array of behavioural, metabolic and environmental risk factors accounted for 1.73 billion disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and 43 million deaths.1 Of these, around 35% of DALYs and 50% of deaths were attributable to cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) such as cardiovascular diseases (428 million DALYs; 19.4 million deaths) and diabetes (79 million DALYs; 1.7 million deaths). Elevated blood pressure, raised blood glucose levels and abnormal body weight and blood lipid levels are significant metabolic risk factors for CMDs.1 40% of NCD deaths were premature, and 82% of these premature deaths were in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 In LMICs, the incidence of CMDs is rapidly increasing, with a shift towards younger age groups.2 3

In India, for instance, the prevalence of diabetes increased from 3.7% in 1980 to 9.1% in 2014.3 In 2021, an estimated 101 million people had diabetes, a further 136 million were prediabetic,4 and diabetes accounted for 2.57 million of the total of 13.7 million DALYs.1 Unchecked, the population with diabetes is expected to reach 125 million by 2045.5 This has significant economic implications, with annual costs estimated at US$73 billion at the country level in 20143 and at US$230 at the individual level in 2020.6

Current preventive approaches to CMDs mainly target high-risk adults and do little to prevent the development of these risks.7 8 Offering an alternative ‘developmental programming’ paradigm,9 the concept of the developmental origins of health and disease (DOHaD) posits that metabolic risk factors in adults may have their origins in childhood adiposity due to adversity faced during critical ‘plastic’ phases of their early life.10 11 The DOHaD hypothesis suggests that adverse environments encountered during conception, fetal, infancy and early childhood phases can permanently influence an individual’s risk of future CMD, by affecting the capacity of key metabolic tissues through epigenetic, metabolic and endocrine mechanisms.12,14 It is now well established that low birth weight (LBW), poor infant nutrition and excessive childhood weight gain are risk factors for poor health trajectories and the development of CMDs in later life.15 16

India is currently facing a double burden of malnutrition. In women of reproductive age group (15–49 years), 19% are underweight, 57% are anaemic and 18% of their babies have LBW.17 The undernourished Indian newborn has a ‘thin-fat phenotype’ with an average birth weight of 2.7 kg and is disproportionately adipose with a low lean body mass.18 19 This undernourishment persists in early childhood with high prevalence of stunting (36%), wasting (19%), underweight (32%) and anaemia (67%) in children under 5 years of age.17 Rates of overweight and obesity are also increasing and are estimated at 3.4% in children under 5 years of age and 24% in women of reproductive age.17 Cumulatively, this thin-fat phenotype in newborns due to in-utero undernutrition, exacerbated by poor weight gain in infancy and excessive weight gain in early childhood, leads to childhood adiposity and enhanced risk of CMDs in adult life at relatively low levels of obesity/overweight compared with Caucasian populations.

Adding complexity to this is rising gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), prevalent at 13% in India.20 Women who have experienced fetal undernutrition during their development phase have an increased risk of developing GDM, a strong metabolic risk factor for the development of CMDs in their offspring.21 Thus, India’s dual burden of high rates of adult CMDs and maternal, fetal and early childhood malnutrition is inextricably linked. In addition, psychological stress and abnormal stress responses may be an important pathway linking early life factors to adult CMD risk.11 22

Application of DOHaD concept offers a novel approach to preventing CMDs by delivering interventions in an integrated manner across the life course: in adolescent or young women to ensure they approach pregnancy in optimum health; in pregnant women to ensure adequate in utero nutrition and healthy birth weight and in infancy and early childhood to achieve optimal adiposity and development. The DOHaD approach focuses on reducing the long-term disease risk by targeting the interaction between the environment and genes during early life. Interventions need to be multifaceted as previous single-intervention trials (eg, multiple micronutrient supplementation) have shown only modest benefits for pregnancy and child outcomes, and that integrated interventions are needed to create impact in populations with multiple challenges. Previous pregnancy trials have missed the important periconceptional period; starting preconceptionally addresses this issue.

The HeLTI- EINSTEIN Trial

Set against this context, the Healthy Life Trajectories Initiative (HeLTI) is an international consortium comprising four harmonised but independently powered randomised controlled trials (RCTs) in India, South Africa, Canada and China. Deriving from the DOHaD concept, the trials aim to evaluate the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of integrated longitudinal preventive public health interventions when started at prepregnancy in reducing the risk of childhood adiposity in the offspring. More details about the trial can be accessed at the trial website.23

The Early Interventions to Support Trajectories for Healthy Life (EINSTEIN), the India-site trial under HeLTI, is a cluster RCT (cRCT) having two intervention arms (preconception and pregnancy) and one control arm. The trial interventions, which include optimising nutrition and providing micronutrient supplements, promoting healthy lifestyle behaviour and supporting maternal stress management, are targeted at women during prepregnancy, pregnancy and postpregnancy phases. The interventions are designed to achieve the primary health outcome of optimal adiposity at the 60th month of the offspring, and a host of secondary health outcomes related to nutrition, cognition and health-related quality of life (HRQoL), in both the mother and the offspring. The rationale for selecting adiposity as the primary outcome is based on the ‘thin-fat’ Indian phenotype’ characterised by low lean body mass, present at birth. Cumulatively, this thin-fat phenotype in newborns due to in utero undernutrition, exacerbated by poor weight gain in infancy and excessive weight gain in early childhood, leads to childhood adiposity and enhanced risk of CMDs in adult life at relatively low levels of obesity/overweight compared with Caucasian populations. We hypothesise that a reduction in fat mass index (FMI) (a measure of adiposity) will reduce the cardiometabolic risk in later life. The EINSTEIN trial is described in detail in our protocol paper.24

Economic evaluation of the EINSTEIN trial

Research on public health approaches aimed at preventing the development of risk factors for CMDs is gaining salience.12,14 The translation of these approaches through policy and practice needs evidence about their effectiveness, affordability and scalability.25 However, such evidence is sparse in the Indian context.26

A systematic review of within-trial economic evaluations of healthy behaviour interventions aimed at preventing and managing diabetes, set in Asian countries, reported 11 studies, of which three were based in India.27 All 11 studies used cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA). Of these, eight studies had a health system perspective, two studies had both societal and health system perspectives and one study had a societal and multipayer perspective. The India-based studies had health system, payer and societal perspectives.

In the Indian context, a handful of economic evaluation studies have reported on the cost-effectiveness,28 29 cost-utility30 31 and cost-consequences32 of nutrition-related interventions in improving health outcomes for mothers and their offspring. These studies found that, from payer,28 29 31 provider29 30 and societal28 32 perspectives, the interventions were effective when related to their costs.

To the best of our knowledge, there is no economic evaluation study that reports on the costs and effectiveness of integrated longitudinal public health interventions when introduced at the prepregnancy phase in achieving optimal adiposity in the offspring. It is important to bridge this evidence gap for an effective allocation of scarce public health resources across the life course to mitigate the health, economic and social burden of CMDs. This is critical to India’s aim to achieve sustainable development goal 3.8 and universal health coverage by 2030.26

RCTs have well-established within-trial systems for systematically tracking and measuring outcomes, the effectiveness data required for the economic evaluation. Tracking and measuring cost data can be integrated into these systems with relatively modest additional resources.33,35 The RCTs provide access to high-quality granular data at the individual level, enhance internal validity of estimates based on these data and facilitate translation of trial results through policy and practice. Leveraging RCTs in this way is substantially more efficient than designing and conducting a standalone study solely for economic evaluation.

A within-trial economic evaluation of the EINSTEIN trial interventions will be performed using CEA, cost-utility analysis (CUA) and cost-consequence analysis (CCA) techniques from a provider, health system and limited societal perspective.36,38 Towards providing a full and transparent description of the methods, this article presents the protocol for the economic evaluation.

Aims and objectives

The economic evaluation aims to inform policy and practice on the costs and effectiveness of the EINSTEIN trial interventions, when introduced at the prepregnancy phase, in reducing the risk of childhood adiposity in the offspring and achieving a range of health-related outcomes in mothers and offspring. The specific objectives of the economic evaluation are:

To estimate the cost of the resources used for producing and delivering the interventions (provider costs).

To estimate the change in costs to the health system due to changes in the health-seeking behaviour of the trial participants and their household members (health system costs).

To estimate the costs incurred by the trial participants and their households to take part in the trial (household costs).

Methods and analysis

Trial setting and population

The EINSTEIN trial is set in rural areas of the Mysore district in the southern state of Karnataka, India. The formative work for the trial commenced in 2018, while recruitment of participants for the main trial started in 2021. The economic evaluation is built into the main study and is expected to conclude in December 2032 (after the primary outcome is achieved).

Trial design

The EINSTEIN trial is a community-based, three-armed cRCT with individual villages serving as bases for the cluster. The trial covers 105 villages, with 35 villages randomly allocated to each arm. Married women residents of these villages aged over 18 years and who plan to have a child within the next 2 years are eligible to participate in the EINSTEIN trial.

For all three arms of the trial, the participants are recruited preconceptionally and are followed through in four distinct phases: prepregnancy (from recruitment date to detection of pregnancy), pregnancy (up to birthing), early infancy (from birth to 24th month of the offspring) and early childhood (from 25th to 60th month of the offspring). The goal is to detect a 0.25 SD difference between intervention and control arms, in the offspring’s FMI at the 60th month. The sample size (n=6000) has been calculated using a minimum power of 90% at a 5% significance level to achieve this goal. The detailed design of the trial can be found in our trial protocol paper.24

Intervention and comparator arms

Intervention arms

The trial participants in the preconception and pregnancy arms will receive a set of longitudinal integrated community-based public health interventions delivered by trained community health workers (CHWs). The interventions will start during prepregnancy in the preconception arm and at the time of diagnosis of pregnancy in the pregnancy arm. The interventions are discussed ahead.

Preconception arm (group 1)

Micronutrient supplementation

Participants will have daily micronutrient supplement tablets from recruitment preconceptionally, throughout pregnancy and during breastfeeding. The composition is based on the WHO/UNICEF/United Nations University international multiple micronutrient formula, given the high likelihood of multiple deficiencies.

Lifestyle behaviour change support

Participants will receive support to improve their knowledge about the importance of dietary quality, dietary diversity and healthy lifestyles and adoption of this knowledge in their daily routines. This will be provided in a village-level group and individual settings by CHWs trained in healthy conversation skills (HCS).40 The HCS, a behaviour change communication technique, improves the translation of knowledge into actual practice by improving the sense of self-efficacy in women.41 The HCS is found to be effective in inducing behavioural changes in women from socio-economically disadvantaged sections, including those in LMICs.42 HCS emphasises the role of increasing self-efficacy in promoting behaviour change. It is based on the understanding that providing participants with knowledge alone is not sufficient to change their behaviour unless they are also motivated and empowered to change. The aim will be to promote a diverse diet, achieve a normal body weight and achieve an adequate intake of micronutrients before and during pregnancy, and while breastfeeding. CHWs will provide support postnatally to encourage exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months and the timely introduction of diverse and nutritious infant weaning foods. Women will be educated about the importance of using safe water for feeding their infants after 6 months and advised to use boiled and cooled water. They will receive support to ensure that their infants are fully vaccinated, and that they adopt appropriate hygiene measures, particularly hand washing after using the toilet, changing ‘nappies’ and before preparing food, eating and feeding their infants. During the preconception stage, the group work will be held at approximately monthly intervals. There will be six modules, with the first module serving as an introductory and general health module. This will be delivered first to all women in the arm. The other five modules will be delivered cyclically (every 3–4 weeks) and address diet, physical activity and sleep, environmental exposure and hygiene, mental health and preparing for pregnancy.

Group parenting and cognitive–behavioural support (Learning Through Play Plus)

Learning Through Play Plus is a group parenting programme, integrated with a cognitive–behavioural therapy intervention (Thinking Healthy Programme) designed to address perinatal depression and improve child development in LMIC settings.43 44 It is a manual-assisted, low-literacy, potentially sustainable programme whose activities enhance children’s development. It simultaneously promotes attachment security through building parents’ ability to be sensitive to their children’s cues, and be actively involved in their children’s development. This will be delivered in 10 sessions during pregnancy and postnatally, in group and individual settings, by trained CHWs. These sessions will be delivered in phases, which will match the woman’s pace and the gestational period antenatally and the infant’s developmental stages postnatally.

Pregnancy arm (group 2)

Women in this group will receive the same package of interventions described above, but starting only after they become pregnant, which, in practice, will mean late in the first trimester. Last menstrual period dates will be monitored monthly, and women will be offered a urine pregnancy test when they report missing two consecutive periods.

Control arm (group 3)

Women in this group will receive an enhanced standard of care. In addition to encouraging vaccinations (two doses of tetanus toxoid) during pregnancy, provision of 100 tablets of iron and folate, and promoting institutional delivery, they will have a similar number of contact sessions with CHWs untrained in our behaviour change and parenting interventions.

We have a total of 36 CHWs involved in the study; nine of them exclusively deliver the intervention. On average, each intervention CHW will cover ~300 women during the course of the study. Micronutrient supplements are delivered on a monthly basis to all participants in the intervention groups. The behaviour change modules are delivered cyclically (every 3–4 weeks) during the preconception and pregnancy periods, while the parenting and cognitive–behavioural support modules are delivered as per the developmental stages.

Measurement of health outcomes

Primary health outcome

The primary health outcome, FMI, a metric of adiposity, is calculated as fat mass in kilograms divided by height in metres squared (fat mass (kg)/height (m2)). The hypothesis is that the FMI of the offspring in the intervention arms will be less than that of the offspring in the control arm. The trial interventions are considered effective when there is a difference of 0.25 SD in FMI between intervention and control arms, at the 60th month of the offspring. The FMI will be measured using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry at the 60th month of the offspring.45

Secondary health outcomes

In offspring: at the 60th month, HRQoL will be estimated from quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) derived using the EQ-5D-Y-5L Proxy instrument.46 47 Obesity will be measured using body mass index (kg/m²) through anthropometry.48 Hyperglycaemia will be measured using venous plasma glucose levels with a biochemical autoanalyser.49 Cognitive development will be assessed using a modified Kaufman Assessment Battery, second edition.50 At the 24th month, nutritional status will be assessed by stunting (height-for-age below −2 SD of the WHO Child Growth Standards median) and wasting (weight-for-age below −2 SD of the WHO Child Growth Standards median).51 At birth, weight will be measured in kilograms.

In women: at the third month after delivery, mental health status will be assessed using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale.52 At the seventh month of pregnancy and at the 24th, 36th and 60th months after delivery, HRQoL will be estimated from QALYs derived using the EQ-5D-5L instrument with India-specific weights.53 54

Costing interventions: identification, measurement and collection of cost data

The within-trial economic evaluation will be performed from a provider, health system and limited societal perspective.36,38 The resources required to produce and deliver the interventions will be identified using the Theory of Change (ToC) framework.55 The ToC is a causal method that allows explicit mapping of the throughput relationship between inputs, activities and outcomes using backward induction.56 It provides a structured, actionable, logical framework (logframe) to trace the outcomes to underlying interventions and then to the resources required to produce and deliver these interventions, and develop a detailed implementation strategy based on that.

Cost categories

The evaluation will consider three categories of costs: provider (programmatic) costs, health system costs and household (limited societal) costs.

Provider costs

The evaluation will assume a steady-state approach to identify the resources required to produce and deliver the interventions and estimate their costs. The costs of resources related to the design and development of interventions will be estimated and reported separately, as this might be useful for translational purposes. The unit cost refers to the cost of delivering one single unit of an intervention, for instance, one session of lifestyle behaviour change support. The Activity-Based-Costing (ABC) technique can be applied to estimate the unit cost of an intervention in a bottom-up manner by systematically measuring and costing the resources specified within the ToC-derived framework for that intervention.57 Such ABC-derived costing can help in estimating costs to different kinds of provider-stakeholders (Government, for-profit and not-for-profit) who might be interested in translating trial interventions through policy and practice.58

Health system costs

The interventions are expected to induce changes in the health-seeking behaviour of the trial participants and their household members.59 Increased awareness about health may motivate them to access healthcare services more appropriately and frequently, thereby increasing the demand. At the same time, adoption of a healthy lifestyle and reduced parental stress may lessen the probability of developing adverse health events, thereby decreasing the demand for healthcare services.

Household costs

These are costs to the trial participants and their households because of their participation in the study. These costs can include the opportunity cost of participation in the trial, and costs related to outcomes of the interventions, such as changes in health-seeking behaviour and improved dietary diversity.36

Types of costs

In all three cost categories, the costs will include direct and indirect costs as well as capital and recurring costs. The capital costs will be annualised over the useful period of the asset.60 An appropriate cost driver will be used to allocate the costs of the joint (shared) resources to individual interventions. Costs associated with the research and grant-related activities will be excluded from the analysis.

Costs will be presented in Indian Rupees (INR) with 2025 serving as the base cost year. Costs will be presented in international dollars as well, using the purchasing power parity conversion factor.61 The costs will be adjusted for inflation using the Indian consumer price index.62 Where required, the costs and benefits will be discounted at the recommended 3% discount rate.63 The scenarios of costs and benefits discounted at different rates will be presented.

Collection of cost data

Resource use and associated costs will be tracked and extracted using Microsoft Excel from multiple data sources, including the accounting and administrative records maintained by the provider organisation, time sheets of key personnel and periodic participant surveys conducted across trial phases.

The opportunity costs of donated resources, volunteer time and usage of public facilities will be obtained from project administrative records and project accounts. Within-trial economic evaluation

The within-trial economic evaluation will conduct CEA, CUA and CCA with an intention-to-treat approach, and results will be presented individually for both the intervention arms. For each trial participant, the costs and outcomes accumulate up to the 60th month of the offspring, beginning with the start of the interventions.

The ICER will be presented for the FMI. The ICER will be calculated as the difference in the arithmetic mean of the costs between an intervention arm and the control arm, divided by the difference in the arithmetic mean of FMI between these arms.

CUA results for the offspring will be presented as costs per unit change in the HRQoL in the intervention arms alongside those for the control arm, measured at the 60th month of the offspring. CUA results for mothers will be presented as costs per unit change in HRQoL in the intervention arms alongside those for the control arm, measured at the seventh month of pregnancy, and at 24th, 36th and 60th months after delivery.

CCA results will be presented as costs per unit change for each of the secondary health outcomes in mothers as well as their offspring in the intervention arms, alongside those for the control arm. A tabular format will be used to present the results.

Results of disaggregated analysis, across cost categories and four phases, will be presented.

This protocol aligns with the established benchmarks for performing economic evaluation. Specifically, the WHO Choosing Interventions that are Cost Effective (WHO-CHOICE) guidelines will inform the measurement of intervention effectiveness, the specification of comparators, time horizon, costing approaches, discounting and cost-effectiveness thresholds.63 The 19 criteria listed by the Consensus Health Economic Criteria (CHEC) checklist will guide the methodological quality.64 The 24 criteria listed by the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) statement will guide the transparent and comprehensive reporting of the study design, methods and results.65 If feasible, an India-specific methodological reference case will be presented.66

Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

Considerable uncertainty can exist in economic evaluation results due to randomness in parameter estimates, methodological assumptions and model (structural) misspecifications.67 68 Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to systematically examine the influence of these uncertainties on estimates of cost and effectiveness.67 68

Missing data will be handled using an appropriate imputation method, selected based on an assessment of the patterns and assumptions on underlying mechanisms of missingness in the data.69 The imputation approach and assumptions will be explicitly reported. SEs will be adjusted for clustering effects with survey design features (weights and stratification) incorporated where applicable.70 Interval estimates will be provided for each point estimate presented.

The uncertainty in parameter estimates due to randomness arising from sampling variability will be assessed by non-parametric bootstrapping technique (1000 iterations) with resampling conducted at the cluster level where applicable, and 95% uncertainty intervals will be presented.67 Joint distribution of incremental cost and effectiveness will be presented on the cost-effectiveness plane, and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves will be generated for a range of willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds. The median ICER, along with the 2.5th and 95th percentiles, will also be presented.

The uncertainty arising from methodological assumptions will be assessed using deterministic sensitivity analyses.67 68 Scenario analyses will be conducted to assess the impact of alternative analytical choices, including study perspectives, imputation techniques, inclusion or exclusion of certain cost categories, costing approaches and cost drivers. One-way sensitivity analyses will be conducted by varying key parameters across plausible ranges, for instance, varying discount rates from 0% to 5%. Extreme case analysis will be conducted to explore the best (worst) case scenarios.

Model misspecification will be identified using appropriate diagnostic tests, including the Ramsey regression specification error test, and tests for heteroskedasticity and multicollinearity.71 If misspecified, appropriate corrective measures such as transformation of variables, using robust SEs, inclusion of non-linear terms or interaction effects and use of alternative model specifications will be undertaken.72 Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of results to alternative model specifications, including different functional forms and distributional assumptions.

All statistical analyses will be conducted using Stata V.19

Equity impact

The potential benefits to the participants of integrated longitudinal public health interventions in a trial, the costs to the household of the trial participants and hence the cost-effectiveness of the interventions, may not be equitably distributed among subgroups of trial participants.73 Inequitable distribution is more acute if the trial participants are located differentially on the socio-economic gradient and if the interventions are aimed at lifestyle changes.74 Informed by the social determinants of health framework, the participants will be grouped based on heterogeneous characteristics such as ethnicity, education, household income and remoteness.75 76 These characteristics are already incorporated in the design of the scheduled data collection time points to measure the health outcomes across different phases of the trial. Generalised linear models incorporating these subgroup characteristics, intervention status, relevant covariates and interaction terms between them will be used to estimate equity-informed cost-effectiveness across the subgroups.77 In the presence of substantial non-compliance, instrumental variable methods using randomised assignment as the instrument will be employed.78 This analysis can inform the estimation of allocation weights required by policymakers and practitioners while designing and implementing such interventions.

The trial will use repeat cross-sectional surveys administered by the trained CHWs using REDCap to measure and estimate the primary and secondary health outcomes.

Discussion

Healthcare financing in India is characterised by low government spending (48.00% of total health expenditure, 1.84% of gross domestic product (GDP) and INR 3169 per capita) and high out-of-pocket household expenditure (39.4% of total health expenditure, 1.51% of GDP and INR 2600 per capita).79 CMDs contribute significantly to the health, economic and social burden in India.6

Preventive strategies are a cost-effective way to avert the disease burden.25 Evidence on preventive interventions, which are effective, affordable and scalable, to lessen the burden of CMDs is sparse in the Indian context. Economic evaluation, by objectively identifying interventions that provide value for money, can shape the evidence-based health policies towards the optimal allocation of scarce public health resources.

The protocol for the economic evaluation of the EINSTEIN trial interventions is the first in the Indian context for integrated preventive longitudinal interventions introduced at the prepregnancy phase to reduce the risk of adiposity in the offspring. This protocol will contribute to the limited body of evidence on the cost-effectiveness of preventive interventions in reducing the burden of CMDs in LMICs, including India. In addition, it can also inform the design of the economic evaluation of complex public health interventions. The modular design of the protocol segregates cost categories and effectiveness measures across the four phases of the trial. This modularity allows disaggregation of results across these categories and phases, which may facilitate selective translation to improve resource allocation and to overcome feasibility constraints.

Estimating cost-effectiveness for each of the trial interventions may be challenging due to their integrated design and the synergistic way they contribute to the outcomes. The health set score of offspring, measured using the EQ-5D-Y-5L Proxy instrument, must be converted into QALYs for use in CUA. This requires the application of context-specific statistical weights to the health set score. In the absence of India-specific weights, it will be necessary to rely on weights drawn from comparable settings, which may affect the validity of the estimates. At present, India does not have an explicit WTP threshold for a unit of health gain. The absence of this benchmark may pose challenges to the use of cost-effectiveness evidence in informing policy decisions on the adoption of trial interventions.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has been approved by the ethics committees of the lead local institutes, CSI Holdsworth Memorial Hospital (Ref CSIHMH/ERU2019/1) and Swami Vivekananda Youth Movement (Ref IRB 004/2020-21).

Written informed consent is obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. Participants and their families are provided with a participant information sheet in Kannada, the local language and its contents are explained verbally in an accessible manner. They are given time to consider the information and adequate opportunity to ask questions. Participants are informed that their participation is voluntary and that they may withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences.

Data related to the economic evaluation will be appropriately encrypted and password-protected. The results will be disseminated to relevant stakeholder groups, including academics, policymakers, practitioners and the wider public, through scholarly articles, conference presentations, interactive sessions, policy briefs, editorials in popular print and electronic media and HeLTI website.23 The results will be shared with the trial participants and local community in an accessible manner through community meetings, newsletters and local radio.

The HeLTI-EINSTEIN trial has been registered with ISRCTN primary clinical trial registry, recognised by WHO and ICMJE, under registration number ISRCTN20161479, and with the Clinical Trials Registry of India under registration number CTRI/2020/12/030134.
