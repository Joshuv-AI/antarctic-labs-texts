The impact of FinTech technology on financial stability of the UAE
Authors: Ahmed H. Elsayed, Ibtissam Guedira, Tasnim Alghussein, Hind Almheiri, Malak Alomari, Moataz Elmassri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Financial markets have witnessed significant disruptions driven by ongoing waves of financial innovation, and digitalization brought about by technological advances which are referred to as “FinTech”. Consequently, this paper examines connectedness and risk transmission between FinTech industry, Blockchain, and stability of the financial sector in the UAE. Furthermore, it explores the impact of economic and financial turbulences such as COVID-19 pandemic on the transmission of shocks across FinTech and financial instability using a Time-varying Parameter Vector Autoregressive model. Empirical results indicate that VIX and Global Fintech are the dominant player of the system, and significantly impacting vulnerability of the UAE financial system. However, the post-COVID analysis display shifts in the roles of indices within the connectedness network, such as the OVX transitioning from being a volatility receiver to a transmitter, emphasizing evolving market sentiments in the wake of major crises. Finally, volatility spillovers increased across markets under extreme market conditions indicating that UAE is fully integrated within the global financial markets.

Introduction

The global financial system has been greatly impacted in recent years by the remarkable progress in financial innovation and the integration of new Information and Communications Technologies (ICTs). ICT has significantly transformed the world, bringing about profound changes in global environments, restructuring economic systems, and establishing new social and organizational networks [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. ICTs have had a significant impact on various aspects of economies worldwide. They have facilitated the movement of capital across borders, reduced the costs associated with transactions, stimulated productivity growth, addressed information imbalances, and uncovered new investment prospects for economies at different stages of development [[5], [6], [7]]. Simultaneously, the financial industry has experienced substantial disruption due to the continuous surge of innovation, digitization, and digitalization1 resulting from liberalization and technical advancements that began in the 1970s [9,10]. The significant progress in technology and communication, along with the widespread adoption of technology in finance, has given rise to a new sector within the financial industry known as FinTech. This sector has the potential to profoundly influence the future of the financial market [[11], [12], [13], [14]].

Fintech, the blend of finance and technology, has remarkably influenced financial stability through markets and reshaped business models, perpetuating an innovative evolution in the global financial landscape [15]. In developed economies such as the UK and Singapore and USA, this confluence of technology and finance has not only fostered advanced financial solutions but also spawned regulatory frameworks that adapt to and encourage the proliferation of innovative financial technologies [[16], [17], [18]]. Conversely, in emerging and developing economies, FinTech has been a pivotal force in driving financial development by enabling access to critical financial services such as credit, investment, and insurance, especially for consumers and micro-enterprises, thus addressing pervasive financial inclusion issues [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. Furthermore, it significantly impacts customer and producer decisions by enhancing accessibility and providing novel, customer-centric financial products and services [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Additionally, FinTech promotes the transformative and sustainable development of enterprises by leveraging technological advances to create scalable and efficient financial solutions and enables financial institutions to augment their service offerings, cater to a wider clientele, and navigate through the complex, dynamic global economic environment [21,22,28,29].

On the other side, MENA countries have pioneered regional efforts to allow a greater adoption of technology in the financial industry by supporting and encouraging the development and the expansion of FinTech industry in the region. In particular, the United Arab Emirates (UAE) has emerged as a regional hub serving as an incubator of the FinTech ecosystem, where regulators implemented several policies and initiatives aiming at encouraging the growth of Fintech ventures since 2017. In addition, the UAE government launched the Emirates Blockchain Strategy in April 2018 aiming at improving citizen and resident happiness, enhancing the efficiency of government, advancing legislation, and leading internationally. Consequently, the Dubai International Financial Centre (DIFC), Abu Dhabi Global Market (ADGM), and venture capital corporations are looking to tap into the prospering FinTech industry. Currently, the UAE economy is home to the highest number of FinTech startups in the region (around 67 startups), followed by 44 in Turkey and 30 in Jordan and Lebanon.

With a foundation cemented by robust policies and a well-structured financial environment, the UAE has rapidly developed its financial institutions, becoming a significant player in the international market [30]. This development not only substantiates its competitive position but also enables it to demonstrate resilience in the intricate milieu of the international market [31]. In this context, studying the impact of Fintech—financial technology—on the stability of UAE's financial sector becomes pivotal. The insinuation of technological advancements in the financial domain is vast, ranging from automated trading systems and blockchain to digital wallets and robot-advisors. Therefore, understanding how these technological advancements affect financial stability is paramount, as it has the potential to reshape traditional banking systems, influence consumer behaviors, and modify regulatory frameworks, ensuring that the UAE's financial sector not only sustains its stability but also thrives in a perpetually evolving digital world.

On the dark side, Kirilenko and Lo [32] emphasized the potential for FinTech to amplify financial contagion, procyclicality, and market volatility, thereby undermining the stability of the financial system. Algorithmic trading, a prominent aspect of FinTech, was identified as a source of contagion and vulnerability in financial markets. Similarly, Mild et al. [33] argued that lenders in peer-to-peer markets failed to effectively assess the creditworthiness of borrowers. Furthermore, unlike banks, they are unable to accurately price default risk. Furthermore, Pantielieieva et al. [34] highlighted the transformation of traditional financial intermediation principles and models due to FinTech's global trends. They discussed the threats posed by the increasing compliance costs for traditional financial institutions, leading to collaborations with FinTech firms that introduced regulatory complexities and reduced accountability. FinTech's lower regulatory standards, interconnectedness, susceptibility to cyber-attacks, operational failures, and the potential for AI-driven disruptions further compound the risks to financial stability.

The literature also reveals the disruptive impact of FinTech on the financial sector, as noted by He et al. [35]. While FinTech innovations offer improvements, they also blur established boundaries among service providers and challenge entry barriers, raising regulatory challenges that can disrupt stability and trust within the financial system. Li et al. [36] conducted a study focusing on risk spillovers between FinTech firms and traditional financial institutions, especially in bearish market conditions. Their findings indicated that the risk spillover was notably stronger during times of rapid technological advances, suggesting a higher risk of negative impacts on the traditional financial sector. Moreover, a positive correlation between risk spillover from FinTech institutions and increased systemic risk for traditional financial institutions underscores the potential contribution of FinTech to heightened financial instability, emphasizing the need for careful monitoring and regulation in this evolving landscape. Nguyen and Dang [37] in a recent study extended this discussion to emerging markets, demonstrating that FinTech development can have adverse effects on financial stability, particularly in regions like Vietnam, where lower stability in banks and increased state ownership can exacerbate these negative consequences.

On the bright side, the comprehensive body of research consistently demonstrates that FinTech has a significant impact on several aspects of financial stability. The various means by which financial inclusion is facilitated, traditional market structures are altered, transparent financial reporting is enabled, decentralization is achieved, and institutional vulnerability is effectively addressed. Financial technology (FinTech) plays a pivotal and indispensable role in the modern financial landscape. Daud et al. [38] presents an exploration of the correlation between financial stability and Fintech adoption across a diverse panel of countries, discovering that Fintech implementation, through artificial intelligence, cloud, and data technology, propels enhancements in financial stability, a dynamic complemented by a concurrent concentration of banks. Aduba et al. [39] delve into the effects of Fintech on financial development, elucidating how its permeation is not merely contributory to financial evolution but also notably influences nations burdened by underwhelming financial sectors and restrictive financial inclusion. Investigating the vulnerability of financial institutions in the wake of Fintech and its regulatory sandboxes, Fung et al. [40] identify a dual-faceted impact: a mitigating effect on financial institutions' vulnerability in emerging markets and a potential escalation of the same in developed financial ecosystems. Wen et al. [41] focus on Fintech's influence on the quality and reliability of company financial reporting, noting a critical role in dampening the actual earnings management practices of companies, thereby enhancing the credibility of financial disclosures. Highlighting the potentialities embedded within blockchain technology, Zamani et al. [42] unveil its capacity to spawn innovative business models and decentralized organizations while acknowledging barriers to its widespread adoption and exploration. Bian et al. [43] traverses the impact of Fintech, especially the innovation and technology applied in the insurance sector (InsurTech),2 on conventional market structures, unearthing a significant impact on market concentration within the non-life insurance sector, though limited effect on life insurance. Yeyouomo et al. [44] underscore the tangible impact of fintech companies in attenuating gender disparity within financial inclusion in Sub-Saharan Africa, albeit indicating the insufficiency of Fintech development alone in bridging this divide completely and emphasizing the necessity of targeted policy interventions. Recently, Koranteng and You [45] assessed the impact of Fintech and its various lending sub-components on financial stability at a macro level across 25 countries from 2013 to 2020, concluding that fintech bolsters both local and cross-border financial stability, with a more pronounced positive effect in regions with smaller fintech sectors and among neighboring countries. However, recent literature highlights the negative side of Fintech's impact on financial stability. For example, Cevik [46] investigates the impact of fintech, excluding cryptocurrencies, on financial stability across 198 countries from 2012 to 2020, finding that while fintech has the potential to enhance efficiency and inclusion, it also introduces systemic risks. The study reveals that the impact of fintech on stability varies by instrument type, with digital lending having a negative overall effect due to its large share, and digital capital raising showing mixed results between advanced and developing economies. Despite its current small size relative to traditional institutions, fintech's rapid growth in riskier areas poses new challenges for policymakers. In this regard, De Sant'Anna and Figueiredo [47] highlight the dual impact of fintech on financial stability and inclusion, noting that while fintech innovations can integrate unbanked populations into the financial system, promoting health and development, they also pose risks to the financial system and vulnerable groups. Their study, synthesizing findings from a review of 163 academic articles, aims to understand how fintech drives inclusion and stability, especially in emerging economies, and its role in mitigating the pandemic's effects. However, it also warns of digital exclusion for women, the low-income, rural residents, and the elderly if infrastructure and capabilities are not improved.

Despite the importance of this topic, studies on the impact of FinTech on financial stability are relatively scarce. Besides, previous studies examined the impact of FinTech on financial contagion and financial stability in the context of UAE focused solely on information transmitted across one segment of the financial system such as the effects of FinTech on UAE stock markets [48,49], bond markets [50], and the banking sector [[51], [52], [53]]. Contrary to previous literature that used a single proxy to capture the impact of FinTech on financial stability see for example, Alves [54] and Elsayed et al. [55], among others3, our Composite Financial Stress Indices are far more accurate and provide informative measures of financial conditions and the soundness of the UAE's financial system. To this end, Composite Financial Stress Index comprises six standardized variables that are grouped together using the variance-equal weighted approach and capture the financial conditions of the banking sector, the stock market, and the foreign exchange market is calculated. In doing so, this study tries to answer the following research questions.i.What is the relationship between FinTech and financial instability in the UAE?ii.What is the pattern of risk transmission and spillovers across the FinTech industry and the financial instability of the UAE's financial system?iii.What is the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on financial connectedness and risk transmission across the underlying markets?

The objectives of this study are threefold: (i) to thoroughly analyze the dynamic relationship between the FinTech industry and the financial instability of the UAE's financial system, considering historical context and current trends. (ii) to investigate the patterns of connectedness and risk transmission between FinTech and financial instability, including an in-depth assessment of the impact on financial markets and institutions. (iii) to extensively explore the impact of economic and financial turbulences, such as COVID-19, on the connectedness and transmission patterns between the underlying markets under extreme conditions, considering various stress periods and their implications.

The study's primary results indicate that the FinTech industry in the UAE demonstrates a strong connection with measures of financial stability, displaying increased sensitivity to fluctuations in the oil market and global financial circumstances, especially in times of economic uncertainty like the COVID-19 pandemic. These findings emphasize the increasing incorporation and impact of the sector into the wider financial environment. The research highlights the significance of implementing effective economic policies and developing resilient business models inside FinTech companies to protect financial markets from potential instability and promote long-term growth. The findings are essential for strategic planning and risk management, emphasizing the necessity for the UAE's financial system to maintain flexibility in order to react to various market situations for continuous economic growth and stability.

Econometric methodology and data sources

Following the literature, we have constructed a Composite Financial Stress Index for the UAE economy [56,57]. This index captures different types of risk and sources of financial instability across different dimensions of the country's financial sector, namely the banking sector, equity market, and foreign exchange market as follows.i.The banking sector is comprised of the beta banking sector, the negative return of the banking index, and the volatility of the banking sector;ii.Similarly, the stock market includes two proxies: the negative stock returns, and the stock market volatility; andiii.Finally, the exchange rate volatility captures the foreign exchange market's condition.

Econometric methods

TVP-VAR dynamic connectedness

In this research, we employ the Time-Varying Parameter Vector Autoregressive (TVP-VAR) model, combined with the dynamic connectedness approach. It offers a robust framework for understanding interdependencies in economic variables. Moreover, this strategy addresses a number of shortcomings of earlier models and integrates the dynamic connectedness concept by Koop and Korobilis [58] and Diebold and Yilmaz [59]. Key advantages include its ability to provide a more accurate measure of connectedness, avoiding the overestimation common in rolling window approaches. Unlike traditional methods, the TVP-VAR model does not require a fixed rolling window, allowing VAR parameters to adjust over time, thus capturing the dynamic nature of connectedness more effectively. This flexibility also prevents the loss of observations, a common issue with fixed rolling windows. The TVP-VAR-based connectedness technique is also strong against outliers, which means that economic analyses will get more accurate and stable results [58,[60], [61], [62]]. This makes it a valuable tool for accurately assessing the evolving relationships between different economic indicators and variables. The TVP-VAR model is defined primary by equations (1), (2)):(1)Yt=βtYt−1+ϵtϵt|Ωt∼N(0,St)(2)vec(βt)=vec(βt−1)+vtvt|Ωt∼N(0,Rt)

Here Yt represents a N×1 vector of the variables at time t. The model hinges on the time-varying coefficient matrix βt , an N×Np dimensional matrix, which encapsulates the dynamic interactions between the variables. The term ϵt is an N×1 vector of disturbance errors, following a normal distribution with a mean of zero and a time-varying N×N variance-covariance matrix St.

In equation (2), (βt) , represents the vectorized form of (βt) , transformed into an N2p×1 dimensional vector. The vector vt is an N2p×1 vector of error terms associated with the time-varying coefficients, adhering to a normal distribution with mean zero and an N2p×N2p covariance matrix Rt.

Utilizing the Wold representation theorem, the TVP-VAR model can be transferred into its Moving Average (VMA) representation. This transformation is essential as it enables the model to be expressed as an infinite sum of past errors influencing the current value of Yt , as denoted in equation (3):(3)Yt=∑j=0∞Aitεt−jWhere Ait are coefficients that link past errors εt−j to the current value of Yt. .

In the realm of the TVP-VMA framework, we extend our analysis by incorporating the calculation of Generalized Impulse Response Functions (GIRF) and Generalized Forecast Error Variance Decomposition (GFEVD). These advanced econometric techniques, grounded in the seminal work of Koop, Pesaran, and Potter [63] and Pesaran and Shin [64], are fundamental to the dynamic connectedness approach. These functions are essential in analyzing the response of the system to shocks and in decomposing the forecast error variances, respectively. The H-step-ahead GFEVD function is expressed as in equation (4):(4)θij(H)=∑t=1H−1ξij2∑j=1N∑t=1H−1ξij2

Here, ξij2 denotes the response of all variables j to a shock in variable i. By construction, the sum of θij(H) over j equals 1, and the sum of θij(H) over both i and j equals N. This normalization ensures that the total variance is partitioned across the different variables.

The Total Connectedness Index (TCI) is then computed as the ratio of the sum of all off-diagonal elements to the total forecast error variance. It quantifies the average contribution of volatility spillovers from all variables to the total forecast error variance. The TCI is a crucial measure, capturing the essence of interconnectedness across the entire variable system.

Additionally, the framework allows for the estimation of directional spillover indices among variables. Specifically, the total directional connectedness to a given variable i from all other variables j is given by equation (5):(5)CTCI=∑i,j=1,i≠jNθij(H)∑i,j=1Nθij(H)×100=∑i,j=1,i≠jNθij(H)N×100

The CTCI measures the percentage of forecast error variance in variable i that can be attributed to shocks in all other variables j, providing insight into the magnitude and direction of spillovers across the network of variables under study.

The spillovers are mathematically captured through the H-step-ahead GFEVD. For a given variable i, the total directional connectedness received from all other variables, denoted as Ci←∙ , is calculated as the ratio of the sum of off-diagonal forecast error variances attributed to variable i, to the total forecast error variances, scaled by 100. Mathematically, this is represented by equation (6):(6)Ci←∙=∑j=1,j≠iNθij(H)∑i,j=1Nθij(H)×100=∑j=1,j≠iNθij(H)N×100

Conversely, the total directional connectedness to others, Ci→∙ , which quantifies the outflow of shocks from variable i to all other variables, is given by a similar ratio, reflecting the proportion of the transmitted variances. The mathematical representation of Ci→∙ is presented in equation (7):(7)Ci→∙=∑j=1,j≠iNθji(H)∑i,j=1Nθji(H)×100=∑j=1,j≠iNθji(H)N×100

The net directional connectedness for each variable is given by equation (8) where Ci is subsequently derived as the difference between the total directional connectedness to and from the variable:(8)Ci=Ci→∙−Ci←∙

Positive values indicate that a variable is a net transmitter of shocks, while negative values signify it as a net receiver.

We further construct a directional connectedness network, equation (9), mapping the Net Pairwise Spillover (NPS) connectedness between each pair of variables, i and j, where:(9)NPS=Ci→j−Ci←j

This metric serves as an indicator of the net effect of volatility spillovers transmitted from variable i to variable j, taking into account the reciprocity of their interaction. The total connectedness within the network is then the sum of these NPS values across all variable pairs, providing a scalar measure of the overall interconnectedness in the system. Notably, since θij≠θji , the NPS matrix is asymmetric, reflecting the non-equivalence of volatility spillovers transmitted and received between each pair of variables. This asymmetry is pivotal as it allows us to discern the predominant directionalities of shock transmission across the network, identifying which variables serve as key transmitters or receivers of volatility within the interconnected system.

Quantile vector autoregression

To analyze the relationship between financial technology and financial stability in the UAE at different levels, we employ the connectivity technique developed by Diebold and Yilmaz [59,65] using Quantile Vector Autoregression (QVAR) introduced by Ando et al. [66]. This approach is particularly useful for capturing the behavior of variables in extreme situations, such as financial crises or extreme market movements, which are often not well-described by average relationships. For our analysis, we choose the lag order of the VAR based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and a 10-step forecast error variance decomposition to estimate the spillovers. As for the extreme market analysis, we focus on connectedness across quantiles at τ = 0.05, τ = 0.50, and τ = 0.95 levels to capture the interactions among assets during extremely bearish conditions, normal market states, and excessively bullish conditions, respectively. In addition, following the literature, a rolling window of 250 days is used to assess the temporal dynamics of connectedness.

Connectedness based on TVP-VAR model

Table 2 represents the static interconnectedness between the UAE's financial sector and financial technology derived from a TVP-VAR model. The results indicate that the Financial Stress Index (FSI) primarily functions as a self-contained variable with 85.88 % of the variance in FSI explained by its own shocks, reflecting a degree of insularity from other indices' influences. However, there is still a 14.12 % connectedness to other variables, indicating potential channels through which external economic factors can impact financial stability. The STOXX Global Fintech Index (RSTOXX) shows a substantial level of external influence, with 36.85 % of the forecast error variance coming from other indices. This underscores the sector's sensitivity to the broader market and its role as an integrator of various risk factors. Conclusion

This research paper explores the relationship between financial technology (FinTech) and the stability of the financial system in the UAE. It specifically examines the impact of blockchain technology and other financial innovations on market dynamics. The paper used a Quantile Vector Autoregression (QVAR) model to analyze the interconnections and risk transmission patterns between the FinTech industry and financial instability. It also considers the impacts of economic and political disruptions, such as the COVID-19 pandemic. The motivation for this inquiry stems from the significant impact FinTech has on modern financial markets. Its incorporation with blockchain technology has the potential to redefine stability frameworks within the fast-changing financial environment of the UAE.

The empirical findings offer a detailed comprehension of the intricate interconnections within the financial ecosystem of the UAE. The analysis demonstrates notable disparities in connectivity under diverse market circumstances, suggesting that the FinTech industry, although progressively essential to the financial system, remains vulnerable to external disturbances, such as those arising from oil market volatility and global market sentiments. During times of economic recovery and stability, financial markets demonstrate resilience, as indicated by the stabilization of financial stress levels. This is a positive sign for the sustainable growth of FinTech. In contrast, in times of crisis, the greater interconnection across financial indices indicates a higher level of systemic risk, highlighting the intricate and fragile nature of markets when faced with global shocks.

The findings have substantial consequences for policymakers, investors, and market players. The research highlights the significance of strong economic policies and the creation of safeguards to shield financial markets from instability, especially in relation to oil prices and geopolitical events. FinTech companies are advised to create robust business models that can withstand both external disruptions and internal legislative changes. Furthermore, as the UAE further assimilates into the global financial system, the capacity to adjust to diverse market conditions becomes crucial. The ability to adapt is crucial for ensuring financial stability and promoting ongoing economic expansion, therefore making the findings from this study essential for strategic planning and risk management in the FinTech industry. However, this study is not without limitations. The findings of the study may be specific to the context of the UAE and may not be generalizable to other markets or economies, especially those with different levels of fintech development, regulations, and economic structures. In addition, while the study considers the impact of oil market volatility and geopolitical events, there could be other external factors that should be considered, such as technological changes, international competition, or shifts in consumer behavior.
