Stroke: Another Delayed Hazard of Traumatic Brain Injury, Even in Young Adults
Authors: Douglas I Katz, Brigid Dwyer
Published: 2024

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of injury‐related death and disability, and for a substantial number of patients after TBI, problems may persist, worsen, or develop later in life. TBI has been recognized as a chronic condition that may have multiple, lasting effects on health and functioning, especially at moderate‐to‐severe levels of injury. 1 Over the long term, TBI can have important and devastating consequences for patients and their families and present a substantial economic burden to society. 2 The chronic and later‐life consequences of TBI may be caused by persisting pathophysiologic brain dysfunction, related comorbidities, or a variety of secondary disorders that can develop after injury. Secondary psychiatric and neurological disorders, including stroke, are among the long‐term consequences of TBI. 3 Post‐traumatic epilepsy is one well‐recognized neurological complication of TBI. TBI has also been associated with neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer disease and others, with the strongest evidence to date linking TBI to Parkinson disease. 3 Chronic traumatic encephalopathy is a neurodegenerative disorder associated with substantial exposure to repetitive head impacts, even at a subconcussive level, most commonly occurring in contact sports; however, there has not been a clearly established relationship of chronic traumatic encephalopathy with TBI, in the absence of significant exposure to repetitive head impacts. 4 , 5

The article by Choi et al in this issue of Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA) 6 adds to the growing evidence associating TBI with a risk of stroke. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Previous reports from retrospective cohort studies in the United States and Taiwan support an increased risk of stroke in patients with a history of TBI, using large databases comparing those with TBI to a matched population control group or non‐TBI injury controls. Two meta‐analyses demonstrated increased risk of stroke after TBI among multiple studies with comparison groups, reporting hazard ratios of 1.86 (95% CI, 1.46–2.37) and 2.06 (95% CI, 1.28–3.32). 14 , 15 These earlier studies demonstrated increased risk among the full range of injury severities but highest in moderate‐to‐severe TBI. In a prospective longitudinal cohort study, Izzy et al analyzed large groups with mild TBI, moderate‐to‐severe TBI, and non‐TBI controls (n=4351 in each group) who had re‐presented for care at least once between 6 months and 10 years post‐TBI. 16 There was a significantly higher risk of hypertension, diabetes, and ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack in both the mild TBI and moderate–severe TBI groups (mild TBI: hazard ratio, 2.2 [95% CI, 1.4–3.3] and moderate–severe TBI: hazard ratio, 3.6 [95% CI, 2.4–5.3] for stroke/ transient ischemic attack). The emergence of these comorbidities was detectable within a median of 3.49 (1.76–5.95) years postinjury and occurred with less latency than controls. Another prospective cohort of patients with moderate‐to‐severe TBI demonstrated that 2.5% experienced acute ischemic stroke. 10 The increased risk of stroke after TBI involves all stroke types, but several studies have shown a greater risk for hemorrhagic stroke than ischemic stroke. 15 Although risk is highest in the early months after injury, the risk can extend for years after injury. 15

This study by Choi and colleagues further supports an increased risk of stroke after TBI with a retrospective population‐based, cohort study in Korea. 6 A unique feature of this study is that it focuses on risk in a population of young adults, an age group that has fewer other age‐ and health‐related risks for stroke. Of note, young adults are a segment of the population that has recently been demonstrating an overall increasing incidence of stroke. 17 The study finds that the hazard ratio is similar to other reports that included older age groups (hazard ratio 1.89 [95% CI, 1.84–1.95]). 6 There is some evidence from previous studies that TBI‐related risk of stroke was even higher in those under age 50 years. 8 As in other retrospective population‐based studies in Taiwan and the United States, the risk of stroke in this investigation in South Korea involved all stroke subtypes, extended beyond 1‐year postinjury, and is substantially greater in those with moderate‐to‐severe TBI than those with concussion (mild TBI). The risk of hemorrhagic stroke was higher than ischemic stroke, particularly at moderate‐to‐severe levels of injury. However, it is noteworthy that the risk of stroke was still increased in this younger population, even when concussion was analyzed separately. It is also noteworthy that the risk of stroke remained increased by 84% in this group of young adults even when adjusting for stroke risk factors such as obesity, high blood pressure, hyperlipidemia, and health‐related behaviors.

Stroke is a significant independent factor worsening mortality and level of disability in patients with a history of TBI. 10 In a Taiwanese population study, a history of TBI among patients with stroke increased the risk of mortality (Liao et al, 2014). 9 In a prospective database of patients with moderate‐to‐severe TBI, controlling for demographic and acute postinjury neuroanatomic characteristics, the occurrence of stroke after TBI was associated with higher disability and worse outcome. 10

A variety of mechanisms likely account for the increased risk of stroke after TBI but the relative contributions of these factors at varying times post‐TBI are yet to be elucidated. During the acute and subacute period after TBI, alterations in the coagulation cascade, vascular injury and dissections, vascular regulation abnormalities, microvascular damage, ischemia associated with increased intracranial pressure and herniation syndromes may account for the bulk of early stroke pathogenesis after TBI. 8 The major mechanisms that account for long‐term increased risk of post‐TBI stroke are yet to be clarified. As suggested by Choi et al in this issue, vascular wall damage or increased risk of amyloid angiopathy after TBI may increase vulnerability to later hemorrhagic or ischemic stroke. 6 Factors such as alcohol and substance misuse, and sedentary lifestyle, may contribute to increased risk. 8 Further studies are needed to explore the causes of prolonged risk of stroke after TBI.

Should this growing awareness of stroke risk after TBI affect clinical practice? Are there implications for changes in surveillance and prevention that should be implemented now? The findings in the study by Choi et al and previous research suggest that the index of suspicion for stroke should be raised for any changes in neurologic status in those with a history of TBI, even in younger patients, without typical risk factors for stroke. Nevertheless, the risk is still relatively very low for those with concussion (mild TBI) who make up the vast majority of patients with TBI. The risk is substantially greater in those with moderate to severe TBI, especially in the earlier months after injury, suggesting that there should be a greater concern for stroke when assessing new neurological problems in this population.

Medication choice may affect stroke risk and prevention after TBI. Some medications may increase risk of stroke after TBI. For instance, some evidence suggests that +antidepressants increase risk of stroke after TBI; selective noradrenergic reuptake inhibitor and phenylpiperazine antidepressants may increase risk of ischemic stroke, and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressants may increase risk of hemorrhagic stroke after TBI. 18 , 19 Atypical antipsychotic medications, commonly used after TBI, may also increase the risk of stroke. 20 More research is needed to confirm and clarify the level of risk in the use of these medications.

Is there a role for secondary stroke prevention after TBI? There is evidence that stroke prevention medications such as statins and vitamin K antagonists (eg, warfarin) reduce stroke risk after TBI. 15 Some indications for secondary prevention are better established, such as antiplatelet or anticoagulant treatment for vascular injuries causing arterial dissection. Ultimately, clinical practice guidelines for use of secondary stroke prevention after TBI will require more clarification of the elements of risk and benefit by way of large observational studies and clinical trials, such as were performed to guide use of antiseizure medications in the prevention of post‐traumatic epilepsy. At a minimum, this and other studies suggest a need for increased awareness of stroke risk after TBI and added impetus to recommend lifestyle changes that reduce risk of stroke. It is still too early to make definitive recommendations regarding contraindications for medications that increase stroke risk or medications for secondary prevention of stroke in patients with a history of TBI.
