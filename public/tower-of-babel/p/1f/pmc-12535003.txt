The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on all-cause mortality among oncological patients: a population-based study
Authors: Kai Liu, XiaoHui Wang, Yubo Wang, Zhuoran Wang, Yao Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Background

It is widely believed that cancer patients are more likely to experience severe clinical outcomes and have higher rates of mortality due to coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) than patients without cancer [1–4]. During the pandemic, COVID-19 was not the only threat to the health of patients with tumors. Rather, the response policy and collapse of the health system also reportedly posed serious threats to patients with tumors [5–7].

The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program released incidence and mortality data for the United States in 2020 and 2021, providing a comprehensive source for investigating the potential impacts of the pandemic. We aimed to elucidate the effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on oncological patients and quantify changes in all-cause mortality among this population in the United States, employing a population-based analysis of data from the SEER database.

Methods

This study was exempt from our institution’s requirement of research ethics board approval and informed consent because the study participants were identified from a deidentified and publicly accessible database. The SEER Research Limited-Field Data, 22 Registries (excl IL and MA) database was used. This database covers approximately 47.9% of the U.S. population based on the 2020 census data [8]. All the data were retrieved and computed using SEER*Stat software version 8.4.4.

Variables

We retrieved information about sex, race and origin, year of death, cause of death, age at death, survival months, urbanicity, median household income, tumor behavior, histology records, and primary site. The cause of death was classified according to the variable “COD to site recode ICD-O-3 2023 Revision Expanded (1999+)”. Age at death was calculated according to age at diagnosis, year of diagnosis and year of death. Urbanicity was categorized into metropolitan and nonmetropolitan counties, as determined by the “Rural‒Urban Continuum Code” variable. The median household income was inflation-adjusted and derived from the “median household income inflation adj. to 2021” variable. The tumor behavior code was confirmed according to the third edition of the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O-3) and obtained from the variable “Behavior code ICD-O-3”. Histology records were defined by the “Histology recode-broad groupings” variable. The tumor site was classified on the basis of the “Site rec ICD-O-3/WHO 2008 (individual sites only)” variable.

Distribution of deaths

Oncological patients who died between 2018 and 2021, who were identified according to the variable “year of death”, were included in this study. The exclusion criteria included patients with unknown ages, unknown races and origins, unknown median household income levels and unknown urbanicities. The demographic and clinical information was extracted from the case listing session of the Incidence-Based Mortality SEER Research Limited-Field Data, 22 Registries (excl IL and MA) database, Nov 2023 Submission [9]. Descriptive analysis was adopted to analyze the distribution of deaths attributed to COVID-19 among oncological patients in 2020 and 2021.

Age-adjusted all-cause mortality

We obtained the age-adjusted all-cause mortality rates of oncological patients from the rate session [10] of the Incidence-Based Mortality SEER Research Limited-Field Data, 22 Registries (excl IL and MA), Nov. 2022 Submission [11]. We excluded patients whose ages were unknown and selected only the first matching record for each person. The mortality rates are presented as the number of deaths per 100,000 people and were age adjusted to the 2000 US Standard Population (19 age groups, Census P25-1130). We obtained mortality rates for the entire cohort and subsequently stratified the data by sex, race, urbanicity, median household income level, tumor behavior, histology record and tumor site. We then compared the age-adjusted all-cause mortality rate during the pandemic (between 2020 and 2021) with that before the pandemic (between 2018 and 2019). The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the rates and rate ratios were calculated using the Tiwari modification method [12]. A p value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Distribution of deaths

A total of 1,917,070 patients who died between 2018 and 2021 were included. After the exclusion criteria were applied, 0.3% (5193/1917070) of the patients were excluded, and a total of 1,911,877 patients were included in this study. Between 2018 and 2019, 903,521 deaths occurred among oncological patients, and this number increased to 1,008,356 deaths between 2020 and 2021. In addition, COVID-19 accounted for 63,947 deaths among oncological patients in 2020 and 2021. Among the oncological patients who died from COVID-19 between 2020 and 2021, 58.3% were male. A total of 63.0% of the patients were non-Hispanic white, and 86.5% lived in counties within metropolitan areas. A total of 92.5% of the patients were older than 60 years. A total of 59.5% and 35.4% of the deaths occurred in patients with a time since primary tumor diagnosis exceeded five years and ten years, respectively (Table 1). Patients with prostate tumors and breast tumors were the most common in this group, accounting for 18.7% and 13.7% of the cohort, respectively (Table 2).

Age-adjusted all-cause mortality

Owing to the disproportionately high impact of COVID-19-related deaths on mortality rates in groups with fewer deaths, our analysis was restricted to groups with more than 5,000 deaths in both 2018–2019 and 2020–2021. However, we included all the results in the supplementary materials for reference.

The mortality rate among oncological patients increased by 7.1%, from 243.12 deaths per 100,000 people per year before the pandemic (2018–2019) to 260.33 deaths per 100,000 people per year during the pandemic (2020–2021). The mortality rate during the pandemic was significantly greater than that before the pandemic for all oncological patients, even when stratified by sex, race, urbanicity, median household income level and tumor behavior. When stratified by median household income level, the proportion of increase in mortality decreased as the income level increased. With respect to tumor behavior, the mortality rate among patients with benign and borderline malignant tumors increased by 29.0%, from 4.87 to 6.29 deaths per 100,000 people per year, and the mortality rate increased by 18.6% and 6.1% for patients with in situ and malignant tumors, respectively (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1).

Age-adjusted mortality rates among patients with tumors during the COVID-19 pandemic (between 2020 and 2021) compared with those before the pandemic (between 2018 and 2019). Patients were stratified on the basis of demographic characteristics and tumor behavior.

With respect to the various histological types, patients with meningiomas (9530–9539), lymphoid leukemias (9820–9839) and chronic myeloproliferative disorders (9950–9969) had the greatest increases in mortality rates (28.3%, 20.0% and 17.7%, respectively). Conversely, mortality significantly decreased only among patients with epithelial neoplasms (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 2).

Age-adjusted mortality rates among patients with tumors during the COVID-19 pandemic (between 2020 and 2021) compared with those before the pandemic (between 2018 and 2019). Patients were stratified on the basis of histology type.

In terms of tumor site, the greatest increases in mortality were observed in patients with tumors of the cranial nerves and other parts of the nervous system, thyroid and chronic lymphocytic leukemia, with increases of 28.9%, 24.1% and 19.3%, respectively. Moreover, mortality significantly decreased only among patients with lung and bronchial tumors (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 3).

Age-adjusted mortality rates of patients with tumors during the COVID-19 pandemic (between 2020 and 2021) compared with those before the pandemic (between 2018 and 2019). Patients were stratified on the basis of tumor site.

Discussion

Cancer is a leading cause of death and a substantial barrier to increasing life expectancy in every country worldwide [13]. According to reports from the WHO in 2022, cancer accounted for nearly 10 million deaths in 2020, or nearly one in six deaths [14]. However, after the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 at the end of 2019, the outbreak created an unprecedented global health crisis that led to significant changes in healthcare systems [15, 16]. We found that many long-term tumor survivors died during the COVID-19 pandemic, with 59.5% of deaths attributed to COVID-19 occurring in patients with a time since primary tumor diagnosis exceeded five years. Recent estimates from the WHO indicate that the number of excess deaths that were directly or indirectly associated with the COVID-19 pandemic from January 1 st, 2020, to December 31 st, 2021, was approximately 14.9 million, ranging from 13.3 million to 16.6 million [17]. Changes in cancer incidence and mortality during the pandemic have been a major focus of research in recent years [18–22]. Mani et al. [21] conducted a population-based analysis and concluded that people living with cancer are at a twofold greater risk of dying from COVID-19 than the general U.S. population is. However, COVID-19 was not the only threat to the health of patients with tumors during the pandemic. The isolation policy and the collapse of health systems also posed serious threats to patients with tumors. We conducted this population-based analysis on the basis of the SEER 22 registries database, which accounts for approximately 47.9% of the U.S. population (based on the 2020 census) [8]. As the largest retrospective cohort study to date, we believe that our study can provide a more comprehensive description of the challenges faced by oncological patients during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Most of our findings are consistent with those of previous research indicating that oncological patients had a greater mortality rate during the COVID-19 pandemic [22, 23]. Evidence suggests that cancer patients who are receiving treatment and who are hospitalized with SARS-CoV-2 infection face a greater risk of death than noncancer patients do, regardless of the presence of symptoms. Notably, the greatest relative risk of death was observed in younger patients undergoing cancer treatment [22]. Increased mortality rates among oncological patients are associated with delayed cancer screening, diagnosis, vaccination, and treatment [24, 25]. Furthermore, the pandemic has hindered progress in cancer research and advances in treatment [26]. The COVID-19 pandemic is estimated to have set back breakthroughs in cancer research by approximately 18 months [26]. In addition, increased mortality rates among cancer patients are reportedly due to delays in treatment, precautions taken following the outbreak and is also directly correlated with the COVID-19 pandemic [22].

However, we identified several novel and interesting findings. First, we found that the lower the median income level was inversely correlated with the mortality rate during the pandemic. Previous reports have also shown a progressively higher rate of loss of life among socioeconomically disadvantaged communities [27, 28]. The median income level has been reported to be associated with unequal access to quality healthcare [28] and disparities in the allocation of resources to control the spread of infection [27]. Second, we observed a 28.3% increase in the mortality rate among patients with meningioma (one of the most common primary central nervous system tumors [19]), which was the highest among all histology types. Rubens et al. examined the effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on intracranial meningioma resection using data from the California State Inpatient Database in 2022 and concluded that neurosurgery practices in the United States successfully adapted to the unforeseen challenges of the pandemic [29]. However, deferring medical care for semiurgent patients in neuro-oncology [30] may have resulted in significantly increased mortality rates among patients with meningiomas.

Despite a general increase in mortality rates among most oncological patients, our study revealed a notable anomaly, a significant decrease in mortality rates for patients with lung and bronchial tumors. Mortality rates following COVID-19 in patients with lung cancer have been reported to be exceptionally high in almost all studies [31], indicating a strong correlation between lung cancer and an increased probability of complications and death from COVID-19 [32]. According to Global Cancer Statistics, lung cancer was the leading cause of cancer-related death, accounting for 18.4% of all cancer deaths in 2018 [33], but this figure decreased to 18.0% of total cancer deaths in 2020 [13]. A report [34] from the National Cancer Institute (NCI) published in 2020 and a report [35] from the American Cancer Society (ACS) published in 2021 indicated that the number of lung cancer-related deaths in the United States has plummeted. The sharp decrease in the rate of mortality due to lung cancer is largely due to recent advances in treatment [34]. Even during the pandemic, the mortality rate among patients with lung and bronchial tumors was still significantly lower from 2020 to 2021 than from 2018 to 2019. We also observed a significant decrease in the mortality rate among patients with epithelial neoplasms (NOS) during the pandemic. Epithelial neoplasms refer to benign or malignant tumors originating from epithelial cells encompassing a range of lesions, such as adenomas, papillomas, and carcinomas, across various organs [36]. To date, few studies have investigated the association between epithelial neoplasms and COVID-19, making it difficult to identify any potential reason for this pronounced decline. We hope that future research will explore this relationship more deeply.

The limitations of our research must be considered. We chose to use data from the SEER 22 registries database in this study to include the maximum number of patients for a comprehensive description of the difficulties faced by oncological patients during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, at the cost of analyzing only a limited number of variables, even the treatment history cannot be analyzed due to limited data. Owing to the disproportionately high impact of COVID-19-related deaths on mortality rates in groups with fewer deaths, we presented results with more than 5,000 deaths for both groups in terms of the age-adjusted mortality rate. Previous studies investigating the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on incidence and mortality rates have focused on selected histological tumor types [18, 20, 21]. To illustrate the comprehensive effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on the mortality of patients with tumors, we deviated from this method and uploaded the complete results as supplementary material, providing detailed data for interested readers. As an epidemiological analysis based on a public database, our study was limited by the variables that were available in the SEER database and could not establish causal mechanisms to explain the changes that were observed during the pandemic. Moreover, the potential for data collection inaccuracies must be acknowledged. However, as the most comprehensive study to analyze the changes in mortality during the pandemic, we believe our findings should be informative for policymakers considering additional policies to mitigate the effects of pandemics on the most vulnerable patients. We hope our research will shed some light on the treatment of tumors and the strategies implemented by patients with tumors during such an unexpected pandemic.

Conclusion

We conducted comprehensive research to analyze the effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on the mortality of oncological patients using a population-based approach. Our findings suggest that the COVID-19 pandemic significantly affected mortality rates among patients with tumors, with most groups exhibiting a notable increase in mortality rates during the pandemic. Patients with meningiomas had the greatest increase in mortality. Conversely, the mortality rates among patients with lung and bronchial tumors significantly decreased. Hopefully, public health authorities and medical professionals can derive valuable insights from the experiences of patients with lung and bronchial cancer during the pandemic.
