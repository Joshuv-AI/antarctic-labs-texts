Curative antimalarial efficacy and hepato-renal protective effects of Melochia umbellata leaf extract in rats infected with Plasmodiumberghei
Authors: Vincent Ngouana, Patrick Valere Tsouh Fokou, Marius Jaures Tsakem Nangap, Raoul Kemzeu, Aubin Kamche Youbi, Fabrice Fekam Boyom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria remains a major global health issue, particularly in endemic regions. Traditional medicine offers promising remedies, such as Melochia umbellata, a plant used in Cameroonian folk medicine for malaria treatment. While its in vitro antiplasmodial activity has been reported, the safety and curative efficacy of M. umbellata aqueous leaf extract (MULAE) in vivo required further investigation. This study evaluated the curative antimalarial and hepato-renal protective effects of MULAE in a Plasmodium berghei-infected rat model, alongside its phytochemical characterization. Qualitative and quantitative analyses confirmed the presence of pharmacologically active secondary metabolites, including alkaloids, phenols, flavonoids, and saponins. In acute toxicity tests (OECD guideline 423), female rats received single oral doses of MULAE (2000, 5000 ​mg/kg). No mortality or signs of toxicity were observed throughout the 14-day monitoring period, indicating an LD50 greater than 5000 ​mg/kg. For the curative assay, P. berghei-infected rats were treated for five days with MULAE (100, 200, 400 ​mg/kg), chloroquine (10 ​mg/kg), or distilled water. MULAE significantly suppressed parasitemia dose-dependently; the 400 ​mg/kg dose achieved 86.20% chemosuppression. The extract also improved hematological markers (restoring hemoglobin levels to 14.9 ​g/dL at 400 ​mg/kg), mitigated infection-induced weight loss, and reduced organ damage. This organ protection was evidenced by improved blood serum markers (creatinine, ALAT, ASAT, bilirubin, total protein), restored oxidative status, and ameliorated histopathological changes in the liver, kidneys, and spleen. These findings validate the traditional use of M. umbellata and support its potential as a source for novel antimalarial and organ-protective agents.Keywords: Melochia umbellata, Antimalarial efficacy, Plasmodium berghei, Acute toxicity, Nephro-hepatoprotection

Introduction

Malaria, a parasitic disease, affects millions globally, with an estimated 249 million cases and 608,000 deaths reported in 2022 [1]. Endemic in intertropical regions, the disease primarily affects sub-Saharan Africa, which accounts for 94% of cases and 96% of deaths worldwide [1]. In Cameroon, malaria is a leading cause of hospitalization and death, with 6,459,000 cases and 2481 deaths reported in 2022 [1]. While control strategies such as awareness campaigns, vector control, and preventive measures for pregnant women are in place, challenges remain. The widespread prevalence of malaria, limited access to modern drugs, increasing drug resistance, and the lack of alternative treatments has spurred the search for new antimalarial drugs [[2], [3], [4]] The quest for effective antimalarial agents has led researchers to explore a variety of natural resources, including the traditional use of plant-based remedies.

Indeed, traditional medicine plays a crucial role in addressing this challenge. The use of herbal remedies has grown significantly, with up to 80% of the global population relying on them for primary healthcare [5]. The rising prevalence of drug-resistant malaria strains has intensified the need for new remedies from medicinal plants [6], prompting research into ethnopharmacological information from traditional healers [7]. The Malvaceae family, particularly the Melochia genus, has been investigated for its antimalarial properties. Studies have shown the efficacy of various Melochia species, including Melochia chamaedrys for treating malaria and inflammation, Melochia corchorifolia for its anti-cancer properties, and Melochia odorata for inhibiting HIV P24 [[7], [8], [9]]. Melochia umbellata, a plant native to tropical Africa and Asia, has a long-standing history of traditional use as a malaria treatment [10].

We previously reported that M. umbellata extract demonstrated potent antimalarial activity against P. falciparum (IC50: 11.91 ​μg/mL (Dd2), 11.62 ​μg/mL (3D7)), with rapid kill kinetics and low hemolysis (IC50 ​> ​500 ​μg/mL) [11]. UHPLC-MS analysis revealed thirteen potential antiplasmodial compounds (flavonoids, pyrimidones, phenolics, tannins, terpenoids) [11]. However, the safety and efficacy of M. umbellata in animal models, particularly its organ-protective capacity alongside antimalarial action, remained unexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the in vivo safety and curative antimalarial efficacy of Melochia umbellata aqueous leaf extract (MULAE) in a Plasmodium berghei-infected Wistar rat model. Our work specifically investigated MULAE's impact on parasitemia, hematological parameters, oxidative stress, and its hepato-renal protective effects through biochemical and histopathological analyses. These findings are crucial for validating the traditional medicinal use of M. umbellata and provide a critical basis for the potential development of novel, effective, and safe plant-based antimalarial therapeutics that also address organ pathology associated with malaria.

Material & method

Plant collection, identification and extraction

The fresh leaves of Melochia umbellata were harvested, authenticated and processed following the method described in Ref. [11]. Briefly, fresh leaves of Melochia umbellata were harvested in January 2023 from Mbanga, Littoral Region, Cameroon. The plant material was authenticated by Dr. Mounmemi Kpoumi Hubert at the Cameroon National Herbarium (Yaoundé) by comparison with voucher assigned the reference number 58216 HNC. Leaves were air-dried, ground, and extracted using a decoction method. 300 ​g of powdered plant material was boiled in 3 ​L of water for 20 ​min. The mixture was filtered, and the filtrate was concentrated using a rotary evaporator and dried in a convection oven at 45–50 ​°C to obtain a constant dry mass of M. umbellata leaf aqueous extract (MULAE). Subsequently, a part of the extract was subjected to qualitative phytochemical analysis [12].

Phytochemical analysis of MULAE

To characterize the chemical profile of the MULAE used in our in vivo experiments, we performed both qualitative screening to identify major classes of secondary metabolites and quantitative assays to determine the total content of key phenolic compounds and flavonoids. This preliminary phytochemical characterization is crucial for understanding the potential chemical basis of the observed biological activities and for guiding future research toward potential active principles. These compound classes are widely recognized for their diverse pharmacological properties, including antiplasmodial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory effects. For instance, flavonoids are known free radical scavengers and antioxidants, properties highly relevant to mitigating oxidative stress during malaria infections [13,14]. Similarly, phenolic compounds including flavonoids are well-documented for their potent antimalarial activity and potential as neuro-hepatoprotective therapeutic agents [15]. While other phytochemicals like alkaloids and saponins were also screened for presence, their specific quantification was beyond the scope of this initial in vivo validation study, which focused on the crude extract.

Qualitative phytochemical screening

Qualitative phytochemical screening of MULAE was conducted to identify the presence of major classes of bioactive metabolites, including alkaloids, phenols, flavonoids, saponins, tannins, quinones, steroids, triterpenes, glycosides, and anthocyanins. These analyses were performed using established standard chemical tests [[16], [17], [18]] as follows:

Tannins: To 1 ​mL of the extract, 1 ​mL of 10% KOH was added. A dirty white precipitate indicated the presence of tannins.

Alkaloids: Five milliliters of 1% HCl were added to 3 ​mL of the extract, heated for 20 ​min, then cooled and filtered. Subsequently, 1 ​mL of the filtrate was combined with picric acid solution. Turbidity or precipitation confirmed alkaloids.

Flavonoids: One milliliter of 10% NaOH was mixed with 3 ​mL of the extract. A yellow coloration indicated the presence of flavonoids.

Phenols: Two drops of 5% FeCl2 were added to 1 ​mL of the extract in a test tube. A green precipitate signified the presence of phenols.

Glycosides: Ten milliliters of 50% H2SO4 were added to 1 ​mL of the extract, followed by boiling in a water bath for 15 ​min. Subsequently, 10 ​mL of Fehling's solution were added and boiled, yielding a brick-red precipitate that indicated the presence of glycosides.

Steroids: Five drops of concentrated H2SO4 were mixed with 1 ​mL of the extract. A red coloration indicated the presence of steroids.

Triterpenoids: Ten milligrams of extract were dissolved in 1 ​mL of chloroform. Subsequently, 2 ​mL of concentrated H2SO4 were added, followed by 1 ​mL of acetic anhydride. The formation of a reddish-violet color indicated the presence of triterpenes.

Anthocyanins: Two milliliters of aqueous extract were combined with 2N HCl, followed by the addition of ammonia. A conversion from pink-red to blue-violet indicated the presence of anthocyanins.

Saponins: Zero point 5 ​g of crude powder were shaken with water in a test tube, then warmed in a water bath. The persistence of froth indicated the presence of saponins.

Quinones: One milliliter of extract underwent the addition of alcoholic KOH. A resulting red to blue coloration indicated the presence of quinones.

These qualitative methods provide a foundational understanding of MULAE's phytochemical diversity, relevant to its traditional uses and pharmacological potential, by confirming the presence or absence of these compound classes within the crude extract.

Quantitative determination of total phenolic and flavonoid content

Given the widespread recognition of phenolic compounds including flavonoids for their significant antioxidant, antiplasmodial, hepatoprotection, and nephroprotection properties, we performed quantitative analyses to determine their total content in MULAE. Therefore, quantifying them provides an important metric for standardization and allows for potential correlations with the observed efficacy.

Total Phenolic Content: The total phenolic content was determined spectrophotometrically using the Folin-Ciocalteu method [19]. To 100 ​μL of the extract solution, 500 ​mL of the 10% Folin Ciocalteu reagent (diluted 10 times) was added. Then, 400 ​μL of 7.5% sodium carbonate was introduced and the contents were mixed thoroughly. After incubation for 30 ​min at room temperature in darkness, the absorbance of the mixture was read with a spectrophotometer (UVmini-1240, UV–Vis Spectrophotometer, Shimadzu-Japan) against the blank reagent at 765 ​nm. Each test was carried out three times for each concentration of the sample. The quantification of the polyphenols was done based on a linear calibration curve (y ​= ​2.6156x −0.0153; r2 ​= ​0.9997) produced by a standard Gallic acid at different concentrations ranging from 0 to 1000 ​μg/mL under the same conditions as the sample (suppl 1). The results were expressed in milligrams of Gallic Acid Equivalent (GAE) per gram of dry weight (μg GAE/g DW).

Total Flavonoid Content: Total flavonoid content was quantified using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method [20]. A volume of 400 ​μL of each extract was mixed with 120 ​μL of NaNO2 and allowed to stand for 5 ​min. Subsequently, 120 ​μL of 10% aluminium chloride was added, and the mixture was incubated for 6 ​min at room temperature. Then, 800 ​μL of 1 ​M sodium hydroxide was added, and the final volume was adjusted to 2.5 ​mL with distilled water. The absorbance of the reaction mixture was measured at 510 ​nm. A calibration curve (y ​= ​0.2019x ​+ ​0.0012; r2 ​= ​0.9998) was constructed using catechin standards (0–1000 ​μg/mL) under the same conditions (suppl 1). The content of total flavonoids was calculated from the calibration curve and expressed in milligram quercetin equivalent per gram of dry weight (mg QE/g DW).

These quantitative assessments provide valuable data on the relative abundance of these key bioactive groups within MULAE. While further isolation and purification of individual compounds would be a subsequent step for drug development, this initial quantification helps in understanding the extract's potency and serves as a crucial parameter for reproducibility and future comparative studies.

Acute oral toxicity study of MULAE

The acute oral toxicity of Melochia umbellata aqueous leaf extract (MULAE) was evaluated in accordance with the OECD Guideline for the Testing of Chemicals, No. 423: Acute Oral Toxicity – Acute Toxic Class Method (2001) [21]. Nine adult non-pregnant female Wistar rats, weighing between 180 and 220 ​g, were randomly assigned to three groups of three animals each. Group 1 served as the control and received distilled water (10 ​mL/kg, orally). Groups 2 and 3 received MULAE at single oral doses of 2000 ​mg/kg and 5000 ​mg/kg, respectively.

Prior to dosing, animals were fasted for 12 ​h with free access to water. Food was withheld for an additional 4 ​h post-dosing. The extract and distilled water were administered orally via gavage. Following administration, individual rats were meticulously monitored for the initial 4 ​h, with particular attention to the first 30 ​min, and then daily for a total of 14 days.

Behavioral and clinical observations were systematically documented. These included, but were not limited to, changes such as increased aggression, altered mobility (such as ataxia, reduced movement), tremors, convulsions, restlessness, or lethargy. Physical appearance changes included piloerection, excessive salivation, lacrimation, and changes in stool consistency. Sensory responses were marked by heightened sensitivity to sound and touch. Additionally, general toxicity signs such as respiratory changes, diarrhea, or other overt signs of discomfort or illness were noted. Daily records were also maintained for food and water consumption to assess appetite changes, and individual body weights were recorded on days 0, 7, and 14. Mortality was recorded daily. At the conclusion of the 14-day observation period, all surviving rats were humanely sacrificed under anesthesia (Ketamine/Diazepam; 40/5 ​mg/kg, ip). A thorough macroscopic necropsy was performed, and vital organs, including the liver, kidneys, spleen, lungs, and heart, were carefully excised and weighed. In vivo antimalarial activity of MULAE

Female Wistar rats aged 8–10 weeks with an average body weight of 190 ​g were used in this study. The animals were bred and housed under standard laboratory conditions in the animal house of the Laboratory of Animal Physiology, University of Yaoundé 1.

The Plasmodium berghei NK-65 strain, obtained from Bei Resources (USA), was utilized to assess the in vivo antimalarial potential of the MULAE. This parasite strain was maintained in the laboratory through serial blood passage between infected and healthy rats.

To initiate the experiment, healthy female rats were intraperitoneally inoculated with 500 ​μL of blood containing 1 ​× ​106 ​P. berghei-infected erythrocytes. Three days post-infection, healthy rats (n ​= ​5) and infected rats (n ​= ​25) were randomly divided into six groups (n ​= ​5/group):

Sacrifice, blood and organ collection

On the fifth day of treatment, animals were fasted, followed by anesthesia with ketamine (intraperitoneal route) on the sixth day. Subsequently, the animals were euthanized. Blood was collected from each animal and divided into two portions: one in an EDTA tube for complete blood count (CBC) analysis and the other in a dry tube for biochemical analysis. The blood in the dry tubes was centrifuged at 3000 ​rpm at 4 ​°C for 15 ​min, and the resulting serum was stored at −20 ​°C.

Organs including the liver, spleen, and kidneys were harvested. A small portion of each organ was homogenized with Tris buffer to create a 20% homogenate, which was stored in 1.5 ​mL tubes for oxidative stress analysis. The remaining organs were fixed in 10% buffered formalin for histopathological examination.

Evaluation of the effects of the extract on some hematological and biochemical parameters

Hematological analysis

The effect of MULAE on hematological parameters in malaria-infected rats was assessed using a “Mindray BC-5300″ hematology analyzer at Yaoundé Central Hospital. This device draws blood from an EDTA tube to perform counts of red blood cells and platelets, as well as measurements of hemoglobin, hematocrit, and erythrocyte levels. It then conducts differential lysis of red blood cells to quantify various leukocyte populations based on specific enzymatic activity. The measured parameters are subsequently plotted and printed.

Analysis of some biochemical parameters of the renal and hepatic function

Liver and kidney function in malaria-infected rats was assessed by measuring serum transaminases (ALAT and ASAT), total bilirubin, total protein, and creatinine levels. Total protein was evaluated using the Biuret method [22], while other parameters were determined with commercial diagnostic kits (LABKIT, France).

Analysis of some oxidative stress parameters

Liver, spleen, or kidney sections were homogenized in 2 ​mL of 50 ​mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4). The 20% homogenates were centrifuged at 3000 ​g for 25 ​min at 4 ​°C, and the supernatants were collected for assessing malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), nitrite (NO) levels, and catalase activity using standardized methods [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27]].

Histopathological analysis

Liver, kidney, and spleen tissues from each animal, fixed in 10% buffered formalin, were dehydrated through graded alcohol concentrations and embedded in paraffin. Serial 5 ​μm sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) for examination under an Olympus light microscope and photographed using a 20 auricular 100 objective (HE ​× ​200).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as the mean ​± ​standard error of the mean (SEM). To compare differences between treatment groups, statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Tukey's post-hoc test was subsequently applied for pairwise multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value <0.05. All statistical computations were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.1, GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA).

Results

Phytochemical analysis of MULAE

Qualitative phytochemical screening of M. umbellata leaf extract (MULAE) revealed the presence of a rich profile of bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, tannins, alkaloids, saponins, triterpenes, glycosides, and phenols. Quinones, steroids, and anthocyanins were not detected.

Quantitative analysis determined the total phenolic content to be 5.72 ​± ​0.13 ​mg GAE/g DW and total flavonoid content to be 5.35 ​± ​0.29 ​mg QE/g DW. Statistical analysis indicated no significant difference between the total content of phenolic compounds and total flavonoids.

Acute oral toxicity profile of MULAE

MULAE's acute oral toxicity was assessed in female Wistar rats over 14 days. Clinical signs, mortality, and body weight changes were monitored. Single doses (2000 and 5000 ​mg/kg) were evaluated for effects on organ weights (liver, kidney, spleen, heart, lung).

Administration of MULAE at single oral doses of 2000 ​mg/kg and 5000 ​mg/kg resulted in no observed signs of toxicity or adverse clinical effects throughout the 14-day observation period. Specific parameters monitored included aggressiveness, mobility, tremors, changes in coat appearance, convulsions, stool appearance, and sensitivity to sound and touch. No deaths occurred during the 14-day observation period, indicating an LD50 value greater than 5000 ​mg/kg. The clinical observations are summarized in Table 1.

Discussion

The escalating challenge of drug-resistant malaria necessitates the continuous exploration of novel therapeutic agents, particularly from natural sources. While several plant-derived compounds have demonstrated promising antimalarial activity, the present study uniquely investigates the in vivo antimalarial and organ-protective effects of Melochia umbellata aqueous leaf extract (MULAE), building upon our previous in vitro findings. This research provides crucial insights into MULAE's potential as a multifaceted therapeutic agent, addressing both parasite burden and malaria-induced organ damage.

Qualitative analysis of MULAE revealed the presence of pharmacologically active phytochemicals, including alkaloids, phenols, flavonoids, and saponins. Previous UHPLC-MS analysis further identified pyrimidones and terpenoids [11,28], collectively suggesting a rich chemical profile. The observed significant reduction in parasitemia (p ​< ​0.05) strongly supports MULAE's antimalarial potential, which can be attributed to the synergistic action of these diverse compounds.

Prior to evaluating its efficacy, the safety of MULAE was rigorously assessed. Our toxicological investigations determined a high LD50 value (>5000 ​mg/kg), classifying MULAE in category 5 of the Globally Harmonized Classification System [29], which indicates minimal to no toxicity. This finding is consistent with WHO criteria and aligns with similar acute toxicity studies on other herbal extracts, providing a strong rationale for the doses selected in our antimalarial studies. The observed lack of significant weight loss or gain in treated rats further supports MULAE's favorable safety profile, as malaria infection is typically associated with decreased body weight due to disrupted metabolism and hypoglycemia [30].

Our study utilized an in vivo antiplasmodial model with P. berghei, which shares pathophysiological similarities with P. falciparum [[31], [32], [33]], allowing us to evaluate MULAE's effects within a complex biological system involving the immune response and potential prodrug effects. The significant reduction in parasitemia observed with MULAE, particularly at 200 ​mg/kg (65.53%) and 400 ​mg/kg (86.20%), demonstrates its potent antimalarial properties. These chemosuppression effects are considered ‘very good' (>65%) according to the criteria established by Deharo et al. [34]. The dose-dependent increase in effectiveness suggests a potential optimal dosage for therapeutic outcomes.

This suppressive activity aligns with previous research on plants within the Malvaceae family. For instance, Abdulelah and Zainal-Abidin [35] reported that the aqueous extract of Nigella sativa, a closely related species, significantly suppressed Plasmodium berghei infection in mice at doses of 200 and 400 ​μg/kg. While direct in vivo comparative studies with M. umbellata are limited, our current in vivo findings corroborate our earlier in vitro results that demonstrated potent antiplasmodial activity of M. umbellata extract against P. falciparum [IC50: 11.91 ​μg/mL (Dd2), 11.62 ​μg/mL (3D7)] [11]. This suggests a direct antiplasmodial effect of the extract rather than solely an immunomodulatory one. Our study further suggests that MULAE inhibit parasite multiplication rather than causing complete clearance, as evidenced by the persistence of parasites by day seven.

The antimalarial efficacy of MULAE likely stems from the known mechanisms of its constituent phytochemicals. Flavonoids, such as quercetin, are recognized for their ability to disrupt mitochondrial function and inhibit essential parasite enzymes like heat-shock protein (HSP) and acetylcholinesterase [8,36]. They also interfere with heme detoxification by inhibiting β-haematin formation, a mechanism similar to that of chloroquine [37,38]. Alkaloids, exemplified by quinine, exert direct anti-parasitic activity [39]. While saponins and terpenoids also exhibit potent antimalarial activity [36]– a characteristic seen in other medicinal plants like Artemisia annua (source of artemisinin) and Azadirachta indica (neem)–their specific contributions in MULAE require further elucidation. Other potential mechanisms involve prenylated chalcones disrupting heme degradation and bromopyrrole alkaloids targeting fatty acid synthase (FAS II) enzymes [[36], [37], [38]]. Additionally, some natural compounds are known to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), inhibit food vacuole falcipains, and target Plasmodium falciparum glyoxalase I (PfGLOI). Although this study does not precisely delineate the specific antiplasmodial mechanisms of MULAE, the observed effects (IC50∼: 11.91 ​μg/mL (Dd2), 11.62 ​μg/mL (3D7) [11]) are likely a culmination of these varied pathways. This potent in vitro activity is comparable to IC50 ranges reported for other clinically relevant plant-derived antimalarials or extracts under investigation, such as Cryptolepis sanguinolenta root extracts similar IC50s–0.9–14 ​μg/mL [40]. Furthermore, the significant in vivo chemosuppression (86.20 ​% at 400 ​mg/kg) observed in the P. berghei-infected rat model approaches the efficacy of the chloroquine control (10 ​mg/kg) in this curative assay, while demonstrating a favorable therapeutic index (hemolytic IC50∼ ​> ​500 ​μg/mL) [11].

Malaria infection is characteristically associated with severe hematological alterations, primarily anemia due to increased destruction of both parasitized and non-parasitized red blood cells [41]. In our study, P. berghei-infected rats exhibited a significant decrease in red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (HGB) levels, and mean corpuscular volume (MCV), consistent with established literature on malaria-induced anemia. Conversely, we observed an increase in white blood cells (WBC) and various leukocyte types (lymphocytes, monocytes, and granulocytes), indicative of an immune response to the infection [42]. Crucially, administration of MULAE at all doses effectively dose-dependently corrected these hematological changes. The MULAE-treated groups showed a significant rise in erythrocyte counts compared to the negative control, indicating the extract's ability to counteract malaria's harmful effects on blood parameters. This suggests MULAE could alleviate malaria-associated anemia, a key contributor to morbidity and mortality. The findings support MULAE's therapeutic potential for malaria, both in reducing parasitic load (implied by improved hematological markers) and addressing related complications. Future research should identify the active compounds in MULAE, their mechanisms, and assess in vivo efficacy for parasitic clearance.

Beyond its direct antimalarial activity, MULAE demonstrated significant organ-protective effects, particularly on the liver and kidneys, which are highly vulnerable to malaria-induced damage. In the malaria control group, we observed considerable liver and kidney alterations, including hepatomegaly, sinusoidal dilatation, impaired venous outflow leading to hepatocyte death, and accumulation of hemozoin causing oxidative stress [[43], [44], [45]]. Liver damage was further indicated by hyperbilirubinemia, suggesting hepatobiliary dysfunction [46]. Renal impairment was evidenced by increased creatinine levels and decreased total protein, consistent with hemodynamic disturbances, hypoxia, and impaired glomerular filtration during malaria [47,48]. Splenomegaly, characterized by congestion and hemozoin accumulation, was also a frequent observation [49]. Remarkably, MULAE administration led to the repair of these liver and kidney alterations. This restorative effect is likely attributed to the extract's antioxidant properties, primarily due to the presence of phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and tannins [50]. These compounds are crucial for mitigating the oxidative stress induced by malaria infection, which results from the degradation of hemoglobin and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as superoxide (O2−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and hydroxylradicals (•OH) [51]. Our findings confirmed this oxidative stress in the liver, kidneys, and spleen by observing decreased antioxidant defenses (catalase and glutathione [GSH]) and increased levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and nitrite. The significant decrease in MDA and nitrite and the increase in GSH and catalase in MULAE-treated groups suggest that the extract directly enhances antioxidant status, thereby ameliorating anatomical damage [52,53]. This protective mechanism aligns with the known antioxidant capacities of polyphenolic compounds [54] and alkaloids [55].

Limitation

This study provides preliminary insights but is subject to several limitations that impact the definitive interpretation and translational potential of its findings. More specifically, the specific bioactive compounds within the Melochia umbellata leaf aqueous extract (MULAE) responsible for the observed antimalarial and organ-protective effects remain unidentified. This makes it difficult to standardize the extract for consistent results and future clinical application. Even if compound classes were characterized, the absence of isolated and identified active constituents means conclusions regarding direct compound-specific efficacy must be interpreted with caution. Future research is essential to pinpoint these active constituents, ideally through bioassay-guided fractionation, and to elucidate their precise molecular targets and pathways within Plasmodium parasites and host cells. This step is critical for understanding the mechanism of action and for rational drug development. Additionally, unassessed chronic toxicity represents a significant hurdle for clinical translation, as long-term safety data are paramount for any therapeutic agent. Lastly, while the in vivo rodent model provides valuable insights into the complex interactions within a living system, direct extrapolation of these findings to human clinical outcomes requires further validation through preclinical and clinical trials.

Conclusion

This study confirms that the Melochia umbellata aqueous leaf extract (MULAE), administered orally, has significant antimalarial activity in Plasmodium berghei-infected rats. The extract not only effectively reduced parasitemia and prevented weight loss but also improved key hematological parameters and mitigated organ damage by reducing oxidative stress and restoring organ function. These findings provide strong preclinical support for the traditional use of M. umbellata as a therapeutic agent for malaria. Future research should focus on isolating the specific bioactive compounds responsible for these effects through bioassay-guided fractionation and using in vitro assays to precisely determine their mechanisms of action.
