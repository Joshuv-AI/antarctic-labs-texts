Developing new methods for the accurate evaluation of children’s bone age based on X-ray images: a study on the accuracy and consistency of artificial intelligence and manual bone age evaluation
Authors: Ji-Yun Zhang, Ying Shi, Jie Pan, Jian-Hua Yin, Ai-Ping Wang, Ying Xie, Zhi-Yong Ding, Bin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBone age assessment is crucial to monitoring children’s growth and development. Artificial intelligence (AI)-based bone age assessment systems are widely used in clinical practice, significantly enhancing the efficiency of evaluations and enabling the objective quantification of results. However, obtaining precise assessments can be challenging, and the available evaluation methods for bone age involve certain limitations. This study aimed to propose a novel evaluation method based on a comparative study between an AI system and specialized pediatric radiologists to achieve the goal of precise bone age assessment.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted involving the continuous collection of 1,083 images from children with comprehensive clinical data. Bone age was evaluated with both an AI system and three experienced pediatric radiologists applying the Greulich and Pyle (G-P), radius-ulna-short bones Chinese (RUS-CHN), and Tanner-Whitehouse 3 (TW3)-Carpal methods. The children’s chronological age served as the reference standard. For intergroup comparisons, AI results were compared with chronological age, manual results with chronological age, and AI results with manual results. This design was applied to identify children with nonstandard bone morphological development. For these comparisons, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) and Bland-Altman plots were used to assess the consistency, the accuracy of the results were each calculated, and the mean absolute error (MAE) was determined.ResultsThe detection rate of nonstandard bone morphological development was significantly higher (29.19%) than the rate of children clinically diagnosed with growth and developmental abnormalities (7.83%). The AI evaluation results demonstrated high agreement with manual assessment results (ICC: 0.962–0.982; MAE: 0.38–0.45 years). In contrast, both AI and manual evaluation results showed variable and poor agreement with chronological age across different age groups. The MAE of chronological age ranged from 0.75 to 1.45 years for AI and from 0.68 to 0.95 years for manual assessments. Performance analysis revealed that the different methods had higher consistency and better accuracy for certain age groups. Specifically, the G-P method performed better in the lower age group (approximately 3–5 years old), TW3-Carpal method in the middle-to-lower age group (approximately 7–10 years old), and the RUS-CHN method for the middle-to-higher age group (approximately 11–13 years old).ConclusionsAlthough the capability of AI evaluation is comparable to that of specialized pediatric radiologists, both manual and AI evaluations are lacking in precision compared to chronological age. However, the development of a novel AI evaluation method that involves automatically allocating weights and values can address this deficiency to provide greater accuracy in future bone age assessment.

Introduction

Bone age reflects the regular pattern of timing for the appearance of ossification centers, the speed of ossification, and the closure timing and morphological changes of the epiphyses and metaphyses during the normal skeletal development process in humans. It is important to note that chronological age serves as a clinical benchmark for comparison rather than a true gold standard, as bone age represents the typical bone development level of healthy children at a given chronological age. However, due to population variations in growth and development, the relationship between indicators of skeletal maturity and chronological age may differ across populations or even within the same population over time (1). The measurement of bone age is crucial in monitoring the growth and development of children, as it accurately reflects the level of growth and maturity in children (2). Widely used bone age assessment methods include the Greulich and Pyle (G-P) method, the Tanner-Whitehouse 3 (TW3)-Carpal method, and the radius-ulna-short bones Chinese (RUS-CHN) method. In the G-P method, bone age is assessed by comparing a left-hand radiograph to the G-P atlas and determining the best visual match among the bone samples provided (3). In the TW3-Carpal method, bone age is determined by a 4-step process: the maturity of 20 regions of interest (ROIs) (13 RUS and 7 carpal bones) is assessed, a score is assigned to each ROI, the scores are used to sum a total RUS maturity score and a total carpal maturity score, and finally the totals are converted into a bone age via a reference table (4). The TW3-Carpal method has been adopted by most countries in the world, but in 2005, Chinese researchers introduced the RUS-CHN method to provide a standard better tailored to the pediatric population in China (1). The established bone age assessment methods (G-P, RUS-CHN, and TW3-Carpal) inherently emphasize different skeletal elements—the G-P method relies on overall pattern recognition of the entire hand, the RUS-CHN method scores certain long bones that mature earlier, and the TW3-Carpal method focuses on carpal bones which ossify later. These fundamental differences could lead to age-dependent variations in performance, a potential discrepancy which prompted us to conduct a detailed analysis across age strata to identify potential method-specific advantages. With the rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI) bone age assessment systems based on deep learning (DL) in the field of medical imaging, the efficiency and accuracy of bone age assessments have been significantly improved, and evaluation results have been objectively quantified (5). However, in clinical practice, the application of AI for assessing bone age has encountered several limitations. First, the result outputs appear to be inconsistent, with clinical reports often having two sets of bone age results, classified as R series and C series. Secondly, there are frequent discrepancies between the assessment results and chronological age, with the R series and C series results also being inconsistent. Finally, AI systems do not perform a second comparison for the before and after results, and manual adjustment is needed to obtain the final results (6). In order to address these issues, a multitude of studies have been conducted in which the accuracy and consistency were compared between AI assessment and manual assessment. The results consistently indicate that compared to experienced pediatric radiologists, AI assessment exhibits a high degree of consistency and greater accuracy (7,8).

Previous studies have often focused either on a single method to compare AI and manual assessment results or on multiple age groups to examination method—specific differences between AI and manual assessment (9,10). These works have typically employed a single point of comparison, lacked comprehensiveness in assessment, and exhibited a certain degree of bias in the evaluation results. Consequently, their results have been insufficient for extending the application to bone age assessment more generally. Overall, there is a lack of related studies that have compared AI assessment, manual assessment, and chronological age within the same dimension.

In this study, we applied a grid-based evaluation comparison with children’s chronological age serving as the reference standard. Specifically, we plotted the comparison of the assessment results between AI and chronological age and between manual and chronological age on the vertical axis, and we plotted age groups and three assessment methods (G-P, RUS-CHN, and TW3-Carpal) on the horizontal axis. Each assessment result was plotted to systematically compare the differences between AI and manual assessments for each method and age group, as well as the differences between the assessment results (AI and manual) and chronological age. The overall of aim of work was to conduct the most detailed evaluation possible of the assessment results in order generate an optimally comprehensive and rational reference. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1754/rc).

Methods

Research design

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and with China’s Ethical Review Measures for Life Sciences and Medical Research Involving Humans. The protocol for the study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of The First People’s Hospital of Kunming (IRB No. YLS2024-88). The requirement for informed consent for this retrospective analysis was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis.

A total of 1,156 left hand and wrist X-ray films of children who underwent bone age assessment under physical examination or due to complaints of abnormal growth and development were collected from February 2024 to February 2025 at The First People’s Hospital of Kunming, The First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University, and Qujing Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital. Among these, 1,083 cases with complete clinical data were selected and divided into 15 age groups according to their chronological age. Bone age was evaluated with the G-P, RUS-CHN and TW3-Carpal methods for both AI assessment and manual assessment. Cases with discrepancies in bone age results of less than or equal to positive or negative 1 year (≤ ±1) from chronological age were placed in the bone age standard development group; meanwhile, cases with discrepancies of more than positive or negative 1 year (> ±1) from chronological age were placed in the nonstandard bone age development group.

The chronological age of the children enrolled was used as the reference standard, and the consistency between the results of AI and manual evaluation was assessed according to the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and the 95% confidence interval (CI).

The accuracy of AI and manual evaluation was determined through comparison with the chronological age of children across three methods: G-P, RUS-CHN, and TW3-Carpal. The evaluation focused on discrepancies within specified error ranges (i.e., within 0.3 years, between 0.3 and 0.5 years, and between 0.5 and 1 year) for both AI and manual assessments. The mean absolute error (MAE) was calculated for comparisons between AI results and chronological age, manual results and chronological age, and AI and manual assessment results. Furthermore, Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to analyze the accuracy discrepancy between AI and manual evaluations and to identify the underlying reasons for the discrepancies.

Inclusion criteria

A retrospective, continuous collection of left hand and wrist X-ray films from 1,156 children who underwent bone age assessment from physical examination or due to complaints of abnormal growth and development was carried out from February 2024 to February 2025. Among these cases, 1,083 with complete clinical data were selected for the study, including 502 males and 581 females. To avoid bias, data were collected from children aged from 2 to 18 years. The chronological age of all children was obtained from birth certificates or identity cards, accurate to the month, and finally converted into chronological decimal age (e.g., 2 years and 6 months was converted into 2.5 years old). Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) administration of medications that could affect bone metabolism and growth; (II) presence of systemic diseases that could affect bone growth and metabolism; (III) lack of clinical information such as height, weight, and laboratory tests; (IV) images showing skeletal deformities or developmental variations; and (V) images of poor quality unsuited for evaluation.

Imaging acquisition standard

Hand and wrist X-ray imaging was performed with a digital X-ray imaging system (Philips, Amsterdam, the Netherlands). Children were standing or lying down (age ≤3 years old) for the examination, and the left hand and wrist images were collected for all examinees. For the imaging position, the left palm was placed face down in the center of the flat panel detector, the middle finger axis was aligned with the forearm axis, the thumb formed an angle of approximately 30° with the palm, and the central line was aligned with the head of the left third metacarpal. The imaging range included all the metacarpal bones and the complete wrist bones of the left hand. During imaging, a lead collar and lead apron were used to shield the child’s thyroid and gonads, among other endocrine glands, protecting the above-mentioned areas not intended for exposure. For children unable to cooperate independently, a family member was able assist them in staying still during the examination while corresponding radiation protection measures were implemented. Imaging conditions were set at a tube voltage of 50 kV, a tube current of 5 mAs, and a source-to-image distance of 100 cm, with manual exposure and an exposure index of 400–800 ms.

Image assessment procedure

To ensure the utmost accuracy and to prevent any evaluation bias, manual assessments were conducted by the same three highly specialized pediatric radiologists (with 12, 15, and 20 years of experience in pediatric imaging diagnosis, respectively). All readers performed double-blind bone age assessments using the G-P, RUS-CHN, and TW3-Carpal methods on the picture archiving and communication system workstation (JUSHA-M33D, Nanjing Jusha Display Technology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) at a screen resolution of 2,048×1,536. The average of the three clinicians’ assessment results was taken as the final manual evaluation result. If any of the results differed by > ±1 year, a department-wide consultation was held (with more than five clinicians being involved in the discussion) to obtain a relatively consistent result, and the average of this result was taken as the final manual evaluation result.

Bone age assessment

Bone age was assessed with three different methods: G-P, RUS-CHN, and TW3-Carpal. (I) Manual assessment was performed as mentioned above. (II) For the AI system assessment, uAI-XRImaging (v. 20240130sp1) bone age prediction software (United Imaging, Shanghai, China) was used. This software is based on a DL AI system (that combines both image-level classification loss and pixel-level localization loss) and can automatically obtain bone age assessment results directly. The uAI-XRImaging software is a commercially available tool that has received regulatory clearance. Performance metrics from its initial internal validation, demonstrating high accuracy against radiologist consensus, are reported in the manufacturer’s publication titled “Joint detection of ossification centers help increase the accuracy of the bone age estimation” (11). Independent external validation on a diversity of populations remains an area for future research.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The primary study endpoint was the agreement and consistency between the AI results and chronological age, manual results and chronological age, and AI results and manual results as quantified by the Cohen’s kappa coefficient, ICC, and Bland-Altman analysis. Secondary end points included the error (bias) of each method versus chronological age as assessed by MAE, and the proportion of estimates within specified error ranges (≤0.3, 0.3–0.5, and 0.5–1 year). The chronological age of the children enrolled was used as the reference standard to calculate the accuracy and MAE of both the AI and manual assessment systems. The agreement between AI and manual assessments in terms of accuracy was analyzed via the Cohen’s kappa coefficient. The ICC was used to assess the consistency between AI assessments and manual assessments compared to the reference standard, with P<0.05 indicating a statistically significant difference. Bland-Altman plots were constructed to determine the 95% limits of agreement (LoA) between AI assessment, manual assessment, and the reference standard and to compare the differences between the AI and manual assessment results. The McNemar test was used to evaluate the proportions within 0.3, 0.3–0.5, and 0.5–1 year between the AI and manual assessment results. GraphPad Prism 10 software (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA) was used to create clear and concise graphs, aiding in the visualization of our findings.

Results

Detection results

Detection rate in the nonstandard bone age development group

The three methods, G-P, RUS-CHN, and TW3-Carpal, could detect children in the nonstandard bone age development group across all age ranges, with a high positive detection rate (Table 1). Reviewing the general information of children in the nonstandard group and clinically diagnosed cases of abnormal growth and development (advanced or delayed) (Table 1), we found that the average proportion of bone age morphological abnormalities was 29.19% (7.23–45.45%), which was significantly higher than the average proportion of clinically diagnosed cases 7.93% (0–18.18%). Pearson correlation analysis indicated that the two values were moderately correlated but not significant (r=0.423).

Age distribution characteristics of the nonstandard bone age development group

Horizontal comparison of the different age ranges in the nonstandard group yielded the following results: children ≤3 years exhibited accelerated growth, with a progression of more than 1 year ahead of the expected growth. After the age of 4 years, a combination of accelerated and delayed growth and development was observed, with 42.25% exhibiting accelerated progress and 46.65% delayed development.

In the longitudinal comparison of the methods, it was found that RUS-CHN method was more sensitive at detecting accelerated growth (more than 1 year in advance) within the 5 to 11-year age range. The consistency between AI and manual assessment results was high (ICC: 0.982; 95% CI: 0.970–0.993). On the other hand, the TW3-Carpal method was more sensitive in detecting delayed growth (less than 1 year delay) in the 11 to 15-year age range. The consistency between AI and manual assessment results for this method was also high (ICC: 0.968; 95% CI: 0.942–0.994).

Analysis of distribution characteristics of bone age assessment results compared to chronological age

Overall tendency of bone age assessment results

The difference between the evaluation results and the chronological age in this study is shown in the box plot in Figure 2. The results of G-P evaluation fluctuated around the baseline. Overall, they tended to be lower first and then higher; that is, the assessment results for the younger age group (3–8 years old) were generally less than the chronological age, and those for ages 9–11 years were closer to the chronological age. The assessment results for the age groups older than 11 years tended to exceed the chronological age. The RUS-CHN assessment results were generally above the baseline, indicating that the assessment results were likely to be older than the chronological age. TW3-Carpal assessment results tended to be lower at both ends, with the middle region close to the baseline, suggesting that the assessment results for ages 6 to 11 years were close to the chronological age, while for the ages 3–5 years and 12–15 years, they were likely to be less than the chronological age.

Accuracy

The AI assessment capability was close to that of the specialized pediatric radiologists, showing high agreement; however, compared to the chronological age of children, the agreement of both AI and manual assessment results was relatively unsatisfactory.

For error thresholds within 0.3, 0.3–0.5, and 0.5–1 year (Figure 4), the accuracy between AI and chronological age for the G-P method was 24.65%, 31.95%, and 52.72%, respectively; for the RUS method, 23.08%, 33.52%, and 62.14%, respectively; and for the TW3-Carpal method, 20.59%, 30.66%, and 54.20%, respectively. Discussion

In this multicenter, retrospective study of 1,083 children’s hand and wrist X-rays, we employed grid processing to simultaneously compare the differences between bone age AI assessment results, bone age manual assessment results, and chronological age. The principal findings were as follows. First, the accuracy of the AI system and its consistency with manual assessment results were both high, suggesting that the AI system’s capability is close to that of highly specialized pediatric radiologists. Second, in certain age groups, the assessment result of one of the three methods was the closest to chronological age, more accurately reflecting the normal growth and development of children. Third, at the same time, both AI and manual assessments differed to extent from the chronological age, indicating that the current assessment methods have shortcomings and need to be further improved.

Bone age assessment, a crucial area of research in pediatric radiology, has traditionally relied on manual assessment procedures that are time-consuming, require high levels of expertise, and often yield unreliable results. However, with the rapid advancements in computer technology and AI algorithms in medical imaging, the potential of AI-assisted bone age assessment systems to efficiently and accurately obtain bone age results is becoming increasingly evident (12,13). Studies by Kim et al. (14) and Lee et al. (15), among others, have reported an impressive accuracy rate of 92.2% within an error margin of 1 year for AI bone age assessment. In 2019, Son et al. (16) reported an MAE of 0.46 years compared to experienced pediatric radiologists. Similarly, in the same year, Song et al. (17) reported an MAE of 0.37 years. These findings suggest that compared to experienced pediatric radiologists, AI tools demonstrate high consistency and accuracy (18).

Previous work (19) on this subject has selected a specific age group to analyze and compared the differences between various methods or used a single assessment method to analyze and compare the differences in assessment results between different age groups. These previous research approaches have been relatively fragmented and lacked a comprehensive comparison, which inevitably leads to limitations in the relevant results. Our study is the first to compare two evaluation systems (AI and manual) and three methods (G-P, RUS-CHN, and TW3-Carpal) across all pediatric age groups. In this study, we used the chronological age of children as the reference standard, continuously selected children aged from 2 to 18 years (to avoid age bias), and analyzed AI assessments and assessments by highly specialized pediatric radiologists using three assessment methods across 15 age groups. This study is the first of its kind to attempt to derive grid-based comparison results of different variables from multiple age groups and methods in order to obtain the comprehensive evaluation results (for AI and manual assessments).

The principal findings from this study are interpreted in the following sections.

Accuracy, consistency, and efficiency of AI bone age assessment

The consistency and accuracy of the AI and manual assessment results are promising. Manual assessment was slightly more accurate than AI, but the AI system produced results close to those of the highly specialized pediatric radiologists.

Consistency

Under AI and manual assessment, the mean ICC was 0.976 (95% CI: 0.971–0.981) for G-P, 0.977 (95% CI: 0.971–0.982) for RUS-CHN, and 0.969 for TW3-Carpal (95% CI: 0.962–0.976). All the ICC values were greater than 0.95, indicating a high degree of consistency between the AI and manual assessments.

Accuracy

For error thresholds within 0.3, 0.3–0.5, and 0.5–1 year, the mean accuracy of the G-P, RUS-CHN, and TW3-Carpal methods was 95.23%, 93.77%, and 93.49%, respectively; meanwhile, the MAE was 0.42 (95% CI: 0.39–0.45), 0.39 (95% CI: 0.34–0.43), and 0.45 (95% CI: 0.36–0.53), respectively.

With comparison to specialized pediatric radiologists, AI assessment demonstrated a high degree of consistency (ICC: 0.972; 95% CI: 0.962–0.982); the accuracy was 95.23%, 93.77%, and 93.49% at error thresholds within 0.3, 0.3–0.5, and 0.5–1 year, respectively; and the mean MAE was 0.41 years (range, 0.34–0.53 years).

The AI assessment of bone age aligned with the evaluations performed by specialized pediatric radiologists. These results are consistent with reports from China and other countries (20-22), and the AI evaluation of children’s bone age has good application prospects (23,24). In our study, we applied three different evaluation methods to assess 15 different age groups, obtaining the consistency and accuracy of these methods for each specific age point. This allowed us to gain a more detailed and comprehensive comparison of the similarities and differences between AI and manual evaluations, as well as their differences with chronological age. The results are thus relatively comprehensive and may inform the selection of evaluation methods in clinical practice depending on the given age.

Advantages of different methods depending on age group

Overall, the bone age assessment results indicate that there was smaller overall data variability and good stability for G-P in the 3 to 7-year age group, TW3-Carpal in the 8 to 10-year age group, and RUS-CHN in the 11 to 13-year age group. Compared with the chronological age, the difference was well concentrated, the degree of dispersion was low, and the bone age assessment results were closest to the chronological age.

In the comparison with chronological age, the G-P method bone age assessment had an accuracy of 28.29%, 34.68%, and 58.76% in the 3 to 7-year age group for error thresholds within 0.3, 0.3–0.5, and 0.5–1 year, respectively. Overall, G-P method demonstrated greater stability than did the RUS-CHN and TW3-Carpal methods in this age group.

In the 8 to 10-year age group, TW3-Carpal method bone age assessment had an accuracy of 23.77%, 35.77%, and 62.47% for error thresholds within 0.3, 0.3–0.5, and 0.5–1 year, respectively, which was higher than that of the G-P and RUS-CHN methods in this age group.

In the 11 to 13-year age group, the RUS-CHN method bone age assessment had an accuracy of 24.24%, 36.88%, and 76.02% for error thresholds within 0.3, 0.3–0.5, and 0.5–1 year, respectively, which was significantly higher than that of the G-P and TW3-Carpal methods in the same age group.

These results indicate that in the 3 to 5-year age group, the G-P method has high consistency, while in the 3 to 7-year age group, the G-P method has better accuracy, with high concentration and low dispersion. Therefore, the G-P method is recommended as the primary assessment method for children 3–5 years old. Meanwhile, in the 6 to 11-year age group, the RUS-CHN method has high consistency, while in the 11 to 13-year age group, it has better accuracy, with a high concentration and low dispersion. Thus, the RUS-CHN method is recommended as the primary assessment method for children aged 11–13 years old. Finally, in the 7 to 9-year age group, the TW3-Carpal method had high consistency, while in the 8 to 10-year age group, it had better accuracy, with a high concentration and low dispersion. Consequently, the TW3-Carpal method is recommended as the primary assessment method for children 7–10 years old.

Overall, these findings are consistent with the physiological development process of children’s hand bones. In the early stages, the carpal bones develop rapidly, with their morphology and volume undergoing significant, easily identifiable changes with age. However, the development of the distal ends of the ulna and radius, as well as that of the epiphyses and metaphyses of the metacarpal and phalangeal bones, occurs later than that of the carpal bones. They enter a rapid growth phase during puberty, exhibiting distinct morphological and volumetric changes with age (1,25).

Limitations of current bone age assessment methods

High positive detection rate in the nonstandard group is relatively high

In our study, a large proportion of children were classified into the nonstandard group, but there was no sufficient clinical evidence to support their diagnosis of abnormal growth and development (advanced or delayed). This indicates that abnormal bone age assessment can occur in normally developing children, whereas normal evaluations for morphological bone age development may occasionally occur for children clinically diagnosed with abnormal growth. Thus, the currently available methods for bone age assessment may not fully and accurately reflect the growth and development status of children.

Limitations of the methods for certain age groups

The G-P assessment results fluctuated around the baseline, and except for the 3 to 7-year age group, the overall results were unstable, showing a pattern of being low first and then high, with significant variability. The RUS-CHN assessment results were generally above the baseline, indicating that the assessment results were generally greater than the chronological age. The TW3-Carpal method exhibited better consistency with the reference standard in the 7-year age group, had the lowest dispersion in the 9 and 12-year age groups, and had relatively high accuracy in the 8 to 10-year age groups. Overall, the assessment results form a pattern of being low at both ends and close to the baseline in the middle area, indicating that the assessment results are either less than or greater than the chronological age.

Consistency and accuracy of the AI and manual assessments

The AI and manual assessment results had better consistency and high agreement, but there was a large interval span and instability between manual results and chronological age and between the AI results and chronological age.

Consistency

For AI and chronological age, the ICCs for the G-P, RUS-CHN, and TW3-Carpal methods were 0.745 (95% CI: 0.527–0.963), 0.741 (95% CI: 0.504–0.978), and 0.709 (95% CI: 0.466–0.951), respectively.

Accuracy

For AI and chronological age, the accuracy for error thresholds within 0.3, 0.3–0.5, and 0.5–1 year (Figure 4) was 24.65% (267/1,083), 31.95% (346/1,083), and 52.72% (571/1,083) for the G-P method, respectively; 23.08% (250/1,083), 33.52% (363/1,083), and 62.14% (673/1,083) for the RUS-CHN method, respectively; and 20.59% (223/1,083), 30.66% (332/1,083), and 54.20% (587/1,083) for the TW3-Carpal method, respectively.

Moreover, the MAE (in years) for the G-P, RUS-CHN, and TW3-Carpal methods was 0.75 (95% CI: 0.33–1.17), 0.93 (95% CI: 0.58–1.28), and 1.45 (95% CI: 0.55–1.79), respectively.

These results indicate that, except for certain age groups, no single method has both high consistency and accuracy and stable assessment results, with each method possessing distinct limitations. Currently, the AI bone age assessment systems applied in clinical practice provide RUS-CHN and TW3-Carpal assessment results, but there is occasionally a significant difference between the two assessments for the same child, which could interfere with subsequent clinical diagnosis and treatment. Pediatric radiologists are devoted to research aimed at improving the precision of bone age assessment and developing effective methods to address the issues present in bone age assessment.

Reasons for challenges in pediatric bone age assessment

Growth trends in Chinese children

There is an accelerating trend in the growth and development of Chinese children. The 2005 RUS-CHN has been used for nearly 20 years, and the G-P and TW3-Carpal methods have been used for an even longer period. This study demonstrated that RUS-CHN method, which primarily evaluates long bones (radius, ulna, and metacarpals), frequently results in advanced assessment in the 4 to 14-year age range. Meanwhile, the results of the TW3-Carpal method, which mainly evaluates the carpal bones, lag in the 3 to 6- and 11 to 15-year age ranges. Advanced development is more common in long bones than in carpal bones (26,27), and the evaluation methods must be revised to reflect modern children’s accelerated growth and development.

Unsuitability of fixed development stages

Bone development is a continuous, uninterrupted process, but the presently used evaluation methods artificially divide this continuous process into fixed developmental stages. Bone age results obtained through a cumulative scoring system (RUS-CHN with 14 stages for the radius and 12 stages for other long bones; TW3-Carpal with 8 stages). The atlas method also defines the maturity signs of each bone in the standard image as a developmental stage, applying definite boundaries to a continuous developmental process (1). With fewer division points (14 stages for long bones and 8 stages for carpal bones), each level has a wide range of score changes (for example, nearly 100 scores differ between stages 7 and 8). This results in excessive weight for any ossification center in the total score, and slight morphological changes can affect the accuracy of the final evaluation result.

Limitations in weighting

The age of appearance of ossification centers, the speed of growth after their appearance, the mode of growth, and the trend of epiphyseal fusion are not conducted in a single fixed standard pattern (Table 5).

The current evaluation methods equally divide the weight between two types of bones (long bones and carpal bones). In the TW3-Carpal scoring method, it is believed that different factors regulate the development of carpal bones and long bones; therefore, it was decided that each category would account for 50% of the weight. In the G-P atlas method, it is assumed that each bone contributes equally, and each bone’s average level of development is used as the basis for evaluation results (1). Such methods assume that each bone’s maturity levels and growth periods are roughly similar, leading to discrepancies in the evaluation results, with some weights being too high or too low, and thus there is a tendency for the results to over- or underestimate age (28,29).

Application prospects of the novel AI system

In order to achieve greater accuracy in bone age assessments and address the above-mentioned limitations, we employed a novel AI system that can sufficiently refine the feature extraction of skeletal morphological changes and dynamically adjust scoring values and weights.

Based on findings from our study, we recommend the following measures to significantly enhance the accuracy of the assessments following measures. (I) ROIs should be sufficiently detailed to ensure that changes in every ossification center are considered and analyzed. (II) Based on the age of appearance of ossification centers and the speed of growth and development, the dynamic weight ratio of each ossification center should be calculated via substitution in the formula, which can more accurately reflect skeletal development and obtain an evaluation result that matches the actual level of development. (III) Based on results from two assessments, minute morphological changes and trends in skeletal growth and development should be detected with high sensitivity, which can determine whether the bone is in a period of rapid growth, slow growth, or stagnation. This is more scientifically rigorous than merely determining the age stage or ascertaining whether growth is advanced or delayed. Currently, evaluations are primarily performed manually, and thus it remains difficult to sufficiently subdivide ROIs and to dynamically and reasonably allocate score weights according to the continuous development of ossification centers. The ideal method can be realized with the rapid development of AI technology, particularly the improvement in big data algorithms, mainly built on DL architectures. Our research group developed a novel bone age evaluation model, whose purpose is to outline the dynamic growth changes of each ROI. The AI artificial neural network, based on DL algorithms, rationally allocates weights and score values to produce a more accurate age assessment and can also predict each ossification center’s growth (Figures 5,6). This trial study obtained good results in small sample size, but its feasibility remains to be verified in a larger sample size.

Evaluating bone age in children is a complex and multifactorial task with significant clinical value and remains a focus of research in pediatric imaging. The advancement of AI-based evaluation methods may represent a new, promising era in pediatric bone age assessment.

Conclusions

Compared with manual evaluation results, those of AI demonstrated both high accuracy and consistency, and the AI system’s assessment ability was similar to that of specialized pediatric radiologists. In certain age stages, the results aligned with chronological age to an extent; however, there remained appreciable discrepancies between the results obtained from the two evaluation systems compared to chronological age, indicating insufficient precision. The currently used bone age assessment methods remain to be improved, and a novel AI-assisted manual assessment system that is highly refined and sensitive to variables, with reasonably allocates weights and values, may achieve the goal of accurate bone age assessment.
