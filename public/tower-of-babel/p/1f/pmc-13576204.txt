Simultaneous quantification of total and free protein-bound uremic toxins in serum by LC-MS/MS: method development, validation, and clinical application in hemodialysis patients
Authors: Mert Andaç Temel, Erhan Canbay, Ebru Sezer, Nazlıcan Şeren, Soner Duman, Eser Yıldırım Sözmen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein-bound uremic toxins (PBUTs), including indoxyl sulfate (IxS), p-cresol sulfate (pCS), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), and 3-carboxy-4-methyl-5-propyl-2-furanpropionic acid (CMPF), accumulate in patients with End-Stage Kidney Disease (ESKD) due to their resistance to conventional dialysis clearance and have been implicated in cardiovascular complications, oxidative stress, and disease progression. Accurate quantification of both total and free fractions of these toxins is essential for understanding their clinical significance, yet validated methods with population-specific reference intervals remain scarce. In this study, a rapid, sensitive, and fully validated LC-MS/MS method was developed for the simultaneous quantification of total and free serum concentrations of four PBUTs using a 4-minute chromatographic run and a single-step protein precipitation with an acetonitrile/acetone (1:1, v/v) solvent system. For the first time, indole-3-carboxylic acid was employed as an internal standard for IAA and CMPF quantification, while dihydrochlorothiazide (DHCT) was used for IxS and pCS. Method validation was performed in accordance with European Medicine Agency (EMA) guidelines, demonstrating excellent linearity (R² > 0.99), recovery (90–110%), minimal matrix effects (95–105%), and intra- and inter-day precision below 10% coefficient of variation (CV) for all analytes. Reference intervals for total PBUT concentrations were established using data from 120 healthy volunteers from a Turkish population cohort, representing the largest reference dataset reported to date for these analytes. The validated method was applied to serum samples from 118 maintenance hemodialysis patients, revealing significantly elevated total PBUT concentrations compared to healthy controls (p < 0.001 for all analytes), with IxS and pCS showing the most pronounced accumulation (approximately 29- and 23-fold higher, respectively). ROC curve analysis demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance for IxS (AUC = 0.986), pCS (AUC = 0.980), and CMPF (AUC = 0.943) in discriminating hemodialysis patients from healthy controls, while IAA showed limited discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.633), likely reflecting its distinct protein binding dynamics in uremic conditions. Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant associations between total PBUT levels and biochemical markers of renal function, further supporting the clinical relevance of the developed method. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately 8–16% of the adult population globally and is characterized by irreversible loss of kidney function, accompanied by various complications such as cardiovascular disease, inflammation, and oxidative stress [1, 2]. CKD is most commonly caused by diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and glomerulonephritis, each of which can independently lead to progressive nephron loss and declining renal function, and often progresses silently until late stages [1, 3, 4]. Cardiovascular complications are particularly prominent and represent the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with CKD [3]. Accumulating evidence suggests that the progressive retention of uremic toxins, especially protein-bound uremic toxins (PBUTs), plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of these complications [2, 5]. PBUTs such as p-cresol sulfate (PCS) and indoxyl sulfate (IxS) are poorly cleared by conventional dialysis methods due to their high protein-binding capacity, and they contribute to endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and pro-inflammatory pathways [6].

PBUTs, including PCS, IxS, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), and 3-carboxy-4-methyl-5-propyl-2-furanpropionic acid (CMPF), are generated primarily by the intestinal microbiota through the metabolism of dietary amino acids such as tyrosine and tryptophan [5, 6]. These toxins are absorbed into the bloodstream and exhibit a high affinity for albumin, resulting in strong plasma protein binding that hinders their effective clearance by conventional dialysis modalities [7, 8]. Due to this characteristic, PBUTs tend to accumulate in patients with CKD, contributing to a range of systemic toxic effects. Studies have demonstrated that elevated levels of PBUTs contribute to increased cardiovascular risk through the induction of vascular calcification, endothelial dysfunction, and pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative states [6, 9]. Additionally, certain PBUTs such as IS and PCS have been implicated in the progression of renal fibrosis and neurotoxicity [10, 11]. Because of their resistance to dialysis removal and their pathogenic effects, PBUTs are increasingly recognized not only as contributors to uremic toxicity but also as potential biomarkers for disease severity and treatment response in CKD patients [2, 6, 12].

PBUTs presents significant analytical challenges due to their typically low concentrations and strong affinity for plasma proteins, especially human serum albumin. These toxins, including indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate, are small molecules that nonetheless resist removal by conventional dialysis membranes due to their high-affinity binding to plasma proteins, particularly albumin, which prevents free diffusion across standard dialysis membranes designed for the clearance of low molecular weight, hydrophilic, unbound solutes [13–16]. Due to this strong protein binding, only approximately 30% of circulating PBUTs are removed by conventional hemodialysis, while the vast majority (> 90%) remains albumin-bound and thus resistant to dialysis clearance [17, 18]. Traditional analytical techniques, such as immunoassays and gel filtration chromatography, often suffer from inadequate sensitivity and specificity, leading to underestimation of toxin concentrations and their clinical relevance [18, 19]. In contrast, liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) has emerged as a highly sensitive and specific tool for detecting PBUTs in complex biological matrices like serum, offering a low lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) and the ability to monitor multiple analytes with rapid turnaround [19, 20]. This technique enables the accurate detection of PBUTs at trace levels—even in the parts-per-trillion range—thereby providing critical insights into their physiological and pathological roles in CKD and related disorders. Consequently, LC-MS/MS significantly enhances the ability to evaluate and monitor uremic toxin burden, supporting more effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in nephrology.

Although the EUTox work group has formally classified approximately 90 uremic retention solutes, including ~ 45 small water-soluble, ~ 22 middle-molecule, and ~ 25 protein-bound compounds [21], the total number of compounds accumulating in CKD continues to grow with advances in metabolomics. Only a limited number are routinely investigated in clinical practice and analytical studies. In the present study, four protein-bound uremic toxins, PCS, IxS, IAA, and CMPF, were selected following consultation with a senior nephrologist who is also a member of the research team, based on their clinical relevance and importance in chronic kidney disease and hemodialysis patients. While additional metabolites such as hippuric acid may also be of interest, the present work was designed as an initial targeted analytical panel. Despite growing interest in PBUTs, key gaps remain in the literature. Notably, reference intervals for PBUT concentrations are lacking, limiting the clinical interpretation of laboratory findings. Many studies also overlook the distinction between total and free toxin forms, although the free fraction is thought to have greater biological activity. Additionally, data on PBUTs in the Turkish population are scarce, despite population-specific factors that may meaningfully influence toxin levels. PBUTs are generated from dietary amino acids, principally tyrosine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan, by the intestinal microbiota, and their serum concentrations are therefore shaped by both dietary composition and gut microbiome profile. The traditional Turkish diet is characterised by relatively high consumption of red meat, legumes, and fermented dairy products, providing a substantial dietary load of PBUT precursor amino acids. Furthermore, gut microbiome composition is known to vary across geographic regions and ethnic populations due to differences in dietary habits, antibiotic use patterns, and host genetic factors, all of which may affect microbial PBUT production rates. These population-level differences in diet and microbiome composition may translate into distinct PBUT concentration distributions in healthy individuals, making the establishment of Turkish population-specific reference intervals both scientifically justified and clinically necessary for accurate interpretation of laboratory results in this cohort.

Experimental

Chemical and reagents

High-purity analytical standards of p-cresol sulfate, indoxyl sulfate, indole-3-acetic acid, and CMPF, as well as internal standards dihydrochlorothiazide and indole-3-carboxylic acid, were purchased from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Formic acid and ammonium acetate, used as mobile phase modifiers and pH adjusters, were also obtained from the same supplier. LC-MS grade methanol, acetone, acetonitrile, and water were supplied by Isolab (Wertheim, Germany). All chemicals and solvents were of LC-MS or analytical grade.

Preparation of standards and quality control (QC) samples

Stock solutions of the uremic toxins IxS, CMPF, and IAA, as well as internal standards dihydrochlorothiazide (DHCT) and indole-3-carboxylic acid, were prepared in methanol at a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL and stored at − 80 °C. PCS stock solution was prepared in ultrapure water at the same concentration and storage condition. Since uremic toxins (UTs) are endogenous compounds, toxin-free (blank) serum matrix could not be directly obtained. However, such a matrix was essential for accurate calibration, particularly in samples from patients with preserved kidney function (low UT levels) and those with renal failure (high UT levels). To obtain a blank matrix, human serum was treated with activated charcoal overnight at 4 °C, as described by André et al. [22]. The serum was then centrifuged multiple times at 2200 rpm for 15 min to remove the charcoal completely. While this treatment successfully reduced IxS, pCS, and IAA to below 20% of the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ), CMPF could not be sufficiently eliminated. Therefore, a synthetic serum matrix resembling human serum was prepared based on the method of Wang et al. [23], with modification using 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). The synthetic matrix consisted of 111 mM NaCl, 2.9 mM NaHCO₃, 2.2 mM K₂HPO₄, 0.8 mM MgCl₂, 2.5 mM urea, 5 mM KCl, and 5% BSA, and was adjusted to pH 7.4. A mixed UT working solution was then prepared by spiking this synthetic matrix with the analytes to reach a final concentration of 50 µg/mL. Calibration standards and quality control (QC) samples were prepared by serial dilution of this working solution with the same synthetic serum matrix.

Isotopically labeled internal standards are considered the gold standard for quantitative LC-MS/MS analysis. However, due to limitations related to commercial availability and cost, isotopically labeled analogues for all analytes could not be employed in the present study. Therefore, dihydrochlorothiazide (DHCT) and indole-3-carboxylic acid (I3CA) were selected as structural analogue internal standards based on their physicochemical similarity to the target analytes, chemical stability, reproducible ionisation behaviour, and consistent chromatographic performance under the applied LC-MS/MS conditions. The selection was informed by comparative physicochemical profiling of all analytes and internal standards (Supplementary Table S3). DHCT shares a comparable logP (− 0.16 vs. −0.48 for IxS and − 0.55 for pCS), identical physiological charge (− 1), and similar logS values with IxS and pCS, ensuring analogous chromatographic retention and electrospray ionisation behaviour in negative ESI mode. I3CA shares an indole bicyclic scaffold, identical polar surface area (53.09 Å²), and closely matched logP (1.79 vs. 1.87 for IAA) with IAA, providing near-identical chromatographic retention characteristics, while also offering comparable physiological charge (− 1) and refractivity to CMPF. These internal standards effectively compensated for analytical variability during sample preparation and instrumental analysis, as confirmed by matrix effect values within 95–105% and stable response factors across the full calibration range for all analyte–IS pairs. DHCT was used as the internal standard for IxS and pCS, whereas I3CA was used for IAA and CMPF.

LC-MS/MS conditions

Chromatographic separation and mass spectrometric detection were performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class system coupled with a Xevo TQD triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source (Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA). The separation was achieved on an ACQUITY BEH Phenyl column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at ambient temperature. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in ultrapure water (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile (mobile phase B). The flow rate was set at 0.4 mL/min, and the gradient elution program was as follows: 0–1.0 min, 98% A; 1.0–1.5 min, linear gradient to 0% A; 1.5–3.0 min, 0% A; 3.0–3.5 min, linear return to 98% A; 3.5–4.0 min, 98% A. The injection volume was 10 µL. The mass spectrometer was operated in negative ionization mode using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). Desolvation gas (nitrogen) temperature and flow rate were set at 350 °C and 650 L/h, respectively, while the cone gas flow was 40 L/h and the capillary voltage was set to 3.5 kV. Argon (99.99%) was used as the collision gas. The optimized MS parameters for the analytes and internal standards were as follows: p-cresol sulfate (187.06 → 107.06, 36 V, 22 eV), indoxyl sulfate (212.05 → 132.10 and 79.90, 38/35 V, 20/16 eV), indole-3-acetic acid (174.09 → 130.08, 24 V, 12 eV), CMPF (239.20 → 195.16, 32 V, 12 eV), dihydrochlorothiazide (internal standard; 295.90 → 205.20 and 269.20, 43 V, 25 eV), and indole-3-carboxylic acid (internal standard; 160.06 → 116.10, 32 V, 14 eV). Data acquisition and processing were conducted using MassLynx 4.1 and TargetLynx software (Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA).

Optimization of protein precipitation agent

Since serum was selected as the target matrix for uremic toxin analysis and contains high amounts of protein, effective removal of proteins prior to analysis was necessary. In the literature, simple protein precipitation procedures using acetonitrile or methanol have commonly been applied (Al-Dajani et al. 2024). This study aimed to optimize the protein precipitation agent to achieve the highest extraction efficiency and obtain a clean matrix for four analytes. Acetone, acetonitrile (ACN), and methanol (MeOH) were tested as candidate protein precipitating solvents. A serum matrix spiked with 1 µg/mL of each uremic toxin was used in each trial.

The general sample preparation procedure was as follows: 50 µL of serum was mixed with 150 µL of cold organic solvent to precipitate proteins. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min. The supernatant was transferred to an LC-MS/MS plate and evaporated under nitrogen. The dried wells were reconstituted with 100 µL of acetonitrile/water (50:50, v/v). Extraction efficiency and matrix effect data were used for evaluation. The formulas used to calculate extraction recovery and matrix effect are given below:

Prespiked serum: blank serum pool spiked with a uremic toxin mixture to a final concentration of 1 µg/mL.

Prespiked serum: blank serum pool spiked with a uremic toxin mixture to a final concentration of 1 µg/mL.

Postspiked serum: blank serum processed according to the sample preparation procedure and then spiked with 50 µL of 1 µg/mL uremic toxin mixture in acetonitrile, 100 µL water, and 50 µL acetonitrile.

Postspiked serum: blank serum processed according to the sample preparation procedure and then spiked with 50 µL of 1 µg/mL uremic toxin mixture in acetonitrile, 100 µL water, and 50 µL acetonitrile.

Solvent matrix: 50 µL of 1 µg/mL uremic toxin mixture in acetonitrile, 100 µL water, and 50 µL acetonitrile added to a blank well (no serum).

Optimized sample preparation procedure

For total uremic toxin analysis (including both free and protein-bound forms), 50 µL of serum was mixed with 150 µL of an internal standard solution containing 1 µg/mL DHCT and indole-3-carboxylic acid in an acetonitrile/acetone (1:1, v/v) solvent system. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s and centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was evaporated under nitrogen, and the dried residues were reconstituted in 100 µL of acetonitrile/water (50:50, v/v).

For free uremic toxin analysis, ultrafiltration was applied using regenerated cellulose centrifugal filters with a 30 kDa molecular weight cutoff (0.5 mL, Amicon, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). A volume of 300 µL of serum was added to the filter and centrifuged at 13,300 g for 20 min. Since uremic toxins are primarily bound to albumin (65 kDa), they are retained, allowing only the free fraction to pass into the filtrate. The 30 kDa MWCO was specifically selected based on the molecular weight of human serum albumin (~ 66.5 kDa), ensuring complete retention of albumin and all albumin-bound PBUT fractions on the membrane while allowing only the unbound free fraction to pass into the ultrafiltrate. This approach is consistent with published LC-MS/MS methods for free PBUT quantification by ultrafiltration [15, 24].

Method validation

Method validation was performed in accordance with the guidelines of the European Medicines Agency (EMA) [25].

Selectivity

Selectivity was evaluated by comparing the peak areas of each uremic toxin and internal standards (IS) in blank serum (without IS) and in spiked serum samples at the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ). Blank serum matrix samples were analyzed six times to assess background noise in the LC-MS/MS system. Likewise, six replicates of LLOQ-level calibrators were prepared and analyzed. Samples were arranged on the plate as follows: blank–blank–blank–blank–blank–blank–LLOQ–LLOQ–LLOQ–LLOQ–LLOQ–LLOQ.

For each analyte, the average peak area at the retention time in blank serum was divided by the average peak area at LLOQ level, and the ratio was multiplied by 100 to calculate interference. This ratio was required to be ≤ 20% for analytes and ≤ 5% for internal standards.

Analytical specificity was assessed by evaluating potential interferences from endogenous matrix components. No interfering peaks were observed at the retention times of the analytes or internal standards in blank serum samples. Furthermore, the use of compound-specific MRM transitions provided high selectivity for each analyte, ensuring reliable identification and quantification in complex serum matrices.

Carryover

Carryover was evaluated by injecting a blank sample immediately after the ULOQ calibrator. Blank samples were prepared using the same matrix and sample preparation procedure as the calibrators. Peak areas observed at the retention times of PCS, IxS, IAA, and CMPF in the blank injection were compared with those obtained at the LLOQ. Carryover was considered acceptable if the response in the blank did not exceed 20% of the analyte response at the LLOQ and 5% of the internal standard (IS) response.

To further assess potential sample-to-sample contamination, three consecutive ULOQ calibrators were followed by three blank matrix injections and analyzed in replicate. No additional wash steps beyond the routine autosampler needle wash were applied. Responses in the blank injections were evaluated using the same acceptance criteria (≤ 20% of LLOQ for analytes and ≤ 5% for IS).

Linearity, LOD, and LOQ

Linearity of the developed method was initially explored over a broad concentration range (0.095–50,000 ng/mL) during preliminary experiments in order to define the full analytical working range of the analytes. Based on these exploratory data, the final calibration model for method validation was constructed using a reduced number of concentration levels in accordance with regulatory guidance.

A common calibration set consisting of 12.5, 25, 50, 100, 500, 1,000, 5,000, 10,000, 25,000, and 50,000 ng/mL was prepared from a mixed working solution. From this common set, analyte-specific calibration curves were generated according to the respective LLOQ values. For pCS, eight calibration levels (12.5–50,000 ng/mL) were used. For IxS, seven levels (50–50,000 ng/mL) were applied. For IAA and CMPF, six calibration levels (100–50,000 ng/mL) were used. Concentrations below the analyte-specific LLOQ were excluded from regression analysis.

Calibration curves were constructed using the peak area ratios of analytes to their respective internal standards and fitted using weighted linear regression. The LLOQ was defined as the lowest calibration level meeting the predefined acceptance criteria for accuracy (80–120%) and precision (CV ≤ 20%).

Analytical sensitivity was determined based on the LLOQ, the validated calibration range, and method performance at low concentration levels. The method demonstrated adequate sensitivity to quantify all analytes across the clinically relevant concentration ranges observed in both healthy individuals and hemodialysis patients.

Precision

Precision was evaluated by assessing both intra-day and inter-day variability. Intra-day precision was determined using ten replicate analyses of serum samples spiked with IS at six different concentration levels. Inter-day precision was assessed using the same procedure over a five-day period. The mean concentration, standard deviation, and coefficient of variation (CV, %) were calculated for each level.

Recovery and matrix effect

Recovery was assessed by analyzing serum samples spiked with low, medium, and high levels of uremic toxins. The calculated concentrations obtained from the calibration curves were divided by the actual values, and the resulting percentage was used to determine recovery. The acceptable range was defined as 85–115%. Matrix effect was evaluated by comparing the response of standard solutions added to post-extraction matrix with those prepared in mobile phase at the same concentrations. Calculations were performed as described in Sect. 2.4. All peak areas were normalized to IS signals. Acceptable matrix effect values were between 85% and 115% for each analyte.

Collection of clinical samples

This study included 118 patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) undergoing maintenance hemodialysis treatment and 120 healthy individuals over the age of 18 with no known chronic diseases and normal serum creatinine levels. The healthy control group consisted of volunteers who visited Ege University for routine check-ups, while the patient group comprised individuals receiving hemodialysis treatment at the Karşıyaka Dialysis Center.

Participants in the patient group had a confirmed diagnosis of ESKD and were receiving regular hemodialysis therapy. Venous blood samples from hemodialysis patients were collected prior to the dialysis session (pre-dialysis), immediately before the start of the routine treatment, to reflect baseline circulating levels of protein-bound uremic toxins. The healthy control group included individuals without any chronic diseases, such as diabetes mellitus or hypertension.

Venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected into serum separator tubes from both groups under appropriate clinical conditions. Serum samples were separated by centrifugation and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Both total and free concentrations of uremic toxins were measured in this study.

The demographic and biochemical characteristics of both study groups are summarized in Table 1. Since the primary aim of the clinical application phase was to demonstrate the analytical performance of the developed method in a clinically relevant population rather than to conduct a case-control comparison, no age- or sex-matching was applied between groups. The observed differences in age and sex distribution reflect the known epidemiological characteristics of end-stage renal disease.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and MedCalc 20.1 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium). Data distributions were evaluated using histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since the distributions of uremic toxin levels were non-Gaussian (p < 0.05), non-parametric percentile methods were used to calculate reference intervals (2.5th–97.5th percentiles), following CLSI C28-A3 guidelines. Outliers were excluded using the IQR rule.

Group comparisons between healthy controls and hemodialysis patients were conducted using Welch’s t-test due to unequal variances. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Estimation plots and 95% confidence intervals were used for visual interpretation.

To evaluate the discriminative power of total PBUT levels in distinguishing ESKD patients from healthy individuals, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. Area under the curve (AUC), optimal cut-off values (based on the Youden index), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated for each analyte. An AUC > 0.9 was considered excellent diagnostic performance. The ROC analysis results are summarized in Table 5.

Results and discussion

LC-MS/MS method development

Based on both literature data and the aromatic ring structures of the uremic toxins analyzed in this study, the Acquity UPLC BEH Phenyl column was selected for chromatographic separation. Optimization of LC conditions focused on the choice of organic solvent and the appropriate pH of the mobile phase. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2, all analytes exhibited significantly stronger peak responses under low pH conditions, approximately 20-fold higher, compared to high pH conditions (p < 0.0001). While no statistically significant differences were observed between acetonitrile and methanol for most analytes, p-cresol sulfate yielded significantly higher peak area and peak height with acetonitrile (p < 0.05). Consequently, acetonitrile was selected as the organic component of the mobile phase. Chromatographic peaks obtained at high pH were generally less symmetrical and showed lower intensity. Similar trends were observed for all other uremic toxins.

After determining that all uremic toxins elute under low pH conditions with either ACN or MeOH as the organic phase, the LC gradient conditions were optimized as shown in Sect. 2.3. As can be seen in the section, the mobile phase consists of 98% aqueous phase (0.1% formic acid in water) during the first minute. This initial step helps to flush out water-soluble salts and other matrix components that may cause ion suppression, thereby enhancing peak clarity for uremic toxins. Between 1.0 and 1.5 min, the gradient shifts from aqueous to organic (acetonitrile) phase, creating suitable elution conditions for the target analytes. From 1.5 to 3.0 min, 100% acetonitrile flows through the column, allowing for sharp and symmetrical peaks across all analytes. Representative chromatograms obtained from a hemodialysis patient serum matrix under these optimized LC conditions are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. The retention times for IAA, PCS, IxS, CMPF, indole-3-carboxylic acid, and DHCT were 1.79, 1.82, 1.78, 1.84, 1.77, and 1.73 min, respectively.

Optimization and selection of protein precipitation agent

To achieve optimal extraction efficiency for simultaneous quantification of PCS, IXS, CMPF, and IAA in serum, four different precipitation/extraction solvents were evaluated: methanol, acetone, ACN, and a 1:1 mixture of ACN and acetone. As shown in Fig. 1, extraction recoveries were compared using one-way ANOVA, which revealed statistically significant differences among the solvents for all analytes (p < 0.05).

Serum samples spiked at 1 µg/mL were used for each evaluation. The best extraction yield for PCS was obtained with acetonitrile (~ 80%), followed by the ACN/acetone mixture (~ 75%), while methanol yielded lower recovery (~ 65%). For IXS, the ACN/acetone mixture provided the highest recovery (~ 40%), and methanol and acetone gave moderate yields (~ 30–35%). CMPF was best extracted with acetone (~ 80%), followed by the ACN/acetone mixture (~ 60%). In contrast, for IAA, methanol and acetonitrile both gave satisfactory recoveries (~ 60–65%), whereas acetone performed less efficiently (~ 50%).

Overall, the ACN/acetone (1:1) mixture demonstrated the most consistent performance across all four uremic toxins, offering balanced extraction efficiency and enabling simultaneous quantification without the need for separate sample preparation protocols. Thus, this solvent system was selected for the final sample preparation method. These findings highlight the importance of solvent selection in LC-MS/MS method development, particularly when analyzing multiple analytes with different physicochemical properties.

Linearity

Calibration curves were constructed by plotting the peak area ratios (analyte/IS) of 16 calibration standards against their nominal concentrations using a 1/x weighted linear regression model. The linearity ranges were determined as follows: 50–50,000 ng/mL for IxS, 12.5–50,000 ng/mL for pCS, 100–50,000 ng/mL for CMPF, and 100–50,000 ng/mL for IAA. All analytes exhibited correlation coefficients (R²) greater than 0.99, indicating excellent linearity across the tested ranges. The back-calculated concentrations of the calibration points were within ± 15% of the nominal values.

The regression equations and parameters of the calibration curves for each analyte are presented in Table 2. The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) was defined as the lowest concentration level of the calibration curve that could be quantified with acceptable precision, in accordance with bioanalytical validation criteria. Accordingly, the lowest calibration level was selected as the quantitation limit, provided that the coefficient of variation (%CV) was ≤ 20%. Based on this criterion, the LLOQ values were determined as 50 ng/mL for IxS, 12.5 ng/mL for pCS, and 100 ng/mL for both IAA and CMPF. Concentrations below the LLOQ were not quantified.

Dilution integrity and stability

Dilution integrity was evaluated to assess whether samples containing analyte concentrations above the upper limit of quantification (ULOQ) could be accurately quantified after dilution. A serum sample spiked at 100,000 ng/mL was diluted with blank matrix at dilution factors of 1:2, 1:5, and 1:10 and analyzed in five replicates for each level. The results are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

For indoxyl sulfate (IxS), the accuracy values ranged between 97.97% and 103.22%, with CV values between 2.85% and 4.97%. Similarly, p-cresol sulfate (pCS) showed accuracy values between 99.61% and 108.26% and CV values ranging from 3.24% to 6.12%. For indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), the calculated accuracy ranged from 104.26% to 113.39%, with CV values between 5.15% and 6.06%. CMPF exhibited accuracy values between 95.82% and 104.34% and CV values between 3.83% and 7.12%.

All calculated accuracy and precision values were within the generally accepted bioanalytical validation limits (accuracy within 85–115% and CV ≤ 15%). These results demonstrate that samples containing concentrations above the calibration range can be reliably quantified after appropriate dilution without compromising analytical accuracy or precision. Therefore, the developed LC–MS/MS method exhibits acceptable dilution integrity for the determination of PBUTs in human serum.

Sample stability was evaluated under two conditions to ensure the reliability of the analytical method during routine sample handling. Freeze-thaw stability was assessed by subjecting crude human serum samples spiked at four QC levels (LQC, MLQC, MHQC, and HQC) for IxS and pCS, and three QC levels (LQC, MQC, and HQC) for IAA and CMPF, to three consecutive freeze (− 80 °C) / thaw (room temperature) cycles. Autosampler stability of processed extracts was evaluated by storing prepared samples at + 4 °C for 24 h prior to re-injection. For all analytes and QC levels, %CV values remained below 15% across all three freeze-thaw cycles, and the percentage deviation from nominal concentrations did not exceed ± 15%. Similarly, the percentage difference between T = 0 and T = 24 h measurements in the autosampler stability experiment was within the ± 15% acceptance criterion for all analytes and QC levels. These results confirm that the analytes are stable under the conditions encountered during routine sample handling and analysis. Detailed stability data are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

The reference intervals established in the present study offer several clinical utilities. First, serial PBUT monitoring against these thresholds may facilitate early detection of accumulation in pre-dialysis CKD patients. Second, the pronounced elevation of IxS and pCS in ESKD patients undergoing hemodialysis (~ 29- and ~ 23-fold above the reference range) underscores dialysis inadequacy and may inform decisions regarding modality escalation (e.g., online haemodiafiltration, haemoadsorption). Third, longitudinal PBUT monitoring relative to these intervals may serve as a sensitive complement to eGFR for tracking disease progression. Finally, given established PBUT–cardiovascular associations, these reference values may support risk stratification in CKD management.

Hemodialysis patients showed significantly higher total concentrations of all four PBUTs compared to healthy controls (p < 0.001 for all analytes). The most pronounced elevations were observed for IxS and pCS, with median concentrations approximately 29-fold and 23-fold higher in hemodialysis patients than in healthy controls, respectively. These findings are consistent with the well-established resistance of IxS and pCS to conventional dialysis clearance due to their high albumin-binding affinity, which limits their removal across standard dialysis membranes [6–8]. CMPF also showed a marked accumulation in hemodialysis patients, with a median concentration approximately 8-fold higher than in healthy controls, further supporting its role as a clinically relevant uremic retention solute.

In contrast, IAA exhibited a comparatively modest elevation in hemodialysis patients (median: 789.8 vs. 668.7 ng/mL in healthy controls), despite reaching statistical significance (p = 0.0004). This attenuated accumulation is likely related to the substantially reduced protein binding of IAA observed in the patient group (60.94 ± 19.31% vs. 94.74 ± 10.24% in healthy controls, p < 0.001), which represents the most dramatic shift in protein binding among all four analytes. The increased free fraction of IAA in uremic conditions may facilitate its partial removal during dialysis sessions or through residual renal function, thereby limiting its net accumulation relative to IxS and pCS [5, 6]. These findings underscore the importance of measuring both total and free PBUT fractions, as protein binding dynamics differ substantially across analytes and between patient populations.

Regarding protein binding in hemodialysis patients, where free fractions were directly quantifiable for all analytes, binding was largely preserved for pCS (94.28 ± 4.51%) and CMPF (96.63 ± 3.24%), while a statistically significant reduction was observed for IxS (88.34 ± 8.81%, p < 0.001) and, most notably, for IAA (60.94 ± 19.31%, p < 0.001). The reduced protein binding in uremic conditions may be attributed to competitive displacement by accumulated uremic solutes, conformational changes in albumin, or post-translational modifications under the oxidative stress conditions frequently observed in CKD patients [6–9]. In particular, IAA, IxS, and pCS have all been shown to bind to Sudlow’s Site II of human serum albumin, while CMPF binds to the distinct Site I with exceptionally high affinity [26, 27]. In the uremic milieu, the markedly elevated concentrations of IxS and pCS (approximately 29- and 23-fold accumulation in the present cohort) therefore directly compete with IAA for the same Site II binding pocket, selectively increasing the free IAA fraction and thereby attenuating its net accumulation and discriminatory power in ROC analysis (AUC = 0.633). CMPF, binding at Site I, may additionally contribute through allosteric conformational changes that indirectly reduce Site II binding capacity.

Diagnostic performance and ROC analysis

To evaluate the diagnostic utility of total PBUT concentrations in distinguishing hemodialysis patients from healthy controls, ROC curve analysis was performed for each analyte (Fig. 5; Table 5). IxS demonstrated the highest discriminatory performance, with an AUC of 0.986 (95% CI 0.973–0.996), a sensitivity of 94.1%, and a specificity of 96.7% at an optimal cut-off of 3,204.9 ng/mL. Similarly, pCS yielded an AUC of 0.980 (95% CI 0.961–0.994), with a sensitivity of 92.4% and a specificity of 99.2% at a cut-off of 13,423.5 ng/mL. CMPF also exhibited excellent discriminatory ability, with an AUC of 0.943 (95% CI 0.910–0.969), a sensitivity of 88.1%, and a specificity of 88.3% at a cut-off of 1,007.2 ng/mL. In contrast, IAA showed poor discriminatory performance, with an AUC of 0.633 (95% CI 0.560–0.704); despite a high sensitivity of 91.5%, its specificity was only 34.2% at the optimal cut-off of 380.7 ng/mL. All cut-off values were determined using the Youden index.

The excellent diagnostic accuracy of IxS, pCS, and CMPF (AUC > 0.94) is consistent with previous reports documenting markedly elevated concentrations of these toxins in end-stage renal disease, attributable to their resistance to conventional dialysis clearance due to high albumin binding [6–8]. The high specificity of pCS (99.2%) is particularly noteworthy, suggesting that elevated pCS concentrations are strongly indicative of dialysis-dependent renal failure rather than physiological variation within the healthy population. The notably lower AUC of IAA (0.633) likely reflects its distinct metabolic fate compared to other PBUTs. Unlike IxS and pCS, which accumulate to concentrations 20–30-fold higher in hemodialysis patients, IAA showed only modest elevation in the patient group (median: 789.8 vs. 668.7 ng/mL). This limited discrimination may be partly explained by the substantially reduced protein binding of IAA observed in hemodialysis patients (60.94 ± 19.31% vs. 94.74 ± 10.24% in healthy controls), suggesting that a greater free fraction facilitates partial clearance through residual or alternative elimination pathways [5, 6]. These findings indicate that while IAA contributes to uremic toxicity, its utility as a standalone discriminatory biomarker is limited and should be interpreted in conjunction with other PBUTs.

Correlation between PBUT concentrations and biochemical parameters

The correlations between total and free PBUT concentrations and biochemical parameters in hemodialysis patients are presented in Fig. 6. Among total PBUT concentrations, IxS demonstrated a significant positive correlation with creatinine (r = 0.395, p < 0.001) and albumin (r = 0.292, p = 0.007), but not with urea (p = 0.055). CMPF showed significant positive correlations with all three biochemical parameters, including the strongest association observed with albumin (r = 0.410, p < 0.001), followed by creatinine (r = 0.266, p = 0.014) and urea (r = 0.224, p = 0.039). IAA was positively correlated with urea (r = 0.325, p = 0.002) and albumin (r = 0.234, p = 0.031), whereas pCS showed a significant association only with albumin (r = 0.236, p = 0.030) and no significant correlation with urea or creatinine. Free PBUT concentrations showed markedly weaker associations with biochemical parameters overall; only free IxS demonstrated borderline significant correlations with creatinine (r = 0.232, p = 0.033) and albumin (r = 0.231, p = 0.033), while free pCS, IAA, and CMPF showed no significant associations with any of the biochemical parameters examined.

All four PBUTs exhibited significant positive inter-correlations at the total level (r = 0.247–0.448, p < 0.05 for all pairs), with the strongest association observed between pCS and CMPF (r = 0.448, p < 0.001). These inter-correlations likely reflect a shared metabolic origin, as IxS, pCS, and IAA are all derived from intestinal microbial metabolism of dietary amino acids [5, 6]. Strong correlations between total and free fractions were observed for IxS (r = 0.730, p < 0.001), pCS (r = 0.781, p < 0.001), and IAA (r = 0.511, p < 0.001), supporting the validity of total concentration measurement as a reliable surrogate for free fraction estimation in clinical practice. Notably, no significant correlation was observed between total and free CMPF concentrations (r = − 0.051, p = 0.644), This lack of correlation is likely attributable to a technical limitation rather than a biological phenomenon; a substantial proportion of free CMPF concentrations in hemodialysis patients fell below the LLOQ, which precluded reliable quantification of the free fraction and consequently undermined the statistical relationship with total concentrations. Therefore, the free CMPF data should be interpreted with caution, and the absence of correlation does not necessarily reflect true dissociation between total and free fractions in vivo. Future method iterations may improve free CMPF quantification through two complementary approaches: (1) increasing the ultrafiltration input volume from 300 µL to 600–800 µL to proportionally enrich the ultrafiltrate concentration, and (2) optimising the MRM transition for CMPF (currently 239.20 → 195.16 m/z, 32 V, 12 eV) by evaluating alternative product ions at reduced collision energy to improve signal-to-noise ratio at low concentrations. These modifications may reduce the effective LLOQ by 2–3-fold, enabling more reliable free CMPF quantification in future clinical studies.

The absence of a significant correlation between pCS and creatinine is particularly noteworthy. Unlike small water-soluble solutes such as urea and creatinine, which are primarily cleared by glomerular filtration, PBUTs are eliminated predominantly through active tubular secretion via organic anion transporters (OAT1/OAT3) in the proximal tubule. In ESKD patients undergoing hemodialysis, progressive loss of functional tubular mass, rather than reduced GFR per se, is the principal determinant of PBUT accumulation. Consequently, creatinine, as a surrogate marker of glomerular filtration, does not adequately reflect the tubular secretory capacity that governs pCS clearance. Furthermore, it should be noted that residual kidney function (RKF), including residual tubular secretory capacity, may persist in some hemodialysis patients and could contribute to inter-individual variability in PBUT levels independently of dialysis adequacy. However, formal assessment of RKF was not performed in the present cohort, which represents a limitation of the study. This finding is also consistent with the exceptionally high albumin-binding affinity of pCS, which renders it largely resistant to dialysis clearance regardless of the degree of renal impairment [8]. The weak correlation between pCS and serum albumin (r = 0.236) likely reflects albumin binding dysfunction: in CKD, progressive carbamylation, oxidative modification, and glycation of albumin alter Sudlow’s Site II independently of total albumin concentration, reducing pCS binding capacity [28, 29]. Albumin symmetry factor, a measure of structural integrity, has been shown to correlate with PBUT levels in CKD, further supporting the primacy of albumin quality over quantity in determining free PBUT fractions. Taken together, these results indicate that PBUT accumulation in hemodialysis patients is influenced by a complex interplay of residual renal function, albumin binding capacity, and analyte-specific metabolic pathways, underscoring the clinical importance of simultaneous multi-analyte PBUT monitoring.

To highlight the advantages of the developed method, it was compared with previously reported LC-MS/MS-based methods for the quantification of PBUTs such as IxS, pCS, IAA, and CMPF (Table 6). In terms of analytical performance parameters—including sample preparation, linearity, LOD/LOQ, and run time—the proposed method demonstrates several key improvements. Notably, the total chromatographic run time was under 4 min, and sample preparation was simplified to a single protein precipitation step without the need for laborious extraction procedures, unlike some methods that rely on solid-phase extraction or lengthy chromatographic gradients.

Another important strength of this study is the larger number of healthy individuals (n = 120) included in the reference interval determination. Compared to many previous studies with limited sample sizes, this provides a more robust and clinically relevant foundation for the interpretation of results in population-based studies.

In addition, the choice of internal standards in this method adds further novelty. Specifically, indole-3-carboxylic acid was employed as an internal standard for both IAA and CMPF—an approach not commonly reported in the literature. This compound demonstrated high stability, appropriate retention time, and similar ionization behavior to the target analytes, making it a practical and effective internal standard choice that enhances quantification reliability.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, isotopically labeled internal standards could not be employed for all analytes due to limited commercial availability and cost constraints. Instead, two structurally related analog internal standards were selected and validated based on their physicochemical similarity to the target analytes (Supplementary Table S3): DHCT for IxS and pCS, which has been widely adopted in the literature for this purpose [33, 39], and indole-3-carboxylic acid (I3CA) for IAA and CMPF, selected on the basis of its shared indole scaffold and comparable chromatographic retention behaviour. Although isotopically labeled internal standards represent the gold standard for LC-MS/MS quantification, the use of structurally related analogs is an accepted alternative when isotope-labeled compounds are unavailable, provided that matrix effects are adequately characterised [25, 41–43]. In the present study, matrix effect experiments demonstrated ion suppression/enhancement within ± 15% for all analytes, and response factors remained stable across the full calibration range for all analyte–IS pairs, collectively supporting the reliability of the quantitative results obtained.

Second, the reference intervals established in this study are based on a Turkish population cohort, and their generalizability to other ethnic or geographic populations requires further validation.

Third, no age- or sex-matching was applied between the healthy control and hemodialysis patient groups, as the primary aim of the clinical application phase was to demonstrate the analytical performance of the developed method in a clinically relevant population rather than to conduct a case-control comparison. The observed differences in age and sex distribution reflect the known epidemiological characteristics of ESKD. To assess whether the age distribution of the control cohort may have influenced the established reference intervals, Spearman correlation analysis revealed no significant association between age and PBUT concentrations in healthy controls (IxS: r = 0.003, p = 0.97; pCS: r = − 0.085, p = 0.36; IAA: r = − 0.014, p = 0.88; CMPF: r = 0.063, p = 0.49). Furthermore, Mann–Whitney U comparisons between younger (< 40 years, n = 53) and older (≥ 40 years, n = 67) controls, as well as Kruskal–Wallis testing across five age groups, confirmed the absence of age-related variation for all analytes (all p > 0.10 and p > 0.20, respectively), supporting the applicability of the reference intervals across the adult age range (Supplementary Note 1).

Fourth, urinalysis — including urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio — and serum albumin measurement were not performed in the healthy control group; therefore, subclinical kidney damage cannot be formally excluded in all participants. However, all volunteers were selected on the basis of normal serum creatinine levels and the absence of any known chronic disease, diabetes mellitus, or hypertension, substantially reducing the likelihood of undetected CKD in this cohort.

Fifth, formal assessment of residual kidney function (RKF), including residual GFR or urine output measurements, was not performed in the ESKD patient cohort. RKF may persist in some hemodialysis patients and could contribute to inter-individual variability in PBUT levels independently of dialysis adequacy, particularly for IAA whose free fraction dynamics may be influenced by residual tubular secretory capacity.

Sixth, dialysis vintage (duration of hemodialysis treatment) was not systematically recorded and therefore could not be incorporated as a covariate in the analyses. Dialysis vintage may influence PBUT accumulation through progressive loss of residual renal function and cumulative changes in gut microbiota composition and albumin modification. Future studies incorporating dialysis vintage as a covariate would provide valuable insight into the longitudinal dynamics of PBUT accumulation.

Seventh, free CMPF concentrations in both healthy controls and the majority of ESKD patients fell below the LLOQ of 100 ng/mL, precluding reliable quantification of the free fraction and correlation analyses. This reflects the exceptionally high albumin binding affinity of CMPF (Ka ~ 10⁷ M⁻¹). Future method iterations may address this through increasing the ultrafiltration input volume or optimising the MRM transition for improved sensitivity at low concentrations.

Finally, the cross-sectional design of the clinical comparison limits causal inference regarding the relationship between PBUT accumulation and disease progression.

Conclusion

In this study, a robust and validated LC-MS/MS method was developed for the simultaneous quantification of total and free fractions of four key protein-bound uremic toxins: pCS, IxS, IAA and CMPF. The method demonstrated high sensitivity, accuracy, and reproducibility, supported by optimized sample preparation and chromatographic conditions. For the first time in the literature, indole-3-carboxylic acid was successfully applied as an internal standard for CMPF and IAA quantification. Moreover, the study presents the largest dataset of healthy individuals to date for reference interval determination of these toxins, offering valuable benchmarks for clinical interpretation.

Comparison with previous analytical methods revealed the advantages of the developed protocol in terms of analytical performance, simplicity, and cost-effectiveness. The application of this method in a clinical cohort confirmed significantly elevated PBUT levels in CKDpatients, underscoring their role in uremic toxicity and disease progression. Strong correlations between total and free toxin levels highlight the potential utility of free fractions as clinically relevant biomarkers.

In conclusion, the presented method holds strong potential for integration into routine clinical workflows for PBUT monitoring and may guide therapeutic strategies in CKD management. Future studies are warranted to explore its applicability in broader patient populations and to evaluate its impact on clinical decision-making.
