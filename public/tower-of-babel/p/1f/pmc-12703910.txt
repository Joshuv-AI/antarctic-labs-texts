Residue depletion study and withdrawal period in milk for intramuscular gentamicin in dairy cows using LC-MS/MS
Authors: DoHui Kim, Su Min Nam, Hyoungjoon Moon, Mi-Young Park, Hyun-Ok Ku, Hee Yi, Hwan Goo Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceGentamicin is an aminoglycoside antibiotic used to treat infections in dairy cows. Because of its potential toxicity and milk residue risk, an evidence-based withdrawal period is essential to ensure consumer safety.ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and validate a liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method to quantify gentamicin residues in milk and to establish a withdrawal period for an intramuscular gentamicin product in dairy cows.MethodsNine Holstein cows received a gentamicin product (50 mg gentamicin sulfate/mL) at 0.1 mL/kg twice daily at 12 h intervals via intramuscular injection. Milk samples were collected before dosing and at 12 h intervals up to 168 h after the final administration and analysed with a validated LC-MS/MS method using heptafluorobutyric acid as an ion-pairing reagent.ResultsThe method showed recovery rates of 86%–104%, coefficients of variation of 3%–11%, and limits of detection and quantitation of 23 and 70 ng/mL, respectively. Mean gentamicin concentrations exceeded the maximum residue limit (MRL; 0.2 mg/kg) at 12 h during treatment and 12 h after the final administration, and were < MRL in all cows from 36 h after the last dose. Based on residue depletion data and a national non-statistical guideline, a withdrawal period of 40–48 h was derived, and the regulatory withdrawal period for this product was established as 3 days.Conclusions and RelevanceThe validated LC-MS/MS method enables rapid quantitation of gentamicin residues in bovine milk. A 3-day regulatory withdrawal period for this product supports milk safety, regulatory compliance, and practical use in dairy herds.Keywords: Drug residues; injections, intramuscular; aminoglycosides; liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry; drug residues

INTRODUCTION

Gentamicin is an aminoglycoside antibiotic consisting of four isomers (C1, C1a, C2, and C2a) that is used in veterinary medicine to manage infections resulting from gram-negative bacteria. Oral absorption is restricted due to its high polarity and water solubility [1]. Therefore, gentamicin is primarily administered intramuscularly or subcutaneously and is excreted unchanged via glomerular filtration [2]. However, prolonged gentamicin can cause nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity [2]. Several countries have established maximum residue limits (MRL) for gentamicin in edible tissues and milk to mitigate these risks and ensure the safety of animal-derived food products. In South Korea, the MRL for gentamicin residues in milk is set at 0.2 mg/kg, which is defined as the sum of gentamicin isomers C1, C1a, C2, and C2a. This value is consistent with the Codex Alimentarius standard, which sets an identical limit (0.2 mg/L) [3].

More precise and accurate analytical methods capable of detecting gentamicin residues in milk at extremely low concentrations (including those below the MRL) have been developed. Various techniques, including enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay [4,5] and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) [6–11], have been employed to determine the amount of gentamicin residue in milk. However, HPLC analysis presents challenges owing to the high polarity of gentamicin and the lack of chromophores, necessitating derivatization [10,11,12]. Recent studies have proposed a liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) with an ion-pairing reagent (e.g., heptafluorobutyric acid, HFBA) as an approach to overcome this challenge instead of derivatization [7,8].

The withdrawal period refers to the time required for a drug to be eliminated from the edible tissues or reduced to levels below the MRL after administration. This period varies depending on factors such as product characteristics, dosage, administration route, and animal physiology. Therefore, it must be established based on residue depletion studies conducted on specific products of the target animal species. Previous studies have reported gentamicin residue data from cattle, dairy cows, pigs, eggs, and turkeys [13,14,15,16,17,18], except Pedersoli et al. [14], who reported data from milk in 1995.

In South Korea, although withdrawal periods for gentamicin products have been established for various animal species [19], a re-evaluation of gentamicin products conducted in 2016 resulted in the removal of the withdrawal period for gentamicin from milk because of a lack of residue depletion data, and its use in dairy cows was prohibited [20]. Consequently, there are currently no products with an established withdrawal period for gentamicin from milk. However, the livestock industry continues to emphasize the necessity of gentamicin in dairy cows. To ensure the safe use of gentamicin in dairy cows, it is essential to conduct residue depletion studies following intramuscular administration in dairy cows and establish an evidence-based withdrawal period for gentamicin products.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to develop an LC-MS/MS-based quantitative method for detecting gentamicin residues in milk and investigate residue depletion in milk after intramuscular administration to dairy cows to determine an appropriate withdrawal period.

METHODS

Apparatus

A multi-tube vortexer was purchased from Daihan Scientific (Korea), and a tabletop centrifuge was obtained from Eppendorf (Germany). A liquid chromatography (LC) system, autosampler, and mass spectrometer controlled by LabSolutions software were purchased from Shimadzu (Japan). A Sunfire C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 3.5 μm) was obtained from Waters Corporation (USA).

Reagents and solutions

All the solvents were of LC grade or equivalent. Acetonitrile, trichloroacetic acid (TCA), n-hexane, and HFBA were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). HPLC-grade water was purchased from Honeywell (USA). A dispersive kit (50 mg PSA, 15 mg C18EC, and 900 mg Na2SO4) was purchased from Agilent (USA), and 0.2 μm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) syringe filters were obtained from Hyundai Micro (Korea). The mobile phase solutions were prepared as follows: mobile phase A, 20 mM HFBA in 5% acetonitrile; mobile phase B, 20 mM HFBA in 50% acetonitrile.

A gentamicin standard (gentamicin sulfate as the reference standard) was purchased from USP (USA). To prepare the stock solution (1 mg/mL), gentamicin sulfate was dissolved in water, and the concentration was adjusted according to the purity of the standard and the conversion factor for gentamicin sulfate. A working solution (10 μg/mL) was prepared by diluting the stock solution with 20 mM HFBA solution. Both stock and working solutions were stored in aliquots at −80°C.

Experimental animals

Nine Holstein dairy cows (aged 2–10 years, mean body weight: 600 kg) with no prior exposure to gentamicin or other antibiotics were selected from healthy groups. Before the study, the cows were acclimated to the experimental environment on a farm in Anseong-si, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea. The mean milk production per cow before the experiment was 11.92 ± 3.15 L per milking. Feed and drinking water were provided ad libitum, and health status, abnormal behavior, and milk production were monitored daily throughout the experimental period.

This study was conducted in compliance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines for veterinary medicinal products and Good Laboratory Practice regulations. This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Semyung University (approval No. smecae 23-03-02).

Administration and sample collection

The gentamicin test product, containing 50 mg gentamicin sulfate/mL, was provided by Daesung Microbiological Labs Co., Ltd. (Korea). Each animal was administered 0.1 mL of a gentamicin product per kg of body weight twice daily every 12 h via intramuscular injection. Blank milk samples were collected before the first administration of the test products. In Table 1, the 0 h time point represents the baseline sample collected before the first administration, ensuring that no residual gentamicin was present at the beginning of the study. Additional samples were collected immediately before the second administration, representing time points during the administration period. Following the final administration, milk was sampled at every 12-h milking for up to 168 h to evaluate drug residue depletion. Milk collected from each animal was thoroughly mixed, and three 50-mL aliquots were transferred into separate centrifuge tubes. The samples were immediately frozen at −20°C and transported on ice to the Animal Clinical Research Center at Semyung University (Korea). Upon arrival, the samples were stored at −20°C until analysis.

Sample extraction and purification

A thawed sample (1 mL) was transferred into a 15-mL centrifuge tube. Then, 0.2 mL of 20 mM HFBA solution and 0.2 mL of 30% TCA solution were added. The mixture was mixed for 5 min at 2,500 rpm using the multi-tube vortexer, and then centrifuged at 2,700 ×g for 10 min at 4°C. After transferring the supernatant to a 15 mL centrifuge tube containing 0.1 g of dispersive kit powder, 3 mL of acetonitrile-saturated n-hexane was added. The mixture was mixed for 5 min at 2,500 rpm using the multi-tube vortexer, and centrifuged at 4°C, 2,700 ×g for 5 min. After removing 2 mL of the upper layer, 2 mL acetonitrile-saturated n-hexane was added. The vortexing and centrifugation steps were repeated under the same conditions, after which the upper layer was discarded. The remaining lower layer was transferred to a new 15 mL centrifuge tube. Thereafter, 0.6 mL of acetonitrile containing 20 mM HFBA was added, mixed for 30 sec, and centrifuged at 4°C, 2,700 ×g for 5 min. The supernatant was transferred into a glass test tube and concentrated to 1 mL under nitrogen at 40°C. Following collection via syringe, the solution was filtered through a 0.2 μm PVDF filter, and transferred to an HPLC vial. A 3-μL aliquot of the sample was injected into the LC-MS/MS system and analysed.

Analytic instrument

The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min, and LC gradient program with mobile phase A and B as follows (min, B%): 0.0–0.5 min, 20%; 0.5–5.0 min, linear increase to 95%; 5.0–7.0 min, 95%; 7.0–7.1 min and 7.1–12 min, 20%. The column oven was maintained at 40°C. Mass spectrometry was used to analyze gentamicin in milk using electrospray ionization in positive ion mode. The mass spectrometry settings were as follows: the interface temperature was set at 300°C, the desolvation temperature at 526°C, and the dissolution line temperature at 250°C. The nebulizer and heating gas flow rates were 3 and 10 L/min, respectively. The pressure of collision-induced dissociation gas was maintained at 270 kPa. Validation of analytical method

The method was validated in accordance with the guidelines of the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) [21] and the Veterinary International Conference on Harmonization (VICH) GL49(R) [22]. Selectivity, accuracy, precision, linearity, limit of detection (LOD), and limit of quantitation (LOQ) were evaluated to validate the method. The selectivity was evaluated by observing the chromatogram of the blank milk sample for potential interfering peaks that could affect the quantitation of the gentamicin. Linearity was assessed by constructing a matrix-matched calibration curve using extracts obtained from the control milk after pretreatment. Standard solutions, each at a concentration 10 times higher than the target concentration, were added to the extracts at concentrations of 25, 50, 100, 200, 500, and 1,000 ng/mL. Linear regression was performed using the peak area values, and the coefficient of determination (R2) was calculated. The residuals refer to the values obtained by subtracting the response value on the instrument from the response value on the calibration curve at each concentration. Accuracy was confirmed by evaluating the recovery rate, and precision was assessed using the coefficient of variation (CV) derived from the average recovery and standard deviation over 3 days of repeated analyses. The fortification levels for gentamicin were set to 0.1, 0.2, and 0.4 μg/mL, based on the MRL (0.2 mg/kg). Samples were spiked with 100 μL of a standard solution 10 times the concentration of the target. Recovery and precision were calculated for three replicate analyses (n = 3) at each concentration, and the results were compared with the accepted ranges of the guidelines.

Calculation of gentamicin concentration in milk

The total gentamicin concentration in standard solutions and milk samples was determined by summing the response values (peak areas) of gentamicin C1, C1a, C2, and C2a. This combined response was used to construct the calibration curve and to quantify total gentamicin in the samples.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, 95% confidence interval, and 99% upper limit) were calculated using standard parametric formulas under the assumption of approximate normality of residues at each time point.

Determination of withdrawal period

The withdrawal period of the gentamicin test product was initially planned to be estimated using a statistical model (Withdrawal Time Calculation Program, WT1.4; BRD Agency for the Registration of Veterinary Medicinal Products, The Netherlands). However, because the residue depletion data did not meet the requirements for the statistical approach (see Results), the withdrawal period was ultimately established using the non-statistical method described in the Guidelines for Establishing Withdrawal Periods for Veterinary Drugs, which is part of the Guidelines for Review of Safety and Efficacy of Veterinary Products [23]. In this method, the withdrawal period is defined as the first time point at which all samples are below the MRL, with an added safety margin.

RESULTS

Validation of analytical method for gentamicin in milk

The LC-MS/MS chromatogram confirmed that no interference peaks were observed at the retention times of gentamicin isomers C1, C1a, C2, and C2a in the blank milk sample (Fig. 1). A matrix-matched calibration curve was constructed using spiked milk samples. The slope was 2,755.5, y-intercept was 721.0, and R2 was 0.9997 (Fig. 2). The LOD and LOQ were 23 and 70 ng/mL, respectively. The intra-day recovery rates ranged from 86%–104%, with CV between 3% and 11%. Time-dependent concentration of gentamicin in milk

The gentamicin residue levels in milk were analysed using a developed analytical method. The average gentamicin concentration exceeded the MRL (0.2 mg/kg) during 12 h during administration and 12 h after the final administration. The highest concentration was detected 12 h after the final administration, followed by a decrease over time. At 24 h, gentamicin levels were below the LOQ in all but three animals. At 36 h, one animal had a concentration of 104 ng/mL, one had a concentration below LOQ, and the remaining seven had concentrations below LOD (Table 1).

Withdrawal period of the test product in milk

In the gentamicin depletion phase, three time points showing a time-dependent dose-response pattern were not identified, making a statistical approach infeasible. Therefore, a non-statistical approach based on the withdrawal period establishment guideline of the APQA was applied [23]. The non-statistical approach used in this study incorporated a safety margin to account for uncertainty and determined the withdrawal period as the time when gentamicin residues in milk from all animals fell below the MRL. The first time point at which gentamicin concentrations in all samples were below the MRL (0.2 mg/kg) was 36 h after the final administration. This value was used as the baseline for the withdrawal period. To account for the potential uncertainty in the data, a 10% safety margin was applied, resulting in a calculated withdrawal period of 40 h. In accordance with APQA’s regulatory decision, the withdrawal period for milk was ultimately established as three days.

DISCUSSION

In this study, HFBA was used as an ion-pairing reagent for the analysis of gentamicin in milk to enhance the separation efficiency of the C18 column by reducing peak tailing and increasing resolution. In contrast, Arsand et al. [7] employed nonafluoropentanoic acid, which has lower hydrophilicity than HFBA, leading to longer retention times. However, in this study, HFBA facilitated greater ionization efficiency, resulting in an increase in signal intensity in the LC-MS/MS analysis. These findings support the use of HFBA as a suitable ion-pairing reagent for gentamicin analysis in milk.

Heller et al. [6] used 100 μL of 30% TCA to pretreat centrifuged milk and remove fat. TCA was used for protein precipitation in both the present study and the study by Heller et al. [6]. However, the latter method includes a centrifugation step to separate the fat layers. Arsand et al. [7] added 150 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid and 15% TCA for extraction and using C18 powder. Although both studies utilized TCA, Arsand et al. [7] employed a different extraction solution and a larger amount of C18 powder. Lim et al. [8] purified 2% TCA in 10 mM KH2PO4 buffer using the hydrophilic and lipophilic balance cartridge. Although TCA was used for protein precipitation, the buffer solution and purification methods used in this study differ significantly from those used in previous studies. Therefore, TCA-based protein precipitation was effective for gentamicin analysis.

A preliminary test was conducted to evaluate the recovery rate and to validate the precision of the method. The recovery rate was evaluated using 1,000 ng/mL gentamicin-spiked milk versus a standard solvent. Tests were conducted to evaluate the number of extraction cycles using acetonitrile-saturated n-hexane and the amount of dispersive kit powder required. For liquid-liquid extraction, two different approaches were tested: 1) 5 mL acetonitrile alone and 2) a two-step process using 3 mL acetonitrile-saturated n-hexane followed by 2 mL acetonitrile-saturated n-hexane. The latter method yielded better recovery rates. Additionally, 0.1 g of dispersive kit powder yielded a higher recovery than 0.2 g. The final protocol employed 0.2 mL of 30% TCA for protein precipitation, followed by a two-step process of liquid-liquid extraction and purification with dispersive kits powder, yielding approximately 100% recovery.

The analytical method in this study showed recovery rates (intra-day: 87%–104%, inter-day: 88%–99%) and CVs (intra-day: 3%–11%, inter-day: 7%–9%) within the acceptable ranges specified by the VICH GL49(R) and MFDS guidelines (recovery rate: 70%–110%, CV: ≤ 18% or ≤ 14%) [21,22]. In previous studies validating gentamicin analysis in milk, Lim et al. reported recovery rates and CVs ranging from 74.8%–75.3% and 3.4%–4.1%, respectively [8], and Arsand et al. [7] reported an 87% recovery rate and CVs ranging from 6.0%–9.9% (intra-day) and 4.3%–11.1% (inter-day). Compared to previous studies, this study showed higher recovery rates and lower inter-day CVs, but intra-day CVs comparable to that of Arsand et al. [7], indicating the reliability and suitability of the method for gentamicin analysis in milk.

Isomers of gentamicin have similar chemical structures and are expected to exhibit similar depletion patterns in milk, making them suitable marker residues. The Committee for Veterinary Medicinal Products (CVMP) concluded that further data on the ratio of these isomers were considered unnecessary, as gentamicin is not metabolized and is excreted primarily via the kidneys, with the primary residues consisting of the sum of four isomers, which account for over 90% of gentamicin’s antimicrobial activity [2]. Thus, in the present study, we used the sum of these isomers as a marker residue for gentamicin in milk.

Pedersoli et al. [14], administered gentamicin intramuscularly at 5 mg/kg to Holstein cows, detecting 91.62 ± 14.55 ng/mL in milk at 12 h, which decreased to < 30 ng/mL after 36 h, which was approximately twice as high as the 12-h residue amount observed in the present study, likely due to inter-individual variation, with some animals showing elevated concentrations. Lower amounts of milk residues are generally observed following intramuscular versus intramammary administration, likely due to the low lipid solubility of aminoglycosides, which limits their transfer in milk [14].

In another study, the Food and Agriculture Organization administered intramuscular gentamicin at 4 mg/kg to five dairy cows for three consecutive days, and showed that milk residue fell below the detection threshold (0.05 mg/kg) between 24- and 90-hour post-administration [24]. In comparison, we found higher residue amounts at 24 h, likely owing to the twice-daily dosing regimen, which resulted in higher systemic exposure and slower elimination compared with the once-daily regimen. Given that 80%–90% of aminoglycosides are eliminated within 24 h of intramuscular injection [2], the increased dosing frequency may have contributed to delayed depletion.

Gentamicin residues have not been reported in the edible tissues of dairy cows. However, several studies have reported the presence of gentamicin in calves and pigs. In a CVMP study, calves administered 4 mg/kg gentamicin intramuscularly for three days showed the highest concentration of gentamicin in the kidneys and liver on day 7, while the amount of muscle residue was below the LOQ (50 μg/kg) [2]. Similarly, Li et al. [15] reported that gentamicin residue in pigs was the highest in the kidneys and lowest in the muscle. Previous studies have shown that gentamicin accumulates at lower levels in milk and muscle but at higher concentrations in the kidneys. This is likely due to its renal excretion via glomerular filtration and its hydrophilic nature, which results in its accumulation in kidneys with high perfusion and water content. Therefore, the kidney is considered the primary target organ for gentamicin accumulation, while low milk residue levels support the validity of our milk residue analysis for withdrawal period evaluation.

The European Medicines Agency (EMA) outlines guidelines for establishing the withdrawal period in milk using a WT1.4 statistical model [25]. However, the lack of sufficient time points showing dose-dependent residue levels in this study poses a challenge for the application of this statistical approach. Therefore, a non-statistical approach was adopted to determine the withdrawal period.

The APQA recommends a non-statistical approach to determining the withdrawal period when statistical methods are not applicable [23]. This method defines the withdrawal period as the first time point at which residue levels in all edible tissues fall below the MRL, applying a safety margin of 10%–30%. A 10% margin was applied when all residues were below the MRL at the first time point or when all samples were below the LOQ. In this study, gentamicin residue levels in milk fell below the MRL (0.2 mg/kg) at 36 h after administration, with most samples being below the LOQ, supporting the application of a 10% safety margin. Accordingly, the present study suggested a 40-h withdrawal period using a non-statistical approach. Considering the 12-h milking interval commonly practiced on dairy farms, a 48-h withdrawal period was suggested as a more practical option.

No studies have established a withdrawal period for gentamicin from milk following intramuscular administration in dairy cows. However, the EMA has set a maximum withdrawal period of 7 days for milk, based on available data and a broad safety margin, consistent with the withdrawal period assigned to approved veterinary products in the European Union (EU) [13]. This suggests that the absence of specific gentamicin residue data in milk led to the adoption of a conservative withdrawal period. In contrast, the 48-h withdrawal period proposed in the present study is shorter but supported by actual depletion data, providing a scientifically evidence-based recommendation. However, to ensure consumer safety, the APQA established a 3-day withdrawal period for this product from milk. Therefore, although the depletion data support a withdrawal period of 40??8 h, the 3-day withdrawal period for gentamicin in milk established by APQA represents a conservative, evidence-informed regulatory decision that ensures consumer safety while being more practical than the 7-day EU standard.
