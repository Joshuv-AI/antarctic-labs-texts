Disparities in the Use of Annual Heart Health Screenings Among Latino, Black, and Asian Immigrants: Evidence from the 2011 to 2018 National Health Interview Survey
Authors: Chitchanok Benjasirisan, Sabrina Elias, Arum Lim, Samuel Byiringiro, Yuling Chen, Suratsawadee Kruahong, Ruth‐Alma Turkson‐Ocran, Cheryl R. Dennison Himmelfarb, Yvonne Commodore‐Mensah, Binu Koirala
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmigrants are disproportionately affected by cardiovascular disease burden. Heart health screenings, including blood pressure, fasting blood glucose (FBG), and blood cholesterol screenings, can help identify cardiovascular disease risk. Evidence on heart health screenings among diverse immigrant groups is still limited. This study examined the disparities in heart health screenings among the immigrant population compared with US‐born White adults.Methods and ResultsA cross‐sectional design was used to analyze data from the 2011 to 2018 National Health Interview Survey. Generalized linear models with Poisson distribution were applied to compare the prevalence of annual blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, and blood cholesterol screenings among Latino, Black, and Asian immigrants and US‐born White adults. The analysis included 145 149 adults (83.60% US‐born White adults, 9.55% Latino immigrants, 1.89% Black immigrants, and 4.96% Asian immigrants), with a mean age of 50 years and 53.62% women. Latino (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.92 [95% CI, 0.91–0.93]) and Asian (aOR, 0.93 [95% CI, 0.92–0.94]) immigrants were less likely to have blood pressure screening than US‐born White adults. Latino (aOR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.19–1.25]), Black (aOR, 1.15 [95% CI, 1.09–1.21]), and Asian (aOR, 1.12 [95% CI, 1.08–1.15]) immigrants were more likely to have fasting blood glucose screening, and Latino (aOR, 1.11 [95% CI, 1.09–1.13]), Black or (aOR, 1.12 [95% CI, 1.09–1.16]), and Asian (aOR, 1.05 [95% CI, 1.04–1.07]) immigrants were more likely to have blood cholesterol screening than US‐born White adults.ConclusionsLatino and Asian immigrants have lower odds of annual blood pressure screenings than US‐born White adults. More studies exploring facilitators and barriers to the accessibility and use of heart health screenings are needed.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a significant cause of morbidity and death in the United States for all genders and people of most racial and ethnic groups, accounting for 695 000 deaths in 2021. 1 However, not all segments of the population are equally affected by CVD and its risk factors. Immigrants, who comprise 13.6% of the US population in 2021, face significant disparities in CVD compared with US‐born people. 2 Given the variation of experiences of immigrants within racial and ethnic categories, the health trajectories of immigrants are not uniform. Instead, they are shaped by myriad factors, including cultural practices, access to health care, and social determinants of health. 3 For instance, Mexican immigrants have the highest prevalence of obesity (77.2%) and high cholesterol (36.3%), and Puerto Rican immigrants have the highest prevalence of hypertension (35.7%) and diabetes (15.9%), compared with US‐born non‐Latino White adults. 4 Black immigrants had a lower prevalence of hypertension (22% versus 32%), diabetes (7% versus 10%), and high cholesterol (4% versus 5%) compared with African American adults. 5 Conversely, Asian immigrants, such as foreign‐born Filipino immigrants, had a higher prevalence of hypertension (32.4%), diabetes (10.8%), high cholesterol (30.1%), physical inactivity (48.7%), and overweight (67.9%) compared with US‐born non‐Latino White adults. 6 Foreign‐born Indian immigrants had a quohigher prevalence of diabetes (12%), physical inactivity (42.3%), and overweight (74.7%) compared with US‐born non‐Latino White adults. 6 The varying CVD risk factors on immigrants highlight the importance of considering factors such as race and ethnicity, nativity, and acculturation in targeted public health initiatives aimed at detecting and reducing CVD.

The disparities in CVD among immigrants are driven by social determinants of health, such as poverty, food and housing insecurity, lack of educational attainment, and especially challenges with health care access. 7 , 8 Even immigration status itself can influence health insurance coverage, which contributes to health care access and quality in the United States. Depending on the type and duration of their visa, immigrants may have different options and eligibility for public or private health insurance plans. 9 According to the Migration Policy Institute, in 2021, 19% of immigrants were uninsured, compared with 7% of the US‐born population. Furthermore, immigrants were less likely to have public health insurance (32%) and private health insurance (57%) compared with the US‐born population (38% public and 69% private). 2 Moreover, some immigrants may face barriers to accessing health care, such as communication ability, cost, fear of deportation, or complex paperwork or systems required to access health care. 10 As a result, immigrants may experience barriers to accessing timely and appropriate health care, such as preventive services, screening tests, and treatment options. These barriers can lead to delayed diagnosis, undertreatment, and poor health outcomes for immigrant populations. 7

Heart health screenings are essential strategies to help identify cardiovascular health and prevent delays in treatment. According to the American Heart Association's recommendations, blood pressure (BP), fasting blood glucose (FBG), and blood cholesterol screenings are parameters of heart health screenings. 11 These screenings can help identify individuals who have or are at risk of developing CVD and provide them with appropriate interventions to prevent or treat CVD. 11 Adequate heart health screenings can also help reduce the disparities in CVD outcomes among different populations, such as racial and ethnic minorities, low‐income groups, and rural residents, who may have less access to health care services and resources. 12 Therefore, it is important to investigate the disparity and associated factors in heart health screenings among immigrants.

The previous literature has shown the disparities in using heart health screenings among immigrants. Ogungbe et al 13 found that the length of stay and percentage of life spent in the United States, which are proxies for acculturation, were positively associated with screening for BP, FBG, and blood cholesterol among Black immigrants among first‐generation Black immigrants living in the Baltimore–Washington area. A study conducted among the Latino population in Los Angeles communities, where two thirds of the participants were foreign born, found that having health insurance and a usual source of care were significant factors in timely blood cholesterol screenings. 14 However, health insurance status was not a significant predictor for timely blood pressure screening. 14 Additionally, another study using a national data set found that foreign‐born individuals from the Middle East, India, Asia, and Southeast Asia had lower percentages of BP, FBG, and blood cholesterol screenings compared with their U.S.‐born White counterparts. 15 Despite the importance of identifying optimal screening methods, frequencies, and target populations, there is still limited research assessing disparities in heart health screenings among immigrants in the United States. Therefore, this study aims to examine the disparities in heart health screenings, particularly BP, FBG, and blood cholesterol, among Latino, Black, and Asian immigrants compared with US‐born non‐Latino White adults.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

A comparative cross‐sectional study was conducted using National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) data from 2011 to 2018. The NHIS is a nationally representative survey of the civilian noninstitutionalized population of the United States, conducted annually by the National Center for Health Statistics. 16 The survey collects information on health status, health behaviors, sociodemographic characteristics, and health care access and usage of the respondents, who voluntarily and agreed to participate. 16 The NHIS 2011 to 2018 data were purposefully chosen to provide a meaningful interpretation for the analysis due to the sample size and consistency of the questions of all the interested variables for each year and to prevent the disruption of the data from the COVID‐19 pandemic in 2019, which may impact individuals' health care access and the survey method. 17 The NHIS survey design and methods are described elsewhere. 16 This study used only the deidentified data in the public domain of the National Center for Health Statistics. Therefore, an ethical review and informed consent were not required to conduct this study. The deidentified data and materials provided by NHIS can be accessed via the following link: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhis/data‐questionnaires‐documentation.htm.

Sample

The individuals were included in the study if they participated in the NHIS core questionnaire, were aged ≥18 years, and had data available in self‐reported race as Latino, Black or African American, Asian, or White, and at least 1 of the heart health screening questions, resulting in a total sample size of 145 149. The immigrant population was categorized as Latino, Black, and Asian on the basis of participants' answers to the question asking single‐ or multiple‐race groups with Hispanic origin and the question asking the geographical region of birth to determine the nativity. Non‐Latino White participants who reported their region of birth as the United States were selected to be the reference group of this study because they are often used as a comparison group in health disparities research. 4 , 18 , 19 They are also expected to have a large sample size and fewer barriers to access health care than other racial and ethnic groups. For example, they may not face language difficulties, have higher socioeconomic status, and maintain a dominant position in society that contrasts with the disadvantaged groups. 20

Variables

The outcomes of the study are the self‐reported use of annual BP, FBG, and blood cholesterol screenings. For the BP screening, the respondents were asked, “During the past 12 months, have you had your BP checked by a doctor, nurse, or other health professional?” For the FBG screening, the respondents were asked, “Have you had a fasting test for high blood sugar or diabetes during the past 12 months?” Finally, for the blood cholesterol screening, the respondents were asked, “During the past 12 months, have you had your blood cholesterol checked by a doctor, nurse, or other health professional?” These questions are the supplement questions that were asked in 2011 to 2018 and are comparable. 21 The above outcome variables were defined as binary (yes/no), indicating whether they occurred or not.

Covariates

Based on the conceptual framework of Andersen's Behavioral Model for Health Service Utilization, we selected the covariates at the individual determinant level, including predisposing factors, such as age, sex, and socioeconomic factors, and enabling factors, such as the health care access variable, to examine the use of screening among the immigrant population. 22 , 23 Covariates for predisposing factors included age in years, sex (male/female), marital status (married/not married), education (less than high school, high school graduate, some college, higher than bachelor), poverty–income ratio (PIR) (PIR <1: below the federal poverty level; PIR 1–2: between 100% and 200% of the federal poverty level; PIR >2: >200% of the federal poverty level), and employment status (employed/unemployed). The variable on employment status was defined from the question, “Which of the following was (person) doing last week?,” and those who answered “working for pay at a job or business” or “with a job or business but not at work” were considered as “employed”; otherwise, those who reported “looking for work,” “working, but not for pay, at a family‐owned job or business” or “not working at a job or business and not looking for work” were considered “unemployed.” In addition, the enabling factors, such as health care access variables, including health insurance status (insured/uninsured) and having a usual place to go when sick (yes/no), were included in the analysis. In addition, post hoc analyses were conducted to assess the influence of health‐related factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and high cholesterol on the outcomes of interest. Despite conducting these analyses, the results did not reveal any significant changes in the significance levels or the direction of the relationships observed in our main analysis. Due to the lack of substantive differences, these post hoc findings were not included in the manuscript. The study sample without missing values for covariates was analyzed by “svy, subpop” option in Stata (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Therefore, all missing values in the study sample were excluded from the analyses but used for weights.

Statistical Analysis

NHIS data from 2011 to 2018 were pooled and merged in accordance with the National Center for Health Statistics recommendations regarding survey design, sampling weights, and data analysis. Descriptive data analysis was performed to present sample characteristics and health care access characteristics, using mean and SD for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. The χ2 test was used in categorical variables, and 1‐way ANOVA was used in continuous variables to compare the sociodemographic and health care access characteristics of the participants between US‐born non‐Latino White adults, Latino immigrants, Black immigrants, and Asian immigrants.

Survey‐weighted generalized linear models with Poisson distribution were applied to identify age‐adjusted and fully adjusted prevalence and odds ratios (ORs) of the use of heart health screenings among Latino, Black, and Asian immigrants compared with non‐Latino White adults. 24 The adjusted models were structured in 2 ways: Model 1 adjusted only sociodemographic variables (age, sex, marital status, education, employment status, and poverty status), and model 2 further adjusted health care access characteristics (health insurance and having a usual place to seek care). Individuals with any missing data in variables included in logistic regression models were excluded. The association with a 2‐sided α <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Stata SE version 17 was used for statistical analyses. 25

Results

Sample Characteristics

The analysis included 145 149 adults from 4 groups: US‐born non‐Latino White adults (83.60%), Latino immigrants (9.55%), Black immigrants (1.89%), and Asian immigrants (4.96%). The mean (±SD) age of the participants was 50 (±0.12) years, and 53.44% were women. Black immigrants were the youngest group, with a mean age of 45 (±0.44) years. In contrast, US‐born non‐Latino White adults were the oldest group, with a mean age of 51 (±0.13) years. The characteristics of each group varied in terms of marital status, education level, PIR, length of stay in the United States, health insurance status, and usual place of care. Some notable findings are that Asian immigrants had the highest proportion of married (62.58%) and educated beyond high school (76.83%) and the lowest proportion of participants who lived in the United States for >10 years (70.29%), compared with other groups. Black immigrants had the highest proportion of employment (69.27%) compared with the 4 groups. Finally, Latino immigrants had the highest proportion of having less education than a high school diploma (43.40%), lower income (living below the federal poverty level [28.35%]), being uninsured (33.86%), and not having a usual place to go when sick (24.66%) compared with other groups. Regarding the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and high cholesterol, Black immigrants had the highest prevalence of hypertension (27.20%), Latino immigrants had the highest prevalence of diabetes (11.84%), and Asian immigrants had the highest prevalence of high cholesterol (26.38%) compared with other immigrant groups. Adjusted Prevalence of Use of Heart Health Screenings

After adjusting for sociodemographic variables (model 1, Table 2), Latino (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.86 [95% CI: 0.84–0.87]), Black (aOR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.95–0.99]) and Asian (aOR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.90–0.93]) immigrants still showed lower odds of having BP screening during the past 12 months than US‐born non‐Latino White adults. After adjusting for sociodemographic variables, the directions or statistical significance of the odds of getting FBG and blood cholesterol screenings from the age‐adjusted model changed among Latino immigrants (FBG screening: from AOR, 1.02 [95% CI, 0.99–1.04] to aOR, 1.11 [95% CI, 1.08–1.11]; blood cholesterol screenings: from aOR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.92–0.96] to aOR, 1.01 [95% CI, 1.00–1.03]).

With additional adjustment for the health care access variables (model 2, Table 2), the result showed that the OR of getting BP screening among Black immigrants became insignificant (aOR, 0.99 [95% CI, 0.97–1.01]), and the OR of blood cholesterol screening became significantly higher (aOR, 1.11 [95% CI, 1.09–1.13]) than US‐born non‐Latino White adults.

Discussion

This study compared heart health screening prevalence among Latino, Black, and Asian immigrants to US‐born non‐Latino White adults using NHIS from 2011 to 2018 data. We observed disparities in using heart health screenings across different immigrant populations compared with US‐born non‐Latino White adults. This study showed that the Latino, Black, and Asian immigrant subgroups had higher odds of having FBG or blood cholesterol screening compared with US‐born non‐Latino White adults after adjusting for sociodemographic and health care access variables. In contrast, the Latino and Asian immigrant groups had lower odds of using BP screening compared with US‐born non‐Latino White adults.

We found that Latino immigrants had a lower likelihood of BP screening than US‐born non‐Latino White adults and had the lowest prevalence of getting BP screening in the past 12 months compared with others. The results are also consistent with previous studies that have reported disparities in preventive health services usage among this population. Reynolds and Childers 15 , 26 found differences in heart health screening rates among immigrants from 8 world regions using the 2011–2017 NHIS data set. Specifically, those who were born in Mexico, Central America, and the Caribbean, including Puerto Rico, have a lower prevalence of getting BP screening compared with US‐born White adults. The authors suggest that factors such as language barriers, lack of health insurance, and cultural beliefs may influence the screening behaviors of Latino immigrants. The results also showed that screening rates tend to increase with a longer duration of residence in the United States, indicating a possible acculturation effect. 15 , 26 Another qualitative study reported that cultural perceptions of preventive care made Latino immigrants hesitant to seek screenings, as they often feel they are not at risk of illness. 27 These findings highlight the importance of increasing awareness about hypertension screening among Latino immigrants and addressing the structural and cultural barriers that prevent them from accessing these services.

Among Latino immigrants, we also observed that there was a greater likelihood of FBG screening in the past 12 months compared with US‐born non‐Latino White individuals after controlling for the covariates. However, previous research reported a significant portion of Latino immigrants remain underscreened and undiagnosed. 28 Referencing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2009 to 2010, ≈70% of participants screened had not been screened previously or informed of their diabetes status by health care providers. 28 A lack of health care provider visits was identified as a key obstacle to screening uptake. To address this, integrating FBG and BP screenings into community events frequented by Latino immigrants, such as church gatherings and flea markets, or offering screenings during routine or dental visits could reduce the barrier to health care access and enhance early detection of diabetes prevalence within the Latino immigrant community. 28 , 29

Similarly, the odds of getting blood cholesterol screened among Latino immigrants are also higher compared with US‐born non‐Latino White adults. One factor that could explain the results is the high prevalence of high cholesterol within this population. A study found that specific subgroups of Latino immigrants, particularly individuals from Central America, the second highest region of origin for Latino immigrants, were more likely to have high cholesterol compared with non‐Latino White adults and may currently be under monitoring and getting blood cholesterol tests. 4 In addition, the study by Rodriguez et al 30 reported that foreign‐born Latino individuals were more likely to be aware of their high cholesterol than US‐born Latino individuals. Therefore, this population might have a better advantage in getting blood cholesterol screenings, resulting from a higher awareness of their conditions and receiving treatment accordingly.

The study indicated that the likelihood of Black immigrants receiving BP screenings was not significantly different compared with White adults. This observation could stem from high awareness and intensive health community promotion campaigns that focus on encouraging regular BP monitoring and adherence to prescribed medication among individuals of African descent. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 In addition, there was a higher likelihood of getting other heart health screenings, such as FBG and blood cholesterol screening, compared with US‐born non‐Latino White individuals. Previous studies have suggested that factors such as length of stay in the United States and awareness and availability of preventive health services in their host country influence the heart health screening rate among Black immigrants. For instance, a study by Ogungbe et al 13 found that acculturation and immigration‐related factors might influence cardiovascular screening among Black immigrants. The study reported that Black immigrants who had lived in the United States for >10 years had higher odds of screening for blood cholesterol than those who had lived in the United States for <10 years; however, those who resided in the United States >10 years had lower odds of screening for BP. This acculturation may influence the likelihood and frequency of cardiovascular screening among Black immigrants in different ways. Black immigrants who recently moved to the United States may have a lower awareness of the importance and availability of preventive health services in their host country. 36 Other reasons might relate to their mistrust of the health care system or difficulty finding a primary care provider who can refer them to screening programs. 36 These factors may prevent them from seeking timely and appropriate care for their cardiovascular health. More research is needed to understand the mechanisms and pathways through which acculturation influences cardiovascular screening. This would help design and implement effective interventions and policies to promote heart health among diverse immigrant groups.

We also observed that Asian immigrants have lower odds of having BP screening during the past 12 months than US‐born non‐Latino White immigrants. Similar results were also found in the study examining preventive services usage patterns across the Asian subgroups from the 2013 to 2016 Medical Expenditure Panel Survey. 37 The results reported that Asian immigrants had lower usage rates of BP checkups than non‐Latino White immigrants after the Affordable Care Act's preventive services provisions were implemented. 37 Moreover, another study also reported that the patterns of preventive services usage differed across the Asian subgroups but were relatively lower than non‐Latino White immigrants (79.9%). 37 Among them, Asian Indian individuals had the lowest prevalence of getting BP screening (67.4%), followed by other Asian groups (69.3%), Chinese individuals (72.8%), and Filipino individuals (76.1%). 37 This phenomenon might be explained by the low awareness of hypertension among this population, as the Asian population tends to have higher rates of undetected hypertension than the general population. For example, a study using data from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey found that Asian immigrants had 41% higher odds of having undiagnosed hypertension compared with White individuals. 38 Another study based on community data found that the prevalence of hypertension measured by direct BP assessment among Chinese, Korean, and Vietnamese subgroups was 27.5%, which was higher than the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey estimate of 24.9% among Asian immigrants only on the basis of self‐reported diagnosis. 39 These findings suggest that Asian immigrants are more likely to be unaware of their hypertension and are, therefore, having a lower rate of BP screenings and a higher prevalence of undiagnosed hypertension among this population.

In contrast with BP screening, Asian immigrants are more likely to have FBG and blood cholesterol screenings. The result could be explained by a high prevalence of dyslipidemia among the Asian immigrant population in this study and also in previous studies. According to the article by Koirala et al, 18 Asian immigrant subgroups, particularly Southeast Asia, had a higher prevalence of high cholesterol and diabetes, and Indian Asian immigrants had a higher prevalence of diabetes when compared with US‐born non‐Latino White individuals. These 2 subgroups comprise 71% of the Asian immigrants in the United States. 18 The higher prevalence of diabetes and dyslipidemia among Asian immigrants might affect the blood glucose and cholesterol screening rates. However, the reasons for the higher odds of having these screenings among Asian immigrants are unclear. Further studies are needed to better understand this phenomenon.

The discrepancy found in this study yields the need for interventions to increase the screening rates among different subgroups of immigrants in the United States, especially for those who are at higher risk of underscreening. In addition, previous literature has also shown that immigrant status and sex differences may impact the use of heart health screenings due to variations in cardiovascular risk and cultural perceptions among immigrant populations and between sexes. 40 , 41 Future research could explore the interaction between immigrant status and sex on the use of heart health screenings. Such interventions may include providing culturally and linguistically appropriate health education, expanding health insurance coverage and access to primary care services, enhancing provider–patient communication and trust, and addressing the legal and social barriers that deter the immigrant population from seeking preventive screenings. These interventions may help to reduce the disparities in screening rates among the immigrant population and improve their cardiovascular health outcomes.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, using annual screening as the variable of interest might introduce biases because the recommendation for getting heart health screenings differs on the basis of guidelines and individual risk factors. Given the available database, there were no perfect variables that measured exactly on the basis of the recommended screening. For example, the American Heart Association recommends blood cholesterol screening every 4 to 6 years for adults aged ≥20 years. 42 The American Diabetes Association recommends testing for prediabetes and risk for future diabetes for all people beginning at age 45 years. If tests are normal, it is reasonable to repeat testing at a minimum of 3‐year intervals. 43 However, the study was able to capture the annual BP screening for which the American Heart Association recommends having a BP check at every health care visit or at least once per year if BP is <120/80 mm Hg, starting from age 3 years. 11 This study intends to use the existing variables to provide a picture of screening prevalence for immigrant populations. Therefore, a comprehensive study on the variables of cardiovascular screenings that adhere to the national guidelines is needed to obtain more precise and consistent data on cardiovascular screenings. Second, using immigrant status and nativity as determinants of health might prompt a broader concern, given the limitations associated with the diverse and dynamic nature of immigration patterns. Despite some studies suggesting nativity as a determinant of health, the oversimplified categorization of individuals solely on the basis of nativity overlooks critical factors such as language proficiency, cultural adaptation, and immigrant generation, all of which significantly influence socioeconomic outcomes. 3 , 44 , 45 Moreover, certain policies, such as eligibility criteria for Medicaid and the Children's Health Insurance Program, with a mandated 5‐year waiting period for many qualified noncitizens, may contribute to geographic and temporal variations, resulting in fluctuations in the socioeconomic characteristics of immigrant populations. 46 Relying on nativity alone may fail to capture the multifaceted factors shaping an individual's economic position, necessitating careful consideration of diverse elements for a more accurate assessment of immigrant status. A more nuanced approach, recognizing the intersectionality of identities and the dynamic nature of socioeconomic status, is imperative for the study of the immigrant population. Third, the NHIS data are based on self‐reported information, which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias. Fourth, the NHIS data do not include information on some potential confounders or mediators of screening behavior, such as health beliefs or attitudes, perceived barriers or facilitators of screening, or quality of health care services. Fifth, while complete case analysis can be a practical approach to handling missing data, it is crucial to acknowledge its limitations. One significant drawback of complete case analysis is its potential to introduce selection bias into the analysis, especially when the missing data are not completely random, as an outcome for FBG screening excluding those who provide direct care to the patients. 47 The representation of the remaining sample may not accurately reflect the characteristics of the entire population. Finally, the NHIS data do not capture some subgroups of immigrants who may have different screening patterns or needs, such as refugees or undocumented immigrants. Future studies that address these limitations would help evaluate the screenings' effectiveness and quality and identify the gaps and challenges in the current practice.

Conclusions

This study examined the disparities in heart health screenings among the immigrant population in the United States. The results indicated that Latino immigrants had the lowest rates of screenings for BP and blood cholesterol compared with US‐born non‐Latino White adults and other immigrant subgroups. Also, the study showed significantly lower odds of getting BP screening among Latino and Asian immigrants compared with US‐born non‐Latino White adults. These disparities remained significant even after controlling for sociodemographic and health care access factors. The study suggested that Latino and Asian immigrants may encounter barriers related to their socioeconomic status and health care access that prevent them from accessing preventive health services. The study also highlighted the need for further research to understand the underlying complex interplay of these disparities and to develop effective interventions to promote health equity among immigrant populations in the United States.
