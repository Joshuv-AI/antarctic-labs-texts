Employee perceptions of AI adoption across service domains in a Finnish public health and social care organization: a cross-sectional mixed-methods study
Authors: Jarmo Pulkkinen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmpirical evidence on how employees across different service domains within a single multi-sector public organization perceive AI adoption is limited. This study, therefore, examined differences in self-reported AI usage, perceived competence, training needs, barriers, and motivations across four service domains within a Finnish public wellbeing services organization responsible for health and social services.MethodsA cross-sectional, mixed-methods web-based survey was conducted among all employees of the organization in August–September 2025 (N = 437; response rate 10.3%; domain-level rates 6%–25%). The survey design and analytical lens drew on the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, one-way ANOVA, and Spearman correlations. Open-ended responses were analyzed through reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsSelf-reported AI usage varied across four service domains, from 78% among strategy and shared services respondents to 28% among elderly and disability services respondents (χ²(3) = 59.81, p < .001, Cramér’s V = 0.38). Perceived competence among AI users (M = 2.7/5) and motivation orientation did not differ across domains. Training need was high overall (M = 3.6/5), and the strongest bivariate association was between perceived competence and training need (ρ = −0.51). Among non-users, the highest-ranked barriers reflected facilitating conditions. Three of six qualitative themes converged on the same facilitating conditions UTAUT dimension.ConclusionsDomain-level variation in generative AI adoption was consistent with an interpretation that structural facilitating conditions may shape whether training-focused interventions can translate into AI use in multi-sector public organizations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14855-x.Keywords: Health and social care workers, Artificial intelligence, UTAUT, Mixed methods, Workforce development

Background

Public health and social care organizations face systemic challenges from aging populations, workforce shortages, and funding constraints, which have increased interest in artificial intelligence (AI) solutions to enhance service production and delivery [1, 2]. AI tools and features adopted span from conversational assistants to clinical documentation aids, and to decision-support systems [3]. In this study, AI is defined as technologies that mimic human capabilities and can function as standalone programs (e.g., Copilot, ChatGPT) or as embedded features within software, databases, or physical devices [4].

Adoption of AI in public service organizations, however, remains cautious and uneven, with many initiatives stalling at the pilot stage [5–7]. Even in Finland, where this study was conducted, recognized for advancing public-sector digitalization [8], a gap persists between national political expectations, organizational goals, and the actual use of AI in the production of health and social care services [9]. A recent systematic review pointed to the systemic and multi-layered nature of these implementation challenges from healthcare professionals’ perspectives [10]. Research has identified barriers operating at technical (infrastructure, data quality), individual (skills, self-efficacy, attitudes, motivations), organizational (change management, resource constraints, infrastructure), and environmental levels (regulatory frameworks, public accountability demands), with interplay among these levels shaping whether adoption advances or stalls [11–14].

Despite extensive AI research literature, comparative empirical studies examining how perceptions of AI adoption differ across service domains within a single organization remain limited [2, 15]. Most studies focus on a single domain, predominantly healthcare or education, without testing whether patterns vary across contexts in the same organization [16]. This gap is consequential because public health and social care organizations are internally heterogeneous: administrative units, hospital wards, home care teams, mental health clinics, and social work departments operate at different levels of digital maturity, with varying resource constraints and AI use cases [17]. Research has demonstrated that even within a single clinical domain, professional groups differ in their awareness, knowledge, and attitudes toward AI, with these differences linked to existing work roles and professional identities [18]. A German study of healthcare workers found that technology use varied by job role, with administrative staff reporting higher daily use of some digital tools, while advanced technologies such as AI remained underutilized [19]. These findings suggest that within-organization variation may be as important as between-organization differences for understanding AI adoption.

AI use cases in Finnish public health and social care span three broad categories [9]. Client and patient work involves AI-assisted interpretation of diagnostic imaging, real-time speech translation, documentation aids drafting notes from clinical visits, summarization of patient/client records, decision support for clinicians, and conversational chatbots directing citizens to appropriate services. Administrative and knowledge work includes automatic meeting notes, document drafting and condensation, translation, intelligent search across document repositories, and AI-assisted work shift planning. Data-driven management includes predictive models for service demand, cost and resource forecasting, and analysis of client feedback. Several of these applications remain at the pilot stage. Everyday AI usage among the workforce centers on general-purpose generative tools, which are the focus of the present study. Concerns recurring in Finnish research include data security and accountability, limited Finnish-language training data, the scarcity of context-appropriate tools, equity risks, fiscal constraints, and competence gaps among professionals [9, 20, 21]. These AI-specific concerns sit within a broader picture in which professionals and managers report both positive and negative effects of digitalization shaped by task characteristics and organizational context [22], expect leaders to facilitate competence development and to remain informed about digital work practices [23], and raise equity concerns regarding vulnerable groups’ access to digital services [20].

In this Finnish context, this study examines how respondents in a public wellbeing services organization perceive AI in terms of usage, competence, barriers, training needs, and motivations. We draw on the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) [24] as the framework for survey design and result interpretation. UTAUT has been widely applied in public sector technology adoption research [25, 26].

The study addresses three descriptive research questions: (1) How do self-reported AI usage, perceived competence, training needs, perceived barriers, and motivations differ across four service domains within the organization? (2) How are service domain, supervisory role, and self-assessed digital readiness associated with AI adoption? (3) How do qualitative open-ended responses contextualize and complement the quantitative patterns?

Methods

Study context and setting

This study was conducted in a mid-sized public wellbeing services county in Finland. Finnish wellbeing services counties are regional public authorities established by the 2023 national welfare reform, which transferred responsibility for organizing health, social, and rescue services from 309 municipalities to 21 new counties, the City of Helsinki, and the HUS Helsinki region University Hospital [27]. Research examining AI adoption within these reformed health and social care organizations remains limited [9]. Public health and social services are universally available to all residents and funded through taxation and moderate fees.

The target wellbeing services county we chose as research location serves approximately 150,000–250,000 residents and employs approximately 4,000 staff at the time of data collection. The organization encompasses four main service domains in wellbeing services: (1) Strategy, development, and shared services, including central administration, development units, ICT, human resources, and shared support functions; (2) Elderly and disability services, including home care, round-the-clock residential care, and disability services; (3) Health services, including outpatient care, rehabilitation, oral healthcare, and hospital services; and (4) Mental health, substance abuse, social and family services, including adult mental health and substance abuse services, child and family services, and child protection. Work across these domains involves sustained and direct contact with diverse populations and is governed by strict ethical, regulatory, and data protection requirements. Workforce shares across the four domains at the time of data collection were approximately 9% in strategy and shared services, 37% in elderly and disability services, 32% in health services, and 22% in mental health and social services, based on organizational personnel records.

Study design and survey instrument

To collect empirical data, we employed a cross-sectional mixed-methods [28] web-based survey. The survey instrument was developed from the UTAUT framework [24] and prior research on technology adoption in public sector service production organizations [29–31]. The survey included conditional routing: respondents who reported AI use were presented with items on usage details and perceived competence, while those who reported no AI use were presented with items on perceived barriers. Motivations, training needs, and the open-ended question were presented to all respondents. Barrier items were targeted at non-users because the survey was designed to support practical organizational planning, with the priority of identifying the obstacles preventing current non-users from adopting AI. The survey comprised the sections described below; the full instrument is provided in Supplementary file A.

Background variables

Respondents indicated their primary service domain (four categories), whether they held a supervisory, manager, or director position (yes/no/prefer not to say), and their self-assessed general digital readiness. Digital readiness was measured with a single item using three descriptive proficiency levels (beginner, basic, and advanced user).

AI usage

Respondents indicated whether they had used AI applications or features in their work (yes/no/cannot say). Those reporting use specified the tools used through a multiple-choice item listing. Usage frequency was rated on a five-point scale: daily (several times a day or nearly every day), weekly (several times a week but not daily), monthly (a few times a month), rarely (have tried or use occasionally), and never. Respondents also described the work tasks for which they used AI in an open-ended question.

Perceived AI competence

Respondents who reported AI use or selected cannot say, rated their perceived competence in utilizing AI in their work on a five-point scale (1 = poor, 2 = fair, 3 = moderate, 4 = good, 5 = excellent).

Perceived barriers to AI use

Respondents who reported not using AI were presented with 19 barrier items. Each item was rated on a four-point scale (0 = does not affect at all, 1 = affects slightly, 2 = affects moderately, 3 = affects very much).

Training needs

All respondents rated their perceived need for AI-related training on a five-point scale (1 = no training need at all, 2 = slight training need, 3 = moderate training need, 4 = substantial training need, 5 = very high training need).

Motivations for AI use

All respondents rated ten motivational factors on a four-point scale (0 = does not motivate at all, 1 = motivates slightly, 2 = motivates moderately, 3 = motivates very much).

Open-ended question

The last item at the end of the survey asked: “What else would you like to say regarding AI?”. This item was available to all respondents.

Application of the UTAUT framework

UTAUT was originally formulated as a predictive model in which performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions explain technology use through validated multi-item scales [24]. Empirical UTAUT applications in healthcare have typically retained this predictive logic, employing structural equation modeling or multiple regression to estimate construct paths [25, 26]. In this study, UTAUT serves an analytical rather than a predictive function. The framework organizes the survey design, item interpretation, and the structuring of findings.

Data collection

The survey was conducted over a six-week period in August and September 2025 using the Webropol online survey platform and took approximately 10 min to complete. The organizational administration distributed survey invitations via email to all employees with a work email address. Because the survey was anonymous, it was not possible to identify non-respondents; consequently, no targeted reminders could be sent. The survey received 437 responses, yielding a response rate of approximately 10.3%, consistent with earlier internal studies conducted in this organization.

Ethical considerations

The organizational administration and research committee granted approval before data collection. This study did not require ethical review by an ethics committee, as it was based on a voluntary, anonymous survey of employees’ work-related opinions and experiences, involving no patient data or medical interventions, in accordance with Finnish national research ethics guidelines [32]. Respondents were informed of the study’s purpose and data handling procedures, and their participation in the survey constituted informed consent. No personal identifiers were collected. Data are stored within the EU region, with access restricted to the research team.

During the writing process of this work, the author(s) have used Grammarly, ChatGPT, and Claude programs to translate and check spelling. After using these tools, the author(s) reviewed and edited the content as needed and take full responsibility for the article’s content.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed in IBM SPSS Statistics version 29. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were computed for all study variables. Group differences in categorical outcomes were examined with Pearson chi-square tests. Effect sizes are reported as the phi coefficient (φ) for 2 × 2 tables, Cramér’s V for larger contingency tables, and Cohen’s w for the goodness-of-fit comparison against the organizational workforce distribution. Group differences in continuous outcomes were tested with one-way ANOVA, and η² is reported as the effect size. Kruskal-Wallis tests were conducted as nonparametric robustness checks for single-item outcomes and composite indices, with η²H = (H − k + 1)/(n − k) reported as the effect size. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were corrected for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction. Bivariate associations among ordinal and dichotomous study variables were examined with Spearman rank-order correlations. Statistical significance was set at p < .05 (two-tailed).

The barrier composite was computed as the respondent-level mean of the 19 barrier items (presented to non-users), and the motivation composite was computed as the respondent-level mean of the 10 motivation items (presented to all respondents). Both composites are treated as descriptive summaries of overall barrier load and motivational orientation rather than as validated latent scales. Composite scores were calculated as row means across all available valid items, yielding n = 225 for the barrier composite and n = 427 for the motivation composite in the descriptive and group-comparison analyses. Cronbach’s alpha (listwise deletion) was 0.89 for both composites, based on n = 178 and n = 389, respectively; these values indicate internal consistency within this sample but do not establish unidimensionality. Qualitative analysis

The thematic analysis was conducted on responses to the final open-ended item (“What else would you like to say regarding AI?”), with 78 substantive answers (~ 2,400 Finnish words). Responses to the four other open-ended items (n = 154 for AI use cases; n = 9, n = 3, and n = 6 for training preferences and additional tools) provided contextual background but were not coded as thematic data. The first author conducted the analysis following the six-phase reflexive thematic analysis approach of Braun and Clarke [33]. In phase one, the full dataset was read twice to develop familiarity with the material. Phase two consisted of inductive, semantic line-by-line coding in Atlas.ti, with codes assigned close to the explicit content of the text. In phase three, candidate themes were constructed from the coded data as patterns of shared meaning organized around central organizing concepts. In phase four, the candidate themes were reviewed against both the coded extracts and the full dataset. Phase five comprised theme definition and naming, and phase six involved writing up the analysis, including the selection of illustrative quotations and their English translation from Finnish. Memos were written throughout the analysis to record interpretive choices and emerging tensions. Inter-rater reliability and saturation were not applied as quality criteria. The qualitative findings contextualize the quantitative results.

Results

Analytic samples

Conditional routing produced different analytic n’s across items. Of the full sample (N = 437), 423 respondents were available for chi-square comparisons of AI usage by service domain, supervisory role, and digital readiness after excluding 14 “cannot say” responses. Perceived AI competence and AI usage frequency were assessed only for the 201 AI users and “cannot say” respondents who received these items. The 19 barrier items were presented only to the 236 non-users (item-level n = 199–232 due to within-item missing). Training need was reported by 428 respondents. The motivation composite was available for 427 respondents, and the barrier composite for 225. For the Spearman correlation matrix in Table 5, the valid n is reported separately in each cell.

Respondent characteristics

A total of 437 respondents completed the survey, yielding an overall response rate of 10.3%. Table 1 presents respondent characteristics by service domain. The largest group worked in elderly and disability services (33%), followed by mental health and social services (26%), strategy and shared services (22%), and health services (19%). Among all respondents, 54 (12%) held supervisory, managerial, or director positions. Most respondents (62%) assessed their general digital readiness at the basic-user level. Self-assessed digital readiness differed across service domains (χ²(6, N = 437) = 24.68, p < .001, Cramér’s V = 0.17), with strategy and shared services showing the highest proportion of advanced users and elderly and disability services showing the highest proportion of beginners.

AI use was more common among supervisors than among non-supervisors (70% vs. 40%; χ²(1, N = 411) = 17.07, p < .001, φ = 0.20, small-to-medium effect). Self-assessed digital readiness was strongly associated with AI use: 12% of beginner-level respondents reported AI usage, 46% of basic-level respondents, and 68% of advanced-level respondents (χ²(2, N = 423) = 53.29, p < .001, Cramér’s V = 0.36, medium-to-large effect).

Among AI users (n = 187), usage frequency was predominantly low to moderate, with 16% reporting daily use, 40% weekly, 22% monthly, and 22% rarely.

The most commonly reported tools were Microsoft Copilot (74% of users) and ChatGPT (64%). Other tools had limited uptake among users, including embedded AI features in domain-specific software (5%), Gemini (4%), DeepL (4%), Claude.ai (2%), Perplexity AI (2%), and Midjourney (1%).

Perceived barriers to AI use

Barrier items were presented to the 236 respondents who reported not using AI, with item-level n ranging from 199 to 232. The barrier composite, computed as the mean across the 19 items at the respondent level (composite n = 225; Cronbach’s α = 0.89), did not differ significantly across service domains (Kruskal-Wallis H(3) = 6.26, p = .100, η²H = 0.015). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons did not identify significant differences between any pair of domains.

Table 3 presents the 19 barrier items ranked by mean score. The highest-ranked items concerned knowledge and organizational support, including limited knowledge of available AI tools (M = 2.4), inadequate training (M = 2.4), and unclear concrete benefits for one’s own tasks (M = 2.3). A second tier comprised organizational infrastructure items, including lack of organizational guidelines (M = 2.2), absence of suitable AI tools in the organization (M = 2.1), and insufficient peer support (M = 2.0). Attitudinal items ranked lowest, with fear of expertise devaluation (M = 0.8) and previous negative experiences (M = 0.4) as the lowest-rated items.

Perceived AI competence and training needs

Among the 201 respondents to whom the competence item was presented, mean self-assessed competence was 2.7 on a 1–5 scale, indicating moderate perceived competence. The distribution comprised 16% rating their competence as poor, 27% as fair, 36% as moderate, 17% as good, and 4% as excellent. Perceived competence did not differ across service domains (F(3, 197) = 0.97, p = .407, η² = 0.015; Kruskal-Wallis H(3) = 2.27, p = .518, η²H < 0.001). The four domain means were statistically indistinguishable, with strategy and shared services at M = 2.7 (SD = 1.02), elderly and disability services at M = 2.8 (SD = 1.22), health services at M = 2.5 (SD = 1.15), and mental health and social services at M = 2.7 (SD = 0.85).

Training need was high across the full respondent group (M = 3.6/5, n = 428). Among respondents, 24% rated their training need as very high, 31% as substantial, 31% as moderate, 11% as slight, and 4% as none. The omnibus comparison across service domains was statistically significant but small in magnitude (F(3, 424) = 2.93, p = .033, η² = 0.020; Kruskal-Wallis H(3) = 9.23, p = .026, η²H = 0.015). Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons did not reach significance for any individual domain pair. Numerically, health services (M = 3.8) and mental health and social services (M = 3.8) reported somewhat higher training needs than strategy and shared services (M = 3.5) and elderly and disability services (M = 3.4).

Motivations for AI use

The motivation composite, computed as the mean across the 10 motivation items at the respondent level (n = 427; Cronbach’s α = 0.89), was 1.9 on a 0–3 scale, indicating a moderate overall motivation orientation. The composite did not differ significantly across service domains in either the parametric (F(3, 423) = 2.32, p = .075, η² = 0.016) or the nonparametric robustness check (Kruskal-Wallis H(3) = 5.48, p = .140, η²H = 0.006). Strategy and shared services showed the numerically highest mean (M = 2.0, SD = 0.53), with elderly and disability services (M = 1.8, SD = 0.71), health services (M = 1.9, SD = 0.58), and mental health and social services (M = 1.9, SD = 0.61) closely clustered.

Table 4 presents the individual motivation factors ranked by mean score. The highest-ranked motivators were personal and pragmatic: task efficiency (M = 2.3), work quality improvement (M = 2.3), and learning excitement (M = 2.3). The lowest-ranked motivators were organizational strategic goals (M = 1.5), perceived competence gap (M = 1.5), and AI pilot participation (M = 1.5). Even the lowest-ranked factor reached around the midpoint of the scale, indicating at least moderate motivational value across all items.

Associations among key study variables

To examine bivariate relationships among the study’s core variables, Spearman rank-order correlations were computed for all pairs of variables with sufficient overlap (Table 5).

Self-assessed digital readiness showed the broadest and most consistent pattern of associations across the variable set. It correlated positively with AI use (ρ = 0.35, p < .001), with perceived AI competence among users (ρ = 0.42, p < .001), and with usage frequency (ρ = 0.26, p < .001). The same variable correlated negatively with perceived training need (ρ = −0.24, p < .001) and with the composite barrier score among non-users (ρ = −0.34, p < .001). The strongest association in the dataset was between perceived AI competence and training need (ρ = −0.51, p < .001).

Usage frequency correlated positively with perceived AI competence (ρ = 0.47, p < .001). Both AI use (ρ = 0.31, p < .001) and usage frequency (ρ = 0.31, p < .001) correlated positively with the motivation composite. Supervisory role showed modest but statistically significant associations with AI use (ρ = 0.20, p < .001) and usage frequency (ρ = 0.20, p = .006).

Qualitative findings

The reflexive thematic analysis identified six themes. Because individual responses could address more than one issue, the theme counts are not mutually exclusive. The UTAUT facilitating conditions dimension was the most prominent, with gaps in organizational guidance, infrastructure limitations, and limited access to training recurring across identified themes and service domains. Performance expectancy appeared among users and adoption-oriented non-users who grounded their enthusiasm in concrete task-level gains. Social influence was the least prominent UTAUT dimension, consistent with the quantitative finding that colleague examples and organizational mandates ranked as the weakest motivators.

Theme 1: Enthusiasm and perceived potential (UTAUT: Performance expectancy)

Of the 78 substantive responses, 30 contributed to this theme. The most prominent theme expressed a positive orientation toward AI adoption and a recognition of AI’s potential to improve work tasks. This theme was particularly common among strategy and administrative staff and among AI users, but was also articulated by non-users who anticipated benefits. Enthusiasm was most often framed in terms of concrete task-level gains, particularly time savings and reduced administrative burden:

AI does not directly replace an employee, but it replaces one who does not use it (R3, Strategy, AI user, supervisor)

One of the best colleagues for a remote worker! (R106, Elderly, AI user, supervisor)

I could get a great new colleague (R35, Elderly, uncertain)

Several respondents explicitly connected their enthusiasm to the prospect of reducing time spent on documentation and administrative tasks. One mental health services professional described a detailed use case: “I would especially like AI assistance for documenting client meetings, as documentation in various systems takes a very large amount of working time” (R89, AI user).

Theme 2: Practical irrelevance and skepticism (UTAUT: Effort expectancy; Facilitating conditions)

Eight responses articulated this theme. A smaller group of respondents expressed skepticism toward AI or perceived it as irrelevant to their daily work tasks. These responses were concentrated among frontline care workers in elderly and disability services, and were typically grounded in concrete descriptions of task characteristics and working conditions:

In a practical nurse’s fieldwork, there is no place for AI, because you cannot use applications when you have no other devices than a computer, and you cannot keep leaving fieldwork to use a computer (R208, Elderly, AI user)

It is pointless to dream about AI in care work IN THE FIELD – operations should be made more efficient by ensuring that staff numbers are sufficient for basic care. Currently they are not. AI can perhaps be used for activities such as recreational sessions, but with what time when there is no time for that now either? (R213, Elderly, non-user)

AI does not interest me at all” (R91, Elderly, non-user)

Two respondents raised environmental concerns, like: “What concerns me most are the emissions and environmental impacts of AI use, which do not motivate use in the current global situation” (R375, Mental health/social services, non-user).

Theme 3: Organizational guidance and role-specific direction (UTAUT: Facilitating conditions)

Fifteen responses contributed to this theme. Respondents across all service domains expressed a need for concrete organizational guidance regarding which AI tools to use, for which purposes, and under what conditions. This theme appeared among both users and non-users, reflecting a perceived gap between organizational AI ambitions and practical task level:

I need information about which AI application is worth using in my own work. Is it possible or even sensible to use several AI applications? (R7, Strategy, AI user)

I don’t even know what possibilities the use of AI offers in the work of a student welfare psychologist (R191, Mental health/social services, non-user)

I would like to know what AI functions I can generally utilize in my work. I have no information about these at all (R242, Health, non-user, supervisor)

Respondents in clinical services also highlighted the need for domain-specific working with sensitive client data: “A closed environment would be needed where one could securely store [data] … and from which the AI could then quickly search what the supervisory authority has instructed regarding the matter” (R13, Mental health/social services, AI user).

Theme 4: Training preferences and access to learning (UTAUT: Facilitating conditions)

Twenty-two responses addressed training, making it the second-most prevalent theme after enthusiasm. Training-related responses emphasized a desire for practical, accessible, and role-specific learning opportunities. Respondents across domains stressed the importance of hands-on formats with the possibility of asking questions, and highlighted the need for training to use the same software tools that employees have access to:

Live training or real-time web training is desirable so that you can ASK questions at the same time! (R150, Strategy, AI user)

When you choose a trainer, it is important to consider which programs we have in use. For example, during [one] course I could only watch from the sidelines because I did not have a sufficient license to participate in the exercises (R300, Strategy, AI user)

AI in nursing requires a lot of learning of new things from me, and I am skeptical about it, given that my own knowledge and skills are at an elementary level (R335, Elderly, non-user)

Some respondents also noted that current training deficits are not limited to AI but extend to basic digital tools already in use: “Orientation to the programs currently in use is deficient; these things should be sorted out first before ‘intelligence’” (R244, Mental health/social services, non-user).

Theme 5: Infrastructure limitations and tool-task mismatch (UTAUT: Facilitating conditions)

Eight responses described infrastructure-related concerns. Respondents identified mismatches between available AI tools and the practical requirements of their work. This theme was most prominent among elderly and disability services staff and clinical professionals, who described situations where AI tools either did not integrate with existing information systems or were poorly adapted to Finnish-language social and health care contexts:

Copilot is not ‘trained’ as a social work tool. It slows things down and produces errors. (…) We need AI in the systems we use. First, they should learn to correct Finnish (R238, Elderly, non-user)

On the social and health care side, we do not even have spell-checking in [software name]. If these basic things could be fixed first (…) In practice, we are still in the stone age in many respects (R38, Elderly, uncertain)

I URGENTLY WANT an AI assistant for the supportive tasks of outpatient work, such as reading large volumes of text (…) assistance in composing medical records directly from a consultation event, or structuring stream-of-consciousness writing or dictation on the fly (R207, Health, AI user)

Theme 6: Information security and verification concerns (UTAUT: Facilitating conditions; Effort expectancy)

Ten responses raised concerns about data protection or the trustworthiness of outputs. Respondents raised concerns about data protection, the trustworthiness of AI outputs, and the responsibilities that accompany AI use in sensitive public sector work. Concerns of this kind were expressed across service domains, for example:

It is really great that we are encouraged in this, and that we have gained tools to make work more efficient as resources decrease. As long as we remember that AI does not replace human encounters, and you cannot trust it blindly (R205, Strategy, AI user)

A great thing, as long as you remember your own responsibility and always verify the results (R310, Strategy, AI user)

Discussion

Variation in AI usage and connections to facilitating conditions

The nearly threefold difference in self-reported AI use between strategy and shared services respondents (78%) and elderly and disability services respondents (28%) is the most prominent quantitative finding. This pattern points to UTAUT’s facilitating conditions construct [24] in this organization: domain-specific conditions, including task characteristics, technological infrastructure, device availability, and work organization, appear to shape AI adoption within a service context. Comparable within-organization variation has been documented previously, with administrative staff reporting higher technology utilization than clinical staff [19] and professional responses to AI shaped by existing work roles and identities within individual clinical contexts [18].

Two findings narrow the range of plausible alternative explanations. Perceived competence among AI users did not differ statistically across domains, and neither did motivation orientation. The cross-domain adoption gap is therefore not readily attributable to differences in individual capability or willingness, redirecting attention to organizational and structural conditions.

The barrier ranking among non-users provides direct quantitative support for this reading. Facilitating-condition items (inadequate training, lack of organizational guidelines, insufficient suitable tools) and the effort-expectancy item on limited knowledge of available tools ranked highest, while social-influence and attitudinal items clustered at the bottom. The pattern is consistent with prior research identifying training, workflow integration, and organizational support as central conditions for healthcare professionals’ acceptance of AI [10, 34], and with leadership-focused work emphasizing organizational infrastructure and professional inexperience over attitudinal resistance [35].

The qualitative material converges on the same picture and locates concrete structural barriers in the lower-adoption domain. Skepticism toward AI (Theme 2) was concentrated among frontline workers in elderly and disability services, who grounded their evaluations in device, time, and staffing constraints. Infrastructure mismatches (Theme 5) were most prominent in the same domain, alongside clinical settings, where respondents described AI tools as ill-adapted to Finnish-language social and health information systems. Three of the six themes converge on a coherent set of needs: role-specific guidance (Theme 3), accessible hands-on training tied to tools currently licensed (Theme 4), and infrastructure integration with the clinical and social information systems already in use (Theme 5).

One qualifier sharpens the facilitating-conditions reading. The barrier composite did not differ statistically across service domains (Kruskal-Wallis H(3) = 6.26, p = .100, η²H = 0.015), even though self-reported AI use varied by a factor of three. Perceived organizational-support deficits, therefore, appear distributed broadly across the workforce rather than localized to the domains with the lowest adoption. The variation in actual usage is more plausibly explained by objective structural conditions, such as device access, task structure, and the availability of domain-suitable tools described in the qualitative material, than by differential perceptions of organizational support. This interpretation aligns with research showing that AI implementation challenges are primarily organizational, relating to internal capacity and professional practice transformation, with technological readiness as a necessary but insufficient precondition [36–38]. Cross-national studies of digital competence have similarly shown that organizational structural barriers, such as insufficient training time and limited resources, constrain adoption even in digitally advanced public sector contexts [39].

A related finding at the individual level is that high barrier load and high training need were positively associated among non-users in the exploratory correlation matrix (ρ = 0.46). Perceived organizational-support gaps and individual-level skill gaps may therefore be interrelated, consistent with the systemic nature of AI adoption in Finnish public health and social care organizations [9].

Motivations, perceived competence, and training need

Performance expectancy stands out in the motivation ranking. Task efficiency and work quality improvement were the most strongly endorsed motivators across all four service domains, while social influence items (colleague examples and organizational strategic goals) were the least endorsed. The motivation orientation composite did not differ across domains. The dominance of task-level benefits over social and strategic motivators is consistent with prior research on healthcare technology adoption [26], and qualitative studies have reached similar descriptive conclusions regarding efficiency and workload as the most cited motivators of AI acceptance [40].

The qualitative material reinforces a task-based reading of performance expectancy. Respondents who expressed enthusiasm anchored their interest in concrete tasks such as documentation, information retrieval, and scheduling. Skepticism was similarly grounded, with frontline respondents evaluating available tools against the concrete demands of their fieldwork and finding the match insufficient. This pattern parallels broader observations that public sector AI initiatives stall at the pilot stage when strategic ambition is not connected to daily work practice [5, 6].

Effort expectancy is visible in the competence and training findings. Self-assessed AI competence among users was moderate (M = 2.7/5) and did not differ significantly across service domains, suggesting that respondents who have tried AI share a comparable early-stage learning experience regardless of professional context. This pattern is consistent with research documenting recognized skill deficits across professional groups in Finnish health and social care [21]. It also aligns with recent evidence that healthcare workers’ AI readiness is positively, although weakly, associated with openness to organizational change [41]. Training demand was high overall (M = 3.6/5), with a statistically significant but small omnibus effect across domains that did not localize to any specific domain pair after Bonferroni correction.

The strongest bivariate association in the dataset, between perceived competence and training need (ρ = −0.51), captures the effort-expectancy dimension at the individual level. Respondents at lower competence levels reported substantially greater training demand. The cross-sectional design does not establish directionality, but the clustering of low competence with high demand is more consistent with deficit-driven demand than with a generalized appetite for learning. The same training need also correlated with the barrier composite among non-users, connecting these individual-level skill gaps to the organizational-support gaps discussed in the previous subsection.

Implications for practice

Performance expectancy seems to anchor respondents’ uptake in concrete task-level gains. The qualitative responses point to documentation, scheduling, information retrieval, and decision support as recurring desired use cases. Anchoring implementation strategies to these tasks may improve employees’ perceived relevance.

Facilitating conditions appear to be a potential rate-limiting factor. Adoption rates differed across the four domains, while motivation orientation and competence ratings were broadly similar, consistent with the interpretation that organizational facilitating conditions may shape whether interest translates into AI use, with individual readiness operating as a secondary factor. The data tentatively suggest FC priorities: portable device access for frontline care, integration of AI features into the clinical and social information systems already in use, and role-specific guidance that maps approved tools to common work tasks.

Effort-expectancy investments, such as training, may gain traction more readily once these conditions are in place. As the earlier correlation between training need and the barrier composite among non-users suggests, training delivered into a low-FC environment may raise demand without producing behavior change.

Limitations

The study was conducted in a single Finnish wellbeing services organization, which limits generalizability to other organizational and national contexts.

The overall response rate of 10.3% is a significant constraint. Response rates varied substantially across service domains, and the sample distribution departed significantly from the workforce distribution. The finding of higher self-reported AI use among strategy and shared services respondents is therefore vulnerable to differential nonresponse, since respondents already engaged with AI may have been more likely to participate, particularly in domains where invitations reached desk-based workers more reliably than frontline staff. The qualitative material contains both strongly positive and strongly critical perspectives, suggesting that the sample captured a range of orientations, but it cannot establish whether reported usage rates reflect actual cross-domain adoption ratios in the workforce. Given the unequal domain-level response rates and the overrepresentation of strategy and shared services respondents, these percentages should be interpreted as respondent-level differences rather than as direct estimates of workforce-level AI adoption.

All measures were self-reported, and perceived competence and usage rates may not correspond to observed behavior or skill. Barrier items were presented only to non-users by design, so the analysis cannot provide quantitative insights into the barriers current users experience in ongoing engagement with AI. The qualitative material partially compensates by indicating that users also reported infrastructure gaps and tool-task mismatches (Themes 3 and 5), but this remains a structural limitation of the survey design.

The cross-sectional design captures associations without establishing causal direction. Single-item measures of general digital readiness, perceived competence, and training need limit psychometric estimation; the digital-readiness item likely captures variance attributable to age, tenure, role type, or device access, and should not be read as a clean indicator of digital skill alone.

Conclusions

Self-reported generative AI adoption differed substantially across four service domains within a single Finnish public wellbeing services (health and social care) organization. Strategy and shared services respondents reported AI use at approximately three times the rate of elderly and disability services respondents. Across domains, perceived competence and motivation orientation were broadly similar, while training need was high overall and showed only a small omnibus domain difference that did not localize to any specific domain pair after correction.

A hypothesis worth testing in future work is that, in multi-sector public organizations of this kind, cross-domain variation in AI adoption is more attributable to differences in facilitating conditions and knowledge-related items than to differences in social-influence or attitudinal items. Three observations are consistent with this hypothesis. Self-reported adoption varied substantially across domains, while motivation orientation and perceived competence did not. Among non-users, barrier items reflecting facilitating conditions and knowledge-related effort expectancy ranked highest; social-influence and attitudinal items ranked lowest. Perceived competence and training need correlated at ρ = −0.51, and non-users with the highest barrier composite also reported the highest training demand, indicating that organizational-support deficits and individual-level skill gaps are interrelated within this sample. Establishing comparative variance claims would require multivariate testing, which was not undertaken here.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In Finland, the Medical Research Act (488/1999) mandates ethics committee review for medical research involving interventions that affect participants’ physical integrity. The present study, a voluntary and anonymous survey of employees’ work-related perceptions, does not constitute medical research under this Act. For non-medical research with human participants, the Finnish National Board on Research Integrity (TENK) has issued national guidelines specifying the research designs that require ethical review [32]. The study design did not meet any of the criteria requiring ethical review under these guidelines: participation was voluntary and based on informed consent; no personal identifiers were collected; participants were adults; and the survey did not involve physical intervention, exceptionally strong stimuli, or risk of harm exceeding that of normal daily life. Accordingly, ethics committee approval was neither required nor sought. The target organization’s senior management and research committee reviewed and approved the research plan prior to data collection. Respondents provided informed consent by accepting a privacy statement before accessing the survey. No demographic or background information was collected that would enable the identification of individual respondents.
