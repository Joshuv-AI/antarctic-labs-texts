Accelerating the establishment of a new science-policy panel to address the triple planetary crisis
Authors: Junyu Wang, Fengchang Wu, Huan Zhong, Xiaoli Zhao, Zhi Tang, Lin Niu, Xia Wang
Published: 2024

The triple planetary crisis—climate change, biodiversity loss, and pollution—threatens planetary health. In response to these challenges, the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) was established in 1988, followed by the formation of the Intergovernmental Science–Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES) in 2012. Ongoing global initiatives through IPCC and IPBES have significantly advanced scientific understanding, raised public awareness, and informed policy-making in relation to climate change and biodiversity loss. However, pollution remains a pressing concern in all three crises.

Pollution, including from chemicals and waste, poses a significant global threat, endangering human life, degrading ecosystems, and severely impacting economies. Preventing, controlling, and managing pollution is crucial for human well-being and economic prosperity; however, gaps in scientific knowledge persist. Existing multilateral agreements, such as the Basel, Rotterdam, and Stockholm Conventions and the Minamata Convention, are increasingly inadequate to address the growing complexity of chemical and waste pollution on a global scale, as their scopes and mandates are limited to specific chemicals, geographical areas, or jurisdictions [1]. The resumed fifth session of the United Nations Environment Assembly decided to establish a new science–policy panel to enhance the sound management of chemicals and waste and to prevent pollution (hereafter termed “the Panel”) [2]. The Panel will complement existing initiatives by focusing on the breadth and complexity of global chemical production, use, discharge, and disposal while offering policy-relevant scientific advice on relevant issues.

An ad hoc open-ended working group (OEWG) was convened to prepare proposals for the Panel, with the ambition of completing it by the end of 2024. However, during the third session of the OEWG—the final scheduled meeting held in Switzerland from June 17 to 21, 2024—delegates failed to reach a consensus on the “foundational document” outlining the basic structure of the Panel. This failure is expected to delay the Panel's establishment significantly. A large number of unresolved issues remain, particularly concerning the scope, functions, operating principles, conflicts of interest, and institutional arrangements of the Panel, which are to be discussed at the resumed OEWG meeting. Regarding financial arrangements, for example, a trust fund will be established, although considerable debate surrounds the financing scheme and the use of funds (e.g., whether additional contributions can be earmarked for specific activities). It has been proposed that the trust fund be established concerning the IPBES, receiving voluntary contributions from all sources, allocated and used openly and transparently, and governed by financial rules and procedures adopted by the governing body. Such funding cannot be earmarked for specific activities. Exceptionally, subject to approval by the governing body, additional contributions may be accepted outside the trust fund for specific activities of the Panel's work program.

As repeatedly emphasised by the United Nations Environment Programme Executive Director Inger Andersen, delegates have existing templates, such as IPCC and IPBES, to design this new panel, and discussions should focus on essential matters. Greater attention to the formation of expert working groups on related subjects is recommended to facilitate the clarification of the Panel's deliverables, the identification of knowledge gaps in pertinent areas, and the development of policy-relevant scientific evidence in the future. Currently, the IPCC operates three working groups: Working Group I on the physical science basis of climate change; Working Group II on impacts, adaptation, and vulnerability to climate change; and Working Group III on mitigation of climate change, in addition to a Task Force on National Greenhouse Gas Inventories. Similarly, it is envisioned that the Panel should establish three or four working groups to examine the natural science basis of chemicals and wastes; potential risks to the environment, ecosystems, and humans; pollution prevention and mitigation; and the avoidance of regrettable substitution. Additionally, one or two task forces could be dedicated to global inventories of toxic and hazardous chemicals and wastes.

Moreover, emphasis is placed on the appropriate involvement of global experts. Different countries and regions approach the management of hazardous chemicals and waste through diverse frameworks. An inclusive and interdisciplinary approach is essential to integrate a wide range of disciplines and knowledge systems, including natural and biomedical sciences (e.g., chemistry, exposure sciences, (eco)toxicology, ecology, epidemiology, and risk assessment), social sciences (e.g., economics, political sciences, communication sciences, and law), humanities, traditional and local indigenous knowledge, and other knowledge sources [1]. In addition to ensuring contributions from experts with a broad range of expertise, geographical, regional, and gender balance should be ensured while promoting the effective contribution and participation of experts from developing countries and increasing the involvement of more early-stage researchers [3].

From negotiation to panel formation, numerous challenges must be addressed to ensure the effective functioning of the Panel, including intense work pressure, the complexity of interdisciplinary and international cooperation, and difficulties in negotiation and translating the results of scientific reports and assessments into practical action. The Panel shall pursue formal strategic partnerships with United Nations entities, multilateral agreements, and other relevant stakeholders to avoid overlap and duplication of work while promoting coordination and cooperation. The inputs and suggestions from relevant stakeholders—such as other intergovernmental organizations, international and regional scientific organizations, environmental trust funds, nongovernmental organizations, indigenous peoples and local communities, and the private sector—should be encouraged and appropriately considered. The Panel will strengthen the science–policy interface for global chemicals and waste management, assess the latest science to reach an authoritative consensus and provide scientific evidence for decision-makers to develop rational and effective policies and measures. The Panel will support the Basel, Rotterdam, andStockholm Conventions, the new Global Framework on Chemicals, and the global plastic treaty [4]. Once established, the Panel can collaborate with existing science–policy bodies to engage in broader discussions across all sustainability issues [5], ensuring that each element of the triple planetary crisis has a dedicated science–policy body. The future of humanity and the planet depends on immediate action. We call for heightened attention and coordination across all sectors to bring the Panel to life promptly to advance solutions to planetary crises.
