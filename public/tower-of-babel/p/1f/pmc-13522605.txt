Molecular and biological characterization of a newly discovered Kayfunavirus bacteriophage targeting multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli from swine feces
Authors: Phitchayapak Wintachai, Renuka Thonguppatham, Duncan R Smith, Supayang Piyawan Voravuthikunchai, Sutthirat Sitthisak, Ratchadaporn Boripun, Martha R J Clokie, Komwit Surachat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultidrug-resistant (MDR) Escherichia coli infections and their biofilms in swine and swine farms are a major concern due to their impact on animal health, economic losses, and the spread of antibiotic resistance. There is also increasing concern about the potential transmission of antibiotic resistance from animals to humans. To address these issues, alternative strategies to control E. coli infections are being explored, with bacteriophage (phage) therapy emerging as a promising approach. In this study, Escherichia phage vECPPW10 was isolated and characterized. Genomic analysis revealed a 40,621-base-pair linear double-stranded DNA genome encoding 48 coding sequences organized into four functional modules: phage morphology, DNA replication, DNA packaging, and host cell lysis. No temperate lifestyle-related genes, bacterial, or antimicrobial resistance genes were detected, indicating the genomic safety of the phage. Phylogenetic and PhageCloud analyses classified vECPPW10 as a newly discovered species within the genus Kayfunavirus and the subfamily Studiervirinae. Biologically, the phage demonstrated a moderate adsorption rate, short latent period, moderate burst size, and strong lytic efficiency. At a low MOI, the phage completely suppressed MDR E. coli growth within 1 h. Scanning electron microscopy revealed phage-induced structural damage to bacterial cells, leading to cell death. The presence of halos around plaques suggested depolymerase activity and corresponding antibiofilm potential. Consistent with these observations, phage vECPPW10 inhibited biofilm formation and eradicated preformed biofilms. Overall, these findings indicated that phage vECPPW10 is a strong candidate for controlling MDR E. coli infections and biofilms in swine and swine farms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-54099-7.Keywords: Kayfunavirus, Escherichia coli, Bacteriophage, Biofilms, Phage therapySubject terms: Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Escherichia coli, a Gram-negative bacterium belonging to the family Enterobacteriaceae, is a major cause of intestinal to extraintestinal infections in humans, and animals worldwide1. In swine, enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC) and Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC) are the main pathotypes responsible for diarrhea in neonatal and post-weaned piglets, leading to high morbidity and mortality rates2. The acquisition of virulence determinants through horizontal gene transfer enables certain commensal E. coli strains to evolve into pathogenic and multidrug-resistant (MDR) variants3,4. These infections result in significant economic losses for breeders, the swine industry, and national economies. When outbreaks occur on farms, antibiotics are commonly used in swine production systems. However, the increasing emergence of MDR E. coli in farm environments and their environmental dissemination pose significant risks to animal health, food safety, and public health due to potential transmission from livestock to humans and surrounding ecosystems.

Biofilm formation by E. coli further complicates control strategies. Biofilms enhance bacterial persistence, increase antimicrobial resistance, and promote horizontal gene transfer, thereby accelerating the spread of antibiotic resistance5,6. The extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) matrix of biofilms protects embedded bacteria, making eradication difficult. In farm environments, biofilms can develop on abiotic surfaces, such as equipment, feeding infrastructure, and water systems, as well as on biotic surfaces, such as animal skin7. These factors contribute to persistent biofilm contamination. Therefore, strategies targeting both planktonic MDR E. coli and biofilm-associated populations are urgently needed.

Various alternative strategies have been explored for controlling E. coli infections and biofilms, including natural compounds8, nanoparticles9, antibodies10, and probiotics11. Among these, bacteriophages (phages), viruses that specifically infect and lyse bacteria, have emerged as promising alternatives to antibiotics due to their host specificity and bactericidal activity. Several phages targeting swine-associated E. coli have been characterized and shown antibacterial activity against E. coli isolated from swine and farm environments. For example, phages vB_Eco-RPNE4i3, vB_Eco-RPNE6i4, vB_Eco-RPNE7i1, and vB_Eco-RPNE8i3 effectively controlled E. coli causing enteric colibacillosis in swine12. A phage cocktail comprising vB_EcoM-RPN170, vB_EcoM-RPN171, vB_EcoM-RPN187, vB_EcoM-RPN226, vB_EcoM-RPN242, and vB_EcoP-RPN243 reduced fecal E. coli levels following oral administration in swine13. Phage vB_EcoM_FJ1 reduced ETEC infection in the neonatal pig cell line14, while phage vB_EcoS_Psq-1 inhibited MDR E. coli derived from pigs15. Similarly, phages vB_EcoP_ETEP21B and vB_EcoS_ETEP102 lysed hemolytic E. coli strains isolated from pigs16. Interestingly, Phageguard ETM, which is used to control STEC serotypes on livestock hides prior to harvest, had been approved by the USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service for application on cattle, buffalo, bison, wisent, and goats. Despite these, commercial phage products targeting E. coli in swine and other farm environments remain limited in Thailand. This highlights the importance of discovering and developing phages for practical application in Thailand.

In this study, we isolated and characterized a newly discovered Escherichia phage, vECPPW10, targeting MDR E. coli isolated from swine feces. Comprehensive molecular and biological analyses were performed, including whole-genome sequencing, phylogenetic classification, determination of virion morphology and host range, adsorption kinetics, one-step growth curve analysis, lytic activity assessment, ultrastructural changes in bacteria following phage infection, environmental stability evaluation, and antibiofilm efficacy on abiotic surfaces.

Results

Isolation and purification of phage

A phage targeting E. coli was isolated using the MDR E. coli isolate PPW027 as the host and was purified through three successive rounds. After incubation, small, clear lysis plaques measuring 0.2–0.3 mm in diameter were observed on an E. coli lawn (Fig. 1(a)). The morphology of the phage, as observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), is shown in Fig. 1(b). The phage exhibited a head and a very short tail, with a capsid width of 74.07 ± 4.06 nm, and a total particle length of 82.59 ± 4.4 nm. The phage was designated Escherichia phage vB_EcoP_PPW10 (phage vECPPW10).

Plaque and morphological characteristics of Escherichia phage vECPPW10; (a) representative plaque morphology of Escherichia phage vECPPW10 on a double-layer agar plate overlaid with MDR E. coli PPW027; (b) transmission electron microscopy image of the phage at 50,000× magnification. The scale bar represents 100 nm, and the write arrow indicates the short tail of the phage.

Genome characterization of the phage

The genome of Escherichia phage vECPPW10 was sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq platform, followed by de novo assembly and annotation. The phage genome is linear, consisting of 40,621 base pairs with a GC content of 49.4%. The results from PhageTerm analysis, a program for the prediction of phage DNA termini and genome packaging, showed that the phage vECPPW10 genome contains fixed termini with short direct terminal repeats (180 bp), indicating a direct terminal repeat-type packaging mechanism. This finding was further supported by Li’s method, which identified obvious termini at both genome ends, consistent with a T7-like packaging strategy. Annotation and linear genome mapping were conducted using the PHASTEST web server (Fig. 2). A total of 48 coding sequences (CDSs) were identified, encoding proteins with various predicted functions. No tmRNA, rRNA, ncRNA, CRISPR/Cas elements, temperate lifestyle genes, bacterial genes, or antimicrobial resistance genes were detected in the genome. Functional analysis grouped the proteins encoded by the 48 CDSs into 4 main modules: phage morphology, DNA replication, DNA packaging, and host cell lysis (Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, each CDS was analyzed with BLASTp to determine the corresponding protein products. Phyre2.2 analysis predicted the presence of a depolymerase domain in CDS5 of the phage vECPPW10 genome with 99.2% confidence. Moreover, InterPro analysis revealed a pectin lyase-like domain in CDS5, which encodes a tail-associated protein. Host prediction using the PhageScope tool indicated that phage vECPPW10 specifically targets Escherichia, within the family Enterobacteriaceae. PhageScope classified phage vECPPW10 as a virulent phage.

Genome map of Escherichia phage vECPPW10. Genes are color-coded according to their predicted functional categories, as indicated in the legend on the right. The genome map was constructed using the PHASTEST web server.

Phylogenetic and comparative genomic analysis of phage

To analyze the genetic relationship of Escherichia phage vECPPW10 with other phages, a nucleotide-level comparison was performed using BLASTn. The analysis revealed that Escherichia phage vECPPW10 shares similarity with several phages classified within the genus Kayfunavirus. The twenty most closely related phages were retrieved from the NCBI database, and used to construct a comparative phylogenetic tree using the VICTOR web server. Pseudomonas phage Epa40 (NC_069747.1) was included as an outgroup. The results indicated that phage vECPPW10 is most closely related to phage CHX131 (PV611674.1), a member of the genus Kayfunavirus (Fig. 3(a)).

The whole-genome sequence of phage vECPPW10 was used as a query in a BLASTn search, and genomes of the closely related phages were retrieved for tree construction using VICTOR. Pseudomonas phage Epa40 was used as an outgroup. (b) Genome alignment of phage vECPPW10 and Escherichia phage CHX131 generated using DigAlign.

To further investigate genomic relatedness, the genome sequences of phage vECPPW10 and phage CHX131 were compared (Fig. 3(b)). Several CDSs in both phages shared more than 80% sequence similarity, whereas others, including those encoding endonuclease, ligase, and hypothetical protein, showed less than 80% similarity. Additionally, an unlinked CDS encoding the tail protein was observed in phage vECPPW10 compared with phage CHX131.

The PhageClouds database was used to assess the newly discovered phage vECPPW10 (Fig. 4(a)). The results suggest that, while phage vECPPW10 is related to other phages in the PhageCloud database, its genome is distinct from those of previously characterized phages. Therefore, phage vECPPW10 is proposed to represent a newly discovered species within the genus Kayfunavirus.

Evolutionary relationship of the major capsid and terminase large subunit proteins

The evolutionary relationships of the major capsid and terminase large subunit proteins of phage vECPPW10 were analyzed using BLASTp, followed by phylogenetic tree construction with the NGPhylogeny web server using sequences from other phages in the NCBI database. The sequences were compared with proteins from phages within the class Caudoviricetes. The major capsid protein of phage vECPPW10 showed close similarity to capsid-related proteins from phages in the genus Kayfunavirus within the subfamily Studiervirinae, family Autotranscriptaviridae, order Autographivirales, and class Caudoviricetes. Among the identified homologs, the minor capsid protein of Klebsiella phage Pkp-1 (XRQ67116.1) showed the closest evolutionary relationship to phage vECPPW10, followed by the major capsid protein of Escherichia phage Baskent phicoli1 (XCD08717.1) (Fig. 4(b)). The results suggested that phage vECPPW10 is a newly discovered phage belonging to the genus Kayfunavirus. For the terminase large subunit protein analysis, phylogenetic analysis showed that the protein clustered with phages in the genus Kayfunavirus within the subfamily Studiervirinae, family Autotranscriptaviridae, order Autographivirales, and class Caudoviricetes. The closest match was identified as the terminase large subunit of Escherichia phage HC13 (URY99533.1) (Fig. 4(c)).

Host range determination and efficacy of plating (EOP)

The host range of phage vECPPW10 was tested against 20 MDR E. coli isolates using a spot test assay, with the results shown in Supplementary Table S2. Phage vECPPW10 showed lytic activity against 6 MDR E. coli isolates, including its host, MDR E. coli PPW027. Isolates that were susceptible to phage vECPPW10 were further assessed by an EOP assay. The phage exhibited high and moderate activity against 4 and 2 isolates, respectively.

Adsorption of phage

The adsorption efficiency of Escherichia phage vECPPW10 to its host bacterium was assessed. The results (Fig. 5(a)) showed that the adsorption rate steadily increased with increasing incubation time. After 20 min, more than 80% of the phage particles had adsorbed to the bacterial cells, and by 25 min, adsorption exceeded 95%. The adsorption rate constant (k) was calculated using a standard method and found to be 9.23 × 10− 10 mL/min.

One-step growth curve of the phage

The one-step growth analysis of Escherichia phage vECPPW10 against MDR E. coli PPW027 revealed a latent period of approximately 20 min (Fig. 5(b)). Phage replication reached a plateau at 100 min post-incubation. The average burst size was calculated to be 30 ± 6 progeny virions per infected bacterial cell.

Assessment of bacterial lytic activity of the phage

The ability of Escherichia phage vECPPW10 to inhibit E. coli growth was assessed by infecting MDR E. coli PPW027 with the phage at various multiplicities of infection (MOIs), ranging from 0.01 to 1. As shown in Fig. 5(c), phage vECPPW10 at MOIs from 0.01 to 1 significantly inhibited bacterial growth starting at 1 h post-infection, with a sustained reduction over time. At 6 h post-infection, an increase in bacterial growth was observed under treatment with MOIs of 0.01 and 0.1. The induction of bacterial regrowth was also observed under treatment conditions with MOIs of 1 at the 7-hour mark. By 24 h post-incubation, the optical density of untreated E. coli cultures was significantly higher than that of cultures treated with phage at all MOIs. Additionally, bacterial cell viability at 24 h post-incubation revealed that phage vECPPW10 reduced viable cell counts by approximately 1.11 to 2.07 log units, depending on the MOI (Fig. 5(d)). A higher MOI resulted in a greater reduction in viable cell counts.

Effect of phage on the cellular ultrastructure of E. coli

MDR E. coli cells were incubated with Escherichia phage vECPPW10 and examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM). SEM images of the untreated control group showed intact bacterial cells with smooth, undamaged cell membranes (Fig. 6(a)). In contrast, E. coli cells treated with phage vECPPW10 exhibited extensive cell damage and complete cell lysis (Fig. 6(b)), indicating a strong lytic effect of the phage on bacterial cellular integrity.

Cellular structural changes of E. coli following treatment with Escherichia phage vECPPW10. (a) E. coli cells without phage treatment; (b) E. coli cells treated with Escherichia phage vECPPW10 for 2 h. Images were acquired at 15,000× magnification; scale bar = 1 μm.

Sensitivity of the phage to physical and chemical factors

The stability of Escherichia phage vECPPW10 under various environmental conditions was evaluated. After 2 h of exposure to temperatures ranging from − 20 °C to 50 °C, the phage titer remained stable at approximately 108 PFU/mL (Fig. 7(a)). However, a significant reduction in titer was observed at 60 °C, and no viable phages were detected after incubation at 70 °C and 80 °C. In terms of pH sensitivity, phage vECPPW10 remained stable after 2 h of exposure to pH values between 5 and 9 (Fig. 7(b)). At pH 2, 3, 4, 10, 11, and 12, the phage titer significantly decreased to between 9×102 and 6.83 ×107 PFU/mL. No viable phages were detected following exposure to extreme pH levels of 1, 13, and 14.

Error bars represent the standard error of the mean (SEM), and asterisks indicate statistically significant differences (* p ≤ 0.05).

Antibiofilm activity of phage vECPPW10

The antibiofilm efficacy of phage vECPPW10 was assessed by measuring biofilm biomass and enumerating bacterial cells within biofilms at days 1 and 3. Phage vECPPW10 at concentrations ranging from 101 to 108 PFU/well significantly reduced biofilm biomass and bacterial cell viability by 27.5 to 68.73% and 0.83 to 1.983 log units, respectively, at day 1 post-treatment (Fig. 8(a), (b)). Similarly, at day 3 post-treatment, both biofilm biomass and bacterial cell viability were further significantly reduced by 28.85 to 68.93% and 0.92 to 2.08 log units, respectively, at the same concentrations (Fig. 8(c), (d)).

The antibiofilm activity of phage vECPPW10 against preformed biofilms was examined using 1-day- and 3-day-old biofilms. For 1-day-old biofilms, treatment with phage vECPPW10 (101 to 108 PFU/well) significantly decreased biofilm biomass and bacterial cell viability by 26.78 to 64.95% and 0.64 to 1.87 log units, respectively (Fig. 9(a), (b)). For 3-day-old biofilms, the phage significantly reduced biofilm biomass and bacterial cell viability by 23.28 to 63.34% and 0.6 to 1.56 log units, respectively (Fig. 9(c), (d)).

Moreover, structural alterations in the biofilms following phage vECPPW10 treatment were examined using scanning electron microscopy and compared with the untreated control. One-day-old preformed biofilms were used as the experimental model. In the control group (Fig. 10(a)), a dense population of bacterial cells was observed under biofilm conditions. The bacterial cells maintained their typical rod-shaped morphology and appeared structurally intact. Limited extracellular material was visible, with minor contact points between bacterial cells, suggesting intercellular association. Following phage treatment, noticeable changes in biofilm structure were evident (Fig. 10(b)). Disruption of bacterial cell integrity and the presence of scattered cellular debris were frequently observed.

Discussion

E. coli infections in livestock production systems such as swine and poultry farming represent a major global challenge in agriculture due to their impact on animal health, productivity, and the potential for zoonotic transmission17. Due to the overuse and misuse of antibiotics, several antibiotic-resistant strains of E. coli from livestock infections have been reported. Therefore, alternative treatments to control E. coli infections in livestock production have been considered. This study aimed to isolate and characterize a phage specific to E. coli to control MDR E. coli isolated from swine feces. The molecular and genomic biology of the phage, as well as its antibacterial and antibiofilm activities, were evaluated to develop it as a promising alternative antimicrobial strategy.

Phage vECPPW10 was isolated from environmental water, and its successful isolation aligns with previous studies showing that phages are abundant in natural samples18. The phage formed plaques with halos on a bacterial lawn, indicating the presence of a depolymerase domain in its genome19. Previous reports on the morphology of Escherichia phages within the genus Kayfunavirus, as observed under TEM, have shown variability. Most phages, such as Escherichia phage vB_EcoP-Ro45lw20, Escherichia phage DY121, and Escherichia phage ZH422, exhibit a morphology consisting of an icosahedral head and a very short tail, whereas Kayfunavirus CY1 has been reported to possess a head and a long tail (approximately 180 nm)23. Based on TEM observations, phage vECPPW10 also consists of an icosahedral head and a short tail. To further compare morphological characteristics, whole-genome sequencing results were used to identify closely related phages. Phages closely related to the vECPPW10 genome, as determined by BLASTn analysis, were subsequently examined. However, morphological information was unavailable for most of these phages, except for Escherichia phage JSS1 (KX689784.2, percent identity 93.3%, query coverage 83%), which was reported to exhibit an icosahedral head and a short tail24. Klebsiella phage Pkp-1 (PV485104.1, percent identity 93.97%, query coverage 79%) was also reported to possess an icosahedral head and a short tail25. These findings indicate that phage vECPPW10 exhibits morphological characteristics consistent with most phages within the genus Kayfunavirus.

Whole-genome sequencing of the phage also helps to understand its genetic makeup, the function of each coding sequence, and the potential applications, including safety considerations. The genome size of phage vECPPW10 falls within the range of other previously reported Escherichia phages, such as fBC-Eco 01 (43,466 bp)26, UDF157lw (46,604 bp)27, KIT06 (167,059 bp)28, and iGC_PHA_EC001 (148,445 bp)29. Variations in genome size impact the biological diversity of Escherichia phages, influencing properties such as host range, one-step growth curve, stability, and bacterial lysis efficacy. When compared to some other Escherichia phages, the genome of phage vECPPW10 is smaller, suggesting that it may encode only the essential genes required for its life cycle, including those involved in phage structure, replication, packaging, and host lysis. Previous studies have reported that phages with larger genomes may encode unnecessary genes, such as anti-sigma factor-like proteins related to bacterial transcriptional regulation26. Genome annotation revealed that the vECPPW10 genome contains 48 CDSs encoding many important proteins. Of particular interest are proteins related to host lysis, such as CDS4, which encodes holin, a small transmembrane protein that acts as a molecular clock determining the phage infection cycle duration by forming pores in the inner membrane to release endolysin30,31. This allows endolysin, encoded by CDS6, to move from the cytoplasm to the peptidoglycan layer, where it lyses the cell wall by exerting catalytic activity32,33. E. coli is a Gram-negative bacterium with an outer membrane covering the peptidoglycan layer. Spanin, encoded CDS2, is composed of an outer membrane lipoprotein (o-spanin) and an inner membrane protein (i-spanin), forming the bridge between both membranes34. This structure will complete the lysis process by disrupting both outer and inner membranes, resulting in bacterial cell lysis. Moreover, NCBI Conserved Domain Database analysis identified a phage lysozyme conserved domain located between nucleotides 28,993 and 29,463 of the genome. Phage lysozymes are known to function in host cell lysis, facilitating the release of newly formed virions and contributing to the progression of infection35. Interestingly, the phage vECPPW10 genome also encodes a tail protein (CDS5), which is responsible for binding to host receptors and injecting DNA36. Previous reports have shown that depolymerase domains are commonly associated with phage tail protein or phage tail fiber protein37. Depolymerases are phage-encoded enzymes responsible for degrading bacterial polysaccharides, including exopolysaccharides (EPS), capsular polysaccharides (CPS), and lipopolysaccharides (LPS). The detection of depolymerase and pectin lyase-like domains in the genome of phage vECPPW10 is consistent with previous reports on Acinetobacter phage vB_AbaM_AB4P2, supporting the role of pectin lyase-like domains in depolymerase activity and their contribution to antibiofilm effects38. Pectin lyase is a pectolytic enzyme capable of degrading galacturonic acid, a major component of certain bacterial polysaccharides39. Furthermore, galacturonic acid has been reported as a constituent of EPS within the biofilm matrix, and is also found in CPS40,41. This suggested that phage vECPPW10 may exhibit strong activity in degrading LPS attached to the outer membrane and CPS that overlays the LPS of E. coli. Additionally, EPS is a major biopolymer and a key component of biofilms, serving as an important virulence factor31. EPS prevents antibiotic penetration and protects bacteria from the effects of antibiotics32. Thus, phages with a depolymerase domain may enhance their efficacy and possess antibiofilm activity.

Phylogenetic analysis of the whole genome and specific structural genes, such as the capsid protein, which is commonly used to identify related phages, was evaluated42. The results from phylogenetic trees based on the whole genome and capsid protein, together with whole-genome alignment, suggested that phage vECPPW10 belongs to the genus Kayfunavirus within the subfamily Studiervirinae, family Autographiviridae. Differences in the whole-genome sequence and capsid protein may suggest that phage vECPPW10 has diverged along a distinct evolutionary lineage from other phages. Moreover, the phylogenetic tree of the terminase large subunit was performed because it can be used to predict putative DNA packaging strategies42,43. The results showed that the terminase large subunit of phage vECPPW10 is closely related to that of Escherichia phage HC13, a member of the genus Kayfunavirus. However, the packaging strategy of this phage has not been reported. Therefore, the packaging strategy of phage vECPPW10 was further investigated using PhageTerm, which indicated a direct terminal repeat-type packaging mechanism. PhageClouds analysis further confirmed that this phage had not been previously described44. Lifestyle prediction indicated that phage vECPPW10 has a lytic lifestyle. These findings demonstrated that phage vECPPW10 is safe and suitable for therapeutic use, as lytic phages do not integrate their genomes into the host genome45.

The biological characterization of phage vECPPW10 demonstrated its ability to lyse multiple E. coli isolates, confirming its host specificity. Its adsorption efficiency contributed to effective infection, and the adsorption rate of phage vECPPW10 was comparable to those reported for other Escherichia phages. For example, phage AUBRB02, a member of the genus Tequatrovirus and family Straboviridae, adsorbed to E. coli ATCC 25922 at a rate exceeding 80% within 5 min46. Similarly, Escherichia phages P1 and P2, formerly classified within the families Myoviridae and Siphoviridae (both families were formally abolished by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses in 2022), exhibited adsorption rates of more than 60% and 80% within 30 min, respectively, while phage SPEC13, a member of the family Ackermannviridae, reached 93% adsorption within 10 min47,48. Escherichia SR04 phage, one of the few reported phages within the genus Kayfunavirus, has also been investigated for adsorption efficacy and exhibited an adsorption rate of more than 80% within 5 min49. Based on these comparisons, the adsorption efficiency of phage vECPPW10 can be considered moderate relative to some previously reported Escherichia phages, suggesting that it is capable of efficiently attaching to and initiating infection in susceptible bacterial cells. Previous studies have reported varying latent periods and burst sizes among Escherichia phages belonging to the Kayfunavirus genus. For example, phage Ro45lw, which is specific to Shiga toxin-producing E. coli O45, exhibited a latent period of 15 min and a burst size of 55 progeny virions per infected cell20. Escherichia phage DY1 showed a latent period of 20 min and a burst size of 30 ± 2 phage particles per infected cell21. Escherichia phage UFJF_EcSW4 had a latent period of 9 min and a burst size of 49 PFU/infected cell50, while phage ZH4 exhibited a latent period of 20 min and a burst size of 25 PFU/infected cell22. Phage vECPPW10 exhibited a burst size of 30 PFU/cell, which is lower than those of most previously described phages, except for phage ZH4, for which vECPPW10 showed a higher burst size. However, the latent period of phage vECPPW10 (20 min) was similar to that of phage ZH4. These results suggest that phage vECPPW10 may utilize cell resources more efficiently, resulting in a slightly larger burst size than that of phage ZH451. Lytic activity assays showed that vECPPW10 at a low MOI could inhibit bacterial growth within 1 h. However, bacterial regrowth was observed at 6 h, indicating the emergence of phage resistance. Resistance may occur through spontaneous mutations that modify bacterial surface receptors, preventing phage adsorption52,53. The stability of phage under environmental stresses, such as temperature and pH, is essential for product development. Phage vECPPW10 retained activity after incubation at -20 to 60 °C and at pH 2 to 12. These results demonstrate that phage vECPPW10 can be stored, transported, and applied under diverse conditions without losing infectivity. Such stability of phage vECPPW10 also supports its potential development into practical products, including spray formulations, therapeutic agents, or food additives.

The ability of phage vECPPW10 to prevent biofilm formation and to eliminate preformed biofilms was also assessed. Phage vECPPW10 reduced both biofilm biomass and bacterial cell viability in biofilm formation and preformed biofilms at 1 and 3 days. Higher phage concentration produced greater reductions in both biofilm biomass and bacterial viability, indicating a dose-dependent efficacy. The presence of a putative depolymerase domain may enhance the antibiofilm activity of phage vECPPW10 by degrading EPS, thereby facilitating phage penetration into the bacterial community beneath the EPS layer54,55. Several studies have shown correlations between depolymerase and the antibiofilm effects of Escherichia phages. For example, phage 590B at an MOI of 0.01 to 0.1 reduced preformed biofilm of E. coli strain ATCC 25922 at both 24 and 48 h, with the authors concluding that depolymerase contributed to antibiofilm activity56. Similarly, a depolymerase named Dpo42, derived from Escherichia phage vB_EcoM_ECOO78, reduced biofilm formation at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h in a dose-dependent manner57.

In summary, phage vECPPW10 demonstrates promising potential for both prophylactic and therapeutic applications against E. coli infections in swine production systems, with the added benefit of potentially reducing antibiotic usage. Its demonstrated antibacterial and antibiofilm activities highlight its suitability as a biocontrol candidate for mitigating MDR E. coli contamination in swine farm environments. Nevertheless, considering the potential emergence of phage-resistant bacterial variants, future studies should explore the formulation of phage cocktails and evaluate their efficacy and stability under field conditions to enhance long-term effectiveness and sustainability.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics and biosafety approval

This research received biosafety and ethical approval from the Institutional Biosafety Committee of Walailak University, Thailand (WU-IBC-67-019) and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Walailak University (WU-ACUC-67090) prior to the commencement of the study.

Bacterial strains and culture

Twenty MDR commensal E. coli isolates used in this study were obtained from fecal samples collected from swine farm environments and maintained as stock cultures in the laboratory. These isolates were provided by Ratchadaporn Boripun, Akkhraratchakumari Veterinary College, Walailak University, Thailand58. The bacteria were cultured on tryptic soy agar (TSA; Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and incubated overnight (18 h) at 37 °C. Selected colonies were then subcultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB; Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for either 6 h or overnight (18 h).

Isolation and purification of the phage

The phage was isolated using previously described methods59. Briefly, 10 mL of environmental water samples collected from a pond near a swine farm in Thasala, Nakhon Si Thammarat, Thailand, were centrifuged at 6000 × g for 10 min to remove debris. The supernatant was subsequently filtered through a sterile 0.22 μm syringe filter (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Phage enrichment was carried out by incubating the filtrate with 1 mL of diluted MDR E. coli PPW027 in TSB (OD600 = 0.08), which served as the host strain for phage isolation. The mixture was supplemented with 5 mL of TSB and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 6 h. After incubation, the culture was centrifuged at 6000× g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was filtered using a sterile 0.22 μm syringe filter. The presence of phage was determined via spot testing using the double-agar overlay method. In brief, MDR E. coli diluted in TSB (OD600 = 0.08) was mixed with molten semisolid top agar (0.75% agar in TSB; bacteriological agar; Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA; cooled to 50 °C), and poured onto TSA plates. Once the top agar solidified, 10 µL of the filtered supernatant was spotted onto the surface. The plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C. For phage purification, clear lysis zones (plaques) were picked using sterile micropipette tips and transferred into Eppendorf tubes containing SM buffer (100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgSO4, and 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH7.5)). After overnight incubation at 4 °C, the supernatant was filtered using a sterile 0.22 μm syringe filter and serially diluted tenfold in SM buffer. Each dilution was then tested using the double-agar overlay method. Following overnight incubation, individual plaques were picked and reprocessed as described. These purification steps were repeated three times to obtain a homogeneous phage population.

Phage propagation

The phage was propagated using the double-agar overlay method to produce a phage stock. This method provides a simple and practical approach, allowing clear visualization of plaque formation for assessing phage activity and detecting contamination59–61. Briefly, the phage was ten-fold serially diluted in SM buffer. Two hundred microliters of phage supernatant and 200 µL of bacterial suspension were added to a conical tube containing top agar, and the mixture was vortexed. The mixture was then poured onto TSA plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Semi-confluent lysis plates were overlaid with 5 mL of SM buffer and incubated with shaking at 150 rpm for 5 h. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 6000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove bacterial debris. The supernatant was filtered through a sterile 0.22 μm filter. The phage titer was determined by the standard plaque assay and expressed as PFU/mL. The phage stock was stored at 4 °C until further use.

Phage concentration

The phage was concentrated as previously described61. Fifteen milliliters of phage stock was added to the upper reservoir of an Amicon® Ultra Centrifuge filter (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The Amicon® tube was centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The filtrate was discarded, and additional phage stock was added to the filter cup, followed by repetition of the centrifugation step. After concentrating the phage stock to 5 mL, 10 mL of SM buffer was added to the filter unit and centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to wash the phage. The washing step was repeated. When the phage volume in the filter unit was reduced to less than 10 mL, the concentrate was collected. The phage titer was determined by the standard plaque assay and expressed as PFU/mL. The concentrated phage stock was stored at 4 °C for further use in transmission electron microscopy and whole-genome sequencing.

Phage morphology by TEM

The concentrated phage stock was deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid, and excess liquid was removed with filter paper. The grid was washed with distilled water, followed by blotting to remove excess liquid. The sample was negatively stained with 1% (v/v) uranyl acetate, and excess stain was blotted. After air drying, the grid was examined under a JEOL JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV to observe phage morphology. Digital images were captured using an EMSIS Xarosa digital camera (Münster, Germany) with Radius software (version 2.2). The capsid width and total particle length were measured and are reported as mean ± standard deviation (n = 10).

Genome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Fresh phage samples were submitted to Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea) for DNA extraction and whole-genome sequencing. Phage DNA was extracted from the concentrated phage stock using the standard protocol of the Maxwell RSC Tissue DNA Kit (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA). DNA concentration and quality were initially assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer by measuring absorbance at 260 and 280 nm and evaluating the A260/A280 ratio. Only DNA samples meeting the quality criteria (concentration > 50 ng/mL and A260/A280 ratio of 1.8 − 2.0) were used for library preparation. Sequencing libraries were constructed using a ligation-based method involving random fragmentation of DNA, followed by adapter ligation at the 5′ and 3′ ends and PCR amplification to generate library templates. Library quantification was performed using qPCR on a LightCycler system, following the Illumina qPCR Quantification Protocol Guide. Library size distribution was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with a DNA 1000 chip. Qualified libraries were loaded onto a flow cell, where DNA fragments were captured on surface-bound oligonucleotides complementary to the library adapters, followed by clonal amplification through bridge amplification. Sequencing was performed using Illumina sequencing-by-synthesis technology (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), which was serviced by Macrogen Inc. Raw image data were generated by the sequencing platform and processed using real-time analysis software for base calling. The resulting BCL files were converted into FASTQ format using bcl2fastq. The quality of the FASTQ files was assessed, and adapter trimming and low-quality base removal were performed using Trimmomatic (version 0.38). Data quality was further evaluated using FastQC (version 0.11.7). Subsampling was performed during preprocessing when the sequencing depth exceeded 150× of the target genome size. Filtered reads were randomly subsampled, and the resulting reduced dataset was used for de novo genome assembly.

De novo assembly was performed with SPAdes (version 3.15.0) to produce a FASTA file. PhageTerm was used to predict the genome packaging mechanism and termini62. Based on the PhageTerm analysis, the genome sequence was reoriented such that the predicted left terminus corresponds to nucleotide position 1 and was used for downstream analyses. Gene annotation and genome map construction were performed using the PHASTEST web server, including the prediction of tRNA and tmRNA genes (https://phastest.ca/)63. Moreover, the absence of tRNA and tmRNA genes was confirmed by the Aragorn program64. The presence of temperate lifestyle genes, antimicrobial resistance genes, and virulence genes was analyzed using the PhageLeads web server (https://phageleads.ku.dk/)65. BLASTp was used to compare the predicted protein sequences encoded by each CDS of the phage genome against sequences in the NCBI database. The depolymerase domain was predicted using Phyre2.2 (https://www.sbg.bio.ic.ac.uk/phyre2/html/page.cgi?id=index)66. InterPro analysis (version 108.0) was performed using the online InterPro web server (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) to identify the pectin lyase-like domain67. The host and lifestyles of phage vECPPW10 were predicted using the PhageScope (https://phagescope.deepomics.org/)68. NCBI Conserved Domain Database analysis was used to identify a phage lysozyme conserved domain69.

Phylogenetic tree analysis of the phage genome

Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the VICTOR web server to construct a phylogenetic tree. The whole-genome sequence of phage vECPPW10 was used as a query in a BLASTn search against the NCBI database. Twenty closely related phages with more than 80% sequence identity and 79% query coverage, including Pseudomonas phage Epa40 as an outgroup, were retrieved and subsequently analyzed using VICTOR70.

Genome alignment between phage vECPPW10 and its two closest relatives was also conducted to assess sequence similarity using the DigAlign web server (https://www.genome.jp/digalign/)71. The newly discovered phage was further confirmed using the PhageClouds database (https://phageclouds.dk/)44.

Phylogenetic trees of the major capsid and terminase large subunit proteins

To analyze the evolutionary relationships of the major capsid and terminase large subunit proteins of phage vECPPW10 and other phages in the NCBI database, the amino acid sequences of the major capsid and terminase large subunit proteins were searched in the NCBI database using BLASTp. Amino acid sequences of fifteen phages that have the highest percent identity (more than 80%) and query coverage (more than 80%) were retrieved from the database. The phylogenetic trees were constructed by NGPhylogeny.fr (https://ngphylogeny.fr/)72. Briefly, the sequences were multiple aligned with MAFFT, and the alignment was curated with BMGE. Phylogenetic tree inference was performed with FastTree, and the tree rendering was carried out using Newick Display. The capsid protein (QIQ66013.1) and terminase large subunit (YP010597693.1) of Pseudomonas phage Epa40 were used as outgroups for the phylogenetic analyses of the major capsid proteins and terminase large subunit, respectively.

Host range analysis and EOP

The host range of the phage was primarily assessed using the spot test as previously described59. Briefly, the phage vECPPW10 stock was serially diluted in SM buffer to concentrations ranging from 101 to 108 PFU/mL. Ten microliters of each phage dilution was spotted onto a bacterial lawn, allowed to air-dry, and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Clear zones or plaques, indicating the ability of the phage to lyse bacteria, were observed. The lytic and non-lytic activities of the phage were recorded as + and -, respectively. Bacterial isolates that showed sensitivity to the phage were selected for the EOP assay.

The EOP was determined according to previously published methods, using the double-agar overlay method as described above59,73. Briefly, phage suspensions were serially diluted in SM buffer to final concentrations from 101 to 108 PFU/mL. After overnight incubation under static conditions, plaques were counted, and titers were calculated (PFU/mL). The EOP was calculated as the ratio of the PFU/mL in the test bacterial isolate to those in the original host isolate. EOP values were categorized as follows: High (EOP ≥ 0.5), moderate (0.1 ≤ EOP < 0.5), low (0.001 < EOP < 0.1), or inefficient (EOP ≤ 0.001). All experiments were performed independently in triplicate.

Phage adsorption studies

The ability of the phage to adsorb to bacterial cells (MDR E. coli PPW027, which was the host used for phage isolation in this study) was assessed as previously described73,74. Briefly, E. coli cells were mixed with the phage at an MOI of 0.1. Samples were collected at 1-min intervals from 0 to 10 min, and then at 5-min intervals from 10 to 30 min, followed by filtration through a sterile 0.22 μm syringe filter. The filtrates were then subjected to a double-layer agar assay to determine phage titers. All experiments were independently performed in triplicate.

One-step growth curve of phage

A one-step growth curve was performed to determine the latent period and burst size of the phage, following a previously described method59. Briefly, MDR E. coli PPW027 was cultured in TSB at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 6 h and then diluted with TSB to an OD600 of 0.08. A phage suspension at an MOI of 0.1 was added to a tube containing bacterial cells, and the mixture was incubated for 10 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 6000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in TSB and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm. Aliquots were collected at 10-minute intervals from 0 to 120 min. At each time point, the aliquots were immediately filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter, and the filtrates were used to determine the phage titer by a double-layer agar assay. All experiments were independently performed in triplicate. The burst size of the phage was calculated by dividing the number of phage particles released during the plateau phase by the initial number of infected bacterial cells.

In vitro phage kinetics

The in vitro lytic activity of the phage at various MOIs was assessed as previously described59. Briefly, MDR E. coli PPW027 was cultured in TSB at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 18 h and then diluted with TSB to an OD600 of 0.08. The diluted bacterial culture was infected with the phage at MOIs of 0.01, 0.1, and 1. The samples were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm, and aliquots were collected at 1-h intervals from 0 to 8 h for optical density measurement at OD600 nm using a UV spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Afterward, the samples were further incubated overnight under the same conditions. At 24 h post-infection, turbidity was measured again at OD600. The samples were then serially diluted in TSB to determine bacterial cell viability under each condition. Appropriate dilutions were plated onto TSA plates and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Viable bacterial colonies were counted and expressed as CFU/mL. A phage-free bacterial culture grown under identical conditions served as the control. All experiments were independently performed in triplicate.

Determination of bacterial cell morphology after phage treatment using SEM

MDR E. coli PPW027 was cultured in TSB at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 6 h and then diluted with TSB to an OD600 of 0.08. The bacterial suspension was infected with phage vECPPW10 at an MOI of 0.1. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 2 h, and cells were then collected by centrifugation at 6000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS. The pellets were washed twice with PBS and resuspended again in PBS. The suspension was dropped onto glass slides and allowed to air-dry. The cells and bacterial debris were fixed using 2% (v/v) glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M PBS for 1 h at 4 °C, followed by two PBS washes. Subsequently, the fixed samples were dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and absolute ethanol), with each ethanol concentration applied twice. The dehydrated samples were dried using a critical-point dryer assay (Quorum K850, Quorum Technologies, UK). After gold coating using a sputter coater (Cressington 108 Auto, Cressington Scientific Instrument, UK), the samples were examined under a field-emission SEM (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Phage stability studies

Phage stability was assessed under various temperatures and pH levels, following a previously described method59. For thermal stability, the phage stock was diluted in SM buffer to a final concentration of 108 PFU/mL and exposed to different temperatures (-20, 4, 25, 37, 50, 60, 70, and 80 °C) for 2 h. The phage titer was determined using the double-agar overlay method. To evaluate pH stability, SM buffer was adjusted to pH values ranging from 1 to 14. A stock of phage vECPPW10 was diluted in each pH-adjusted buffer tube to achieve a final concentration of 108 PFU/mL. The mixture was vortexed and then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The phage titer was then determined using the double-agar overlay method described above. All experiments were independently conducted in triplicate.

Antibiofilm activity of the phage

The efficacy of phage vECPPW10 in preventing biofilm formation and removing established biofilms was evaluated using the host bacterial isolate at days 1 and 3 post-incubation, as previously described59,75. Biofilm biomass and bacterial cell viability within the biofilms were assessed using crystal violet staining and colony-forming unit (CFU) enumeration, respectively. To evaluate the ability of the phage to prevent biofilm formation, an overnight culture of MDR E. coli PPW027 was diluted in TSB to an OD600 of 0.08. One hundred microliters of the bacterial suspension was added to 96-well culture plates, and phage vECPPW10 was added to the wells at final concentrations ranging from 101 to 108 PFU/well. An additional 100 µL of TSB was added to each well. After gently mixing, the plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight without agitation. At 1 and 3 days post-infection, the contents of the wells were removed, and the wells were washed twice with PBS. For biofilm biomass quantification, 200 µL of 0.1% crystal violet was added to each well, followed by incubation in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Excess stain was discarded, and the wells were washed twice with distilled water. After air-drying, 200 µL of ethanol was added to solubilize the crystal violet, and absorbance was measured at OD595 using a standard microplate reader. To determine bacterial cell viability within the biofilms, 200 µL of TSB was added to each well to resuspend the biofilm. The resulting suspension was serially diluted in TSB and plated onto TSA plates. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, bacterial colonies were counted, and cell viability was expressed as CFU/mL.

To assess the ability of phage vECPPW10 to remove established biofilms, 100 µL of the diluted bacterial culture was added to 96-well plates, followed by the addition of 100 µL of TSB and incubation under static conditions. At 1 day post-incubation, planktonic cells were removed, and the wells were gently washed twice with 200 µL of PBS. Phage vECPPW10 was then added at final concentrations ranging from 101 to 108 PFU/well, followed by incubation for 24 h. Biofilm biomass and bacterial cell viability were subsequently determined using the same procedures as described above. For 3-day-old biofilms, the medium in the wells was removed and replaced with fresh media every day. On day 3 post-incubation, phage vECPPW10 was added to the wells at final concentrations ranging from 101 to 108 PFU/well. The plates were then incubated for 24 h, followed by determination of biofilm biomass and bacterial cell viability as described above. All experiments were independently conducted in triplicate.

Observation of biofilm structure by scanning electron microscopy

The structure of biofilms was examined using scanning electron microscopy. Briefly, MDR E. coli PPW027 was cultured under standard conditions for 6 h and subsequently diluted in TSB to an OD600 of 0.08. Sterile glass slides (0.5 cm × 0.5 cm) were placed in the 24-well plates, and 2 mL of the bacterial suspension was added to each well. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h without agitation to allow biofilm formation. After incubation, the culture medium in the wells was removed, and the wells were gently washed twice with 0.1 M PBS to eliminate non-adherent cells. The biofilms were treated with phage vECPPW10 at 108 PFU/well, while TSB alone was added to the control group. The plates were further incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Following incubation, the medium was removed, and the wells were then washed twice with 0.1 M PBS. The biofilms were then fixed with 2% (v/v) glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M PBS and subsequently processed for SEM analysis as described above.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data for bacterial cell viability was log10-transformed prior to analysis to ensure normal distribution. Differences among groups were analysed using One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test to compare each treatment group with the untreated control. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistical significant.
