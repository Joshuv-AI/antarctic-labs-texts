Effects of cotton waste fibers on the strength development and flexural toughness of densified high-strength concrete
Authors: Joel Kimarai Musyoka, Naftary Gathimba, Silvester Ochieng Abuodha, Victoria Okumu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCotton Waste Fibers (CWF), a non-synthetic industrial by-product, form a type of micro-diameter concrete reinforcing fibrous material. Fiber dispersion and fiber balling reduce the application of CWF in structural concrete due to discontinuous interfacial transition zones (ITZ) in the concrete matrix. In this paper, compressive strength development and flexural toughness of concrete with 0%–1.5% 0.25 mm diameter and 50 mm length CWF fibres, designed using the densified mix design algorithm (DMDA) method, were studied. Two coarse aggregate (CA) grades: S1(10–12.5) mm and S2(5–10) mm; an intermediate aggregate (IA), S3(2.36–5) mm; and Natural River Sand (NRS) of 5 mm maximum aggregate size (MAS) fine aggregate (FA), and CEM II were adopted. An intermediate and coarse aggregate blend ratio of 1:1.33:5.34 in S3:S2:S1, enough to saturate the aggregate-to-aggregate pores, and an overall concrete mix proportion of 1:1.3:2.6, were achieved. A 0.38 water-cement (w/c) ratio and a maximum 0.0703 water-solid (w/s) ratio for 1.5% CWF dosage were used. A total of 84 cubes, 42 cylindrical, and 42 unnotched prisms, and 21 notched beam specimens were cast and cured at ambient temperature to determine the compressive strength, tensile strength, flexural strength, and toughness properties of CWF-HSC, respectively. A 62.71 MPa 28-day compressive strength was achieved in the control specimens, while a 0.37%, 0.88%, and 3.05% improvements in 0.25%, 0.50%, and 0,75% CWF was followed by a strength decline in 1%, 1.25%, and 1.50%, respectively. All CWF-HSC specimens achieved a higher early strength gain from 0.02 MPa/hr. to 0.13 MPa/Hr. in 0.25% and 1.5% CWF dosages, and a 0.08 MPa/Hr. gain rate at optimal 0.75% fiber dosage, compared to the control specimens. At all CWF dosages, an increase in tensile strength from 3.703 MPa up to 26.36% at 0.75% fiber content; while the flexural strength improved from 7.201 MPa in control specimens by 8.75%, 14.05%, and 16.44% in 0.25%, 0.50%, and 0.75% CWF content, followed by a slight decline up to 1.5% dosages. The peak and limit flexural toughness, I5 and I10, in specimens with CWF improved from 1 KJ/m2 to 1.73 KJ/m2 and 4.21 KJ/m2 at 0% and 0.75% CWF dosages, respectively. These experimental results confirm the feasibility of using sorted industrial cotton waste fibers in structural concrete within a DMDA framework for better fiber dispersion to enhance both strength development and flexural toughness properties.Keywords: Cotton waste fibers, Densified mix design algorithm, Strength development, Flexural toughnessSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Concrete’s strength development forms the broad picture which the functional parameters, like compressive, tensile, flexural, shear, and durability properties depend on1–3. From the concrete’s mixing stage; raw materials, water-cement ratio, and water-solid ratio; to placing, compacting, and curing, strength development depends on the rate of hydration of the cementitious material and the density of the concrete matrix1. However, in structural elements, concrete’s maturity age and its behavior in load support; from first crack formation to failure, distinguishes the strength development phenomenon4,5. Studies by Tiberti et al.6, Noaman et al.7, and Arunakanthi et al.8, show that the brittle nature of plain concrete solely depends on the maturity strength. The formation of the first crack in plain concrete prevents any additional strength from developing to support loads. This situation is overcome by different types of fiber-reinforced concrete (FRC), as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Stress–strain relating to behavior after crack formation9.

FRC contains fiber admixtures, as solid materials, incorporated into the concrete’s matrix, and is considered a ductile material10–12. This type of concrete contains a higher water-solid (w/s) ratio than plain concrete. When aggregate blend densification is considered, as proposed by the densified mix design algorithm (DMDA)13–16, this ratio increases, affecting the density of the resulting concrete matrix. For 90% mature concrete, the first crack load in FRC is greater than in plain concrete since the fibers in the matrix need to be fully pulled out or broken for the crack to grow17. The residual strength that the element can sustain under external loads, as well as its toughness properties, also increases with fiber dosage18,19. These can provide improved avenues of accounting for the strength of cracked sections at serviceability limit states, considering the pullout strength and fiber rupture parameters as the link to interfacial transition zone (ITZ) strength. Short and long fibers incorporated into concrete contribute to this improved behavior, depending on the fiber type and its dispersion in the concrete matrix11. Synthetic: sisal, jute, coir, and cotton20–23; and non-synthetic: steel, polypropylene, and basalt24–27; fibers form part of the class of these admixtures adopted in concrete. Most of these fibers are derived from waste materials, and their production depends on the different stages of industrial development. Recently, the textile industry has been one of the sectors that has grown rapidly due to rising demand for textile-based products, driven by the evolution of modern fashion and high population growth. The raw materials are selected as fibers, both natural and synthetic, and defined by their strength, availability, and the target textile product. This has led to increased textile waste generation28,29.

Industrial textile waste (TW) fibers form the residues obtained as by-products of various stages of garment production, such as spinning, weaving, and tanning. These fibers exist as lightweight, water-absorbent materials. According to studies on Kenya’s waste management practices30,31, Cotton, a textile raw material widely used in Kenyan industries, has led to increased cotton waste. Over 400,000 tons of these wastes are generated in landfills annually, with only 100,000 kg recycled, leaving the rest as environmental pollutants. The re-use of textile wastes in concrete has brought a new type of recycled TW fiber that functions as a crack bridging material, like synthetic fibers32–36. TW fibers are lightweight and highly hydrophilic, such as cotton waste fibers (CWF). The incorporation of TW fibers, shredded cotton cut from denim jeans, has shown improvement of overall mechanical properties like tensile and flexural strength; however, with reduced workability and compressive strength32,37, limited to application in non-structural members. The post-cracking behavior of concrete with these fibers has improved, with enhanced bridging action against crack growth and propagation and a more uniform porous matrix structure.

CWF forms a sorted type of TW fibers from cotton-related textile raw materials, whose reuse in concrete would reduce the environmental impacts of these industrial wastes. The increased biodegradable textile wastes directed to landfills, after only 1000 tons are recycled, would be significantly reduced with their reuse in commercial concrete production, and achieve an eco-friendly and cost-efficient FR concrete. However, as reported by Sasi et al.26 and Tran et al.28, concrete with TW fibers exhibits fiber agglomeration at high volume dosages, which can lead to voids; therefore, densifying the matrix is required. The DMDA method15,29 has been proposed as a concrete design method to achieve a denser concrete matrix by saturating the pores between solid particles. Densification by selecting cementitious materials has been studied to affect the hydration process, leading to the realization of pozzolana-blended cements38–41. However, aggregates’ bulk density significantly contributes to concrete’s strength, due to increased concrete density and the greater load required to crack the material42. Aggregates aid in dispersing fibers within the concrete matrix. With well-packed aggregate, dispersion improves because the solid particles are closer together. However, the hydrophilic nature of TW fibers, like CWF, undermines the dispersion process34,43. To commercialize the use of CWF in structural concrete, this study focuses on a novel methodology for integrating sorted cotton waste fibers (CWF) within a DMDA framework to simultaneously optimize packing density, strength development, and flexural toughness in sustainable high-strength FRC concrete. Beyond the conventional fiber-reinforced concrete approaches that treat fiber addition in concrete as an external parameter independent of the adopted mix design, this study links fiber-solid material interactions and densification effects to the hydration process, strength evolution, and fiber bridging efficiency. An experimental investigation of CWF concrete’s strength development and its flexural toughness was conducted under varying fiber dosages, maturity ages, and notched beams under varying bending loads. The results of this study contribute to advancing the utilization of industrial textile wastes as CWF sources in enhancing the concrete’s crack-bridging properties, thereby providing a sustainably-driven and mechanically justified design methodology.

Materials and experimental schemes

Raw materials

Blended pozzolana cement (CEM II/B-P 42.5 N) was used in this study for its high-strength at concrete’s maturity and complies with the BS EN 197-144 Standards. A 12.50 mm maximum-sized coarse aggregates (CA), comprising a blend of two CA types (10 mm and 12.5 mm), and an intermediate aggregate (IA) of 5 mm size, was locally sourced and adopted in this study. Natural river Sand (NRS) was sourced at Kasarani River, Kenya, and used as a fine aggregate (FA). Sika Visco flow 20HE Superplasticizer (SP) manufactured by Sika Chemicals in Kenya was adopted as a high-range water-reducing admixture (HRWRA) Class E to enhance the workability and strength development of CWF concrete. Portable water from the JKUAT civil engineering laboratory was used in the concrete mix. Finally, the sorted CWF used in this study was sourced from Thika Cloth Mills (TCM), Kenya, as an industrial waste of textile tanning, with each thread measuring approximately 0.25 mm thick and sized in 5 mm lengths. The sorted CWFs from TCM were prepared for concrete production by cutting them into sizeable pieces of 50 ± 5 mm using scissors. Five rolled threads were sampled to measure the diameter of a single thread using a 150 mm stainless steel electronic vernier caliper, revealing an average diameter of 0.2 ± 0.05 mm per CWF thread, and an overall aspect ratio of at most 200. The coarse aggregate (CA) of MAS of 12.5 mm was used and grouped into two types: S1(10–12.5) mm and S2(5–10) mm; and an Intermediate aggregate (IA) of (2.36–5) mm was identified as S3, respectively. NRS was used as the fine aggregate (FA) material. Quantification of cement

The amount of cement, CEM II/B-P 42.5 N, used in the mix was determined as a composition in a 1 m3 concrete. CEM II/B-P 42.5 N (Power Plus) is a pozzolana-blended cement supplied from the Bamburi Cement Factory, of 2.94 g/cm3 density and a target 28-day cube strength of 42.5–62.5 MPa, according to KS EAS 18 − 1 standards. The volume of the wet material, described as cement paste, was determined using Eq. 742. A paste enhancement factor k, proposed by15,42, was adopted for mix’s workability enhancement. This factor was taken as the k-means value of the [1–1.5] range, hence k = 1.3 was adopted. The total mass of cement was then determined from the composition of the identified paste as a value of water, cement, and superplasticizer, using Eq. 8. A water-cement ratio of 0.38 and a superplasticizer dosage of 0.4% by mass of cement were used in the concrete mix, achieved after conducting six trial mixes with w/c ratios ranging from 0.35 to 0.40 for high-strength concrete as recommended by ACI 211.1 standards, and eight SP dosages from 0.2% to 0.8%. The sorted 0.25 mm diameter and 50 mm length CWF dosage was done in increasing proportions of 0.25% by mass of cement in the concrete, from 0% to 1.5%. The increase in CWF quantities at each percentage dosage led to a decrease in the w/s ratio, from 0.0706 at 0.25% to 0.0703 at 1.5% CWF dosages. Testing programs

This study focused on determining the strength development and flexural toughness properties of concrete with CWF. CWF concrete with a mean target compressive cube strength of 60 MPa was designed for the two objective areas. Fresh and hardened concrete properties were studied. Concrete preparation followed a sequential introduction of constituent materials from NRS, 50% CWF, CEM II, 70% Water and SP, IA, 30% CWF, 20% water and SP, CA, 10% CWF, and 10% water and SP; to achieve an enhanced fiber dispersion in the concrete matrix.

Properties of fresh concrete

The workability of fresh concrete was determined by the slump test, according to ASTM C14352. Three successive layers, followed by 25 uniformly distributed tamping layers, were applied to achieve the desired slump value. The effect of CWF on the slump value was determined. Using the different classes of slump values proposed in ASTM C143 standards, a 25 mm slump value was considered as a limiting value for use where manual compaction is required.

Mechanical and strength development properties

The compressive, flexural, and split tensile strength test properties were determined according to En 12390–353, En 12390–554, and En 12390–655 standards, respectively. Cubes of 100 × 100 × 100 mm were cast to determine the compressive strength, while cylinders of 100 mm diameter by 200 mm height were used for the split tensile strength test. Prisms measuring b=100 mm, h=100 mm, and l=350 mm were adopted for flexural strength tests. The compressive strength properties were tested after 3, 7, 14, and 28 days of curing at ambient temperatures, while both split tensile and flexural strength were tested after 14 and 28 days. A correlation of the CWF fiber % dosage in concrete with the compressive strength evolution per maturity age was created to determine its strength development properties.

Flexural toughness properties

The notched beam specimens with a size of 100 mm x 150 mm x 500 mm, in breadth, depth, and length, respectively, were fabricated to test the flexural toughness properties of CWF high-strength concrete. The notches were made at midspan of the beams and used as initiation cracks with defined width, length, and depth. This used a metal plate of 100 mm length, 30 mm depth, and 3 mm thick. The ratios of crack properties: width w0, length l0, and depth a0, with respect to the overall depth of the specimen h0, were 0.02, 0.67, and 0.2, respectively, following RILEM TC 50-FMC56 standards. CWF concrete was filled in notched beam specimens, compacted in three layers, demolded after 24 h, and cured in water at ambient temperature for 28 days.

Three samples for each mix were tested after 28 days of curing through a three-point bending load, according to RILEM TC 50-FMC standards, loaded at a 5kN/min loading rate, as proposed by Meng et al.57. The test results were analyzed following ASTM C 160958 and ASTM C 101859 standards. The peak load was determined at maximum deflection and marked as the crack opening stage, and the limit load at failure. ASTM C 1609 was used to determine the peak and limit flexural toughness values (J/M2) at these two points, respectively. The integration of the load-deflection curve from 0 to L/600 and from L/600 extrapolated to L/150 was used, according to ASTM C 1018, to determine the toughness indices I5, I10, and R(5,10). The residual strength of CWF high-strength concrete was determined at the post-crack-opening stage. The flexural toughness average values for each concrete mix and the tested element were considered final if the difference between the maximum and minimum was less than 15%; otherwise, a median value was considered. The comparison of the flexural toughness values determined using the two standards was made, and a conclusion was drawn on the effectiveness of transitioning from AST C1108 to ASTM C1609. Load-CMOD and CMOD-deflection curves were found to depict the energy amounts required to open a crack in each mix specimen, and as a reference deflection for continued cracked specimens, respectively. The CMOD data was recorded for the displacement of the metal plate attached to LVDT 1 and the deflection taken as the mid-span deformation of the beam measured by LVDT 2, as shown in Fig. 6.

Results and discussions

In this section, test results for fresh and hardened concrete are discussed. Workability, split tensile, flexural, compressive strength and its development, and flexural toughness properties are discussed.

Workability

As illustrated in Fig. 7, a slump test was conducted to determine the effect of different percentage dosages of CWF on the workability of FRC. For the control mix, a slump of 132 mm was obtained. A slump loss of 29.5%, 51.5%, 76.5%, 90.2%, 93.9%, and 97.8% at 0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75%, 1%, 1.25%, and 1.50% CWF dosage was observed. This loss can be attributed to the increased w/s ratio resulting in reduced viscosity, resistance to flow, increased stiffness to the compaction process, and a densified matrix59,60. Again, the hydrophilic nature of CWF leads to high water absorption, hence, poor adhesion with the cement matrix43,62,63. Concrete mixes with up to 0.75% CWF dosage exhibited a 31 mm slump value, which is above the 25 mm recommended in ASTM C143 standards for concrete application where manual compaction is required. These results are in agreement with research conducted on textile waste fibers in concrete33,34,37,64.

Slump variation with changing CWF dosage.

Compressive strength development properties

Hardened concrete, with and without CWF, was tested for compressive strength at 3, 7, 14, and 28 days of curing. The 28-day compressive strength, shown in Fig. 8, was used to determine the optimum CWF dosage. Concrete without CWF attained an overall cube strength of 62.71 MPa after 28 days of curing using the DMDA method.

CWF – HSC 28-day Compressive Strength (MPa).

A 0.37%, 0.88%, and 3.05% increase in compressive strength at 0.25%, 0.50%, and 0.75% CWF fiber content, respectively, was observed. This increase can be associated with the densification property that CWF, as a solid material, has to the matrix. DMDA mix design aims at densifying the concrete matrix by saturation of pores between solid materials, which the cement paste engulfs with improved bonding properties65. An optimum CWF dosage of 0.75% by weight of cement was achieved, associated with the point of saturation of pores between solid materials. A decline in strength at 1%, 1.25%, and 1.50% fiber dosages compared to 0.75% CWF dosage was observed, denoting the effect of high fiber volumes on the concrete’s matrix strength43,66–68. After saturation of solid-to-solid material pores, fiber clumping, and the formation of weak zones lead to faster debonding and accelerated failure of specimens under compressive load effects.

Figure 9 illustrates the effect of varying fiber dosages in the concrete matrix on its strength development. An early 3-day compressive strength of 42.56 MPa was achieved for densified HSC without CWF. At 0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75%, 1%, 1.25%, and 1.5% CWF dosages, early compressive strengths of 44.06 MPa, 43.52 MPa, 42.93 MPa, 36.98 MPa, 34.62 MPa, and 29.5 MPa were achieved, respectively. However, the strength gain rate for HSC concrete with CWF at all dosages after 168 h exceeded that of the control specimen. Early strength gains from 72 h to 168 h were achieved with 0.02, 0.06, 0.08, 0.07, 0.07, and 0.13 MPa/Hr recorded in 0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75%, 1%, 1.25%, and 1.5% CWF dosages. The increase in early strength gain rate is observed up to a 0.75% CWF dosage at 0.08 MPa/Hr, a drop at 1%, and a steady rise at 1.25% and 1.5%, as shown in Fig. 10. After 168 h, the strength gain rate reduces in concrete with CWF compared to a steady increase in concrete without CWF. This shows that the normal percentage strength development at different curing ages in concrete does not adhere in the presence of fiber admixtures69.

Compressive Strength Gain Rate/Curing Age (MPa/Hr).

An early strength gain rate in CWF-HSC can be associated with the internal curing caused by the release of water initially absorbed by the fibers. This is achieved up to 0.75% dosages, past which poor dispersion of CWF manifests. A steady increase in strength gain rate past 1% CWF dosage can be associated with the regrouping and hydration of cement particles dispersed far from one another by the heavy volume of fibers at the mixing stage. According to the cement hydration model by Wang et al1., the interaction with water molecules at any curing stage contributes to the formation of strength molecules. This phenomenon in CWF concrete can be attributed to the internal and external curing processes facilitated by the fibers and the curing tank, on the slow-reactive pozzolan in CEM II/B-P 42.5 N cement, respectively.

Split tensile strength properties

Figure 11shows the variation of split tensile strength with changing CWF dosage after 28 days of curing. Control specimens showed a sudden brittle failure, splitting into two, just after the formation of the first tensile cracks at 3.703 MPa tensile strength. A 3.21%, 27.49%, 26.36%, 24.63%, 7.21%, and 6.59% increase in split tensile strength was observed in specimens with 0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75%, 1%, 1.25%, and 1.5% CWF dosages, respectively. The gradual increase in split tensile strength with CWF dosages supports research by Plizzari et al12. and Anas et al60. regarding FRC concrete. The increase is attributed to the presence of CWF in the concrete matrix, delaying failure by reducing the crack width growth through their bridging effects. The 6.667 N/mm stiffness factor, k, of the CWF improves the stiffness properties of the concrete matrix.

Split tensile strength curve of CWF-HSC.

A peak tensile strength average value of 4.721 MPa was recorded by specimens with 0.50% CWF dosage. This is in line with Sai et al.34, who proposed a textile waste fiber dosage of between 0.50% and 5%. Beyond 0.50% CWF dosage, a 0.90%, 2.25%, 15.9%, and 16.4% decline in strength at 0.75%, 1%, 1.25%, and 1.5% was observed, respectively. This can be attributed to poor dispersion and distribution of CWF in the concrete matrix, creating weak and inconsistent zones, which aid in propagating cracks faster under tensile stresses6,17. The CWF densified HSC shows its potential for application in structural elements subjected to tensile loadings.

Flexural strength and toughness properties

Flexural strength

The 28-day curing flexural strength properties, described as modulus of rupture (MoR), with and without CWF, are shown in Fig. 12. The control specimens showed a brittle failure, breaking instantly at peak load, and achieving a 7.201 MPa strength. An 8.75%, 14.05%, 16.44%, and 3.04% increase in maximum MoR at 0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75%, and 1% CWF dosages was achieved. A 0.75% CWF optimum dosage in concrete was achieved. A 7.65% and 12.91% decline in MoR value at 1.25% and 1.5% CWF dosage compared to the control specimen was obtained. These results are in line with studies by Falliano et al.68, Wang et al.70, and Asheghi Mehmandari et al.71 which shows that fiber dosage in concrete helps in the crack-bridging mechanism of specimens. Wide crack mouth opening is inhibited, however, to an optimum fiber dosage where dispersion and bonding improve the concrete’s matrix. High fiber volumes promote fiber balling in the concrete specimen, leading to less paste binding them together. Lee et al.72 and Falliano et al.68 show that during loading, zones dominated with CWF balls would promote faster flexural crack propagation, leading to accelerated failure and reduced ability to resist bending loads.

Flexural strength curve of CWF-HSC after 28-day curing.

Flexural toughness properties

The load-deflection curves, load-cmod curves, and cmod-deflection curves of notched beam specimens of a0/h0 = 0.20 and subjected to a three-point load test are plotted in Figs. 13 and 14, and 15, respectively. Notched control beams experienced a severe brittle failure after the start of the crack opening, characterized by a sharp drop after the limit load, without any residual strength. However, improved failure, characterized by a small stagnated limit point, prolonged deflection, and controlled crack mouth opening properties, was achieved at each CWF dosage. Specimens with plain concrete have low tensile strength and high inability to resist crack propagation, while CWF dosages in the tested beam specimens acted as stress-transfer mechanisms that bridge cracks and prevent rapid brittle failure by improving the ductility and energy absorption properties in the concrete matrix12,60. CWF dosage showed improved peak load, termed as first crack load in ASTM C 1018 and P(600) in ASTM C 1609, owing to improved matrix strength and more energy required to open the crack18,73–75. Conclusion & recommendations

This research presents an experimental study on the effect of concrete’s densification process through aggregate packing and cotton waste fibers (CWF) on its strength development and flexural toughness. The results show that sorted CWF from industrial wastes introduced into concrete, adopting a DMDA mix design, improves the compressive strength of concrete. DMDA mix design improves the compactness of the aggregates and integrates CWF with their micron diameter into the concrete matrix as a new solid material to reduce the solid-to-solid pores. The high specific surface area of CWF showed an early absorption of the mixing water, leading to a 76.5% slump loss at an optimum (0.75%) CWF dosage recommended in this study, achieving a blend of workability and compressive strength target properties in structural concrete. A maximum 3.05%, 26.36%, and 16.44% increase in compressive, tensile, and flexural strength properties was achieved at the optimum 0.75% CWF dosages, highlighting the role of fibers in improving the concrete’s resistance levels to tensile stresses than compression forces. However, a gradual release of the absorbed water with increasing curing duration recorded a 0.08 MPa/Hr. strength gain rate of concrete with CWF due to internal concrete curing by the released water. This showed the role of a water-absorbent fiber in the internal curing process of concrete. Under a controlled loading rate of 5 kN/min, notched beams show a relationship between the pre- and post-peak flexural toughness properties. Specimens without CWF experienced a sudden brittle failure. Both low to high CWF introduction in the concrete matrix improves the toughness network, achieving more than 50% increase in energy needed to bring to failure a cracked section. The pre-peak properties have shown to depend on the densification played by the fibers in improving the compactness and compressive strength of the matrix. The ductility property of the concrete element, defined from the post-peak behavior of cracked concrete, depends on the presence of fibers and not on the densification of the matrix through DMDA mix design. The limit flexural toughness properties obtained using ASTM C 1018 correlate well with those obtained using ASTM C 1609, with a variance ratio of 1.14 at optimum CWF dosage. However, the dependence of high limits of deflection (L/150) in ASTM C 1108, which concrete fails to achieve, limits its application in determining the peak flexural toughness. For a conservative toughness performance analysis, practitioners should adopt ASTM C1609 in determining the post-crack performance behavior of concrete specimens. However, since the standard falls short of providing the methodology for determining the pre-peak flexural toughness properties, the correlation coefficient obtained for post-peak should be used to determine a conservative and repeatedly accurate value using ASTM C1108. Hence, even with overestimation, the two standards should work hand-in-hand in determining the toughness performance in concrete specimens. The results prove the need for a design mix choice in the densification of the matrix and improvement of the toughness network created by the CWF fibrous reinforcement material. The flexural toughness indices provide an avenue to compute strength properties of cracked beams, slabs, and other structural concrete sections at serviceability limit states (SLS). However, the commercial application of industrial cotton waste could be met with intense labor requirements in sourcing, sorting, sizing, and quartering CWF during the concrete batching process, which might require machine incorporation for reduced human errors. Further studies should focus on modelling the fracture energy dissipation rate in CWF concrete, accounting for the fiber orientation and efficiency in the matrix for varied material mixing alteration processes. More experimental works should be done on the long-term effects of creep and shrinkage effects of concrete with CWF on the strength and toughness properties evolution to ascertain the long-term behavior of structural elements realized with CWF-HSC.
