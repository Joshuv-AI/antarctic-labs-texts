Comparison of costoclavicular with infraclavicular block for upper limb surgeries: A meta-analysis
Authors: Jiali Zhao, Wei Ren, Xiaosai Hou, Chaoqian Han, Zhentang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The influence of costoclavicular versus infraclavicular nerve block on the analgesia for upper limb surgeries remains elusive. This meta-analysis and systematic review aim to study the analgesic efficacy of costoclavicular versus infraclavicular nerve block for upper limb surgeries.Methods:We have searched several databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, EBSCO, and Cochrane Library databases from inception to January 2024, and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) assessing the effect of costoclavicular versus infraclavicular nerve block for upper limb surgeries were included. The inclusion criteria were presented as follows: study design was RCT, patients underwent upper limb surgeries, and intervention treatments were costoclavicular nerve block versus infraclavicular nerve block. Studies without English abstracts were excluded. The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated by the modified Jadad scale. Standard mean difference with 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to assess continuous outcomes, while odds ratio (OR) with 95% CI was applied to evaluate dichotomous outcomes.Results:Six RCTs and 434 patients were included in this meta-analysis. Compared with infraclavicular nerve block for upper limb surgeries, costoclavicular nerve block was able to significantly decrease block onset time (SMD = −0.29; 95% CI = −0.53 to −0.05; P = .02), but demonstrated no influence on performance time (SMD = −0.26; 95% CI = −0.85 to 0.34; P = .40), number of passes (SMD = −0.08; 95% CI = −0.40 to 0.24; P = .61), vascular puncture (OR = 0.25; 95% CI = 0.04 to 1.56; P = .14) or Horner syndrome (OR = 1.02; 95% CI = 0.04 to 27.64; P = .99).Conclusion:Costoclavicular nerve block may need shorter block onset time for upper limb surgeries compared to infraclavicular nerve block, but more RCTs were needed to confirm these findings.

1. Introduction

It is well known that brachial plexus block is applied as the main anesthetic method and postoperative analgesia of upper extremity surgery.[1–3] The brachial plexus is adjacent to large vessels and vital structures such as the pleura extending from the interscalene region to the axillary region, and thus it is vital to use ultrasonography to help conduct regional anesthesia interventions.[4–6] Brachial plexus block can provide adequate anesthesia, and has many advantages, including minimal physiological and metabolic alterations, less stress response, minimal monitoring, excellent longer duration of postoperative analgesia, and short stay in the postanesthesia care unit.[7–12]

Costoclavicular and infraclavicular approaches are commonly used to obtain a brachial plexus block. However, their comparison was not well established. Yayik et al revealed similar anesthetic effects between costoclavicular and infraclavicular plexus blocks during upper extremity surgery.[13] In another randomized controlled trial (RCT) involving 100 patients scheduled for elective forearm and hand surgery, the costoclavicular plexus block is able to provide a shorter performance time and a faster onset of the sensory block compared to the infraclavicular plexus block.[14] There have been no other meta-analyses or systematic reviews on this topic. Considering these inconsistent results, this meta-analysis aimed to compare the block efficacy of costoclavicular with infraclavicular axillary approaches for upper limb surgeries.

2. Materials and methods

This was a meta-analysis of previously published studies, and thus ethical approval and patient consent were not needed. We conducted this meta-analysis based on the Preferred Reporting Items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA 2020 for Abstracts checklist).[15] This meta-analysis, review, and protocol have not been registered.

2.1. Search strategy and study selection

Two authors independently searched several databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, EBSCO, and Cochrane Library databases from inception to January 5, 2024, and we used the search terms: “costoclavicular nerve block” AND “infraclavicular block” AND “upper limb surgery” odds ratio (OR) “forearm surgery” OR “distal arm surgery.” The inclusion criteria were presented as follows: study design was RCT, patients underwent upper limb surgeries based on the corresponding surgical indication, intervention treatments were costoclavicular nerve block versus infraclavicular nerve block, and age was <90 years old. Studies without English abstracts were excluded.

2.2. Data extraction and outcome measures

We extracted the following information from the original articles: first author, number of patients, age, sex, and weight, duration of surgery, and details of interventions of each group. Data were extracted independently by 2 investigators, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The primary outcome was block onset time. Secondary outcomes included performance time, number of passes, vascular puncture, and Horner syndrome. The number of passes indicated the number of needle passes. Some studies did not report the weight or duration of surgery.

2.3. Quality assessment in individual studies

Two authors independently evaluated the methodological quality of the included studies by using the modified Jadad scale,[16] which included 3 items: randomization (0–2 points), blinding (0–2 points), and dropouts and withdrawals (0–1 points). The score of the Jadad Scale varied from 0 to 5 points; a Jadad score ≤2 suggested low quality, while a Jadad score ≥3 suggested high quality.[17]

2.4. Statistical analysis

Standard mean difference (SMD) with 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to assess continuous outcomes (block onset time, performance time, and number of passes), while OR with 95% CI was applied to evaluate dichotomous outcomes (vascular puncture and Horner syndrome). I2 statistic was applied to assess heterogeneity, and an I2 > 50% suggested significant heterogeneity.[18] A random-effect model was used for significant heterogeneity, and otherwise, the fixed-effect model was applied. The potential heterogeneity was assessed through omitting one study in turn or performing subgroup analysis. Due to the limited number (<10) of included studies, publication bias was not evaluated. All statistical analyses were conducted using Review Manager Version 5.3.

2.5. Quality of evidence

The quality of evidence for each outcome was evaluated based on the methodological quality and the confidence in the results, and it was assessed by GRADE recommendations as high quality, moderate quality, low quality, or very low quality.[19]

3. Results

3.1. Literature search, study characteristics, and quality assessment

The flowchart of the search and selection results was presented in Figure 1. 157 potential articles were searched through the initial search. 49 duplicates and 99 papers were excluded after checking the titles/abstracts. Three studies were removed because of the study design, and 6 RCTs were ultimately included in this meta-analysis.[13,14,20–23] The baseline characteristics of 6 eligible RCTs in the meta-analysis are shown in Table 1. They were published between 2017 and 2021, and the total sample size was 434. Costoclavicular nerve block and infraclavicular block were administered by using 0.5% bupivacaine,[13,14] 0.25% bupivacaine,[23] 0.5% ropivacaine,[21,22] or the combination of 1% lidocaine and 0.25% bupivacaine.[20]

3.2. Primary outcome: block onset time

Three studies were included for the primary outcome. The results found that compared to infraclavicular nerve block for upper limb surgeries, costoclavicular nerve block showed significantly reduced block onset time (moderate quality, SMD = −0.29; 95% CI = −0.53 to −0.05; P = .02) with no heterogeneity remaining among the studies (I2 = 0%, heterogeneity P = .49, Figure 3).

Forest plot for the meta-analysis of block onset time. CI = confidence interval, SD = standard deviation.

3.3. Sensitivity analysis

No heterogeneity was seen among the included studies, and thus, we did not perform the meta-analysis by omitting one study or subgroup analysis to detect the heterogeneity.

3.4. Secondary outcomes

Compared to infraclavicular nerve block for upper limb surgeries, costoclavicular nerve block showed no impact on performance time (very low quality, SMD = −0.26; 95% CI = −0.85 to 0.34; P = .40; Figure 4), number of passes (moderate quality, SMD = −0.08; 95% CI = −0.40 to 0.24; P = .61; Figure 5), number of vascular puncture (moderate quality, OR = 0.25; 95% CI = 0.04 to 1.56; P = .14; Figure 6) or number of Horner syndrome (very low quality, OR = 1.02; 95% CI = 0.04 to 27.64; P = .99; Figure 7).

4. Discussion

In order to study the nerve block efficacy of costoclavicular versus infraclavicular approaches for upper limb surgeries, our meta-analysis included 6 RCTs and 434 patients. The results found that compared with infraclavicular nerve block, costoclavicular nerve block was able to significantly decrease block onset time, but showed no impact on performance time, number of passes, vascular puncture, or Horner syndrome.

The Jadad scores of 6 eligible RCTs varied from 3 to 5, suggesting that each study has high quality. Regarding the sensitivity analysis, no heterogeneity remained for the primary outcome. However, several factors may result in the heterogeneity. Firstly, various kinds of upper limb surgeries were included, such as elbow, wrist, and hand surgeries. Secondly, costoclavicular nerve block, infraclavicular nerve block, and axillary nerve block are conducted by using ropivacaine or bupivacaine. Thirdly, the concentrations of local anesthetics were different in each RCT. Fourthly, adults and pediatric patients were included in this meta-analysis.

Our results found that the costoclavicular nerve block may be superior to the infraclavicular nerve block for the decrease in block onset time during upper limb surgeries. In the costoclavicular fossa, the cords are located more on the surface and are clustered with each other on the immediate lateral side of the axillary artery.[24,25] Costoclavicular nerve block offers many advantages, including short onset time of blockade in single-injection technique and effective distribution of the drug to all the cords in block catheter applications.[26–30]

In terms of safety, this meta-analysis revealed a similar incidence of vascular puncture and Horner syndrome between the costoclavicular nerve block and infraclavicular nerve block. Our meta-analysis confirmed the benefits and safety of the costoclavicular nerve block to reduce block onset time for upper limb surgeries compared to the infraclavicular nerve block. We also should consider several potential limitations. Firstly, our analysis was based on 6 RCTs, and more RCTs should be conducted to confirm these findings. Secondly, there was no significant heterogeneity; different kinds and methods of costoclavicular and infraclavicular nerve block may affect the pooling results. Future studies should find the ideal method of costoclavicular and infraclavicular nerve block. Thirdly, various kinds of surgical types were included in this meta-analysis and may produce various levels of pain intensity.

5. Conclusions

Costoclavicular nerve block may have a shorter block onset time for upper limb surgeries compared to the infraclavicular nerve block, and future studies should find the ideal method of costoclavicular nerve block.
