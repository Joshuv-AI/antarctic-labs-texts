HORshe: A novel-systematic method for safety risk assessment using shell and hierarchy of control
Authors: Adithya Sudiarno, Muhammad Bahruddin Majid, Ega Rizkiyah, Muhammad Naufal Fikri, Azizan Ramli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Current safety risk assessments, such as HIRA (Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment) and FMEA (Failure Mode and Effect Analysis), have several weaknesses. Meanwhile, the House of Risk approach (HOR), commonly applied in the supply chain, has the potential to be adapted to the safety context. Therefore, this study aims to propose a new method called HORshe (House of Risk for Safety, Health, Etc.). As the extension and further development of the original House of Risk (HOR) framework, HORshe is designed to implement SHELL (Software, Hardware, Environment, Liveware), a human factors-based model, and Hierarchy of Control. HORshe consisted of three stages, where stage #1 used SHELL to facilitate the identification of Risk Event (Ei) and stage #2 adapted the Hierarchy of Control and an AHP-style scoring system to determine the priority of Preventive Actions (PAk). Finally, stage #3 was used to conduct a residual risk assessment. To validate the HORshe method, a case study of maintenance activities was carried out at a steam power plant in Indonesia, posing a high risk to worker safety. The results showed 8 Risk Events (Ei), 6 Risk Agents (Aj), and 12 alternative Preventive Actions (PAk), which collectively represent the general risk profile of the scenario.The case study explained that 3 main Risk Agents (Aj) and 3 corresponding Preventive Actions (PAk) aligned with available resources were identified. While the existing HOR method does not include residual risk assessment, HORshe incorporates this step, and the results demonstrated that it reduced average risk exposure by 82.42% compared to the existing condition. Although specific Risk Event (Ei), Risk Agent (Aj), and Preventive Actions (PAk) may differ between case studies, the three-stage framework remains applicable across contexts. Furthermore, HORshe has the potential to be used for assessing risk outside the context of safety, including project risk management, lean manufacturing, and other domains. The key insights from this method include:HORshe implement SHELL, a human factors-based model, and Hierarchy of Control with AHP-style scoring system to determine the priority of Preventive Actions.HORshe consisted of three stages and adaptable for assessing risk outside the context of safety.

Background

Risk assessment generally refers to a mathematical framework or model used to assess the impact of uncertainty [1,2]. This assessment comprises identification of all risk factors, including both unsafe conditions (hazardous work equipment and work environments) and unsafe actions, which refers to careless behavior of workers [[3], [4], [5]]. Furthermore, it estimates the magnitude of risk score based on the combination of two important parameters, namely the level of frequency of occurrence and severity [6,7]. Common methods found in safety risk assessment are HIRA (Hazard Identification and Risk Analysis), FTA (Fault Tree Analysis), JSA (Job Safety Analysis), FMEA (Failure Mode and Effect Analysis), and others. However, these methods have shortcomings, for example, HIRA does not provide details of risky work carried out in the workplace [8]. FMEA uses RPN (Risk Priority Number), which is based on the assessment of Severity (S), Occurrence (O), and Detection (D) [1,2,[9], [10], [11]]. Given that FMEA does not consider weight, the different combinations of S, O, and D scores may produce the same RPN score [12]. Several failure modes in FMEA cannot be distinguished, making it difficult to evaluate the failure mode more precisely [13]. Furthermore, traditional FMEA application, as in human factor–based operation and maintenance often neglects residual risk assessment, although standards such as ISO 14971 recommend its inclusion for comprehensive risk evaluation [14].

A combination of various methods can be carried out to sharpen the risk assessment process. For example, HIRA and FTA have been combined to identify the root cause of accidents in container management [15]. The combination of JSA and HIRA methods produced JSHIRA (Job Safety Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment), which uses JSA to conduct job analysis in the initial procedure, and HIRA for risk assessments, along with recommendations for appropriate control [8].

HOR used in the context of supply chain risk is a combination of FMEA and HOQ (House of Quality) to identify Risk Event (Ei) and analyze Risk Agent (Aj) by considering the multi-relations between both factors. According to various studies, HOR has the potential to be used in other risk contexts, including gas and steam power plant construction projects [16], Light Rail Transit (LRT) construction project [17], preventive maintenance [18], lean manufacturing [19], shipyards [20], and for product development [[21], [22], [23]]. The potential adaptation of HOR for safety risk assessment can improve the sharpness of safety risk assessment in existing methods. This study proposed the HORshe (House of Risk for Safety, Health, Etc.) method to assess risk in the context of occupational safety and health, both holistically and systematically.

Method details

The proposed HORshe method

HORshe is used for organizations, companies, or stakeholders in the context of occupational safety and health to conduct effective and efficient risk assessment in a business process. Aside from occupational safety and health, HORshe can also be used in other contexts, including project risk management, lean manufacturing, and other domains. This method implements the SHELL model to identify the occurring risk. SHELL is a replacement for the 5 Supply Chain major business processes used by HOR. In addition, HORshe applies the Hierarchy of Control as a guide in determining the best alternative priority for Preventive Actions (PAk) [24]. The detection level assessment is considered a form of improvement from the existing HOR model. The basic purpose is to provide a more comprehensive understanding of risk, for possible early prevention [25,26]. Moreover, residual risk assessment is carried out in the HORshe model to analyze the performance of Preventive Actions (PAk) in the field. Residual risk is a derivative concept, which considers the existence of unresolved risk for control actions considered reasonable to have been implemented [27,28]. This risk should be evaluated to determine the status of the remaining risk exposure, thereby providing a more comprehensive assessment approach [14].

Risk assessment using HORshe method tends to be dependent on the subjectivity of the assessor, making it vulnerable to inconsistency. To minimize this bias, the method adopts principles from Focus Group Discussions (FGD) and the Delphi principle by incorporating selected experts as risk assessors. This is particularly important for scoring risk parameters, defining relationships, and other components across each stage. A risk assessor can be selected based on some criterias such as specific expertise (technical and risk), professional experience, years of service, and other relevant qualifications. By applying FGD and Delphi principles in defining severity, occurrence, detection, relation score, etc., HORshe promotes collective consensus rather than relying on individual opinions. This principle resulting in less biased risk assessment outcomes [29]. To enhance the reliability measure and validation of the assessment process, a two-round evaluation can be conducted, in line with the Delphi principle [30]. According to [30,31], Delphi-based risk assessments typically comprise two to four rounds, depending on the progress made toward achieving a reliable consensus.

SHELL in the proposed HORshe method

SHELL is used as a conceptual model to represent various components in a business process within an industrial system [[32], [33], [34]]. The concept was first developed by Edwards in 1972 and then modified by Hawkins in 1975. It helps clarify the location and cause of pani as the main factor causing accidents [32]. The model is coined from the initial letters of each component system (Fig. 1), namely Software, Hardware, Environment, and Liveware, that interact with one another [32,33].Fig. 1The SHELLL model – Human Factors components and interfaces.Fig. 1(Source: [32])

The SHELLL model – Human Factors components and interfaces.

The SHELL model is widely used to analyze the interaction between multiple system components in the aviation industry. The interaction between various components in a complex industrial system is, in principle, related to various Risk Event (Ei) [35]. The SHELL causality model tested on real accidents showed a 30 % increase in the accuracy and effectiveness of human factor identification. More importantly, the term human factors has become increasingly common in recent years among several industries, especially those with a safety focus [[36], [37], [38]]. The use of SHELL model will simplify the risk assessment process as Risk Event (Ei) can be identified in accordance with each type of interaction within industrial system components as follows:•Liveware-Liveware (L-L)

•Liveware-Liveware (L-L)

Liveware-Liveware (L-L)

L-L is an interaction between human factors in an industrial system, generally related to leadership, interpersonal interaction or communication, teamwork, healthy working relationships, and good coordination [32,[39], [40], [41], [42]]. This interaction, whether between cockpit crews and air traffic control officers or with flight attendants, is oriented toward improving communication functions. This new paradigm has principles grounded in assertiveness, management, delegation of tasks, teamwork, leadership/ followers, and decision making [41]. In the healthcare sector, l-L interaction occurs as communication between doctors, nurses, and other personnel in the hospital. This communication interaction emphasizes aspects of honesty, trust, and external participation of the organization with other professional parties [42].•Liveware-Hardware (L-H)

•Liveware-Hardware (L-H)

Liveware-Hardware (L-H)

L-H refers to human interaction with physical attributes such as equipment, machinery, or work facilities. Potential hazards posing threats to humans can be caused by poor equipment that does not comply with quality standards [32,[39], [40], [41], [42]]. l-H interaction occurs between the pilot in the cockpit with the aircraft systems (including the aircraft wings, surface control, along with the entire hydraulic systems, and flight control instruments), thereby creating man-machine interaction. Ergonomic man-machine interaction design reduces the potential for human error that leads to accidents [41,43]. In the healthcare case study, l-H interaction occurs between personnel (doctors, nurses, and staff) and medical equipment, enhancing these interactions through structured training is crucial to reducing human error and ensuring patient safety [42].•Liveware Software (L-S)

•Liveware Software (L-S)

Liveware Software (L-S)

L-S describes human interaction with support systems detected in the workplace, namely regulations, work instructions, SOPs, and computer software. l-S interactions include issues such as accuracy, format and presentation, vocabulary, clarity, use of symbols, and others [32,[39], [40], [41], [42]]. In the aviation industry, interactions occur when pilots access the necessary information on the tablet (contain procedures, maps, flight documents, and checklists). Easy access to information on the tablet facilitates the pilot work [41]. In healthcare, l-S interaction involves regulations, software systems, SOPs, and checklists, requiring strict supervision and regular training to minimize human error and adverse events. [42,43].•Liveware Environment (L-E)

•Liveware Environment (L-E)

Liveware Environment (L-E)

L-E comprises human interaction with the internal and external environment of an industrial system. The internal environment includes temperature, lighting, noise, vibration, and air quality. The external environment includes operational aspects such as infrastructure [32,[39], [40], [41], [42]]. Psychological and physiological effects such as illness, fatigue, financial uncertainty, relationship, and career problems may be caused by l-E interactions. Organizational attributes in the work environment may cause stress, significantly increasing deviations from work processes or SOP [32,44].

In the aviation industry, many cases of human error that cause accidents are related to l-E interactions, such as flying an aircraft over hills, between other aircraft, and in bad weather conditions. To overcome the high number of accidents and deaths, the industry is committed to reducing risk by increasing l-E interaction through safety-based computerized technology [41]. In hospital case studies, this interaction is related to layout design, lighting, and ventilation. Adequate environmental factors support good workflow and time management. Meanwhile, exposure to viruses in l-E interaction will have a significant impact on hospital personnel [42].

Hierarchy of control in the proposed HORshe method

The current HOR method has limitations in terms of hazard control, which can be addressed by implementing Hierarchy of Control approach. This concept is a standard in safety risk management, offering a systematic framework for identifying and managing hazards based on priority solutions [24]. It supports the Prevention through Design (PtD) initiative inaugurated by National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) to emphasize the importance of preventing or reducing work-related injuries, diseases, and fatalities in all designs that impact safety [45].

The Hierarchy of Control in Table 2 offers a priority of control strategies, including elimination and personal protective equipment, to provide structured guidance in addressing hazards. In the HORshe method, this principle was operationalized through the weighting of control hierarchy levels. This weighting process used the AHP scale to prioritize the Hierarchy of Control from the most effective level (Elimination) to the lowest level of effectiveness by using Personal Protective Equipment/ PPE [46]. The scale applied in AHP has a significant impact on the prioritization of criteria [47]. The purpose of this scale is to ensure that the priority ratios among the criteria are proportional, ensuring the Hierarchy of Control with the highest risk reduction effectiveness will receive the greatest priority weight. The implementation of Hierarchy of Control is expected to foster safer, more efficient, and sustainable business processes [24]. When the risk score is considered extremely high, additional mitigation measures must be taken until an acceptable risk level is achieved [51,52] Although OSH laws require complete workplace safety, it is practically impossible to eliminate all risk [53]. Risk management incurs significant costs for the affected company [54], hence, the Hierarchy of Control assists in determining the proper control strategy.

Stages in the proposed HORshe method

To facilitate risk assessment using HORshe, the spreadsheet template is available at the link: https://its.id/HORshe. This spreadsheet application can be reliably run within the Microsoft Excel 365 environment. With this spreadsheet, risk assessors are able to conduct evaluations and compute risk scores in an automated manner. HORshe method application

Case study illustration

The newly developed method, HORshe, was applied to conduct risk assessment of steam power plant company in Indonesia as a case study. This aims to demonstrate its applicability and to provide clear guidance on how the HORshe method can be applied. The following case study descriptions are useful to provide an overview of the risks faced. The description obtained from direct observation, secondary data review, and focus group discussion (FGD) involving the experts of maintenance, engineering, operational, and OSH teams from a steam power plant company. These experts in the HORshe method act as risk assessors.

The case study used was two steam power generation units with a capacity of 400 MW each. In the process of producing electricity, certain machines, such as turbines, boilers, and generators, pose a very high risk towards the safety of workers. Steam power plants pose a high safety risk due to the emission of heat, noise, vibration, fire, and even explosions when the machinery experiences problems. For the smooth running of the electricity generation process, good maintenance is highly required. The specific topics in the case study was related to turbine maintenance. The turbine is a very important component because it functions to rotate the generator in producing electricity. Aside from the crucial function in electricity production, the turbine can cause an explosion when not properly maintained.

The internal conditions in the turbine maintenance area comprise numerous activities carried out in accordance with standard operating procedures (SOPs) and work permits. However, the level of occupational risk is relatively high due to continuous exposure to heat, vibration, and noise generated by maintenance activities and surrounding machinery. Maintenance is conducted on turbine units located in confined areas, thereby increasing the risk of workers becoming trapped in narrow spaces. The maintenance team consists of both power plant’s employee and third-party contractors, supervised by a joint occupational health and safety (OHS) team. Despite the implementation of joint supervision, incidents of worker negligence and non-compliance with SOPs remain frequent, particularly with regard to the use of personal protective equipment (PPE). Sometime safety officer is also negligent in supervising the maintenance team. Several occupational risks during maintenance activities may include body parts being caught in rotating equipment within the turbine area, falling objects during lifting operations, exposure to coal dust from the coal yard, and inhalation of glass wool particles due to degradation of insulation materials intended to serve as thermal barriers.

An external condition affecting the risk level is the tight scheduling policies set by the holding company to ensure the continuity of electricity supply. These scheduling pressures can affect the overall effectiveness of the maintenance process and onsite risk control. The availability of spare parts depends on coordination with external vendors, and any delays or disruptions may negatively impact maintenance readiness. Furthermore, the presence of additional temporary workers during maintenance periods requires intensive coordination with security personnel and local communities to maintain order in and around the plant area. Another external condition affecting the risk level is the tropical climate in the coastal region where the steam power plant is located, which contributes to high ambient temperatures and increases the risk of heat exposure for workers.

The case study illustration presented serves as a foundation for identifying critical information, particularly in recognizing Risk Event (Ei) and Risk Agent (Aj). All stages of HORshe were carried out based on the results of focus group discussions involving experts. Result and discussion of HORshe stage #1

Risk Event (Ei) identification was carried out by analyzing maintenance activities and identifying any risk in the turbine area. As briefly outlined in sub-section 3.1, there are 8 Risk Events (Ei) that occur in the turbine maintenance area. Please refers to Table 12 below to know the Risk Event (Ei) identified.

In the next step, the Severity (Si) of each Risk Event (Ei) was assessed, and the results are also shown in Table 12. During the assessment, a score of 1–5 was assigned, where 1 indicates a relatively small impact, and 5 implies a very large and significant impact for the company. For instance, highlighted in yellow shading, E3 (a worker is hit by an object during lifting process) has a severity score of 3. The provision of this moderate score is based on the level of severity, suggesting the worker requires medical treatment. This will affect the productivity factor of the victimized worker and cause large financial losses.

After identifying the Risk Event (Ei) and determining the Severity level (Si), then Risk Agent (Aj) identification along with its Occurrence (Oj) and Detection (Dj) score was carried out through FGD with experts/ risk assessors. Only relevant root causes of Risk Event (Ei) can become Risk Agent (Aj). Table 13 shows 6 Risk Agents (Aj) along with Occurrence (Oj) and Detection (Dj) score in turbine maintenance activities.

The Occurrence (Oj) score represents the probability of each Risk Agent (Aj) occurring. The occurrence assessment used a score of 1–5, which respectively indicate categories ranging from rare to almost certain. The detection assessment was also carried out on a scale of 1–5, which respectively indicate detection levels ranging from those easily performed through simple visual inspection to those difficult to perform due to the lack of effective methods. For instance, highlighted in yellow shading, is A6 (workers’ limited knowledge of hazards), which has an Occurrence (Oj) score of 2 and a Detection (Dj) score of 3. In the real conditions, there are few workers still have little knowledge about workplace hazards, thereby endangering safety. Meanwhile, the Detection (Dj) score of 3 is assigned because such Risk Agent (Aj) are moderate possibility to identify after conducting a sequence of knowledge tests.

In the next step, the relation score between Risk Agent (Aj) and Risk Event (Ei) was arranged using a score of 0,1,3,9 (Table 14). For example, highlighted with orange shading in Table 14, a score of 9 for the relation A6 (workers’ limited knowledge of hazards) with E3 (worker is hit by work object during lifting process) indicates that workers who have little knowledge about workplace hazards will have a stronger tendency of being hit by an object during lifting process.

Based on the severity, occurrence, detection score, and relationship score, the Aggregate Risk Potential (ARPj) score of each Risk Agent (Aj) can be calculated. For example, the ARPj score of A6 can be calculated using the formula (1):ARPj=0jDj∑iSiRijARP6=O6×D6×[S1(R16)+S2(R26)+S3(R36)+S4(R46)+S6(R66)+S7(R76)+S8(R86)]ARP6=2×3×[2(3)+3(9)+3(9)+2(1)+2(9)+3(3)+3(1)]ARP6=2×3×[6+27+27+2+18+9+3]=552

This calculation results are presented in Table 14, highlighted in yellow shading.

Based on the calculation from formula 1, ARP6 has a value of 552. Compared to other risk agents, this value is relatively high, where ARP6 ranks second-highest. As shown in Table 14, the results of ARPj score calculation, range from 920 (ARP3 as the first rank) to 99 (ARP5 as the last rank). The Pareto diagram of ARPj score for all Risk Agents (Aj) is shown in Fig. 3. By using the pareto principle flexibly, 3 highest Risk Agent (Aj) out of 6 were selected by the risk assessor as priority Risk Agent (Aj) which will be continued in HORshe stage #2. Highlighted with red box in Fig. 3, the three prioritized Risk Agent (Aj) consecutively are A3, A6, and A2.

Result and discussion of HORshe stage #2

To determine Preventive Actions (PAk) for the case study under discussion, the output from Pareto diagram of the HORshe Stage #1 serves as the foundation for the assessment in HORshe Stage #2. Based on the Pareto results, 3 Risk Agent (Aj) with the highest ARPj score, namely A3, A6, and A2, were selected for further analysis. A total of 12 alternative Preventive Actions (PAk) in Table 15 were successfully formulated through focus group discussion with risk assessors to respond A3, A6, and A2. The formulation of Preventive Action (PAk) was carried out by considering the Hierarchy of Control, from the highest category level as first priority to the lowest one. Several alternative Preventive Actions (PAk) can be generated to address a Risk Agent (Aj). For example A6, the experts managed to formulate 5 Preventive Actions (PAk) ranging from the highest category level in the hierarchy of control, namely elimination, to personal protective equipment as the lowest category level. In Table 15, 5 alternatives of Preventive Actions (PAk) for A6 have been presented in the yellow shading highlight. In fact, the experts are challenged to be able to recommend the highest-level Preventive Action (PAk), namely the hazard elimination category, because of its most significant impact in controlling the hazard. Preventive Actions (PAk) that are the most effective for controlling A6, workers' limited knowledge of hazards, are disqualifying workers who fail the test (PA4).

Table 16 shows the complete results for HORshe stage #2. Table 16 displays the PA Scoring (SCk) which shows the weight of each Preventive Action (PAk) based on the AHP scale (9, 7, 5, 3, or 1). Elimination receives the highest score due its most effectiveness in risk controlling, while personal protective equipment receives the lowest one. For example, highlighted with yellow shading, PA4 (disqualifying workers who fail the test) in Table 16, which is an elimination category in the hierarchy of control, received the highest score of 9.

Table 16 also shows the relationship score between Preventive Action (PAk) and Risk Agent (Aj) as 0, 1, 3, or 9, representing no relationship, weak relationship, moderate relationship, and very strong relationship, respectively. For instance, in yellow shading, PA4 (disqualifying workers who fail the test) has a strong relationship to solve A6 (workers' limited knowledge of hazards) resulting in a score of 9. Meanwhile, disqualifying (PA4) is not necessarily the best option for Risk Agent A2, as workers who lack of focus during maintenance tasks may still be managed through disciplinary measures, thus, it has a medium relationship and resulting in a score of 3. HORshe stage #2 does not stop at the TEk calculation, because a high Total Effectiveness (TEk) does not guarantee that the Preventive Action (PAk) can maximally control the Risk Agent (Aj), especially if the Preventive Action (PAk) turns out to be difficult to execute due to many obstacles. As a part of “quick win”, HORshe stage #2 facilitates the calculation of Effectiveness to Difficulty Ratio (ETDk) so that the Preventive Action (PAk) implemented is not only based on its effectiveness to control the Risk Agent (Aj) but also considering the degree of difficulty level of implementation (Dk). The last column in Table 16 shows the Rank of PA Priority (Rk) for each Preventive Action (PAk). Its rank was determined based on the score of the ETDk ratio. The higher the ratio (ETDk), the better the PAk in controlling risk and the more feasible it is to implement.

In the actual implementation of Preventive Actions (PAk), resource limitations often pose a critical constraint. The highest Preventive Actions (PAk) scores represent impactful solutions, strongly related to dominant Risk Agent (Aj). In some cases, certain Preventive Actions (PAk) may have closely ranked scores. Consequently, risk assessors/ experts may consider the implementation priority Preventive Actions (PAk) based on Pareto identification. At the same time, the risk assessors may also evaluate the possibility of allocating additional resources to increase the number of Preventive Actions (PAk) implemented. This process should be carried out based on the consensus of the risk assessors/ experts. For this turbine maintenance case study, based on Table 16, the risk assessor decided to focus on implementing PA4, PA1, and PA9 due to limited resources. Based on the results, the most effective and efficient (feasible) Preventive Action (PAk) is PA4, which is disqualifying incompetent workers based on the established passing standards (highlighted with yellow shading in Table 16). In the Hierarchy of Control, PA4, included in the elimination category, is the most effective Preventive Actions (PAk) in controlling existing risk, due to its highest ETDk score, 18.468. It is also easy to implement due to the fewer resources required. PA1, the next rank of chosen Preventive Actions (PAk), is replacing all heat, noise, and dust insulation materials with better insulation materials. Meanwhile, in the third place, PA9 is to substitute unfocused workers with those who are better prepared to carry out maintenance work. PA1 and PA9 highlighted with orange shading in Table 16. The three highest Preventive Actions (PAk) successfully demonstrated the advantage of the hierarchy of control application, by prioritizing the top 2 levels in controlling the hazards, namely elimination and substitution.

In the hierarchy of control, elimination and substitution are the highest-priority options. Therefore, the Preventive Actions (PAk) scores for these two options in HORshe are the highest, with elimination scored at 9 and substitution at 7. However, implementation is often limited by practical constraints, as the hazard is typically an integral part of the system. For example, the magnetic field in MRI is a core technology of the device, hence, eliminating it would compromise the function of the MRI [69]. In the chemical industry [70], flammable hydrogen is difficult to substitute because it is currently the safest and most efficient option, and already integrated into the production system. Substitution would be costly and could interrupt operations. Elimination and substitution should be prioritized when designing Preventive Actions (PAk) but practical and technical limitations remain a challenge.

In the HORshe stage #3, residual risk assessment was carried out as continuous improvement efforts towards the implementation of selected alternative Preventive Actions (PAk) from HORshe stage #2. The assessment has similar steps as HORshe stage #1. The New Severity (Si), New Occurrence (Oj), and New Detection (Dj) scores may/ may not be reduced significantly, depending on the effectiveness of implementing the Preventive Action (PAk) in reality. Following the consensus of the risk assessors, PA4, PA1, and PA9 were identified as the selected solutions from HORshe stage #2 to be assessed for its residual risk. The implementation of each Preventive Action (PAk) would be monitored and observed for changes in residual risk. On the other hand, when the risk is not considered ALARP, a decision must be made on whether the current Preventive Actions (PAk) are still sufficient to manage the risk or whether updated solutions and a reassessment process are required, starting again from HORshe stage #1.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents a new method for conducting safety risk assessment. The turbine maintenance case study in a high-risk industry was presented as an illustrative case for initial implementation. The results showed 8 crucial Risk Event (Ei) in turbine maintenance and 6 Risk Agent (Aj) as root causes. A total of 12 alternative Preventive Actions (PAk) were successfully formulated through focus group discussion. The experts decided to focus on implementing PA4, PA1, and PA9. The residual risk assessment indicated a significant reduction in the New Aggregate Risk Potential (NARPj). According to Table 17, the risk level was reduced by 85.86 % for A2, 82.07 % for A3, and 79.35 % for A6. On average, this indicates a total risk reduction of 82.42 % following the implementation of the three PAk (PA4, PA1, and PA9).

The proposed method is a development of the original HOR model with adjustments to the implementation in the context of Occupational Safety and Health. HORshe is a more holistic and systematic method in assessing safety risk compared to similar methods. Due to the generic nature, the method can be applied to any type of company without the need for a significant change. The HORshe stages will remain the same, even though Risk Event (Ei), Risk Agent (Aj), and Preventive Actions (PAk) may differ between case studies. Good collaboration in a cross-functional expert team is essential to arrive at a sharp risk assessment, both in defining Risk Event (Ei), Risk Agent (Aj), as well as determining Severity (Si), Occurrence (Oj), Detection (Dj), and the score of Relation (Rij) in HORshe. Moreover, formulating appropriate alternative Preventive Actions (PAk) to select the most effective and efficient (feasible) PAk, also requires analytical acumen from the expert/ risk assessor team. Aside from being applied to the context of occupational safety and health, HORshe can also be used in other contexts, such as project risk management, lean manufacturing, etc..

Limitations & future research

This article aims to introduce a new method in risk assessment. Therefore, the discussion is limited to a single case study examined in detail. The safety risks assessment in turbine maintenance are used as the illustrative application of the HORshe method. Based on the consensus of risk assessors/ experts, PA4, PA1, and PA9 assumed can be carried out quite effectively. Assumptions were due to time constraints for waiting the real results of Preventive Actions (PAk) implementation when this article was written. In the future, this assumption should be avoided to accurately capture field conditions. Therefore, future research should evaluate residual risk of the selected Preventive Actions (PAk) after real implementation. This would provide a complete depiction of the HORshe method since stages #1 to stage #3.

HORshe can be used also to assess risk outside the safety context, such as the project-based sector, where a potential Risk Event (Ei) may occur when a project officer fails to consider regulatory changes, representing a Liveware-Environment (L-E) interaction. This Risk Event (Ei) is driven by Risk Agent (Aj), namely changes in political and economic conditions combined with a lack of competent resources. It can be mitigated by applying periodic project performance evaluations as a Preventive Action (PAk), which reflects an administrative control. Another Risk Event (Ei) may arise when the project officer fails to design according to standards (L-S interaction), caused by insufficient supervision and resource capacity as Aj. The same Preventive Action (PAk) from the previous Risk Event (Ei) can also be applied in this context. Meanwhile, in the lean manufacturing sector, Risk Event (Ei) may include material buildup due to inefficient manual material handling (L-H interaction) and production defects resulting from poor interdepartmental communication (L-L interaction). These Risk Event (Ei) are driven by the existence of Non–Value-Added Activities (NVAA) as a Risk Agent (Aj), which can be addressed through unnecessary motion removal as an elimination-level control. In cases outside the safety context, the philosophy of the hierarchy of control may not be easily applicable. Therefore, the risk assessor does not need to determine Preventive Actions (PAk) rigidly based on the terminology of elimination, substitution, engineering control, administrative control, or PPE. PA Scoring (SCk) can be used by considering each level of preventive action effectiveness, starting from the most effective (score 9) to the lowest level of effectiveness (score 1). The implementation examples in the project and lean manufacturing sectors indicate a potential direction for further studies, suggesting that HORshe is a generic risk assessment instrument adaptable to different sector-specific cases. Thus, future studies may take this opportunity to apply the HORshe method to their respective industrial cases, such as project-based sectors, lean manufacturing, construction, healthcare, petrochemical, public services, and others.
