MDPD reveals specific microbial signatures in human pulmonary diseases
Authors: Paramita Roy, Dibakar Roy, Sudipto Bhattacharjee, Abhirupa Ghosh, Sudipto Saha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulmonary diseases are becoming a serious threat worldwide, and enormous data from different human microbiomes have been generated to understand these complex diseases. Here, we introduce Microbiome Database of Pulmonary Diseases (MDPD), an open-access, comprehensive systemic catalog of pulmonary diseases by manually curating global studies from 2012 to 2024 (13 years). We have compiled 59 362 runs from 430 BioProjects, encompassing data from 10 body sites related to 19 pulmonary diseases and healthy groups covering 278 distinct sub-groups. MDPD enables users to analyze each BioProject and customize analysis with multiple BioProjects to identify taxonomic profiles and disease group/sub-group specific microbial signatures. The re-analyzed intermediate Biological Observation Matrix files are provided for each BioProject for the accessibility of users for further applications, such as machine learning-based classification. Identified microbes (bacteria, fungi, viruses) in MDPD are annotated with several attributes, providing further insights into their disease-causing potential and specificity to certain diseases and body sites. MDPD is freely available at: https://bicresources.jcbose.ac.in/ssaha4/mdpd/.Keywords: pulmonary diseases, metagenomics, microbial signatures, database, BIOM files

Introduction

The human microbiomes provide insight into the complex interplay of microbes and their products, such as metabolites and peptides, in pulmonary diseases. Over the past years, respiratory microbiome studies have been growing [1–3] with the advent of sequencing technologies [4], as these culture-independent methods can capture a wider range of microbes, which play a crucial role in maintaining host physiology [5]. In this context, research on pulmonary diseases, which are life-threatening as well as lifestyle affecting, has also gained attention in the post-COVID era due to the unprecedented global impact of COVID-19 on the respiratory system. Moreover, pulmonary diseases like asthma [6], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [7] which were already a global burden in the healthcare system with higher morbidity and mortality, were observed to have worsened outcomes in the post-COVID era [8]. Other pulmonary diseases, such as pulmonary hypertension (PH) [9], tuberculosis [10], idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [11, 12] and malignancies like lung cancer [13] also pose potential threats to respiratory health.

Different body site microbiomes have been explored in recent years in pulmonary diseases using amplicon sequencing and whole metagenome sequencing (WMS) to understand their role in disease pathogenesis and the identification of microbial biomarkers or targets [14, 15]. Not only bacteria, but also fungi [16] and viruses [17] play a significant and distinct role in pulmonary disease, as evident from some studies. Many studies connect imbalance in the microbial communities in several body sites like the oral [18], nasal [19], upper and lower respiratory tract [20, 21], lung [22], gut [23], to different pulmonary diseases, including obstructive [24], restrictive [25], infectious [19, 26], vascular [27], and others [28], inferring a critical role of microbiome. As a result, an ample amount of raw sequencing data has been generated and deposited in several public databases in the last decade. Thus, a standardized analysis of these existing data can provide new insights to improve our understanding of pulmonary diseases.

To enhance our knowledge about the vital role the microbiome plays in health and disease, it is crucial to access data from a diverse range of studies and cohorts. Collecting and processing microbiome data from public resources is essential for the challenging yet necessary task of standardizing this information and making it accessible to all. The databases are considered a useful resource for accessing relevant data, and there are many databases that provide information on the microbiome and its relation to diseases. Despite having a good number of databases, there is a lack of a microbiome database focusing on pulmonary diseases. Recently many microbiome databases became available with three very relevant databases namely the data repository for Gut Microbiota v2 (GMrepo v2) [29], Lung Disease Microorganisms Database (LDMD) [30] and mBodyMap [31] but they fall short in pulmonary diseases. Still, to our knowledge, no comprehensive database contains all types of microbiome information of wide range of pulmonary diseases.

Here, we introduce an open-access database MDPD - Microbiome Database of Pulmonary Diseases, a first effort to integrate microbiome data from 10 body sites studied in a wide range of pulmonary diseases. In 2010, the first-ever study reported lung microbiome has a distinct role in pulmonary diseases like asthma and COPD [32]. After that, a large amount of microbiome data from both amplicon sequencing and WMS for pulmonary diseases was generated. A pilot study with these data (WMS data comprising 55 runs from five diseases) has shown significant differences in the microbial profiles across pulmonary diseases [33]. There is a huge amount of data across various types of microbiomes studied in different pulmonary diseases like asthma or cystic fibrosis, but they are studied individually, thus making it challenging to compare across pulmonary diseases. To address this issue, and to enable accessibility and re-usability of the ample amount of available metagenomic data of pulmonary diseases, we processed amplicon (16S and ITS) and WMS microbiome data from a wide range of samples and compiled them into a single platform.

This study provides two main contributions to enable the identification of pulmonary disease-specific microbial signatures. First, it compiles large-scale metagenomic data on pulmonary diseases (including 278 defined sub-groups) on one platform, MDPD. Secondly, MDPD includes individual BioProject-wise analysis of taxonomic profile, differential abundance, multivariate association, and co-occurrence. In addition, pooled BioProjects analyses were used to identify specific microbial signatures of these disease groups and sub-groups. Here, identified microbes include not only bacteria but also viruses, fungi, and others, and are annotated with their pathogenic potential and distribution across body sites and disease sub-groups. Together, the ample data source and on-the-fly analyses in MDPD are a much-needed resource for pulmonary disease research, focusing on microbiome community profiling.

Methods

Data acquisition of sequencing reads and manual curation of metadata

A list of BioProjects was obtained for 10 different body sites of 19 pulmonary diseases broadly categorized into obstructive, restrictive, infectious, vascular, and malignancy, along with healthy (smoker and non-smoker) groups from PubMed using ‘Advanced PubMed Search.’ The search terms used are listed in Supplementary File 1: Fig. S1. Additionally, the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) BioProject database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject) was searched with disease names. Relevant BioProject IDs with proper metadata were obtained from the literature search (Supplementary File 1: Fig. S2) and the NCBI BioProject Database. Data curation was done till November 2024. The curated metadata includes valuable information for analysis, like sequencing information, phenotype (disease, healthy, or control), data type (Amplicon-16S/Amplicon-ITS/WMS), and many more. PRISMA workflow for data curation (Supplementary File 1: Fig. S3) is explained in Supplementary File 1: S1.

Downloading and processing of raw data

Raw sequencing reads were downloaded either from the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) or using a tool, namely ‘Faster Download and Extract Reads in FASTQ format from NCBI SRA’ [34], on the Galaxy [35] web server in FASTQ format. Next, Trim Galore! [36] was used for quality control and adapter trimming of the raw reads.

Taxonomic assignment

For amplicon data, FASTQ files were processed to obtain an amplicon sequence variant table using the Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm 2 (DADA2) [37] version 1.26 in R. The default filtering parameters were used to filter out low-quality reads and chimeras. The SILVA nr99 v138.1 [38] for 16S data and General FASTA release files from the UNITE ITS database (4.04.24) [39] for ITS data were used to assign taxonomy. For WMS data, taxonomic assignment was done using Kraken2 [40] (version 2.1.1) with a confidence threshold of 0.1. Prebuilt RefSeq indexes: PlusPF (standard plus protozoa and fungi) (Version: 2022-06-07 – Downloaded: 2022–09-04165121Z) database was for mapping WMS data. Bracken (with reference database Prebuilt Refseq indexes: PlusPF (Version: 2022-06-07, k-mer: 35, Read: 100)) was used to re-estimate abundance at the species level from Kraken2 output. Kraken-style reports were generated and merged with a metadata file to generate a Biological Observation Matrix (BIOM) file using the kraken-biom tool [41]. This taxonomic assignment pipeline was performed on the Galaxy web platform. Single-end and paired-end data were processed separately for both amplicon and WMS data. Some runs with low-quality reads (<1% annotation) were excluded. The percentage cut-off of processed reads and corresponding data loss is shown in Supplementary File 1: Table S1.

BIOM file generation

DADA2 and kraken2 generated output files, i.e., BIOM files, were saved as R Data Serialization (RDS) files for each BioProject-assay type combination. These BIOM files were further used for downstream analyses using various R packages and tools. A quality control step was applied in the downstream analysis, where filtering was done based on abundance (0.01%) and detection (5%) in all individual BioProjects, as used in previous studies [42, 43]. BIOM files are not available for a few BioProjects as they could not be advanced to subsequent analysis due to various reasons. A comprehensive list of these excluded BioProjects, along with the corresponding rationale for their exclusion, is provided in Supplementary File 1: Table S2.

The workflow outlines the steps taken to develop a systemic catalog of pulmonary diseases (Supplementary File 1: Fig. S4).

Discriminant analysis

Discriminant analysis was performed to identify statistically significant differentially abundant taxa distinguishing one disease group or sub-group from another. For discriminant analysis, four methods, namely LEfSe [44], ALDEx2 (t-test) [45], LinDA [46] and ANCOM-BC2 [47] were implemented using the R package microeco [48]. The analysis methods and parameters are adjustable as shown in Supplementary File 1: Fig. S5, with the default settings configured as follows: Method = ‘LEfSe (without FDR p-value adjustment)’, p-value = 0.1, Filter threshold = 0.0001, Taxa level = ‘Genus’, and Cut-off value = 2. For the LEfSe method, the default cut-off value is a log10(LDA score) ≥ 2. In user-defined discriminant analysis with multiple BioProjects, batch effect correction was done using the ‘adjust_batch’ function of MMUPHin [49] package.

Multivariate association analysis

Multivariate association analysis was conducted for the BioProjects, consisting of confounding factors such as gender, age group, and smoking status. The MaAsLin2 [50] package, which utilizes a linear mixed-effects model to find correlated microbiome data with covariate data, was employed with default parameters for this analysis.

Microbial co-occurrence analysis

Microbial co-occurrence analysis was conducted for each BioProject sub-group to identify microbial association networks while reducing the impact of spurious correlations from the compositional effects of microbiome data. In order to achieve this, we employed General Boosted Linear Models (GBLM) [51], a complex network inference algorithm along with bootstrap and renormalization (ReBoot) to scrutinize for spurious interactions. GBLM was implemented over 100 iterations to get stable edge weights. Additionally, Spearman correlation-based network inference coupled to ‘ReBoot’ was employed. Edge weights were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini and Hochberg FDR (False Discovery Rate) procedure, and only edges with a corrected p-value <0.05 were considered [51–53]. R packages mboost and boot were used for generating the networks.

Taxa annotation

Taxa annotation was conducted using various methods tailored to different domains. For bacteria, data was extracted from an R package called bugphyzz [54] and a relevant database [55]. In the case of Eukaryota and Archaea, manual curation was performed using information from literature and Wikipedia. For viruses, literature search and the ICTV database [56] were utilized. The NCBI taxonomy ID of each identified taxon was fetched using the R package taxonomizr. A set of bacterial species was annotated with their pulmonary disease association information using the database MDIPID [57].

Machine learning utilising BIOM files

Two datasets of COPD with stable and exacerbation sub-groups were selected based on Amplicon-16S data of sputum microbiome: Dataset 1 includes 12 relevant BioProjects of COPD consisting 1388 runs (Supplementary File 1: Table S3), while Dataset 2 consists of clean data consisting 1235 runs of the same 12 BioProjects after application of specific inclusion and exclusion criteria (Supplementary File 1: Table S4). Here, the BioProjects were pooled to generate the datasets, as machine learning (ML) has the ability to capture the complex, hidden patterns in microbiome data. Thereafter, to minimize heterogeneity across studies, batch effect correction was performed on each dataset using the R package MMUPHin and the ‘adjust_batch’ function, where the BioProjects were treated as the batch variables (Supplementary File 1: Fig. S6). Finally, the batch-effect-corrected abundance matrix was used as input for machine learning (ML). This input data was split into an 80–20 proportion using the splitstackshape [58] R package, and stratified splitting was performed based on BioProject and sub-group (COPD stable/COPD exacerbation) to ensure proportional distribution of samples from each study and each sub-group. Additionally, a fixed seed was maintained to ensure the reproducibility of the splits. 80% of the data was used for training and testing using 5-fold cross-validation (CV). Here, in each iteration, the dataset was divided randomly into five subsets, where one subset was used as the test set and the remaining four were used as the training set. This process was repeated 5 times, and the average of the results was calculated. The remaining 20% was used as a blind/independent set for validation. Four classical ML algorithms, namely Naïve Bayes [59], Generalized Linear Model [60], Support Vector Machine [61], Random Forest (RF) [62], and an artificial neural network-based model Multilayer perceptron [63] were used for model training–testing and blind-set validation using the R package caret and hyperparameter tuning was also done to obtain the best-performing models.

Implementation of the database and the web application

The database was designed as a relational model and implemented using My Structured Query Language (MySQL). The database mainly constitutes of six tables, namely (i) bioproject, (ii) run, (iii) disease, (iv) taxa, (v) abundance_body_site, and (vi) abundance_subgroup. The attributes of the tables and the logical relationships among the tables are depicted in the database schema provided in Supplementary File 1: Fig. S7.

A web application was developed for users to access the database and perform the analyses from a web browser. The web pages were written with Hyper Text Markup Language, Cascaded Style Sheets, and JavaScript. The server-side processing was performed with Hypertext Preprocessor (PHP): PHP scripts. For the operations that require database querying, the PHP scripts are responsible for establishing a connection with the MySQL server, querying the database with appropriate MySQL query statements, and processing/displaying the output. The database and the web application were deployed on a web server (‘Apache HTTP’) running with Linux operating system. The BIOM files were also stored in the web server. The analyses are performed with scripts written in R. All analyses (except microbial co-occurrence analyses and Krona plots) are performed dynamically (on-the-fly) from the stored BIOM files, and do not require any pre-computation. For serving the analysis queries, the PHP scripts call the appropriate R script by passing the input parameters as command-line arguments, and processes/displays the analysis output—received in JavaScript Object Notation format from the R script. A help page is provided to explain how users can access MDPD (http://bicresources.jcbose.ac.in/ssaha4/mdpd/help.html). The tools and libraries used in the implementation are given in Supplementary File 1: Table S5. Moreover, a data submission page is provided for users to submit new data (https://bicresources.jcbose.ac.in/ssaha4/mdpd/submission.php).

Detailed instruction on how to navigate the database is provided in Supplementary File 1: Fig. S8. Comprehensive information of the web application is provided in Supplementary File 1: S2 and snapshots of home, browse and analysis pages are shown in Supplementary File 1: Figs. S9–S11. The information about curated metadata and availability of BIOM files is given in Supplementary File 1: S3.

Results

MDPD: A comprehensive database of pulmonary microbiome studies

MDPD is a comprehensive resource of re-analysed data from several human microbiome niches associated with a broad range, i.e., 19 pulmonary diseases and also a healthy group. The database has been developed with data from studies (430 BioProjects comprising 59,362 runs) covering 13 years, where a two-fold increase in the number of BioProjects has been observed in the last five years after the outbreak of COVID-19, as shown in Fig. 1a.

MDPD, a manually curated database of experimentally derived microbiome studies of pulmonary diseases. (a) The bar plot shows the distribution of BioProjects (n = 430) and runs (n = 59 362) incorporated in MDPD, spanning around 13 years. (b) The choropleth shows the distribution of runs across the globe (55 countries). (c) The 10 different body site microbiomes and the number of runs included in the database. (d) The proportions of runs in MDPD from the healthy group and 19 pulmonary diseases, categorized into six broad types: Infectious, obstructive, restrictive, vascular, malignancy, and healthy where the obstructive and restrictive pulmonary diseases comprise 60% runs. (e) The number of runs from various groups is represented, where colours represent six broad categories. Notably, cystic fibrosis and COVID-19 contribute predominantly with 23% and 16% of runs. (f) Distribution of runs across three sequencing assay types, with 79% amplicon-16S data, 13% WMS data, and 8% amplicon-ITS data.

These studies on pulmonary disease microbiomes come from nearly 55 countries worldwide, with a significant number originating in the United States (26% runs) and China (19% runs) (Fig. 1b). The database comprises data of 10 different body sites (Fig. 1c), enlisting 58 sample types or isolation sources, with the majority from lower respiratory tract (37.1%), upper respiratory tract (24.7%), and gut (19%) samples. The detailed information of curated body sites, their corresponding isolation sources, and the number of runs and their corresponding BioProjects is shown in Supplementary File 1: Table S6. Lower respiratory tract samples are predominant in diseases such as COPD and cystic fibrosis, while upper respiratory tract samples are more common in COVID-19 and cystic fibrosis. In contrast, lung (tissue) samples are mainly found in lung cancer cases, and gut samples are prevalent in asthma and COVID-19 (Supplementary File 1: Fig. S12).

The human microbiome data of the pulmonary diseases in MDPD is broadly categorized into six groups namely obstructive diseases (30% runs), restrictive diseases (30% runs), infectious diseases (25% runs), vascular diseases (5% runs), malignancy (5% runs) and healthy (5% runs), where obstructive and restrictive groups taken together contribute most (60%) as shown in Fig. 1d. A large number of studies have been conducted on cystic fibrosis (13,579 runs), COVID-19 (9585 runs), asthma (6335 runs), COPD (5921 runs) and lung cancer (4581 runs) whereas studies on PH (277 runs), ARDS (209 runs) and some others are comparatively fewer (Fig. 1e). The disease groups consist of a total of 278 defined sub-groups categorized by disease severity stages or endotypes. Some notable sub-groups include: eosinophilic and non-eosinophilic asthma sub-groups; and adenocarcinoma and small cell carcinoma sub-groups of lung cancer. The disease groups and all their respective sub-groups are illustrated in Supplementary File 2.

Among the studies, the most prevalent study designs are case-control (25.35% BioProjects), case only (24.19% BioProjects) and prospective study (18.14% BioProjects). A detailed classification of all study types and their prevalence is shown in Supplementary File 1: Table S7. Out of the total runs, the majority were generated by Amplicon-16S sequencing (79% runs), the most convenient sequencing technique, and the remaining by Amplicon-ITS sequencing (8% runs) and WMS (13% runs), as shown in Fig. 1f.

Thus, MDPD fulfils the unmet need that provides a comprehensive landscape between human microbiome and pulmonary diseases (comparison with other relevant databases: Supplementary File 1: S4 and Supplementary File 1: Table S8). The database is freely available at https://bicresources.jcbose.ac.in/ssaha4/mdpd and all other detailed information about data statistics is available at https://bicresources.jcbose.ac.in/ssaha4/mdpd/statistics.php.

Analysis of a single BioProject of COPD

MDPD provides four types of analysis for each BioProject and a set of demonstrative single-BioProject analyses focusing on COPD (BioProject ID-PRJNA377739) was performed. The study contains sputum Amplicon-16S data from COPD stable and exacerbation sub-groups, and more detailed information is provided in Supplementary File 1: S5.

The Krona plot illustrates the taxonomic composition of COPD exacerbation (Fig. 2a), revealing the phylum Firmicutes as most abundant, followed closely by the phylum Proteobacteria. The genus Veillonella is found to be more abundant in the 'COPD Stable' stage (Fig. 2b), as also reported in a study [64].

Analyses of a BioProject of COPD (BioProject ID-PRJNA377739) in MDPD. (a) Krona plot represents the taxonomic profile of the ‘COPD stable’ sub-group. The plot illustrates hierarchical taxonomic classification, ranging from domain to genus. (b) The abundance profile of the top 10 taxa across the sub-groups. (c–d) Co-occurrence network analysis of the stable (c) and exacerbation (d) sub-groups to reveal the community structure. The nodes represent the taxa, and the edges are the interactions (green = positive and red = negative) between them. The edge transparency is proportional to the interaction strength between microbes with reference to edge weights. (e) The heatmap illustrates the taxa associations with age groups and gender across sub-groups, identified by MaAsLin2. (f) The differential markers of the two sub-groups predicted by LEfSe where the bars (orange = COPD exacerbation and green = COPD stable) denote log10LDA scores. (g) The bar plot of the top 10 abundant taxa of a specific run (run ID: SRR5383456) of the BioProject.

Microbial co-occurrence networks of the sub-groups were explored to gain insights into changes in interactomes during stable and exacerbation stages. The community structure of the exacerbation sub-group involves a reduced level of negative interactions among microbes as compared to the stable COPD, which was previously stated in a study [65]. In the stable sub-group, specific microbes such as Anaeroplasma, Turibacter, Romboutsia, ASF356, and Erysipelatoclostridium interact to form a distinct module. In contrast, genus Moraxella is present exclusively in the exacerbation interactome (Fig. 2c and 2d).

MaAsLin2 [50] identified a total of 47 taxa associated with various confounders, such as age groups and gender, across both stable and exacerbation sub-groups. The genera Selenomonas, Neisseria, and Corynebacterium are found to be positively associated with males in COPD exacerbation. Similarly, the genus Lactobacillus is positively associated with the age group 81–91 in the COPD stable (Fig. 2e).

Discriminant analysis by LEfSe with the default parameters (Method: LEfSe without FDR p-value adjustment, p-value: 0.1, Filter threshold: 0.0001, Taxa level: Genus, and Cut-off value: 2) identified 17 taxa discriminating stable and exacerbation states. Genus Veillonella was recognized as a marker for the stable sub-group, which is consistent with a previous study by Li et al. [66] while Moraxella was identified as a marker for the exacerbation sub-group (Fig. 2f), which was also found to be associated with the exacerbation group through T-cell induced pro-inflammatory signalling and interferon signalling of the host, in a previous study [67].

In MDPD, every single run-wise taxonomic analysis is also possible. Figure 2g shows the top 10 abundant taxa of run number SRR5383456 (COPD stable sub-group), where Veillonella is the most abundant genus, and the Krona plot for the same run is shown in Supplementary File 1: Fig. S13. Taken together, it is shown that several analyses can be performed on a single BioProject from the MDPD platform.

User-defined analysis of multiple BioProjects in MDPD

MDPD also allows to perform analysis of multiple BioProjects according to the user’s need by filtering data based on biologically relevant questions. There are two main types of analyses available: (i) microbial profile across multiple BioProjects and (ii) discriminant analysis of microbes among the individual and pooled BioProjects.

The demonstrative user-defined analyses focusing on bronchiectasis are performed here. The snapshot of the search query is provided in Supplementary File 1: Fig. S14. A set of 25 abundant species signatures, including Rothia mucilaginosa, Streptococcus salivarius, and Neisseria mucosa, were identified in sputum and nasopharyngeal swab (Fig. 3a). Our observations are in line with a previous study where the genera Streptococcus and Neisseria were abundant in bronchiectasis sputum samples [52].

Analyses of multiple BioProjects identify bronchiectasis-specific microbial signatures and differential markers. (a) User-defined taxonomic analysis for 'bronchiectasis'. The heatmap represents the top 25 abundant taxa and the associated BioProjects, where each tile represents relative abundance. Discriminant analysis for identification of disease-specific markers between ‘bronchiectasis’ and ‘Cystic Fibrosis Exacerbation’ for (b) individual BioProjects and (c) pooled BioProjects. Each tile denotes the log10(LDA) score, and the colour intensity is proportional to the score.

Discriminant analysis was performed by comparing bronchiectasis with cystic fibrosis exacerbation based on specific parameters (Supplementary File 1: S6 and Fig. S15). The results can be viewed in two distinct ways: i) by individual BioProjects (Fig. 3b), and ii) by pooled BioProjects (batch-effect corrected) (Fig. 3c). Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus were identified as key markers for the cystic fibrosis exacerbation, as reported in several studies where P. aeruginosa is reported as an opportunistic pathogen that infects the lungs and causes lung function decline and the other one uses its virulent factors like adhesins and toxins [20, 68–70]. Haemophilus influenzae was identified as a marker of Bronchiectasis, which evades host immune defence and causes infection by biofilm formation and evasion of antibiotic treatments and was associated with exacerbations [71, 72]. Thus, MDPD can be a useful tool for identifying microbial signatures from different pulmonary disease groups coming from multiple BioProjects.

Microbe profiling of pulmonary diseases in MDPD

In MDPD, 2721 microbes were identified through taxonomic and discriminant analyses. These taxa are listed in MDPD and can be accessed via the Browse and Analysis pages. The total number of microbes includes 742 genera and 1554 species of bacteria, 173 genera and 46 species of eukaryota, 193 species of viruses and five genera, and eight species of archaea. Different attributes for various taxa domains are listed in MDPD as illustrated in Fig. 4a. Various attributes are specific to each domain. For the bacterial domain, some key available attributes include Gram stain, spore formation, aerophilicity, coding genes, pathogenicity, antimicrobial resistance, and pulmonary disease association among others, as shown in Fig. 4b. In the case of viruses, such as the Alphainfluenzavirus (Influenza A virus), the available information includes the presence or absence of a capsid and envelope, genetic material, host organisms, pathogenicity, and other characteristics (Fig. 4c). On the other hand, for eukaryotes like Aspergillus fumigatus, attributes such as biofilm formation, spore production, ploidy, mycelium structure, hosts, and others are documented (Fig. 4d).

(a) The circular bar plot represents the total number of domain-wise identified taxa and the distribution of available information in MDPD. (b–d) Different attributes available in MDPD for the domains - bacteria, viruses, and eukaryota are tabulated, with Staphylococcus aureus, Alphainfluenzavirus influenza a virus, and Aspergillus fumigatus serving as representatives, respectively. MDPD also allows enrichment analysis and highlights the microbial specificity to different body sites as well as their associations with various pulmonary disease groups or sub-groups. As illustrated in Fig. 4e, S. aureus is more prevalent in the nasal passages, upper and lower respiratory tract, compared to other body sites, such as the gut. Additionally, Fig. 4f demonstrates that S. aureus is more abundant in restrictive diseases like cystic fibrosis and its sub-groups, including cystic fibrosis exacerbation and cystic fibrosis treatment, as indicated in several studies [70, 73, 74]. Thus, MDPD is a unique platform, where microbe attributes are provided, and it also allows to identify body site and pulmonary disease-specific microbial signatures.

Utility of BIOM files exemplified by COPD datasets: stable versus exacerbation

The BIOM files in MDPD can be downloaded and used by researchers for various types of applications, like ML-based prediction and meta-analysis. Such an example implementation of these BIOM files is discussed, where ML-based classification was performed to predict microbial features differentiating COPD stable and exacerbation states. The detailed workflow is shown in Fig. 5a. RF was seen to be the best performing model for Dataset 1 and Dataset 2 (see methods), and detailed performance metrics of the datasets can be found in Supplementary File 1: Figs. S16 and S17, respectively. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) plots of RF showed better area under the curve (AUC) scores for training (5-fold CV) and the blind set of Dataset 2, as compared to Dataset 1 (Fig. 5b). For Dataset 1, the RF model demonstrated optimal performance with an accuracy of 69.88%, MCC of 36.48%, sensitivity of 41.79% and specificity of 89.57% during 5-fold CV on the training–testing set and an accuracy of 68.82%, MCC of 34.55%, sensitivity of 38.79% and specificity of 90.18% on the blind validation set. In contrast, the refined Dataset 2 achieved accuracy of 74.85%, MCC of 49.07%, sensitivity of 64.65% and specificity of 83.30% for 5-fold CV on the training–testing set and accuracy of 72.69%, MCC of 44.62%, sensitivity of 64.60% and specificity of 79.41% for the blind validation set (Fig. 5c and 5d).

The utilization of BIOM files of COPD employing ML approaches identifies key features (taxa) that facilitate sub-group classification. (a) Workflow for implementing ML-based classification of COPD stable and exacerbation sub-groups. Here, two types of datasets are used (Dataset 1 and Dataset 2) for model development. (b) ROC plot showing AUC for 5-fold CV and blind validation on Datasets 1 and 2. (c and d) The bar plots represent the accuracy, MCC, sensitivity, and specificity values of the 5-fold CV (deep red and deep blue bars) and blind validation (light red and light blue bars) of Datasets 1 and 2, respectively. The top 20 features were extracted from the optimized RF models to identify the key microbes that distinguish the two sub-groups (stable versus exacerbations). Certain features, such as Ligilactobacillus, Ralstonia, Granulicatella, and Cutibacterium, are unique to Dataset 1, while others, including Rothia, Atopobium, Selenomonas, Actinomyces, and Aggregatibacter, are unique to Dataset 2 (Fig. 5e and 5f). These distinct features of Dataset 2 (strict selection of runs) may contribute to improved classification between the two sub-groups.

Discussion

We compiled large-scale data to develop a comprehensive microbiome database of various pulmonary diseases from different body sites with consistently annotated metadata to identify disease group and sub-group-specific microbial signatures through analyses. MDPD allows users to comprehend the potential of the wealth of data by giving access to the integrated data (Amplicon- 16S, ITS, and WMS) in a single platform and allowing on-the-fly analyses.

Concerns about the replicability of human microbiome studies arise from inconsistencies in various factors, such as instruments used, geographical locations, sample types, library layouts, and others. Different instruments differ in several factors like read length, mapping efficiency, challenging repetitive regions, and mismatch rates [75]. Similarly, the microbial profile varies according to geographical region. Recently, a study reported that Asian and non-Asian asthmatic cohorts exhibit different microbial profiles. They have stated that in non-Asian asthmatic studies, the abundance as well as prevalence of established airway pathogens (like Haemophilus and Streptococcus) are generally higher [76]. Likewise, the microbiome profile also changes depending on the sample type. Microbial abundances in sputum and the nasal microbiome differ in bronchiectasis, as stated in a study [77]. MDPD provides individual BioProject-wise reanalyzed information using a standard computational pipeline to overcome these problems.

Analyses of a single BioProject in MDPD are exemplified by PRJNA377739 of COPD consisting of two sub-groups, COPD stable and exacerbation. Here, genus Veillonella was seen to be abundant in the stable sub-group, also serving as a marker of this sub-group by LEfSe, a finding that has been reported earlier in studies. Genus Moraxella was identified as a marker of the exacerbation sub-group and has already been reported in several studies [78–80]. The multivariate association analysis revealed that the genera Selenomonas, Neisseria, and Corynebacterium are significantly associated with males going through exacerbation, a novel observation that needs further research. A distinct network module of the identified markers was identified in the stable sub-group, which can be given special attention and can be targeted as a treatment strategy. Moreover, user-customizable taxonomic analysis has been exemplified, where P. aeruginosa, S. salivarius, and R. mucilaginosa were found to be abundant in bronchiectasis [81]. Besides, users can pool multiple BioProjects based on their research question to obtain batch-effect-corrected and reliable results. This was explained by discriminant analysis, wherein S. aureus and P. aeruginosa were identified as markers for ‘Cystic Fibrosis Exacerbation’, a well-established finding [69, 82, 83].

MDPD facilitates taxonomic information and various attributes of all 2721 identified microbes, including lung infection/pulmonary disease association information for 99 bacterial species, 28 viruses, and 209 fungi/ prokaryotes. Enrichment of these microbes across body sites and disease sub-groups is also available in MDPD. This information may contribute to our understanding of disease mechanisms and their clinical implications. For example, S. aureus and P. aeruginosa, which are associated with the pulmonary microbiome and cystic fibrosis, as reported in MDPD, exhibit attributes such as biofilm formation and antimicrobial resistance. It is clear from multiple studies that biofilm formation is a key event in cystic fibrosis, and bacterial biofilms can withstand high levels of antimicrobials, which worsens the disease and complicates treatment. This emphasizes the need for developing effective anti-biofilm strategies [83, 84].

MDPD is a wide and diverse collection of available literature and data but it has a few limitations: (i) MinION sequencing data were not included in the database due to the limited availability and incompatibility with other sequencing data; (ii) analyses were not performed on a small subset of data due to either its unmanageable size (approximately one terabyte) or its failure to meet our stringent quality control criteria; (iii) multivariate analysis was not possible for BioProjects lacking confounder metadata; (iv) being a manually curated database, it may encompass some manual inaccuracy. However, manual curation is preferred over text mining because the information being curated is complex, and relying on text mining algorithms can lead to data loss. (v) GBLM was used for co-occurrence network analysis, which can be computationally intensive for larger datasets and may cause overfitting with very small datasets. However, this method is known to handle the compositionality of microbiome data and provides more robust and biologically meaningful networks that accommodate nonlinearities and directions, compared to other methods such as SparCC and propr. Nevertheless, a huge magnitude of data is being re-analyzed using advanced methods that incorporate rigorous statistical interventions, such as the application of FDR-adjusted p-values. Furthermore, MDPD provides a scope for uniform analysis of microbiome profiles across pulmonary diseases, paving the way for the development of microbiome panels for clinical diagnostic workflows.

There is an ongoing debate about the relationship between cause and effect regarding microbiome dysbiosis in pulmonary diseases. Nonetheless, studies suggest that while microbes may not serve as the primary cause of these conditions, they significantly contribute to disease exacerbation. This is clearly demonstrated by the differences in microbial profiles observed between stable and exacerbation stages of the diseases. These signatures can be further assessed for their pathogenic potential and enrichment profile in different sub-groups as well as body sites. Users can also filter the data for customized analyses according to their specific research questions. Therefore, MDPD can enhance the field of pulmonary research by assisting in microbiome profiling and targeted antibiotic therapy. The data in MDPD can also be used for functional analyses, like identifying microbial gene families and pathways [85] in disease conditions. Further, the list of enriched microbial metabolites in disease conditions and their interaction with host factors can be included in future versions. Also, genome-scale metabolic modelling can be utilized with the help of the existing respiratory genome catalog [86, 87] to effectively identify microbial metabolites that contribute to the aggravation of pulmonary diseases. In this regard, MDPD will undergo regular annual updates and maintenance for a minimum of five years to enhance accessibility for users. Here, we have emphasized the analyses of specific pulmonary diseases like COPD and bronchiectasis among the 19 diseases included in the MDPD. This platform can serve as a valuable resource for microbiome exploratory research into pulmonary diseases like asthma, IPF, and lung cancer.
