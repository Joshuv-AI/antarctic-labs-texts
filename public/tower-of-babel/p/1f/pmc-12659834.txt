27-gauge microincision vitrectomy surgery-assisted scleral fixation of intraocular lens: An anterior segment optical coherence tomography-guided study
Authors: Ashish Markan, Manasi Tripathi, Shivani Chhabra, Vipin Rana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To evaluate the outcomes of 27-gauge (27G) microincision vitrectomy surgery (MIVS)-assisted scleral fixation of intraocular lenses (SFIOLs) using anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT). The study assesses postoperative intraocular lens (IOL) tilt, decentration, and iris to IOL distance and their impact on visual and refractive outcomes.Methods:This retrospective study included 36 patients undergoing 27G MIVS-assisted SFIOL implantation. Preoperative data, including best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (IOP), endothelial cell count, and surgical indications, were recorded. Postoperatively, BCVA, IOP, and endothelial cell count were assessed at 3 months. Additionally, postoperative AS-OCT was performed to assess IOL tilt, decentration, and distance of the IOL from the posterior iris surface. Surgical complications were documented.Results:The mean IOL tilt was 3.58 ± 0.57 degrees, IOL decentration was 0.22 ± 0.47 mm, and IOL to posterior iris distance was 0.76 ± 0.66 mm. At three months, BCVA significantly improved from 0.85 ± 0.53 log MAR to 0.38 ± 0.38 log MAR (P = 0.03). Hyperopic shift (manifest refractive spherical equivalent [MRSE]: +1.14 ± 0.62 D) correlated strongly with increased distance of IOL from the posterior iris surface. Complications included early postoperative IOL tilt (n = 4, requiring refixation), vitreous hemorrhage (n = 3, resolving spontaneously), and choroidal detachment (n = 2, resolving with corticosteroids).Conclusions:The 27G MIVS-assisted SFIOL technique demonstrates favorable outcomes with minimal IOL tilt and decentration. The technique offers a safe and effective alternative for scleral fixation, optimizing surgical efficiency and patient outcomes.

Surgical management of aphakia in the absence of adequate capsular support remains a challenge in ophthalmic practice. In such cases, scleral-fixated intraocular lenses (SFIOLs) offer a viable solution, providing stable visual rehabilitation while avoiding complications associated with anterior chamber or iris-fixated IOLs.[12] Various techniques have been described for scleral fixation of IOLs, including sutured and sutureless approaches.[3] Among these, sutureless methods have gained popularity as these bypass the risks of suture-related complications such as suture degradation and erosion.[4]

In recent times, advancements in microincision vitrectomy surgery (MIVS) have revolutionized posterior segment interventions by offering a minimally invasive approach, improved wound integrity, and reduced postoperative inflammation. The 27-gauge (27G) MIVS system, characterized by smaller instrument size and enhanced fluidics, has expanded the scope of vitreoretinal procedures.[5] Its use in SFIOL surgery facilitates efficient IOL manipulation and precise haptic externalization with minimal trauma.[6] However, despite its advantages, concerns remain regarding IOL stability, refractive outcomes, and postoperative complications.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) has significantly improved our understanding of postoperative IOL positioning, with anterior segment OCT (AS-OCT) serving as a valuable tool for assessing parameters such as IOL tilt, decentration, and the haptic position within the eye.[6] Precise evaluation of these metrics is crucial, as even minor deviations in IOL alignment can induce higher-order aberrations, impacting visual quality and refractive stability. Additionally, understanding the relationship between IOL positioning and refractive outcomes, particularly hyperopic shifts, is essential for optimizing surgical techniques.

This study aims to evaluate the outcomes of 27G MIVS-assisted scleral fixation of IOLs using AS-OCT imaging. By assessing postoperative IOL tilt, decentration, and the distance between the IOL and the posterior iris surface, we seek to determine the impact of these parameters on refractive stability. Furthermore, the study examines visual outcomes, complications, and the overall efficacy of this technique in patients undergoing SFIOL implantation.

Methods

The study was a retrospective analysis conducted at our center from February 2022 to January 2025, evaluating the outcomes of 27G MIVS-assisted SFIOL. The research adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board.

Patients who had a minimum of 3 months of follow-up after surgery were included. Exclusion criteria included as follows: age less than 16 years, pre-existing corneal scar or haze, pre-existing glaucoma or retinal disease, and patients lost to follow-up.

Relevant preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative details were recorded. Preoperative data included patient age, gender, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (IOP), specular endothelial cell count, and a comprehensive anterior and posterior segment evaluation. The indications for performing SFIOL were also documented. All patients underwent 27G MIVS-assisted scleral fixation of an IOL. A pars plana vitrectomy was done only in cases associated with posterior lens/IOL dislocation or associated vitreous hemorrhage. An anterior vitrectomy and a thorough port site vitrectomy were done in all cases. Intraoperative surgical videos were reviewed to assess any complications encountered during surgery.

Postoperatively, all patients underwent AS-OCT [Anterion, Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Germany] at one month to assess IOL metrics, including IOL tilt, IOL decentration, and the distance of the IOL from the posterior iris surface.

IOL tilt[6] was defined as the angle between the corneal topographic axis and the central axis of the IOL. The corneal topographic axis refers to the reference line that connects the fixation point on the corneal topographer to the corneal vertex. The central axis of the IOL is represented by a vertical line passing through its center. Similarly, decentration[6] was described as the distance between the fixation point on the corneal topographic axis and the central axis of the IOL within the cornea. The distance of the IOL from the posterior iris was defined as the perpendicular distance between the posterior surface of the iris and the IOL at the pupillary margin [Fig. 1a and b].

Additional postoperative data included BCVA, IOP, specular endothelial cell count, and manifest refractive spherical equivalent (MRSE) at the final follow-up visit (three months). Any postoperative complications observed during the follow-up period were also recorded.

Surgical technique

A 27G infusion cannula was secured to maintain IOP. Following this, scleral tunnels were created using a 27G trocar, positioned 1.5 mm from the corneal limbus at the 2 o’clock and 8 o’clock positions [Fig. 2a]. The trocar was initially inserted parallel to the limbus for approximately 2 mm before being directed toward the center of the eye.

Surgical steps for scleral fixation of a three-piece intraocular lens (IOL). (a) Creation of scleral tunnels using a 27G trocar placed 1.5 mm from the limbus at the 2 o’clock and 8 o’clock positions. (b) Insertion of the Alcon MA60 IOL into the anterior chamber with the trailing haptic left outside. (c) Externalization of the leading haptic using a 27G max grip forceps by pulling the trocar cannula onto the forceps shaft. (d–e) Flanging of both haptic ends using a heat cautery. (f) Insertion of the flanged haptics into the scleral tunnels for secure fixation

Next, a three-piece IOL [Alcon MA60] was inserted into the anterior chamber using an injector, with the trailing haptic held outside to prevent the IOL from dislocating into the vitreous cavity [Fig. 2b]. A 27G max grip forcep [Grieshaber, Alcon] was used to externalize the haptic, by pulling the cannula onto the shaft of the forceps [Fig. 2c]. The same technique was applied to externalize the trailing haptic. Both haptic ends were flanged using the tip of a heat cautery and subsequently inserted into the scleral tunnels [Fig. 2d-f].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables.

Preoperative and postoperative BCVA, IOP, and endothelial cell count were compared using the paired t-test for normally distributed data and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed data. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Correlations between IOL tilt, decentration, and IOL to posterior iris distance with manifest refraction spherical equivalent (MRSE) were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (for normally distributed data) or Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (for non-normally distributed data). The strength of correlation was interpreted as weak (0.1–0.3), moderate (0.3–0.5), or strong (>0.5).

Results

A total of 36 patients were included in the study, with a mean age of 34.58 ± 18.43 years. Of these, 29 were male, and seven were female. The indications for SFIOL implantation included dislocated or dropped IOL (30.55%), traumatic lens phacodonesis or lens drop (27.77%), ectopia lentis (25%), and prior surgical aphakia (19.44%). The baseline BCVA was 0.85 ± 0.53 logMAR, the mean IOP was 16.66 ± 3.54 mmHg, and the mean endothelial cell count was 2413 ± 456 cells/mm2. Baseline demographic details are summarized in Table 1.

At the three-month follow-up, the mean BCVA improved significantly to 0.38 ± 0.38 log MAR (P = 0.03) from baseline. The mean IOP was 17.21 ± 2.43 mmHg (P = 0.24), and the mean endothelial cell count was 2010 ± 430 cells/mm² (P = 0.44), with no statistically significant changes compared to preoperative values. On postoperative day 1, all patients were evaluated for IOL tilt. Significant IOL tilt was observed in four patients, necessitating immediate refixation. Discussion

The technique of scleral fixation of IOLs has undergone significant advancements since Parry’s initial report in 1954.[7] Techniques such as Yamane’s 30-gauge double-needle method have gained prominence; however, challenges related to procedural complexity and steep learning curves remain.[8] Alternatively, a number of modifications have been suggested to the original Yamane’s techniques, with an aim to decrease the complexities associated with the procedure and enhance the surgical outcomes.[910]

In our study, we used 27G instruments to sclerally fixate the IOL. This approach simplifies the surgical process, reduces complications, and enhances safety by minimizing surgical wounds and facilitating improved IOL manipulation using intraocular forceps. Postoperative IOL stability and positioning were assessed using AS-OCT, providing valuable insights into refining scleral fixation techniques for enhanced surgical efficiency and patient outcomes.

Our findings indicated a significant improvement in BCVA from baseline at 3-month follow-ups. This aligns with previous studies, which have reported favorable visual outcomes post-SFIOL surgery.[611]

The mean IOL tilt observed in our study was 3.58 ± 0.57 degrees, comparable to previous studies employing 27G trocar-assisted SFIOL.[611] In contrast, studies using 25-gauge trocar-assisted SFIOL have reported higher IOL tilt, with mean tilts of 5.97 ± 2.65 degrees.[12] This discrepancy may be attributed to the smaller gauge system used in our study, allowing the flanged haptic to fit more snugly within the scleral tunnel. Yamane et al.[8] reported a mean tilt of 3.30 ± 2.31 degrees using a 30-gauge needle for haptic externalization, supporting the notion that smaller gauge sclerotomies contribute to more stable IOL positioning.

Excessive IOL tilt is associated with visual disturbances such as astigmatism, halos, glare, and higher-order aberrations, with tilts greater than 5 degrees considered clinically significant.[1314] Therefore, minimizing IOL tilt is crucial for improving postoperative visual quality. Our 27G MIVS technique achieves minimal IOL tilt, making it a preferable approach for optimizing visual outcomes.

The mean IOL decentration in our study was 0.22 ± 0.47 mm. Jujo et al.[6] reported an IOL decentration of 0.39 ± 0.39 mm using a similar technique. Contrary to this, Ibrahim et al.[15] reported an average IOL decentration of 0.53 mm using balanced two-string technique. Ni et al.[16] reported a mean decentration of 0.31 ± 0.13 mm in a case of transscleral fixation of foldable 3-looped haptic IOL.

Research indicates that decentration is higher in sutured SFIOLs compared to sutureless techniques, potentially due to improper suture placement, tissue reaction to sutures, or excessive suture tension during surgery.[4] A decentration of 0.2–0.3 mm is generally visually tolerable and clinically unnoticed by patients[13]; none of our patients reported visual symptoms related to decentration.

The average distance of IOL from the posterior iris surface in our study was 0.76 ± 0.66 mm. Kumar et al.[17] reported this distance to be 0.45 mm using the glued SFIOL technique, where the haptics were externalized and tucked inside the scleral tunnel anteriorly. This anterior positioning likely results in a close approximation of the IOL to the posterior iris as compared to techniques using the flanged IOL approach. SFIOL techniques using the flanged haptic technique are known to be positioned slightly posteriorly.[3] This can be attributed to two key factors. First, the flanged haptic technique involves pushing the haptic back into the sclerotomy tunnel. So, if the adequate length of the haptic is not flanged, a major part of the haptic is expected to remain intraocular. Second, the posterior angulation of the haptic in a multipiece IOL can contribute to the posterior positioning of the IOL in the postoperative period. In a study by Schranz et al.,[18] using the Carlevale IOL for scleral fixation, the mean IOL-iris distance was 0.60 ± 0.32 mm, and no cases of postoperative reverse pupillary block were reported. Similarly, no reverse pupillary block-related complications were observed in our study.

Among the IOL metrics, IOL to posterior iris distance positively correlated with the manifest refraction spherical equivalent (MRSE). An increased distance indicates more posterior positioning of the IOL from its effective position, thus inducing a hyperopic shift. Our study showed an MRSE of +1.14 ± 0.62 D. Our study results are consistent with Aykut et al.[19] and Kansal et al.[20] These studies also showed a hyperopic shift ranging from +0.85 D to +1.8 D. Thus, we recommend adjusting for the IOL power calculation in the preoperative period to achieve a near emmetropic state, while performing flanged haptic technique.

Conclusion

The 27G MIVS technique for SFIOL demonstrates favorable outcomes in terms of IOL tilt and decentration, contributing to improved visual acuity and reduced postoperative complications. Authors recommend adjusting for the hyperopic shift while calculating IOL power in preoperative planning. Utilizing AS-OCT for postoperative assessment provides precise measurements of IOL positioning, aiding in the refinement of surgical techniques. Adoption of this approach may enhance surgical efficiency and patient satisfaction, offering a valuable alternative to existing scleral fixation methods.
