Advances in the lectin pathway in systemic lupus erythematosus: from clinical correlations and mechanisms to targeted interventions
Authors: Rongfang Feng, Yufei Zhang, Qin Chen, Yaqi Wang, Yaning Tian, Yumin Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe complement system critically mediates systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) pathogenesis through dual mechanisms: promoting inflammatory organ damage while regulating the initiation of immune tolerance. Among its three activation pathways (classical, alternative, and lectin), the lectin pathway is the most recently characterized.FindingsThe lectin pathway engages pattern-recognition molecules (PRMs: mannose-binding lectin [MBL], collectins, ficolins) and mannose-binding lectin–associated serine proteases (MASPs). These components orchestrate unique biological functions beyond canonical complement activation, including self-antigen clearance, B/T-cell tolerance modulation, and interferon-α production. PRM/MASP genetic variants (particularly loss-of-function genotypes) predispose to SLE and associate with organ-specific damage phenotypes. PRMs detect damage-associated molecular patterns on apoptotic cells, initiating complement activation. Resulting fragments (C3a, C5a) and membrane attack complexes directly drive tissue injury. Clinically, circulating PRM/MASP levels and tissue deposition patterns reflect disease activity and organ involvement. Although MASP-2- and C5-targeting monoclonal antibodies demonstrate therapeutic potential in trials, most lectin pathway interventions remain preclinical.ConclusionsThis review integrates clinical correlations, mechanistic advances in both complement-dependent and complement-independent functions, and emerging SLE therapeutics targeting the lectin pathway.Keywords: Complement system, MBL, Lupus nephritis, Lectin pathway, Systemic lupus erythematosus

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), a classic autoimmune disease, is driven by autoantibody-mediated immune complex deposition in multiple organs, causing diverse manifestations (skin, kidneys, central nervous system, etc.), with lupus nephritis (LN) as the most common internal manifestation. It shows marked demographic disparities, including a global prevalence of 30–150 per 100,000, an incidence of 2.2–23.1 per 100,000, and a 9:1 female predominance linked to estrogen [1, 2]. Despite therapeutic advances, 10-year mortality remains about 10%, mainly due to infections and renal failure [3, 4]. These unmet clinical needs underscore the urgency for novel targeted therapies for SLE.

In recent decades, survival in patients with SLE has improved markedly, attributable to advances in pathogenesis elucidation and targeted therapies. SLE pathogenesis involves gene–environment interactions that breach immune tolerance, leading to autoreactive B-cell expansion and characteristic autoantibody production (anti-dsDNA, antinuclear, anti-Sm). Tissue-deposited immune complexes activate complement cascades, exacerbating inflammatory injury [5, 6]. The complement system, which comprises more than 50 proteins and three pathways (classical, lectin, and alternative) [5, 6], plays dual roles in SLE. It mediates tissue damage through immune complex activation, and it also regulates the establishment of immune tolerance, antibody modulation, and interferon-α responses [5]. These pathways demonstrate both specialized functions and synergistic interactions in disease progression [7]. ‌Such mechanistic complexities highlight the need for pathway-specific therapeutic strategies that balance inflammatory control with tolerance preservation in SLE management.

Targeted biologic agents against B lymphocytes, T lymphocytes, and inflammatory pathways have become cornerstone therapies for SLE. Belimumab (B-lymphocyte stimulator [BLyS] inhibitor) is approved for clinical use; telitacicept (BLyS and a proliferation-inducing ligand [APRIL] inhibitor) has completed Phase III trials but is not FDA-approved [8]; rituximab (anti-CD20), although not indicated for SLE, is supported by observational studies and recommended by the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) guidelines for refractory disease [9]. The pathogenic expansion and aberrant subset distribution of B/T lymphocytes drive most current therapeutic approaches. However, when complement activation plays a central role in the clinical manifestations of SLE, such as thrombotic microangiopathy complications [10] or severe renal involvement with minimal response to other therapies and evidence of intense complement activation [11], complement-targeted therapy can yield rapid and substantial clinical benefits. Complement-targeted agents such as Ravulizumab (anti-C5 monoclonal antibody) have demonstrated favorable efficacy in LN patients with strong complement activation [11]. Such targeted biologics, despite current restrictions, may fill critical therapeutic gaps by offering safer adjunctive options for specific SLE subpopulations.

The lectin pathway, a complement-activation cascade initiated by pattern-recognition molecules binding to pathogen surfaces, has emerged as a pivotal arm of the complement system in SLE research. This review systematically synthesizes contemporary evidence on the clinical correlations, molecular mechanisms, prognostic implications, and therapeutic potential of key lectin-pathway components in SLE.

Overview of the lectin pathway

The lectin pathway is the most recently characterized of the three complement activation pathways [12]. An overview of the lectin pathway is shown in Fig. 1. Activation is initiated by the release of pathogen-associated molecular patterns or damage-associated molecular patterns during microbial invasion or tissue injury, which are bound by pattern recognition molecules, including mannose-binding lectin (MBL), ficolins-1/-2/-3, and collectins-10/-11. This triggers recruitment of ‌MBL-associated serine proteases (MASPs), specifically MASP-1, MASP-2, and MASP-3. Among these, MASP-1 and MASP-2 cleave C4 and C2 to generate the C3 convertase (C4b2a), propagating the complement cascade and culminating in membrane attack complex formation [12, 13]. The membrane attack complex induces pathogen lysis through pore formation in cellular membranes or modulates cellular signaling pathways [14]. By contrast, MASP-3 does not cleave C4 or C2. Instead, it activates the alternative pathway by converting pro–factor D to its active form [12] and has been proposed to negatively regulate lectin pathway activation [15]. Thus, the lectin pathway provides a crucial early surveillance system, linking pathogen or damage recognition directly to core complement effector mechanisms.‌

Overview of the lectin pathway Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, D-mannose, and L-fucose, are exposed or released during microbial invasion or tissue injury. These molecular patterns serve as ligands for pattern recognition molecules (PRMs), including MBL, ficolins (ficolin-1, -2, -3), and collectins (collectin-10, -11). Binding of PRMs to their ligands triggers the recruitment and activation of MASPs, specifically MASP-1, MASP-2, and MASP-3. Activated MASPs cleave complement components C4 and C2 to form the C3 convertase (C4b2a). The C3 convertase cleaves C3 into C3b and C3a, further assembling into the C5 convertase (C4b2a3b). The C5 convertase cleaves C5 into C5a and C5b, ultimately leading to formation of the terminal membrane attack complex (C5b-9)

Polymorphisms of lectin pathway in SLE

MBL

Encoded by MBL1 (a human pseudogene) and MBL2, MBL2 is localized to chromosome 10q11.2-q21 and comprises four exons and three introns [16, 17]. Exon 1 harbors three functional SNPs: codon 54 (allele B: rs1800450), 57 (allele C: rs1800451), and 52 (allele D: rs5030737), which together form allele O (wild-type allele A) [16, 18]. The promoter region contains two additional SNPs: − 550 (H/L: rs11003125) and − 221 (X/Y: rs7096206) [19]. Notably, B-D alleles and the LX haplotype correlate with reduced serum MBL levels, while the HY haplotype elevates them, directly determining interindividual concentration differences [18]. Low MBL-expression genotypes are strongly associated with SLE susceptibility, with the B allele representing a well-documented risk variant and the H allele conferring protection [18–20]. Clinically, these low-expression genotypes significantly correlate with infections, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, malar rash, vasculitis, gangrene, and increased common carotid artery intima-media thickness (ccIMT) in SLE patients [19–21], while homozygous promoter − 221 XX genotypes are linked to earlier disease onset [22]. Conversely, no significant associations exist with lupus pneumonia, neuropsychiatric SLE, or cardiovascular disease [23, 24]. Although central nervous system, pulmonary, and cardiovascular involvement are rare in SLE, they drastically increase mortality, highlighting organ-specific impacts of MBL2 polymorphisms. Allele O prevalence is higher in SLE patients with membranous nephropathy compared with controls, implicating it as a genetic risk factor [25]. Age-related heterogeneity is evident: high MBL-expression genotypes increase renal disease risk but protect against infections in pediatric-onset SLE [26]. Collectively, genetic variability in MBL2 profoundly influences SLE susceptibility, clinical manifestations, and organ-specific pathology.

Ficolins

Ficolin-1

Ficolin-1 (M-ficolin), encoded by the FCN1 gene on chromosome 9q34 and containing nine exons, is primarily expressed in monocytes and neutrophils [27]. Homozygosity for minor alleles at promoter loci -542 (rs10120023) and -144 (rs10117466) significantly elevates serum ficolin-1 levels and correlates with lethality in severe inflammation [28]. While the promoter -1981 SNP (rs2989727) and exon 9 SNP (rs10771583) associate with rheumatoid arthritis susceptibility [29], they confer no increased SLE risk [30]. ‌Thus, although specific FCN1 polymorphisms influence ficolin-1 levels and inflammatory outcomes, they appear distinct from SLE pathogenesis mechanisms.

Ficolin-2

Ficolin-2 (L-ficolin), encoded by the FCN2 gene on chromosome 9q34.3, is secreted by hepatocytes and comprises eight exons [30]. Promoter SNPs (-986 G/A, -602 G/A, -4 A/G) exhibit differential allele frequencies in pediatric-onset SLE compared with controls, with alleles -986 G, -602 G, and -4 A conferring SLE risk. Genotypes -986 GG and -4 AA correlate with reduced serum ficolin-2 levels and increased LN susceptibility in pediatric-onset SLE [31]. In addition, studies in broader age groups link the intron 1 SNP rs3124954 to LN and the promoter SNP rs17514136 (-4 A/G) to more severe phenotypes [30]. ‌Hence, FCN2 polymorphisms influence SLE risk and renal complications, with distinct variants impacting pediatric and general populations.

Ficolin-3

Ficolin-3 (H-ficolin), encoded by the FCN3 gene on chromosome 1p36.11 [28, 30], exhibits a frameshift mutation in exon 5 (+ 1637delC, rs532781899) that impairs activity. However, its frequency doesn't differ significantly between SLE patients and controls, and ficolin-3-deficient individuals exist without identified mutations [32, 33]. Conversely, the minor allele of SNP rs2474288 is associated with reduced ficolin-3 activity in SLE patients, and with lower risks of hematologic and immune complications and higher serum C1q/C4 levels compared with major allele carriers [32]. ‌Therefore, while some FCN3 variants influence activity and clinical outcomes in SLE, their pathogenic role remains complex.

MASPs

MASP-1 and MASP-3

MASP-1 and MASP-3 are encoded by the MASP1 gene (3q27.3), which contains 18 exons [34]. Analysis of six single-nucleotide variants in SLE patients, including exon 10 (rs38343199 G > A), exon 5 (rs38343200 C > T, rs38343202 A > G), exon 12 (rs38343201 A > G), and exon 13 (rs38343203 C > T, rs38343204 A > T), revealed no frequency differences compared with healthy controls, suggesting that these variants are not SLE susceptibility factors [35]. ‌Thus, while critical for complement activation, the examined polymorphisms in MASP1 encoding MASP-1/-3 do not contribute to SLE susceptibility.

MASP-2

MASP-2, encoded by the MASP2 gene (1p36.22) and comprising 12 exons, exhibits significant allele and genotype frequency differences in several SNPs between SLE patients and healthy controls. These include promoter SNP rs7548659 (-175), exon 10 rs2273346, intron 9 rs17409276, exon 12 rs1782455, and intron 7 rs76695096, implicating them as SLE susceptibility factors [36]. Specific SNPs correlate with variations in serum MASP-2 concentrations and with clinical manifestations: rs7548659 with thrombocytopenia and rs17409276 with pleuritis/pericarditis. Notably, homozygosity for the p.D120G mutation (rs72550870) causes MASP-2 deficiency but does not increase susceptibility to infections or autoimmune disorders [37]. ‌Thus, while specific MASP2 polymorphisms contribute to SLE susceptibility and clinical heterogeneity, complete MASP-2 deficiency does not appear to be associated with heightened autoimmunity or infection risk.

MBL

Serum MBL levels show significant geographic variation among SLE patients: lower in Brazilians than healthy controls but elevated in Egyptians [38, 39]. Indian studies report conflicting results (elevations/reductions), potentially attributable to methodological differences in detection, whereas Danish and Swiss cohorts show no disparity [40–42]. SLE patients with antiphospholipid antibody (aPL)-positive exhibit higher levels of MBL than those with aPL-negative [43], as do pediatric-onset SLE patients compared with controls [26, 44]. Genetically, low MBL levels primarily result from MBL2 polymorphisms, linking MBL deficiency to SLE susceptibility [45], with anti-MBL antibodies potentially accelerating depletion through immune clearance [46]. Clinically, MBL deficiency correlates with increased LN risk (OR = 1.68, P < 0.05) and lower MBL levels predict susceptibility to severe bacterial infection in Chinese cohorts (OR = 0.516 per log unit increase, P = 0.01) [38, 47]. The impact of genetic defects on systemic susceptibility, together with fluctuations in MBL levels across clinical manifestations, collectively indicates that MBL contributes to SLE pathogenesis; however, its specific role requires confirmation in future studies.

As a biomarker, MBL levels correlate positively with SLE disease activity index (SLEDAI) (r = 0.36, P = 0.0003) [48, 49], Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics/American College of Rheumatology (SLICC/ACR) damage index (r = 0.232, P = 0.021) [49], anti-dsDNA titers (r = 0.54, P < 0.0001), and proteinuria severity (r = 0.42, P < 0.0001), but negatively with C3/C4 levels (C3: r = − 0.27, P = 0.007, C4: r = − 0.29, P = 0.01). Elevated MBL are reported in nephritis, carditis, pneumonia, and infection [40, 41], and levels > 2000 ng/mL are linked to thrombocytopenia (OR = 2.71, P = 0.007) and seizures (OR = 2.61, P = 0.034) [49]. However, discrepancies exist; some studies find no severity association with MBL blood levels [41, 48], while anti-dsDNA-positive or low-C3 patients show reduced MBL [42, 50], and aPL-positive thrombotic patients have lower MBL than non-thrombotic counterparts [43]. Methodological variations (e.g., ‌enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay inter-plate variability) likely underlie conflicts. Novel nanotracking technology has revealed elevated super-oligomeric MBL in SLE, correlating positively with SLEDAI, dsDNA antibodies, and splenic germinal center B cells in murine models, holding promise as a more precise biomarker [51], though clinical validation is pending.

Beyond the circulation, MBL deposits in SLE tissues. In LN, glomerular MBL deposition correlates with proteinuria and properdin deposition [52–54]. SLE lesional skin demonstrates MBL accumulation, paralleling disease activity, while non-lesional and discoid lupus skin show no deposition [55]. Sural nerve biopsies reveal increased MBL in epineurial vessels of SLE patients versus rheumatoid arthritis, implicating the lectin pathway in the pathogenesis of SLE-related vasculitic neuropathy [56].

Ficolins

Ficolin-1

Serum or plasma ficolin-1 levels are significantly reduced in SLE patients compared with healthy controls [42, 57, 58]. However, its correlation with disease activity remains controversial. SLE patients with elevated ficolin-1 exhibit higher incidences of discoid lupus erythematosus, LN, and a history of arterial thrombosis [50, 58, 59], with positive correlations between ficolin-1 levels and C-reactive protein (r = 0.28, P < 0.001), leukocyte counts (r = 0.53, P < 0.001), and neutrophil counts (r = 0.52, P < 0.001) [42]. Conversely, some studies report negative correlations between ficolin-1 levels and SLEDAI (r = -0.29, P = 0.015), lower ficolin-1 levels in anti-dsDNA-positive patients, and positive associations with C3 levels (r = 0.33, P < 0.001) [42, 58]. Overall, these findings suggest that ficolin-1 exhibits context-dependent immunomodulatory roles in SLE pathogenesis, reflecting interactions within the complement system.

Ficolin-2

Serum ficolin-2 levels are significantly reduced in SLE patients compared with healthy controls [42, 57] and are linked to genetic mutations and elevated anti-ficolin-2 antibodies whose titers negatively correlate with ficolin-2 concentrations [60]. The role of ficolin-2 as a disease-activity marker remains controversial. Low ficolin-2 correlates with increased LN risk (OR = 4.1, P = 0.001), thrombocytopenia, and positively associates with serum C3 (r = 0.24, P = 0.002) [42, 58, 60], suggesting associations with specific clinical phenotype activity. Conversely, one study found no correlation with SLEDAI or C3/C4 levels [60], indicating that ficolin-2 reflects subset-specific disease manifestations. Furthermore, glomerular ficolin-2 deposition in LN correlates positively with proteinuria and properdin deposition, highlighting its pathogenic contribution to renal injury [53, 54]. Together, these findings indicate that ficolin-2 is a complex, context-dependent regulator in SLE pathogenesis, contributing to specific immunopathologic processes rather than serving as a universal biomarker.

Ficolin-3

The concentrations and activity of serum ficolin-3 are significantly elevated in SLE patients compared with healthy controls, with broader ligand binding specificity [32, 33, 50]. SLE patients with high ficolin-3 levels/activity show higher frequencies of hemolysis, positive Coombs test, lymphopenia (OR = 1.6, P = 0.039), discoid erythema (OR = 2.0, P = 0.0013), mucosal ulcers, and autoantibody positivity (OR = 3.0, P < 0.001) [32, 33, 50]. However, ficolin-3 poorly reflects global disease activity, showing no correlation with SLEDAI or C-reactive protein [33]; paradoxically, low serum C3 correlates with reduced ficolin-3 [42]. Sural nerve biopsies reveal greater ficolin-3 deposition in epineurial vessels of SLE patients compared with those with rheumatoid arthritis, suggesting involvement in the pathogenesis of vasculitic neuropathy [56]. Therefore, ficolin-3 elevation is strongly associated with specific clinical and serological features in SLE but demonstrates complex, nonlinear relationships with complement activation and global disease activity.

Collectins

Collectin-10

Serum collectin-10 (CL-L1) levels are reduced in SLE patients compared with healthy controls [42, 50]. Findings on the association between collectin-10 and disease activity are conflicting. In SLE patients with low C3, elevated collectin-10 associates with increased activity and inversely correlates with collectin-11, though no association with overall SLEDAI was reported [50]. Conversely, other studies report negative correlations between collectin-10 and both SLEDAI (r = -0.16, P = 0.001) and C3 levels (r = − 0.464, P = 0.022), with anti-dsDNA-positive patients showing the lowest concentrations [42, 50]. This discordance suggests context-dependent roles for collectin-10 in SLE immunopathology that warrant further investigation.

Collectin-11

‌Serum collectin-11 (CL-11, CL-K1) levels are reduced in SLE patients compared with healthy controls [42, 50]. As with collectin-10, evidence regarding the association between collectin-11 and disease activity is conflicting. In SLE subsets with low C3, elevated collectin-11 correlates with higher disease activity and shows an inverse correlation with C3 levels, while patients with discoid erythema show elevated collectin-11, though no global SLEDAI correlation exists [50]. Conversely, other studies report negative correlations between collectin-11 and both SLEDAI (r = − 0.15, P = 0.001) and C3 (r = − 0.442, P = 0.031), with anti-dsDNA-positive patients exhibiting the lowest levels [42, 50]. Taken together, reduction of collectin-11 in SLE is consistent, while its relationship with disease activity is context-dependent, suggesting complex roles within distinct immunopathologic pathways.

MASPs

MASP-1

Blood MASP-1 levels in SLE yield conflicting results; some reported significant elevation compared to healthy controls [61], while others found no differences [42]. Overall, MASP-1 levels correlate negatively with SLE disease activity. Low MASP-1 is strongly linked to high-activity LN, and MASP-1 levels are inversely correlated with autoantibody titers (r = − 0.311, P = 0.02) and positively associated with C3/C4 levels (r = 0.373, P < 0.01 for C3, r = 0.314, P = 0.01 for C4) [42, 62]. Immunosuppressive therapy induces significant MASP-1 elevation in SLE patients [42, 62]. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrates MASP-1 deposition along glomeruli in LN [52], implicating its role in the pathogenesis of lupus nephropathy.

MASP-2

Serum MASP-2 levels are significantly elevated in SLE patients compared with healthy controls [36, 42, 61]. Its association with disease activity is controversial: while most studies link high MASP-2 to increased SLEDAI scores (r = 0.693, P < 0.001), renal or joint involvement, hematuria, and anti-dsDNA positivity [36, 42], others associate low MASP-2 with high activity, positive C3-MASP-2 correlation (r = 0.24, P < 0.001), and lower levels in anemic patients [42, 61]. Findings in LN are conflicting, with reports of elevated MASP-2 in kidney-involved cases [42] versus lower serum levels in proliferative LN versus membranous LN [62]. MASP-2 demonstrates significant differential diagnostic value for SLE, with serum levels higher than in rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, gout, Sjögren's syndrome, and ankylosing spondylitis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis confirms exceptional discriminative ability (area under the curve [AUC] > 0.96) for distinguishing SLE from other rheumatic diseases, supporting its potential as a specific diagnostic biomarker [39]. Despite inconsistent links to disease activity, MASP-2 exhibits robust potential as a diagnostic biomarker for SLE differentiation.

MASP-3

SLE patients exhibit significantly elevated plasma MASP-3 levels compared with healthy controls [42, 61]. Overall, MASP-3 levels correlate negatively with SLE disease activity. Inverse associations with SLEDAI scores are observed (r = − 0.15, P = 0.004) [42], and MASP-3 levels are lower in patients with LN or dsDNA positivity [42, 61], with C3 concentrations positively correlating with MASP-3 (r = 0.16, P = 0.003) [42]. Immunofluorescence staining reveals MASP-3 deposition along glomeruli in LN [52], implicating a direct pathogenic role in lupus nephropathy.

Mechanism of the lectin pathway in SLE

The lectin pathway plays an important role in SLE pathogenesis. Its activation triggers the conventional complement cascade, producing complement fragments (C3a, C5a) and membrane attack complex, which drive inflammatory injury in target organs and contribute to clinical symptoms [63]. Notably, lectin pathway-specific molecules (MBL, ficolin-1, ficolin-2, ficolin-3, Collectin-10, Collectin-11, MASPs) can function independently of the complement cascade, even in the absence of C4 or C3 [64]. The subsequent section will describe its two core roles in SLE: classical complement activation and non-canonical mechanisms mediated by lectin-specific molecules.

Complement activation

N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, D-mannose, and L-fucose serve as specific ligands for ‌pattern recognition molecules in the lectin pathway, predominantly expressed on pathogen surfaces [28, 65]. While infection acutely activates this pathway in SLE, we focus on non-infectious mechanisms driving its aberrant activation.

Under normal conditions, carbohydrates on human cells are masked by sialic acid or galactose, preventing engagement by pattern recognition molecules. Apoptosis or injury triggers sialic acid shedding, exposing unmasked N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, D-mannose, and L-fucose for ‌pattern recognition molecules recognition and lectin pathway initiation [66–68]. SLE patients exhibit impaired apoptotic cell clearance, leading to the accumulation of apoptotic debris. Concurrently, immune complexes bind C1q in the classical pathway, amplifying complement mediated injury. Autoantibodies exacerbate injury by promoting macrophage-driven inflammation. Collectively, these processes increase the burden of damaged/apoptotic cells, perpetuating lectin pathway activation [63]. LN cases with isolated lectin pathway activation validate this [69]. Aberrant IgG fucosylation in SLE, characterized by increased exposure of L-fucose, provides an additional activation mechanism [70]. Obviously, impaired clearance, autoantibodies, and altered IgG fucosylation create a self-sustaining cycle driving pathogenic lectin pathway overactivation in SLE.

After initial recognition, the lectin pathway enters the activation phase shared with the classical and alternative pathways. In active SLE, C3/C4 fragment generation is mainly driven by the classical pathway and amplified by the alternative pathway, with a comparatively smaller contribution from the lectin pathway. This shared phase yields the C3/C5 convertases that generate C3a, iC3b/C3d, and C5a/C5b. C3b/C4b-opsonized immune complexes are cleared via complement receptor 1 (CR1) + erythrocytes transporting them to hepatic/splenic macrophages [71]. Proinflammatory C3a/C5a recruit leukocytes via G protein-coupled receptors, amplifying nephritic inflammation [74]. iC3b binding to complement receptor 3 on myeloid cells enhances phagocytosis and anti-inflammatory responses. Minor alleles in Complement receptor 3 increase SLE risk by impairing iC3b phagocytosis and activating Toll-like receptor (TLR) 7/8 pathways [72]. C3d binding to complement receptor 2 on B/follicular dendritic cells promotes B-cell expansion and sustained self-antigen presentation, exacerbating autoantibody production [73–75]. Deposition of membrane attack complex causes direct cellular injury [82]. These complement effector mechanisms drive inflammation, impaired clearance, and autoimmunity in SLE.

Non-canonical roles beyond complement activation

The non-complement activation effects of the lectin pathway play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of SLE. Figure 4 illustrates mechanisms of the lectin pathway in SLE that extend beyond complement activation. The following sections elaborate these mechanisms in detail.

Non-canonical roles beyond complement activation of lectin pathway in SLE. In addition to its role in classical complement activation, the lectin pathway plays important non-complement roles in SLE. Molecules such as MBL, ficolins, and collectins directly recognize nucleic acids. Reduced levels of these molecules, due to genetic variations or autoantibodies, can impair central immune tolerance by preventing proper interaction between autoantigens and naive B cells, thus impairing negative selection of autoreactive B cells. Furthermore, after recognizing cellular debris, these molecules bind to phagocyte receptors (e.g., CD91, CR1), and reduced levels hinder phagocytosis, leading to accumulation of autoantigens. This promotes long-term interactions between autoantigens and B cells in peripheral immune organs, facilitating the generation of autoreactive B cells. Lectin pathway molecules also regulate T-cell function. MBL inhibits differentiation of T-cells toward Th1 and Th17 phenotypes, binds to autoreactive T cells to suppress their proliferation, and modulates MAPK and NFκB pathways. Collectin-11 inhibits proliferation of non-Treg CD4 + T cells. In response to viral infections, MBL can induce IFNα production. Ficolin-2 and MASP-1 promote NET formation; ficolin-2 induces pyroptosis via NLRP3 inflammasome signaling. In terms of inflammatory regulation, lectin pathway molecules exert bidirectional effects. MBL promotes macrophage differentiation into the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype while inhibiting monocyte proliferation and NK cell activation. Ficolin-1 drives macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype, while MASP-1 induces endothelial cells to secrete adhesion molecules and chemokines, promoting inflammatory cell infiltration. Collectin-11 recruits inflammatory cells and promotes fibroblast proliferation via binding to L-fucose and D-mannose on leukocytes and fibroblasts

Impaired apoptotic cell clearance and autoantigen exposure

Defective clearance of apoptotic cells is a pivotal pathogenic mechanism in SLE. Under physiological conditions, apoptotic cells are efficiently cleared to prevent the release of intracellular components such as histones and DNA. In SLE, this process is dysregulated, leading to accumulation of autoantigens that prime immune attack. Lectin pathway molecules contribute to apoptotic cell clearance through complement-independent mechanisms.

MBL, ficolin-2, and collectin-11 recognize nucleic acids on apoptotic cells, engaging the calreticulin-CD91 complex to enhance macrophage phagocytosis [76–78]. Ficolin-3 binds non-DNA apoptotic components via calreticulin-CD91, promoting macrophage adhesion and debris clearance [79]. MBL directly interacts with CR1 on phagocytes to boost apoptotic cell engulfment [80, 81]. Collectin-11 binds CD93 on myeloid cells, facilitating phagocytosis through lectin pathway-independent mechanisms [82, 83]. These coordinated receptor interactions establish a multi-layered clearance system for apoptotic material in immune homeostasis.

Disruption of B cell tolerance

In SLE, impaired central and peripheral B cell tolerance contributes to the emergence of autoreactive B cells [84]. During central tolerance, genetic deficiencies in classical complement components such as C1q, C4, and C3 impair presentation of self-antigens to B cells, leading to defective negative selection and release of autoreactive B cells into the periphery [85–87]. Although direct evidence for the lectin pathway in this process remains limited, its recognition molecules, including MBL, ficolins, and collectin-10/collectin-11, can bind nucleic acids [76–78]. Deficiencies in MBL [18–20, 88] and ficolin-2 [31] have been associated with SLE susceptibility, suggesting a possible supportive role in maintaining central tolerance. In peripheral tolerance, impaired lectin-mediated apoptotic clearance causes persistent antigen exposure, promoting B-cell activation, survival, and differentiation into autoantibody-secreting plasma cells [84, 89]. This breakdown ultimately enables autoreactive B-cell persistence and pathogenic autoantibody production.

T cell dysregulation

T cell dysregulation is a critical feature of SLE and contributes to amplification of autoimmune responses [90, 91]. Emerging evidence indicates that components of the lectin pathway, particularly MBL and collectin-11, exert immunomodulatory effects on T-cell activation and polarization. MBL has been reported to inhibit macrophage-driven T helper 1 (Th1)/Th17 polarization induced by lymphocyte-derived DNA [92]. It can also suppress autoreactive T-cell proliferation via surface calreticulin binding and dampen proximal T cell receptor signaling, including mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathways, which reduce inflammation [93]. Similarly, collectin-11 can engage receptors on CD4 + CD25 − T cells (non- regulatory T [Treg] cells), limiting their proliferation and cytokine secretion [94, 95]. Collectively, lectin pathway components constrain pathogenic T-cell responses and autoimmunity by modulating differentiation, proliferation, and function.

Interferon (IFN) α

Elevated serum IFN-α is a hallmark of SLE, directly compromising B cell tolerance and promoting autoantibody production [96]. In SLE, immune complexes or nucleic acids engage plasmacytoid dendritic cell receptors or undergo endocytosis. This activates TLR7/myeloid differentiation protein 88 (MyD88) and cyclic GMP-AMP synthase/stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS/STING) pathways that drive IFN-α overproduction [97]. Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection further exacerbates IFN-α secretion in this context [98]. Although direct evidence for lectin pathway involvement in IFN-α regulation in SLE is lacking, MBL has been reported to correlate with EBV infection. Specifically, MBL-insufficient infants exhibit lower EBV seropositivity [99], and MBL has been shown to enhance viral-induced IFN-α release [100]. Given the role of MBL in nucleic acid binding and viral recognition, the lectin pathway may modulate IFN-α production in SLE.

Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)

NETs are webs of webs of DNA, histones, and granular proteins released by activated neutrophils, and they contribute to SLE pathogenesis. Excessive NET formation and impaired clearance cause persistent nuclear antigen exposure, driving autoantibody production, IFN-α induction, and inflammation [101, 102]. Lectin pathway molecules modulate NET formation and downstream functions. Ficolin-2 promotes NET release via NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and pyroptosis [98], while inhibition of MASP-1 blocks NET generation [84]. NET-derived DNA/histones can bind MBL and collectin-11, activating complement to amplify inflammation [76]. Thus, lectin pathway components critically regulate NET-associated autoimmunity in SLE.

Inflammation

Inflammation is a pivotal terminal pathway in SLE progression. It is triggered by immune complex deposition and complement activation, culminating in target organ injury. Lectin pathway-specific molecules modulate inflammatory networks through direct cellular interactions and regulation of signaling pathway.

MBL bidirectionally regulates macrophage and monocyte inflammation. It suppresses macrophage proliferation by upregulating transforming growth factor-beta (TGFβ) expression and activating downstream Smad-dependent pathways that arrest the cell cycle [103]. It also enhances monocytic phagocytosis [104], and dampens macrophage MAPK/NF-κB pathways [92]. MBL binds TLR4 on THP-1 cells, inhibiting lipopolysaccharide-induced NF-κB nuclear translocation and pro-inflammatory responses [105]. Conversely, MBL can activate NF-κB to drive M1 polarization and cytokine secretion [106]. Additionally, the collagen-like region of MBL interacts with natural killer (NK) cells, suppressing interleukin (IL)-2, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and IFN-γ production and cytotoxic function, while enhancing IL-10 [107]. Taken together, MBL acts as a double-edged immunomodulator through context-dependent regulation of innate immunity.

Ficolin-A (a mouse ficolin similar to human ficolin-2) drives macrophages toward a non-classical inflammatory phenotype distinct from M1 polarization [63]. MASP-1 induces endothelial secretion of adhesion molecules (E-selectin, vascular cell adhesion molecule-1) and chemokines (IL-8), promoting leukocyte recruitment, and upregulates histamine and bradykinin receptors to amplify inflammation [108]. Collectin-11 binds D-mannose and L-fucose on fibroblasts and leukocytes, stimulating renal fibroblast proliferation and leukocyte chemotaxis [64]. Lectin pathway mediators orchestrate diverse pro-inflammatory mechanisms.

Targeted therapies against the lectin pathway

Table 2 presents the current progress, administration methods, and other relevant details regarding targeted drugs for the lectin pathway. Narsoplimab, a MASP-2-targeted monoclonal antibody, has shown safety and efficacy in transplant-related thrombotic microangiopathy and IgA nephropathy [109, 110]. Phase II trials for LN are ongoing, and results have not been published. Completed phase I studies support its clinical potential in SLE [111]. Preclinical studies show that MASP-1/3 deficiency mitigates LN in mice without impairing antibody production [112], while MBL administration improves renal outcomes in murine LN models [92]. These findings position lectin pathway molecules as novel therapeutic targets for SLE.

Conclusion

The lectin pathway is important to the pathogenesis, progression, and treatment of SLE. Genetic variants in core molecules (e.g., MBL, ficolins, collectin-11) influence SLE risk and clinical features, while their protein levels correlate with disease activity. Beyond complement activation, these molecules orchestrate critical immunopathologic events independent of the complement cascade, including impaired apoptotic cell clearance, autoantigen presentation, dysregulated T/B cell immunity, excessive IFN-α production, NET dysregulation, and amplified inflammation.

While targeted interventions (e.g., Narsoplimab [MASP-2]) show early promise preclinically and clinically, most remain in early development. Future research should elucidate pathway-specific mechanisms, particularly how lectin pathway molecules affect tolerance and inflammation, to optimize precision therapies. Integrating mechanistic insights with translational research may unlock improved diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutics, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes and quality of life in SLE. These advances may yield more effective strategies targeting lectin pathway dysregulation.
