Can we predict postoperative complications in octogenarians undergoing lung resection?
Authors: Gizem Kececi Ozgur, Hasan Yavuz, Saltuk Burhan Dal, Ahmet Kayahan Tekneci, Ufuk Cagirici, Tevfik Ilker Akcam, Kutsal Turhan, Ali Ozdil, Ayse Gul Ergonul, Alpaslan Cakan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to determine the parameters that should be taken into consideration to predict the development of postoperative complications in patients aged 80 years and over (octogenarians) who underwent lung resection.MethodsThe data of 49 octogenarian patients who underwent lung resection between January 2016 and June 2025 were retrospectively analyzed. Patient demographics, operative parameters, and predictive indices and scores for postoperative complications (Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), Simplified Comorbidity Index (SCI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA), Glasgow Prognostic Score (GPS), The Assess Respiratory Risk in Surgical Patients in Catalonia (ARISCAT) and Physiological and Operative Severity Score for the Enumeration of Mortality and Morbidity (POSSUM)) were calculated. Postoperative complications were recorded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification.ResultsThe mean age of the patients, 79.6% of whom were male, was 81.98 ± 2.07 years. Statistically significantly fewer complications were observed in those who underwent sublobar resection compared to lobar resection (38.9% vs. 76.9%, p = 0.019). No correlation was found between CCI, SCI, ASA, and GPS scores and the development of complications (p = 0.170, p = 0.246, p = 0.774, p = 0.158, respectively), while ARISCAT and POSSUM scores were found to be predictive of complication development (p < 0.001, p < 0.001). In the multivariate analysis, while the extent of resection lost its status as an independent risk factor (p = 0.222), the classified ARISCAT score and the POSSUM score grouped according to the cut-off value determined by ROC analysis remained independent factors in predicting the development of complications. Patients in the high-risk group according to the ARISCAT score (OR = 16.482, 95% CI = 1.376-197.339, p = 0.027) and those with a POSSUM score above 31.100 (OR = 36.308, 95% CI = 3.889-338.952, p = 0.002) had a significantly higher incidence of postoperative complications.ConclusionsIn this single-centre study, higher ARISCAT and POSSUM scores were associated with the development of postoperative complications in octogenarians. Given the limited sample size, the wide confidence intervals, and the predominance of minor (Clavien–Dindo grade I–II) complications, these scores should be regarded as promising adjuncts for risk stratification that require validation in larger, prospective, multicentre studies before being applied to patient selection.

Background

Life expectancy at birth reached 73.3 years in 2024, increasing by 8.4 years compared to 1995, according to World Health Organization (WHO) data. Furthermore, the population aged 80 and over is expected to triple between 2020 and 2050, reaching 426 million [1]. The increasing trend in the elderly population is also reflected in thoracic surgery, with an increase in patients aged 80 years and older (octogenarians) who are candidates for lung resection. Mortality rates after lung resection in octogenarians range from 0% to 15%, with complication rates reaching up to 50% [2–4]. Given this situation, a detailed preoperative evaluation to determine the suitability of patients for surgery and the planning of the operative approach and extent of resection are particularly important in this patient group.

Various indices and scoring systems can be considered in the preoperative evaluation of patients. Among these, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was developed as a prognostic index for predicting mortality rates based on items corresponding to comorbidity [5]. The Simplified Comorbidity Index (SCI) is a score developed by Colinet et al. [6] based on the CCI for patients with non-small cell lung cancer. The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification system was introduced in 1941 to provide clinicians with a standard method to evaluate patients’ medical comorbidities and predict perioperative risk [7]. The Glasgow Prognostic Score (GPS) is an inflammation-based scoring system consisting of elevated CRP and decreased albumin concentration [8]. The Assess Respiratory Risk in Surgical Patients in Catalonia (ARISCAT) score was developed in 2010 based on prospectively collected data from 59 Spanish hospitals [9]. The Physiological and Operative Severity Score for the Enumeration of Mortality and Morbidity (POSSUM) score aims to predict mortality and morbidity in the first 30 days after surgery [10].

This study aimed to identify parameters that should be considered in predicting the development of postoperative complications by evaluating the general characteristics, comorbidities, functional status, CCI, SCI, ASA, GPS, ARISCAT, and POSSUM scores, and surgical characteristics of octogenarians who underwent lung resection.

Methods

Data of 65 octogenarians who underwent surgery at our clinic between January 2016 and June 2025 were retrospectively examined. To ensure the homogeneity of the patient group, the following were excluded: patients who underwent procedures other than lung resection (mediastinal cyst/mass excision, chest wall resection and reconstruction for chest wall tumour, or pleural decortication/hyperthermic intrathoracic chemotherapy); patients whose preoperative or postoperative data could not be obtained; and patients (or the legal guardians of deceased patients) from whom the consent required by the ethics committee for the use of their data could not be obtained. The data of the remaining 49 patients were analysed (Fig. 1).

Patients’ age, gender, smoking history, presence of comorbidities, preoperative pulmonary function tests and blood gas parameters, surgical method (thoracotomy/videothoracoscopic surgery (VATS)), extent of resection (lobar or sublobar resection), histopathological diagnosis and development of postoperative complication were recorded. Complications were categorized according to the Clavien-Dindo classification. Additionally, the CCI, SCI, ASA, GPS, ARISCAT, and POSSUM scores were calculated. Peritoneal spread, included in the operative criteria of the POSSUM score was revised to pleural spread, similar to previous studies in the literature [11]. Accordingly, the criteria were recorded as no pleural spread/sterile pleural effusion/local purulent effusion/pleural carcinomatosis.

The extent of resection (sublobar versus lobar) was determined on an individual basis by combining the patient’s cardiopulmonary reserve (forced expiratory volume in one second [FEV1] and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide [DLCO]), tumour size, location and stage, performance status, and comorbidity burden. Cardiopulmonary reserve—principally the FEV1—was the main determinant, and patients with limited pulmonary reserve were preferentially offered a sublobar resection. In patients with primary lung cancer, lymph node sampling was performed irrespective of the extent of resection (lobar or sublobar).

Perioperative care was managed in accordance with Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) principles. Preoperatively, patients received counselling, education and optimisation, and smoking cessation was advised as part of rehabilitation, although a minority of patients were unable to stop smoking, as reflected in our cohort. A minimally invasive (VATS) approach was preferred whenever feasible. Conversion from VATS to thoracotomy was performed when safe continuation of the thoracoscopic procedure was not feasible, including inadequate visualization, dense pleural or hilar adhesions, calcified hilar lymph nodes, failure to progress safely, technical difficulties, or when oncologically adequate resection and lymph node dissection could not be ensured. Conversion was not considered a surgical failure but rather a patient-safety measure to achieve a technically and oncologically sound resection. Intraoperative and postoperative management included multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia, goal-directed fluid therapy, and postoperative nausea and vomiting prophylaxis. Chest drainage was managed with a single tube that was removed as early as possible, and patients underwent early mobilisation, early oral intake, and early removal of the urinary catheter. Preoperative optimisation was performed in a multidisciplinary manner according to each patient’s comorbidities. Pulmonary rehabilitation and breathing exercises were carried out routinely, and bronchodilator and inhaler therapy was optimised in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or asthma. Patients with cardiac comorbidities were assessed and optimised by cardiology, perioperative glycaemic control was ensured in patients with diabetes mellitus, and patients with chronic kidney disease were managed with nephrology consultation and avoidance of nephrotoxic agents. Preoperative anaemia was corrected when present, and nutritional support was provided to patients at risk of malnutrition.

In descriptive analyses, numbers and percentages were recorded for categorical variables, and mean ± standard deviation and min-max values were recorded for numerical variables. Relationships between categorical variables were assessed using the Chi-Square test. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to analyze the conformity of numerical data to a normal distribution in independent groups. For numerical data, the independent-samples t-test was used when the parametric assumptions were met, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used when they were not. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Factors with p < 0.05 in univariate analyses were included in the multivariate analysis. Logistic regression analysis was used in the multivariate analysis between the development of complications and the independent variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to determine the cut-off value for the Possum score. IBM SPSS 24.0 package program was used for data entry and analysis.

Ethical statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki developed by the World Medical Association. Ege University Faculty of Medicine Research Ethics Committee granted ethical approval for study with decision number 25-9.1T/46 dated 18.09.2025. All patients were over the age of 18. All living patients and the legal guardians of the deceased patients were informed about the study and gave written informed consent to ensure data confidentiality (‘Clinical trial number: not applicable.’).

Results

The mean age of the 49 participants, all aged 80 years or older, was 81.98 ± 2.07 (min-max: 80–87), with 39 (79.6%) male and 10 (20.4%) female. Comorbidities were present in 46 patients (93.9%). The most frequent comorbidities were hypertension (67.3%), diabetes mellitus (28.6%), coronary artery disease (22.4%), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (18.4%), cardiac arrhythmia (10.2%), and hypothyroidism (10.2%); the full distribution is presented in Table 1. A history of smoking was recorded in 37 patients (75.5%), of whom 33 were former smokers and 4 were active smokers at the time of surgery. The mean smoking exposure was 37.33 ± 28.25 pack-years (range: 0–120). As a result of histopathological examination, 40 patients (81.6%) were diagnosed with primary lung cancer, 5 patients (10.2%) with metastatic lung cancer and 4 patients (8.2%) with benign lung disease. Sublobar resection was performed in 73.5% of patients, and lobar resection in 26.5%. Of these procedures, VATS was used in 53.1% and thoracotomy in 46.9%.

Preoperative mean FEV1 value and FVC percentage were 1964.96 ± 575.1 mL and 93.78 ± 23.50, respectively. Based on ASA classification, 4.1% of the patients were in ASA I, 59.2% in ASA II, and 36.7% in ASA III group. GPS was zero (0) in 75.5% and one (1) in 24.5%. Mean CCI and SCI values, and mean POSSUM and ARISCAT scores were 2.39 ± 1.92 (min-max: 0–7); 7.76 ± 4.46 (min-max: 0–14); 37.67 ± 19.27 (min-max: 12.0-92.7) and 47.47 ± 8.81 (min-max: 40–67), respectively. When patients were classified as low- (< 26), intermediate- (26–44), and high-risk (≥ 45) according to their ARISCAT scores, 53.1% were in the intermediate-risk group and 46.9% in the high-risk group, with no patient classified as low risk (Table 1). Consequently, the ARISCAT comparisons in this study were made between the intermediate- and high-risk groups.

The mean postoperative drainage time was 5.12 ± 4.34 days, and the mean hospital stay was 5.69 ± 3.94 days. While no mortality occurred in any patient, complications developed in 49% of patients. According to the Clavien-Dindo classification, 54.2% of these complications were classified as grade I and 37.5% as grade II. Grade IIIA complications were noted in one patient (4.2%) who had pleural effusion requiring drainage under local anesthesia, and grade IIIB complications were noted in one patient (4.2%) who required surgery under general anesthesia due to prolonged air leak (Table 2). The most frequent complications were prolonged air leak (10 patients, 20.4%) and the need for blood or blood-product transfusion (7 patients, 14.3%), followed by postoperative atrial fibrillation and electrolyte disturbance (4 patients each, 8.2%). Glucose dysregulation, deterioration of renal function, pneumonia and atelectasis each occurred in 2 patients (4.1%), and incomplete lung expansion, pleural effusion and haematuria each in 1 patient (2.0%); as some patients had more than one complication, these categories are not mutually exclusive. The episodes of postoperative atrial fibrillation were all managed medically (Clavien–Dindo grade II).

When factors that may affect the development of complications were analyzed, no significant association was found between the development of complications and gender (p = 0.725), smoking history (p = 0.212), smoking exposure measured in pack-years (p = 0.322), surgical method (VATS vs. thoracotomy) (p = 0.117), FEV1 (p = 0.525), or FVC (p = 0.937). However, complications developed in 38.9% of those who underwent sublobar resection, compared to 76.9% in those who underwent lobectomy, and the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.019).

The predictability of the relationship between risk scores and complication development was also analyzed. Complications developed in 50.0% of patients in ASA class I, 44.8% of patients in class II, and 55.6% of patients in class III, with no significant difference (p = 0.774). Complications developed in 43.2% of patients with GPS 0, while this rate was 66.7% in those with GPS 1, and the difference was not significant (p = 0.158). No statistically significant difference was found in terms of CCI and SCI scores in patients with and without complications (1.92 ± 1.58 vs. 2.84 ± 2.13, p = 0.170; 8.71 ± 3.74 vs. 6.84 ± 4.96, p = 0.246; respectively). When the CCI was dichotomised at a threshold of ≥ 4, complications occurred in 3 of 11 patients (27.3%) with a CCI ≥ 4 and in 21 of 38 (55.3%) with a CCI < 4, without a statistically significant difference (p = 0.171).The POSSUM score was statistically significantly higher in the group of patients who developed complications (49.05 ± 19.40 vs. 26.74 ± 11.31, p < 0.001). Similarly, patients who developed complications had significantly higher ARISCAT scores (51.92 ± 9.08 vs. 43.20 ± 6.11, p < 0.001). Furthermore, when patients were classified according to their ARISCAT scores, complications developed in 26.9% of patients in the intermediate-risk group, while this rate was 73.9% in the high-risk group, and the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.001). Using ROC analysis, the POSSUM score cut-off value for predicting postoperative complications was determined as 31.100 [area under the curve (AUC) 0.873; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.776–0.969; p < 0.001] (Fig. 2). Based on this cut-off value, the POSSUM score’s sensitivity and specificity for predicting complications were recorded as 87% and 76%, respectively.

In the final step of the study, multivariate analysis was performed using logistic regression analysis with statistically significant variables for the development of complications. This analysis revealed that the extent of resection lost its role as an independent risk factor (p = 0.222). On the contrary, the ARISCAT score classified by cut-off value, and the POSSUM score grouped by cut-off value, remained independent predictors of complications. The development of complications was found to be significantly higher in those in the high-risk group according to the ARISCAT score (OR = 16.482, 95% CI = 1.376-197.339, p = 0.027) and those with a POSSUM score above 31.100 (OR = 36.308, 95% CI = 3.889-338.952, p = 0.002) (Table 4). Discussion

The risk of comorbidities, physiological deficits, geriatric syndromes, and frailty increases with age. However, with advancing technology, improvements in surgical techniques, and the widespread use of minimally invasive approaches, age alone is no longer considered a contraindication to surgery. Nevertheless, lung resection requires special care in octogenarians [12]. Patient selection and careful management of the perioperative process are particularly important in reducing postoperative complications. A review of the literature reveals that a study comparing postoperative complications among 106 patients divided into three groups: under 70, between 70 and 79, and 80 years and older. The study reported that complication rates were similar across age groups, with no statistically significant difference (18%, 32%, and 29%, p = 0.4) [13]. Stamenovic et al. [14] reported no difference between groups in terms of postoperative complications in their study of 208 patients who underwent anatomical resection and were divided into two groups: those aged 70–79 and those aged 80 and over (p = 0.78). Another study involving a total of 1232 patients from all age groups emphasized that being 80 years of age or older did not cause an increase in morbidity (p = 0.09) or 30-day mortality (p = 0.06) [15]. Therefore, postoperative complications following lung resection in patients 80 years of age or older appear to be at acceptable levels for this age group. In our cohort, perioperative outcomes were favourable, with no in-hospital mortality, and mean chest-tube and hospital-stay durations of 5.12 and 5.69 days, respectively. Complications occurred in 49% of patients; however, the great majority were minor: 22 of the 24 complications (91.7%) were Clavien–Dindo grade I–II, with only one grade IIIa and one grade IIIb event and no grade IV–V complications or deaths. Postoperative atrial fibrillation is one of the most common complications after lung resection and an important contributor to morbidity. In our cohort, it occurred in four patients (8.2%), all of whom were managed medically (Clavien–Dindo grade II) without haemodynamic instability or related mortality. This relatively low rate, compared with the approximately 10–20% commonly reported after thoracic surgery, may be explained by several aspects of our perioperative management. Patients with cardiac comorbidities—which were frequent in this elderly cohort—were optimised preoperatively by cardiology; perioperative care followed ERAS principles, including multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia, goal-directed fluid therapy that avoided volume overload, and early mobilisation; and all patients underwent continuous rhythm monitoring in the early postoperative period, together with routine electrolyte assessment and correction on the first postoperative day. No pharmacological atrial fibrillation prophylaxis was administered. Nevertheless, given the small sample size, this finding should be interpreted with caution.The composite outcome used in our analyses was therefore the occurrence of any complication, which was driven predominantly by minor events. Consequently, the predictive performance of the ARISCAT and POSSUM scores in this study reflects mainly their ability to identify patients at risk of minor complications; their value for predicting major morbidity or mortality could not be evaluated in this cohort and remains uncertain.

Although age alone is not considered a contraindication for lung resection and postoperative complication rates are acceptable, postoperative complications in octogenarians should be carefully assessed during the preoperative evaluation. Several indices and scoring systems have been developed for this purpose. We deliberately evaluated established, widely used and readily calculable risk scores (CCI, SCI, ASA, GPS, ARISCAT and POSSUM), each derived from routinely available preoperative data and previously applied across surgical—including thoracic—populations, in order to assess their utility specifically in octogenarians undergoing lung resection, a group in which they have rarely been examined together. While the CCI is one of these systems, the SCI, although developed on the basis of the CCI, also takes into account comorbidities (respiratory diseases, cardiovascular diseases, neoplastic comorbidities, renal failure, and diabetes mellitus) as well as alcohol and tobacco use. In our study, the difference between the mean CCI and SCI scores of patients with and without complications for both indices was not significant (p = 0.170, p = 0.246, respectively). We also evaluated the CCI using a pre-specified cut-off (≥ 4 vs. <4) rather than a data-derived threshold, to avoid optimism bias; this dichotomised analysis was likewise non-significant (p = 0.171). Thus, neither the continuous CCI score nor a CCI ≥ 4 threshold was associated with postoperative complications in our cohort, although the small number of patients with a CCI ≥ 4 (n = 11) limits the interpretation of this subgroup. Contrary to these results, Shao et al. [16] reported that a higher CCI score (CCI > 3) was associated with more postoperative complications after lung resection. In a study involving 423 patients who underwent lung resection, the postoperative complication rate was found to be 8% in the group of patients with CCI = 0 and 15% in the group of patients with CCI ≥ 1 (p = 0.024) [17]. Sandfeld-Paulsen et al. [18] found that the SCI score was associated with the development of postoperative complications in patients with lung cancer, while another study involving 216 patients aged 75 years and older who underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic lung resection reported that the SCI score statistically significantly predicted postoperative complications (p = 0.02) [19].

The ASA classification, which consists of 6 sequential scoring categories to concisely convey an overall assessment of the underlying physiological status, ranging from completely healthy (Class I) to brain dead awaiting organ donation (Class VI), is known to be predictive of complications after thoracic surgery [20–22]. However, there are also studies that support the results found in our study. Miura et al. [23] found a relationship between ASA score and postoperative mortality in a group of octogenarians who underwent lung resection, but reported that ASA score had no statistically significant predictive effect on complications. Similarly, in our study, complications were noted in 50.0% of patients in ASA class I, 44.8% in class II, and 55.6% in class III; the difference between them was not significant (p = 0.774). GPS is a prognostic score and is considered the most valid inflammation risk score in cancer patients. While there are studies in the literature using it to determine prognosis in general, no studies have investigated its relationship with postoperative complications. In our study, complications developed in 43.2% of patients with a GPS score of 0, while this rate was 66.7% in those with a GPS score of 1, and the difference was not significant (p = 0.158).

The ARISCAT model was developed for patients undergoing planned or emergency surgery under general or regional anesthesia; patients undergoing obstetric procedures or organ transplantation were excluded. The score estimates the risk of postoperative pulmonary complications based on seven preoperative (age, preoperative oxygen saturation, presence of respiratory infection within the past month, presence of preoperative anemia) and perioperative (surgical incision, duration of surgery, emergency or elective surgical procedure) variables. Patients are classified into low, intermediate, and high risk groups based on their calculated ARISCAT scores. The ARISCAT score has been identified as a predictive factor for postoperative complications in various surgical procedures [24, 25]. In our study, patients who developed complications had significantly higher ARISCAT scores among those who underwent lung resection (51.92 ± 9.08 vs. 43.20 ± 6.11, p < 0.001). Additionally, when patients were classified according to their ARISCAT scores, complications developed in 26.9% of patients in the intermediate risk group, while this rate was 73.9% in patients in the high-risk group, and the difference between them was found to be statistically significant (p = 0.001). Similarly, in a study involving 108 patients who underwent thoracic surgery, a higher ARISCAT score (OR = 1.22 [1.02–1.47]; p = 0.040) and a high ARISCAT grade (OR = 2.77 [1.06–7.21]; p = 0.037) were shown to be important predictors of postoperative complication development [26]. On the contrary, Zorrilla-Vaca et al. [27] reported that the predictive effect of the ARISCAT score was low in their study in which they included 2,104 patients who underwent pulmonary resection and investigated the relationship between different scoring systems and postoperative pulmonary complications.

POSSUM score is a scoring system calculated using various parameters: physiological score (age, cardiac and respiratory status, systolic blood pressure, pulse, Glasgow coma score, electrocardiography (ECG), white blood cell, hemoglobin, urea, sodium and potassium levels) and operational severity score (operation severity, presence of multiple procedures, blood loss, presence of peritoneal contamination, presence of malignancy and surgical method). It has been reported to be an effective scoring system and used to predict postoperative complications in malignancies such as gastroesophageal and head and neck, as well as in thoracic surgery [28, 29]. While it has been reported in the past that the POSSUM score can be used to predict complications after thoracic surgery, studies investigating this relationship in the literature are quite limited [11, 30]. In our study, the POSSUM score was found to be statistically significantly higher in the patient group that developed complications (49.05 ± 19.40 vs. 26.74 ± 11.31, p < 0.001). Furthermore, according to the cut-off value (31.100) determined by ROC analysis, the sensitivity of the POSSUM score for predicting the development of complications was 87% and the specificity was 76%. As this threshold was derived from the same dataset, these values represent apparent performance and require external validation. Supporting these results, Wang et al. [31] included 138 non-small cell lung cancer patients in their study and found that the difference in POSSUM scoring between the complication group and the non-complication group was statistically significant (p < 0.01).

In this study, no significant relationship was found between the development of complications and other factors that may affect the development of complications: gender (p = 0.725), smoking history (p = 0.212), smoking exposure measured in pack-years (p = 0.322), surgical method (VATS vs. thoracotomy) (p = 0.117), FEV1 (p = 0.525), and FVC (p = 0.937). Smoking is a well-recognized risk factor for postoperative pulmonary complications after lung resection. In the present study, however, neither smoking history nor cumulative smoking exposure measured in pack-years was associated with postoperative complications. This finding may be related to the limited sample size and statistical power of the study. Furthermore, the majority of patients with a smoking history were former smokers (33 of 37 patients), which may have reduced the impact of active tobacco exposure on postoperative outcomes. Several studies have demonstrated lower postoperative morbidity rates following VATS compared with thoracotomy, particularly in elderly patients, owing to reduced surgical trauma, less postoperative pain, and earlier mobilization. Prospective and large comparative studies have generally reported lower postoperative complication rates with VATS than with thoracotomy. In the randomised VIOLET trial, VATS lobectomy was associated with fewer in-hospital complications and a shorter length of stay than open surgery [32], and a large propensity-matched analysis from the European Society of Thoracic Surgeons database similarly reported lower postoperative morbidity after VATS [33]. This benefit is, however, not universal: in the randomised trial by Bendixen et al., although VATS provided clear advantages in postoperative pain and quality of life, the overall complication rate was comparable between the two approaches [34]. In our study we did not detect a statistically significant difference between the two approaches (p = 0.117). Importantly, the direction of our results was nonetheless consistent with the literature, as complications were numerically less frequent after VATS than after thoracotomy (38.5% vs. 60.9%). We attribute the absence of statistical significance primarily to the limited sample size and the consequently restricted statistical power. In addition, the retrospective and non-randomised design entails a risk of selection bias and confounding by indication, as the surgical approach was determined by patient- and tumour-related characteristics rather than allocated at random, and was closely intertwined with the extent of resection in our predominantly sublobar cohort. Finally, because the great majority of complications were minor (Clavien–Dindo grade I–II), our composite outcome of any complication may have been relatively insensitive to the specific benefits of VATS, which are often most apparent for major morbidity, postoperative pain, and length of hospital stay. Low FEV1 is known to be an independent risk factor, particularly for postoperative pulmonary complications [26]. However, our study did not find a correlation between FEV1 values and postoperative complications. This can be explained by the fact that patients with low FEV1 values included in the study mostly underwent sublobar resection. As is known, sublobar resection is associated with fewer postoperative complications than lobar resection. Zhang et al. [35] examined the data of 1,579 patients over the age of 65 and reported that fewer complications were observed in patients who underwent sublobar resection compared to those who underwent lobar resection (3.9% vs. 7.0%, p = 0.0238). Similarly, in our study, complications were noted in 38.9% of those who underwent sublobar resection and 76.9% of those who underwent lobectomy, and the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.019). However, as a result of multivariate analysis, extent of resection lost its ability to be an independent risk factor. (p = 0.222). Importantly, this finding should be interpreted as confounding by indication rather than a causal effect: because the extent of resection was selected according to patient characteristics—patients with poorer pulmonary function were preferentially offered sublobar resection—the lower complication rate observed after sublobar resection cannot be attributed to the procedure itself. The loss of significance of the extent of resection in the multivariable analysis (p = 0.222) is consistent with this interpretation. On the other hand, the classified ARISCAT score and the POSSUM score grouped according to the cut-off value remained independent factors in predicting the development of complications. The development of postoperative complications was significantly higher in those in the high-risk group according to the ARISCAT score (OR = 16.482, 95% CI = 1.376-197.339, p = 0.027) and in those with a POSSUM score above 31.100 (OR = 36.308, 95% CI = 3.889-338.952, p = 0.002). Nevertheless, the wide confidence intervals around these odds ratios indicate that, although the associations reached statistical significance, the magnitude of the effects is estimated with substantial uncertainty and should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusions

In conclusion, similar to the literature, chronological age is not a contraindication for surgery and lung resection can be performed with reasonable complication rates in the elderly patient group. Predicting postoperative complications in this patient group and thereby better patient selection and management is extremely important.In our cohort, higher ARISCAT and POSSUM scores were associated with postoperative complications and may therefore be useful for risk stratification. However, because most complications were minor (Clavien–Dindo grade I–II) and the analysis was limited by a small sample size and wide confidence intervals, these findings should be regarded as preliminary and require external validation in larger, prospective, multicentre cohorts before being incorporated into routine patient selection.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the study has a retrospective design, and the data were obtained from existing patient records. This situation brings with it some limitations specific to retrospective studies, such as potential missing information and recording errors. Additionally, the study was conducted at a single center and therefore had a relatively small number of patients, which may limit the generalizability of the results. In addition, although the cohort was predominantly malignant, a small number of benign lesions (8.2%) were included; the subgroup was too small to allow adjustment for histology, and it was not possible to completely exclude any remaining confounding factors. The multivariable model should also be interpreted with caution. Complications occurred in 24 of the 49 patients; with three covariates entered into the model, this corresponds to approximately eight events per variable, below the commonly recommended minimum of ten, with a consequent risk of model overfitting. This limited power is reflected in the wide confidence intervals of the ARISCAT and POSSUM odds ratios (e.g., POSSUM: OR 36.3, 95% CI 3.9–339.0); although these associations reached statistical significance, the corresponding effect sizes are imprecise and likely overestimated, and require confirmation in larger cohorts. Furthermore, in the multivariable analysis the ARISCAT and POSSUM scores were dichotomised at their cut-off values rather than modelled as continuous variables; although this facilitates clinical interpretation, dichotomisation discards information and may reduce statistical efficiency. In addition, the POSSUM cut-off value (31.100) was both derived from and evaluated in the same dataset using ROC analysis. The resulting sensitivity (87%) and specificity (76%) therefore reflect apparent (in-sample) performance and are likely optimistic; as no internal validation (e.g., bootstrap or cross-validation) or calibration assessment was performed, the discriminative performance of this threshold requires confirmation in independent cohorts. However, based on our findings, future multicenter, prospective studies with larger number of the patients and more comprehensive design will be important for furthering the topic and development of the literature. Future studies should be designed prospectively, ideally as multicentre collaborations, with an a priori sample-size estimation adequate for multivariable modelling (e.g., at least ten events per candidate variable), and should include an external validation cohort to confirm the predictive performance and the proposed cut-off values of the ARISCAT and POSSUM scores. Moreover we did not evaluate dedicated thoracic- or cardiothoracic-specific risk models (such as Thoracoscore [36] or the ESTS Eurolung models [37]), which may offer better procedure-specific calibration than the general scores assessed here. Future studies should directly compare these general scores with thoracic-specific models in octogenarians.
