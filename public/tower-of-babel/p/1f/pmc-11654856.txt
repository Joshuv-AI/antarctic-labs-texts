U‐shaped Association Between Weight‐Adjusted‐Waist Index and Arterial Stiffness Among Adult Hypertensive Patients: A Population‐Based Study in the United States
Authors: Taotao Wei, Xin Lin, Jie Ma, Luosha Wang, Jing Su, Jing Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe weight‐adjusted‐waist index (WWI) is an innovative measure of obesity that appears to surpass body mass index (BMI) in assessing lean body mass and fat mass. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between WWI and AS in hypertensive adults in the United States. The study included 9753 adults diagnosed with hypertension from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which spanned the years 2007–2016. WWI was calculated by dividing waist circumference (in cm) by the square root of body weight (in kg), and arterial stiffness (represented by estimated pulse wave velocity [ePWV]) was analyzed as the outcome. Weighted multiple linear regression and smooth curve fitting were used to test for linear and nonlinear associations. Threshold effects were determined using a two‐part linear regression model. Additionally, subgroup analyses and interaction tests were conducted to gain a more in‐depth understanding of the observed associations. The mean WWI of the participants was 11.32 ± 0.76. After multivariable adjustment, WWI showed a significant nonlinear association with ePWV, with a U‐shaped association observed between the two. Specifically, WWI below the threshold of 10.23 was negatively associated with arterial stiffness (β = −0.39, 95% CI: −0.54 to −0.25), while WWI above the threshold of 10.23 was positively associated with arterial stiffness (β = 0.04, 95% CI: 0.01–0.07). To conclude, the present findings imply that maintaining WWI within an optimal range could reduce AS in hypertensive individuals and potentially decrease cardiovascular risk. However, this observation needs to be confirmed in large clinical trials.

Introduction

Arterial stiffness (AS) is considered an independent predictor of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality in the United States and globally [1, 2]. Both age and blood pressure are potential risk factors for AS. Assessing vascular age through AS measurements provides better insight into CVD risk than the measurement of traditional risk factors alone. Carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) is the most extensively studied indicator of AS [2, 3, 4]. However, cfPWV measurements require trained and experienced medical personnel and specialized instruments [5], and this limits their wider application in clinical practice worldwide. To broaden the clinical utility of AS, various methods for assessing AS have emerged, including mathematical equations derived from age and mean blood pressure variables [6]. Among these alternative measures, estimated pulse wave velocity (ePWV) has shown strong correlations with several in vivo assessments and has demonstrated independent predictive power when compared to traditional risk scoring systems [7, 8]. Accordingly, in this study, ePWV was used as the primary indicator of AS.

The overall prevalence of obesity among adults in the United States has reached 39.5% and continues to increase [9]. Obesity is strongly associated with AS and is an independent predictor of the risk of developing AS in the future [10, 11]. BMI, a commonly used index of obesity, has long been studied in relation to AS; however, BMI‐based evaluation of obesity is controversial in the study of AS. Over the past decade, some studies have found a positive correlation between BMI and AS [12, 13], while others have found a negative correlation [14, 15, 16] or no correlation between the two [17, 18]. These conflicting results suggest that the relationship between obesity and AS is still not completely clear. Moreover, the use of BMI as an obesity measure may be inaccurate as it cannot be used to differentiate between fat, muscle mass, and abdominal obesity [19, 20]. In 2018, Park et al. proposed a new index of obesity, the weight‐adjusted‐waist index (WWI). Compared with BMI, WWI was found to better differentiate between fat and muscle mass components and mainly reflects central obesity [21]. In line with this, numerous studies have shown a positive correlation between WWI and hypertension, diabetes, and even all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality [22, 23, 24].

Hypertension is an important risk factor for developing AS [25], and obesity is also a risk factor for hypertension [26]. Importantly, the coexistence of obesity and hypertension significantly increases the risk of developing AS. However, to date, no studies have established a connection between WWI and AS risk. Given that WWI is a common and easily accessible marker in clinical practice, exploring its potential as a risk indicator could prove highly beneficial in terms of identifying patients at risk and adopting preventive measures. Therefore, the objective of this investigation was to examine the relationship between WWI and the risk of atherosclerosis in an adult hypertensive population in the United States.

Methods

Collection of Survey Data

The authors obtained data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a national population‐based cross‐sectional study conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) to collect information on potential health risk factors and nutritional status of noninstitutionalized civilians in the United States [27]. As part of this survey, data are collected using a complex, stratified and multistage probability sampling method in a biennial cycle. This sampling technique and frequency ensure the representativeness of the sample.

The protocol for the NHANES study was approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all survey participants or from parents and/or legal guardians of participants under 16 years of age. The detailed NHANES study design and data are publicly available at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.

Study Population

This cross‐sectional study included participants from the NHANES study, which was conducted between 2007 and 2016. The exclusion criteria for our analysis were (1) age <20 years, (2) pregnancy, (3) missing WWI and blood pressure data, and (4) participants not diagnosed with hypertension. A total of 50 588 participants were initially recruited. After the exclusion of individuals aged <20 years (n = 21 387), pregnant individuals (n = 315), those with missing WWI data (n = 2771) or blood pressure data (n = 2485), and those who were not diagnosed with hypertension (n = 13 875), 9753 eligible subjects aged ≥20 years were finally included in our analysis. Calculation of Blood Pressure, WWI, and ePWV

Blood pressure: Blood pressure was measured by well‐trained medical professionals using a mercury sphygmomanometer with an appropriately sized cuff. The participants were required to rest for 5 min, following which the measurements were taken three times consecutively. The average of the three measurements was calculated and used for further analysis. Hypertension was defined as: (a) systolic blood pressure (SBP) of 140 mm Hg or higher, (b) diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of 90 mm Hg or higher, and (c) self‐reported use of antihypertensive medications or a previous diagnosis of hypertension by a physician.

WWI: This was considered as an exposure variable and was calculated by dividing WC (cm) by the square root of weight (kg).

ePWV: The primary outcome assessed was AS, which is represented by ePWV. ePWV is used to evaluate the severity of AS, with higher ePWV scores corresponding to more severe AS. ePWV was calculated using a previously reported equation derived from reference values for AS, age, and mean blood pressure (MBP) [7, 8]. ePWV=9.587−0.402×age+4.560×10−3×age2−2.621×10−5×age2×MBP+3.176×10−3×age×MBP−1.832×10−2×MBP where MBP was calculated as follows: DBP + 0.4 × (SBP − DBP).

Covariates

Population demographics (age, gender, race, weight, and WC), health‐related behaviors (smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity), health conditions (hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia), physical examination data, and laboratory data were collected and managed by trained personnel according to standardized questionnaires and mobile examination center protocols. BMI was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by the square of height (m). Race/ethnicity was classified as Mexican American, Non‐Hispanic White, Non‐Hispanic Black, and Other. Physical activity was assessed based on whether or not the participant engaged in moderate physical activity (yes/no), and alcohol consumption was classified as “never drinking” (less than 12 drinks per year) or “drinking” (at least 12 drinks per year). Smokers were defined as those who had smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime, while nonsmokers were defined as those who had smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime. Uncontrolled hypertension was defined as SBP above 140 mm Hg and/or DBP above 90 mm Hg, based on the findings of previous studies [28, 29]. Diabetes was defined as (a) hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) ≥6.5%, fasting blood glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, or 2‐h blood glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L and (b) self‐reported use of antidiabetic medications or diagnosis by a physician [30]. Hyperlipidemia was defined as (a) total cholesterol (TCHO) ≥5.18 mmol/L; (b) high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) <1.04 mmol/L (men) or <1.30 mmol/L (women); and (c) self‐reported use of cholesterol‐lowering medications or diagnosis by a physician [30]. Our covariates included the following continuous variables: TCHO (mmol/L), HDL‐C (mmol/L), and HbA1c (%). Detailed measurement procedures for these variables are publicly available at www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted according to the CDC guidelines. To account for the high variability in the dataset, we employed weighted and variance estimation methods. Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations, while categorical variables are presented as percentages. Weighted student's t‐tests (for continuous variables) or weighted chi‐square tests (for categorical variables) were used to assess differences between participants grouped by WWI quartiles. We developed three models based on WWI values: in Model 1, no adjustment for covariates was made; in Model 2, gender, age, and race were adjusted; in Model 3, adjustment was made for sex, age, race, BMI, moderate physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, total cholesterol, HDL‐C, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. Weighted multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to explore the linear relationship between WWI and ePWV, while smooth curve fitting and threshold effect assessment were used to evaluate the nonlinear association between WWI and ePWV. Subgroup analyses of the association between WWI and ePWV were conducted using stratification factors, including gender (male/female), age (<60 years/≥60 years), uncontrolled hypertension (yes/no), smoking status (yes/no), alcohol consumption (yes/no), diabetes (yes/no), and hyperlipidemia (yes/no). These stratification factors were also considered as prespecified potential effect modifiers. An interaction term was added to test for heterogeneity in the associations between subgroups. All analyses were performed using R 3.4.3 and EmpowerStats 2.0, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Participants

The baseline demographic characteristics of the included participants are presented in Table 1. A total of 50 588 participants were included: 50.3% male; 49.7% female; mean age, 57.15 ± 14.90 years. The mean WWI was 11.32 ± 0.76, with the WWI quartiles 1, 2, 3, and 4 corresponding to 8.59–10.86, 10.87–11.38, 11.39–11.89, and 11.90–14.79, respectively. Significant differences were observed among different WWI quartiles in terms of age, gender, race, smoking, alcohol consumption, moderate physical activity, BMI, SBP, DBP, uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, total cholesterol, HDL‐C, HbA1c, and ePWV. Participants with higher WWI were more likely to be older non‐Hispanic white individuals, with an increased likelihood of obesity, dyslipidemia, and diabetes (p < 0.001). The mean overall ePWV was 9.47 ± 2.06, and it increased with higher WWI quartiles: the mean ePWV scores for WWI quartiles 1, 2, 3, and 4 were 8.65 ± 1.86, 9.38 ± 1.97, 9.88 ± 2.06, and 10.19 ± 2.04, respectively.

Correlation of WWI WIth ePWV

Table 2 shows the association between WWI and ePWV. We found that higher WWI was associated with higher ePWV in both the crude and fully adjusted models. Specifically, an increase of 1 unit in WWI was associated with an increase of 0.04 units in ePWV (β = 0.04, 95% CI: 0.01–0.07). We performed sensitivity analyses after converting WWI from a continuous variable to a categorical variable (quartiles). In the fully adjusted model, ePWV increased with increasing quartiles of WWI: compared to the lowest quartile of WWI (Quartile 1), ePWV in Quartile 4 was higher by 0.06 units, but the p value did not reach statistical significance. However, as a continuous variable, WWI was statistically significant with ePWV, and the p value was significant. At the same time, we observed an inverse correlation between WWI and ePWV both before and after adjusting for multiple covariates (Table 2, Models 2 and 3). We considered the following possible explanations: (a) The study population included 9753 individuals, with 5370 (55.06%) aged <60 years and 4383 (44.94%) aged ≥60 years. Notably, subgroup analyses revealed a significant negative association in the group aged <60. Additionally, regression analysis of the covariates adjusted in Model 2 showed a significant negative correlation after adjusting for age. (b) In the smoothed curve fitting, we observed a U‐shaped association between WWI and ePWV, which may also contribute to this negative correlation. However, this difference does not affect the association between WWI and ePWV.

Non‐Linear Relationship Between WWI and ePWV

The nonlinear relationship between WWI and ePWV was described by smoothed curve fitting (penalized spline method) (Figures 2 and 3). By employing a linear regression model, we identified a U‐shaped association between WWI and ePWV. We found the inflection point of WWI to be 10.23. That is, when WWI was below 10.23, for each unit increase in WWI, ePWV decreased by 0.39 units (95% CI = −0.54 to −0.25). Conversely, when WWI exceeded 10.23, for each unit increase in WWI, ePWV increased by 0.04 units (95% CI = 0.01–0.07). Subgroup analyses by gender revealed a similar U‐shaped relationship for both males and females, with inflection points at 10.88 and 10.32, respectively. Additionally, a more pronounced trend was observed in males (Table 3).

Subgroup Analysis

To assess whether the association between WWI and ePWV is consistent in the overall population and to identify potential heterogeneous subgroups, we conducted subgroup analyses and interaction tests based on gender, age, smoking, drinking, BMI, uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. Our results indicate that these associations are not consistent. As shown in Figure 4, we detected significant interactions for age and diabetes (p for the interactions <0.05), while gender, smoking, drinking, uncontrolled hypertension, and hyperlipidemia showed no statistical significance. WWI and ePWV maintained a positive correlation in females, those older than 60 years, hyperlipidemic, smokers, and non‐diabetic participants, while they exhibited a negative correlation in the age group younger than 60 years. In summary, our findings suggest that the association between WWI and ePWV is stronger in males, individuals younger than 60 years old, and nondiabetic subgroups.

Discussion

This study focused on hypertensive adults enrolled in the NHANES study from 2007 to 2016, to evaluate the relationship between WWI and ePWV among hypertensive adults in the United States. In this cross‐sectional study, we found a U‐shaped relationship between WWI and ePWV in hypertensive adults, with the inflection point being WWI = 10.23. ePWV is considered an indicator of AS. Thus, WWI < 10.23 appears to have a negative association with the development of AS, whereas WWI > 10.23 is positively associated with the development of AS. These findings suggest that WWI can be considered as an intervening factor in the prevention and treatment of AS and be monitored to potentially reduce the occurrence of AS.

As far as we know, this is the first study to investigate the relationship between WWI and AS in a population of hypertensive adults in the United States. Previous epidemiological studies have found a significant correlation between obesity and high AS [11, 31]. However, the relationship between adiposity parameters and AS remains controversial in relevant research. That is, some clinical studies have indicated a positive correlation between adiposity indicators and AS [12, 13, 31, 32], while others have reported inconsistent results [14, 17, 33] or no correlation between the two [17, 33]. A study involving 11 061 healthy participants revealed a positive correlation between brachial‐ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV), another indicator of AS, and waist/hip ratio, body shape index, and body roundness index, but a negative correlation with BMI [13]. Another study involving 2647 participants found that baPWV was only correlated with body shape index and visceral adiposity index, but not with BMI [32]. Furthermore, a longitudinal study by Liao et al. involving 1553 participants found no relationship between BMI and AS [18]. These significant differences may stem from several factors, including differences in the study cohorts, ethnic variations, and the obesity paradox observed when BMI is used as an indicator of obesity. The obesity paradox suggests that obesity may not necessarily shorten patients' expected lifespan: that is, compared to individuals with normal weight, overweight individuals may have slightly lower mortality risks and may even exhibit beneficial effects in certain circumstances. WWI is a recently designed anthropometric index, and related studies have confirmed its ability to differentiate between muscle mass and fat mass [34]. In line with these previous studies, the WWI data of this study indicate that central obesity is a risk factor for AS and provides control thresholds for WWI levels.

Currently, the underlying mechanisms related to the observed U‐shaped relationship between WWI and AS in hypertensive populations have not been well elucidated [35], and several mechanisms may be involved. WWI is positively correlated with fat mass and negatively correlated with lean body mass [36]. Lower lean body mass results in lower glucose uptake into muscles, while physical activity increases lean body mass, improving endothelial function, oxidative stress, insulin resistance, and inflammation, subsequently ameliorating AS [37]. Abdominal fat, as a marker of excess ectopic fat, may reflect dysfunction of adipose tissue as WWI increases. Visceral fat actively secretes excessive inflammatory mediators, leading to amplification of insulin resistance and exacerbation of oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction [38], thereby promoting AS [39]. On the other hand, adipocyte hyperplasia and hypertrophy may induce dysregulation of adipokines [39]. Adipokines are bioactive peptides released by visceral adipose tissue with endocrine functions. Dysregulation of adipokines can lead to vascular inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and vascular remodeling, and thus result in AS [41, 42]. The current findings demonstrate sex‐dependent differences in the relationship between WWI and ePWV that can be explained by differences in body composition between men and women. That is, for any given waist circumference or BMI, men have more visceral fat tissue, while women have more subcutaneous fat tissue [43].

One of the strengths of this study is that it is based on data from NHANES, a nationally representative, population‐based sampling dataset obtained using standardized protocols, with a sufficiently large sample size. Moreover, all the analyses considered appropriate NHANES sampling weights to ensure that the sample selection was representative. Another strength is that we adjusted for confounding factors to ensure the robustness of the current findings. However, our study has some limitations. For one, this is a cross‐sectional analysis, so the temporality of the trends cannot be determined. Additionally, despite adjusting for several relevant confounding factors, we cannot rule out the influence of other confounders. Therefore, our study results should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we report a U‐shaped relationship between WWI and AS in a large adult hypertensive population of the United States. These findings imply that maintaining WWI within an optimal range is crucial for effectively managing the occurrence of AS in hypertensive individuals. However, to confirm these conclusions, additional longitudinal studies on a broader cohort are needed.
