Construct Validity of the Toronto IBD Global Endoscopic Reporting Score Compared to Inflammatory Biomarkers After 12-Month Follow-Up
Authors: Eran Zittan, Matthew Levy, Shiraz Vered, A Hillary Steinhart, Raquel Milgrom, Mark S Silverberg, Shira Zelber-Sagi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsThe Toronto IBD Global Endoscopic Reporting (TIGER) score is a single endoscopic scoring index for both patients with Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC). The goal of this study was to assess the direct relationship between TIGER scores and fecal calprotectin (FC) and C-reactive protein (CRP) after 12 months.MethodsA prospective 12-month study conducted in 1 tertiary IBD center. Baseline colonoscopy was performed. Moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement was defined as a TIGER score ≥ 100, Simple Endoscopic Score for Crohn’s disease ≥ 6, Mayo Endoscopic Score > 1. FC and CRP levels were documented at each visit. Baseline TIGER, SES-CD, and MES were utilized as a predictor for FC and CRP levels after 12 months.ResultsThe study population included 107 adults, 52 with CD and 55 with UC. A baseline TIGER score ≥ 100 had a sensitivity and specificity of 0.964 and 0.941, respectively, at predicting a patient having an FC level ≥ 800 μg/g and/or a CRP level ≥ 1.0 mg/dL at 12-month follow-up. A baseline TIGER score ≥ 100 was associated with increased likelihood of having FC > 100 μg/g (P < .001), and ≥ 800 μg/g (P < .001) after 12 months, despite receiving advanced therapy, after the 12-month follow-up period. Similar trends were observed with the SES-CD and MES.ConclusionsThe TIGER score is a simple endoscopic score that can be used for both CD and UC that has been shown to have both construct validity with inflammatory marker and noninferiority to the current best-referenced endoscopic scores over a 12-month follow-up period. Future studies should begin incorporating TIGER as a measure of clinical response to interventions and therapeutics.

Introduction

Objective clinical measurements such as endoscopy play a pivotal role in the appropriate diagnosis and treatment of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) via the assessment of mucosal disease activity.1,2 Mucosal healing has been shown to be a significant predictor of improved patient outcomes3 and has been suggested as a therapeutic goal in clinical practice.4,5 Endoscopic indices in IBD aim to portray intestinal mucosal disease activity in a quantified evaluation of endoscopic severity. These endoscopic indices are necessary for standardized reporting of intestinal mucosal appearance in patients with IBD in both clinical trials and clinical practice.6–9

Several indices have been established in order to describe and portray the level of disease severity based on mucosal involvement.9–16 Despite the importance of utilizing accurate indices to assess disease severity, the widely used best-referenced indices have several limitations; such as lacking the ability to identify the number of intestinal segments with moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement, having low inter-observer agreement, and some have not undergone complete validation.1,2,9,17 Most notably, these indices have been established to be administered to either patients with CD or UC but not both diseases. Current assessments of mucosal appearance lack specificity, are not sufficiently flexible to allow for accurate determination of severity cutoffs, do not account for the full range of endoscopic severity, and lack the ability to describe the number of segments with moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement. This makes it nearly impossible to distinguish between patients differing significantly in disease extent and disease burden, which is highly important in assessing the full breadth of activity.18

Within the scope of CD,12 the Simple Endoscopic Score for Crohn’s disease (SES-CD)12,13 is the most commonly used indices to assess disease severity and response to therapy in both clinical practice and clinical studies. Within the scope of UC, the Mayo Endoscopic Score (MES) is currently the most widely used indices in adult UC patients.16,19 Although MES can evaluate all colonic segments, but unable to adequately identify the precise number of segments with moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement. Moreover, for the MES, only the most severely affected segment is tabulated in the final score, eliminating the possibility of completely measuring the pan-colonic disease burden. Previous studies demonstrated that the MES lacks the ability to accurately reflect mucosal improvement in the early stages of therapeutic interventions, primarily due to deficiencies in differentiating deep from shallow ulcers.20 Based on the current models, it is exceedingly challenging to accurately describe mucosal involvement and disease burden in a way that is reproducible and generalizable.21

These critical limitations led us to develop the Toronto IBD Global Endoscopic Reporting (TIGER) score18, to adequately convey the severity of disease burden in patients with both CD and UC. The index is easy to use in both clinical practice and clinical trial settings, allows for endoscopic assessment of both CD and UC using a single score, provides information regarding disease burden (eg, number of segments with moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement), and can distinguish between inflammation and fibrosis-related stenosis when calculating overall score for disease severity.

In the introductory TIGER study, the index demonstrated excellent inter-observer reliability among 3 blinded IBD experts. There was also high agreement between TIGER and SES-CD and MES in patients with CD and UC, respectively.18 In our previous cross-sectional pilot study The baseline TIGER score was significantly associated with baseline inflammatory biomarkers, fecal calprotectin (FC), and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels.18 Additionally, TIGER scores were significantly associated with patient-reported IBD-related disabilities recorded by the IBD Disk.18 However, this study did not assess the validity of TIGER at predicting inflammatory biomarkers or its use in assessing response to therapy over time. Having a better understanding of disease severity at baseline and having a predictive model for disease outcomes can help in adjusting initial treatment strategies and managing expectations of disease management and progression. Therefore, the goal of this current study is to (1) assess the construct validity of TIGER to baseline FC and CRP levels; (2) assess the construct validity of baseline TIGER to FC and CRP levels prospectively after a 12-month follow-up period; and (3) to compare baseline TIGER’s construct validity outcomes after a 12-month follow-up period to SES-CD and MES in patients with CD and UC, respectively.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This study was a prospective cohort study conducted in 1 tertiary IBD center in Israel. A cohort of 107 adult IBD patients (18-75 years of age), 52 with CD and 55 with who met the inclusion criteria and attended the IBD Center at Ha’Emek Medical Center, Afula, Israel, were identified and invited to participate in this study at least 1 month before baseline ileocolonoscopy visit. Only patients with confirmed disease based on standard clinical, radiological, endoscopic, and histological criteria were recruited for inclusion. Patients were undergoing colonoscopies for various indications. We only included patients with colonic CD, terminal ileum CD, ileocolonic CD, and UC who could be evaluated by ileocolonoscopy and who were not taking steroids and/or advanced therapy at least 3 months before the baseline endoscopic visit. Examples of advanced therapy include medications such as anti-TNF (infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab), integrin inhibitors (vedolizumab), IL12/23 inhibitors (ustekinumab), and Jak inhibitors (tofatinib, upadacitinib). Criteria for exclusion from study analysis included CD patients with known upper GI tract and/or proximal small bowel CD, postoperative CD, inadequate colonic preparation, patients receiving concomitant nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and patients with cancer or acute or chronic enteric infection (eg, Clostridium difficile); 10 patients were excluded at baseline and were not eligible to this study. Included patients (n = 107) were evaluated prospectively with visits at 0, 3, 6, and 12 months. There were no patients lost to follow-up throughout the study.

For eligible patients, the baseline visit took place within 7 days of the initial endoscopic evaluation, which included undergoing assessments such as the Harvey-Bradshaw Index (HBI),22 Mayo 9-point clinical score,23 and IBD Disk questionnaire.24 Blood samples for CRP, and stool samples for FC were also collected at the initial visit before the ileocolonoscopy. In addition, medical records were reviewed with the patient. All subjects were evaluated by endoscopy and mucosal involvement was scored using the TIGER score for all patients, the SES-CD for patients with CD, and the MES for patients with UC. At each subsequent visit (3 months, 6 months, 12 months) patients underwent the same clinical assessments as well as collected and measured CRP and FC levels. All patients underwent colonoscopies and were blinded reviewed and were scored by experienced IBD endoscopists (SES-CD for CD, MES for UC, and TIGER for all patients). All clinical endpoints including HBI, Mayo 9-point clinical score, IBD Disk questionnaire, physician global assessment, blood samples for CRP, FC samples, and medications (dose escalations, class change, compliance) were obtained at each subsequent clinical visit during the remainder of the study (3 months, 6 months, 12 months).

Initiation of advanced therapy and/or steroids after endoscopy visit 1 was according to standard-of-care acceptable guidelines as per clinical and endoscopic feature. Patients who had moderate to severely active disease received advanced therapy (33% of them were failed and were exposed previously to biologic therapy and were off therapy on visit 1). The specific class of advanced therapeutics initiated was based on individual features and history of previous attempts or cycles of advanced therapeutics. In the CD moderate-to-severe endoscopy group who were started at visit 1 an advanced therapy, 77% began anti-TNF therapy (infliximab), 17% began an integrin receptor antagonist (vedolizumab), and 6% began anti-IL12/23 therapy (ustekinumab). In the UC moderate-to-severe endoscopy group who were started at visit 1 an advanced therapy, 83% began anti-TNF therapy (infliximab), 13% began an integrin receptor antagonist (vedolizumab), and 4% began anti-IL12/23 therapy (ustekinumab).

All past and current medication including doses, dose escalation, switch to other advanced therapeutics, compliance to medication, surgeries, hospitalization, side effect, complications, and other medical history were captured and validated by the patient and patient’s EMR.The study was approved by the institutional research ethics board of Ha’Emek Medical Center REB number 17-16-EMC. All patients provided written informed consent.

Measures

Endoscopic indices at baseline

Toronto IBD Global Endoscopic Reporting (TIGER) Score: as per the previous publication, the TIGER score can be applied to patients with either CD or UC.18 Each segment of the colon and terminal ileum is evaluated and given a score based on general mucosa appearance (0-3 points), ulcer/erosion size (0-3 points), percentage of ulcerated surface (0-3 points), percentage of segment involved (0-1 point awarded for 50% of surface affected), and presence of narrowing (0-3 points). Categorizing the per segment TIGER score into 2 levels: inactive-mild segmental mucosal involvement (≤4) and moderate-to-severe segmental mucosal involvement (≥5). Scoring is conducted by segments, whereby a bonus score of 100 is given to each segment that is classified as having moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement, ultimately yielding a 3-digit score, in which the first digit of the total TIGER score represents the number of segments with moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement.18 The TIGER score was recently described and validated in a preliminary study. In patients with CD and UC, there was a 91% and 84% overall agreement between TIGER and SES-CD and MES, per segment, respectively.18 In this current study, TIGER scores were further classified as having more than 1 segment with moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement (TIGER ≥ 100) and as having more than 2 segments with moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement (TIGER ≥ 200). An additional subsequent analysis was conducted whereby TIGER scores were examined based on all potential cutoff values of moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement (ie, 105, 210, 315, 420, 525). These values represent the minimum total TIGER score that corresponds with having the number of colonic segments with moderate-to-severe mucosal as indicated by the first digit in the total TIGER score (Supplementary Table A).

The SES-CD final score between 0 and 5 is indicative of inactive-to-mild mucosal involvement, while a total score ≥ 6 represents a patient with moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement (or isolated ileal > 4).11,13

The MES final score of 0 represents normal or inactive disease, a score ≤ 1 represents mild mucosal involvement (erythema, decreased vascular pattern, mild friability), and a score > 1 represents moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement (marked erythema, absent vascular pattern, friability, erosions, spontaneous bleeding, ulceration).16

Clinical indices

Clinical disease activity such as HBI for CD and Mayo 9-point score for UC were recorded at the time of baseline endoscopic assessment and in each clinical visit (3 months, 6 months, 12 months)

A total HBI score > 7 is indicative of moderate-to-severe clinical disease activity.22

A total Mayo score > 4 is indicative of moderate-to-severe clinical disease activity.23

Inflammatory biomarkers

Inflammatory biomarkers such as FC and CRP were collected at the time of baseline endoscopic assessment and in each clinical visit (3-month, 6-month, 12-month).

Fecal calprotectin (FC): FC levels correlate strongly with clinical and mucosal disease activity in colonic, ileal, ileocolonic CD, and UC.25–27 In the current study, fresh stool samples were used to measure FC using a high-range immunoassay.28,29 FC was further categorized by levels >100 μg/g, ≥200 μg/g, and ≥800 μg/g. FC ≤ 100 μg/g represents inactive disease.30 Achieving a level of inactive disease would be indicative of effective treatment. Stool samples for FC were collected at each visit.

C-reactive protein (CRP): A CRP level ≤ 0.5 mg/dL is defined as being within the normal range. In this current study, CRP levels were further categorized as levels > 0.5 mg/dL and ≥1 mg/dL. Blood samples for CRP were collected at each visit. Achieving a normal level would be indicative of effective treatment.

Patients who continued to have an FC level ≥ 800 μg/g and/or a CRP level ≥ 1.0 mg/dL after the 12-month follow-up despite undergoing advanced therapy and/or requiring steroids based on acceptable standard-of-care guidelines, were defined as refractory to medication. Patients with these exceedingly elevated levels of inflammatory biomarkers represent a subset of the total sample population who continue to not respond to therapeutics, continue to have significant disease burden, and experience a loss of response to standard-of-care therapeutics.

Statistical Analysis

Patient demographics were examined by disease (CD and UC) as well as categorized by TIGER scores (≥100 vs. <100 and ≥200 vs. <200) for the total population. Descriptive statistics included a mean and SD or a median and interquartile range for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables. T-tests were used for independent samples or Wilcoxon 2-sample tests to analyze differences in patient characteristics as well as patient outcomes based on TIGER score classification when the parameters were continuous. Chi-squared tests or Fisher’s exact tests when needed to analyze associations with categorical parameters. Logistic regression was performed to assess the association between baseline TIGER score (≥100 vs. <100 and ≥200 vs. <200) and FC (>100 μg/g, ≥200 μg/g, ≥800 μg/g) and CRP (>0.5 mg/dL and >1 mg/dL) after the 12-month follow-up period. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were performed to assess the sensitivity and specificity of baseline TIGER scores predicting the various FC and CRP cutoff values. These analyses were conducted in the CD group, the UC group, as well as in the entire population. In the CD subgroup analysis, SES-CD (≥6 vs. <6) was included as an additional predictor of all outcomes after the 12-month follow-up period. In the UC subgroup analysis, the MES (>1 vs. ≤1) was included as an additional predictor of all outcomes after the 12-month follow-up period. All predictive analyses were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), cigarette smoking status (current smoker), and disease duration. Subsequent analyses were conducted investigating the subgroup of patients who continued to have an FC level ≥ 800 μg/g and/or a CRP level ≥ 1.0 mg/dL after the 12-month follow-up. These additional analyses utilized all potential TIGER cutoff values (ie, 105, 210, 315, 420, 525) as a predictor of outcomes. The statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc.). P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Patient Demographics

Patient demographics are presented in Table 1, categorized by IBD disease classification. The population (N = 107) was 39.2% female with a mean age of 33.5 ± 11.3 years in CD patients and 39.0 ± 14.5 years in UC patients. The mean disease duration was 6.4 ± 6.8 years in CD patients and 8.4 ± 9.7 years in UC patients. Among patients with CD, 21.2% and 30.8% of patients were current smokers with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, respectively. Among patients with UC, 16.4% and 36.4% of patients were current smokers with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, respectively. In terms of baseline inflammatory biomarkers, 62.8% and 65.4% of patients with CD and 58.2% and 47.3% of patients with UC had an FC > 100 μg/g and a CRP > 0.5 mg/dL, respectively. In terms of baseline clinical scores, 36.5% of patients with CD had moderate-to-severe, HBI > 7, and 47.3% of patients with UC had moderate-to-severe Mayo 9-point scores > 4. In terms of baseline endoscopic scores, 46.2% of patients with CD had moderate-to-severe disease based on SES-CD scores (≥6) and 52.7% of patients with UC had moderate-to-severe disease based on MES (>1). Median baseline TIGER scores were 105 [1.5-116.5] in patients with CD and 106 [0-326] in patients with UC. Among patients with CD, 53.8% and 23.1% had baseline TIGER scores ≥100 and ≥200, respectively; and among patients with UC, 50.9% and 45.5% had baseline TIGER scores ≥100 and ≥200, respectively.

Baseline TIGER Classification and Therapeutic Outcomes After 12 Months

Among the patients with CD, there was a significantly higher proportion of patients with a baseline TIGER score ≥ 100 who required steroid use compared to those having a baseline TIGER score < 100 (15 [53.6%] vs. 1 [4.2%], P < .001), advanced therapy (25 [89.3%] vs. 2 [8.3%], P < .001), or who became refractory to medication/steroid-dependent (18 [64.3%] vs. 0 [0%], P < .001).

Among the patients with CD, compared to having a baseline SES-CD score < 6, there were significantly more patients with a baseline SES-CD score ≥ 6 who required steroid use (15 [62.5%] vs. 1 [3.6%], P < .001), advanced therapy (21 [87.5%] vs. 6 [21.4%], P < .001), or who became refractory/steroid-dependent (15 [62.5%] vs. 3 [10.7%], P < .001) after the 12-month follow-up period.

Among the patients with UC, there was a significantly higher proportion of patients with a baseline TIGER score ≥ 100 who required steroid use compared to those having a baseline TIGER score < 100 (23 [82.1%] vs. 2 [7.4%], P < .001), advanced therapy (23 [82.1%] vs. 2 [7.4%], P < .001), or who became refractory to medication/steroid-dependent (22 [78.6%] vs. 2 [7.4%], P < .001).

Among the patients with UC, compared to having a baseline MES ≤ 1, there were significantly more patients with a baseline MES > 1 who required steroid use (23 [79.3%] vs. 2 [7.7%], P < .001), advanced therapy (23 [79.3%] vs. 2 [7.7%], P < .001), or who became refractory/steroid-dependent (22 [75.9%] vs. 2 [7.7%], P < .001) after the 12-month follow-up period.

Baseline TIGER Classification and Baseline Inflammatory Biomarkers and Clinical Indices

Results describing baseline TIGER score classification and inflammatory biomarkers are described in Table 2. In the total sample population, patients with a baseline TIGER score ≥ 100 had a significantly higher median baseline FC (1055.0 [534.0-2270.0] μg/g vs. 21.5 [9.0-81.0] μg/g, P < .001) and median baseline CRP (1.9 [1.0-6.4] mg/dL vs. 0.3 [0.1-0.5] mg/dL, P < .001) compared to those having a baseline TIGER score < 100. Patients with a baseline TIGER score ≥ 200 had a significantly higher median baseline FC (1420.0 [755.0-4220.0] μg/g vs. 65.0 [12.0-329.0] μg/g, P < .001) and median baseline CRP (2.6 [0.8-7.0] mg/dL vs. 0.5 [0.2-1.0] mg/dL, P < .001) compared to those having a baseline TIGER score < 200. Furthermore, there were significantly more patients with a baseline TIGER score ≥ 100 who had a baseline FC level ≥ 800 μg/g (P < .001) compared to those having a baseline TIGER score < 100. More specifically, out of the 34 patients who had a baseline FC level ≥ 800 μg/g, 33 of them had a baseline TIGER score ≥ 100.

Baseline Best-Referenced Endoscopic Indices and Inflammatory Biomarker Outcomes After 12 Months

The results describing the patient classification (OR disposition) as well as the association between baseline current best-referenced endoscopic indices with inflammatory biomarker outcomes after the 12-month follow-up period are described in Table 3 and Supplementary Table 1, respectively. Figure 1C demonstrates the clinical outcomes based on baseline endoscopic scores among the entire patient population. Among the patients with CD, there were significantly more patients with a baseline SES-CD ≥ 6 who had an FC level > 100 μg/g (58.3% vs. 25%, P = .015), >200 μg/g (37.5% vs. 14.3%, P = .053), and ≥ 800 μg/g (20.8% vs. 3.6%, P = .08) after the 12-month follow-up period compared to those having a baseline SES-CD < 6. Among the patients with UC, there were significantly more patients with a baseline MES > 1 who had an FC level > 100 μg/g (51.7% vs. 23.1%, P = .03), >200 μg/g (44.8% vs. 3.9%, P < .001), and ≥800 μg/g (24.1% vs. 0%, P = .11) after the 12-month follow-up period compared to having a baseline MES ≤ 1 (Table 3).

Within the entire sample population of 107 patients among the patients with both CD and UC, there were significantly more patients with a baseline SES-CD ≥ 6 or MES > 1 who had an FC level > 100 μg/g (54.7% vs. 24.1%, P < .001), >200 μg/g (41.5% vs. 9.3%, P < .001), and ≥800 μg/g (22.6% vs. 1.9%, P < .001) after the 12-month follow-up period compared to those having a baseline endoscopic score of SES-CD < 6 or MES ≤ 1. Moreover, there were significantly more patients with a baseline SES-CD ≥ 6 or MES > 1 who had an FC level ≥ 800 μg/g and/or a CRP level ≥ 1.0 mg/dL (32.1% vs. 9.3%, P < .004), after the 12-month follow-up period (Table 3).

Multivariable Analysis for the Association Between Baseline TIGER or the Best-Reference Endoscopic Score and Negative Outcomes After 12-Month Follow-Up

Within the entire population of patients with both CD and UC, after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, and disease duration; compared to having a baseline TIGER score < 100, those with a baseline TIGER score > 100 had a significantly increased likelihood to have an FC level > 100 μg/g (P < .001) (OR [95% CI] = 3.2 [1.2-8.6]), ≥200 μg/g (OR [95% CI] = 6.9 [1.9-25.5]), and ≥800 μg/g (P < .001) after the 12-month follow-up period. Moreover, compared to having a baseline TIGER score < 200, those with a baseline TIGER score ≥ 200 had a significantly increased likelihood that they would have an FC level ≥ 800 μg/g (OR [95% CI] = 4.4 [1.1-16.8]), and for those with a baseline TIGER ≥ 100 there was a significantly increased likelihood to have a combined FC level ≥ 800 μg/g and/or a CRP level ≥ 1 mg/dL (refractory to therapy) (OR [95% CI] = 3.3 [1.1-11.4]), represent a negative outcome despite advanced therapy after the 12-month follow-up period (Supplementary Table 2).

The same multivariable model using the current best-reference endoscopic score SES-CD and MES was also performed. Within the entire population of patients with both CD and UC, after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, and disease duration, compared to having a baseline SES-CD score < 6 or a baseline MES ≤ 1, those with a baseline SES-CD score ≥ 6 or a baseline MES > 1 had a significantly increased likelihood of having an FC level > 100 μg/g (OR [95% CI] = 3.3 [1.3-8.5]), an FC level ≥ 200 μg/g (OR [95% CI] = 5.7 [1.7-18.5]), an FC level ≥ 800 μg/g (OR [95% CI] = 14.8 [1.6-133.2]), and a combined FC level ≥ 800 μg/g and/or a CRP level ≥ 1 mg/dL (refractory to therapy) (OR [95% CI] = 4.1[1.2-13.4]), after the 12-month follow-up period (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

The goal of this prospective study was to assess the TIGER score’s construct validity by examining the correlation with measured FC and CRP levels in patients with CD and UC. The predictive validity of the TIGER score was also assessed by assessing the inflammatory biomarkers FC and CRP over a 12-month follow-up period and the results obtained with the TIGER score were compared to current best-reference endoscopic scores; the SES-CD for CD and the MES for UC. The results of this study reinforce the results obtained in our previous study, whereby higher TIGER scores were associated with elevated FC and CRP levels.18 The clinical importance of FC and CRP in tracking IBD disease progression is well known,31 so it is encouraging that this endoscopic scoring index continues to correlate well with these biomarkers in a larger sample size over an extended period of time. Moreover, it is intriguing to understand how baseline TIGER scores may play a role in predicting the progression of disease as measured by inflammatory biomarkers. The results clearly demonstrate that having more advanced baseline mucosal involvement and higher disease burden is associated with higher levels of inflammatory biomarkers and being refractory to medication after 12 months, despite enduring advanced therapy. This means that patients with more severe baseline mucosal involvement based on the TIGER score are not only more likely to fail at reaching clinical remission after 12 months of treatment, but also remain with a significant inflammatory burden of disease. This ultimately indicates that the TIGER score can play an expanded role in clinicians’ early decision-making in treatment implementation protocol. Having a tool that can aid in predicting the progression of disease over the course of a year and can generate an endoscopic phenotype that has been shown to be associated with loss of response to standard-of-care treatment protocols, can be tremendously impactful in making decisions about initiating more aggressive therapeutics earlier on in the disease course.

Despite their continued use in practice and clinical trials, the best-referenced most prevalent endoscopy scoring indices (MES, UCEIS, CDEIS, SES-CD) have limited validation and limited prognostic value in therapeutic outcomes.21,32

The results of this study demonstrate that the TIGER score has clinical value as a predictive tool for disease progression and therapeutic outcomes. Our study did not necessarily identify that the TIGER score was more accurate, nor a more precise predictor of these clinical outcomes compared to the SES-CD or MES but suggested that its ability to predict these outcomes was similar to those indices. Many of the significant relationships that were discovered highlighting the relationship between TIGER and biomarker outcomes were significant for baseline SES-CD and MES as well. However, in our opinion, the TIGER score’s added benefit lies in its ability to be able to be used for both patients with CD and UC, generating a single simple and reliable score describing the presence of moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement in multiple intestinal segments, allowing for the appropriate differentiation of patients with mild mucosal involvement from patients with moderate-to-severe mucosal involvement. Ultimately, all these aspects may make it a more viable and practical option for both clinical practice and clinical trials going forward. The results of this study have the potential to help guide more aggressive treatment protocols for initial management of a patient’s disease for those who have elevated baseline TIGER scores.

In a study by Kahanna et al. comparing the responsiveness of the SES-CD to the CDEIS, they suggested that an endoscopic scoring index should be reliable, valid, responsive, and easily implementable.33 Based on the results from our previous usability and validation study, as well as the results obtained in this current study, we believe that the TIGER score has the potential to fulfill all of these criteria for both patients with CD and UC.

Since its conception, the TIGER score has begun gaining more attention and traction as an upgraded practical option to address some of the limitations encountered with the current best-referenced indices such as SES-CD and MES.1 More recently, a retrospective observation study by Liu et al. evaluated the clinical value of the TIGER score in assessing disease severity and therapeutic outcomes in a sample of 166 patients with UC who were admitted to the hospital.34 Results of this study concluded that the TIGER score was significantly correlated with both the MES and the UCEIS score. Furthermore, there were significant positive correlations between the TIGER score and several laboratory and clinical parameters such as CRP, ESR (Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate), white blood cell count, neutrophil count, and platelet count. Additionally, patients who required advanced treatment (systemic corticosteroids, biologics, or immunomodulators) during the hospital admission or within 1 month of discharge had significantly higher TIGER scores compared to those who required 5-aminosalycilic acid treatment alone. Although this study was only conducted in patients with UC and only assessed retrospective treatment data, the trends from this study align with the results obtained in this current study, which only reinforces the notion that elevated TIGER scores are an excellent indicator of more advanced disease burden and treatment outcomes in patients with IBD.33

This study has several limitations to consider. This current study would have been improved with a larger sample size and being multicenter by nature. Being conducted in a single site with a specific patient population might limit broader generalization of results. That being said, the initial validation study was conducted among patients with IBD at Mount Sinai Hospital in Toronto, Canada; which demonstrates encouraging reproducibility. Nonetheless, there is a need for continued multicenter and/or international validation in the future. The strength of this study would have also been improved with a longer follow-up period. Obtaining more data on the progression of patients’ IBD disease burden over a 5-year or even 10-year follow-up period would provide invaluable information on the comprehensive capabilities of the TIGER score to predict various aspects of disease burden, progression, and treatment initiatives. Continued data collection is being conducted by our team, but we would encourage more research facilities globally to begin implementing the TIGER score in their work going forward. Additionally, validating the TIGER score using artificial intelligence would be an exciting step to progress alongside the continued technological advances in the field of gastroenterology.

In conclusion, this study reinforces the validity of the TIGER score and demonstrates that elevated TIGER scores are associated with elevated FC and CRP levels at baseline and after 12 months of follow-up. The TIGER score has the capability in predicting therapeutic interventions as per our previous predictive validity study as well as highlighting patients with high disease burden who became refractory to advanced therapy after a 12-month follow-up period. Moreover, the TIGER score appears to be similar in its operating characteristics to the current best-referenced endoscopic scoring indices (SES-CD for patients with CD and MES for patients with UC), and thus can be used as a single reliable endoscopic score for both CD and UC to capture overall endoscopic disease burden. The TIGER score may also minimize inaccurate depictions of disease severity based on a numerical result alone. It may also improve the performance and interpretation of clinical studies, as the categorization of recruited subjects will be relatively simple and potentially more reflective of therapy response. For example, current a patient with pan-colonic Mayo 3 disease who has significant healing with a single residual ulcer remaining in the rectum would be classified as a non responder/non remitter. Having a segmentally responsive score will allow for better evaluation of UC response to treatment. Although continued validation with additional larger clinical studies is needed, future research should begin considering including the TIGER score as an additional outcome to assess treatment efficacy or to evaluate the progression and responsiveness of different therapeutics to IBD disease burden. Given the novelty of the score, implementation into clinical practice will require potential training and/or added guidelines, but with continued investigation and encouraging results, clinicians can begin considering the use of the TIGER score to evaluate endoscopies as an additional measure to provide more informed and targeted medical treatment to patients with both CD and UC.
