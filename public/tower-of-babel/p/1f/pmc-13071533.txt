Trends in Central Obesity Prevalence Among Adults With Hypertension in the United States, 2001–2023
Authors: Yifei Lin, Ai Chen, Zhihai Chen, Li Luo, Liangdi Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCentral obesity drives hypertension progression, but long‐term trend data in hypertensive populations are lacking. This study using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data (2001–2023), 28,666 adult hypertensive participants were included. Central obesity was defined as waist circumference ≥102 cm (men) or ≥88 cm (women). Central obesity was further categorized into three severity grades: Grade I (men 102–110 cm, women 88–95 cm), Grade II (men 110–120 cm, women 95–105 cm), and Grade III (men ≥120 cm, women ≥105 cm). Age‐standardized prevalence trends were analyzed with Joinpoint software. Overall central obesity prevalence increased significantly from 59.2% (2001–2004) to 69.7% (2021–2023, p < 0.001). Females (72.2%–80.5%) had higher rates than males (49.2%–61.3%). Severe (Grade III) obesity rose most prominently (20.1%–30.9%, p < 0.001). The 20–44 age group showed the largest absolute increase (50.5%–66.1%) and contributed 46.5% to the overall rise. In conclusion, central obesity, particularly severe forms, increased steadily among US hypertensive adults. Younger adults contributed most, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to reduce cardiovascular burden.Keywords: adults, central obesity, hypertension, prevalence, NHANES

Background

Hypertension stands as one of the leading contributors to the global burden of disease, resulting in approximately 10.8 million deaths in 2019 alone and accounting for over 230 million disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) lost [1, 2]. In the United States (U.S.), the prevalence of hypertension among adults has exceeded 45%, with concomitant conditions such as heart failure (HF), coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke, and chronic kidney disease (CKD) imposing a substantial burden on both the healthcare system and public insurance funds [3, 4].

Obesity, particularly central adiposity, is well‐established as a critical driver in the development, progression, and resistance to treatment of hypertension [5]. Epidemiological data revealed a dramatic surge in the prevalence of obesity among US adults, escalating from 15% in 1980 to 42.8% in 2020. Concurrently, approximately half of the adult population in the U.S. is affected by central obesity [6]. Compared to general obesity, central obesity is associated with a more pronounced and earlier activation of the sympathetic‐adrenal system, chronic stimulation of the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system (RAAS), and the induction of systemic low‐grade inflammation and insulin resistance. These pathophysiological alterations inflict more direct and severe damage on vascular endothelial function, myocardial structure, and renal microvasculature [5, 7]. Consequently, the intricate pathological interplay between central obesity and hypertension has emerged as a pivotal target for the precise prevention and control of cardiometabolic risk.

However, previous studies have predominantly focused on general populations [6, 8], either examining temporal trends in the prevalence of general obesity or exploring cross‐sectional associations between obesity and blood pressure [9, 10, 11]. There is a notable lack of longitudinal investigations specifically assessing the trends in central obesity among hypertensive populations. This knowledge gap directly impedes the early identification of high‐risk subgroups and the accurate determination of optimal intervention timing in clinical practice.

In this study, we aimed to examine the trends in the prevalence of central obesity among US adults with hypertension, in order to find new information to reorganizing the management and intervention strategies for this high‐risk population.

Methods

Study Population

This study used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a large‐scale research initiative aimed at assessing the health and nutritional conditions of the U.S. population. NHANES employs a sophisticated multistage sampling design, which ensures that weighted results can accurately reflect the overall characteristics of the U.S. population.

Data collection in NHANES involves a combination of interview‐based surveys and physical examinations, making it a valuable resource widely utilized in epidemiological studies and public health surveillance efforts. Importantly, the NHANES program has obtained ethical clearance from the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, ensuring compliance with ethical standards in research.

This study enrolled adult participants aged 20 years and above who participated in NHANES between 2001 and 2023, with complete data available–‐including information on hypertension status, blood pressure measurements, and waist circumference. Following the application of these eligibility criteria, the final study cohort comprised 28,666 hypertensive adults. A detailed breakdown of the participant selection process is provided in Figure S1.

Definition of Hypertension

In the NHANES survey, blood pressure assessments were conducted by professionally trained clinicians following a unified standard protocol. Specifically, systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of participants were measured using a mercury sphygmomanometer. Before the measurement, participants were asked to sit quietly for 5 min. Subsequently, clinicians took three consecutive blood pressure readings at 30‐second intervals, and the average of these three measurements was calculated to determine the individual's average SBP and DBP levels.

The definition of hypertension in this survey was based on a combination of multiple criteria: self‐reported history of hypertension diagnosis, measured blood pressure results (with SBP ≥ 130 mmHg or/and DBP ≥ 80 mmHg), and current use of antihypertensive medications. If an individual met any one of these criteria, they would be classified as hypertensive [12, 13]

Definition of Obesity

Central obesity was defined as a waist circumference ≥102 cm in men or ≥88 cm in women. Central obesity was further categorized into three severity grades: Grade I (men 102–110 cm, women 88–95 cm), Grade II (men 110–120 cm, women 95–105 cm), and Grade III (men ≥120 cm, women ≥105 cm) [14]

Confounding Variables

Participants reported their age, gender, race/ethnicity, educational attainment and poverty income ratio (PIR). The classification methods of smoking and the diagnostic methods of DM and CVD refer to previous studies [8]. The participants provided blood samples during the survey. During the survey, blood samples were collected from the participants. Specific protocols for blood collection and laboratory testing can be accessed on the official website of NHANES (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted utilizing R Studio version 4.3.1. For weighted analyses, the sampling weights recommended by NHANES were applied. To guarantee that the results are nationally representative, all analyses incorporated these sampling weights, which serve to address nonresponse bias as well as missing data from interviews and examinations. Statistical significance was defined as a p value less than 0.05.

To enhance the precision of the estimates and reduce the influence of small sample sizes, survey years were grouped into three‐year intervals: 2001–2004, 2005–2008, 2009–2012, 2013–2016, and 2017–2020. Due to the COVID‐19 pandemic, survey data from 2021 to 2023 were combined in accordance with guidelines provided on the NHANES website; thus, the two survey cycles spanning 2021–2023 were merged. The midpoint of each three ‐year (or two‐year) interval was treated as a continuous time variable, and Joinpoint statistical software was employed to detect trends in prevalence over time and annual percent change (APC).

To account for potential age‐related variations across different intervals, all prevalence rates were standardized by age groups: 20–44 years (27.9%), 45–64 years (43.4%), 65–74 years (16.8%), and 75 years and older (12.0%). Subgroup analyses were also carried out based on age, sex, and race.

To identify which age group made the greatest contribution to the overall change in obesity prevalence, a contribution decomposition analysis was performed using survey‐weighted population estimates. This analysis took into consideration both the absolute changes in age‐specific obesity prevalence and their corresponding weighted population proportions derived from NHANES data.

Results

Participant Characteristics

As shown in Table 1. A total of 28,666 adults with hypertension aged ≥20 years were included in this study. The mean age of participants was 54.3 years. With respect to sex distribution, 47.83% were female and 52.17% were male. In terms of race/ethnicity, the proportions were as follows: 6.12% were Mexican American, 12.96% were Non‐Hispanic Black, 68.72%were Non‐Hispanic White, and 12.20% belonged to other races. The mean poverty income ratio was 3.03. Regarding educational attainment, 16.91% had education below high school, 26.48% were high school graduates, and 56.60% had education above high school. For smoking status, 29.66% were never smokers, 51.07% were former smokers, and 19.27% were current smokers. The mean SBP was 131.99 mmHg and the mean DBP was 76.31 mmHg, mean waist circumference was 103.96 cm, and the mean BMI was 30.52 kg/m2. Biochemical markers showed a TC of 198.21 mg/dL, mean HDL‐C of 52.45 mg/dL, and a mean HbA1c of 5.81%. In addition, 87.55% of participants were using antihypertensive drugs, 20.98% had DM, and 14.19% had a history of CVD.

Trends in the Prevalence of Central Obesity Among Hypertensive Adults

From 2001 to 2023, the age‐standardized prevalence of central obesity among U.S. adults with hypertension showed a significant upward trend (p trend <0.001). It increased from 59.2% in 2001–2004 to 69.7% in 2021–2023 (Figure 1). Among non‐hypertensive patients, the prevalence of central obesity has not changed significantly (42.0%–45.9%, Figure S2)

Prevalence of central obesity in hypertensive populations from 2001 to 2023. Central obesity was defined as a waist circumference ≥102 cm in men or ≥88 cm in women. SE, standard error.

Stratified by sex, the increasing trend was consistent in both genders. Among males with hypertension, the age‐standardized prevalence of central obesity rose from 49.2% (2001–2004) to 61.3% (2021–2023; p trend <0.001). Among females with hypertension, the prevalence increased from 72.2% (2001–2004) to 80.5% (2021–2023; p trend = 0.006; Figure 1).

Trends in Central Obesity by Severity Grade

Figure 2 and Table 2 presents the age‐standardized prevalence of central obesity stratified by severity grade. In the overall population, Grade I obesity showed minor fluctuations (18.9% in 2001–2004 vs. 18.1% in 2021–2023), with no statistically significant change (p = 0.076). For Grade II obesity, prevalence remained relatively stable too, starting at 20.2% (2001–2004) and ending at 20.8% (2021–2023), also with p = 0.497. In contrast, Grade III obesity exhibited a clear upward trajectory, rising from 20.1% (2001–2004) to 30.9% (2021–2023), with p < 0.001.

Discussion

This study represents the first investigation specifically dedicated to analyzing trends in central obesity prevalence among U.S. adults with hypertension. Our analysis covering the period 2001–2023 reveals a persistent and significant increase in the prevalence of central obesity within this population. The overall age‐standardized prevalence of central obesity surged from 59.2% to 69.7% during the 2001–2004 to the 2021–2023 cycle (APC = 3.64; p for trend < 0.001). Across the entire study periods, females consistently exhibited a higher prevalence compared to males. The prevalence increased from 72.2% to 80.5% in females and from 49.2% to 61.3% in males. The increasing trends were statistically significant for both sexes, indicating a widespread and concerning public health challenge.

Obesity has long been recognized as a significant risk factor for the development and exacerbation of hypertension, with weight management being a cornerstone of blood pressure control in affected individuals [15, 16]. Zhang et al. previously investigated trends in general obesity (defined by BMI, BMI ≥30 kg/m2) among hypertensive patients in the U.S. between 1999 and 2014, reporting a steady increase in prevalence. However, these findings are now over a decade old [17]. More recently, Tu et al. extended this observation period through 2023, demonstrating that the prevalence of general obesity in the hypertensive population rose from 39.6% in 2001 to 55.4% in 202314. Notably, these studies focused exclusively on general obesity and did not account for the critical dimension of central adiposity, which may confer distinct and additional metabolic and cardiovascular risks beyond overall body weight [8].

Central obesity poses a substantially greater health risk than general obesity defined by BMI. The key pathophysiological distinction lies in the detrimental role of visceral adipose tissue accumulation [18]. Visceral fat promotes metabolic dysfunction through the release of free fatty acids and pro‐inflammatory cytokines, which directly contribute to insulin resistance and endothelial dysfunction. These alterations subsequently lead to activation of the renin–angiotensin system, exacerbating sodium retention and endothelial damage. A prospective cohort study further demonstrated that among hypertensive patients, each standard deviation increase in waist circumference was associated with a 31% higher risk of uncontrolled hypertension (HR = 1.31), whereas a comparable increase in BMI was associated with only an 18% increased risk (HR = 1.18). Moreover, long‐term changes in waist circumference showed significantly stronger predictive value for blood pressure control than changes in body weight [19].

Our findings demonstrate an overall significant increasing trend in the prevalence of central obesity, which affected more than half of U.S. adults with hypertension. Notably, the prevalence among women reached a striking 80.5% in 2023. Further stratification by severity of central obesity revealed that although the prevalence of Grade I and Grade II central obesity among women remained relatively stable or even decreased slightly, a marked increase was observed in Grade III central obesity. This pattern suggests that the apparent decline in lower‐grade central obesity may reflect disease progression, with patients advancing into the Grade III category. A similar trend was observed in men, although the absolute prevalence of Grade III central obesity remained significantly lower than in women. Analysis by race and ethnicity indicated rising trends in central obesity across all subgroups, with the most pronounced increases observed for Grade III central obesity. The rising prevalence of severe central obesity underscores the critical need for early intervention strategies targeting abdominal adiposity in high‐risk populations.

Central obesity has traditionally been regarded as a high‐risk metabolic phenotype prevalent in middle‐aged and older adults. Older individuals exhibit a lower basal metabolic rate compared to younger adults, and an imbalance between energy intake and expenditure leads to excessive fat accumulation [20]. In the present study, however, the observed increase in the prevalence of central obesity was primarily attributable to adults under 45 years of age. Notably, by 2023, the prevalence of central obesity among adults under 45 (66.1%) had reached a level comparable to that observed in the 45–64 age group in 2001 (65.8%). This indicates that this high‐risk metabolic phenotype has now been appearing approximately two decades earlier in the life course among U.S. adults with hypertension. Such a shift will likely result in an earlier onset of obesity‐related complications, thereby shifting healthcare expenditure curves leftward and placing premature strain on the liquidity of the U.S. healthcare insurance system. Furthermore, previous studies have indicated that mortality risk decreases with later onset of obesity [21]. Since adults under 45 represent a core segment of the workforce, the rising prevalence of central obesity among young and middle‐aged hypertensive patients also poses a potential threat to the stability and productivity of the U.S. labor market.

Factors associated with central obesity include the consumption of ultra‐processed foods, physical inactivity, prolonged sedentary time, and poor sleep quality [22]. Studies indicate that over the past two decades, ultra‐processed food consumption in the United States has increased by four percentage points, daily sedentary time has risen by approximately one hour, and the prevalence of poor sleep quality has doubled [23, 24, 25]. Adults with hypertension exhibit an amplified response to these same environmental risk factors, likely due to baseline sympathetic overactivation, elevated systemic inflammation, and potential side effects of antihypertensive medications [12, 26]. Consequently, there is an urgent imperative to implement effective interventions aimed at curbing the rise of central obesity within the hypertensive population, in order to mitigate the synergistic clinical and economic burdens imposed by the confluence of these conditions.

This study possesses several notable strengths. First, to our knowledge, it represents the first investigation specifically dedicated to analyzing long‐term trends in central obesity among U.S. adults with hypertension, covering a 22‐year period (2001–2023) with the most recent NHANES data. Second, the severity‐based stratification (Grades I–III) provides novel granularity, revealing that the overall increase is primarily driven by a dramatic rise in severe (Grade III) central obesity. Third, the contribution decomposition analysis quantifies, for the first time, that young adults (20–44 years) account for nearly half of the total increase, with their prevalence now matching middle‐aged levels from two decades ago. Fourth, comprehensive subgroup analyses by sex, race/ethnicity, and age provide actionable data for designing targeted interventions. Collectively, these findings translate epidemiological trends into practical public health strategies, such as workplace interventions for sedentary behavior, digital health education for young adults, and community screening incorporating waist circumference measurement to curb the rising

Limitations

First, potential measurement bias in waist circumference. Although NHANES employs standardized protocols by trained personnel, waist circumference measurements may still be subject to inter‐examiner variability, particularly across the 22‐year study period with potential staff turnover. Changes in measurement techniques or equipment over time, though minimized by NHANES protocols, cannot be completely ruled out. Second, heterogeneity within racial/ethnic categories. As noted in response to a previous comment, the “Other Race” category in NHANES comprises multiple heterogeneous subgroups (including Asian, other Hispanic, Alaskan Native, and multiracial individuals). Due to sample size limitations, we could not perform more detailed stratification within this category, which may mask important between group differences in central obesity trends. Third, non‐response and selection bias. Although NHANES applies sampling weights to adjust for non‐response and oversampling, the possibility of residual bias remains. Individuals who chose not to participate or who were unable to complete the examination may differ systematically from those included, potentially affecting the generalizability of our findings. Fourth, inability to include institutionalized populations. NHANES is designed to be representative of the civilian, non‐institutionalized U.S. population. Therefore, hypertensive adults in long‐term care facilities, nursing homes, or other institutions were not included, which may lead to an underestimation of central obesity prevalence in the most vulnerable segments of the population. Fifth, descriptive nature of the study. As this study is based on repeated cross‐sectional surveys, it can describe temporal trends in prevalence but cannot explain the underlying causes of these trends or track individual‐level changes over time. Future studies are needed to investigate the behavioral, environmental, and policy factors driving the observed increase in central obesity, particularly among young adults.

Conclusions

From 2001 to 2023, the prevalence of central obesity, especially severe central obesity, among adults with hypertension in the U.S has been on the rise. Among them, the increase in central obesity among young people with hypertension is particularly significant.
