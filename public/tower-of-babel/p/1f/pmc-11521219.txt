Potential and Promise: Artificial Intelligence in Pediatric Surgery
Authors: Arvind Sinha, Somya Bhatt
Published: 2024

Arvind Sinha, Somya Bhatt

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a paradigm-shifting technology that gives robots and computer systems the capacity to mimic and carry out tasks normally associated with human intelligence. Rooted in computer science and engineering, AI aims to enable machines to learn from data, adapt to new inputs, and perform tasks autonomously, often surpassing human capabilities in speed, accuracy, and scalability. AI in pediatric surgery holds immense promise for advancing surgical precision, improving patient safety, and enhancing health-care delivery for pediatric patients. The use of AI is increasingly being reported in pediatric surgical scientific literature, and increasingly, pediatric surgery conferences are being organized with AI as the central theme. It behooves us to keep abreast with the latest in the pediatric surgical community in particular and medicine in general.

WHAT IS ARTIFICIAL Intelligence?

AI refers to the simulation of human intelligence in machines that are programmed to think, learn, and problem-solve like humans. It encompasses a broad range of techniques, including machine learning (ML), natural language processing (NLP), computer vision, and robotics, among others.[12] The goal of AI is to create systems that can perform tasks that would typically require human intelligence, such as understanding natural language, recognizing patterns in data, making decisions, and adapting to new situations.

WHAT ARE THE ADVANTAGES OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE OVER HUMANS?

The main advantages of AI could be classified into two domains: cognitive and psychomotor or skill-based domains.

In the cognitive domain, AI has the advantage of processing immense volumes of data in a much shorter time, recognizing subtle patterns in the data, and learning from the data to create algorithms that can predict outcomes or even guide treatments. This has the potential to truly personalize treatment tailored to each unique patient.

In the psychomotor domain, AI has the advantage of guiding machines and surgeons, allowing for skillful maneuvering in the operating room, allowing surgeons to plan surgery better, with greater precision, and to execute procedures with increasing accuracy. AI can also perform real-time monitoring during surgical procedures and improve patient safety by reducing human error or by personalizing treatment through augmented reality. The benefits of indocyanine green to augment the operative field and the precision of surgical robotic systems in pediatric surgery are already evident to everyone.

EVOLUTION OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE OVER DECADES?

The evolution of AI started with the seminal question by Alan Turing in the 1950’s-”Can machines think?” The machines in those era were simple AI machines based on simple “If-Then” rules.[23] Classical and expert systems were introduced in the 1980s with more advanced rule-based systems with symbolic reasoning.[45] The decades of the late 80’s, 90’s, and early 2000’s are often considered the “lost decades” as far as AI is concerned. This was followed by the development of intelligent chatbots such as Alexa, Siri, and ChatGPT from 2010 onward as the development of computer systems progressed with a surfeit of processing power.[678] Subsequent development of convoluted neural networks and advancements in computing prowess led to the development of the field of deep learning (DL).

Increasingly, scientific literature is mushrooming documenting the role of AI in pediatric surgery. We further discuss the three main types of AI (namely, NLP, computer vision, and machine/DL) and the examples of its application in the field of pediatric surgery.

Natural language processing

NLP is probably the most widely used AI in the world today and it includes common chatbots such as Siri and Alexa along with ChatGPT. NLP provides the systems to understand the nuances of the human language to process the questions or statements and provide cogent responses to the queries. The role of NLP in pediatric surgery, in particular, and medical science, in general, can be vast. Using automated computer algorithms, NLP can read text narratives, extract concepts, and convert them into organized data. With the widespread implementation of electronic medical records, there has been a massive generation of patient data sources.[910] Traditional methods of data capture, such as billing codes and/or manual review of free-text narratives in electronic health records, are highly labour intensive, subjective, and are a major source of bias. Medical records, case files, and research data are often very unstructured. It is often very difficult to parse through vast numbers of electronic files to extract relevant data. NLP can easily extract relevant data from unstructured databases. It can also improve clinical documentation and preoperatively define clinical surgical indications and/or identify complications. NLP may also be useful in codifying data according to the International Classification of Disease (ICD) codes. Besides classifying patients in ICD codes, use of NLP may be used in automating surgery reporting according to laid down criteria and generating detailed reports from the data. It may also glean data from literature and give us “wise counsel” regarding the current state of science for the particular condition. Thus, NLP is often used to parse data and files to feed structured datasets for ML models.

A study in 2024 identified 4729 children over a 20-year period utilizing NLP and identified the prevalence of predictors of concomitant meniscal surgery during cruciate ligament reconstruction.[11] In a study from a pediatric surgical center in Colorado, the authors used the Google’s NLP software (BERT) to parse through the texts of radiology reports and skeletal surveys to identify children with physical abuse. Utilizing the NLP, the model could identify abused children with an accuracy of 93.4% with high sensitivity and specificity.[12] ChatGPT has also been utilized to transform a large article into a concise summary that could be easily read and understood.[13] This can dramatically alter the way we read articles and absorb new scientific literature, and this has the potential to be a more efficient way we keep abreast of the latest scientific literature.

Computer vision systems

Just like NLP could process data and text, computer vision systems (CVS) are special systems that can process images. These systems of AI interpret, understand, and process visual information; recognize patterns; and make diagnoses. These train computers to encompass various tasks related to visual perception and recognition, for example, train models to identify anatomical structures, technical skill assessment, coaching, and performance improvement. CVSs have the advantage of delivering consistent results, mitigating interobserver variability that often occurs in manual assessment, and facilitating comparisons across different medical institutions.

Duci et al. in 2023 tackled the challenge in accurately identifying ganglion cells and hypertrophied nerves.[14] The authors used CVS along with DL and trained the AI system over the slides for Hirschsprung’s disease in 108 samples and 1945 slides using an AI-based U-net technique. Their aim was to clearly identify ganglion cells and hypertrophic nerves. The AI system demonstrated an accuracy of more than 90% in accurate identification and has the potential to standardize and streamline the diagnosis of Hirschsprung’s disease, which would benefit both the patients and in training pathologists. In 2023, Abbas et al. used a 691-image dataset of children undergoing hypospadias repair and used an AI model to detect the glans, urethral plate, and mental landmarks in hypospadias.[15] The model achieved an accuracy of 99.5% in detecting the urethral plate landmarks in hypospadias patients.[15]

Machine learning

ML, along with DL, is probably the closest thing to the general public’s understanding and perception of AI. Clinical prediction tools using ML are being increasingly utilized in pediatric surgery. It uses ML rather than human intervention, with statistical or regression models to create a decision-making framework. It has models and algorithms that learn from datasets based on a few defined inputs to guide future clinical decision-making.

Common ML algorithms commonly used are random forests, decision trees, and support vector machines. The results of any common ML algorithms need to be validated before a wider acceptability. These results are validated by three common methods, namely comparison to other ML models or representative statistical models, internal validation, or through external validation.

ML models have been increasingly and extensively used in pediatric surgery, for outcomes ranging from mortality prediction to decision support models. Jia et al. in 2023 used a radiomic-based ML model for predicting surgical risk in children with abdominal neuroblastoma.[16] They used radiological imaging from 74 children and extracted 1874 radiological features. Using support vector machines, the algorithms could identify surgical risks in these neuroblastoma patients with an accuracy of 89%. Mortality prediction has been done in pediatric surgery using ML models. Apart from identifying surgical risk, surgical outcome prediction has also been attempted by utilizing ML algorithms. Guo and Qian in a well-designed study of 2469 children with intussusception, identified factors such as air enema pressure, mass size, age, and duration of symptoms as a predictor for recurrence of intussusception.[17]

Adverse event prediction has also been a fertile ground for the use of AI and ML in pediatric surgery. Son utilized a ML model and applied it to a nationwide cohort of very low birth weight infants to predict the risk of intestinal perforation. They validated the study against both an internal and external dataset and the model had an excellent performance level in predicting intestinal perforation with an area under curve with Receiver operating characteristic of 1.[18] Similarly, a study from Florida used ML analysis to differentiate between necrotizing enterocolitis and spontaneous intestinal perforation.[19] A random forest plot model achieved a creditable 98% area under a curve in differentiating between necrotizing enterocolitis and spontaneous intestinal perforation.

Scott Wang et al. in a 2024 study attempted to use ML to predict the risk of dilating vesicouretral reflux (VUR) among infants with hydronephrosis.[20] The study demonstrated remarkable accuracy in predicting patients with dilating VUR among patients with hydronephrosis on early ultrasonography. Counterintuitively, the AI algorithms suggested that the patients with a lower grade of hydronephrosis would benefit for a voiding cystourethrography, rather than children with more severe hydronephrosis. Bartz-Kurycki et al. in 2018 used a ML algorithm for an enhanced neonatal surgical site infection prediction model utilizing statistically and clinically significant variables in a cohort of more than 16,000 patients and developed a hybrid model that was equally efficacious but simpler than the conventional clinical models.[21]

Similarly, increasing data are becoming available in the literature regarding the use of AI and ML in the diagnosis of acute appendicitis in children.[22]

Another large area where AI and ML are making a difference in pediatric surgery has been the development of decision-support models. Qiu et al. analyzed 526 children with intestinal obstruction and the deep neural networks could identify the need for surgery with an accuracy of 70.4% based on clinical and radiological factors.[23] Saez developed a ML-based predictor algorithm for shunt placement in preterm posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus based on a database of 43 preterm patients with 86% accuracy.[24]

Role of augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR), and use of surgical robotics AI technologies are now increasingly being integrated into surgical practices across different specialties with improved precision, efficiency, and better patient outcomes.

Patient-specific anatomy in a 3D format can be produced using direct volume rendering. However, this does not enable interactive modeling of individual organs and is not suitable for computer-assisted surgery. VR creates a 3D environment in which orientation and interaction with the 3D environment are possible. Employing motion tracking systems, one can interact with this AI-generated environment.[25] Combined with patient-specific VR modeling (VRM), this technology has opened up a new dimension in pre-surgical analysis and preparation. VRMs are especially useful in oncological cases where large tumors can dramatically alter human anatomy and anatomical relationships. Using VRM, a surgeon can rotate, section, separate, enlarge, and manipulate the model in innumerable ways to better delineate the anatomy, thus making preoperative planning easier and also increasing the confidence of a novice surgeon by producing familiarity with the surgical field. For instance, preoperative 3D modeling was employed at St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital in over 300 pediatric oncologic cases using complex light simulation (ray tracing), multiview autostereoscopic free-standing displays, and virtual/augmented reality headsets. Postoperatively, surgeons commented that they felt as if they had been there before.[25]

Augmented reality (AR)-assisted surgery is based on technology that superimposes a computer-generated enhanced image on the surgeon’s view of the operative field, thus providing a composite view. This computer-generated overlay can help in improving the operative experience with increased accuracy in tumor localization and resection as well as a significantly more precise understanding of vascular anatomy.[26]

Robotics can also employ ML and surgical navigation to enhance the skills of the operating surgeon and also improve ergonomics. Ergonomic video assessment software using AI may be used in skill labs to provide real-time feedback to budding surgical trainees and result in improved ergonomics and avoiding fatigue.

Unique to robotics is the stability and steadiness that these systems offer. This can be a boon for a pediatric surgeon with little elbow room in our tiny patients and minimize the damage to surrounding tissues. The surgeon’s armamentarium comprises tools like the Intuitive Surgical® SureForm stapler used with the da Vinci platform. It is an example of a smart tool that takes tissue density into consideration and adjusts such that the staple fires based on the needed thickness.[27] While this type of smart tool is already a reality, in future, robots will be capable of anticipating the needed instruments intraoperatively based on pattern recognition of tissue types and specific operations.

LIMITATIONS OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

In spite of the many advantages of AI and its application in pediatric surgery, it comes with its own limitations. Challenges related to the quality of data available and the ability to discern the difference between the quality of data and the outcomes for which the ML models are being recommended are of paramount importance. Similarly, missing data and data integrity are extremely important for AI models. The quantity of data available is also a problem, especially for medical specialties, especially for pediatric surgery. A large number of AI studies are available for acute appendicitis, and this reflects the caseload and larger datasets available for ML. Databases for less common conditions are often not of sufficient size to develop ML algorithms for. Reporting formats are often not standardized and varied datasets may hamper adequate ML. Clinical heterogeneity may limit the application of ML models to the general population.

Apart from the problems with the datasets, there is often a need for transparency in the ML models. “Black Box” models do not lend themselves well to an intuitive human understanding of the reasons and rationale for the algorithms and models. In the end, the data should be interpretable by the physician. Patient confidentiality and regulatory compliance also play a significant role in ML algorithms. Ethics and accountability for decisions taken based on AI are still being discussed and no clear answers are available.

In conclusion, AI systems such as ML, computer vision models, NLP, and surgical robotics with augmented reality are here to stay. These are increasingly being leveraged to augment the field of pediatric surgery. These should not be used as a replacement, but rather as an adjunct to the work we do as pediatric surgeons. It is our responsibility to stay abreast of these changes and incorporate them into our clinical practice, whereas still being aware of the pitfalls and being cognizant that in the final analysis, the responsibility lies with us humans.
