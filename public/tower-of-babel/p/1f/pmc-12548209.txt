Utilization of insecticide-treated nets for malaria prevention among children in Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Afisu Basiru, Uchenna B. Alozieuwa, Mercy I. Aboh, Umar Y. Usman, Ogochukwu N. Nwaefulu, Oluchukwu P. Okeke, Folahanmi T. Akinsolu, Olajide O. Sobande
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the proven effectiveness of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) in reducing malaria incidence and mortality, their usage among children in Africa remains inadequate. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to ascertain the prevalence of ITN usage for malaria control among children in Africa.MethodsThis review was registered with Prospero (CRD42023474978). A search was conducted in Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar for publications between 2013 and 2023. The study population was children in Africa, the intervention was ITN utilization, and the prevalence of ITN use was the outcome. The Jonna Briggs Institute (JBI) criteria were used to evaluate the quality of the included studies. A random effects model determined the overall pooled prevalence of ITN utilization. A sub-group analysis was carried out based on regions of Africa. Egger's Regression-based test was used to identify publication bias.ResultsThis review included 30 studies with 76,045 children, 50 households, and 1009 caregivers. Among the 30 included studies, 26 (86.7%) determined the ITN utilization in children, three (10%) evaluated the use of ITN by caregivers of children, and one (3.3%) article reported ITN utilization in households with children. In addition, 28 (93.3%) studies were cross-sectional. The pooled prevalence of ITN utilization was 69.50% with a 95% CI (64.5–74.5%). Statistically significant heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 100%, p = 0.001). ITN utilization was highest in Central Africa (85.44%, 95% CI 85.3–85.6), followed by Southern Africa (80.7%, 95%CI 80.6–80.9) and Eastern Africa (69.2%, 95%CI 63.8–74.6), and lowest in West Africa (67.0%, 95% CI 56.2–77.8).ConclusionThe prevalence of ITN utilization among children in Africa is low and varies by region. This utilization needs to be improved.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05599-w.

Background

Malaria remains a major global health challenge, with an estimated 299 million cases and 409,000 deaths recorded worldwide in 2019 [1, 2]. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) bears the heaviest burden, accounting for 85% of global cases. Nigeria alone is responsible for 27% of all cases and 23% of malaria-related deaths globally [1].

This disease disproportionately affects populations in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where over half of the global population remains at risk [2]. In LMICs, malaria contributes significantly to morbidity, mortality, poverty, and reduced productivity [3–5]. Children in endemic regions, particularly those without yet developed sufficient immunity, are especially vulnerable. In SSA, this vulnerability can result in severe outcomes such as cerebral malaria [6–9].

Various global and regional efforts have been implemented to reduce malaria-related morbidity and mortality [10–12]. For example, Nigeria’s National Malaria Strategic Plan (NMSP) aims to ensure that 80% of the population can access vector control interventions by 2025 [13]. Malawi and countries in Southern Africa have also developed strategic plans emphasizing burden stratification and vector control as key approaches [14, 15].

Malaria control typically requires a multifaceted strategy. Key interventions include the use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), indoor residual spraying, seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) for children aged 3–59 months, timely diagnostic testing followed by artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT), and focused treatment during pregnancy [16–24]. Among these, ITNs have emerged as the most cost-effective method for malaria prevention, averting more than 50% of malaria cases and reducing child mortality by 27% [25–27].

Many African countries, including Nigeria, Kenya, Ethiopia, and Rwanda, have undertaken mass ITN distribution campaigns to expand access and coverage [28–30]. Ownership is a major determinant of ITN use, and distribution during antenatal care and immunization visits has been instrumental in improving coverage [31–33].

ITNs function by creating a physical barrier that repels or kills mosquitoes, thereby reducing human-vector contact and malaria transmission [34–36]. In high-transmission areas, ITNs can reduce severe malaria by up to 45% and mortality in children under five by up to 55% [37].

Despite their proven effectiveness, ITN usage among children under five in Africa remains suboptimal, with reported usage at just 57.4% [38]. Barriers such as limited access, cultural beliefs, seasonal variations, and low awareness contribute to this gap [39–43]. In some regions like Kenya, awareness is high, yet usage remains low due to practical challenges [44]. Maternal education and occupation are also strongly associated with ITN ownership and use [45–49].

Notably, recent malaria control successes have led to a shift in disease burden from younger to older children. Historically, pregnant women and children under five were the primary focus of malaria interventions due to their high vulnerability [50, 51]. However, with reduced transmission in many areas, school-aged children (6–15 years) are now experiencing more clinical cases [52–54]. Up to 70% of school-aged children in high-transmission settings harbour malaria parasites, representing a significant reservoir for continued transmission [55, 56]. Despite this, few malaria control programmes specifically target this age group [57].

Recent data show that ITN use among school-aged children rose from 37.8% to 57.6%, yet they remain the least likely group in a household to use ITNs [58, 59]. Given this trend, there is an urgent need to evaluate ITN utilization among under-fives and school-aged children in Africa.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to provide a comprehensive assessment of ITN usage among children in Africa, focusing on children under five and those aged 6–15.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the prevalence of ITN usage among children in Africa. This investigation was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) standards [61]. The review was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42023474978).

Search strategies

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the utilization level of ITNs among children in Africa and identify the factors influencing their utilization. All published studies documenting the utilization rates of ITNs among children in Africa were obtained from databases including PubMed, SCOPUS, and Google Scholar. The investigation utilized key concepts, including insecticide-treated nets, malaria prevention, children, and utilization studies conducted in Africa. Keywords, Boolean operators (AND, OR), and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) phrases were applied to capture all key concepts in PubMed, and search terms were modified for Scopus. The detailed search terms are in Supplementary File 1.

Assessment of outcomes

The primary outcome of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to estimate the prevalence of ITN usage among children in Africa. ITN utilization was assessed by calculating the proportion of children who reportedly slept under an ITN the night before the survey. This was determined by dividing the number of children who slept under an ITN by the total number of children surveyed in the household and multiplying the result by 100 to obtain a percentage.

Study selection

All studies obtained from the initial search across all databases were imported into Rayyan, where duplicates were eliminated. Two independent reviewers (AB and MIA) conducted a preliminary assessment of papers based on their titles and abstracts to exclude those not pertinent to this review. If consensus could not be achieved on the selection of research, any disputes were handled by involving a third investigator (ONN). Two independent reviewers (UBA and UYU) meticulously evaluated the complete text of pertinent papers, utilizing the study protocol's inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies among the reviewers were addressed through dialogue or mediation by a third reviewer (ONN).

Quality assessments of the individual studies

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) technique, modified for cross-sectional and cohort studies, was used to evaluate the quality of the included studies [61]. The tool includes the following evaluation criteria: Was the sample frame suitable for addressing children in Africa? Were the ITN utilization rates among children accurately sampled? Was the sample size sufficient? Were the data regarding the children and their environments accurately documented? Was the data analysis conducted with sufficient coverage of the specified number of children utilizing ITNs; were valid methodologies employed for identifying ITN usage among children; was the ITN utilization among children assessed in a standardized and reliable manner for all participants; was appropriate statistical analysis performed; was the response rate among the children satisfactory, and if not, was the low response effectively addressed?. Two independent evaluators conducted a critical assessment of each study. Discrepancies among reviewers were resolved through dialogue. The final systematic review and meta-analysis encompassed studies that achieved a score of ≥ 50% on the quality rating checklist criteria.

Data extraction

Two researchers (AB and MIA) independently extracted each study's data. Excel spreadsheet software was used to extract the data from the included publications. The author, publication year, study design, country, settings, population, population characteristics, malaria prevalence, analysis, and factors that promote or hinder uptake and utilization were all included in the data extraction format. The precision of data extraction was validated by comparing the independently extracted data. Discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through a collaborative re-extraction of the data, then validated by an additional reviewer (UBA).

Statistical analysis and data management

The data retrieved from a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet was imported into OpenMeta® Analyst Software [62] for analysis. The attributes of the selected studies were delineated utilizing descriptive statistics. The I2 Score was utilized to evaluate the statistical heterogeneity present in the study considered. A Funnel plot was constructed to assess the presence of publication bias. The statistical significance of publication bias was investigated using Egger's statistical test.

Additionally, the Trim and Fill technique was used to determine the number of studies required to correct for publication bias [63]. The pooled prevalence and standard errors were produced using the information retrieved on the prevalence of ITN utilization in children and the total number of participants in each included study. Due to the significant heterogeneity of the data, a random effects model was employed to assess the pooled effect size with a 95% confidence interval. Subgroup analysis was performed according to the region of Africa in which the study took place. Forest plots were utilized to display the individual and aggregated effect sizes.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis revealed a pooled prevalence of 69.5% for ITN utilization among children in Africa. This prevalence aligns with a review conducted in Ethiopia, which reported LLIN usage at 56.3% (95% CI: 44.0–68.5%) [64]. The observed similarities may be attributed to common socio-economic conditions, shared health system challenges, and comparable cultural factors across many sub-Saharan African countries, significantly shaping malaria prevention behaviours and access to interventions.

Despite widespread promotion and distribution of ITNs, their utilization among children remains below the WHO-recommended target of 80% for universal coverage. Regional variation in ITN utilization was evident in the subgroup analysis [65]. While Central Africa had the highest reported ITN usage (85.4%), this finding should be interpreted cautiously as it was based on only one study from the included region. Nevertheless, higher ITN usage in some Central African countries may reflect effective national malaria control programmes, increased community-level awareness, and robust integration of ITN distribution into ANC and CWC. These mechanisms often ensure that ITNs reach the most vulnerable populations, such as pregnant women and young children [66].

Conversely, ITN utilization was lower in Eastern (69.2%) and Western Africa (67.0%). Several intersecting factors may influence the relatively low prevalence in these regions. These include low awareness of ITN benefits, caregiver misconceptions, poor sleeping arrangements, economic constraints, and climate-related barriers—such as discomfort due to heat, which discourages consistent net use. Furthermore, cultural norms and the prioritization of ITNs for certain household members (often adults or infants) may inadvertently reduce access for older children.

Interestingly, while most malaria control efforts traditionally focus on children under five and pregnant women, recent shifts in malaria transmission dynamics suggest increasing vulnerability among school-aged children (6–15 years). Studies have shown that this age group often exhibits high rates of parasitaemia yet is rarely targeted in ITN distribution campaigns [54–56]. These findings reinforce the need for malaria prevention strategies to explicitly include school-aged children, who may serve as reservoirs for continued transmission in high-burden areas.

Another important consideration is the influence of caregiver characteristics, especially maternal education, occupation, and knowledge—on ITN ownership and use. Several studies have highlighted that educated caregivers are more likely to understand malaria risks and the preventive value of ITNs, leading to higher usage among their children [45–49]. In this regard, behaviour change communication and education campaigns remain essential to sustain and scale up ITN coverage across all child age groups.

Although this study provides a valuable overview of ITN utilization patterns, it is important to note the presence of publication bias, which was detected using Egger’s regression test and an asymmetrical funnel plot. A trim-and-fill analysis was applied to adjust for this bias, slightly increasing the pooled prevalence estimate from 69.5% to 71.4%. This suggests that some underreporting or exclusion of studies may have occurred, and the actual usage may be marginally higher than initially estimated.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, although a comprehensive search strategy was employed across three major databases, the regional distribution of included studies was uneven. Only one study was included from Central and Southern Africa, and no studies from North Africa met the inclusion criteria. This limits the generalizability of the findings across all sub-regions of the continent. Additionally, most studies were cross-sectional, limiting the ability to infer causality between ITN usage and influencing factors. Lastly, variations in how ITN use was defined and measured across studies may have contributed to heterogeneity in the pooled estimates.

Conclusion

This review highlights that while progress has been made in improving ITN coverage among children in Africa, utilization remains below the recommended threshold and varies significantly by region. The findings underscore the need for targeted interventions that address contextual barriers to ITN usage, including educational campaigns, routine distribution at key health service points, and policy efforts to include school-aged children in malaria prevention programmes. Enhancing ITN access and usage, particularly among underserved populations, remains a critical strategy for achieving malaria control and elimination goals across the continent.
