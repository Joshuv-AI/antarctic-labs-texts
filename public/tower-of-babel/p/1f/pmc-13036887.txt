Single vs. double good-quality embryo transfer in fresh cleavage-stage cycles: a propensity score-matched analysis of efficacy and risks
Authors: Chen-yue Dong, Xue Wang, Meng-yu Zhu, Hong-wei Geng, Miao-miao Shi, Cui-lian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the clinical outcomes of elective single embryo transfer (eSET) and elective double embryo transfer (eDET) using good-quality cleavage-stage embryos in fresh IVF/ICSI cycles.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 5,725 fresh cleavage-stage embryo transfer cycles at Henan Provincial People’s Hospital (September 2016–March 2024). Patients were categorized into Group G (eSET, n = 2,688) and Group GG (eDET, n = 3,037). Propensity score matching (1:1) adjusted for confounders. Primary outcomes included live birth rate, clinical pregnancy rate, multiple pregnancy rate, and neonatal outcomes. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age and prior treatment cycles.ResultsAfter propensity score matching (2,394 cycles per group), eDET was associated with higher clinical pregnancy (69.76% vs. 52.21%, P < 0.001) and live birth rates (58.77% vs. 43.48%, P < 0.001), but also with a markedly increased risk of multiple pregnancy (35.63% vs. 0.8%, P < 0.001) and preterm birth (23.70% vs. 7.31%, P < 0.001). Among patients under 38 years of age, eDET was associated with higher live birth rates (62.58% vs. 45.23%, P < 0.001), accompanied by a substantial increase in multiple pregnancy risk (36.41% vs. 0.76%, P < 0.001). In patients aged 38–41 years, eDET did not result in a statistically significant improvement in live birth rates (38.20% vs. 26.97%, P = 0.110), while the multiple pregnancy rate remained significantly higher (20.83% vs. 0%, P = 0.008). In patients aged ≥ 41 years, no significant differences in live birth rates were observed between groups. Across different treatment cycles subgroups, eDET was consistently associated with a higher risk of multiple pregnancy.ConclusionIn this retrospective analysis of fresh cleavage-stage good-quality embryo transfers, eDET was associated with higher live birth rates but consistently incurred an increased risk of multiple pregnancy and related adverse outcomes. In the age subgroup of 38–41 years, eDET did not significantly improve live birth rates yet still significantly raised the multiple pregnancy risk. For women ≥ 41 years, no statistically significant benefit of eDET was observed. These findings are descriptive and should not be interpreted as prescriptive recommendations for eDET. In line with current ESHRE guidelines, eSET remains the preferred strategy to optimize safety and resource utilization, and to reduce the public health burden associated with multiple pregnancies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-026-02029-6.Keywords: Elective single embryo transfer (eSET), Good quality embryo, In vitro fertilization (IVF), Advanced female age, Propensity score matching

Introduction

With advancements in assisted reproductive technology (ART), optimizing pregnancy success while minimizing multiple pregnancy risk remains a central challenge in reproductive medicine. The European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) strongly recommends elective single embryo transfer (eSET) as the standard strategy for patients with a good prognosis, given its effectiveness in reducing multiple pregnancies and associated complications [1]. Nevertheless, elective double embryo transfer (eDET) continues to be widely used in clinical practice, particularly among women of advanced maternal age, those with repeated implantation failure, or patients perceived to have a poorer prognosis, in an effort to improve per-cycle pregnancy rates.

Despite this general consensus, important uncertainties remain regarding the optimal embryo transfer strategy in specific clinical settings. Most existing studies comparing eSET and eDET have focused on blastocyst-stage transfers or heterogeneous embryo quality, whereas evidence specifically addressing fresh cleavage-stage good-quality embryo transfer remains limited. Cleavage-stage transfer avoids embryo attrition during extended culture and potential cryopreservation-related damage, and is associated with a lower risk of monozygotic twinning compared with blastocyst transfer [2]. These characteristics confer particular clinical relevance for older women with limited embryo numbers and inherently higher pregnancy risks [3, 4]. However, whether transferring two good-quality cleavage-stage embryos provides a meaningful improvement in live birth rates over eSET—and whether any such benefit outweighs the increased risk of multiple pregnancy—has not been adequately clarified.

Moreover, patient heterogeneity may substantially modify the balance between benefit and risk. Younger women, who generally have lower rates of embryonic aneuploidy, may experience greater gains from eDET [5, 6], whereas in older women the potential benefit may be attenuated by declining embryo competence and endometrial receptivity [7, 8]. Similarly, the influence of repeated prior treatment cycles on the comparative effectiveness of eSET versus eDET has not been well characterized. These unresolved questions highlight the need for large-scale, real-world analyses with appropriate adjustment for baseline differences.

To address these gaps, we conducted a large retrospective cohort study of 5,725 fresh cleavage-stage good-quality embryo transfer cycles, which is, to our knowledge, one of the largest studies to directly compare eSET and eDET in this specific context [9, 10]. We evaluated differences in live birth and multiple pregnancy rates after PSM and performed stratified analyses according to patient age and prior treatment cycles. By focusing on an underexplored embryo stage and clinically relevant subgroups within a real-world setting, this study aims to complement existing evidence and inform more nuanced, individualized embryo transfer strategies aligned with the goal of achieving a “healthy, full-term, singleton live birth.” [11].

Materials and methods

Study population

This single-center retrospective cohort study analyzed patients who underwent fresh IVF/ICSI cycles at Henan Provincial People’s Hospital between September 2016 and March 2024. The inclusion criteria were: (1) fresh cycle transfers, (2) elective cleavage-stage embryo transfers, (3) transfer of only good-quality embryos (7–8 cells at the Day 3 stage with < 10% fragmentation) [12], and (4) use of either a GnRH-agonist or GnRH-antagonist stimulation protocol. All cycles that met the inclusion criteria were included, including repeated treatment cycles for the same patient. Exclusion criteria included: (1) donor egg or sperm cycles, (2) preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) cycles, (3) cycles with chromosomal abnormalities, (4) congenital uterine malformations (e.g., uterine agenesis, uterine didelphys), and (5) incomplete data.

A total of 5,725 cycles met the inclusion criteria and were categorized based on the number of embryos transferred: Group G (elective single good-quality embryo transfer, n = 2,688) and Group GG (elective double good-quality embryo transfer, n = 3,037). Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was approved by the Reproductive Ethics Committee of Henan Provincial People’s Hospital (SYSZLL-2019110401). This study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Ovarian stimulation protocol

All participants underwent controlled ovarian stimulation (COS). The choice between the GnRH agonist protocol and the GnRH antagonist protocol was individualized based on the patient’s age, ovarian reserve parameters (including AMH level and AFC), and previous response to stimulation, in accordance with our center’s standard operating procedures (SOP). This decision was made by the same clinical team to maintain consistency.

For the GnRH agonist protocol, pituitary downregulation was achieved with either a single 3.75 mg injection of a long-acting GnRH agonist on days 2–4 of the menstrual cycle (Diphereline, Ipsen, Tianjin) or daily subcutaneous injections of 0.1 mg of a short-acting GnRH agonist (Decapeptyl, Ferring, Germany) initiated in the mid-luteal phase of the previous cycle and continued for 14–18 days. Once pituitary suppression was confirmed (serum estradiol < 50 pg/mL and endometrial thickness < 5 mm), exogenous gonadotropins (Gn) were administered to stimulate follicular development.

For the GnRH antagonist protocol, stimulation was initiated on days 2–3 of the menstrual cycle. A GnRH antagonist (0.25 mg Cetrotide, Merck Serono, or ganirelix, Organon) was introduced when the dominant follicle reached ≥ 12 mm, serum estradiol exceeded 200 pg/mL, or when luteinizing hormone (LH) levels showed a significant upward trend. Antagonist administration continued daily until the day of triggering.

Final oocyte maturation was induced when at least two follicles reached ≥ 18 mm or three follicles reached ≥ 17 mm in diameter, using 4,000–10,000 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG, Lizon Pharma, China). Oocyte retrieval was performed 35–37 h post-trigger under ultrasound guidance.

Embryo transfer and luteal phase support

The selection of in vitro fertilization (IVF) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) was based on semen quality. IVF was performed when semen analysis results were within normal parameters, whereas ICSI was used in cases of abnormal semen parameters. On day 2 post-retrieval, the fertilization status of the embryos was observed. On day 3 post-retrieval, the embryos were scored based on parameters such as cell number, embryo fragmentation. Day 3 embryos were classified as good quality if they contained 7–8 cells with < 10% fragmentation. The decision to perform eSET or eDET is made in accordance with the unified and SOP of this center. Transplant conversations are handled by a fixed and experienced team of clinicians to ensure the stability and consistency of the decision-making process. The determination of embryo transfer strategies is mainly based on a comprehensive assessment of the following clinical parameters: patient age, number of available embryos, previous ART outcomes, and endometrial conditions, following the routine clinical policy of our center. Although the patient’s preference is regarded as an important component of the final decision, the professional judgment and risk assessment of the clinicians play a leading role in the allocation of the study cycle. Embryo transfer was conducted under ultrasound guidance using a flexible catheter. Both group performed luteal phase support (LPS) commenced on the day of oocyte retrieval with progesterone vaginal gel (Crinone, 90 mg/tablet, Merck Serono, Germany) 90 mg/day placed vaginally and dydrogesterone (Duphaston, 10 mg/tablet, Abbott, Netherlands) 10 mg orally bid. LPS continued until the 10th week of gestation, with dosage adjustments based on serial β-hCG measurements and ultrasound assessments.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measures of this study were live birth rate and multiple pregnancy rate, while secondary outcomes included clinical pregnancy rate and miscarriage rate. Baseline characteristics, ovulation parameters, and laboratory indicators were compared between the two groups. Additionally, pregnancy outcomes such as clinical pregnancy rate, miscarriage rate, live birth rate, multiple pregnancy rate, and neonatal outcomes were analyzed.

Clinical pregnancy was defined as the presence of at least one gestational sac detected via ultrasound 4–5 weeks post-transfer. Miscarriage was defined as pregnancy loss before 28 weeks of gestation, in accordance with the Chinese Expert Consensus [13].The miscarriage rate was calculated as the number of miscarriage cycles divided by the number of clinical pregnancy cycles. Multiple pregnancy rate was defined as the proportion of clinical pregnancy cycles resulting in multiple gestations. Neonates with a birth weight < 2500 g were classified as low birth weight, while those weighing ≥ 4000 g were classified as macrosomic.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (Q1–Q3), while categorical variables were expressed as N (%). Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate, and categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests.

To mitigate potential confounding bias in this non-randomized cohort study, PSM was performed using the “MatchIt” package in R (version 4.4.2). PSM was employed to balance the distribution of measured covariates between the treatment groups [14]. Propensity scores were estimated for each participant via logistic regression, with the exposure variable (elective single-embryo transfer vs. double-embryo transfer) as the dependent variable. Covariates included female age, body mass index (BMI), anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), antral follicle count (AFC), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), prior treatment cycles, infertility duration, fertilization method, infertility type, and controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) protocol. Nearest-neighbor 1:1 matching was applied with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit-transformed propensity score [14]. Covariate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMD), with a threshold of < 0.1 indicating adequate balance. To address potential residual imbalances in stimulation-related variables after PSM, we performed conditional logistic regression that included Gn starting dose, total Gn dosage, E2 level on trigger day, oocyte yield, and endometrial thickness as covariates.

To address the possible heterogeneity of treatment effects among different patient characteristic populations, we stratified patients using clinically recognized age thresholds (< 38, 38–41, ≥ 41 years) and by treatment cycles (first vs. ≥2 cycles). The selection of this layer is based on the key turning point in reproductive biology and the basis for clinical guidelines [11, 15]. The 38 years and 41 years marked the beginning of statistically significant accelerated changes and sharp declines in the aneuploidy rate and live birth rate of embryos [16, 17]. while prior treatment cycles is a key indicator for previous treatment response and potential adverse prognosis [18]. PSM is conducted independently within each pre-defined stratified subgroup, aiming to maximize the balance of measured covariates between groups.

All statistical tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Data analyses were conducted using the EmpowerStats program and R software (version 4.4.2).

Pregnancy outcomes in both groups after matching

There were significant differences in clinical pregnancy rate, live birth rate, multiple pregnancy rate, preterm birth rate, and mode of delivery between the two groups. The clinical pregnancy rate (69.76% vs. 52.21%, P < 0.001) and live birth rate (58.77% vs. 43.48%, P < 0.001) were significantly higher in Group GG. However, the multiple pregnancy rate (35.63% vs. 0.8%, P < 0.001), preterm birth rate (23.70% vs. 7.31%, P < 0.001), and cesarean section rate (77.69% vs. 71.35%, P < 0.001) were also significantly higher in Group GG. Among the 595 multiple pregnancies in group GG, 8 were monozygotic (1.34%). The proportion of full-term singleton live births was significantly higher in Group G than in Group GG (92.41% vs. 52.67%). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in the rates of ectopic pregnancy and miscarriage (see Table 3).

Subgroup analysis after stratified matching according to age and treatment cycles

Age was stratified into < 38 years, 38–41 years, and ≥ 41 years, while the oocyte retrieval times was stratified by first time and ≥ 2 times. PSM was then performed within each stratum. After matching, results indicated that in younger patients (< 38 years), the clinical pregnancy rate and live birth rate were significantly higher in Group GG than in Group G (P < 0.001). However, the multiple pregnancy rate was also significantly higher (36.41% vs. 0.76%). Among patients aged 38–41 years, there was no significant difference in live birth rate between the groups; However, the clinical pregnancy rate (53.93% vs. 32.58%) and multiple pregnancy rate (20.83% vs. 0.00%) were significantly higher in Group GG than in Group G (P < 0.05). In patients aged ≥ 41 years, clinical pregnancy and live birth rates did not differ significantly between the two groups, and no multiple pregnancies were observed in either group. Additionally, the miscarriage rate did not differ between the groups for patients at all ages (Table 5).

Discussion

In this large propensity score–matched cohort, we evaluated the comparative effectiveness and risks of elective single versus double transfer of fresh, good-quality cleavage-stage embryos across clinically relevant subgroups. Consistent with existing evidence, transferring two cleavage-stage embryos was associated with higher clinical pregnancy and live birth rates, but at the cost of a substantially increased risk of multiple pregnancy and related obstetric complications. By focusing on an underexplored embryo stage and applying age-stratified analyses in a real-world setting, this study extends prior literature by clarifying how the balance between benefit and risk varies with patient characteristics.

Among women younger than 38 years, eDET was associated with a higher live birth rate but also a markedly increased risk of multiple pregnancy. In women aged 38–41 years, no significant improvement in live birth rate was observed with eDET, while multiple pregnancy risk remained elevated, suggesting diminishing returns with advancing age. In women aged ≥ 41 years, no clear advantage of eDET over eSET was identified. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to limited post-PSM sample size. Similar patterns were observed across different prior treatment cycles, with higher pregnancy rates consistently offset by increased multiple pregnancy risk.

Advantages of fresh cleavage-stage embryo transfer

The present study focuses on fresh cleavage-stage embryo transfer, a strategy that remains clinically relevant yet relatively underexplored in contemporary literature. Compared with blastocyst transfer, cleavage-stage transfer mitigates the high elimination rate associated with blastocyst culture and the risks of cryopreservation damage. According to Cochrane data, the likelihood of having no embryos available for transfer because of culture failure is approximately 2.5 times higher when blastocyst transfer is planned than with cleavage-stage transfer [19]. This risk is particularly consequential for women of advanced maternal age or those with a limited number of embryos, for whom cycle cancellation may lead to treatment delays and considerable psychological burden.

In addition, concerns have been raised regarding the potential impact of extended culture and cryopreservation on embryo viability and placental development [20]. Several studies have suggested that blastocyst transfer may be associated with a higher risk of preterm birth and multiple pregnancy compared with cleavage-stage transfer [2, 19, 21]. Together, these considerations highlight the continued clinical value of fresh cleavage-stage embryo transfer, particularly in selected patients with poorer prognoses. By enabling earlier utilization of good-quality embryos, this strategy may shorten treatment duration, reduce costs.

Clinical benefits and risk trade-offs of double embryo transfer

In this propensity score–matched cohort, double transfer of good-quality cleavage-stage embryos was associated with higher clinical pregnancy and live birth rates than SET, but at the cost of a substantially increased risk of multiple pregnancy and related adverse obstetric outcomes. These findings are broadly consistent with previous randomized and observational studies demonstrating improved per-cycle pregnancy outcomes with DET alongside a marked rise in multiple gestations [10, 22–25].

However, some prior studies have reported no clear advantage of transferring two high-quality embryos over single embryo transfer, suggesting that embryo quality rather than quantity may be the dominant determinant of success [26, 27]. El-Danasouri et al. [28] further emphasized that the transfer of suboptimal embryos could compromise implantation potential. Our results contribute to this ongoing debate by focusing specifically on fresh cleavage-stage good-quality embryos within a real-world clinical setting and by incorporating age- and prior treatment cycle-based stratification.

Consistent with established evidence, the increased multiple pregnancy rate associated with DET was accompanied by a higher risk of preterm birth. This association likely reflects well-recognized pathophysiological mechanisms in multiple gestations, including twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS), uterine overdistension, placental insufficiency, and increased maternal metabolic demands [10]. Additionally, the study accounted for cycles that involved clinically identified multiple pregnancies but ultimately resulted in singleton births. The results indicated 8 cases in the GG group and 0 in the G group. This difference may also suggest that the uterine environment in twin pregnancies is less capable of supporting the continuous development of embryos. It is important to note that the twin preterm birth rate in the G group of this study was unusually high, possibly due to the small sample size of twin pregnancies in the G group (only 20 cases), which could be influenced by extreme values. Furthermore, monozygotic twins, which are mostly monochorionic, are more likely to experience complications such as TTTS and selective intrauterine growth restriction due to placental sharing [29]. Their preterm birth rate is typically higher than that of dizygotic twins (dichorionic), and this discrepancy may further exacerbate data fluctuations in the G group [30].

Although several stimulation-related variables remained statistically different after PSM, these differences likely reflect real-world clinical decision-making in embryo transfer strategies rather than residual confounding. Importantly, additional conditional logistic regression analyses yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of the main findings.

Stratified decision-making by age and treatment cycles

Age-stratified analyses provided important insights into how the balance between efficacy and risk differs across patient subgroups. Among women younger than 38 years, DET was associated with a higher live birth rate, likely reflecting better ovarian reserve and lower rates of embryonic aneuploidy in this population [31]. However, this apparent benefit was accompanied by a pronounced increase in multiple pregnancy and preterm birth risk, which may lead to a concurrent increase in maternal complications and neonatal morbidity [32]. Also, a health economic model indicated that SET, compared to DET, could save approximately €70,000 per live birth, primarily due to reduced costs associated with multiple pregnancies [33]. This result reinforces the need for caution when considering DET in younger patients despite higher per-cycle success rates.

In women aged 38–41 years, DET resulted in a higher clinical pregnancy rate but did not translate into a statistically significant improvement in live birth rate. This discrepancy may be attributable to increased pregnancy loss related to declining embryo competence with advancing age. Although a numerical difference in live birth rates was observed, the limited sample size in this subgroup resulted in low statistical power, increasing the risk of a Type II error. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Besides, the multiple pregnancy rate remained elevated with DET. These results suggest that the quality of embryos in this age group may be compromised due to the decline in ovarian reserve, and the quantity of embryos transferred may be insufficient to compensate for the quality deficit. Overall, even though we cannot completely rule out the clinical benefits of eDET for patients in this age group, this unproven increase in LBR is insufficient to justify the high risk of multiple pregnancies. eSET remains the preferred strategy when high-quality embryos are available for this age group.

For women aged ≥ 41 years, no statistically significant advantage of DET over single embryo transfer was observed.This observation is biologically plausible given the high prevalence of embryonic aneuploidy and age-related impairments in endometrial receptivity in this age group [7, 34–36]. However, this subgroup had the smallest post-matching sample size, the findings should be considered exploratory rather than definitive. Although multiple pregnancy was not observed in this group, the low event rate may reflect limited statistical power rather than true absence of risk. Given the already elevated baseline risk of pregnancy complications in older women [37], these results underscore the importance of cautious embryo transfer strategies in this population.

Stratification by treatment cycles revealed similar patterns. Patients undergoing their first oocyte retrieval cycle generally achieved higher pregnancy and live birth rates, consistent with better ovarian reserve and embryo quality in this group, but DET remained associated with a persistently higher risk of multiple pregnancy. In patients with multiple treatment cycles, overall success rates were lower than patients with their first treatment. Although DET was associated with higher live birth rates, the accompanying increase in multiple pregnancy risk remained evident. Together, these findings highlight the need for individualized decision-making that carefully balances potential efficacy gains against maternal and neonatal safety.

Clinical implications and individualized embryo transfer decision-making

Overall, our findings are consistent with the 2024 ESHRE guideline recommending elective single embryo transfer as the preferred strategy to minimize multiple pregnancy and its associated complications [1]. By analyzing a large single-center real-world cohort and applying propensity score matching, this study provides complementary evidence that eDET substantially increases the risks of multiple pregnancy and preterm birth, even when only good-quality cleavage-stage embryos are transferred.

Importantly, the age-stratified results indicate that in women aged 38–41 years, improvements in clinical pregnancy outcomes with eDET do not translate into clear gains in live birth, while the risk of multiple pregnancy remains substantial. These findings emphasize the importance of cautious, individualized counseling, particularly for older patients with poorer prognoses, in whom potential short-term benefits may be offset by downstream maternal and neonatal risks.

In real-world clinical practice, embryo transfer decisions should also consider patient preferences, risk tolerance, and the capacity of healthcare systems to manage complications related to multiple pregnancies. Individualized counseling is therefore essential to ensure that treatment strategies align with patients’ reproductive goals while prioritizing safety.

Our findings provide empirical evidence to inform individualized embryo transfer decisions. Despite the limited sample size in the older age subgroup, which is a common challenge in this field, our study represents one of the more substantial real - world datasets that specifically focus on fresh cleavage - stage transfers in this population. This offers valuable insights where data are scarce. However as a retrospective single-center study, our conclusions should be interpreted with caution and are not sufficient to serve as general clinical recommendations. Further large-scale prospective studies are needed to clarify whether specific patient populations might derive net benefit from eDET under carefully selected conditions.

Strengths and limitations

The study utilized a large sample size and performed stratification across multiple dimensions to enhance the reliability of the findings. Besides, PSM was used to balance observed baseline characteristics between groups, thereby reducing potential confounding. However, PSM cannot account for unmeasured or unknown confounding factors, which may still bias our effect estimates. Second, the quality of our matching is contingent on the choice of covariates, the correctness of the model specification, and the success of achieving balance between groups. Despite our efforts to include key clinical variables and achieve good balance, residual imbalance or poor overlap in the distributions of propensity scores could lead to biased estimates. Therefore, while PSM strengthens the causal interpretation of our results compared to an unadjusted analysis, our findings should not be interpreted as possessing the same level of causal validity as an RCT. Another limitation is the inclusion of multiple cycles per patient, which may introduce non-independence of observations related to prior treatment outcomes. Although adjustment and stratified analyses were performed, residual bias cannot be fully excluded. Additionally, ovarian stimulation–related parameters remained statistically different between groups after matching; however, further adjustment for these variables yielded consistent results, suggesting that the primary findings were robust. Nonetheless, potential residual confounding remains possible. Furthermore, the relatively small post-matching sample size in older patients (≥ 38 years), led to a lack of statistical power to definitively rule out a clinically significant difference in live birth rates (Type II error). Necessitating multi-center expansion for validation are also our limitations. Additionally, long-term neonatal health outcomes (e.g., neurodevelopment) and perinatal complications in mothers require further follow-up.

Conclusion

This real-world study demonstrates that while eDET of good-quality cleavage-stage embryos is associated with higher live birth rates compared to eSET, it consistently leads to a substantial increase in multiple pregnancy and preterm birth. In the key age subgroup of 38–41 years, eDET did not significantly improve live birth rates yet still significantly raised the multiple pregnancy risk. For women ≥ 41 years, no statistically significant benefit of eDET was observed, though this finding is limited by sample size. Across all analyses, the elevated maternal and neonatal risks associated with eDET outweigh its potential live birth advantage. These findings provide robust real-world evidence supporting the ESHRE 2024 guideline that eSET should be the preferred strategy to optimize perinatal safety, reduce multiple pregnancy-related complications, and allocate healthcare resources effectively. Our results underscore the importance of individualized counseling that prioritizes singleton birth. Future large-scale, prospective studies are needed to further delineate risk–benefit profiles in specific patient subgroups.
