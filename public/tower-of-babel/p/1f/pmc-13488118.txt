Peptidic Phosphonates as Irreversible Covalent Inhibitors of Plasmodium falciparum Serine Protease PfSUB1
Authors: Armands Kazia, Elina Lidumniece, Chrislaine Withers-Martinez, Liva Eglite, Owain Donnelly, David A. Fidock, Michael J. Blackman, Aigars Jirgensons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Malaria, caused by Plasmodium parasites, remains a major global health challenge, exacerbated by the widespread emergence of drug-resistant plasmodium strains. Subtilisin-like serine protease SUB1 triggers escape of the parasite from the red cell via a process called egress, rendering the enzyme a prospective antimalarial drug target. While several SUB1 inhibitors have been developed, irreversible covalent inhibition has not been explored so far. In this work, we report our studies of peptidic inhibitors bearing covalent serine traps such as β-lactam, β-lactone, epoxide, and diaryl phosphonate. Out of these, peptidic diaryl phosphonates were found to be irreversible PfSUB1 inhibitors, with the best inhibitor 3b showing a PfSUB1 inhibitory potency (IC50) of 167 nM.

Malaria is a life-threatening disease caused by Plasmodium parasites and is responsible for over 600 000 deaths annually, with Plasmodium falciparum accounting for approximately 95% of these. Widespread resistance of P. falciparum strains to currently used antimalarial drugs is a well-recognized problem. − To address this challenge, several strategies are being pursued, including the development of new agents that disrupt the parasite’s life cycle through previously unexploited modes of action. −

The Plasmodium parasite is a single-celled organism which infects and replicates within human red blood cells. Escape from the red cell via a process called egress is triggered by the essential subtilisin-like serine protease SUB1, rendering this enzyme a prospective target for antimalarial drug discovery. − Our previous work resulted in discovery of peptidic boronic acids 1 (representative compound 1a, Figure ) as potent inhibitors of P. falciparum SUB1 (PfSUB1) by forming a covalent reversible bond with a catalytic serine residue of the enzyme. , Besides boronic acid, several reversible covalent warheads, such as β-ketoamides − and difluorostatones, , have been linked to the peptidic substructure, resulting in PfSUB1 inhibitors with low micromolar inhibitory potency. However, so far only peptidic boronic acids 1 were shown to inhibit P. falciparum growth in live-cell assays with submicromolar potency, which remains insufficient for progression to a candidate drug. This prompted our search for irreversible covalent inhibitors 2 and 3, − which are currently an unexplored approach for the inhibition of PfSUB1 (Figure ). While SUB1 acts in minutes after release, substantial evidence indicates that SUB1 is catalytically active in exonemes and potentially can be targeted by covalent inhibitors long before egress. First, pulse-chase and immunolocalization experiments have shown that the p54 and fully mature p47 forms of SUB1 are produced within just minutes of biosynthesis and accumulate within exonemes in the parasite. Both forms of SUB1 are fully functional in the parasite and proteolytically active enzyme can be extracted using detergent from exonemes of mature schizonts.

We have constructed irreversible covalent inhibitors comprising β-lactam (compounds 2a,b), β-lactone (compounds 2c,d), epoxide (compound 2e), and diaryl phosphonate (compounds 3a–3g) warheads.

Peptidic phosphonates 3a,b were obtained by coupling of tripeptide 4a with aminophosphonates 11a,b (Scheme ; see the Supporting Information for the synthesis of building blocks 11). The more lipophilic tripeptide 4b bearing a quinoline carbonyl as an end-capping group was subjected to coupling with several phosphonic acid esters 11a–11d to produce peptidic phosphonates 3c–3f. For comparison, peptidic phosphonate 3g bearing acetyl as an end-capping group was prepared from tripeptide 4c and aminophosphonate 11b.

Compounds 2a–2e and 3a were assayed for their ability to inhibit PfSUB1 by testing them first at a concentration of 0.5 μM as we aimed for submicromolar inhibitors. Compounds 2a–2e consisting of β-lactam, β-lactone, and epoxide warheads were found to have no potency to inhibit PfSUB1 at a concentration of 0.5 μM. In turn, peptidic phosphonate 3a showed 50% inhibition of PfSUB1 at 0.5 μM concentration. For this phosphonate (3a), an IC50 value of 396 nM was determined when incubated for 30 min with the enzyme before addition of the substrate (Table , entry 1). An analogue 3b bearing fluorosubstituted phenoxygroups at phosphonate showed improved PfSUB1 inhibition potency (Table , entry 2), likely due to increased electrophilicity of phosphonate. Compounds 3c,d containing a quinoline carboxylate as an end-capping group (Table , entries 3 and 4) showed decreased PfSUB1 inhibition potency compared to their benzoyl analogues 3a,b. This finding was contrary to the observation from peptic boronic acid series 1, where the end-capping group had little effect on enzyme inhibition potency. Introduction of methyl- and methoxy-substituted phenoxygroups at phosponate of compounds 3e,f further decreased PfSUB1 inhibition potency (Table , entries 5 and 6). For methyl-substituted analogue 3e, it was apparently due to increased steric bulk, while methoxy analogue 3 has reduced electrophilicity. An analogue 3g with acetyl as an end-capping group and fluorosubstituted phenoxygroups at phosponate showed potent PfSUB1 inhibition again suggesting preference of small end-capping groups for high PfSUB1 inhibition potency.

The potency of compounds 3a–f to inhibit growth of P. falciparum in red blood cells was found to be in the micromolar range (Table , entries 1–6) despite their different enzyme inhibition potency. The possible reason for this could be inadequate penetration of inhibitors to the site of the action in the parasite and/or the off-target effects of phosphonates 3a–f, warranting further studies to elucidate their mode of action. Inhibitor 3g was found to be inactive in the parasite growth assay, probably due to higher polarity preventing membrane penetration.

The irreversible covalent inhibition mechanism of compound 3a was confirmed by an incubation time-dependent shift of the IC50 value (Figure ). The IC50 value dropped 10-fold after 5 h of inhibition approaching IC50= 57 nM. An attempt was made to register a covalent adduct of compound 3a with PfSUB1 using MALDI-TOF, however, this was not successful.

Incubation-time-dependent shift of IC50 value of compound 3a, proving the irreversible covalent binding.

In summary, we have explored covalent irreversible PfSUB1 inhibition by linking several covalent warheads to the peptidic part of the inhibitor. Out of these, inhibitors 3a,b and 3g bearing a diphenylphosphonate warhead showed submicromolar inhibition of PfSUB1 after 30 min of pre-incubation. The irreversible inhibition was proved by an incubation time-dependent shift of the IC50 value of the compound 3a. Most of the peptidic phosphonate inhibitors showed an ability to inhibit the growth of P. falciparum parasites with an EC50 = 14–22 μM; however, there was no direct correlation with enzyme inhibition potency. Overall, we have reported the first irreversible covalent inhibitors of PfSUB1, although their potency remains lower than that of peptidic boronic acids 1. Further optimization is required to improve the potency of irreversible PfSUB1 inhibitors in cell-based assays, likely by enhancing their PfSUB1 inhibitory potency and reducing their peptidic character.
