Asymptomatic faecal carriage of vancomycin-resistant Enterococci among inpatients and outpatients in a Kenyan hospital: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Anthony Karoki Maina, John Maingi, Abednego Musyoki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVancomycin-resistant Enterococci (VRE) strains are a growing public health concern globally; however, epidemiological data to inform infection prevention and control interventions in line with antimicrobial resistance (AMR) global and national action plans in our study setting are limited. Here, we assessed VRE asymptomatic faecal carriage, AMR profiles and risk factors among inpatients and outpatients in a county referral hospital in Kenya.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study design among adult patients (≥ 18) at the Kiambu County Referral Hospital outpatient department (OPD) and patients in the inpatient department (IPD) from June to September 2022. A systematic random sampling technique was employed to recruit 155 participants from the OPD, excluding patients presenting with histories of diarrhoea, antibiotics use (≤ 48 h), and ≤ 90 days previous admission. In IPD, 155 patients admitted for ≥ 48 h, were consecutively enrolled, excluding diarrhoea cases. The participants’ sociodemographic and clinical data were collected using a structured questionnaire, with stool samples collected in sterile containers and transported in an icebox to Kenya Medical Research Institute, Nairobi, for analysis within 4–6 h, using standard and automated bacteriological methods.ResultsThe overall faecal carriage of VRE was 5.2%, 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.98–8.25% (16/310), highest among the outpatients (3.9%; 12/310), 95% CI: 2.02–6.66%) where Enterococcus faecium predominated (overall: 62.5%, 10/16; IPD: 18.8%, 3/16; OPD: 43.8%, 7/16). VRE isolates were 100% resistant to erythromycin and tetracycline, with 31.3% (5/16) non-susceptible to teicoplanin, but remained sensitive to linezolid, tigecycline, and nitrofurantoin. Sixty-three per cent (62.5%, 10/16) of VRE isolates were multidrug-resistant, predominated by E. faecium (80%, 8/10). The multiple antibiotic resistance index (MARI) was > 0.2. The independent predictors of VRE carriage were female gender (aOR = 10.8, 95% CI 1.1-110.1, p = 0.045) and antibiotic dose completion behaviour (aOR = 0.122, 95% CI 0.0002-1.0, p = 0.046) among the outpatients.ConclusionWe report asymptomatic faecal carriage of VRE strains that are MDR predominately among outpatients, whereby females and patients with a history of not completing an antibiotic prescription were at increased risk of colonization. To inform infection prevention interventions, establishing the transmission mechanisms and sustained AMR surveillance are warranted to mitigate VRE spread in our study area.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-025-01577-8.

Introduction

Enterococci are non-spore-forming, catalase-negative, and facultative anaerobic Gram-positive bacteria classified as Lancefield group D streptococci [1]. These bacteria are part of normal gastrointestinal tract (GIT) flora in humans and animals and can adapt and survive in harsh and dynamic environments. For instance, enterococci can thrive in 6.5% sodium chloride (NaCl) and 40% bile salts at a pH level of 9.6, and endure exposure to a temperature of 60 °C for 30 min. Genus Enterococci has more than 50 species, with Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis) and E. faecium (E. faecium) being the most common species that cause opportunistic infections in humans. Enterococci are frequent in various environments, including soil, surface runoff waters, and seawater [1, 2]. As such, enterococcal infections can arise from endogenous sources within the individual patient’s GIT or through patient-to-patient transmission. This transmission may occur via direct contact or indirectly through contaminated hands of healthcare personnel or contaminated patient-care equipment and environmental surfaces [3]. Enterococci are associated with a range of infections, which include infective endocarditis, urinary tract infections, intra-abdominal and pelvic infections, bacteraemia, and surgical site infections [4, 5, 6].

Emergence of Vancomycin-resistant Enterococci (VRE) following heightened use of vancomycin or third-generation cephalosporin has exacerbated the burden of enterococcal infections globally [6, 7]. VRE infections are associated with severe adverse outcomes: increased morbidity, mortality, and costs [8, 9, 10]. Global mortality from VRE, especially bloodstream infections, ranges from 60 to 70%, and the economic burden due to VRE is substantial, estimated at $539 million [7]. According to the European Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network, the prevalence of VRE in Europe increased from 8.1% in 2012 to 19.0% in 2018 [11]. A three-year prospective study by Sivaradjy et al. in India reported an increasing trend in VRE-associate bacteraemia: 6.12% (2018), 13.2% (2019), and 19.2% (2020) [12]. Elsewhere, a pooled VRE prevalence of 11.40% was documented in Western Asia, 7.7% in South Asia (7.7%), 3.1% in East Asia, and 1.8% in South East Asia [13]. In Africa, there are a few epidemiological studies on VRE. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis based on a one-health perspective reported an overall pooled prevalence of VRE at 26.8% [14]. The prevalence was highest in South Africa (74.8%), followed by Egypt (37.2%), Uganda (9.8%), Morocco (8.2%), Ethiopia (7.9%), Tunisia (6.5%), Tanzania (6.1%), Nigeria (2.8%), and Algeria (2.8%) [14].

VRE pose a significant public health threat due to limited treatment options, with E. faecium strains recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as ‘High Priority’ pathogens requiring urgent development of new therapeutic agents [15]. Vancomycin, a glycopeptide that inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis, is considered a last-line drug for severe infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-positive bacteria [16]. The two major treatment options for VRE infections are linezolid and daptomycin [5], but these medications are without sufficient clinical trial evidence [7]. Additionally, both treatments face the threat of emerging resistance [17, 18]. These factors underscore the importance of implementing effective infection control programmes, maintaining robust surveillance systems and strictly adhering to antibiotic stewardship programs to mitigate the emergence and global spread of VRE strains.

In May 2015, the World Health Assembly adopted the Global Action Plan on antimicrobial resistance (AMR). This plan aims to strengthen the knowledge and evidence base regarding AMR through improved surveillance and research, reduce infection rates through enhanced sanitation, hygiene, and infection prevention measures, and optimize the use of antimicrobial medicines in both human and animal health [19]. However, many resource-constrained countries have not realized the full benefits of global and national action plans on AMR due to various implementation barriers. In Kenya, for instance, these challenges include limited funding, inadequate AMR knowledge, weak infrastructure supporting implementation, and insufficient investment in communication, data management capacity, and monitoring and evaluation efforts. Additionally, there is inadequate political commitment, limited laboratory capacity affecting surveillance and data collection, and underutilization of established microbiology services [20].

Gastrointestinal colonization with VRE bacteria plays a significant role in antimicrobial resistance genes dissemination among the gut microbiota, increasing clinical infections and intra-hospital transmission. Without sustained surveillance, VRE intestinal carriage can serve as a silent reservoir for drug-resistant organisms’ transmission in both community and hospital settings [21]. Therefore, detecting GIT carriers is critical in designing and implementing effective VRE infection control measures. Predictors of VRE intestinal colonization include age over 65 years, length of stay (LOS) [22], prior infections, poor nutritional status [23], history of hospitalization, habit of eating uncooked food and invasive procedures [24]. However, there is limited epidemiological data on VRE to inform infection prevention interventions in the Kenyan context. This study, therefore, aimed to determine the asymptomatic faecal carriage rate, AMR profiles, and risk factors of VRE among inpatients and outpatients in a county referral hospital in Kenya.

Materials and methods

Study setting, design and population

We conducted this study at Kiambu County Referral Hospital (KCRH), a level five [5] facility with 417-bed capacity, located 14 km on the northern border of the Nairobi Capital City and lies within the Nairobi Metropolitan region, Kenya. KCRH is located in Kiambu town, a residential area surrounded informal settlements including Ndumberi, Kiambaa, Riabai, Kihingo, Kanunga and Ndegu.

This study adopted a cross-sectional design among adult patients (≥ 18) at the KCRH outpatient department (OPD) and those admitted in the facility inpatient department (IPD) from June to September 2022. A systematic random sampling technique was used to recruit 155 participants from the OPD, excluding patients presenting with histories of diarrhoea (to focus solely on asymptomatic VRE carriage), antibiotics use (≤ 48 h) as it could interfere with bacterial isolation, and hospitalization within 90 days of sampling to avoid reporting nosocomial isolates potentially acquired during recent hospital stays. A consecutive sampling technique was used to enrol 155 patients admitted for ≥ 48 h in maternity, surgical and medical wards, and excluded diarrhoea cases.

Sample collection

Patients’ demographic and clinical data were collected using a structured questionnaire. Stool samples were collected in sterile screw-cap single use containers (Zhejiang Aicor Medical Technology Co., Ltd, China), and then transported in an icebox to Centre for Medical Research, Kenya Medical Research Institute (CMR-KEMRI) in Nairobi, for analysis within 4–6 h of collection.

Bacterial culture, identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Stool samples were aseptically inoculated into Tryptone Soya broth (Titan Biotech, India) and incubated aerobically at 37°C overnight. The resultant cultures were inoculated onto Bile-esculin agar (HiMedia, India) with 6 mg/L vancomycin (Scott-Edill Pharmacia Ltd, India) and incubated in the ambient air at 37°C for 18–24 hours. Isolates identification and the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of antimicrobial agents were analysed using the Vitek 2® Advanced Expert System (AES) automated platform (bioMèrieux, Marcy l’.Etoile, France). We based the choice of antibiotics tested, including levofloxacin, erythromycin (ERY), linezolid (LZD), vancomycin (VAN), teicoplanin (TEC), tetracycline (TET), tigecycline (TGC) and nitrofurantoin (NIT), and interpretation of the MICs on Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute (CLSI, 2021) guidelines and Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-Identified Interpretive Criteria [25], with Enterococcus faecalis (ATCC29212) as the quality control organism [26].

Statistical analysis

We entered the Data into Epicollect5 and exported it to Statistical Packages for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 24.0 for Windows (IBM SPSS Statistics, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis. The authors presented the data in frequency and percentage in tables and figures, defined multidrug resistance (MDR) as resistance to at least three antibiotic classes [81], and calculated multiple antibiotic resistance indices (MARI) as a/b, where a = number of antibiotics the organism was resistant to, b = the total number of antibiotics tested [27]. The association between VRE faecal carriage and patients’ demographics and clinical characteristics was calculated by bivariate logistic regression. Varriables showing p-value ≤ 0.2 were further analysed using multivariate logistic regression to determine whether there was an independent association. Associations with a p-value of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant and are bolded in Table 1.

Results

The study enrolled 310 participants, equally drawn from the inpatient (155) and outpatient (155) departments. The male-to-female ratio was 1: 1.5 (39.7%: 60.3%). The majority of the participants were in the age group 18–38 years (61.3%), married (55.5%), self-employed (69.4%), had tertiary education (56.5%), and with no history of travelling outside Kiambu County in the last three months (80.0%). Asymptomatic faecal carriage of VRE in the study populations

The overall faecal carriage of vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) was 5.2%, 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.98–8.25% (16/310), highest among the outpatient (3.9%; 12/310), 95% CI: 2.02–6.66% (Fig. 1). Enterococcus faecium was the predominant isolate (overall: 62.5%, 10/16; IPD: 18.8%, 3/16; OPD: 43.8%, 7/16). Enterococcus faecalis (overall: 37.5%, 6/16; IPD: 6.3%, 1/16; OPD: 31.3%, 5/16) was the only other VRE isolated, and there were no co-carriage of the two species (Fig. 1).

Factors associated with faecal carriage of VRE among the study participants

VRE carriage was associated with gender and antibiotic dose completion behaviour among the outpatients, Table 1. Females were eleven times more likely to be VRE faecal carriers (aOR = 10.8, 95% CI 1.1-110.1, p = 0.045) when compared with male counterparts, while patients who had a history of not completing antibiotic doses were 88% at risk of harbouring VRE (aOR = 0.122, 95% CI 0.0002-1.0, p = 0.046) when compared to patients who completed a prescribed dose. We found no statistically significant association between VRE carriage and inpatients’ demographic and clinical characteristics analysed (data not shown).

Discussion

In this study, the overall faecal carriage of vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) was 5.2% (16/310), higher among the outpatients (3.9%) than in outpatients (1.3%). The overall prevalence of VRE in our study was lower than reported in high-risk patients in university hospitals in Serbia (28.7%) [28], Bulgarian critically ill patients (24.2%) [29], a community setting in Casablanca, Morocco (21%) [30], and HIV-positive patients in health-care facilities in Debre Berhan Town (13. 8%) [24]. However, the VRE faecal carriage in our study was higher than the 1.8% reported by Kibwana and others among newly diagnosed adult HIV patients in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania [31]. The observed discordance in faecal carriage rates could be due to differences in the target population characteristics, methodological variability, adherence levels to antimicrobial stewardship programs and infection prevention and control (IPC) policies.

We found Enterococcus faecium as the predominating isolate, consistent with observation in other studies showing that despite E. faecalis being the predominant Enterococci, the majority of VRE isolates are E. faecium [30, 23]. In a finding of a systematic review and meta-analysis study in India between 2000 and 2022, E. faecium (58.15%, 214/1450) was the most prevalent VRE [4]. Elsewhere in Tanzania [31] and Ethiopia [32], E. faecium was the only VRE isolated. This species has a higher potential to acquire antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes when compared with other Enterococci, such as E. faecalis [4, 33]. Human pathogenic E. faecalis colonize different domestic animals and birds, but clinical E. faecium isolates are reported in humans and the environment [34]. Even though E. faecium from animals might not constitute a human hazard; they can serve as reservoirs of AMR genes for other pathogenic enterococci [33, 34].

In the current study, faecal carriage of vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium was predominant among outpatients (43.8%) than inpatients (18.8%). Hannaoui and colleagues reported a similar finding in a community setting in Casablanca, Morocco, where Enterococcus faecium (55%) was the predominant isolate, followed by Enterococcus faecalis (45%). Prior antimicrobial exposure, including frequent parenteral use of vancomycin for treatment of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and penicillin-resistant enterococcal infections, as well as oral use of vancomycin for Clostridium difficile enterocolitis, can alter bowel flora, favouring the selection of vancomycin-resistant strains. Further, exposure to subtherapeutic doses of glycopeptides used in feeds as growth promotors can allow the selection of VRE strains [35]. Environmental cleaning may play a pertinent role in controlling VRE transmission [36, 37], but in settings with poor sanitation and inadequate IPC, intestinal carriage of VRE may serve as a silent reservoir and a hotspot for nosocomial transmission and colonization, which may precede infection [22]. Our study finding suggests a higher exposure to E. faecium in the community than in the hospital setting, underscoring the need for sustained surveillance in clinical settings and mapping the environmental reservoirs and transmission dynamics to inform infection prevention interventions.

All the VRE isolates exhibited a similar antimicrobial susceptibility profile of 100% resistance to erythromycin and tetracycline. Enterococcus faecium isolates were distinctively resistant to levofloxacin (70%). The observed resistance was higher than previously reported for erythromycin (63.1–97.5%) [32, 38, 39]. Tetracycline resistance in the current study was higher than documented among inpatients in Hawassa city, Sidama Regional State (70.2–94.1%) [32] and Jimma (92.5–94.1%) [38] in Ethiopia. Further in our study, 31.3% of VRE isolates were teicoplanin-resistant, and 68.7% were teicoplanin-sensitive, consistent with VanA and VanB phenotypes, respectively. The VanA phenotype is characterized by acquired inducible non-susceptibility to vancomycin and teicoplanin, whereas VanB by resistance to vancomycin but not teicoplanin [40, 41]. This observation suggests that vancomycin resistance among the current study isolates was attributable to VanA (vancomycin-resistant and teicoplanin-resistant isolates) and VanB (vancomycin-resistant, teicoplanin-susceptible isolates) genes. All VRE in our study remained susceptible to linezolid, tigecycline, and nitrofurantoin, similar to a finding on linezolid (100%) in Nigeria [42] and on nitrofurantoin (97.3%) in Ethiopia [43]. Linezolid, tigecycline and daptomycin are the major antibiotics of the ‘last resort’ for infections caused by VRE that are MDR [5], implying a reserved clinical value of the three antibiotics in our study area.

Sixty-three per cent (62.5%, 10/16) of VRE isolates in our study were MDR, lower than 88% reported in a community setting in Casablanca, Morocco [30], and among HIV-positive patients in healthcare facilities in Debre Berhan Town, Ethiopia (87.4%) [24]; however, the authors did not separately categorize MDR-VRE isolated among the HIV-positive patients. Our study finding is comparable to that documented by Mengesha and others among inpatients at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital in Ethiopia (54.6%) [32] but was higher than reported by Ali et al. among outpatients in Dessie Referral Hospital, Northeast Ethiopia (29.5%) [44]. Enterococcus species possess a repertoire of antibiotic resistance mechanisms, such as overexpression of efflux pumps, drug inactivation and modification of drug targets, and can become the dominant gastrointestinal tract flora under antibiotic pressure [45, 46]. We found E. faecium (80%, 8/10) as the predominant MDR VRE, supporting the available literature that this species acquires antibiotic resistance more frequently than E. faecalis [46]. In the meantime, the multiple antibiotic resistance index (MARI) for the two VRE isolates was > 0.2, indicating a high-risk source of contamination from a setting with frequent antibiotic use or previous exposure of the organism to antimicrobial agents [27].

In our study, paticipant’s gender and antibiotic dose completion behaviour were the independent predictors of VRE carriage among the outpatients. Females were eleven [11] times more likely to be VRE faecal carriers (aOR = 10.8, 95% CI 1.1-110.1, p = 0.045) when compared with their male counterparts. A similar finding is documented in previous epidemiological study reports, showing higher VRE faecal carriage [44] and infections [47] among females than males. Correa-Martínez et al. reported a dissimilar finding in a systematic review and meta-analysis study, documenting sex differences with male preference (59%) in the VRE bloodstream infections (BSI) [48]. The discordant findings could be due to differences in the study sample (stool vs. blood). Besides, socioeconomic, behavioural, genetic and hormonal factors contribute to the sex differences observed in infectious diseases [48]. Further, participants with a history of not completing antibiotic doses in the current study were 88% at risk of harbouring VRE (aOR = 0.122, 95% CI 0.0002-1.0, p = 0.046) compared to patients who completed a prescribed dose. The suboptimal use of antimicrobials can induce the development of resistant bacterial subpopulations harbouring mutated genes compared with the parental strain [49].

Conclusion

We report asymptomatic faecal carriage of VRE strains that are MDR predominately among outpatients, whereby females and participants with a history of not completing an antibiotic prescription were at increased risk of colonization. To inform infection prevention interventions, establishing the transmission mechanisms and sustained AMR surveillance are warranted to mitigate VRE spread in our study area.

Study limitation

We could not elucidate the molecular characteristics of VRE due to inaccessibility to molecular facilities. This analysis could have provided an in-depth understanding of the underlying mechanisms of vancomycin resistance among the isolates and allowed the development of a model to predict transmission dynamics in our study setting. As a single hospital-based study with a small sample size, findings may not present the countrywide situation. A multicentre study is needed to fully understand the extent of this problem and determine the risk factors associated with VRE colonization in hospital settings. The few positive cases among inpatients impended analysis of the putative factors for the colonization. Further, we could not gather detailed information regarding specific antibiotics participants were previously exposed to because most of them had forgotten. This limited the study analysis to establish the association between prior antibiotics exposure and VRE GIT colonization. However, our study findings are critical to public health, especially in settings where healthcare facilities largely lack microbiology laboratories for early detection and reporting of antimicrobial resistance.
