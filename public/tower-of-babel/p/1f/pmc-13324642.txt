Application of Caenorhabditis elegans’ Feeding Behavior-Based Assay in Drug Discovery and Development
Authors: Aaron Taehwan Kim, Yeonhwa Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Feeding behavior is a fundamental biological process regulated by a complex interplay of sensory input, internal metabolic status, and environmental stimuli. Caenorhabditis elegans is a powerful in vivo model for studying the genetic, neural, and molecular mechanisms underlying diverse behaviors, based on its well-characterized nervous system and conserved signaling pathways. This review summarizes recent findings from four feeding behavior-based assays—avoidance, preference, satiety-induced quiescence, and social feeding—in C. elegans, highlighting the key genes, signaling molecules, and external stimuli that influence these behaviors, as well as their potential applications. By integrating insights from these assays, we illustrate how C. elegans serves as a valuable model organism for understanding appetite regulation, decision-making, and feeding-related pathologies that can potentially be translatable to human behaviors.

INTRODUCTION

Feeding behavior is a crucial biological function essential for maintaining energy balance, promoting growth, and ensuring survival (Avery and You, 2012; Berthoud et al., 2017). Thus, feeding behaviors are regulated not only by internal energy balance but also by sensory inputs, environmental stimuli, and prior experiences, reflecting a complex integration of metabolic and neurological processes (Andermann and Lowell, 2017; Berthoud et al., 2017; Sternson and Atasoy, 2014). Moreover, disruptions in feeding regulation are known to be correlated with a wide range of metabolic and neuropsychiatric disorders, including obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, anorexia, and binge-eating disorders, all of which are increasingly recognized as significant global health challenges (Berthoud et al., 2017; McCuen-Wurst et al., 2018).

Neural circuits governing hunger, satiety, and reward-related eating are highly conserved across species (Herrera Moro Chao et al., 2022; Walker et al., 2015). Many organisms, from invertebrates to mammals, exhibit diverse feeding behaviors that are shaped by their internal physiological states and environmental factors (Hanson-Vaux et al., 2013; Lemaitre and Miguel-Aliaga, 2013; Ribeiro and Dickson, 2010). For example, Drosophila melanogaster changes its feeding preference toward protein-rich foods after mating, and mice avoid bitter and sour tastes, which could have evolved as a survival mechanism to prevent the ingestion of toxic or spoiled substances (Hanson-Vaux et al., 2013; Ribeiro and Dickson, 2010). Therefore, understanding various feeding behaviors at both the organismal and molecular levels offers insights into broader physiological responses, such as homeostasis, decision-making, and adaptive behaviors, making it a potentially valuable research tool.

Optimal diet theory provides an evolutionary framework for understanding feeding decisions, proposing that organisms strive to maximize energy gain while minimizing risks, like toxin exposure and metabolic imbalance (Sih and Christensen, 2001). This cost-benefit integration explains behaviors such as selective food preferences, avoidance of harmful stimuli, and changes in foraging based on internal states (Walker et al., 2023). Notably, neural circuits mediating these trade-offs are conserved across species, indicating that even simple invertebrates encode basic decision-making algorithms (Walker et al., 2015).

Caenorhabditis elegans is a powerful invertebrate model organism in the life sciences due to its transparent body, well-studied and relatively simple nervous system with 302 neurons, and its flexible genetic manipulability (Ashrafi, 2007). It contains genomes that are functionally homologous to approximately 65% of human disease-associated genes (Shen et al., 2018). Despite its small size and simple structure, C. elegans possesses major organ systems characteristic of more complex organisms, including the nervous and digestive systems, all of which are known to contribute to its feeding behaviors (Apfeld and Alper, 2018; Avery and You, 2012). Thus, understanding feeding behavior in C. elegans would provide essential insights into its regulatory mechanisms. Based on this, the purpose of this review is to summarize the reports on the various feeding behaviors of C. elegans, as well as its known underlying mechanisms.

REGULATION OF FEEDING IN Caenorhabditis elegans

As a bacterivore, the presence and quality of bacterial food in the worm’s environment, as well as various environmental stimuli, influence the feeding behavior of C. elegans (Avery and You, 2012; Liu and Zhang, 2020). The primary organs involved in C. elegans’ feeding are the mouth and pharynx, a muscular pump that rhythmically ingests and grinds bacteria into the intestinal tract (Doncaster, 1962). The pharyngeal pumping is controlled by both intrinsic pacemaker activity and the external environment, responding to various neuromodulators and neurotransmitters and relying on T- and L-type Ca2+ channels for action potentials (Avery and You, 2012; Shtonda and Avery, 2005; Trojanowski et al., 2016).

Regulation of feeding in C. elegans involves complex integration of sensory inputs, neuropeptide signaling, and metabolic states (Sze et al., 2000; You et al., 2006), including sensory neurons (ASI, ASH, and ASE), interneurons (AIA, AIB, and RIB), command interneurons (AVB, PVB, and AVD), and motor neurons (RIM, VBDB, and RMD) (Haley and Chalasani, 2024; Leinwand et al., 2015; Sze et al., 2000). Additionally, feeding behavior is regulated by internal and external stimuli in response to key neurotransmitters, serotonin, dopamine, tyramine, and neuropeptide-like proteins, such as FMRFamide-related peptide family and neuropeptide-like proteins (Bhat et al., 2021; Calhoun et al., 2015; Li et al., 1999; Nelson et al., 2014; Özbey et al., 2020; Sawin et al., 2000; Van Der Auwera et al., 2020; Vidal-Gadea and Pierce-Shimomura, 2012; Wragg et al., 2007).

In addition to neuronal regulation, the feeding behavior of C. elegans is closely linked to various metabolic signaling pathways, including insulin/insulin-like growth factor-1 signaling (IIS), AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), and Target of Rapamycin signaling (Cunningham et al., 2012; Hahm et al., 2019; Ryu et al., 2018). These pathways integrate nutrient status with behavioral outcomes. For example, starved C. elegans showed enhanced avoidance against ascr#3 pheromone through modulation of DAF-2/DAF-16 activity, which is the homolog of the insulin/insulin-like growth factor pathway in C. elegans (Ryu et al., 2018).

BEHAVIORS ASSOCIATED WITH C. elegans’ FEEDING

Optimal diet theory asserts that when an animal comes across food, the situation does not necessarily result in food intake; instead, it chooses to accept or reject the food upon detection or encounter in order to maximize the net intake of energy while considering constraints such as toxins, predation, hedonic factors, and thermoregulation (Haley and Chalasani, 2024). For example, food can be beneficial (for nutrition or to enhance a rewarding sensation) or harmful (poisonous). In the wild, C. elegans is exposed to a diverse range of bacterial types (approximately 2,400 taxonomic units) with varying nutritional values and toxicities, and C. elegans naturally prefers certain bacteria over others (Avery and You, 2012; Samuel et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2020).

Since C. elegans provide a powerful platform for analyzing how genetic, neural, and metabolic networks integrate to drive complex decision-making in feeding behaviors and many of these pathways are conserved in higher organisms, the next section reviews currently used feeding behavior-based assays of C. elegans, such as avoidance, preference, quiescence, and social feeding behaviors, including known genes and responses that contribute to these behaviors (all are summarized in Table 1 and Fig. 1) (Abada et al., 2009; Chou et al., 2022; Hahm et al., 2019; Sawin et al., 2000).

AVOIDANCE BEHAVIOR OF C. elegans

C. elegans exhibits avoidance behavior to evade harmful environments such as pathogenic bacteria, toxic chemicals, or depleted food sources, which is critical for survival in its natural habitat (Meisel and Kim, 2014; Milward et al., 2011). Avoidance assays (also known as lawn-leaving assays) typically quantify reactive responses to negative stimuli by measuring how quickly or how frequently C. elegans leaves a specific habitual area (Chou et al., 2022; Meisel and Kim, 2014; Milward et al., 2011). For example, C. elegans leaves bacterial lawns when exposed to pathogens like Pseudomonas aeruginosa or when food quality deteriorates, reflecting a trade-off between nutrient acquisition and risk mitigation (Reddy et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2005). This avoidance behavior is influenced by sensory signals, such as bacterial metabolites and oxygen levels, as well as internal conditions, including hunger and reproductive status (Hajdú et al., 2021; Lei et al., 2024).

Studies on the avoidance behavior of C. elegans have described conserved genetic and neural pathways that modulate this behavior, summarized in Table 1. For example, dop-4 (a homolog of the D1-like dopamine receptor), qui-1 (quinine non-avoider protein 1), gpa-3 (G-protein α-3 subunit), and odr-3 (Gi-like G-protein subunit α) mediate sensitization to soluble repellents, such as quinine, to directly sense the presence of adverse compounds to control feeding behavior (Ardiel et al., 2016; Ezcurra et al., 2011; Hilliard et al., 2004). Similarly, the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)/DAF-7 pathway and the maco-1 gene contribute to avoidance behavior in response to environmental cues, such as P. aeruginosa (Hilbert and Kim, 2017; Kaletsky et al., 2020; Meisel and Kim, 2014; Milward et al., 2011). In addition, the tol-1 (Toll-like receptor) triggers rapid avoidance behavior, suggesting that innate immunity is involved in neural circuits governing avoidance behavior (Brandt and Ringstad, 2015; Pradel et al., 2007). Oxygen sensors, such as the gcy-35, regulate hyperoxia avoidance, while OCR-2/transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channels detect pathogenic metabolites, such as pyochelin, to initiate avoidance behavior (Lei et al., 2024).

Applications of the avoidance behavior assay of C. elegans include screening the effects of diverse environmental toxins, bioactive compounds, and the manipulation of surroundings. For example, the avoidance assay determined that eugenol, vanillin, and zingerone from food reduced C. elegans’ nocifensive response to noxious heat by modulating TRPV channels (OCR-2/OSM-9), while zingerone specifically targets OCR-2 (Nkambeu et al., 2021). In addition, others have reported that early-life exposure to polystyrene nanoplastics impairs the avoidance behavior of C. elegans, thereby increasing susceptibility to harmful pathogens through intestine-derived insulin-like neuropeptide and serotonin signaling (Yu et al., 2024). However, it is imperative to control volatile odorant exposure to accurately assess avoidance behavior, as demonstrated with the odorant 1-octanol (White et al., 2024).

Taking advantage of easy genetic manipulation in C. elegans, genetically engineered strains that express human genes or receptors, called ‘humanized’ strains, can be applied in behavior-based assays. For example, Teng et al. demonstrated that C. elegans can be genetically engineered to express human G protein-coupled receptors, enabling the use of simple behavioral assays to screen for receptor-ligand interactions. In a related study, expression of human chemokine receptor 5 and mouse somatostatin receptor 2 in gustatory neurons showed ligand-specific avoidance responses and structure-function interrogation of somatostatin analogs in vivo (Teng et al., 2006). Recently, mammalian μ-opioid receptor in C. elegans displayed opioid-responsive behavioral phenotype and supported genetic screening that identified a conserved orphan receptor system that modulates opioid signaling, with key findings validated in mice (Wang et al., 2019).

Moreover, technologies such as smartphone-based imaging enable high-throughput, cost-effective determination of C. elegans’ behaviors (Kwon et al., 2023). The avoidance behavior of C. elegans itself may not be directly comparable to addictive behavior in humans. However, using humanized strains and/or the many advantages of being relatively easy and fast to complete experiments, the avoidance behavior assay can help identify potential target compounds that affect neural and biochemical systems and integrate sensory stimuli and conditions into risk assessment and decision-making.

PREFERENCE BEHAVIOR OF C. elegans

The preference behavior assay, also known as the binary/multiple-choice assay, is a widely used behavioral method in C. elegans to assess preference and determine decision-making in response to competing options (Avery and You, 2012; Sun et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024). In particular, the preference towards foods is considered analogous to changes in hedonic eating behavior and the rewarding aspects of eating-related, food-addiction behaviors in humans (Paul, 2011). In the typical preference behavior setup, C. elegans are placed between two or more bacterial lawns differing in nutritional content and palatability, then C. elegans’ distribution on the assay plate is used to calculate a preference index (Greene et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2024; Shtonda and Avery, 2006). Preference behavior assay can be modified to determine the behavior towards compounds such as ethanol, addictive substances, metabolic byproducts, or odorants, etc. (Engleman et al., 2018; Ghosh et al., 2016; Katner et al., 2019)

Currently known genes involved in the preference behaviors in C. elegans are included in Table 1. It has been reported that dopamine, synthesized by cat-2 (tyrosine hydroxylase) and signaling through a D1-like receptor (dop-1), together with serotonin, synthesized by tph-1 (tryptophan hydroxylase) and acting through serotonin type-4 receptor (ser-4), modulate glucose-induced odor preference in C. elegans (Schwartz et al., 2021), as well as that nlr-1 (a conserved gene associated with autism and schizophrenia) influences preference via dopamine signaling (Bastien et al., 2024). In addition, involvement of npr-17 (homolog of opioid receptors) and npr-19 (homolog of cannabinoid 1 receptor) on food preference was reported (Cheong et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2024). Moreover, it was discovered that C. elegans can differentiate colors to control its preference behaviors, guided by specific blue-to-amber ratios of light through two cellular stress response genes, jkk-1 (homologous to MKK7, an activator of c-Jun N-terminal kinases) and lec-3 (homologous to galectin) (Ghosh et al., 2021).

The preference behavior assay is relatively fast to complete and can be used to study or screen bioactive compounds, environmental chemicals, or various potential addictive substances, as well as to determine the potential link between preference behaviors and metabolic pathways. For example, Leinwand et al. (2015) showed that exposure to different food odors, such as benzaldehyde, affected preference behaviors in C. elegans, which weakened with age but were reversibly boosted by neurotransmission. This preference assay could also be modified to incorporate prior exposure, combination with various known pharmacological compounds, behavioral type (exploitation vs. exploration), use of strains carrying human genes, and/or a parallel assay to the avoidance assay, thereby allowing a more comprehensive behavior-based screen (Iwanir et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2024). Food preference behavior assay offers unique advantages for screening the effects of bioactive compounds, microbial stimuli, and environmental and addictive chemicals in a whole-animal context.

SATIETY-INDUCED QUIESCENCE ASSAY OF C. elegans

C. elegans exhibits satiety-induced quiescence, characterized by reduced locomotion and feeding after prolonged food consumption (You et al., 2008). This behavior is considered analogous to post-prandial sleep in mammals (You et al., 2008). Assays typically involve fasting C. elegans for 12 to 18 h, followed by refeeding on bacterial lawns, and then quantifying quiescence duration by measuring movement cessation using automated tracking or manual methods (You et al., 2008).

Known genes involved in satiety-induced quiescence are summarized in Table 1. This includes insulin/insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) signaling, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)/DAF-7, serotonin, and neuropeptides, which integrate nutrient status with neural circuits to suppress foraging, and the reciprocal antagonism between ASI sensory neurons (promote quiescence) and ASH sensory neurons (suppress quiescence) (Davis et al., 2018; Pradhan et al., 2025; You et al., 2008). Other factors, such as regulation of fat metabolism pathways (including sterol regulatory element-binding protein, sbp-1), stearoyl CoA desaturase (SCD; fat-5, fat-6, and fat-7), and factors contributing to fat storage (including ets-5 and pdfr-1) are also known to modulate quiescence (Hyun et al., 2016; Juozaityte et al., 2017).

The application of satiety-induced quiescence assays is also reported, such as a Caenorhabditis-in-Drop microfluidic platform that continuously monitors quiescent behaviors for hours (Belfer et al., 2013). Utilizing C. elegans’ satiety-induced quiescence assay would provide a better understanding of the evolutionarily conserved regulation between postprandial rest and active feeding behavior.

SOCIAL FEEDING OF C. elegans

Social feeding behavior or lawn occupancy of C. elegans refers to the aggregation of worms on bacterial lawns, which is in contrast to solitary feeding, where worms disperse (De Bono et al., 2002). Social feeding behavior is tightly regulated by genetic, environmental, and neuroendocrine factors (Cowen et al., 2024; De Bono et al., 2002); normal, healthy C. elegans prefers less crowded feeding environments to improve their chances of feeding. The determination of social feeding behaviors is typically assessed by quantifying the aggregation behavior of C. elegans by counting the number of worms in contact with one or more worms at the border of the E. coli lawn (Cowen et al., 2024; De Bono et al., 2002; Dwyer et al., 2015; Rogers et al., 2003). Another method is to quantify food-leaving by placing adult C. elegans on food lawns and measuring the rate of leaving events per worm per minute after 24 h of exposure, allowing comparison of food-leaving behavior across wild-type and mutant strains (Rawsthorne et al., 2021).

Social feeding behavior is modulated by nociceptive neurons (ASH, ADL) that detect environmental stressors, and by neuropeptides (FLP-18/21) that act through the NPR-1 receptor; this behavior also depends on TRP channels (OCR-2 and OSM-9) and on proper chemoreceptor localization mediated by ODR-4 and ODR-8 (Cowen et al., 2024; De Bono et al., 2002; Rogers et al., 2003). In addition, polymorphisms in the npr-1 gene (neuropeptide Y receptor homolog) suggest that this gene contributes to social feeding behavior: the 215F allele promotes aggregation, while the 215V allele suppresses it (De Bono et al., 2002). A recent study highlights that conserved autism-associated genes (nrx-1, nlg-1, and glr-1) also contribute to C. elegans’ social feeding behaviors (Cowen et al., 2024).

Social feeding assays in C. elegans have not been as extensively studied as avoidance or preference behavior assays. However, applying the C. elegans social feeding behavior can be used to develop a screening model to study the molecular mechanisms and interactions among various pharmacological compounds contributing to asociality relevant to schizophrenia and autism (Dwyer et al., 2015; Rawsthorne et al., 2021). Pharmacological studies further support the translational relevance of social feeding assays for neuropsychiatric research. Several clinically used antipsychotic drugs such as chlorpromazine, trifluoperazine, fluphenazine, haloperidol, and olanzapine have been shown to suppress aggregation behavior of social feeding strains of C. elegans, indicating conserved neuromodulatory mechanisms underlying social behavior (Dwyer et al., 2015). These effects are linked to dopamine and calmodulin signaling pathways, suggesting that C. elegans’ social feeding represents an evolutionarily conserved behavioral protophenotype relevant to asociality observed in disorders such as schizophrenia (Dwyer, 2017; Dwyer et al., 2015). Consistent with this concept, psychotropic drugs broadly alter feeding and metabolic behaviors in C. elegans, reinforcing the utility of this model for screening neuroactive compounds affecting social and motivational states. Further understanding of the social feeding behavior of C. elegans would be valuable, providing insights into mechanisms and neuronal interactions of certain neuropsychiatric disorders.

EMERGING TECHNOLOGIES FOR HIGH-THROUGHPUT BEHAVIORAL ANALYSIS

Traditional feeding behavior assays in C. elegans have depended largely on manual observation, which limits throughput and could introduce observer bias. However, recent technological advances apply automated imaging, machine vision, and microfluidics to provide more standardized and higher throughput behavioral analysis. Open-source computer-vision tracking platforms such as Tierpsy Tracker allow large-scale, high-dimensional behavioral profiling of many worms simultaneously, transforming plate-based assays into quantitative phenotyping suitable for large datasets and compound screening (Javer et al., 2018). Enhancing image-based tracking programs such as microfluidic platforms provide precise environmental control and continuous behavioral monitoring, delivering highly reproducible phenotype analysis with high imaging resolution (Gonzales et al., 2019; Mondal et al., 2016). Special implementations such as the Caenorhabditis-in-Drop system further strengthens these capabilities by allowing long-duration monitoring of satiety-induced quiescence in individual worms (Belfer et al., 2013). A smartphone-based imaging method provides a low-cost approach to automate lawn avoidance assays, suggesting that behavioral assays can be conducted without specialized infrastructure or expensive instruments (Kwon et al., 2023). Lastly, methodological comparisons further highlight the importance of standardized assay conditions for reproducibility (White et al., 2024). Collectively, these recently emerging technologies shift C. elegans feeding behavioral assays from manual observations toward more scalable, higher-throughput, and more reproducible assays suitable for pharmacological and toxicological screening.

CONCLUSION

The diverse feeding behavior assays in Caenorhabditis elegans (avoidance, preference, satiety-induced quiescence, and social feeding) provide insights into the genetics, neural, and environmental regulation of feeding behavior. Many genes, pathways, neurons, and mechanisms have been identified over the years to better understand the feeding behavior of C. elegans. Feeding-related behaviors of C. elegans offer quantifiable, whole-animal phenotypes that arise from conserved neuromodulatory and metabolic pathways. In a drug discovery context, these assays may serve as an in vivo prioritization filter: compounds that modify avoidance, food preference, satiety-induced quiescence, or social feeding can be selected for target or pathway genetic analysis, confirmation in mammalian cell assays, and validation in higher model organisms aligned with the intended disease. Humanized receptor strains strengthen this bridge by directly connecting an in vivo behavioral outcomes to a human target and enabling downstream cross-species validations. By bridging molecular mechanisms to whole-organism behavior, these assays provide a holistic framework for exploring feeding biology and could be applied to understand higher organisms and accelerate discoveries in nutrition, pharmaceutics, toxicology, and applicable therapeutics.
