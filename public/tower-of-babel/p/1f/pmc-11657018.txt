Infantile colic is associated with development of later constipation and atopic disorders
Authors: Jakob Stokholm, Jonathan Thorsen, Ann‐Marie Malby Schoos, Morten Arendt Rasmussen, Sarah Brandt, Søren Johannes Sørensen, Nilo Vahman, Bo Chawes, Klaus Bønnelykke
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfantile colic is a common condition with limited knowledge about later clinical manifestations. We evaluated the role of the early life gut microbiome in infantile colic and later development of atopic and gastrointestinal disorders.MethodsCopenhagen Prospective Studies on Asthma in Childhood2010 cohort was followed with 6 years of extensive clinical phenotyping. The 1‐month gut microbiome was analyzed by 16S rRNA sequencing. Infantile colic was evaluated at age 3 months by interviews. Clinical endpoints included constipation to age 3 years and prospectively diagnosed asthma and atopic dermatitis in the first 6 years of life, and allergic sensitization from skin prick tests, specific Immunoglobulin E, and component analyses.ResultsOf 695 children, 55 children (7.9%) had infantile colic. Several factors were associated with colic including race, breastfeeding, and pets. The 1‐month gut microbiome composition and taxa abundances were not associated with colic, however a sparse Partial Least Squares model including combined abundances of nine species was moderately predictive of colic: median, cross‐validated AUC = 0.627, p = .003. Children with infantile colic had an increased risk of developing constipation (aOR, 2.88 [1.51–5.35], p = .001) later in life, but also asthma (aHR, 1.69 [1.02–2.79], p = .040), atopic dermatitis (aHR, 1.84 [1.20–2.81], p = .005) and had a higher number of positive allergic components (adjusted difference, 116% [14%–280%], p = .012) in the first 6 years. These associations were not mediated by gut microbiome differences.ConclusionsWe link infantile colic with risk of developing constipation and atopic disorders in the first 6 years of life, which was not mediated through an altered gut microbiome at age 1‐month. These results suggest infantile colic to involve gastrointestinal and/or atopic mechanisms.

INTRODUCTION

Infantile colic is a benign condition that resolves with time, where a neonate experiences recurring periods of excessive crying and/or discomfort in the first months of life. This results in concern for parents, who often seek professional advice. 1 , 2 The definitions used for studying colic/excessive crying vary between studies, which has resulted in large differences in the reported prevalence rates from 1.5% to 11.9%. 3 The most widely used research definition for colic has been the rule of three; crying for more than 3 h per day, more than 3 days per week, and lasting more than 3 weeks. 4 The prevalence of infantile colic is highest at weeks 5–6, but whether a child has colic or not, all infants cry more during the first 3 months of life as compared to later. A meta‐analysis, based on 28 studies with diary data, revealed, that among random infants, the duration of fussing and crying was highest and quite stable across the first 6 weeks (mean 117–133 min/day) and declining thereafter to 68 min/day in weeks 10–12, with large individual differences. 5

Maternal atopic disorders have been associated with a higher risk of developing infantile colic 6 alongside being firstborn, 7 and being exposed to smoking. 8 The underlying causes of infantile colic remain unknown; however, several childhood factors have been suggested to contribute to the symptoms. These include behavioral factors such as family or caregiver stress 9 as well as biological factors such as gut dysmotility, gut dysbiosis, food hypersensitivity, and/or allergy. 10 Breastfeeding might be associated with reduced risk of colic, 11 but it has also been proposed that allergens in both breast milk and infant formula could provoke allergic symptoms and cause infantile colic, though results are inconsistent. 12 In a meta‐analysis of dietary interventions for infantile colic management, no conclusive recommendations could be made. 13 Furthermore, smaller studies suggest that the gut microbiota could also be involved in infantile colic, 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 and that probiotic supplementation with Lactobacillus reuteri and rhamnosus show promising treatment effects 18 , 19 , 20 and can be recommended for management. 21

Infantile colic symptoms have been linked with later functional gastrointestinal health outcomes, 22 risk of migraine, 23 and in a smaller high‐risk population with later atopic traits, 24 though no associations were found in a larger study. 25 Here, we present data from the unselected prospective Copenhagen Prospective Studies on Asthma in Childhood2010 (COPSAC2010) mother–child cohort. 26 In this deeply phenotyped population, we evaluated early life factors and gut microbiota in relation to the development of infantile colic and the risk of developing later gastrointestinal and atopic manifestations.

METHODS

Study Population

The COPSAC2010 cohort is a population‐based mother–child cohort of 700 children and their parents, recruited during Week 24 of pregnancy and followed prospectively at the COPSAC research unit with deep clinical phenotyping at 1 week, 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 months, and yearly thereafter during the first 6 years of life as well as visits for acute respiratory or skin events. The research physicians collected all information during these clinical visits and were responsible for all diagnoses and treatment. 26 The study was approved by The Local Ethics Committee (H‐B‐2008‐093), and the Danish Data Protection Agency (2015‐41‐3696).

Colic

Information on infantile colic was obtained by parental interviews at the clinical 3 month visit by the definition based on the rule of three 4 ; unexplained episodes of paroxysmal crying for at least 3 h per day for at least 3 days per week for at least 3 weeks.

Clinical endpoints

Constipation and toddler's diarrhea information was obtained by parental interviews at the clinical 1, 2, and 3‐years visits. Constipation was defined as harder, dryer, and/or more infrequent stool than usual requiring laxative treatment. Toddler's diarrhea was defined as three or more watery, loose stools daily for more than 2 weeks, in children who were not breastfed, and otherwise well and thriving.

Asthma was diagnosed prospectively based on a previously detailed quantitative symptom algorithm requiring all of the following criteria 27 , 28 : (1) verified diary recordings of five episodes of troublesome lung symptoms within 6 months, each lasting at least three consecutive days, 4 weeks of continuous symptoms or a severe acute episode needing oral prednisolone or hospital admission; (2) relieve of symptoms with intermittent rescue use of inhaled β2‐agonist; and (3) response to a 3‐month course of inhaled corticosteroids and relapse upon ending treatment. 27 Remission of asthma was defined by 12 months without relapse upon cessation of inhaled corticosteroid treatment. For analyses, we used the time to onset and yearly cross‐sectional diagnosis to age 6 years.

Eczema diagnosis was made prospectively based on the criteria of Hanifin and Rajka 29 requiring the presence of 3 of 4 major criteria and at least 3 of 23 minor criteria. Remission of eczema was defined by 12 months without relapse upon cessation of topical corticosteroid treatment. For analyses, we used the time to onset and yearly cross‐sectional diagnosis to age 6 years.

Allergic sensitization at age 6 months and 6 years was defined as any positive skin prick test (SPT) ≥3 mm (6 months: ≥2 mm) (ALK‐Abello, Horsholm, Denmark) or specific Immunoglobulin E (sIgE) in serum ≥0.35 kUa/L (6 years) against common inhalant and/or food allergens (ImmunoCAP; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Allerod, Denmark). 30 Children classified as “not sensitized” were both SPT and sIgE negative for all tested allergens.

Component resolved diagnostics was performed at age 6 months and 6 years in serum samples. Allergen component‐specific IgE antibody titers were measured using the ImmunoCAP ISAC assay (ImmunoCAP; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Allerod, Denmark). 30 This represents a fixed panel of the 112 most relevant allergen components. Each component was evaluated for positive reaction (≥0.3 ISU), and the number of positives was counted per child.

Allergic rhinitis at age 6 years was diagnosed based on allergic sensitization and clinical interviews of the parents on history of significant nasal congestion, sneezing, and/or runny nose outside periods with common cold and in the relevant period of exposure to the sensitized aeroallergen.

Lung function at age 6 years was assessed by forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and specific airway resistance (sRAW) and measured by a MasterScope Pneumoscreen spirometer and MasterScope Body whole‐body plethysmograph (Erich Jäeger, Würzburg, Germany), respectively. 26

Covariates

Information on physician‐diagnosed atopy in the mother and father, maternal age at birth, pre‐pregnancy BMI, smoking during pregnancy, educational level, household income, antibiotics in pregnancy, preeclampsia, gestational age, birth weight and length, hospitalization of the child after birth, antibiotics administered to the child, pet ownership, older children in the home, and duration of exclusive and total breastfeeding period was obtained during the scheduled visits to the research clinic. Information on delivery mode and intrapartum antibiotics was validated against birth records from hospitals and information on antibiotic use during pregnancy and childhood was validated against national registries. 31 Living environment was based on home address at birth using the CORINE satellite‐based land cover database. 32 Sensitization in mother and father was evaluated by sIgE in blood samples. Fish oil and Vitamin D intervention groups in pregnancy were included.

Fecal samples

Stool samples were collected 1 month after birth, sent to the laboratory and frozen at −80°C. DNA was extracted using MoBio PowerSoil kits on an epMotion 5075, amplified using a two‐step PCR reaction with 515F and 806R primers flanking the V4 region of 16S rRNA gene, and sequenced using the v2 kit (PE250bp reads) on the MiSeq platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego CA). Primer sequences were removed with Cutadapt v1.15. 33 Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were inferred with DADA2 34 through QIIME 2 v2018.2.0. 35 Forward and reverse reads were trimmed 8 bp at the 5′ end. Forward and reverse reads were trimmed to 180 and 160 bp, respectively. Other DADA2 parameters were kept default. Taxonomy was assigned with the assignTaxonomy function from DADA2 using the GTDB ssu‐r86 database. 36 Unannotated ASVs at Phylum level were discarded. Furthermore, only samples with at least 2000 reads were included. The phylogenetic tree produced by QIIME was rooted by the archaeal clade.

Statistics and data analysis

Logistic regression was used for analyzing associations in the baseline characteristics of the cohort in relation to development of colic. Associations between infantile colic and age of asthma and atopic dermatitis onset were assessed by Kaplan–Meier curves and quantified in terms of hazard ratios (HR) by Cox proportional hazards regression and by odds ratios (OR) using logistic regression for cross‐sectional endpoints. The yearly prevalence of asthma and atopic dermatitis were analyzed by repeated measurement generalized estimation equation (GEE) models. Multiple comparisons were controlled using Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. 37 Number of positive allergen components was analyzed using quasi‐Poisson regression models. All models were adjusted for relevant covariates. Shannon diversity, evenness, richness and Faith's Phylogenetic Diversity (PD) were used as measures of the within sample diversity (α‐diversity), and the between sample diversity metrics (β‐diversity) were computed as Bray‐Curtis, Jaccard, weighted and unweighted UniFrac 38 distances. Differences in microbial α‐diversity and relative abundances were compared using Wilcoxon rank‐sum test and differences in β‐diversity were tested for inference using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (Adonis2 with 999 permutations). 39 , 40 We applied supervised sparse Partial Least Squares (sPLS) 41 models after species filtering (prevalence >5% of children, >0.01% mean relative abundance (mra)) and log‐transforming relative abundances, using half the lowest nonzero value as a pseudocount. We selected the optimum number of input variables using repeated 10‐fold cross‐validation of the Area Under the receiver operator Curve (AUC) statistic. Predicted values from these models were combined to infantile colic bacterial score 42 and analyzed in relation to risk factors and later clinical endpoints. Mediation was evaluated with the R‐package “mediation” using 1000 simulations. 43 All data analysis was performed in the statistical software package R version 4.1.2, with the package phyloseq 44 to handle the microbiome data.

RESULTS

Cohort and sample characteristics

In the entire COPSAC2010 cohort of 700 children, we had information on infantile colic for 695 (99%) children. Of these, 55 children (7.9%) fulfilled the criteria for infantile colic at the clinical visit at 3 months. While most factors were similar between children with or without colic, we found that among children with colic, there were significantly more non‐Northern Europeans (colic 12.7% vs. non‐colic 3.8%, p = .004), and less children who were either breastfed exclusively (55.6% vs. 68.8%, p = .049) or partially (74.1% vs. 85.8%, p = .023) at age 3 months with similar breastfeeding associations at age 1 month. More children with colic had a cat in the home at birth (34.5% vs. 18.9%, p = .007), both cat and dog (14.5% vs. 4.7%, p = .004), while less children with colic had a dog only (1.8% vs. 14.8%, p = .027) (Table 1). Interestingly, we found a significant interaction between cat and dog in the home at birth in relation to the risk of developing colic (p‐interaction = .013).

Early life gut microbiome and infantile colic

To evaluate the influence of the gut microbiome in relation to infantile colic, the composition at 1 month was examined in 613 children. These samples had a median sequencing depth of 51,203 reads and 2571 unique ASVs were identified. The median richness per sample was 29 ASVs. No significant differences existed in α‐diversity (Shannon index, richness, evenness, or PD) between children with or without infantile colic (Table S1). Furthermore, no significant differences were found for β‐diversity indices (Bray Curtis, unweighted uniFrac, weighted uniFrac, and Jaccard) (Table S2).

When performing differential abundance testing on the 30 most common genera at 1‐month in relation to infantile colic, only the unspecific Family Enterococcaceae and Actinomyces were found nominally significant, and none survived FDR adjustment (Figure 1A). At species level, the same pattern was observed (Figure S1). To further evaluate whether a fingerprint of jointly correlated species could be associated with infantile colic, a cross‐validated sPLS model was built. This supervised model identified the microbial profile most associated with infantile colic and was trained on all bacterial species with a prevalence >5% of children and >0.01% mra. Comprising nine species, the final model was to a modest degree able to predict the infantile colic status of a child (median cross‐validated AUC: 0.627, p = .003). Here, the colic sPLS loadings were consistently positive across the cross‐validated repeats for three specific species belonging to 1) Family Enterococcaceae (unannotated at species‐level) (mra 0.49%, prevalence 56%, 2) Mediterraneibacter torques (mra 0.07%, prevalence 6% and 3) Listeria (mra 0.01%, prevalence 6%) (Figure 1B). When evaluating potential early life contributors to this cross‐validated colic bacterial score, only the breastfeeding variables (at 1 and 3 months) were associated (Figure S2).

Infantile colic in relation to later gastrointestinal symptoms

In the first 3 years of life, 17% (112/658) of the cohort children experienced constipation requiring laxative treatment while 6.4% (42/655) reported toddler's diarrhea. Infantile colic was associated with a higher risk of constipation the first 3 years of life (adjusted OR [aOR], 2.88 [1.51–5.35], p = .001), but not with the risk of toddler's diarrhea (Table 2). A higher risk of constipation existed in all 3 years but became especially pronounced in the third year of life where 19% of children with infantile colic developed constipation as compared to 5% of children without colic (aOR, 4.16 [1.76–9.19], p < .001). All models were adjusted for ethnicity, cat at birth, dog at birth, breastfeeding at age 3 months, and delivery mode.

Infantile colic in relation to later atopic disease

Infantile colic was associated with an increased risk of developing both asthma (adjusted HR [aHR], 1.69 [1.02–2.79], p = .040) and atopic dermatitis (aHR, 1.84 [1.20–2.81], p = .005) in the first 6 years of life (Figure 2A1,B1). Furthermore, when evaluating the disease development over time, infantile colic was also associated with an increased asthma prevalence (GEE aOR, 2.24 [1.25–4.01], p = .007) as well as atopic dermatitis prevalence (GEE aOR, 1.77 [1.08–2.92], p = .02). For asthma, this difference between children with and without colic increased over time and remained pronounced at 6 years, while the opposite was observed for atopic dermatitis, where the groups only diverged early but rejoined by age 5 years (Figure 2 A2,B2).

Infantile colic did not associate with allergic sensitization at age 6 months or with lung function measures or allergic rhinitis at 6 years. The risk of allergic sensitization endpoints at 6 years was increased, but not significant and showed a trend for food sensitization (Table 2).

Utilizing ISAC component resolved diagnostics on the 6 years samples, we explored the potential sensitization associations deeper. Children with colic had a higher number of positive allergen components than healthy children (adjusted difference, 116% [14%–280%], p = .012) (Table 2), which was also found, when only evaluating the association among the 110 children with at least one positive component (adjusted difference, 58% [1%–137%], p = .038) pointing towards polysensitization. Out of 112 measured allergen components, 61 were positive in at least one sample and only 27 components were positive in four or more samples (including cases in the colic group). Among these 27 allergen components, infantile colic was associated with higher risk of an FDR‐significant positive reaction in nine Ara h8 (peanut), Cor a 1.0101 (hazelnut), Cor a 1.0401 (hazelnut), Gly m4 (soybean), Mal d1 (apple), Pru p1 (peach), Aln g1 (alder), Art v1 (mugwort), and Can f5 (dog) (Table 3).

Infantile colic bacterial score and later clinical endpoints

The colic bacterial score at age 1 month was not in itself associated with later constipation, asthma, atopic dermatitis, or sensitization (all p > .5), and furthermore adjustment for the infantile colic bacterial score did not affect the clinical associations from colic. Additionally, performing mediation analyses on the clinical endpoints, with the colic bacterial score as potential mediator of colic, did not reveal any mediation (all p > .3). This indicates that the gut microbiome is an independent and unrelated marker of infantile colic, rather than being a mediator of later constipation or atopic disease risk.

DISCUSSION

In a prospective mother–child cohort followed closely throughout childhood with the main focus of precise longitudinal clinical phenotyping, we found that the 8% of children, who experienced infantile colic in the first 3 months of life, had an increased risk of developing constipation in the first 3 years of life, and also asthma, atopic dermatitis, and polysensitization in the first 6 years. The 1 month gut microbiome was only to a modest degree linked with the development of colic, but not associated with later risk of gastrointestinal or atopic disorders.

Strengths and limitations

The COPSAC2010 mother–child cohort including longitudinal collection of exposures, microbial samples, and clinical data with comprehensive follow‐up is the foundation for the study. 26 , 45 All asthma, atopic dermatitis, and allergy diagnoses were performed by COPSAC physicians, according to strict algorithms based on symptom diaries, clinical evaluation, and response to treatment to ensure uniform disease phenotypes. 26 In this deeply characterized cohort, we were both able to evaluate potential risk factors for the development of colic, and whether infantile colic predisposed to various conditions later in life, which is a major strength. We were furthermore able to describe specific temporal clinical disease trajectories associated with colic. Further strengthening the findings with the atopic phenotypes, we also showed a colic association with polysensitization at age 6 years, and described associations with specific allergen components. Finally, we demonstrated a temporally separated association between infantile colic and a later gastrointestinal manifestation, where many more children with colic developed constipation in the third year of life.

Our definitions of infantile colic and gastrointestinal disorders in infancy might be a bit outdated as compared to the newer Rome IV criteria, 46 which could perhaps have been more precise in distinguishing infantile colic from other causes of excessive crying/fussing. However, our definition was based on personal interviews and the interviewer made sure that the crying/fussing was not caused by acute illnesses, making the diagnosis uniform. Importantly, such definition nuances would not limit our conclusions toward associations with later clinical endpoints. Also, our analysis groups are relatively small for some endpoints given the low number of children with colic, which makes negative findings difficult to rule out. The 16S rRNA sequencing was performed in a single laboratory, which strengthens the microbiome description at 1 month. However, the method is limited in terms of taxonomic depth and often does not allow robust characterization below genus level. Furthermore, no functional profiling of the microbiome was done, which might have been more relevant for the development of colic as compared to only evaluating the taxonomic representation. While a microbial fingerprint existed for children developing infantile colic, this composition was not related to the later risk of gastrointestinal or atopic disorders.

All our findings were adjusted for environmental and social factors, which might contribute to the development of infantile colic and strengthens the findings, however other unmeasured risk factors could also contribute. Finally, the observational design of a cohort study like this precludes confirming causal relationships between the clinical features studied. It could be that infantile colic plays a causal role in the development of chronic constipation and childhood atopic diseases, or they may simply be shared expressions of a common latent clinical phenotype, even in the absence of unmeasured confounding. Even though our cohort was recruited to be an unselected population, the prevalence of asthma and atopic dermatitis may be higher than a background population, probably due to the close clinical follow‐up and higher rates among the parents. 26

Interpretation

While many studies have examined the nature of infantile colic in great detail, including crying patterns 5 and early risk factors for the condition, there is a lack of large studies with long‐term prospective follow‐up of a cohort of children with infantile colic information. Here, we find that children with infantile colic have almost a four times higher risk of developing constipation requiring medical treatment, especially in the third year of life. This points towards a long‐term gastrointestinal condition rather than a self‐limiting disorder resolving around age 3 months. A condition which could be caused by a more chronic gut dysmotility among children with colic. 10 This risk of later constipation is supported by a smaller study demonstrating a higher risk of developing functional gastrointestinal disorders at age 13 years among children with infantile colic. 22

In the COPSAC2010 children, we describe a high risk of developing both asthma, atopic dermatitis, and polysensitization in the first 6 years of life after infantile colic, but not early sensitization as previously suggested. 10 Infantile colic was in survival analyses associated with onset of both asthma and atopic dermatitis, however while the difference in prevalence between children with and without colic continued to increase for asthma to age 6 years, the association with atopic dermatitis differed only in the first years of life but disappeared again before school‐age. In a small Finnish prospective birth cohort 24 (n = 116), colic was associated with atopic disease in the first 2 years of life, but the cohort was too small to evaluate different disorders. In a larger American birth cohort 25 (n = 983), they did not find differences in either asthma, atopic dermatitis, or allergy risk according to infantile colic. The reasons for such differences could be many. In the American study, colic was evaluated by a physician at 2 months compared to our 3‐month interview, the populations were recruited almost 30 years apart, in different parts of the world, with different ethnic composition, with much higher breastfeeding rates in our cohort, and with different criteria for asthma and atopic dermatitis diagnoses. Children with colic were more likely to develop polysensitization at age 6 years. Many of the components they were sensitized to were food allergens that all are cross‐reactive with the birch component, Bet v 1, suggesting birch as a potential primary sensitizer. Other aeroallergen‐sensitizations included alder, mugwort, and dog, however, as we found no association between colic and allergic rhinitis, these sensitizations might not have clinical perturbations. The dog sensitization was to the prostate‐specific component Can f 5, suggesting the association only exists for sensitization to male dogs. 47 Interestingly, we found no associations between colic and sensitizations to suggest a primary food allergy, neither at 6 months nor 6 years of age.

Children with colic have been reported to be at higher risk of developing migraine 23 and functional gastrointestinal health outcomes. 22 The link between these symptoms and infantile colic could be through brain/behavioral traits, 9 however here we describe that children with colic had a higher risk of developing both constipation and asthma, atopic dermatitis, and sensitization. These are all disorders, which are not brain related. Our findings thereby support colic also having an atopic, immune‐mediated, or gut dysbiosis‐related etiology.

We observed various early life factors associated with the development of colic. Breastfeeding rates at age 1 and 3 months was higher in non‐colic children, but this could be due to reverse causation, where parents of children with colic might try out formula supplementation for symptom relief. Also, we found a peculiar interaction between the presence of pets in the home and risk of infantile colic. Having a cat increased the risk, especially if a dog was also present, while a dog alone was found protective. We cannot explain this, but it could perhaps be related to the family lifestyle and exposures.

In the gut microbiome at 1 month of age, we did not find overall diversity or compositional differences in relation to colic. A previous study (n = 65) similarly did not observe α‐diversity differences, but did find β‐diversity to differ among children with and without colic. 17 Doing analyses on genus and species level did not reveal specific taxa being differentially abundant according to colic after FDR adjustment. We were, however, able to construct a model of nine bacterial species jointly contributing to the colic risk. Here, species from Family Enterococcaceae, Mediterraneibacter torques and a Listeria species were positively associated across all repeats, whereas other taxa were only found in some repeats. Previous smaller studies have reported specific bacterial taxa to be associated with colic, however these results have not been consistent, and the high dimensionality of microbiome data can lead to spurious findings in small datasets. Lower abundances of Lactobacillus and higher Escherichia has been observed in children with colic by non‐sequencing techniques, 47 , 48 and increased Klebsiella in another study. 49 By 16S sequencing, lower Bifidobacteria, Lactobacilli, and Bacteroidetes has been observed together with higher Proteobacteria in children with infantile colic, 14 and in another study higher abundances of Acinetobacter and Lactobacillus iners. 17 None of these studies are consistent between themselves and not in line with our results. These discrepancies could suggest that specific taxa are not in themselves individual drivers of colic, but rather that the combined composition in a given child could affect dysbiosis and/or inflammation state leading to symptoms. Fecal calprotectin as a marker of gut inflammation has been found to be increased in children with colic, 17 , 50 which could point to a general dysbiosis state or heterogeneous microbiome responses to a latent host immunophenotype.

The infant gut microbiome has previously been associated with increased risk of developing asthma and allergy, 51 , 52 , 53 however in our study the colic associated microbiome did not associate with later disease risk, pointing to other routes by which the colic‐atopy association might be mediated.

Conclusions

In a well‐powered cohort followed extensively since pregnancy, we link infantile colic with the risk of developing constipation, asthma, atopic dermatitis, and allergic sensitization in the first 6 years of life. The 1‐month gut microbiome associated with colic but did not mediate the risk of later disease. Our results suggest colic to involve gastrointestinal and/or atopic/immune mechanisms rather than being a brain‐trait alone.
