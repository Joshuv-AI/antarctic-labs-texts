On the Bona Fide Sampling of Reaction Candidates in Red Moon Method by Replica‐Exchange Molecular Dynamics Method: REMD‐RM Method and Its Efficacy in Polymerization and Cross‐Linking Reactions of Polypropylene
Authors: Kentaro Matsumoto, Yuichi Tanaka, Seiryu Umetani, Takashi Nakano, Masataka Nagaoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIt remains challenging to microscopically simulate chemical reaction systems in which multiple chemical reactions proceed concurrently, thereby determining the overall time evolution of the system and the structure of the resulting products. For this purpose, the Red Moon (RM) method is a promising method that describes complex reaction systems by alternately using the molecular dynamics (MD) and Monte Carlo (MC) methods. However, the efficiency of the RM method strongly depends on how frequently the reactive configurations appear during the MD procedure, which can lead to inefficiencies in some systems where such configurations are rarely sampled. To overcome this limitation, we have proposed an improved version of the RM method, the REMD‐RM method, which incorporates the replica‐exchange molecular dynamics (REMD) method into the RM method, and applied it to two representative model systems: (i) The propylene polymerization reaction catalyzed by C 2‐symmetric ansa–zirconocene complex and (ii) the radical cross‐linking reaction of polypropylene. In addition to improving the efficiency of sampling reactive configurations, the REMD‐RM method successfully reproduced the stereoregularity of the resulting polymer in the former case, and the temperature dependence of cross‐linking reactions in the latter. Finally, we discussed the potential applicability of the REMD‐RM method and the possible extension of the RM method depending on the nature of the target system.

Introduction

With the continuous progress in computational chemistry and the growing power of computing resources, atomistic simulations have become indispensable and complementary tools to experiments for investigating chemical reaction phenomena. For example, conventional methods such as quantum mechanical (QM) and molecular dynamics (MD) methods have successfully provided microscopic insights into the structural and dynamical aspects of the chemical reactions that are often inaccessible experimentally. However, it remains challenging to directly simulate even a single chemical reaction process because bond formation and breaking typically occur on a time scale far longer than that of molecular motions. To overcome this difficulty, a variety of simulation techniques such as generalized ensemble [1], the method of milestoning [2], metadynamics [3], transition path sampling [4], and action‐based path sampling [5] have been proposed. Although these approaches have enabled efficient statistical sampling and the search for minimum‐energy paths for individual elementary chemical reactions, a major challenge remains in describing chemical reaction systems in which multiple chemical reactions proceed concurrently, thereby determining the overall time evolution of the system and the structure of the resulting products.

Under such circumstances, the Red Moon (RM) method [6, 7] has been proposed as one of the promising methods to simulate the chemical reaction processes as a sequence of elementary reactions. It is a computational molecular technology [8] developed for the atomistic simulation of complex chemical reaction systems by alternately using the MD and Monte Carlo (MC) methods. The molecular motions over a relatively short timescale are handled with the MD method, while the chemical reaction process involving the formation and breaking of chemical bonds over a relatively long timescale is handled stochastically with the MC method [6, 7]. Although the RM method can stochastically describe chemical reaction processes in principle by alternating MD and MC procedures, its efficiency strongly depends on how frequently the reaction candidates, that is, molecules in reactive configurations, appear during the MD procedure. For instance, in a system where conformational changes are slow or the diffusion of reactant is limited, the reaction candidates rarely emerge. Such systems require a large number of alternating MD and MC procedures, resulting in significant inefficiencies in RM simulations.

In this paper, we have therefore proposed a new methodology, the REMD‐RM method, in which the replica‐exchange molecular dynamics (REMD) method [9] is incorporated into the RM method. Then, we applied the REMD‐RM method to two model systems, that is (i) propylene polymerization reaction catalyzed by C 2‐symmetric ansa‐zirconocene complex and (ii) the radical cross‐linking reaction of polypropylene, and discussed how our new methodology improves the efficiency of reactant sampling. Finally, we discuss the potential applicability of the REMD‐RM method, and the possibility of extending the RM method by combining it with other types of generalized ensemble methods, depending on the nature of the target system.

The RM Method

In the RM method, a single cycle comprising MD and MC procedures is referred to as an “RM cycle” [6, 7]. By repeating the RM cycle, a series of complex chemical reactions can be simulated stochastically. The REMD‐RM Method

As discussed in the Section 1, in such a system where the reaction candidates rarely emerge, a large number of RM cycles are required to obtain a reaction candidate. To overcome this limitation, we incorporated the REMD method [9] into the RM method, which we refer to as REMD‐RM. In the REMD method, multiple replicas of the same system at different temperatures are simultaneously simulated, and the complete states of two replicas are randomly exchanged with the following probability, (4) Pi↔j=min1exp1kBTi−1kBTjUi−Uj, where Ti and Tj are the reference temperature of the replica i and j, Ui and Uj are their corresponding instantaneous potential energies. This exchange process allows the system to overcome high‐energy barriers, thereby enhancing the sampling efficiency. In the present study, the REMD procedure was incorporated into the MD part in the RM cycle, specifically, process 2(a) in Section 2.1, to facilitate the search for reaction candidates.

Model Reaction Systems

Propylene Polymerization Reaction Catalyzed by C 2 ‐Symmetric Ansa–Zirconocene Complex

The discovery of homogeneous catalysts, particularly the development of metallocene‐based organometallic complexes in the 1980s [10, 11], represents one of the significant breakthroughs in the history of polyolefin synthesis. These catalysts demonstrated that the microscopic features of polymers, such as tacticity, molecular weight, and comonomer incorporation, can be systematically controlled by the ligand architecture through synthetic methodologies in organometallic chemistry [12, 13, 14, 15]. For example, the relationships between the symmetry of the catalyst structure and the tacticity of the resulting polyolefins in ansa–zirconocene complexes have been extensively studied [14, 16]. In particular, the C 2‐symmetric ansa–zirconocene complex is known to produce isotactic polypropylene, reflecting the ligand's spatial arrangement around the active site. Thus, in this study, we focused on a representative C 2‐symmetric ansa–zirconocene complex SiMe2Ind2ZrMe+BMeC6F53−, as illustrated in Figure 1, and performed propylene polymerization simulation using the REMD‐RM method to examine how much the sampling of reaction candidates is enhanced.

Schematic representation of isotactic polypropylene polymerization by C 2‐symmetric ansa–zirconocene complex.

Polypropylene Cross‐Linking Reaction

Cross‐linking is a widely used strategy for producing polymeric materials with enhanced mechanical, thermal, and chemical properties. A representative example is cross‐linked polyolefins (XLPO), which are widely employed in industrial applications such as pipelines, storage tanks, and cable insulations [17]. Thus, in this study, we focused on the cross‐linking reaction of isotactic polypropylene initiated by dicumyl peroxide (DCP) (Figure 2) as an example and performed simulations using the REMD‐RM method to evaluate how the sampling efficiency of reaction candidates is improved.

Computational Details

All the MD simulations and REMD calculations were performed using the PMEMD program in AMBER 16 [18] under the periodic boundary condition. The REMD parameters, including the number of replicas and the temperatures for each replica, were determined in preliminary calculations. The RM simulation was performed by using an in‐house program developed in our group, RMsuiteV3.0. The quantum chemical (QC) calculations were performed by the Gaussian09 rev.D.02 program [19] regarding the isotactic polypropylene polymerization by C 2‐symmetric ansa–zirconocene complex and the Gaussian16 rev.B.01 program [20] regarding the cross‐linking reaction.

Propylene Polymerization Reaction

For the polypropylene polymerization reaction, we considered only the dominant 1,2‐insertion reaction, which leads to four distinct insertion manners depending on the monomer orientation and the anion location (Figure 3). Here, the “monomer orientation” refers to whether re or si face of the monomer is oriented toward the Zr atom, while the “anion location” refers to whether the anion is located on the same (cis) or opposite (trans) side of the catalyst relative to the monomer (Figure 3). In addition, the first insertion and subsequent insertions were treated separately because of the differences in the steric bulkiness around the active center. Accordingly, we considered eight distinct sets of reaction potential energies and activation free energies obtained from QC calculations (Table 1). All the QC calculations were performed using density functional theory (DFT) with the M06 functional, which is known to be appropriate for the transition metals [21]. Geometrical optimization and intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) calculations were carried out using LANL2DZ with the associated effective core potential and additional f orbitals on the Zr atom [22], and 6‐31G(d,p) on all other atoms. The reaction potential energies were further refined by single‐point calculations using the def2TZVPP basis set, while the activation free energies were evaluated at the same level of theory with the SMD solvation model [23] for toluene.

The general AMBER force field (GAFF) [24] version 1.4 was employed for monomeric propylene. The simulation model consisted of a single catalytic ion pair of SiMe2Ind2ZrMe+BMeC6F53− and 800 monomeric propylene molecules in a periodic boundary box with a volume of 44.9873 Å3. The REMD calculation for each reactant sampling, i.e., the process 2(a) described in Section 2.1, consists of 16 replicas with temperatures ranging from 343.15 to 450 K. The replica exchange was attempted 3500 times every 1 ps (for a total of 3.5 ns of MD trajectory), and the final 3.0 ns of the trajectory at 343.15 K was used to sample the reaction candidates. The RM calculations were started with 10 different initial configurations and were carried out for 60 RM cycles. In addition, we also performed RM simulation using the conventional MD procedure, which is exactly the same as that described in Section 2.1, under the same conditions. In this conventional RM method, a single trajectory at 343.15 K is used for sampling the reaction candidates. All other simulation parameters, including the number of initial configurations, the number of RM cycles, and the total MD simulation time of 3.5 ns for sampling reaction candidates, were identical to those employed in the REMD‐RM simulation.

Results and Discussion

Propylene Polymerization Reaction by C 2 ‐Symmetric Ansa–Zirconocene Complex

As described in Section 2.3.1, propylene polymerization reaction catalyzed by C 2‐symmetric ansa–zirconocene complex was selected as a model system to examine the effectiveness of the REMD‐RM method. In this system, it is well known that strong electrostatic and coordination interactions between the cation and the anion affect the catalytic activity. In fact, as shown in Figure 6, the counter anion stably occupies the active site of the cationic active species, thereby hindering the coordination and reaction of monomeric propylene. This is the commonly observed behavior in ion pair active species of olefin polymerization catalysts [33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. Such steric blocking by the anion significantly suppresses the appearance of reaction candidates during sampling by the conventional MD procedure, making it difficult for the conventional RM method to efficiently find reaction candidates. To evaluate how the REMD‐RM method improves this efficiency, we compared the results of the conventional RM method and the REMD‐RM method under the same conditions. Furthermore, we also examined whether the REMD‐RM method reproduces the formation of isotactic polypropylene by C 2‐symmetric ansa–zirconocene complex.

Improved Efficiency of Finding Reaction Candidates: Facilitation of Anion Dissociation and Propylene Coordination

Figure 7 shows the probability of occurrence of the three cases in each RM cycle. “No candidate” represents the case where no reaction candidate was found during the sampling process 2(a), whereas “Accepted” and “Rejected” represent the cases where some reaction candidates were found and the reactions were accepted or rejected, respectively, in the subsequent MC process 2(d). In the conventional RM method, no reactive configurations were observed in approximately 60% of RM cycles, reflecting steric blocking by the counter anion. In contrast, the REMD‐RM method markedly increased the frequency with which the reaction candidates appear, reducing the probability of “No candidate” to almost zero and increasing those of “Accepted” and “Rejected.” Such improvements can be attributed to the fact that the REMD method enables the system to overcome the high energy barriers for the counter anion dissociate from the active site on the cation, yielding the space for the monomer to coordinate. In fact, our previous research demonstrated that the REMD method can efficiently simulate the anion dissociation and monomer coordination process [35]. These results clearly show the efficacy of the REMD‐RM method for the system where the formation of reaction candidate is hindered by the slow structural changes of the catalytic species.

Reproducibility of Polypropylene Stereoregularity

Next, we examined whether the REMD‐RM method can reproduce the stereoregularity of polypropylene, namely, formation of isotactic polypropylene by C 2‐symmetric ansa–zirconocene complex. For this purpose, we calculated the meso‐pentad ratio mmmm, i.e., the fraction of the five successive chiral carbons with the same chirality along the propylene backbone. Table 3 shows the meso‐pentad ratio mmmm and the degree of polymerization in the 10 RM trajectories, together with the experimental value of mmmm [41]. The mean of the 10 RM trajectories gave mmmm of 76.6%, which is in very good agreement with the experimental value of 81.7% within the uncertainty. This result indicates that REMD‐RM method can successfully reproduce the stereoregularity of polypropylene produced by C 2‐symmetric ansa–zirconocene catalyst.

The origin of the stereochemical control in propylene polymerization reactions can be explained by the growing chain control mechanism, which was originally proposed by Corradini et al. for heterogenous Ziegler–Natta catalyst [42, 43, 44]. According to Corradini's model, polymer chain is forced into an open region of the metallocene due to the steric repulsion with the ligand, thereby transferring the chirality of the metallocene to the incoming monomer (Figure 8a). In fact, the reaction candidates that frequently appeared during the REMD‐RM simulation exhibit a sterically favorable arrangement, where the growing polymer chain occupied the open region of the catalyst, while the incoming propylene coordinated to the vacant site with its methyl group oriented opposite to the polymer chain (Figure 8b). This structural feature is consistent with the Corradini's model and successfully explains the reproduction of stereoregularity by the REMD‐RM simulation.

(a) Schematic representation of sterically favorable insertion manner. The red and blue quadrants represent the crowded and open regions of metallocene catalyst, respectively. (b) A snapshot of reactant structure that favorably appeared during REMD‐RM simulation.

In conclusion, the present results clearly show that the REMD‐RM method can not only accelerate the search for reaction candidates by overcoming steric blocking by the counter anion, but also accurately capture the essential stereochemical control of the resulting polymers reflecting the chiral structure of the metallocene complex. Such an ability to achieve both sampling efficiency and stereochemical fidelity within a unified framework opens the way to a quantitative, atomistic understanding of stereospecific polymerization reactions.

Cross‐Linking Reaction of Polypropylene by Dicumyl Peroxide

As outlined in Section 2.4.2, the cross‐linking of polypropylene involves both radical coupling (Figure 4d) and decomposition through β‐scission reactions (Figure 4b) after radicals are generated. Therefore, whether cross‐linking proceeds efficiently depends on whether the radical coupling reaction occurs faster than the β‐scission reaction. The β‐scission reaction is an endothermic process that breaks a carbon–carbon σ bond and forms a π bond, requiring energy corresponding to the difference between the σ and π bond energies. As a result, it is a slow reaction that occurs when sufficient heat is supplied by the outer region of the reacting atomic group involved. In contrast, the radical coupling reaction proceeds very rapidly without an activation barrier, provided that the structures near the radical sites of both reactants are not sterically hindered. Thus, under the conditions where a certain concentration of radicals is present, and heating is insufficient for β‐scission to occur, radical coupling can efficiently take place as long as the mobility of the polymer chains is not severely restricted.

A major challenge in RM simulations is the relaxation process of the polymer aggregation structure. Even at temperatures well above the glass transition point, the mobility of each polymer chain is restricted by mutual steric repulsion, resulting in a low encounter frequency between radical sites. Therefore, only when the encounter frequency is properly accounted for relative to the slow β‐scission reaction, cross‐linking simulations can be performed accurately. In the meantime, it is known that conventional RM methods sometimes underestimate the encounter frequency between radical sites, making it difficult to efficiently identify reaction candidates. Thus, as in the case discussed in Section 3.1, we evaluated how efficiently reaction candidates could be identified for cross‐linking reactions appropriately represented by adopting the REMD‐RM method. Additionally, we examined the temperature dependence of the cross‐linking reaction.

Improved Efficiency in Sampling Reaction Candidates: Adoption Ratio of Cross‐Linking Reactions

It is known that isotactic polypropylene undergoes cross‐linking in the temperature range of 423.15–473.15 K when DCP is used as the initiator [32]. In comparison, the number of accepted β‐scission reactions by the conventional RM method is 534, indicating that decomposition reactions occur more frequently than cross‐linking reactions, and cross‐linking does not proceed sufficiently. On the other hand, when using the REMD‐RM method, the number of accepted β‐scission reactions decreases significantly to 371, and the number of accepted cross‐linking reactions exceeds that of decomposition reactions, indicating that cross‐linking proceeds sufficiently. These results show that the REMD‐RM method enables appropriate representation of cross‐linking reactions in simulations.

Temperature Dependence of Cross‐Linking Reactions

Experimentally, in isotactic polypropylene cross‐linked with DCP, the gel fraction decreases above 423.15 K (Table 5) [32]. This is likely due to more frequent β‐scission reactions than radical coupling at higher temperatures. Therefore, we investigated how the degree of cross‐linking differs at 423.15 and 453.15 K using the REMD‐RM method. At the final RM cycle, after all radicals had disappeared, the gel fraction was estimated by excluding low‐molecular‐weight fragments (those with molecular weights below that of a propylene 20‐mer, which is the initial polymer chain prepared) from the total. As a result, the weight fraction was 45.4% at 423.15 K and 25.7% at 453.15 K, demonstrating that the degree of cross‐linking decreases with increasing temperature. In fact, the total number of accepted reactions for 10 samples in the REMD‐RM simulations (Table 6) demonstrates that the frequency of β‐scission reactions increases relative to radical coupling reactions as the temperature rises.

Two Origins of Inefficiency in Reaction Candidate Sampling and the Effectiveness of the REMD‐RM Method

As stated in Section 2.2, a large number of RM cycles are required when the reaction candidates rarely emerge during the sampling procedure, i.e., process 2(a), which motivated us to introduce the REMD method into the RM framework. Such an inefficiency in the reaction candidate sampling can arise from two distinct origins: (i) The slow structural change of the molecules, and (ii) the limitation in the diffusion of reactant molecules. In the first case, the reactant molecules reside in configurations separated from reactive ones by an energetically large enough barrier, even though this might not involve chemical bond recombination. As a result, structural changes toward the reactive configurations become difficult to occur. In the present study, the propylene polymerization reaction catalyzed by the C 2‐symmetric ansa‐metallocene catalyst serves as an example of this case, as discussed in Section 3.1. In the second case, the translational motion of the molecules is severely restricted by factors such as large molecular size and spatial confinement, resulting in slow diffusion of the reactant molecules and infrequent molecular encounters. Since it is well known that the molecular motion of polymer chains is highly restricted, the radical cross‐linking reaction examined in the present study is a typical example of this latter.

As demonstrated in Sections 3.1 and 3.2, the REMD‐RM method works well for both propylene polymerization reaction and the radical cross‐linking reaction. This result indicates that the REMD‐RM method can successfully address both types of inefficiencies by enhancing configuration rearrangement over energetic barriers and molecular diffusion. These results indicate that the REMD‐RM method can provide a general and versatile approach for efficiently simulating such reaction processes in complex molecular systems.

Concluding Remarks and Future Perspectives

In the present article, we proposed a new framework of RM method for the system where the reaction candidates rarely emerge. Specifically, we incorporated REMD method into the MD part of the RM method to enhance the sampling of reaction candidates. The resulting REMD‐RM method successfully improved the efficiency of reaction candidate sampling in two representative systems suffering from two distinct types of inefficiencies: (i) The propylene polymerization reaction catalyzed by C 2‐symmetry ansa–zirconocene complex, where reaction candidates rarely emerge due to the slow structural changes of the ion‐pair active species. (ii) The radical cross‐linking reaction of polypropylene, where reactant molecules are infrequently encountered due to the limitation of molecular diffusion.

For the former case, we succeeded in reproducing the meso‐pentad ratio [mmmm] is 76.6%, in very good agreement with the experimental value of 81.7%. On the other hand, for the latter case, we observed a clear tendency that β‐scission reactions are relatively more difficult to occur than cross‐linking reactions, indicating that cross‐linking proceeds sufficiently. Furthermore, the temperature dependence of cross‐linking reactions was also reproduced, with gel fractions of 45.4% at 423.15 K and 25.7% at 453.15 K, demonstrating that the degree of cross‐linking decreases with increasing temperature. These results demonstrated that the REMD‐RM method effectively accelerates the search for reaction candidates by enhancing molecular reconfiguration and diffusion in the reactant system.

The effectiveness of the REMD‐RM method demonstrated in this study suggests its potential applicability to various types of complex chemical reaction systems exhibiting similar inefficiencies in reaction candidate sampling. For example, enzymatic reactions and signal transductions in biological systems involve structural changes in the responsible proteins and accompanying chemical reactions [45], which exhibit the same type of sampling inefficiencies as case (i). In contrast, chemical reactions occurring in porous media, such as metal–organic frameworks (MOF) or porous coordination polymer (PCP), are expected to suffer from the same sampling inefficiencies as case (ii) due to the slow molecular diffusion relative to the MD time scale [46].

It is also noteworthy that the present study has indicated the possibility of extending the RM method by combining it with other types of generalized ensemble methods, depending on the nature of the target system. For instance, the application of the REMD method to lipid bilayers is rare because a large number of replicas are required [47]. In such cases, Huang and García proposed that replica exchange with solute tempering (REST), a specific variant of a generalized Hamiltonian replica exchange method, is an efficient algorithm to accelerate lateral equilibration in lipid bilayers [47]. Such extensions are expected to lead to system‐specific variants of the RM method and to broaden its applicability to complex reaction systems.

In conclusion, our study has demonstrated the effectiveness of REMD‐RM method for enhancing reaction candidate sampling, and has indicated the potential for system‐specific extensions of the RM method. Such developments of RM method will contribute to the quantitative understanding of diverse chemical reaction processes from atomistic point of view.
