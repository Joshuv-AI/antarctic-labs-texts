Fluorescence Lifetime Multiplexing (FLEX) for simultaneous high dimensional spatial biology in 3D
Authors: Wonsang Hwang, Tucker Raymond, Tyler McPartland, Sinyoung Jeong, Conor L Evans
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunohistochemistry is a crucial method for detecting specific proteins within tissue samples, yet constrained to one biomarker per tissue section. Multiplexed immunofluorescence, while allowing simultaneous visualization of multiple proteins, faces limitations in the number of simultaneous fluorescent labels due to spectral overlap. Although cyclic immunofluorescence techniques have successfully broadened antibody staining capacities in a single tissue sample, they are plagued by time-consuming and labor-intensive procedures, sample degradation risks, and inability to scale beyond thin sections. In this study, we introduce the use of 3D confocal Fluorescence Lifetime Imaging Microscopy as a high-throughput, multiplexed immunofluorescence platform that can differentiate 11 or more biomarkers in 3D tissue volumes. Leveraging both spectral and lifetime information, this approach allows for practical spatial biology in thin sections that can readily scale to larger volumes of tissue. We believe that this highly multiplexed and versatile biomarker imaging platform will significantly expedite cancer research and enable new translational approaches in the future.Subject terms: Fluorescence imaging, Confocal microscopy

Introduction

By revealing the precise locations and interactions of tissue components, Spatial Biology offers insights into the mechanisms of development, disease, and physiological processes that are challenging to fully appreciate via traditional molecular biology techniques. This knowledge is instrumental in advancing personalized medicine, drug discovery, and therapeutic interventions, enhancing our ability to diagnose, treat, and prevent a wide range of complex diseases, from cancer to neurodegenerative disorders1,2. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) has been the gold standard to detect the presence of specific proteins and spatially characterize molecular expression in tissue sections in both clinical practice and research3. Although IHC makes use of antibodies to visualize spatial distributions of individual biomarkers for research and diagnostic needs, standard IHC throughput is limited to only one biomarker per tissue section4. While directly adjacent tissue sections can be used to assess for multiple biotargets, spatial molecular information can be missed due to heterogeneity. The use of serial sections or microarray processing only exacerbates this problem, as different regions of tissue are probed for different biomarkers, leading to information that can be both incomplete and inaccurate5,6. Though flow cytometry technologies such as CyTOF offer multiplexed analysis7, they require tissue homogenization that throws away spatial information that can be critical for understanding tissue structure.

Multiplexed immunofluorescence methods were developed to overcome this throughput limit8. These techniques enable the simultaneous visualization of multiple proteins or markers within the same tissue sample and are often used to identify the presence and localization of multiple biomarkers simultaneously. Immunofluorescence methods can typically identify between four and six biotargets, with the number of simultaneous targets capped by the wide emission spectra of organic dyes9. To overcome this conventional ”wavelength multiplexing” limit, cyclic immunofluorescence techniques have been developed that include staining, imaging, and bleaching/washing cycles for three or four biomarkers per cycle10–14. Although the cyclic approach can dramatically increase the number of simultaneous immunofluorescence labels, this process can be labor-intensive, time-consuming, and suffer from tissue integrity challenges.

Several simultaneous imaging methods have been proposed to improve the multiplexing of biomarkers. Hyperspectral techniques have been demonstrated to offer superior multiplexing capabilities compared to conventional spectral bandpass multiplexing. One approach utilizes narrow spectral bandwidths (10 nm) to achieve higher spectral selectivity, leading to an increase in the number of multiplexing channels up to 1515. Another method utilizes a tunable white-light laser to sweep excitation, allowing multiplexing of dyes based on their absorption spectra16. This excitation sweep-based approach has expanded the multiplexing channel up to 10. However, these methods require imaging rounds equivalent to the number of multiplexing channels. The increased rounds of excitation expose the sample to more irradiation, potentially leading to the loss of dyes susceptible to photobleaching.

Raman-based approaches show early promise17,18, but these approaches require the preparation of Raman-labeled antibodies as well as complex microscopy systems. Although multiplexed ion beam imaging (MIBI) can analyze samples stained with more than 100 metal labeled antibodies19, this technique necessitates special tissue preparation methods for vacuum usage, the application of metal-chelated antibodies, and the use of expensive and specialized ion beam imaging equipment.

Perhaps equally challenging, these spatial biology approaches have been focused on, and optimized for, thin section samples and in many cases do not scale for 3D tumor specimen profiling. Diseased tissues, such as cancer, are often poorly organized with 3D assessment necessary to adequately map disease state and heterogeneity20–22. Efforts to extend cyclic immunofluorescence for 3D tumor profiling have required dozens of staining cycles and hundreds of imaging sessions to reconstruct single specimens23, presenting barriers for 3D biomarker analysis in both research and clinical diagnostic settings.

Here, we introduce a rapid and sensitive multiplex immunofluorescence biomarker imaging platform called Fluorescence Lifetime imaging microscopy multiplEXing (FLEX). FLEX allows for rapid, non-destructive, and simultaneous imaging of multiple biotargets in a single cycle for 3D profiling of tissues using off-the-shelf fluorophores by leveraging fluorescence lifetime as an additional, independent source of contrast. Multiplexing is achieved by employing fluorophores distributed across a range of both spectral emission wavelengths and lifetimes, with confocal optics allowing for three-dimensional imaging within specimens. This allows for lifetime multiplexing of otherwise spectrally-indistinguishable, commercially-available fluorophores on modified confocal microscopes. Rapid, 3D tissue imaging is achieved using the Direct-Measurement lifetime method24 in conjunction with real-time computing25 for the acquisition of 11-plex or higher images from intact specimens in a single staining and imaging step. We anticipate that this highly multiplexed biomarker imaging platform will accelerate basic cancer research, and enable new translational approaches in the future.

Results

Highly multiplexed imaging via a spectrum-lifetime unmixing process

To build the multiplexed staining palette, We selected 11 distinct fluorescent labels with nonoverlapping characteristic in both wavelength and fluorescence lifetime domains (Supplementary Table 1). Purchased fluorophore-conjugated antibodies were supplemented by in-house fluorescent dye conjugation to specific antibodies to build out fluorescent labels for 11 targets. The protein targets were chosen for their importance in cancer immunology research; the roles of the protein targets in cancer research can be found in Supplementary Table 2. For practical multiplexing, up to three fluorophores with similar excitation and emission spectra but uniquely different fluorescence lifetimes were multiplexed within a single color ”channel”. Using this palette, tissue samples could be simultaneously stained in a single staining step. Stained tissues were imaged using nano and picosecond pulsed lasers at four different wavelengths (405, 450, 520, and 640 nm), creating four distinct spectral color channels. (Fig. 1a).

For rapid and practical imaging across both fluorescence spectra and lifetimes, the temporal fluorescence response from the sample was captured a custom confocal microscopy system featuring photomulitplier tubes and high-speed digitizers (Fig. 1b), and processed using phasor analysis. Details about the fluorescence lifetime setup can be found in Supplementary Fig. 1. In phasor analysis, the lifetime distribution measured at each pixel is mathematically processed into a pair of phasor coordinates (G and S) via Fourier transform-like functions. These G and S coordinates can be plotted to generate a phasor plot where the lifetime properties of a fluorophore cluster into distributions whose position, shape, and size encode features of a molecule’s distribution of exponential fluorescence lifetime decays26. The digitized fluorescent pulse signal from each pixel underwent real-time Fourier transformation via GPU computation into phasor space27. Because of the inherent properties of exponential functions, the Fourier-transformed signal follows a counterclockwise trajectory along a semicircular path with a fixed radius of 0.5. As the three different fluorescent molecules excited by a single laser exhibit distinct fluorescence lifetimes, each dye which has mono-exponential decay property localizes to distinct position along the semicircular path and forms a single cluster.

These individual positions are referred to as ’phasor clusters’, and consequently, each fluorophore-antibody conjugate is associated with a specific phasor cluster. When multiple antibodies co-localize within a single pixel, the shifted phasor serves as a representation of their combined proportions, illustrating their colocalization (Fig. 1c).

The inherent linearity exhibited by the phasor plot facilitates the straightforward decomposition of multiple fluorescence components from within a single pixel. Specifically, in scenarios where there are three distinct fluorescent lifetime contributions (A, B, and C), the phasor point(P) of a pixel which are linear combination of the three different fluorophores locates within a geometric triangle defined by the vertices corresponding to the phasor clusters of the individual fluorophore components (A, B, C). As the phasor position has a linear relationship with the weight allocation attributed to each constituent component, a decomposition approach allows for the calculation of the individual fluorophore contributions within each pixel.

In this study, a double-line decomposition method28,29 was employed (Fig. 1c). In the first step, the decomposition involves the line connecting the phasor of component B (point B) and the phasor of the triexponential decay (point P). Along this first line, the decomposition leads to fractions representing both component B and the combined mixture of components A and C. When projecting the point onto the line connecting point A and point C as point I, the fraction pertaining to component B can be computed as IP¯IB¯ and the fraction corresponding to the combined mixture of components A and C can be calculated as 1 − fB. Subsequently, the second line subjected to decomposition is the line connecting point A and point C. Here, the fraction attributed to component A is determined by IC¯AC¯×fAC, while the fraction associated with component C is calculated as IA¯AC¯×fAC. Using this approach, each pixel’s contribution can be linearly and quantitatively decomposed into its three fluorophore components.

To validate this quantification approach, we experimentally investigated three solutions of different fluorophores that had distinct fluorescence lifetimes but similar emission spectra. The fluorescence characteristics of these solutions were evaluated using the confocal FLEX system, employing the 640 nm excitation laser with operated at 25 MHz repetition rate with a pixel dwell time of 10 μs. As illustrated in Fig. 1d, both the fluorescence intensity (top row) and lifetime (bottom row) images were acquired for the three distinct fluorescence solutions: Alexa647, Atto647, and Attorho14. On average, approximately 1000 fluorescence photons were collected per pixel. Phasor analysis of the acquired images is shown in Fig. 1e, showing three unique and well-separated phasor clusters corresponding to each fluorophore. Using the decomposition algorithm to separate out each individual fluorophore contribution allows for clean separation of each fluorophore into individual contribution maps (Fig. 1f), demonstrating the capability of the FLEX technique.

Effective autofluorescence reduction via an LED array bleaching method

Like any fluorophore-based imaging technique, tissue autofluorescence can interfere with FLEX imaging due to its broad spectral and lifetime emission properties. To effectively eliminate autofluorescence, a bleaching method using a high power LED array was developed to pre-treat Formalin-Fixed Paraffin-Embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks prior to antibody staining. The LED array consisted of 390, 430, 460, 630, 660, and 850 nm wavelengths with a total power of 864 Watts. Light exposure was coupled with immersion in an H2O2 solution to expedite the bleaching process. To validate this method, 5 μm thin sections of FFPE tonsil tissue were prepared, deparaffinized, and exposed to a 90 min duration illumination session. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, the autofluorescence intensity profiles were acquired via excitation at four discrete wavelengths (405, 450, 520, and 640 nm), both prior to (left column) and following to (right column) bleaching treatment. Quantitative results detailing the reduction of autofluorescence across tissue specimens are presented in Fig. 2b. Notably, the 450 and 520 nm excited channels exhibited pronounced autofluorescence signals prior to the autofluorescence reduction protocol. Upon the exposure of the tissue to LED illumination, a reduction in autofluorescence across all channels was found, with significant reduction observed for the 450 and 520 nm channels.

To demonstrate the capabilities of this autofluorescence suppression method, Fig. 2c shows FLEX images of tissues stained with Alexa555-CD3, both before (upper) and after (lower) to autofluorescence bleaching. Prior to bleaching, image data is dominated by autofluorescence: the average autofluorescence lifetime of unbleached tonsil tissue was 2.5 ns, considerably greater than the 1.4 ns of Alexa555-CD3, implying that autofluorescence overwhelmingly contributed to the signal prior to the application of the autofluorescence reduction protocol. With autofluorescence suppressed the fluorescence lifetime map of Alexa555-CD3 revealed the distinct immunostaining pattern of CD3, matching results obtained from immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2). The inset in Fig. 2c exhibits the phasor plot for each image. The upper inset clearly shows the large phasor contribution of autofluorescence which is effectively eliminated in the bleached samples (lower inset).

Validating FLEX imaging performance

To validate both the staining protocol and the FLEX imaging method, we first carried out staining with single fluorophore-antibody conjugates. Alternatively sliced FFPE tonsil sections 5 μm thick were stained for each of the individual 11 fluorophore antibody conjugates. We employed the following fluorophores and conjugates in each spectral channel: DAPI, Phalloidin-Atto390, and CD20-CF405 for the 405 nm channel; PanCK-Seta470 and CD45-CF405 for the 450 nm channel; CD3-AF555, ECAD-PE, and CD19-Alexa546 for the 520 nm channel; and CD4-Alexa647, PanCK-Atto647, and Ki67-Attorho14 for the 640 nm channel. PanCK was intentionally included twice in the panel as it is an abundant marker that can be used as a quality control metric allowing for an internal FLEX validation. The phasor histogram of individual 11 fluorophore antibody conjugates in PBS solution can be found in Supplementary Fig. 3.

The intensity-weighted fluorescence lifetime images of single-stained tissue specimens are depicted in Fig. 3a. The distribution of fluorescence lifetimes corresponding to each image is presented in Fig. 3b. The phasor histogram corresponding to each image can be found in Supplementary Fig. 4. These results show that FLEX possesses sufficient resolving power to distinguish between the individual fluorophores without crosstalk under routine imaging conditions. To check for potential spectral crosstalk, staining of tissues was carried out in pairs where tissues were stained with fluorophores from neighboring spectral channels. Spectral crosstalk was found to be minimal across channels, with values less than 5% when fluorescent intensities were similar. So that any residual cross talk is removed, an algorithm was developed and applied, the details of which can be found in Supplementary Fig. 5.

Note that the antibodies depicted in Fig. 3 represent just a subset of the available options for FLEX. This method is applicable to commercial antibodies, as long as the chosen antibody-fluorophore selection exhibits distinct phasor characteristics. However, it is important to emphasize that the efficiency of multiplexing depends primarily on the choice of antibody-fluorophore combinations, rather than the system itself. Establishing a comprehensive framework for consistent multiplexing quality can offer valuable insights into antibody-fluorophore selection.

To establish a framework for quantifying FLEX lifetime resolution, we introduce the concept of the FLEX quality factor, denoted as Q = D/σ (Fig. 4a). In this context, D represents the phasor distance between the centroids of two distinct clusters, while σ denotes the standard deviation associated with these clusters. The standard deviation of the clusters is influenced by both the pulse width of the laser as well as the number of photons per pixel. The quality factor is graphically represented in Fig. 4b as a function of the lifetime difference and the number of photons per pixel. We conducted a quantitative study on lifetime crosstalk as a function of the quality factor using Monte Carlo simulations, as depicted in Fig. 4c. The red dot signifies the mean value derived from the Monte Carlo simulation results, while the error bar represents the standard deviation of these results. Note that 1000 Monte Carlo trials were performed to ensure statistical robustness, and the geometrical concept of crosstalk in the phasor domain is depicted in Fig. 4a. This result gives us a quantitative sense of the FLEX method’s unmixing capabilities. For example, a quality factor at a level over 50 results in crosstalk of approximately 2% or less.

Next, the potential for antibody-specific perturbations on fluorescence lifetime was investigated. We conducted experiments using seven PE solutions, each conjugated with distinct antibodies (CD324, CD3, CD45, CD68, CD235, CD33, and CD15). The analysis revealed that the standard deviation of the phasor clusters for the seven PE-antibody was just 0.011, which translates to approximately 0.08 ns when converted to fluorescence lifetime, representing a minimal error of only 3%. This implies that the influence of antibody dependence on measured fluorescence lifetime is negligible. In addition, we also investigated antibody dependence on fluorescence lifetime in stained tissues. Figure 4f illustrates fluorescence lifetime images obtained after conjugating three distinct fluorophores with two different antibodies for tissue staining. It is apparent that there was only a negligible alteration in fluorescence lifetime when the antibody was changed. Figure 4e presents the mean and standard deviation of fluorescence lifetimes corresponding to the images in Fig. 4f, verifying that the antibody of each multiplexed channel can be replaced with negligible lifetime shift.

11-plex, single-step immunofluorescence imaging with FLEX

To demonstrate the multiplexing capabilities of FLEX, we carried out 11-plex staining of 5 μm FFPE tonsil sections across four distinct color channels (Fig. 5). Up to three antibodies were assigned to each color channel corresponding to different laser excitation wavelengths. To capture a comprehensive field of view across tissue sections, the imaging system captured individual high-resolution images that were stitched together to create a mosaic spanning 6 × 6 mm2 (6072 × 7224 pixels). The total acquisition time was approximately 30 min. To illustrate FLEX results from a single spectral channel, Fig. 5a shows a confocal fluorescence intensity image on one tissue section excited at 640 nm. The corresponding phasor histogram is depicted in Fig. 5b, with the red, green, and blue dots symbolizing reference clusters for AF647-CD4, ATTO647-PanCK, and ATTOrho14-Ki67, respectively. The top three images in Fig. 5c portray the multiplexed intensity maps obtained through FLEX unmixing, showing the contributions of CD4, PanCK, and Ki67 from left to right. The patterns of the unmixed individual biomarker images from the triplex immunofluorescence closely mirror the corresponding immunohistochemistry pattern (Fig. 5c bottom), highlighting the successful separation of the three antibodies within a single wavelength channel in the phasor domain.

3D spatial biology at 11-plex with FLEX

As FLEX is based on confocal principles, a key advantage is that it can be extended to highly multiplexed immunofluorescence imaging of 3D tissue volumes. To illustrate the feasibility of FLEX as a robust 3D immunofluorescence profiling technique, FFPE tonsil blocks were sectioned into 40 μm thick sections and stained with the same palette of 11 fluorophore-antibody conjugates using the developed staining protocol. The pinhole was adjusted to set the average axial resolution across all spectral channels to 2 μm, more than double that of standard histological preparation. A large tonsil tissue section was mosaic imaged in 3D, with a lateral field of view encompassing an impressive 6.5 × 4 mm area; z stacks were acquired comprising 20 slides at 2 μm intervals. The process of acquiring data across all four distinct wavelengths took approximately 8 h, demonstrating the ability to efficiently generate a multiplex 3D imaging dataset within a comparatively short timeline. Figure 7 presents the 11-plex 3D image of the thick FFPE tonsil slide. The xy, xz, and yz cross-section images are shown in Fig. 7a, with the xz and yz sections indicated by the yellow dotted lines. Discussion

The 3D immunofluorescence profiling achieved in this study is particularly important as it allows for a more accurate and comprehensive analysis of the spatial distribution of different cell types and molecular markers within tissues. This approach is specifically valuable in understanding the complex interactions between cells in different tissue types and how such interactions may be disrupted in disease. The ability to achieve highly multiplexed 3D imaging rapidly with the FLEX system represents a significant advance in the field of Spatial Biology, with the ability reshape our understanding of complex biological processes within tissues. This study demonstrates the potential of FLEX as a powerful tool for both high-throughput and high-resolution multiplex immunofluorescence imaging, carrying broad implications for a diverse array of biomedical research applications.

It is worth to emphasize that FLEX is a technology with abundant potential for further improvement. The ability to separate three antibodies per single wavelength, as demonstrated in this paper, is not a limitation inherent to FLEX technology. From a systems viewpoint, the FLEX system directly records the distribution of fluorescent signals, and the stored data contains multi-harmonic components. Of these, two harmonics have been processed in real-time through GPU-based computation. Considering that, in theory, up to five unique cluster separations are possible with two harmonic components in a single wavelength channel, the FLEX system is already capable of achieving considerably greater levels of multiplexing. In fact, until now, the bottleneck has been the number of dyes available. However, synthetic probes such as halo-tag32 and snap-tag33 have been introduced, affording precise control over fluorescence lifetime. This innovative development is anticipated to significantly expand available fluorescent lifetime dyes, thus potentially leading to a substantial leap forward in this fluorescence lifetime-based multiplexing technology.

Another important point to discuss is the photobleaching effect during z-stacking. We found that the fluorophores assigned to the 520 nm channel are relatively susceptible to photobleaching. The tonsil tissues stained individually with CD3-Alexa555, ECAD-PE, and CD19-Alexa546 exhibited an average decrease of approximately 13% in fluorescence intensity after exposure to 200 μW laser light at the focal plane for a minute. In imaging, fluorescence molecules in the out-of-focus planes are exposed to excitation light while acquiring multiple z-stacks. Considering that acquiring 20 z-stacks requires approximately one minute, the final z-stack is exposed to excitation light for one minute, with the majority of the planes out of focus during this time. As the fluorescence molecules in the out-of-focus plane are exposed to a lower photon flux than those in the focal plane, the intensity reduction due to photobleaching for the 20 z-stacks would be less than the measured 13%. It is worth noting that in larger samples that require a greater number of axial optical sections, the photobleaching effect would increase; this could be a limitation for confocal microscope based FLEX systems. Integrating FLEX with light sheet microscopy or two-photon microscopy could potentially overcome the limitations imposed by photobleaching in thicker samples.

For many cancer research and diagnostic applications molecular profiling would target 5–6 tumor biomarkers along with 5–6 immune system biomarkers in addition to nuclear and cell-surface staining, making 10–15 plex a key goal for practical Spatial Biology. The 11plex achieved here is already sufficient for many needs in research and clinical practices, in particular for immunooncology and immune profiling research. This platform is also applicable to a wide range of samples, including fragile samples such as fine needle aspirates that do not readily survive multiple IF cycles. Importantly, FLEX enables simultaneous multiplexed image acquisition, such that 3D specimen profiling imaging can be accomplished in a day instead of the current scale of days, weeks, or even months, as is the case with cyclic IF methods. As the high multiplexing is not solely based on spectral separation, this approach also has the ability to be more sensitive towards low abundance markers than existing hyperspectral techniques.

Methods

FLIM system

We utilized a custom-developed FLIM (Fluorescence Lifetime Imaging Microscopy) system specifically designed for our FLEX application (Intek Medi, iRT-FLIM-001, Supplementary Fig. 1). This system incorporates various laser sources, including a nanosecond laser operating at 405 nm (Thorlabs, NPL41B) and picosecond lasers with wavelengths of 450, 520, and 640 nm (PicoQuant Prima). Supplementary Fig. 6 provides additional details regarding the impact of varying pulsewidths on system performance.

The laser repetition rates were set at 10 MHz for the 405 nm laser and 25 MHz for the 450, 520, and 640 nm lasers. The collimated pulse laser beam was reflected by a dichroic mirror following transmission through a 99:1 beam splitter (99% transmission). Subsequently, the beam passed through a non-resonant x-y scanner (Thorlabs,model: GVS002), a scan lens, tube lens, mirror, and objective lens. We employed a 20x objective lens with a numerical aperture (NA) of 0.75 for both excitation and signal collection (Olympus, Model: UPlanSApo 20x).

The fluorescence signal from the sample was transmitted through the dichroic mirror and further filtered by a bandpass filter within a chromatic filter wheel. The filters have distinct spectral ranges: 415–450 nm for 405 nm excitation (Semrock, FF01-450/70-25), 510–550 nm for 450 nm excitation (Semrock, FF01-535/50-25), 550–610 nm for 520 nm excitation (Semrock, FF01-582/64-25), and 660–700 nm for 640 nm excitation (Semrock, FF01-679/41-25). Subsequently, the filtered fluorescence signal is focused by a lens and passed through a pinhole.

Subsequently, the fluorescence signal was converted to electric pulses by a photomultiplier tube (PMT, Hamamatsu, H10720-01). The electric pulse signal from the PMT was transmitted through an electric lowpass filter (Minicircuits, SLP250) for anti-aliasing and amplified. The amplified signal was connected to channel 1 of a digitizer with a sampling frequency of 1 GB/s (Intek-plus) and converted to a digitized signal. The digitized signal was processed in real time to calculate g and s values using cosine and sine transforms, utilizing parallel computing with NVIDIA’s CUDA (Compute Unified Device Architecture) technology.

Simultaneously, the impulse response function was measured along with the fluorescence signal. The reflected beam (1% reflection) at the 99:1 beam splitter was directly acquired by the PMT and then passed through an electric lowpass filter and amplifier, similar to the fluorescence signal processing. The impulse response function was utilized to compensate for any system jitter34. the results of the measurement precision experiment are presented in Supplementary Fig. 7.

Data analysis

For the analysis of acquired data, a commercial FLIM software package (Intek Medi) was employed for the initial phasor analysis. This software conducts real-time processing by implementing fast Fourier transforms for dual modulation frequencies at 50 MHz and 100 MHz. Subsequent data processing involved the utilization of custom Python code for the calculation of triplex parameters. The visualization of acquired data in three dimensions (3D rendering) was accomplished using the Volume Viewer35 plug-in within the ImageJ software platform.

Bleaching solution

To expedite the bleaching process, a bleaching solution36 was employed. This solution was prepared by combining 25 ml of 1 × PBS with 4.5 ml of 30% (wt/vol) H2O2 and 0.8 mL of 1 M NaOH, resulting in a final bleaching solution containing 4.5% (wt/vol) H2O2 and 20 mM NaOH in PBS.

Pre-bleaching process with an LED array

The bleaching solution was introduced into a petri dish, and then, tissue slides were immersed in the bleaching solution. The slides were subjected to an illumination process using 864 Watt full-spectrum grow light source emitting six different wavelengths (390, 430, 460, 630, 660, and 850 nm) for a duration of 90 minutes.

Antibody staining

Slides with paraffin tissue sections were baked in 60 ∘C oven for one hour for melting the wax. After cooling down to room temperature, the slides were cleared (de-waxed) in CitriSolv (Xylene substitute) for 3 times; then went through gradient ethanol (EtOH) for rehydration: 100% EtOH three times, 95% EtOH 2 times, and once in 75% EtOH. The slides were washed under running tap water for 5 min and then treated with antigen retrieval buffer (containing 25 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.05% SDS), in a 95 ∘C water bath for 30 min. After cooling down to room temperature, slides were washed in distilled water six times and were then ready for immunostaining. Primary antibodies were diluted to concentrations that had been optimized (1–5 μg/mL) and stained for overnight incubation at 4 ∘C in PBS. Finally, the samples underwent sequential washing in PBS several times before imaging.
