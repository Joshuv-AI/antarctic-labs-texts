Insights into the plastome evolution and phylogenetic relationships of Lepidium (Brassicaceae)
Authors: Ting Ren, Lulu Xun, Yun Jia, Huijuan Zhou, Bin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLepidium represents one of the most species-rich genera in the Brassicaceae, encompassing numerous taxa with excellent medicinal value. Although the plastome architecture of Brassicaceae has been extensively characterized, its evolutionary dynamics within Lepidium remain poorly understood. Besides, the intrageneric taxonomy has long been problematic, primarily due to subtle morphological differences or overlapping morphological characteristics. Here, 15 Lepidium plastomes (11 newly sequenced and 4 published) were subject to comparative genomic and phylogenetic analyses to investigate plastome evolution and phylogenetic relationships.ResultsThe total length of the 11 newly sequenced Lepidium plastomes varied from 153,803 bp to 154,997 bp, with 111–112 unique genes. The Lepidium plastomes exhibited strong conservatism in genome size, gene order, plastome structure, IR/SC borders, codon usage, mVISTA analysis, Pi value, and genetic distance. However, minor variations were observed in IR border shifts. Analysis of repeat sequences revealed interspecific differences in both long repeat sequences (24–40) and simple sequence repeats (78–98) among 15 Lepidium plastomes. Six divergence hotspots (ycf1, ccsA, matK, rps15-ycf1, trnH-GUG-psbA, and rpl32-trnL-UAG) were identified as potential DNA barcodes for both species identification and phylogenetic analyses of Lepidium. The ycf1 gene was under relaxed selection. Furthermore, our phylogenetic studies clarified that all 15 Lepidium species formed a strongly supported monophyletic group within the tribe Lepidieae, while the cytonuclear discordance between ITS and plastome trees was found within Lepidium.ConclusionOur findings suggested that cytonuclear discordance within Lepidium likely results from hybridization/introgression and incomplete lineage sorting. Meanwhile, we proposed the reclassification of L. perfoliatum within Section Lepia. This study clearly documented global patterns of plastome structural evolution, and constructed a well-supported phylogeny of Lepidium. The reliable phylogenetic tree not only provides a new viewpoint into the intrageneric relationships and sectional classification of Lepidium, but also advances our understanding of the evolutionary history of this genus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07524-4.

Background

Lepidium L., one of the largest genera within Brassicaceae, consists of about 180 species [1, 2]. It is distributed predominantly in temperate and subtropical regions, excluding the Far North and Tropics [3]. This genus has approximately 27 species in China, with most distributed in the north [2, 4, 5]. Many plants within the Lepidium are xerophytes or drought-tolerant species, and grow in wastelands, deserts, and saline alkali lands, etc [2, 6].

The genus Lepidium was first described by Linnaeus in 1753, with L. latifolium L. designated as the type species [7]. Lepidium is one of the most natural and easily identifiable genera in the Brassicaceae [4]. However, the intrageneric taxonomy has long been problematic, mainly due to subtle morphological differences or overlapping morphological characteristics [1, 8]. Thellung [9] found that only a few reliable key traits can be used for intrageneric grouping, while Jonsell [10] considered that this classification concept mainly based on the morphological characteristics of flowers and fruits is largely artificial. Nevertheless, most scholars still followed Thellung’s [9] sectional classification, sometimes with some modifications [1]. Subsequent studies supported merging Coronopus, Cardaria, Stroganowia, Stubendorffia, and Winklera into Lepidium [4, 11], which further complicates the intrageneric taxonomy of the genus. Over the years, numerous molecular phylogenetic and morphological studies of Lepidium have been conducted. Mummenhoff’s [12] study based on RUBISCO data preliminarily suggested that Lepidium draba (= Cardaria draba) should belong to the Section Lepia. The molecular systematics study by Mummenhoff et al. [1] used chloroplast DNA restriction site data to clearly identify Sect. Lepia and proposed merging Sect. Lepiocardamon and Sect. Cardamon due to their close phylogenetic relationships. According to the micromorphological characters of seed coats and the characters of cotyledon accumbent, the study established a new section–Sect. Accumbentum [13]. Although earlier studies utilizing traditional methods have promoted our understanding of the intrageneric taxonomy of Lepidium, more extensive genomic evidence is still needed to resolve the phylogenetic relationships within this genus and establish a reliable framework for its classification.

Additionally, Lepidium plants usually contain chemical components, such as glucosinolates, flavonoids, triterpenoids, sterols, coumarins, and alkaloids, etc [14]. Therefore, many of them possess excellent medicinal value and are traditional Chinese medicine or folk medicine in China. The dried ripe seeds of L. apetalum, traditionally known as “Lepidii Semen”, are generally used for inducing diuresis to alleviate edema and purging the lung to calm panting [15]. L. salivum seeds have wide therapeutic applications, including anti-inflammatory, hypoglycemic, and antihypertensive [16]. L. latifolium seeds have antiprotozoal, anthelmintic, and insecticidal properties [17]. Despite its significant medicinal value, genomic resources for Lepidium remain limited, complicating species identification. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop reliable DNA barcodes through comparative plastome analysis to ensure accurate species identification and maintain medicinal quality standards.

Chloroplast, an important photosynthetic organelle in green plants, possesses its own genome. It facilitates the conversion of light energy into carbohydrates, thereby supplying essential energy for plant growth and development [18]. In angiosperms, the chloroplast genome (plastome) is typically characterized by a quadripartite structure, which includes a large single-copy (LSC) region, a small single-copy (SSC) region and two inverted repeat (IR) regions [19]. Most plant plastomes are 115–165 kb in size and harbor 110–130 genes, including approximately 80 protein-coding, 30 transfer RNAs (tRNAs), and four ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) genes [19, 20]. Compared to nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, plastomes demonstrate higher conservation, smaller size, and lower nucleotide substitution rates, making them particularly valuable for species identification and phylogenetic studies [21–23]. For example, the study of Guo et al. [24] showed that plastome is an effective molecular marker for precise identification of Asparagus cochinchinensis and nine other congeneric species. Yao et al. [25] employed the plastomes of 182 palm species to establish a plastid phylogenomic framework for Arecaceae. Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have significantly reduced DNA sequencing costs, thereby accelerating comparative plastomics and phylogenetic studies. Meanwhile, a growing number of studies on the Brassicaceae plastomes have been published [26–30]. Therefore, we employed genome skimming sequencing to acquire both plastomes and nuclear ribosomal DNA (nrDNA) sequences for inferring the phylogenetic relationships of the genus Lepidium.

Here, we sequenced and assembled 11 Lepidium whole plastomes and combined with 4 previously published Lepidium plastomes to perform a comprehensive analysis, including genome features, codons, IR borders, repeats, dN/dS, mutational hotspots, and phylogenetic relationships. Our aims were to: (1) characterize the plastomes and infer the plastome evolution of Lepidium species; (2) identify divergence hotspots as potential DNA barcodes for species identification of Lepidium; (3) construct the phylogenies of Lepidium species using plastid protein-coding sequences and nrDNA ITS to elucidate the phylogenetic relationships within this genus.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

We collected 11 plant materials of Lepidium species from the wild, and the voucher specimens were stored in the herbarium of Xi’an Botanical Garden of the Shaanxi Province (XBGH) (Xi’an, China). No permission is needed to sample these plants, since they are not protected species. The specimen identification was done by Professor Bin Li. The sample information is listed in Table S1. In addition, we also downloaded four published complete plastomes of Lepidium from NCBI, namely L. cordatum (ON598366), L. ferganense (ON598364), L. meyenii (NC_034363) and L. ruderale (ON598356). We obtained a total of 15 Lepidium plastomes for subsequent analysis.

The total genomic DNA of newly collected samples was extracted from silica-dried fresh leaves using a modified CTAB protocol [31]. DNA quality and concentration were assessed using Agilent 5400 system (Agilent, CA, USA) at Novogene (Tianjin, China).

Plastome sequencing, assembly and annotation

Genomic DNA was fragmented by sonication to a size of 350 bp. The paired-end library was prepared with NEBNext® UltraTM DNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) following manufacturer’s protocols, and then sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq sequencing platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) (2 × 150 bp) at Novogene (Tianjin, China) (Table S1).

We performed quality control of raw reads using fastP v0.20.0 [32] to remove low-quality reads and adapter contamination. For plastome assembly, all 11 newly sequenced samples were de novo assembled using GetOrganelle v1.7.2 [33] with default parameters. The assembled plastomes were annotated using GeSeq [34], with subsequent manual correction of protein-coding gene boundaries in Geneious Prime 2025.0.3 (Biomatters Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand) based on annotations from congeneric species. Plastome visualization was conducted using OrganellarGenomeDRAW v1.3.1 [35]. Additionally, we assembled complete or partial nrDNA sequences for all 11 samples using GetOrganelle v1.7.2 [33] under default parameters. The plastomes and nrDNA sequences of 11 newly assembled and annotated Lepidium species were deposited to the GenBank (PV712657-PV712667 and PV700193-PV700203) (Table S1).

IR borders and codons analyses

The IR/SC borders of 15 Lepidium plastomes were comparatively analyzed to elucidate the expansion and contraction dynamics of IR regions. For codon analyses, Geneious Prime 2025.0.3 (Biomatters Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand) was employed to extract the plastid protein-coding genes (PCGs) from 15 Lepidium plastomes. Then, the PCGs with coding sequences shorter than 300 bp were excluded. Finally, the codon count and relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) were computed in MEGA 6 [36], and the resulting RSCU values were visualized as a heatmap using TBtools v1.0.0 [37].

Long repeat sequences and simple sequence repeats analyses

The online program REPuter [38] was employed to check the four categories (forward, palindromic, complementary, and reverse repeats) with parameters of ≥ 30 bp minimum repeating size and Hamming distance = 3. The redundant repeat sequences were excluded. The MISA v2.1 [39] was employed to count the simple sequence repeats (SSRs) with minimum repeat thresholds of 10 (mono-), 5 (di-), 4 (tri-), and 3 (tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide) repeats.

Plastome sequence divergence analyses

A sequence identity plot was generated by comparing the 15 Lepidium plastomes using mVISTA [40]. The annotation of L. apetalum plastome was used as a reference. Furthermore, we used Geneious Prime 2025.0.3 (Biomatters Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand) to extract the plastome regions for identifying mutation hotspots and quantifying sequence divergence. The extracted regions were aligned using MAFFT v7 [41], and then regions with aligned lengths < 200 bp were discarded. Finally, the nucleotide diversity (Pi) was computed in DnaSP v5.1 [42]. The pairwise genetic distances among the 15 Lepidium plastomes were derived using MEGA v6 [36].

Selective pressure analysis

The evolutionary selective pressure acting on plastid PCGs was assessed by computing the ratio (ω) of non-synonymous (dN) to synonymous (dS) substitution rates for 15 Lepidium plastomes. We calculated the ω (dN/dS) for the 77 plastid PCGs using site-specific models implemented in Codeml [43] of the PAML4.9 package [44]. The 77 PCGs were extracted using Geneious Prime 2025.0.3 (Biomatters Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand), aligned using ClustalW (Codons) method in MEGA v6 [36], and the stop codons were then manually removed. The final 77 protein-coding sequences were employed as the input seqfiles, alongside an ML tree constructed using RAxML v8.2.8 [45] as the treefile. The model fit was assessed by likelihood ratio tests (LRT) with chi-square approximation. The selected sites were detected by Bayes Empirical Bayes (BEB) analysis, with sites having posterior probabilities ≥ 0.95 considered statistically significant.

Phylogenetic analyses

We constructed the phylogeny of Lepidium using both Maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) approaches. Two datasets were analyzed: (1) 76 shared plastid protein-coding genes extracted from 56 plastomes using PhyloSuite v1.2.2 [46], aligned using MAFFT v7 (codon mode) [41], trimmed using trimAl v1.2 (automated1 option) [47], and then concatenated using PhyloSuite v1.2.2 [46] prior to phylogenetic analysis; and (2) 56 nrDNA ITS sequences aligned using MAFFT v7 (auto algorithm) [41] followed by manual refinement. Phylogenetic analysis included 15 Lepidium species along with 41 other members of the Brassicaceae, among which Aethionema grandiflorum and A. cordifolium were set as outgroups.

ML analyses were conducted using RAxML v8.2.8 [45] with the GTRGAMMA model and 1,000 bootstrap replicates (BS). For Bayesian inference (BI), we determined the optimal nucleotide substitution models by Modeltest v3.7 [48], identifying GTR + I + G for the 76 shared plastid protein-coding sequences and SYM + I + G for the 56 nrDNA ITS sequences. BI analyses were performed in MrBayes v3.2 [49] with two independent Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains, each comprising 2 million generations (sampling every 100 generations). After discarding the first 25% of trees as burn-in, we confirmed convergence (average standard deviation of split frequencies < 0.01) and calculated posterior probabilities (PP) from the remaining trees.

Results

General features of newly sequenced Lepidium plastomes

The assembled reads of 11 Lepidium species, arranged from a total of 20,388,008 clean reads of L. chalepense to 25,098,516 clean reads of L. didymum (Table S1). The total size of the 11 Lepidium plastomes varied from 153,803 bp (L. chalepense) to 154,997 bp (L. sativum) (Fig. 1; Table 1). The Lepidium plastomes showed the characteristic quadripartite structure of angiosperms, containing two inverted repeat regions (IRs: 26,363 bp in L. densiflorum and L. virginicum to 26,498 bp in L. cuneiforme that split the large single-copy region (LSC: 82,906 bp in L. chalepense to 84,012 bp in L. virginicum) and small single-copy region (SSC: 17,526 bp in L. latifolium to 18,048 bp in L. chalepense) (Fig. 1; Table 1). The Lepidium plastomes exhibited conserved GC content (36.4–36.5%), with IR regions displaying significantly higher GC% (42.3–42.4%) than the LSC (34.2–34.3%) and SSC (29.1–29.6%) regions (Table 1). These newly sequenced Lepidium plastomes encoded 111–112 unique genes, comprising 77–78 protein-coding, 30 tRNA, and four rRNA genes (Fig. 1; Table 1 and S2). The rps16 gene in L. latifolium plastome was a pseudogene. Contraction and expansion of IRs

The borders of IR/SC were described for all 15 Lepidium plastomes (Fig. 2). The Lepidium SC/IR borders were conservative with slight shifts (Fig. 2). The LSC/IRb borders were consistently positioned in rps19 genes, and overlapped with these borders by 103–114 bp. The ycf1 and ndhF genes overlapped 2–97 bp and 37–103 bp in the IRb/SSC borders. The IRa/SSC borders were identified within the ycf1 genes, and the ycf1 genes were located in the SSC and IRa regions with 4,277-4,318 bp and 904-1,039 bp, respectively. The IRa/LSC borders were consistently positioned between rpl2 genes and trnH-GUG genes, with a distance of 158–168 bp from rpl2 genes and 2–4 bp from trnH-GUG genes.

Statistics of codon usage

In each of the 15 Lepidium plastomes, we identified 64 codon types encoding 20 amino acids from a set of 53 merged protein-coding sequences (Fig. 3; Table S3). The total number of codons ranged from 21,195 (L. cordatum) to 21,262 (L. sativum) (Table S3). AUU was the most-used codon (925–937), whereas UGC was the least (63–68), excluding the stop codon (Table S3). As for the three stop codons, UAA (31–35) was the most-used codon than UAG (12–15) and UGA (6–7). Leu was encoded by 2,242-2,264 codons, whereas Cys was encoded by 247–251 codons. The heatmap of RSCU values is shown in Fig. 3, and the values of all codons ranged from 0.33 to 0.35 (CUG) to 2.13–2.25 (UUA) in the 15 Lepidium plastomes. According to the RSCU value, 30 codons exhibited preferential usage (RSCU > 1), all of which terminated in A or U with the sole exception of UUG among the 15 Lepidium plastomes (Fig. 3). Moreover, 32 codons were used infrequently (RSCU < 1); as well as AUG and UGG displayed no preferences (RSCU = 1) (Fig. 3).

476 long repeats were discovered from the 15 Lepidium plastomes in total, comprising 283 forward, 180 palindromic, 8 reverse, and 5 complement repeats (Fig. 4a; Table S4). The number of repeats was highest in L. campestre (40) and lowest in L. ferganense (24) (Fig. 4a). We discovered 1,330 SSRs across the 15 Lepidium plastomes (Table 2). The number of SSRs in Lepidium ranged from 78 in L. densiflorum to 98 in L. perfoliatum (Table 2). Mononucleotide repeats were the most universal (915; 68.8%), followed by dinucleotide (224; 16.84%), tetranucleotide (114; 8.57%), trinucleotide (66; 4.96%), pentanucleotide (7; 0.53%), and hexanucleotide repeats 4; 0.3%) (Table 2). The hexanucleotide motifs AAATTC and TTGTAT were found in L. meyenii, while AATTGA and TTTAAC were found in L. didymum and L. cuneiforme, respectively (Table S5). The vast majority of SSRs (1248; 93.83%) were A and T nucleotide repeats, such as T (584), A (322), AT (113), TA (111), ATA (17), TAT (17), AAT (14), TTA (4), AAAT (13), AATA (9), ATAA (12), TAAA (19), TAAT (10), TATT (2), and TTTA (1) motifs (Table S5).

Lepidium plastomes variation

The mVISTA results showed that the 15 Lepidium plastomes had collineation and high sequence similarity (Fig. 5). Furthermore, the IR and coding regions exhibited significantly higher sequence conservation compared to the two SC and non-coding regions (Fig. 5). We also calculated the Pi value for 149 regions (84 non-coding and 65 coding regions). The overall average Pi value was 0.012057 (Fig. 6; Table S6). The Pi value also implied significantly lower divergence in the IR (mean = 0.001622) and coding regions (mean = 0.006079) compared to the two SC (mean = 0.01397) and non-coding regions (mean = 0.016083). The mutation hotspots in the Lepidium plastomes were identified based on Pi value and are shown in Fig. 6. Some peaks were detected, containing three coding regions [matK (0.016923), ccsA (0.017176), and ycf1 (0.023109)] and three non-coding regions [rpl32-trnL-UAG (0.040986), trnH-GUG-psbA (0.041918), and rps15-ycf1 (0.044826)] (Fig. 6; Table S6). Selective pressures in Lepidium

The ω values for the 77 plastid PCGs are listed in Table S8, revealing patterns of evolutionary selection pressure across the 15 Lepidium plastomes. The values ω > 1, ω = 1, 0.5 < ω < 1, and ω < 0.5 imply positive, neutral, relaxed, and purifying selection, respectively. The vast majority of ω values were smaller than 0.5, indicating these genes have undergone purifying selection. Nonetheless, we also detected an increase in ω value, implying relaxed selection in petN and ycf1 genes (Table S8). However, only the ycf1 gene was significant (P < 0.05) after the LRT (Table S9).

Phylogenetic relationships of Lepidium

We used 76 shared plastid protein-coding sequences and 56 nrDNA ITS sequences to explore the phylogenetic relationships of Lepidium (Fig. 7; Table S10). The two datasets contained 61,734 and 615 nucleotide sites, including 10,638 and 314 variable sites, and 6,251 and 257 parsimony informative sites, respectively. The ITS and plastid phylogenies demonstrated topological discordance among the tribes within Brassicaceae, consistent with previously reported incongruence patterns in this family [30, 50]. Based on both datasets, the Lepidium species in this study formed a strongly supported monophyletic group (BS = 100/PP = 1) within the tribe Lepidieae (Fig. 7). However, the sections within Lepidium were not monophyletic (Fig. 7). Two phylogenetic trees emphasized two clades (Clade I and II), embodying 4 species (L. chalepense, L. draba, L. campestre, and L. perfoliatum) (BS = 100/PP = 1, BS = 96/PP = 0.98) and the remaining 11 species (BS = 100/PP = 1, BS = 99/PP = 1), respectively (Fig. 7). Also, L. chalepense and L. draba togethered with L. campestre, which subsequently clustered with L. perfoliatum in both phylogenetic trees (Fig. 7). The BI and ML trees inferred from 76 shared plastid protein-coding sequences exhibited the identical topology (Fig. 7a), and most phylogenetic relationships obtained strong support. In Clade II, 11 members of Lepidium were divided into four clades. It was noticed that Subclade A contained only one species: L. latifolium. In Subclade B, L. didymum was resolved as a sister to L. meyenii with strong support (BS = 100/PP = 1). L. apetalum, L. cuneiforme, and L. cordatum formed a strong Subclade C (BS = 100/PP = 1), of which L. apetalum togethered with L. cuneiforme, then clustered with L. cordatum. L. densiflorum, L. virginicum, L. sativum, L. ferganense, and L. ruderale belonged to Subclade D with high support (BS = 98/PP = 1), of which L. densiflorum and L. virginicum togethered with L. sativum, then clustered with L. ferganense, and L. ruderale.

The BI and ML trees inferred from 56 nrDNA ITS sequences exhibited slightly inconsistent tree topologies (Fig. 7b). The phylogenetic trees based on nrDNA ITS sequences received less support than those from the plastid protein-coding sequences. Moreover, the phylogenetic relationships of most Lepidium species were consistent with the plastome trees, but some conflicts were observed in Clade II. For example, L. latifolium clustered with L. cordatum in the ITS tree, while it formed a Subclade A independently in the plastome tree. L. densiflorum and L. virginicum clustered with L. meyenii in the ITS tree but with L. sativum in the plastome tree. In the ITS tree, L. meyenii, L. sativum, and L. didymium formed 3 separate clades, respectively. L. apetalum and L. cuneiforme clustered with L. ruderale in the ITS tree but with L. cordatum in the plastome tree.

Discussion

Plastome evolution of Lepidium

Here, the plastomes of 11 Lepidium species were sequenced by the high-throughput sequencing method. The plastome size ranges from 153,803 bp (L. chalepense) to 154,997 bp (L. sativum), which was similar to most species in the Brassicaceae [27]. As observed in several plant lineages, gene loss (including pseudogenes) is common [51, 52], and the Brassicaceae is no exception. In particular, the rps16 gene has been reported as a pseudogene in some species of Rorippa [30], Arabidopsis and its allies [53], as well as other Brassicaceae species [27]. Studies have also indicated that the ycf15 gene is a pseudogene across multiple lineages within this family [27]. Here, all 11 Lepidium species have 111–112 unique genes, including 77–78 protein-coding, 30 transfer RNA and four ribosomal RNA genes. As for the above two genes, the ycf15 gene was lost in Lepidium, while the rps16 gene is functional in Lepidium, except for L. latifolium. Additionally, the gene for translation initiation factor A (infA) has been lost in all 11 Lepidium plastomes. Likewise, the infA gene has been lost numerous times independently during land plant evolution, including in some Brassicaceae species [21, 54–56]. Loss or pseudogenization of the infA gene implies it has been transferred to the nucleus multiple times in many plant lineages [57]. Generally, the total GC% typically accounts for 30–40% of the plastome, and the IR regions exhibit higher GC% than the two SC regions [58], which were consistent with Lepidium (36.4–36.5%). The rRNA genes with a high level of GC% (55.3–55.4%) were located in the IR regions, which contributed to the high GC% of the IR regions (42.3–42.4%) compared with that of the LSC (34.2–34.3%) and SSC (29.1–29.6%) regions [58, 59].

The dynamic contraction and expansion of the IR regions represent significant evolutionary mechanisms that may give rise to plastome size variations [60, 61]. The IR/SC border regions among the 15 Lepidium plastomes were conserved, with these borders undergoing slight changes. This indicated that the structural variation of the Lepidium plastomes is not significant. The conserved genomic structures have been reported in most Brassicaceae members, like the genera Arabis [62] and Cardamine [56].

Codon usage bias represents a significant evolutionary characteristic in the plastome, shaped primarily by two major factors, namely natural selection and mutation pressure on nucleotide sequences [63]. Our codon analysis in 15 Lepidium plastomes also revealed a pronounced bias toward A/U-ending codons at the third nucleotide position, which has been widely recognized [64]. Perhaps AT richness in the plastome is the main factor that biased codons ending with A or U [65]. Leu was the richest amino acid, while Cys was the rarest, consistent with patterns observed across other Brassicaceae [30, 66, 67].

Previous studies supported that long sequence repeats may facilitate plastome recombination, rearrangement, and mutation [68–70]. Among the 15 Lepidium plastomes, forward repeat was the majority of the repeat types, which has also been reported in watercress [71] and Ligusticum [59]. However, there are slightly more palindromic repeats than forward repeats in the plastome of the Arabis stellari [62], Broccoli [72], and Cardamine [56]. Consistent with other plants, reverse and complement repeats are rare in the plastomes [56, 59, 62, 71, 72]. Moreover, most repeat sequences were 30–50 bp in length, which is similar to other findings [30, 73, 74]. SSR is very abundant in the plastome and represents a major source of molecular markers, making it widely applied in population genetics and evolutionary studies [75, 76]. Compared with other Brassicaceae taxa, the number of SSRs in Lepidium is lower than in Rorippa [30] and Draba oreades [77] but higher than in watercress [71]. Our results found that the richest SSRs were mononucleotide A/T SSRs, and the SSRs predominantly consist of polyA or polyT instead of G or C repeats, which are identical to previous studies [71, 78, 79]. The overall AT richness in the plastome may be contributed to the abundance of polyA or polyT SSRs [80].

Candidate DNA barcodes

The sequence variation analysis implied that the 15 Lepidium plastomes have high sequence similarity. Moreover, the IR and coding regions show greater evolutionary conservation than SC and non-coding regions, which was proved by many plant lineages [30, 81]. The more conservative IR regions compared to the SC regions in plants may be because of a copy correction among the IR sequences during gene conversion [56]. The six highest variable coding and non-coding regions (ycf1, ccsA, matK, rps15-ycf1, trnH-GUG-psbA, and rpl32-trnL-UAG) were selected as the mutational hotspots. With adequate sequence variation, the matK gene has been accepted as a pivotal plant DNA barcode for phylogenetic reconstruction and species discrimination [21, 82]. The ccsA gene, located in the SSC region of the plastome, encodes a protein that is essential for heme attachment to C-type cytochromes [21, 83]. It has been ascertained as a mutational hotspot in many angiosperms, such as Lagerstroemia [84], Ligusticum [59], and Pilea [85]. The ycf1 gene and trnH-GUG-psbA have also been used as the DNA barcode in plant phylogenetic studies [86, 87]. In addition, the rpl32-trnL-UAG was reported in Camellia [88] and Zanthoxylum [22], and rps15-ycf1 was reported in Prunus [89], Hepatica [90], and Zingiberoideae [91]. These mutational hotspots could be good candidate DNA barcodes for Lepidium species identification and phylogeny.

Selective pressures in Lepidium

We estimated the dN/dS (ω) of 77 plastid PCGs of 15 Lepidium plastomes. The vast majority of PCGs have apparently undergone purifying selection (ω < 0.5), which reflects the conservatism of plastid genes in plant lineages [92]. Only one gene (ycf1) was under relaxed selection. The ycf1 gene is important for plant viability as it is responsible for photosynthetic protein import [93]. Relaxed or positive selection acting on ycf1 has also been described in Lennoaceae [94] and Bulbophyllum [95]. The genus Lepidium is mainly distributed in Northern China and grows in wastelands, deserts, and saline alkali lands, etc. We therefore hypothesized that the relaxed selection on the ycf1 gene may have facilitated the adaptation of Lepidium to harsh habitats.

Phylogenetic relationships of Lepidium

In the present study, the phylogenetic analyses clearly revealed that the supports and resolutions of the plastome tree were significantly improved compared to those of the ITS tree. All Lepidium species in this study formed a strongly supported monophyletic group within the tribe Lepidieae. Furthermore, the cytonuclear discordance between ITS and plastome trees was found within Lepidium, which is a universal phenomenon in the genus of Brassicaceae [26, 30]. This discordance is potentially attributable to hybridization/introgression and incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) [26, 59, 96]. Some evidence indicated that hybridization/introgression has occurred within the genus Lepidium [1, 97, 98]. Therefore, we deduced that both hybridization/introgression and ILS could be used to explain the cytonuclear discordance within Lepidium.

The sections within Lepidium were not monophyletic, which indicated the current sectional classification is inappropriate and needs to be improved and revised. L. campestre, L. chalepense, and L. draba belong to Sect. Lepia [1, 4, 12], and L. perfoliatum belongs to Sect. Lepidium [6]. Yet, the two phylogenetic trees showed that 4 species (L. chalepense, L. draba, L. campestre, and L. perfoliatum) clustered together and formed Clade I with strong support (BS = 100/PP = 1, BS = 96/PP = 0.98). Therefore, we suggested that L. perfoliatum also belongs to Sect. Lepia. These four taxa share several morphological characteristics, including the upper cauline leaves sessile; leaf blade cordate, ovate, suborbicular, elliptic, lanceolate, or oblong, base amplexicaul, margin dentate or entire, apex acute or acuminate. Despite the limitations of incomplete sampling, our findings provide new insights into the sectional classification of Lepidium and contribute to its taxonomic studies. Future phylogenetic studies should incorporate additional nuclear gene data and broader taxon sampling across Lepidium to clarify the interspecific relationship and sectional classification of this genus.

Conclusions

Our study described a comprehensive plastome feature and constructed a reliable phylogenetic tree for Lepidium. We first assessed the evolutionary conservation of the Lepidium plastomes by examining genome size, gene order, plastome structure, IR/SC borders, codon usage, mVISTA analysis, genetic distance, and Pi value. We also detected 24–40 long repeat sequences and 78–98 SSRs. Six coding and non-coding regions (ycf1, ccsA, matK, rps15-ycf1, rpl32-trnL-UAG, and trnH-GUG-psbA) were selected as promising candidate DNA barcodes to facilitate species identification and phylogenetic studies of Lepidium. The ycf1 gene was under relaxed selection. Our phylogenetic studies clarified that all 15 Lepidium species formed a strongly supported monophyletic group within the tribe Lepidieae. Furthermore, we observed the cytonuclear discordance between ITS and plastome trees within Lepidium, which is potentially attributable to hybridization/introgression and incomplete lineage sorting. Based on phylogenetic results, we suggested that L. perfoliatum should belong to Sect. Lepia. Overall, this study advances our knowledge of plastome evolution and phylogenetic relationships within Lepidium, and helps to clarify the taxonomy and evolutionary history of this genus.
