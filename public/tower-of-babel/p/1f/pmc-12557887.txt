SCA-MobiPlant: smartphone-deployed multistage attention fusion model for accurate field detection of chili leaf curl complex
Authors: Samrat Paul, Venu Emmadi, Mehulee Sarkar, Shubhajyoti Das, Anirban Roy, Parimal Sinha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundField-scale assessment of chili leaf curl complex presents a significant diagnostic challenge, as both chili leaf curl virus (ChiLCV) and mite infestations produce visually overlapping symptoms difficult to distinguish by untrained personnel. This diagnostic confusion frequently leads to inappropriate application of either insecticides or acaricides, resulting in economic losses and environmental concerns. To address this issue, we propose SCA-MobiPlant, an improved MobileNetV3-Small model integrated with a novel multistage Squeeze-and-Excitation Coordinate Attention (SCA) fusion mechanism, designed for accurate differentiation of these apparently similar symptoms and precise field assessment of the disease.ResultsThe proposed model effectively focuses on subtle diagnostic features including leaf texture, petiole elongation, and irregular curling patterns to achieve reliable classification. The multistage SCA fusion module demonstrated superior performance, achieving 99.64% accuracy, 99.61% precision, 99.64% recall, and 99.62% F1-score through K = 5 cross-validation, outperforming other attention modules such as the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) and Coordinate Attention (CA). Gradient-Weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) provided visual interpretability of the model’s decision-making process. Comparative evaluation against state-of-the-art architectures, including EfficientNetB0, ResNet50, VGG19 and YOLO advanced series, confirmed the computational efficiency of the proposed model for mobile deployment.ConclusionsThe final system, termed SCA-MobiPlant, has been successfully implemented on smartphones, along with a Disease Incidence (DI) calculation module, enabling rapid and accurate field assessment of the disease. This facilitates appropriate intervention strategies while minimizing unnecessary pesticide use. The study highlights the potential of lightweight, attention-enhanced models for real-world plant disease diagnostics, particularly in resource-constrained agricultural settings.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13007-025-01453-x.

Background

Growing environment-sensitive commercial crops requires a comprehensive crop health monitoring system where sensing, imaging, and/or machine learning technologies are aptly in use to detect, identify, monitor, predict, and/or manage plant pathogens/diseases and other biotic stresses [1–4]. Advances in deep learning algorithms have transformed plant disease detection by enabling automated diagnosis with high-accuracy [5–9]. Image-based sensor technologies and machine learning tools offer promising solutions for identifying and managing complex crop disease problems of global importance [6, 10]. Leaf curl in chili, a complex disease, poses a significant threat to chili cultivation worldwide [2]. India, the largest producer and exporter of chili (Capsicum annuum), contributes 43% of global production, producing 2.7 million tons annually, with a productivity rate of approximately 8578.4 kg/ha (FAOSTAT, 2023). In recent years, changing climatic conditions and pest resurgence have led to the emergence of leaf curl, causing substantial losses in production [11]. The disease is caused primarily by chili leaf curl virus (ChiLCV) transmitted by the whitefly vector Bemisia tabaci which is prevalent across tropical and subtropical regions [2, 11–13]. Infected plants present distinctive symptoms, including upwards curling of leaf margins, yellowing of veins, reduced leaf size, and stunted growth—all of which significantly impact yield, particularly when infection occurs early in the growing season [14, 15]. This situation is further complicated by mite infestations that exacerbate plant stress, leading to downwards leaf curling and defoliation [16–18]. The overlapping symptoms associated with this disease complex, which vary across time and space, and make detection and identification difficult through conventional approaches such as PCR detection and/or visual surveys. Inaccurate diagnosis consequently drives farmers toward excessive pesticide application as a desperate control measure, resulting in significant environmental contamination and ecological disruption [19, 20]. Precise disease detection in real time is one of the major components essential for the development of intervention strategies as well as for phenotyping resistance [5, 6, 10].

For precise detection and diagnosis of plant diseases, deep learning algorithms such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are being employed for image-based disease classification, leveraging hierarchical feature extraction for robust identification of plant diseases [6–8, 21–23]. Advanced CNN architectures, including VGG, GoogleNet, AlexNet, ResNet, and MobileNet, have enhanced symptoms and pathogen identification capabilities [21–25]. Advanced object detection algorithms, including YOLO series and Transformer-based models (ViTs), enable direct detection of multiple targets in images through target localization and instance segmentation capabilities. While these approaches are well-suited for disease severity analysis, they require extensive image annotation, creating a significant burden for personnel lacking expertise in the field of plant pathology [26–31]. All these algorithms, despite achieving 85–96% accuracy in controlled environments, field deployment faces challenges from overlapping background, environmental factors, limited datasets, and insufficient understanding of disease complexity [6, 30, 32–34]. Additionally, high computational demands and limited model interpretability hinder real-world application [33, 35, 36]. Moreover, the implementation of these algorithms in edge devices such as mobile applications often requires modifications or adjustments with respect to their size, power consumption and computational constraints [37]. MobileNetV3 is an enhanced lightweight CNN and optimized architecture balances computational efficiency with classification accuracy, making it suitable for field deployment [33, 37, 38]. Designed for mobile and embedded vision applications, MobileNetV3 combines hardware-aware network architecture search with the NetAdapt algorithm to achieve a small size, low latency, and reduced power consumption [22, 24, 30, 34–41]. Although it is already efficient and accurate for many vision tasks, several strategies are available to improve its accuracy further, especially for custom tasks such as crop disease detection, medical imaging, or other specialized domains [30, 42–44]. Recent innovations incorporate attention mechanisms (SE, CBAM) and hybrid models (CNN-RNN, Transformer-based models) to improve spatial and temporal understanding, particularly for diseases with seasonal trends. Attention mechanisms (AMs) have been adopted for plant disease detection and identification in rice, tomato, apple and pepper by making a network focused on disease-affected regions [34, 45]. AM helps focus on ‘what’ and ‘where’ in the input image are important for enhancing interpretability and efficiently extracting relevant features in complex or noisy environments. Squeeze and Excitation (SE) and Convolutional Block Attention Mechanism (CBAM) helps focus in the input image to improve the representational power of neural networks by making the network focus more on important channels or feature maps and suppress less useful ones [42, 45, 46]. Coordinate Attention (CA) is a lightweight and powerful attention mechanism that improves feature representation by embedding positional information into channel attention. It was proposed to overcome the limitations of other attention mechanisms (like Squeeze-and-Excitation and CBAM), which often lose precise positional (spatial) information when aggregating features globally [43].

Leaf curl symptoms complicated by virus infections through whiteflies and thrips as well as mite attacks pose difficulties in instant identification necessary for management decision [47]. While whiteflies and thrips transmits plant virus, the mite infection causes epinasty of tender leaves. This study aims to develop an accurate and novel chili leaf curl dataset alongside a resource-efficient deep learning model for disease detection. The objectives are to optimize computational cost while maintaining high classification accuracy, and to integrate the model with a disease incidence (DI) calculation module, making it suitable for smart phone based field deployment in epidemiological surveys. In this experiment, two hypotheses were formulated and tested. First, we accept the null hypothesis that the AM improves the accuracy of disease identification and prediction. Second, the attention mechanism helps the model focus on specific parts of the plant image that show signs of disease, improving performance and interpretability.

The proposed MobileNetV3-based classification model with a visual attention mechanism successfully detects ChiLCV infection, separating it from mite infestation. The model is deployed on a smartphone and linked with a module for one-shot estimation of the disease incidence required for quick disease assessment in any surveillance program.

Materials and methods

The image dataset (RGB) was produced through an experimental setup to train the MobileNetV3 model with AM in combination. The best combination of AM was selected and deployed in a smartphone system with a user interface for one-shot assessment of leaf curl incidence in chili.

Leaf curl symptoms and preparation of the image dataset

To prepare the image dataset, approximately 3,000 seedlings of the susceptible Pusa Jwala variety were grown in earthen pots filled with a soil mixture (60% soil, 20% sand, 20% compost). One seedling in each pot was maintained and placed under glasshouse conditions. Hoagland nutrient solution was provided weekly to ensure the normal growth of the plants. For the healthy plant samples, approximately 1000 seedlings were sprayed with imidacloprid (0.01%) at 15 days after sowing and maintained in a separate glasshouse chamber. For virus-infected plant samples, 100 seedlings were placed in cages (200 mesh), and 10 viruliferous whiteflies (Institute’s rearing facility; maintained in the ChiLCV-infected plant chamber) were released following the suction method [2, 48]. Cages of 10 replicates each with 100 seedlings were inoculated, releasing 10 viruliferous whiteflies in each enclosure. Ten days after inoculation, the seedlings in the cages were sprayed with imidacloprid (0.01%) to eliminate the vectors. For confirmation of virus transmission by whiteflies, leaves were randomly collected from the cages inoculated with whiteflies, and RPA detection was performed via ChiLCV-specific primers designed from the AC1 gene of the ChiLCV Maharashtra isolate (FP2: 5’ggaagatagcgggaattccacctttaatttga3’ and RP7: 5’tctgccaacgacgcatatgccgaggcaatca3’) (Supplementary 1). The remaining 1000 seedlings were transplanted into the Institute’s experimental field (IARI, New Delhi, 28°38’30"N 77°10’15"E, 246 m) to allow natural mite infestation where mite attack is common. Typical mite-infested symptoms were confirmed after comparison with the symptoms explained by the authors in their experimental setup [16, 47, 49].

At 45 days after transplanting RGB images for healthy plants, virus infection and mite attacks were taken from a fixed height (40 cm from the top of the canopy) via a Motorola edge 40 neo smartphone (designed and manufactured by Motorola Mobility LLC). The phone was equipped with a 50 MP camera capable of 2160p resolution and a f/1.8 wide aperture. The generated images were 3072 × 4096 pixels in size. Approximately 705 healthy, 812 virus-infected, and 758 mite-infected images were taken. Mite infestation typically causes downwards curling of leaves, petiole elongation, necrosis, and overall stunting, with leaves often becoming smaller and malforming. In contrast, virus infection is characterized by irregular curling and plant stunting but lacks the necrosis and petiole elongation typical of mite damage.

Data processing and augmentation

The images were cropped appropriately to avoid model interference due to the presence of multiple targets in the images [50, 51]. The initial dataset contained 2275 images for 3 classes: healthy, virus-infected and mite attacks. To increase the size of the dataset, the following augmentations were applied: Through augmentation, random flipping, random rotation (± 20%), random translation, random brightness (± 20%), random zoom (up to 0.2), random blur, and conversion to grayscale (Fig. 1). the size of the dataset was increased to 10,939 images. All the images were then resized to a resolution of 224 × 224 pixels and split into 80:10:10 ratios for training, testing and validation, respectively. On the other hand for YOLO models and RF-DETR, the original dataset was annotated using the LabelImg tool in YOLO annotation format, with input images resized to 640 × 640 pixels.

Construction of a modified classification model for chili leaf curl detection

MobileNetV3 network model

MobileNetV3 is an enhanced lightweight convolutional neural network (CNN) featuring depthwise separable convolution consisting of two components: depthwise convolution and pointwise convolution (38). The depthwise convolution applies a single convolutional filter to each input channel, whereas the pointwise convolution combines the outputs of these channels via a 1x1 convolution kernel. Compared with standard convolutions, this approach significantly reduces computational complexity. It retains the depth wise separable convolution introduced in MobileNetV1 and the linear bottleneck residual structure from MobileNetV2, which improves performance while reducing computational costs. MobileNetV3 leverages neural architecture search (NAS) to optimize the global network structure and the NetAdapt algorithm to determine the optimal number of convolutional filters and channels, further enhancing model efficiency. Additionally, MobileNetV3 introduces two key innovations: the squeeze-and-excitation (SE) attention block and the h-swish activation function. The SE block assigns adaptive weights to feature maps during training, allowing the network to emphasize important feature channels and suppress less useful channels. In this study, for more accurate detection of the disease at the field level, an attempt was made to increase the performance of the base model with modifications and leverage the pretrained weights of ImageNet for faster training with minimal computational resources.

CA module with MobileNetV3_Small

The coordinate attention (CA) module enhances channel attention by integrating spatial coordinates and improving the network’s ability to capture long-range dependencies with low computational overhead [43]. Unlike traditional attention mechanisms, which collapse spatial dimensions into channel descriptors, CA preserves positional data via directional pooling, separately encoding height and width attention. It processes an input feature tensor X∈RC×H×W into an output Y of identical dimensions, maintaining the spatial sensitivity critical for tasks such as object localization. CA uses a dual-branch structure: feature maps are pooled along the height/width axes and then transformed via grouped convolution and batch normalization. The generated attention maps are split, normalized with a sigmoid function, and broadcast back to the original dimensions for targeted feature recalibration. By encoding spatial information directionally and using lightweight operations, CA balances precise contextual modelling with minimal computational cost, enabling real-time deployment in resource-limited settings. Unlike traditional attention mechanisms, CA uses heightwise and widthwise mean pooling to preserve positional data, which are concatenated and processed through a grouped convolution (groups = 32) with H-Swish activation and batch normalization applied once. The resulting features are split into separate height/width attention maps (generated by 1 × 1 convolutions with sigmoid activation), tiled to the original dimensions, and multiplied elementwise with the input (Fig. 2c). The intermediate channel dimension was reduced by a factor of r = 32 for efficiency.

CBAM module with MobileNetV3 small

The convolutional block attention module (CBAM) is a lightweight yet powerful attention mechanism that seamlessly integrates CNN architectures and consists of a channel attention module (CAM) and a spatial attention module (SAM) [46]. The channel attention module enhances feature representation by computing global max pooling and global average pooling along the spatial dimensions (height and width) to generate two one-dimensional channel descriptors. These descriptors are processed through a shared multilayer perceptron (MLP) with two fully connected layers, and their outputs are summed elementwise before a sigmoid activation function is applied. The resulting attention weights are multiplied elementwise with the original feature map to produce the refined feature map F′. The spatial attention module then refines F′ by applying global max pooling and global average pooling along the channel dimension, generating two separate spatial feature maps. These maps are concatenated along the channel dimension and passed through a 7 × 7 convolution to capture spatial dependencies. The output undergoes a sigmoid activation function, and the resulting attention weights are multiplied elementwise with F′, producing the final enhanced feature map F′′, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Squeeze and excitation coordinated attention

The squeeze-and-excitation coordinate attention (SCA) mechanism [52] is an enhanced version of the original coordinate attention (CA) approach that introduces several architectural improvements [43]. The key innovation lies in the channel attention module, where traditional single-path fully connected (FC) layers are replaced with dual parallel FC paths (fc1 and fc2). These independently process globally average-pooled features before their outputs are concatenated and fed through a final FC layer to generate channel weights. This design enhances cross-channel interactions while preserving efficiency through a bottleneck reduction ratio of 16. The spatial attention component undergoes similar refinement through a two-stage process. First, features are aggregated along the height and width dimensions via separate pooling operations. These pooled features are concatenated and processed through a grouped convolutional layer (groups = 32) with batch normalization and h-swish activation [38]. The resulting representations are split back into spatial components and further refined through dedicated convolutional layers with sigmoid activation to produce final attention weights.

The complete SCA mechanism synergistically combines these spatial and channel attention components. Spatial attention first emphasizes relevant regions through height- and width-specific weighting, whereas channel attention subsequently highlights semantically important features. This dual attention strategy enables the model to focus dynamically on both critical spatial locations and their channelwise significance, significantly enhancing feature discriminability (Fig. 4a). The h-swish activation function further optimizes computational efficiency, making the module particularly suitable for resource-constrained deployment scenarios.

Proposed MobileNetV3_Small model

This study systematically investigated the impact of attention mechanisms on MobileNetV3 architectures through targeted experimental configurations, exploring modifications for MobileNetV3-Small. Distinct attention mechanisms, including the convolutional block attention module (CBAM), standard squeeze-and-excitation (SE), coordinate attention (CA), and squeeze-and-excitation coordinate attention (SCA), are tested on the final feature map, ensuring that each configuration is mutually exclusive to isolate its individual effects [36, 53]. In one experiment, the MobileNetV3 architecture was enhanced by integrating four squeeze-and-excitation coordinate attention (SCA) modules at distinct stages of the backbone network, forming the proposed SCA-MobiPlant model.

These modules were strategically placed to handle feature extraction at different levels of abstraction: the early stage (after the first bottleneck block, 56 × 56 × 16 features were produced), the middle stage (after the third bottleneck block, 28 × 28 × 40), the late stage (after the sixth bottleneck block, 14 × 14 × 48), and an additional stage (after the final bottleneck block, 7 × 7 × 96). This multistage placement enables hierarchical feature fusion while preserving spatial and channelwise relationships. For the early and middle stages, the feature maps underwent downscaling via separate convolutional layers with strides of s = 4 (early stage) and s = 2 (middle stage), reducing their spatial dimensions to match the late-stage resolution (14 × 14). The late-stage features were processed directly, while the output of the additional stage was upsampled before fusion. All resized features—downscaled early (14 × 14 × 48) and middle-stage outputs (14 × 14 × 48), original late-stage (14 × 14 × 48), and upsampled additional-stage (14 × 14 × 96) features—were concatenated to form a unified multiscale representation (Fig. 4b).

The training process employed the Adam optimizer and experimented with different loss functions to refine the optimization strategy. Through these experiments, the impact of SCA modules on feature extraction and their role in enhancing MobileNetV3 performance were evaluated. This systematic approach allows us to assess how multistage attention fusion improves hierarchical feature learning and prediction accuracy.

To evaluate the computational efficiency and diagnostic performance of the final improved model, a comparative analysis was conducted against widely adopted architectures—ResNet50, EfficientNetB0, InceptionNetV3, VGG19, the advanced YOLO series (YOLOv8n, YOLOv9c and YOLOv10m), and transformer based RF-DETR —across key metrics: classification accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, training time, parameter count, and model size [53–56]. The models used pretrained weights for better convergence and the fine-tuning was made on training data set having images namely, healthy, virus infected and mite infested [26, 27, 57].

Selection of the loss function

The training process employed the Adam optimizer and systematically evaluated multiple loss functions—including categorical cross-entropy (CCE), sparse categorical cross-entropy (SCCE), and polynomial loss—to optimize feature fusion and classification performance. Sparse categorical cross-entropy (SCCE) is a loss function tailored for multiclass classification tasks where target labels are provided as integer indices rather than one-hot encoded vectors. The SCCE directly leverages sparse integer labels. This eliminates the computational overhead of converting labels to one-hot representations, making it memory efficient and scalable for large-scale datasets with high class counts. The SCCE is particularly advantageous in scenarios where memory constraints or real-time processing are critical, such as edge devices or drone-based monitoring systems. Empirical studies have shown that SCCE maintains comparable accuracy to CCE while reducing the number of preprocessing steps and computational complexity [58, 59]. For example, when training deep learning models such as EfficientNet or ResNet on the PlantVillage dataset, SCCE achieves convergence rates similar to those of CCE but with 20–30% lower memory usage during label handling [60, 61]. Polynomial loss (a generalized form of cross-entropy with adjustable class margins) was explored to mitigate potential class imbalances [32, 62].

Transfer learning

This study addresses the dual challenges of limited training data and computational constraints by optimizing a MobileNetV3 architecture for diagnosing the chili leaf curl complex. Transfer learning with pretrained ImageNet weights was leveraged to mitigate overfitting risks while accelerating model convergence [45]. This approach balances diagnostic precision with computational efficiency, enabling robust performance across diverse leaf morphologies and imaging conditions. Through calibrated fine-tuning of the MobileNetV3 model with domain-specific data, the developed framework is capable of detecting subtle symptomatic variations in chili leaves with high reliability via minimal resources [63]. To evaluate the training methodology impacts, comparative experiments were conducted with three different training conditions while using transfer learning: unfrozen (fully trainable) or frozen base model configurations and training from scratch.

Visual verification of the predicted classes

The gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) technique was used to provide visual explanations and understand which regions of an image are important for model prediction, especially for image classification and object detection [51, 64, 65]. Grad-CAM works by first performing a forward pass to obtain the feature maps from the last convolutional layer. Then, it computes the gradients of the target class score with respect to these feature maps. These gradients are globally averaged to obtain importance weights, which are then used to weight the feature maps. The weighted maps are summed and passed through a ReLU to highlight only the positive, class-relevant regions. Finally, the resulting heatmap was resized and overlaid on the input image to visualize where the model focused for its prediction.

Inference speed for edge deployment

The TFLite model format demonstrated exceptional performance, achieving an average inference time of 0.0112 s per image (≈ 89.04 FPS) on a mid-range smartphone, substantially outperforming the base MobileNetV3-Small (0.105 s/image; 9.54 FPS) and the modified Keras model (0.0714 s/image; 14.01 FPS). This enabled the final incidence module to process a set of unseen field-level images (as shown in the application interface, Fig. 5c) in ≈ 15–25 s, calculating the disease incidence result shown in Fig. 5e. .While YOLOv8n reported a faster single-image runtime (8.4 ms), it did so with noticeably lower mAP50. Heavier detection models — YOLOv9c (44.4 ms), YOLOv10m (24.9 ms), and transformer-based RF-DETR-N (55.0 ms) — although competitive in detection metrics, are impractical for smartphone deployment because of larger memory footprints and slower runtimes on mid-range devices. Integrating such object detectors into the disease incidence pipeline also requires additional steps for handling multiple detections per image, which increases computational cost and response time. TFLite’s speed advantage is attributable to its specialized optimizations for lightweight deployment (static graph execution and efficient memory management). Overall, the TFLite format (with SCA-MobiPlant) offers the best trade-off between accuracy and real-time feasibility on resource-constrained mobile devices.

Discussion

Accurate assessment of leaf curl disease in chili involves a series of tasks including detection, identification, categorization, and quantification—a tedious process that is manually impractical for large farms. The use of artificial intelligence algorithms and computer vision technologies offers the potential to perform all these tasks in an automated framework, thereby simplifying the assessment process. This study demonstrates the significant impact of attention mechanisms on lightweight convolutional neural networks for diagnostic classification of leaf curl complex. Integration of spatial attention modules after CNN layers facilitates feature learning by assigning higher weights to diseased regions and aligning local part features with global representations [35, 36, 39]. The proposed model focuses on important diseased areas in leaves and employs an attention weighting scheme for utilizing useful neighbourhood information. The integration of squeeze and excitation coordinated attention (SCA) with MobileNetV3-Small markedly improved performance from a baseline accuracy of 94.16% to an exceptional accuracy of 99.64% through multistage fusion implementation [35, 36, 39, 63].

Among the various attention mechanisms evaluated, SCA consistently outperforms other modules, including SE, CBAM, and CA, while maintaining a smaller parameter footprint. The progressive improvement observed from single-stage SCA (97.27%) to multistage SCA fusion (99.64%) validates the hierarchical feature integration approach. This strategic deployment of SCA modules at different network stages enabled effective capture of features at varying abstraction levels, resulting in more stable learning and better generalization capabilities, as evidenced by smooth convergence patterns in training curves.

The SCA-MobiPlant strikes an optimal balance between performance and efficiency, with only 0.68 million parameters—ideal for deployment in resource-constrained agricultural settings. Comparative analysis highlights this efficiency: although EfficientNetB0 achieved similar accuracy (98.36 ± 0.20%), its 5.36 million parameters introduce substantial computational overhead. ResNet50 also performed well (97.26 ± 0.24%) but requires 23.85 million parameters. In contrast, MobileNetV2 (72.08% ± 0.45%) and InceptionNetV3 (69.18% ± 0.45%) showed notably lower performance. MobileNetV2, while lightweight (2.26 million parameters), lacks attention mechanisms needed to capture subtle diagnostic cues—such as leaf texture, petiole elongation, and irregular curling—especially in complex real-world images where infected and healthy regions often coexist. InceptionNetV3, despite its larger capacity (22.85 million parameters), also struggles to generalize on such images due to overfitting. Both models exhibit high validation loss despite high training accuracy, reflected in reduced precision and recall. In contrast, SCA-MobiPlant, EfficientNetB0, and ResNet50 maintain low and stable validation loss, indicating strong generalization to unseen data.

Unlike classification models, YOLO and vision transformer-based object detectors operate at the object level by simultaneously predicting bounding box locations and class labels. Consequently, their precision, recall, and F1-scores are calculated across multiple objects within a single image, whereas classification models output only one label per image. This fundamental distinction makes classification approaches more straightforward for calculating disease incidence (DI), which primarily depends on the presence or absence of the pathogen rather than precise localization. In contrast, object detection requires detailed image annotation with bounding boxes, and overlapping or co-occurring symptoms further complicate the preparation of large-scale datasets.

Moreover, plant viral infections present unique challenges as symptoms often manifest predominantly on younger leaves at the top of the plant, while older leaves may appear healthy. This spatial heterogeneity complicates the aggregation of detection outputs into meaningful DI estimates, limiting the direct utility of object detection models for epidemiological calculations. Although transformer-based detectors such as RF-DETR achieve strong detection metrics, they rely on substantially larger parameter counts and computational requirements, often necessitating large-scale pretraining to reach peak performance. For field-focused, on-device applications where smartphones have limited RAM and storage constraints, lightweight CNNs with multistage attention mechanisms (SCA-MobiPlant) provide a more practical balance between accuracy and deployment feasibility. Therefore, while we benchmarked RF-DETR to represent transformer-based detectors, we prioritized developing a lightweight, deployable solution optimized for end-user applications.

The modified MobileNetV3-Small (SCA-MobiPlant), with 684,227 parameters, stands out as a robust and efficient model, offering a compelling trade-off between performance, size, and training time—well-suited for real-world agricultural deployment [55, 64].

Despite the model’s high accuracy, limitations include dataset constraints due to single-location data collection (IARI, New Delhi), which may not fully represent variations in lighting, climate, or complex field backgrounds. While data augmentation and the 224 × 224 resolution mitigate some noise, future work should expand multi-location data collection to enhance robustness across diverse environmental conditions.

The lack of prior reliable high-quality datasets and research on this topic limits the detection of this disease complex to molecular setups only [11, 15]. Compared to related deep learning studies on plant diseases, such as tomato leaf curl detection [21], corn leaf disease [34], SCA-MobiPlant achieves superior performance with a compact model size (0.68 million parameters), ideal for resource-constrained agricultural settings.

The Grad-CAM visualization results enhance model interpretability by revealing its focus on class-specific discriminative features: leaf surface color and texture for healthy samples, petiole elongation and downward curling for mite-affected samples, and irregular leaf curling for virus-infected samples. This alignment between the model’s attention patterns and known diagnostic criteria validates the classification approach and enhances user trust [57]. Similarly, the progressive fine-tuning approach in transfer learning has proven highly effective, allowing the model to retain general features while adapting to task-specific characteristics [34, 43].

The exceptional class-specific performance metrics—99.60 ± 0.12% accuracy for healthy samples, 99.40 ± 0.18% accuracy for mite-affected samples, and 99.70 ± 0.10% accuracy for virus-infected samples—demonstrated balanced effectiveness across all target classes, which is particularly noteworthy given potential similarities in symptom presentation between different stress factors in chili plants. Despite these promising results, limitations include potential dataset constraints in capturing the full spectrum of environmental conditions and incidence levels as based on the agro-climatic factor the degree of disease response could vary significantly.

To enhance practical utility, the optimized model was integrated into an Android-based application with an additional module to calculate disease incidence (DI) for both mite and virus classes. Field-level experimental testing of this application demonstrated its effectiveness in real-world agricultural settings with an inference time of 0.0112 s per image (89.04 FPS), providing farmers with accessible, on-the-spot disease diagnosis and incidence assessment capabilities [65]. Future work should focus on expanding the dataset, further optimizing the attention module configuration, and new approaches that handles the output from a complex object detection or segmentation models without compromising the performance. Integration with other sensing modalities could provide more comprehensive crop health monitoring capabilities [29].

Conclusion

For precise assessment of leaf curl complex in chili a modified MobileNetV3-Small with multistage SCA fusion is developed in order to facilitate an automated disease diagnosis framework. The model has shown a milestone accuracy while maintaining a compact model size, making it suitable for field applications. The successful integration of this model into an Android application with disease incidence assessment capabilities provides farmers and extension workers with a practical diagnostic tool, bridging the gap between deep learning research and field-level application. The study contributes a novel dataset that provides clear insight into the symptomatic differences between virus-induced curling and mite-induced curling. Use of the classification model simplifies the task of DI calculation without the burden of high computation. The system is optimized for integration with micro-computer and IoT devices such as Raspberry Pi, Orange Pi, and Jetson Nano to enable robot-based monitoring in commercial protected cultivation. The precision of the final model directly addresses misdiagnosis challenges, potentially reducing environmental hazards from inappropriate pesticide applications while optimizing intervention strategies and reducing crop losses. Further improvement refining overfitting issues in the model and semi-supervising learning approach may bring further adaptability in field application.
