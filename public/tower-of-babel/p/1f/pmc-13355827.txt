Comparisons of anxiety, depression, and general health and well-being in a gender diverse sample of LGBTQ+ adults
Authors: Ethan Moitra, Nicole A Gonzalez, Zachary J Kunicki, Natalie Fenn, Mary Kathryn Cancilliere, Trisha Arnold
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople who identify as LGBTQ+ report significantly higher rates of mental illness compared to cisgender, heterosexual populations. Transgender and non-binary (TNB) individuals show substantial mental health challenges when compared to non-LGBTQ+ people. However, few studies have explored TNB mental health compared to cisgender sexual minority individuals within a clinical LGBTQ+ sample.MethodThis observational study examined differences in anxiety, depression, general health and well-being, and psychiatric diagnoses in a sample of adults who identified as LGBTQ+ according to their self-reported gender identity (TNB vs. cisgender). The sample consisted of 100 TNB and 139 cisgender sexual minority adults (n = 239 total). Participants were in their early to mid-30s, mostly identified as non-Hispanic/Latino White, and were employed. Self-report measures of anxiety, depression, general health/well-being, and interviewer-administered measures of anxiety, depression, and psychiatric diagnoses were used.ResultsAfter adjusting for age, ethnicity, and race in an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model, compared to cisgender sexual minority individuals, participants who identified as TNB demonstrated significantly worse anxiety (self-report: d=0.33; interview: d=0.56), depression (self-report: d=0.27; interview: d=0.25), and general health/well-being (d=-0.34). TNB individuals were also significantly more likely to meet criteria for depressive or anxiety disorders (OR=2.19, 95%CI: 1.17, 4.08, p = .014).ConclusionsThis is among the first studies to compare mental health and well-being within a LGBTQ+ adult sample according to individuals’ gender identity. Results indicate that people who identify as TNB have a particularly high mental health burden, underscoring the importance of careful screening and tailored interventions for this subgroup.Keywords: LGBTQ+, Transgender and non-binary, Anxiety, Depression, Global health

Introduction

In the United States (U.S.), individuals who identify as lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer plus (LGBTQ+) demonstrate significantly higher rates of mental illness compared to cisgender, heterosexual populations [1]. However, the LGBTQ+ population is highly diverse and experiences of psychological distress can vary accordingly. For example, observational studies show that transgender and non-binary (TNB) adults (i.e., people whose gender identities do not align with cultural expectations of the gender associated with their sex assigned at birth) face a particularly high likelihood of mental health challenges throughout their lifetime [2], [3], [4]. According to one study, 44% of TNB adults reported a history of depression, 33% reported anxiety, and 40% reported overall psychological distress [5]. Eating disorders and substance misuse are also highly prevalent among TNB individuals [4], [6]. Unfortunately, up to 41% of this population attempts suicide each year, which is about 10 times higher than the U.S. general population [7] and two times higher than rates among lesbian, gay, and bisexual adults [8]. A similar proportion of TNB individuals engages in non-suicidal self-injury each year [9]. TNB individuals are 11% of the total LGBTQ+ community [10] and 2% of the U.S. population [11], representing 2.6 million adults living in the U.S. who experience a heightened mental health burden that might differ from others in the LGBTQ+ community.

A prevailing theory to explain the connection between identifying as TNB and mental health, and why TNB psychological distress could differ from others in the LGBTQ+ community, is the minority stress model [12], [13], [14]. According to this framework, exposure to stressors related to identifying as TNB limits resources and hinders coping abilities, which then negatively impacts mental and physical health. These stressors can be driven by structural (e.g., discriminatory policies), interpersonal (e.g., harassment), and individual factors (e.g., internalized stigma). These processes are not unique to TNB individuals; indeed, gay, lesbian, and bisexual individuals demonstrate worsened mental health due to minority stress exposure [15]. But, experiences of discrimination and prejudice are particularly common among TNB persons and are major drivers of symptoms [16], [17], undermining mental health treatment utilization [18]. In a large observational study comparing transgender to cisgender individuals, results showed that transgender individuals were significantly more likely to report experiences of discrimination and that this discrimination was associated with depression and suicide attempts [19]. The discrimination and systemic oppression experienced by > 80% of TNB individuals in the U.S. takes many forms, including bullying, denial of health care services, sexual and physical assault, and workplace harassment [20], [21], [22]. Lack of access to gender-affirming health care is associated with increased psychological distress, depression, anxiety, and suicidality among TNB individuals [23], [24]. According to the American Civil Liberties Union, there were at least 616 anti-LGBTQ+ bills pending legislation in the U.S. in 2025 [25], with many of these bills targeting the TNB community (e.g., bathroom bans, prohibiting discussions of gender in classrooms). In sum, within the LGBTQ+ community, individuals who identify as TNB demonstrate striking mental health needs, possibly making them the most vulnerable people amidst an already marginalized population due to the prevalent and unique forms of discrimination they face.

Despite data indicating that TNB individuals have pronounced mental health needs, as well as unique minority stressors, few studies have compared their mental health and overall well-being to other people from the LGBTQ+ community. Borgogna and colleagues [26] conducted the largest comparison of mental health between cisgender sexual minority-identifying individuals (e.g., gay men, lesbian women) and gender diverse TNB persons. Among 43,632 undergraduate and graduate college students, results showed that TNB individuals had significantly worse anxiety and depressive symptoms. Similar findings were reported in a large Finnish study of LGBTQ+ adolescents [27]. In a sample of 8589 adolescents, individuals who identified as TNB reported significantly greater anxiety and depressive symptoms compared to their cisgender sexual minority peers. Despite these important findings, both studies had limitations, including reliance solely on brief self-report measures and being conducted among albeit large, but non-clinical community samples. Consequently, further investigation of these differences is warranted, particularly among a broader sample of LGBTQ+ persons where prior findings could be strengthened with interviewer-assessed measures of symptoms and mental health diagnoses. According to numerous reviews, such as Eaton et al. [28], the use of interviewer-assessments of mental health symptoms are more rigorous, less prone to bias, and have better sensitivity and specificity than self-reports.

Thus, in the present study, we use self-report and interviewer data to compare anxiety, depression, general health and well-being, and Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders−5 (DSM−5;[29]) anxiety and depressive disorders diagnoses among a sample of 239 LGBTQ+ adults recruited from a primary care medical clinic. We hypothesized that compared to cisgender sexual minority individuals, participants who identified as TNB would demonstrate significantly higher anxiety and depressive symptoms. We also hypothesized that compared to cisgender sexual minority individuals, participants who identified as TNB would demonstrate significantly worse general health and well-being. Lastly, we hypothesized that TNB individuals would be significantly more likely to meet diagnostic criteria for one or more DSM−5 anxiety and/or depressive disorders compared to cisgender sexual minority participants.

Method

Overview of procedures

Participants in this study were recruited for a randomized controlled trial further discussed in Moitra et al. [30]. In the present study, only baseline data collected prior to randomization were used. Recruitment occurred at an outpatient primary care medical clinic in Providence, Rhode Island, which offers primary and sexual health care services. Recruitment occurred via flyers posted in clinical spaces, referrals from clinic providers, and electronic outreach to patients. After staff obtained informed consent by reviewing the form with potential participants, based on Brown University IRB-approved procedures (Protocol # 2022003397), participants completed a battery of self-report and interviewer-administered measures. Participants were compensated $50 for completing the baseline assessments.

Participants

Participants were 239 patients from the recruitment site, 100 of whom identified as TNB. One participant was excluded from analyses due to not reporting their gender identity. To increase external validity in the parent study, we set a minimal inclusion threshold of “mild” anxiety and/or depressive symptoms. Inclusion criteria were: (a) receive services at the recruitment site; (b) identify as LGBTQ+; (c) self-reported ≥ mild anxiety and/or depressive symptoms, based on the Generalized Anxiety Disorder−7 (GAD−7; [31]) scoring ≥ 5 and/or the Patient Health Questionnaire−9 (PHQ−9; [32]) scoring ≥ 5; (d) 18 years or older; (e) ability to speak and read English; and, (f) have telephone access. This study did not have any exclusion criteria, beyond not meeting the above criteria. One hundred eighty-six individuals who completed an online screener based on these criteria were not eligible for participation.

Measures

Demographics

A brief measure of demographics was used to characterize participants. In addition to reporting their age, ethnicity, and race, participants provided information on employment status, highest level of education, sex assigned at birth, and current gender and sexual identities. Individuals could report identifying with ≥ 1 gender identity. Participants were categorized as cisgender if their reported gender was “man” or “woman.” Participants were categorized as TNB if they self-identified as gender non-conforming, genderqueer, multigender, non-binary, transgender, or a combination of these identities.

Self-report

Generalized Anxiety Disorder – 7 (GAD−7; [31]). The GAD−7 is a brief 7-item measure used to screen for anxiety symptoms. It was specifically developed for use in primary care settings. Total scores range from 0 to 21, with 0–4 indicative of no or minimal anxiety, 5–9 indicative of mild anxiety, 10–14 indicative of moderate anxiety, and scores ≥ 15 indicating severe anxiety. The GAD−7 has shown strong reliability and validity in primary care [33]. In this study, the GAD−7’s reliability was good (α=.87).

Patient Health Questionnaire – 9 (PHQ−9; [32]). The PHQ−9 is a brief 9-item measure used to screen for depressive symptoms in primary care. Total scores range from 0 to 27, with 0–4 indicative of no or minimal depression, 5–9 indicative of mild depression, 10–14 indicative of moderate depression, 15–19 indicative of moderately severe depression, and scores ≥ 20 indicating severe depression. The PHQ−9 is widely used and consistently demonstrates good reliability and validity in primary care [34]. In this study, the PHQ−9’s reliability was good (α=.82).

PROMIS Global Health (PROMIS-GH; [35]). The 10-item PROMIS-GH questionnaire is a global measure of health and well-being, with items related to physical and mental health, as well as pain severity. Higher total scores indicate better overall health. The PROMIS-GH has shown strong psychometric properties [36]. Its reliability in this study was also good (α=.84).

Interviewer-administered

Two master’s level raters with public health backgrounds were trained to administer the following measures at baseline. Random reliability checks were conducted throughout the data collection process to maintain an ICC > 0.90. This entailed both raters coding the same interviews and then comparing results.

Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A; [37]). The HAM-A, a 14-item measure, yields a total score between 0 and 56, with 0–7 indicative of minimal or no anxiety, 8–17 indicative of mild anxiety, 18–24 indicative of mild to moderate anxiety, 25–30 indicative of moderate to severe anxiety, and scores ≥ 31 indicating severe anxiety. Administration lasts approximately 10–15 min.

Quick Inventory of Depression Symptoms – Clinician Rating scale (QIDS-C; [38]). The QIDS-C, a 16-item measure, yields a total score between 0 and 27, with 0–5 indicative of no depression, 6–10 indicative of mild depression, 11–15 indicative of moderate depression, and scores ≥ 16 indicating severe or very severe depression. Administration lasts approximately 10–15 min.

QuickSCID−5 [39]. The QuickSCID−5 is a fully structured diagnostic instrument that uses interview items adapted from the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM−5 (SCID−5; [40]). It was designed to be a shorter, more time-efficient version of the SCID by using closed-ended questions to assess the presence of DSM−5 diagnostic symptoms. For this study, only select mood and anxiety disorder modules were administered. We assessed current agoraphobia, generalized anxiety disorder, major depressive disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, panic disorder, and social anxiety disorder. In this study, QuickSCID−5 administration lasted 15–30 min. To further characterize severity of mental health symptoms, QuickSCID−5 data were dichotomized as Yes/No if participants met diagnostic criteria for ≥ 1 current diagnosis or not.

Data analytic plan

All analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 31). We generated descriptive statistics to summarize sample characteristics, comparing TNB to cisgender sexual minority individuals with t-test and χ2 analyses. Product-moment correlations were conducted to evaluate the unadjusted associations between measures of anxiety, depression, and global health and well-being. We used a one-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to estimate adjusted differences in measures of anxiety, depression, and global health and well-being (dependent variables) in relation to identifying as TNB (independent variable), controlling for age, ethnicity (Hispanic/Latino) and race (covariates). These covariates were selected given prior research showing that younger adults and individuals of color experience greater severity of anxiety and depressive symptoms [41], [42], [43]. Using the same covariates, we also developed a logistic regression model to determine the likelihood of meeting DSM−5 diagnostic criteria for at least one disorder in TNB vs. cisgender sexual minority participants.

Results

Demographic and diagnostic characteristics

Most participants (n = 197, 82%) were currently employed and reported completing at least some college education or more (n = 212, 89%). The overall sample was mostly non-Hispanic/Latino White (n = 184, 77%). Participants in both groups were in early to mid-adulthood, although the TNB sample (n = 100) was significantly younger than the non-TNB group (t(1) = 2.68, p = .006). Significantly more participants in the TNB cohort were assigned female at birth (TNB: n = 72, 72%; cisgender sexual minority: n = 50, 36%; t(1) = 38.61, p < .001). There was a wide range of reported sexual orientations in both groups, with pansexual / multi-orientation (39%) and queer (29%) being most common in the TNB cohort and gay or lesbian (60%) being most common in the cisgender sexual minority group. Mental health and well-being comparisons between TNB and cisgender sexual minority participants

Covariates used in the ANCOVA model were age, ethnicity (Hispanic/Latino identity), and race. None of these covariates emerged as being significantly related to the dependent continuous variables of anxiety, depression, or general health and well-being. Results from the ANCOVA analysis indicate significant differences in anxiety, depression, and general health and well-being for the TNB group compared to the cisgender sexual minority participants. Specifically, GAD−7 and HAM-A data show that the TNB group had statistically significantly worse anxiety symptoms than the cisgender group, with small to medium effect sizes (Cohen’s d=0.33, p = .01, and d=0.56, p < .001, respectively). Similar results with small effect sizes were found with the PHQ−9 and QIDS-C (Cohen’s d=0.27, p = .06, and d=0.25, p = .045, respectively). Although trending to statistical significance, the PHQ−9 effect is directionally consistent with our hypotheses. Lastly, after accounting for covariates, results indicated that TNB participants had significantly worse overall health and well-being compared to cisgender sexual minority participants (Cohen’s d=−0.34, p = .01). ANCOVA results with 95% Confidence Intervals are summarized in Table 3. After adjusting for age, ethnicity, and race in the logistic regression model, identifying as TNB was positively and significantly associated with meeting criteria for at least one DSM−5 diagnosis based on rater assessment (OR=2.19, 95%CI: 1.17, 4.08, p = .014).

Discussion

This study builds on prior literature suggesting that within the LGBTQ+ community, people who identify as transgender, non-binary, or other gender minority identities, are particularly at risk for significant mental health challenges. Consistent with our hypotheses, results indicated that TNB individuals had worse anxiety and depressive symptoms, based on self-report and interviewer assessments, worse general health and well-being, and were significantly more likely to meet DSM−5 diagnostic criteria for a depressive or anxiety disorder compared to cisgender sexual minority participants. As such, our results not only replicate prior findings but add rigor and generalizability. To our knowledge, this is the first study to include interviewer-assessed mental health measures when comparing TNB to other people in the LGBTQ+ community. Further, this is the first clinical community sample of adults in which these differences were examined. Additionally, this is the first study using DSM−5 diagnostic data when comparing these groups. Overall, results from this cross-sectional study show that TNB individuals have a higher mental health burden compared to cisgender people in the LGBTQ+ community who also report anxiety and/or mood concerns in primary care.

Unfortunately, even in the mental health field, transgender and non-binary identities have been historically stigmatized and it is likely that pathologizing the TNB identity or related aspects of gender incongruence with societal expectations might contribute to this group’s mental health challenges. It was only in 2018 that the World Health Organization declassified “transgender” as a mental health disorder. The latest iteration of the DSM no longer includes the diagnosis of gender identity disorder but it was a diagnosis in the DSM-IV [44]. However, the DSM−5 does include a gender dysphoria diagnosis. Diagnoses of gender identity disorder or gender dysphoria might reflect the friction between an individual’s self-identity compared to societal norms or expectations, thereby pathologizing being gender non-conforming [45]. Despite the change in terminology, gender dysphoria remains classified as a “disorder,” continuing the risk of pathologizing TNB identities [46]. Furthermore, the common requirement that TNB undergo multiple psychological evaluations prior to obtaining medical transitional treatment, such as gender-affirming surgery, might complicate the therapeutic relationship between TNB patients and mental health care providers. Clinicians often function as “gate keepers” to these additional services, which can exacerbate patients’ stigmatization fears [47]. Exposure to these minority stressors could contribute to worse mental health and well-being among TNB adults.

Indeed, the minority stress model suggests that stigma-related stressors contribute to psychological distress among marginalized groups, including people who identify as sexual and gender minorities [12], [14]. For TNB individuals, this can include misgendering, barriers to gender-affirming healthcare, and internalized transphobia [48]. These findings align with this theoretical perspective and suggest that severity of psychopathology can relate to social and structural conditions that shape mental health.

Limitations

Although this study has numerous strengths, its limitations should be acknowledged. The sample was sizeable (n = 239) but further replication in larger samples would add confidence to the findings. Moreover, replication in other geographic areas and clinical settings (e.g., outpatient psychiatry) would help generalize these results. We assessed the most common mood and anxiety disorders with the QuickSCID−5 [49]. But future studies would benefit from comparing other diagnoses, such as posttraumatic stress disorder and substance use disorders, given the prevalence of trauma exposure among TNB [50] and data suggesting TNB people might be particularly at risk for substance use [6]. Additionally, although diverse in terms of sexual and gender identities, this sample was mostly non-Hispanic/Latino White and well-educated. Given the additive effects of minority stressors, future studies should include a more diverse representation of people of color with a variety of socioeconomic and educational backgrounds. Participants who identified as TNB could report identifying with ≥ 1 gender identity (e.g., self-identify as gender queer and gender non-conforming). Given this approach, our data could not be used to further characterize outcomes in TNB subcategories. Future work would benefit from exploring differences in these subgroups as emerging data suggest that gender non-conforming identities, as well as assigned sex at birth, relate to mental health outcomes. Recently, Kirakosian and colleagues [51] showed that transgender women, non-binary individuals assigned male at birth, and non-binary individuals assigned female at birth had significantly higher odds of current suicidal ideation, even compared to other TNB identities (e.g., transgender man). Lastly, it is important to acknowledge that these data were collected from January 2023 to December 2024 – a period during which the U.S. experienced over 600 proposed anti-LGBTQ+ bills. TNB individuals were often targeted in public discourse during this period regarding bathroom use, inclusion in sports, and healthcare decision-making. Future studies should continue to evaluate the unique treatment needs of every individual while considering broader sociopolitical context.

Clinical implications

Although many clinical services have been designed for the treatment of gender and sexual minorities, these resources tend to treat LGBTQ+ and TNB adults as a homogenous identity, instead of recognizing the unique needs of distinct identities. This study was limited by its relatively well-educated and non-Hispanic/Latino White sample, meaning that conclusions should be carefully considered. Further, given the small to moderate effects sizes, results were highly context dependent. Therefore, this study illustrates the importance of careful screening for mental health symptoms among TNB patients, particularly those who might be similar to this sample or presenting for care in a primary care medical setting. Further, these results suggest that TNB, such as those enrolled in this study, would likely benefit from tailored interventions that are particularly responsive to the challenges associated with identifying as gender minority individuals.

Despite an overwhelming need for treatment, TNB individuals do not consistently utilize mental health care. For example, in one of the larger U.S. studies of TNB adults (n = 130), 52% of participants endorsed symptoms of psychological distress but had not received any mental health services in the past year [52]. Barriers to outpatient care included cost but also underscored the prevalence of discrimination against this group as many TNB avoided seeking care due to past negative experiences with providers and fear of further stigmatization. Multiple studies show the impact of negative mental health treatment experiences on symptom severity, treatment engagement and satisfaction, as well as willingness to seek future care [5], [53]. Interestingly, in addition to negative experiences in the mental health system influencing willingness to seek care, data also show that many TNB persons do not seek treatment because they know someone who had a bad experience [52]. This suggests that peer support, referral, or testimonial could influence some TNB to seek care. Overall, accessing and engaging in evidence-based care is acutely challenging for TNB adults. Therefore, creative solutions that are informed by service users (i.e., TNB individuals) are needed now more than ever, with particular attention to novel pathways to encourage treatment access and engagement. Lastly, TNB individuals may benefit from revisions to the gender dysphoria diagnosis to reduce the pathologization of gender nonconformity.
