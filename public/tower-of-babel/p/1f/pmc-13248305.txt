White matter microstructural alterations in episodic cluster headache outside attacks: a tract-based spatial statistics study
Authors: Francesca Caramia, Antonio Di Renzo, Giada Giuliani, Davide Chiffi, Cherubino Di Lorenzo, Alessio Colangelo, Alessandro De Maio, Luigi Della Volpe, Marco Fiorelli, Giulia Di Stefano, Marta Altieri, Gianluca Coppola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCluster headache (CH) is a highly disabling primary headache disorder increasingly conceptualized as a disorder of distributed brain networks rather than an isolated trigeminovascular dysfunction. While structural and functional MRI studies have demonstrated persistent brain alterations outside the ictal phase, the role of white matter microstructural changes supporting network-level dysfunction remains incompletely understood.MethodsWe investigated cerebral white matter microstructure in 27 patients with episodic CH examined during the bout outside active attacks using diffusion tensor imaging and tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS). Fractional anisotropy (FA), axial diffusivity (AD), mean diffusivity (MD), and radial diffusivity (RD) were compared between patients and healthy controls (N = 20) on a whole-brain white matter skeleton. Associations between diffusion metrics and clinical variables were explored using an ANCOVA-based TBSS framework.ResultsCompared with controls, patients with episodic CH showed widespread white matter microstructural alterations, characterized by reduced FA and AD and increased MD in multiple clusters involving long-range associative and projection pathways connecting frontal, parietal, temporal, subcortical, and cerebellar regions. No significant associations were observed between diffusion metrics and clinical variables, including disease duration, attack frequency, and pain intensity.DiscussionWhite matter alterations predominantly affected pathways supporting large-scale pain-related and cognitive–affective networks, including fronto-parietal, thalamo-cortical, and cerebello-thalamo-cortical connections. These findings are consistent with a network-based model of CH and integrate coherently with recent multimodal MRI evidence of cortical thickness and functional connectivity alterations observed outside attacks in a largely overlapping cohort.ConclusionsOur results provide evidence of persistent white matter microstructural abnormalities in episodic CH outside the ictal phase, supporting the concept of CH as a disorder of distributed brain networks. White matter alterations may represent a structural substrate underlying enduring network dysfunction predisposing patients to recurrent attacks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02396-x.

Introduction

Cluster headache (CH) is a primary headache disorder characterized by recurrent attacks of excruciating unilateral pain, typically accompanied by prominent cranial autonomic symptoms. It is considered one of the most disabling pain conditions, with a substantial impact on quality of life and daily functioning. CH is classically divided into episodic and chronic forms, with episodic cluster headache (eCH) representing the most common presentation and defined by the alternation of active bout periods and pain-free remissions [1].

Although historically interpreted as a disorder primarily involving the trigeminovascular system, accumulating evidence supports a more complex pathophysiological model in which CH is conceptualized as a disorder of distributed brain networks. Neurobiological models now integrate the hypothalamus, brainstem nuclei, thalamus, cerebellum, and widespread cortical regions involved in pain processing, salience attribution, autonomic regulation, and circadian rhythms [2, 3].

Structural and functional neuroimaging studies have substantially contributed to this network-based framework. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) investigations have consistently demonstrated abnormalities involving subcortical and cortical regions, including the hypothalamus, thalamus, cerebellum, frontal and parietal cortices, as well as large-scale functional networks such as the salience and default mode networks [4–11]. Importantly, several studies have reported that these alterations are detectable not only during the bout but also during the remission phase, suggesting the presence of persistent, trait-like brain changes in CH [4, 12–15].

Within this network perspective, the integrity of white matter pathways is critical for effective communication between cortical and subcortical nodes. White matter tracts provide the structural substrate supporting large-scale brain networks, and alterations in their microstructural organization may contribute to dysfunctional information integration across pain-related systems. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) offers a unique opportunity to investigate white matter microstructure in vivo by characterizing the directionality and magnitude of water diffusion along axonal pathways. Commonly used diffusion metrics, such as fractional anisotropy (FA), axial diffusivity (AD), and mean diffusivity (MD), are sensitive to changes in axonal coherence, organization, and tissue integrity [8, 16].

Previous DTI studies in CH have reported widespread white matter abnormalities, including reduced FA and increased diffusivity across associative, projection, and cerebellar pathways [8, 16–18]. These findings support the hypothesis that CH is associated with diffuse microstructural alterations rather than focal tract-specific damage. More recent diffusion-based investigations have further reinforced this view, suggesting that white matter involvement extends across multiple networks related to pain modulation, sensorimotor integration, and autonomic regulation [11, 19, 20].

Despite these advances, several limitations characterize the existing DTI literature in CH. Many studies have pooled episodic and chronic forms or have not systematically distinguished between in-bout and out-of-bout phases, complicating the interpretation of whether observed abnormalities reflect transient pain-related changes or stable disease traits [4, 11, 13, 17]. Moreover, methodological heterogeneity—particularly the use of MRI scanners with different field strengths and of different region-of-interest–based approaches rather than whole-brain frameworks—has limited comparability across studies and may have underestimated the extent of network-level alterations [11, 16, 18, 19]. Finally, associations between diffusion metrics and clinical variables such as disease duration, attack frequency, or pain intensity have yielded inconsistent or null findings, leaving their clinical significance unresolved [6, 8, 18, 19].

Against this background, the present study aimed to investigate white matter microstructural alterations in patients with eCH examined outside attacks using a whole-brain tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) approach. By focusing on diffusion metrics consistently reported in prior work—fractional anisotropy, axial diffusivity, and mean diffusivity—we sought to characterize persistent microstructural changes in cerebral white matter tracts and to explore their relationship with key clinical features. We hypothesized that patients with eCH outside attacks would exhibit widespread white matter microstructural alterations reflecting enduring, trait-related network dysfunction.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-seven patients with eCH (ICHD III code 3.1.1 [1]) attending the Headache Centers of Rome and Latina were recruited. Patients were scanned during the bout period but outside active headache attacks. Other primary or secondary headache types were excluded by clinical and/or instrumental evaluation, as appropriate. In particular, patients and controls with any additional headache disorder, including low-frequency episodic tension-type headache, were not included. We collected information on various patient clinical characteristics at the time of either the screening visit or the day of the scanning session, including daily attack frequency, mean severity of headache attacks (0–10), duration of the attacks (minutes), and duration of history of eCH (years). The exclusion criteria were severe systemic, neurological, neuro-ophthalmological, or psychiatric disorders that could reasonably affect brain structure, white matter microstructure, or the reliability of MRI assessment. Mild and clinically stable comorbidities without known central nervous system involvement were not considered exclusionary. Patients with eCH and a family history of migraine (first-degree relatives) were also excluded. No preventive drugs were permitted during the 3 months preceding the observations in eCH. For comparison, 20 healthy controls (HCs) of comparable age and sex distributions were recruited. They were devoid of any overt medical conditions, personal or family history of primary headaches or epilepsy, or regular drug intake. None of the enrolled participants experienced sleep deprivation or alcohol ingestion on the day preceding the recordings. Caffeinated beverages were not allowed on the day of the recording. All the participants provided written informed consent to participate in the study, which was approved by the local ethics committee (N° 0295/2023). The study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for Human Experimentation.

MRI data acquisition

All participants were scanned at the Umberto I Hospital MR Research Center, Sapienza University of Rome (Italy), using a 3T Verio Siemens scanner equipped with a 12-channel head coil. MRI acquisition parameters were identical to those used in our previously published work [6]. Structural anatomical images were acquired using a T1-weighted sagittal magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 1900 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.93 ms, 176 sagittal slices, voxel size 0.508 × 0.508 × 1 mm³. In addition, an interleaved double-echo Turbo Spin Echo (TSE) sequence was acquired to obtain proton density (PD) and T2-weighted images, with the following parameters: TR = 3320 ms, TE = 10/103 ms, matrix = 384 × 384, field of view (FOV) = 220 mm, slice thickness = 4 mm, interslice gap = 1.2 mm, 50 axial slices.

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data were acquired using a single-shot echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with the following parameters: TR = 12,200 ms, TE = 94 ms, FOV = 192 × 192 mm, matrix = 96 × 96, in-plane resolution 2 × 2 mm, slice thickness = 2 mm, and 72 contiguous axial slices with no gap. Diffusion weighting was applied along 30 non-collinear directions with a b-value of 1000 s/mm², together with one non-diffusion-weighted (b = 0 s/mm²) volume acquired in the anterior-to-posterior (AP) phase-encoding direction, resulting in 31 AP volumes. Additionally, one b = 0 s/mm² volume was acquired in the posterior-to-anterior (PA) phase-encoding direction for distortion correction.

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS)

All DTI datasets were visually inspected by two expert neuroradiologists (F.C. and M.F.) to identify motion artefacts, signal dropout, or cardiac pulsation effects. Preprocessing was carried out using the FMRIB Software Library (FSL, version 6.0.7; https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl) [21–23].

The topup tool was applied to correct susceptibility-induced distortions using paired b = 0 volumes acquired in the AP and PA phase-encoding directions [24]. Brain extraction was performed using BET [25]. Motion and eddy current–induced distortions were corrected using eddy [26] with outlier detection and replacement (--repol), slice-to-volume motion correction (--mporder = 6), susceptibility-by-motion modelling (--estimate_move_by_susceptibility), and a linear second-level model for eddy-current field variation (--slm=linear).

Quality control was performed according to the framework proposed by Bastiani et al. [27], using eddy-derived subject-level metrics together with visual inspection. Specifically, we evaluated mean absolute RMS motion, mean relative RMS motion, the percentage of volumes with relative RMS motion > 1 mm, the percentage of outlier slices identified and replaced by --repol, and the variability of eddy-current-induced distortions, expressed as the standard deviation of the linear EC terms in the x, y, and z directions.

Study-wise QC distributions were generated using the FSL eddy_quad/eddy_squad framework. In accordance with this framework, values exceeding two standard deviations from the study-wise mean were flagged as potential QC outliers and underwent further visual inspection of the corrected diffusion images, residual distortions, signal dropout, and eddy-derived motion parameters. Quantitative QC metrics were compared between groups using two-sample t-tests. These distribution-based thresholds were used as quantitative warning thresholds rather than as automatic exclusion criteria. Final dataset exclusion was predefined in the presence of failed topup/eddy correction, incomplete diffusion acquisition, gross residual geometric distortion, severe residual signal dropout not adequately corrected by --repol, or visually evident residual motion artefacts affecting image interpretability.

Lastly, the DTIFIT toolbox was used to fit the diffusion tensor model and to derive fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD). Voxel-wise statistical analysis of FA was performed using TBSS [28]. FA images were nonlinearly registered to a 1 mm isotropic FMRIB58_FA template using FNIRT [29, 30]. A mean FA image was computed, skeletonized (FA threshold > 0.2), and individual FA maps were projected onto the common skeleton; the same procedure was used for MD, AD, and RD analyses.

Voxel-wise group comparisons for FA, AD, MD, and RD were performed within the TBSS framework using FSL randomise (version 2.9), with 10,000 permutations, including age and sex as covariates. Associations between diffusion metrics and clinical variables (disease duration, attack frequency, attack duration, and VAS score) were assessed using the same TBSS/randomise framework, specifying a general linear model with age as a covariate and sex as a factor. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, family-wise error corrected using threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE).

Descriptive and inferential statistics for demographic and clinical variables were performed using SPSS version 30.0.0.

Diffusion tensor imaging (TBSS)

Tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) revealed widespread alterations in white matter microstructure in patients compared with HCs, involving multiple association, projection, and cerebellar pathways.

Fractional anisotropy

Compared with healthy controls, patients with eCH showed significantly reduced FA in posterior and lateral white matter regions and involved long-range association tracts, as illustrated in Fig. 1a–c. In particular FA reductions were observed predominantly in seven small clusters involving mainly left posterior associative pathways, including the optic radiation, retrolenticular internal capsule, inferior longitudinal fasciculus, sagittal stratum, and posterior thalamic radiation, with TFCE corrected p-values ranging from 0.046 to 0.050 (Fig. 1; Table 3).

MD was increased in ten clusters, with the largest clusters involving the left middle cerebellar peduncle and right fronto-subcortical associative pathways including the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, external capsule, superior longitudinal fasciculus, and anterior thalamic radiation, with TFCE corrected p-values ranging from 0.038 to 0.050.

Overall, the combined pattern of reduced FA and AD together with increased MD suggests a diffuse microstructural white matter alteration in patients, affecting both supratentorial and infratentorial networks.

Radial diffusivity

Group comparisons for radial diffusivity (RD) did not reveal any statistically significant alterations.

Clinical correlations

TBSS analyses based on the ANCOVA model did not reveal any significant associations between clinical history and diffusion tensor metrics (FA, AD, MD or RD). Similarly, no significant relationships were observed between the number of attacks and any of the investigated diffusion measures. Furthermore, disease duration was not significantly associated with FA, AD, or MD, and RD across the white matter skeleton.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated white matter (WM) microstructural alterations in patients with episodic cluster headache (eCH) examined during the bout period but outside active attacks using a whole-brain TBSS approach. The main findings can be summarized as follows: compared with healthy controls, patients showed reduced fractional anisotropy (FA) and axial diffusivity (AD), together with increased mean diffusivity (MD), across multiple cerebral WM tracts, whereas no significant differences were observed in radial diffusivity (RD). These abnormalities were spatially distributed and involved associative, projection, and cerebellar pathways, suggesting diffuse microstructural reorganization rather than focal tract damage. No significant associations were observed between diffusion metrics and clinical variables.

Reduced FA combined with increased MD is generally interpreted as reflecting decreased microstructural coherence and increased extracellular water diffusion, consistent with altered axonal organization or packing density [31]. The concurrent reduction in axial diffusivity observed in the present study further suggests involvement of axonal integrity or orientation coherence rather than purely myelin-related changes [16–19]. At the same time, these diffusion measures remain indirect markers of tissue architecture and should not be interpreted as specific histopathological signatures.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of previous diffusion studies in cluster headache, which have yielded partly heterogeneous results. Teepker et al. (2012) first reported reduced FA and increased diffusivity in eCH outside attacks, suggesting that WM abnormalities may persist beyond the pain paroxysm itself [16]. Longitudinal data from Chou et al. (2014) further indicated that many white matter abnormalities detected during the in-bout phase remain present during the out-of-bout phase, supporting the view that these changes may reflect a relatively stable component of the disorder rather than a purely ictal phenomenon [17]. More recent PSMD-based work by Kim et al. (2025) also supported diffuse WM involvement and found no significant hemispheric differences, reinforcing the concept of non-focal and non-strictly lateralized white matter abnormalities in cluster headache [19].

At the same time, previous whole-brain structural studies have not been entirely concordant. Absinta et al. (2012) reported no significant TBSS abnormalities of the WM skeleton in cluster headache patients studied in a strictly headache-free condition, but found reduced grey matter volume in several pain-matrix regions, including the thalamus, posterior cingulate cortex, middle frontal gyrus, inferior parietal lobule, caudate, and insula [32]. By contrast, Szabó et al. (2013) described widespread WM abnormalities with reduced FA and increased mean, axial, and perpendicular diffusivity across frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital pathways, together with a significant relationship between axial diffusivity and cumulative headache days [18]. Our results are overall more consistent with this latter pattern of diffuse white matter involvement, while also extending the evidence provided by Absinta et al., suggesting that cluster headache affects not only pain-related grey matter regions but also the structural pathways interconnecting them.

Differences across studies should, however, be interpreted cautiously. The discrepancy with Absinta et al. (2012) may partly reflect differences in clinical phase, since their patients were studied in a more strictly headache-free condition, whereas our cohort was examined during the active bout but outside individual attacks. Divergences from Szabó et al. (2013), particularly in the direction of axial and radial diffusivity changes, may reflect more specific methodological and clinical differences between the two studies. Szabó et al. used a 1.5T scanner with 60 diffusion directions, mirrored images according to the symptomatic side, calculated laterality indices, and explored cumulative headache days as their main burden variable. By contrast, our study was performed on a clinically homogeneous eCH cohort examined during the active bout but outside attacks, did not use image flipping, and modeled disease duration, attack frequency, attack duration, and pain intensity. In addition, our cohort was larger than those included in these previous whole-brain studies, which may have provided a more stable estimate of the white matter abnormalities detectable in episodic cluster headache. Overall, the most robust point of convergence across studies is not the direction of each individual diffusion metric, but the consistent indication that structural abnormalities in cluster headache extend beyond a focal pain generator toward distributed pain-related networks.

Thus, the added value of the present study does not lie in the use of TBSS per se, which has already been applied in cluster headache, but rather in the characterization of WM abnormalities in a clinically homogeneous cohort of patients with episodic cluster headache studied during the bout but outside attacks, and in the opportunity to define more clearly the anatomical distribution of the affected pathways in this specific disease phase. Our whole-brain TBSS approach also allowed us to characterize these alterations at the skeleton level, reducing partial volume effects and increasing sensitivity to subtle microstructural changes compared with earlier region-of-interest–based strategies.

From a pathophysiological perspective, the diffuse pattern of WM abnormalities observed here aligns with contemporary models conceptualizing cluster headache as a disorder of dysfunctional large-scale brain networks rather than isolated pain pathways [2, 11]. Structural and functional neuroimaging studies increasingly implicate thalamic, hypothalamic, cerebellar, limbic, and associative cortical systems in cluster headache, and WM pathways provide the structural scaffold enabling communication among these regions. Within this framework, altered WM microstructure may contribute to abnormal integration between pain-processing, autonomic, salience-related, and cognitive-affective systems [6, 8, 11, 13, 17, 18, 33, 34].

This network-based interpretation is further supported by comparison with migraine. Neuroimaging studies in migraine have also shown structural and functional abnormalities involving thalamic, hypothalamic, cerebellar, and pain-processing cortical regions, and DTI studies have highlighted dynamic thalamic and hypothalamic changes across the interictal and ictal phases. In particular, migraine has been associated with higher thalamic FA and lower thalamic MD during the interictal phase, with normalization during the pain phase, as well as increased hypothalamic mean, axial, and radial diffusivity during spontaneous attacks [35–39]. By contrast, our findings, together with previous DTI studies in cluster headache, support a pattern of more persistent WM involvement outside attacks, affecting not only thalamo-cortical systems but also long-range associative, limbic, and cerebello-thalamo-cortical pathways. Thus, although migraine and cluster headache likely share abnormalities within pain-related networks, the spatial distribution and temporal profile of white matter changes do not appear to be fully overlapping.

Recent multimodal 7T MRI evidence further supports this view. Zhang et al. (2026), in a direct comparison between episodic cluster headache and migraine, found that cluster headache patients showed increased fiber number between the right lentiform nucleus and the right cerebellum, together with generally reduced fiber number among cerebellar regions. Although these measures are not directly comparable with voxel-wise TBSS-derived diffusion metrics, they support the idea that cerebellar and subcortical structural organization may contribute to differentiating cluster headache from migraine [40].

A broader comparison with chronic pain conditions also supports the present interpretation. Diffusion abnormalities characterized by reduced anisotropy and/or increased diffusivity have been reported in chronic neck pain, fibromyalgia, and neuropathic pain, often involving the corpus callosum, corona radiata, internal capsule, superior longitudinal fasciculus, thalamic pathways, cingulum, or other tracts linked to sensory-discriminative and cognitive-affective pain processing. However, the anatomical distribution and clinical correlates of these alterations vary across disorders [41–47]. This suggests that widespread WM involvement may represent a shared feature of chronic pain states, whereas the prominence in our cohort of cerebello-thalamo-cortical, associative, and limbic-related pathways may be particularly relevant to cluster headache.

At a more anatomical level, the distribution of abnormalities across diffusion metrics provides additional insight into the organization of these network alterations. FA reductions were predominantly observed in left posterior associative pathways, including the inferior longitudinal fasciculus and posterior thalamic radiations, structures implicated in multimodal sensory integration and visuo-limbic processing [48–50]. In contrast, AD reductions were bilateral and mainly involved limbic and cerebellar pathways, such as the fornix and cerebello-cortical tracts, suggesting altered microstructural organization within circuits supporting affective regulation, autonomic integration, and sensorimotor processing [51, 52]. MD increases primarily affected right-lateralized fronto-parietal and fronto-thalamic associative pathways, including the superior longitudinal fasciculus and anterior thalamic radiation, which are key components of networks involved in salience attribution and cognitive control [53–55]. The predominance of long-range associative and projection pathways connecting frontal, parietal, temporal, subcortical, and cerebellar regions further supports altered large-scale integration. In particular, fronto-parietal and fronto-temporal connections compatible with the superior longitudinal fasciculus and fronto-occipital pathways may contribute to dysfunctional attentional control and top–down modulation of nociceptive information [54, 56, 57].

These tract-level findings also fit well with our previous multimodal MRI results obtained in a largely overlapping cohort, in which we demonstrated cortical thinning in frontal associative regions together with altered resting-state functional connectivity within salience and cognitive-control networks [3]. The present TBSS findings therefore provide complementary structural evidence, suggesting that cortical and functional alterations may be supported by disrupted white matter pathways linking these regions. Taken together, our diffusion results and previous multimodal findings point to a coherent picture of eCH involving interconnected frontal, thalamic, hypothalamic, and cerebellar systems [3].

Interpretation of the observed asymmetry nevertheless requires caution. Although pain side was available and approximately balanced across patients, we did not perform a formal subgroup analysis according to pain laterality because the sample size was not sufficient for a reliable stratified TBSS comparison. Therefore, the observed asymmetry should not be interpreted as evidence of a simple ipsilateral or contralateral effect. Rather, it may reflect differential vulnerability of distributed associative systems, a view that is also compatible with the absence of hemispheric differences reported in recent PSMD-based work.

Finally, no significant associations were found between diffusion metrics and clinical variables, including disease duration, attack frequency, or pain intensity. Although this may initially appear counterintuitive, similar negative or weak findings have been reported in previous studies, and weak or inconsistent clinic-radiological correlations are not uncommon in diffusion studies of chronic pain [8, 17]. These converging observations suggest that WM microstructural alterations may represent trait-like characteristics or enduring network abnormalities rather than markers linearly proportional to current clinical severity. Alternatively, diffusion changes may reflect complex, nonlinear biological processes not fully captured by standard clinical metrics. In this framework, the absence of significant clinical correlations should not be interpreted as evidence against biological relevance, but rather as support for a network-level model of disease vulnerability.

In summary, the present study provides a clinically focused characterization of white matter microstructural abnormalities in a homogeneous cohort of patients with episodic cluster headache examined during the bout but outside attacks, extending and refining previous diffusion evidence in this condition.

The overall pattern of reduced FA and AD with increased MD across associative, thalamo-cortical, limbic, and cerebello-thalamo-cortical pathways further supports the concept of cluster headache as a disorder of distributed brain networks and strengthens the interpretation of white matter abnormalities as part of a broader structural and functional reorganization in this condition.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the temporal evolution of WM alterations. Longitudinal studies integrating diffusion, structural, and functional imaging across bout and remission periods would be valuable to further disentangle trait and state components. Second, although we examined multiple diffusion metrics, DTI remains an indirect measure of tissue microstructure; future studies employing advanced diffusion models may provide more specific insights. Finally, the integration of detailed clinical and behavioral measures may help clarify the functional significance of WM alterations.

An additional limitation is that smoking status was not available in a form suitable for formal covariate analysis, although smoking may influence white matter microstructure and is particularly relevant in cluster headache populations. Furthermore, we did not perform stratified analyses according to pain laterality because the sample size was not sufficient to support reliable subgroup TBSS comparisons.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this TBSS study shows widespread WM microstructural alterations in patients with eCH examined in-bout outside attacks, characterized by reduced FA and axial diffusivity and increased mean diffusivity. These findings support persistent structural reorganization within distributed brain systems, consistent with contemporary network-based models of CH, and suggest that white matter microstructural alterations may represent enduring trait markers of the disorder rather than purely state-dependent phenomena.
