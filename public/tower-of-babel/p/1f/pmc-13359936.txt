Load–velocity relationship during chain-based variable resistance in the smith machine back squat
Authors: Zhuorong Huang, Jie Li, Zhaoqian Li, Xing Zhang, Ruizhi Liu, Zongwei Chen, Xiuli Zhang, Amador García-Ramos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVelocity-based training (VBT) is well established for prescribing and monitoring intensity in traditional constant resistance training; however, its applicability to chain-based variable resistance training has not been examined. This study examined how different magnitudes of chain-based variable resistance influence commonly used VBT-derived metrics.MethodsThirty-four resistance-trained men (age: 20.50 ± 2.00 years, constant-load one-repetition maximum [1RM]: 1.85 ± 0.22 × body mass) performed Smith machine back squats under three loading configurations (0%, 20%, and 60% of the total external load supported by chains). Across six sessions (two per configuration), participants’ mean concentric velocity (MV) was assessed at 45%, 60%, 75%, and 90% of their constant-load 1RM. In three additional sessions (one per configuration), a subset of 21 men (age: 20.29 ± 1.76 years, constant-load 1RM: 1.91 ± 0.19 × body mass) performed four sets to failure at 70% of constant-load 1RM.ResultsThe individual load–MV relationships were highly linear across the three configurations (R2 ≥ 0.934). The terminal MV obtained in the sets to failure did not differ between configurations (≈ 0.35 m·s− 1; p = 0.849). Compared to the 0% and 20% configurations, the 60% configuration elicited greater MVs at 75% and 90% of constant-load 1RM (p ≤ 0.043), with no differences at lighter loads (p ≥ 0.340). Consequently, higher chain proportions were associated with higher predicted 1RMs (p < 0.001) and produced lower MVs at submaximal relative loads (up to 0.07 m·s− 1).ConclusionsAllocating a greater proportion of the load to chains enhances movement velocity at heavy loads and consequently increases the predicted 1RM during the Smith machine back squat. The preserved linearity of the load–MV relationship supports the use of VBT for prescribing and monitoring intensity under variable resistance training; however, the %1RM–MV relationship is configuration-specific and therefore requires tailored calibration according to the proportion of chain load used. Given the growing popularity of combining variable resistance with VBT, the importance of this study lies in providing the first empirical framework for prescribing and monitoring chain-based training intensity using individual %1RM–MV relationships.Keywords: Kinematic, Monitoring, Training intensity, Velocity-based training

Background

Variable resistance training (VRT) is implemented to manipulate the external resistance curve to elicit specific neuromuscular adaptations [1, 2]. While often categorized together, elastic bands and chains possess distinct mechanical characteristics: elastic bands provide non-inertial tension, whereas chains introduce a variable inertial load [3]. When comparing chain-based VRT against constant resistance training (CRT) under equated mean load conditions, total external work is identical, yet the spatial mass distribution differs. The reduced system mass at the concentric onset in VRT mechanically permits greater initial acceleration. However, in free-weight settings, this potential benefit is frequently negated by chain oscillation, which suppresses mean concentric velocity (MV) relative to CRT [4, 5]. By eliminating stability demands and constraining bar trajectory, the Smith machine may permit the reduced system mass at concentric onset to enhance acceleration through the sticking region, ultimately resulting in greater MV. Unfortunately, empirical evidence on chain-based VRT performed in a Smith machine remains scarce, despite growing evidence that machine-based training elicits adaptations comparable to those observed with free weights [6–8].

Velocity-based training (VBT) is well established for prescribing and monitoring intensity in traditional CRT [9]; however, its applicability to VRT is underexplored. Existing literature indicates that when VRT is performed at matched nominal relative loads (i.e., the percentage of one-repetition maximum [1RM], denoted as %1RM), elastic bands typically facilitate faster MVs, whereas chains generally result in slower MVs compared to CRT [5, 10]. Furthermore, the magnitude of these velocity differences may be influenced by the loads tested and the variable resistance ratio applied, with heavier loads and higher ratios exacerbating the discrepancies [5, 10]. While previous research establishes that the %1RM–MV relationship is highly specific to exercise variations (e.g., back squats performed with versus without a stretch-shortening cycle) [11, 12], it remains undetermined whether altering the resistance modality (i.e., CRT versus VRT) elicits similar model specificity. If VRT possesses a distinct %1RM–MV relationship, the divergent velocities observed at the same nominal %1RM in existing literature imply a mismatch with the actual %1RM [5, 10, 13]. Currently, the %1RM–MV relationship for VRT remains uninvestigated, limiting the ability of strength and conditioning practitioners to prescribe training intensity using velocity-derived metrics.

Previous research consistently reports near-perfect goodness-of-fit when applying the linear %1RM–MV relationship to CRT [11, 14, 15]. However, it remains undetermined whether the altered external resistance curve inherent to VRT compromises this linearity. Furthermore, establishing this relationship requires converting absolute testing loads (kg) into relative loads (%1RM) via a directly measured or predicted 1RM [11, 15]. Because assessing an actual 1RM under precise loading configurations (e.g., ensuring 60% of the total external load supported by chains) is technically complex, estimating a predicted 1RM from submaximal loads offers a pragmatic alternative. Yet, predicting a 1RM necessitates a minimum velocity threshold [16]. Due to the aforementioned complexity of direct 1RM testing, obtaining individual (i.e., obtained in a prior session through direct 1RM testing) or optimal (i.e., the velocity value that minimizes the discrepancy between the actual and predicted 1RMs within the same testing session) minimum velocity thresholds becomes highly impractical [16, 17]. Consequently, the group-averaged MV of the last successful repetition (MVlast) from submaximal sets performed to failure offers a viable alternative [18]. Supporting this approach, evidence shows that the magnitude of MVlast does not significantly differ across varying sets and sessions [18, 19], as well as exercise variations and training levels during CRT exercises [18]. However, it remains unclear whether the magnitude of MVlast is similarly unaffected by different loading configurations in VRT.

The primary objective of the current study was to examine how different magnitudes of chain-based VRT (0%, 20%, and 60% of the total external load supported by chains) influence commonly used VBT-derived metrics. Based on previous literature and the biomechanical characteristics of the Smith machine [10, 11, 20], we hypothesized that: (i) the individual load–MV relationships would remain highly linear across configurations; (ii) MVlast would not significantly differ among configurations; (iii) increasing the proportion of load supported by chains would produce higher MVs at the same absolute loads, with progressively greater differences as load increased; (iv) predicted 1RMs would increase as chain proportion increased; and (v) the %1RM–MV relationships would differ systematically between configurations, with greater chain proportions leading to progressively lower velocities as relative load decreased. By doing so, this study aimed to address the critical gap regarding the lack of training intensity monitoring models for chain-based VRT. Practically, it provided evidence-based load–velocity relationships to help practitioners predict 1RM and prescribe intensity during such exercises.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a randomized, repeated-measures crossover design nested within a broader 10-week, 20-session structured resistance training program. For the purposes of the current investigation, data from 15 experimental sessions were extracted and analyzed. The experimental timeline was divided into three distinct testing phases. Phase one (four sessions) was dedicated to familiarizing the participants with the experimental procedures and determining the individualized chain loads for the loading configurations. Phase two (seven sessions) was designed to establish the individual load–MV relationships. This phase commenced with a standardized constant-load 1RM assessment. Subsequently, submaximal load testing (45%, 60%, 75%, and 90% of the constant-load 1RM) was conducted over six sessions (twice per week). To prevent order effects, the three loading configurations (0%, 20%, and 60% chains) were performed in a randomized order generated by a random number sequence in Microsoft Excel, with each week dedicated to a single configuration. Phase three (four sessions) aimed to determine the group-averaged MVlast, used as the minimal velocity threshold for 1RM estimation. To account for training-induced strength adaptations, the third phase began with a second constant-load 1RM reassessment. Participants completed the repetitions-to-failure tests at 70% of the constant-load 1RM under the 0%, 20%, and 60% configurations across three sessions, which were also allocated in a randomized order. To ensure adequate recovery, consecutive experimental sessions were separated by 48 to 96 h. Furthermore, to minimize circadian and environmental variance, all testing sessions were supervised by the same investigator, conducted within the same general time of day (consistently in the morning, afternoon, or evening; ±3 h) in a university laboratory, and maintained under similar temperature (~ 20 °C) and relative humidity (~ 65%) conditions. To control for confounding variables, participants were instructed to maintain their regular dietary and sleep patterns, refrain from additional back squat training throughout the study period, avoid any high-intensity lower-body exercise for 48 h prior to testing, and abstain from caffeine and alcohol for 24 h before each session. This was actively reinforced through daily reminder messages sent by a researcher.

Participants

Thirty-nine resistance-trained men initially volunteered for this study. Inclusion criteria required: (i) ≥ 1 year of resistance training experience, performing back squats at least once per week; (ii) proper Smith machine back squat technique at maximal intended velocity; (iii) a constant-load 1RM of ≥ 1.5 times their body mass, alongside an absolute weight of ≥ 111 kg; and (iv) no reported musculoskeletal injuries in the past three months. This absolute strength threshold was required because the empty barbell (20 kg) constituted the minimum possible load for the lightest testing load (45% of the constant-load 1RM) under 60% configuration. During Phase two, one participant withdrew for personal reasons, and four were excluded for failing to meet the predetermined strength thresholds, leaving 34 participants for the Phase one and two data analysis. During Phase three, an additional 13 participants withdrew for personal reasons. Therefore, 21 participants were ultimately included in the Phase three data analysis (Fig. 1). Descriptive characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of South China Normal University (IRB approval: SCNU-SPT-2025-055).

Procedures

Familiarization and exercise execution

During the initial laboratory visit (Session 1), participants provided written informed consent and completed a health and training history questionnaire. Each participant’s basic physical function and back squat technique were then evaluated. Subsequently, body height and body mass were recorded using a stadiometer (Yunpeng Technology, Dalian, China) and an InBody 270 body composition analyzer (Biospace, Cerritos, CA, USA). Sessions 2–4 served as familiarization sessions on a Smith machine (HuiPao Fitness Equipment, Xuzhou, China) utilized for all subsequent testing. Each session commenced with a standardized general warm-up (3 min of jogging, 5 min of dynamic stretching, 5 box jumps, and 10 empty-barbell squats) [16, 21]. Participants then performed incremental sets, increasing the load in increments of ~ 30% of their body mass from the empty barbell, until an MV of ~ 0.5 m·s− 1 was reached. A velocity-dependent repetition scheme was applied per set: 3 repetitions for light loads (> 1.0 m·s− 1), 2 repetitions for moderate loads (0.7–1.0 m·s− 1), and 1 repetition for heavy loads (< 0.7 m·s− 1). All consecutive incremental sets were interspersed with 3-minute rest intervals. After a 5-minute rest, participants completed two sets at a load corresponding to ~ 0.6 m·s− 1 until reaching a subjective 2 repetitions in reserve, separated by a 5-minute rest interval [6]. To progressively acclimate participants to VRT, these three familiarization sessions employed chain loads of 0%, ~ 20%, and ~ 40%, respectively.

Individual stance width and foot placement were established during the first familiarization session and meticulously replicated via floor markings across all experimental trials. For each repetition, participants descended at a controlled tempo (1.0–1.5 s) until their femurs were parallel to the floor. To guarantee consistent depth, an individualized tactile feedback device (i.e., consisting of a custom stack of wooden boards equipped with an adjustable protruding PVC pipe) provided a soft physical cue at the bottom position. The mechanical design of this device inherently prevented participants from bouncing or utilizing a rebound effect to assist the concentric phase. Once the required depth was reached—confirmed by a researcher’s verbal cue—participants immediately executed the concentric phase as fast as possible [16]. Heel lifting at the apex of the movement was permitted, provided this technique was maintained consistently across all trials; however, jumping was prohibited [22] (Fig. 2). Strong verbal encouragement was provided continuously throughout the concentric phase of every repetition, followed by immediate velocity feedback upon completion, to ensure maximum intent [23]. For sets comprising more than one repetition, a 2-second pause was incorporated between repetitions under both loading configurations. This pause served to allow the chains to stabilize (during VRT) and to ensure maximal intent for the subsequent repetition. Furthermore, to ensure participant safety, one researcher served as a spotter to provide immediate physical assistance in the event of momentary muscular failure.

Constant-load 1RM testing

The Smith machine back squat constant-load 1RM was assessed at the onset of Phase two and Phase three. Following the standardized general warm-up, the barbell load was initially increased in increments of ~ 30% of the participant’s body mass. A velocity-dependent repetition scheme was applied: 3 repetitions for light loads (MV > 1.0 m·s− 1) and 2 repetitions for moderate loads (0.7–1.0 m·s− 1) [22]. Once the MV dropped below 0.7 m·s− 1, the load was increased by 1–10 kg for each subsequent single-repetition attempt [6]. Inter-set rest intervals were maintained at 3–5 min. The 1RM was determined when a participant met at least one of the following criteria: (i) momentary muscular failure during the concentric phase; (ii) a reported rating of perceived exertion of ≥ 9.5; or (iii) volitional termination of the test [24]. The last successfully completed load was recorded as the measured constant-load 1RM. Five minutes after the completion of the constant-load 1RM assessment in Phase three, participants performed two additional familiarization sets to failure. One set was performed using a constant load, while the other incorporated a chain load of ~ 40%. These two sets were separated by a 5-minute rest interval.

Submaximal incremental loading test

Participants first completed the standardized general warm-up consistent with the protocol described in the familiarization sessions. Following a 3-minute rest, a specific warm-up was performed, consisting of 3 repetitions at ~ 40% 1RM, 2 repetitions at ~ 60% 1RM, and 1 repetition at ~ 80% 1RM. Subsequently, participants performed incremental sets at 45%, 60%, 75%, and 90% of their constant-load 1RM. The repetition scheme was applied as follows: 3 repetitions at 45% 1RM, 2 repetitions at 60% and 75% 1RM, and 1 repetition at 90% 1RM. Inter-set rest intervals were maintained at 3 min [15].

Repetitions-to-failure test

Participants first completed a standardized general warm-up and a specific warm-up consistent with the protocol described previously. Subsequently, participants performed 4 sets at 70% constant-load 1RM to momentary muscular failure. Inter-set rest intervals were maintained at 5 min.

Quantification of the chain load

Six different specifications of chains were utilized in this study. To minimize chain stacking at the bottom position, the chains were hung directly onto the barbell sleeves with appropriate spacing maintained between individual chains. The chain loads were meticulously quantified for each participant based on their individualized range of motion. For the 34 participants included in Phase two, the barbell height from the floor was 88.24 ± 3.46 cm (range: 82.50–96.00 cm) at the lowest position and 151.57 ± 5.47 cm (range: 141.00–163.00 cm) at the highest position. The load provided by each chain specification was directly measured using a DY-920 dynamometer (2000 Hz; Daysensor, Bengbu, China). Prior laboratory testing demonstrated a high reliability for this device (within-subject coefficient of variation = 0.03%). Furthermore, validity was established by comparing its measurements against a criterion force plate (VALD Performance, Brisbane, Australia), which revealed a minimal systematic bias of 0.017 kg (p = 0.788). For each participant, the dynamometer first recorded the top load at their highest squat position. The chains were then allowed to naturally descend to the participant’s lowest squat position to record the bottom load. To minimize potential measurement errors caused by chain sway or irregular bottom stacking, this procedure was repeated three times for each chain specification, and the average values were calculated (Fig. 3). The individualized chain load and the variable load were subsequently determined using the following equations: Chain Load = (Top Load + Bottom Load) / 2, and Variable Load = Top Load − Bottom Load [5, 25] (Table 2).

Data collection and analysis

The MV, defined as the average velocity of the barbell during the concentric phase, was recorded using a valid and reliable linear position transducer (GymAware RS [Kinetic Performance Technologies, Canberra, Australia]) [12, 26]. The device was positioned 55 cm to the left of the Smith machine’s center, with the sensor directly beneath the barbell [27]. During the submaximal incremental loading tests, the repetition with the highest MV at each load was selected for analysis. This procedure ensured the capture of the participants’ maximal intent, mitigating the confounding effects of fluctuations in intent or technique across multiple repetitions. Furthermore, previous research has demonstrated that using the fastest MV yielded identical reliability and did not present a meaningful effect on the subsequent modeling of the load–MV relationship compared to the averaged MV [28]. During the repetitions-to-failure tests, the MV of the final completed repetition in each set was recorded as the MVlast. No data were missing during the collection process, and no values were excluded as outliers (e.g., > 3 standard deviations) to maintain the ecological validity of the testing.

Individual linear load–MV relationships were constructed using the least-squares four-point method (45%, 60%, 75%, and 90% of constant-load 1RM): Load (MV) = Intercept − (Slope × MV). The selection of the group-averaged MVlast used to predict the 1RM was determined by the statistical main effects: (i) when a significant main effect of load configuration was revealed, the individual MVlast values were averaged for each configuration; (ii) when a significant main effect of set was observed, only the individual MVlast from the first set was averaged; and (iii) when no significant main effects were revealed, the MVlast was averaged across all participants, sets, and configurations. The selected group-averaged MVlast was substituted into each participant’s load–MV relationship to calculate the predicted 1RM. Using this predicted 1RM, the four testing loads were converted into %1RM using formula: %1RM = (Total External Load / Predicted 1RM) × 100. Individual linear %1RM–MV relationships were subsequently established using the least-squares four-point method: %1RM (MV) = Intercept − (Slope × MV). A series of MVs corresponding to 40%–90% 1RM (in 5% increments) were calculated from these established %1RM–MV relationships. To mitigate the interference of random measurement errors, the MVs derived from the two experimental sessions were averaged prior to comparisons [15].

Statistical analyses

Descriptive data were assessed for normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test and are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The goodness-of-fit for the individual load–MV relationships was represented by the coefficient of determination (R2). Separate two-way repeated-measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were performed to analyze: (i) MVlast values during the repetitions-to-failure test (loading configuration [0% vs. 20% vs. 60%] × set [1 vs. 2 vs. 3 vs. 4]); (ii) MVs during the submaximal incremental loading test (loading configuration × testing load [45% vs. 60% vs. 75% vs. 90% of constant-load 1RM]) for the first and second experimental sessions, respectively; (iii) predicted 1RMs (loading configuration × experimental session [first vs. second]). Additionally, separate one-way repeated-measures ANOVAs were conducted to compare the intercepts and slopes of the %1RM–MV relationships across the three loading configurations. The Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied when Mauchly’s test of sphericity was violated, and Bonferroni adjustments were used for post hoc comparisons. The partial eta squared (ηp2) was calculated for the ANOVA and the magnitude was interpreted as follows: trivial (< 0.010), small (0.010–0.059), moderate (0.060–0.140), and large (> 0.140) [29]. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) with the significance level set at α = 0.05. An a priori power analysis using G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.7) for a repeated-measures ANOVA to detect moderate differences (effect size f = 0.25) in the %1RM–MV relationships across the three loading configurations (α = 0.05, power = 0.80, correlation = 0.5) determined a minimum sample size of 28. Thus, our Phase two cohort of 34 participants ensured adequate statistical power.

Regarding the predicted 1RM, a significant main effect of loading configuration was revealed (F = 61.4, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.650), while neither a significant main effect of experimental session nor an interaction was observed (F = 0.3–1.3, p = 0.276–0.619, ηp2 = 0.008–0.038) (Table 6). Discussion

This study primarily sought to investigate how different magnitudes of chain-based VRT influence commonly used VBT-derived metrics in the Smith machine back squat. The principal findings indicate that: (i) the individual load–MV relationships were highly linear across all configurations; (ii) MVlast was unaffected by loading configurations; (iii) compared to the 0% and 20% configurations, the 60% configuration elicited greater MVs at 75% and 90% of constant-load 1RM, with no differences at lighter loads; (iv) the 1RMs predicted using the group-averaged MVlast increased alongside the proportion of the chain load; (v) the MVs derived from the %1RM–MV relationships were significantly altered by different loading configurations; specifically, compared to the 0% configuration, the velocity discrepancies for the 20% and 60% configurations progressively widened as the relative load decreased. Collectively, these results suggest that utilizing a sufficient chain load (e.g., 60% configuration) can acutely enhance the MVs during heavy-load Smith machine back squats. Furthermore, while these findings support the viability of using the %1RM–MV relationship to prescribe VRT intensity, practitioners should construct configuration-specific models, particularly when monitoring lighter loads. Given the growing popularity of combining VRT with VBT, the importance of this study lies in providing the first empirical framework for prescribing and monitoring chain-based VRT intensity using individual %1RM–MV relationships.

Fundamentally, the individual load–MV relationships remained highly linear, and the MVlast was unaffected when chains replaced a portion of the external constant load. Previous research has established that this linearity is robust against exercise variations, such as the inclusion of a stretch-shortening cycle [11, 12]. This is particularly relevant given that VRT shares a similar kinematic profile, characterized by increased acceleration at the concentric onset [13, 30, 31]. Furthermore, the use of a group-averaged MVlast is corroborated by García-Ramos et al. [18] and Mendonca et al. [19], who reported that the magnitude of MVlast does not significantly differ across varying sets, sessions, exercise variations, or training levels during CRT. Collectively, the preserved linearity and stable MVlast demonstrate the feasibility of using VBT for 1RM prediction and intensity prescription during the chain-based Smith machine back squat.

The primary finding of this study was that a 60% configuration increased MVs at near-maximal loads. At lighter loads (45% and 60% constant-load 1RM), the MVs remained similar across all configurations. This lack of difference is likely attributable to the less pronounced sticking region at lighter loads, which diminishes the mechanical advantage typically provided by variable resistance. However, at heavier loads (75% and 90% constant-load 1RM), the 60% configuration elicited significantly greater MVs compared to the 0% and 20% configurations. Notably, at 90% constant-load 1RM, even the smaller 20% configuration altered the resistance curve sufficiently to help lifters produce higher velocities through the sticking region, although the 60% configuration clearly maximized this enhancement.

These results contrast with previous findings involving free-weight exercises, such as the deadlift [5] and bench press [4], where chain usage often resulted in decreased velocities under equal mean load conditions. This discrepancy is primarily explained by the stabilization demands of free weights: chain sway introduces instability, necessitating a voluntary reduction in lifting velocity to maintain control [32]. By utilizing a Smith machine, the present study effectively eliminated the stabilization demands associated with chain sway [20]. Consequently, this study was the first to demonstrate that, under equal mean load conditions, allocating a 60% load to chains during the Smith machine back squat effectively enhanced the MV at heavy loads compared to CRT.

In parallel with the velocity enhancements, 1RMs predicted using the group-averaged MVlast increased alongside the proportion of the chain load, demonstrating that the maximal load-bearing capacity increases as the proportion of chain load increases. This is indirectly supported by previous studies reporting that greater absolute loads can be lifted during VRT for a given xRM (e.g., 2RM and 6RM) in both back squats and deadlifts compared to CRT [33, 34]. However, practitioners should interpret these predicted values with caution. In the present study, the predicted 1RM for the 0% configuration systematically overestimated the directly measured constant-load 1RM by 6.3–8.4% (146.3–147.9 kg vs. 135.0–139.2 kg), a phenomenon consistent with previous literature [35]. Although the actual 1RM values are likely lower than predicted, implying that the MVs corresponding to specific %1RMs should be slightly greater in magnitude, the direct comparisons and observed trends across the three loading configurations remain valid. Consequently, future research aiming for more accurate estimations may necessitate the application of a higher minimal velocity threshold (e.g., 0.40 m·s− 1 rather than the 0.35 m·s− 1 used herein) [16]. Nonetheless, given that the same absolute load corresponds to a different %1RM between CRT and VRT, monitoring training intensity via lifting velocity becomes imperative during chain-based Smith machine back squats [4, 5, 10, 13, 36].

To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first to demonstrate that the MVs derived from the %1RM–MV relationships were significantly altered by the loading configurations. Specifically, compared to the 0% configuration, the MV discrepancies progressively widened for both the 20% (from 0.00 to 0.03 m·s− 1) and 60% configurations (from 0.01 to 0.07 m·s− 1) as load decreased. This can be primarily attributed to the load-dependent effects of VRT on MVs of the testing loads. From a practical standpoint, Banyard et al. [14] and Orange et al. [37] have suggested that a training intensity variation of less than 5% might not yield divergent training adaptations. In the back squat, this 5% variation translates to a MV difference of approximately 0.06 m·s− 1, which serves as a widely adopted threshold for load adjustments in velocity-based training [9, 38], though some practitioners may adopt an even stricter 0.03 m·s− 1 threshold [6]. Evaluated against these practical frameworks, if practitioners aim to develop maximal strength capacities utilizing heavier loads (≥ 75% 1RM), the %1RM–MV relationships across different load configurations can be applied interchangeably. Conversely, if the objective is to enhance maximal velocity or power utilizing lighter loads (40–60% 1RM) [39], it is imperative to construct configuration-specific models to accurately prescribe training intensity.

Conclusions and practical applications

Allocating a greater proportion of the load to chains enhances MVs at heavy loads and consequently increases the predicted 1RM during the Smith machine back squat. The preserved linearity of the load–MV relationship supports the use of VBT for prescribing and monitoring intensity under the chain-based Smith machine back squat; however, the %1RM–MV relationship is configuration-specific and therefore requires tailored calibration according to the proportion of chain load used. Furthermore, these findings should be extrapolated with caution to other variable resistance modalities (e.g., elastic bands) or chain proportions exceeding 60%.

Limitations

Certain limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of the present study. First, the maximum chain proportion investigated was the 60% configuration (equivalent to a ~ 35% variable load or ~ 17.5% mean variable contribution). Because previous literature indicates that excessively high variable loads may impair MVs, the current findings should not be extrapolated beyond this threshold [5, 40]. Second, this study exclusively examined the Smith machine back squat with chain-based variable resistance. Given that previous research has identified distinct MV characteristics across different VRT modalities (e.g., free-weight exercises with chains or elastic bands, or Smith machine exercises with elastic bands), the resulting %1RM–MV relationships established here may differ under those conditions [5, 10]. Third, 1RMs were derived from the load–MV relationship. Given the observed systematic overestimation, future research should investigate methods to improve prediction accuracy under VRT conditions, thereby refining the magnitude of the resulting %1RM–MV relationships. Finally, the strict strength requirement (1RM > 111 kg) made it difficult to recruit eligible female participants from the local population. However, biological sex is considered to exert a trivial effect on the %1RM–MV relationship metrics (e.g., goodness of fit and reliability) [41, 42].
