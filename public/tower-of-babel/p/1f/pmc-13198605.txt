Efficacy and Safety of Firsekibart in Treatment of Active Systemic Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis: A Randomized Phase 2 Study

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSystemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA) is an autoinflammatory disorder that impacts children aged ≤ 16 years. This study evaluated the efficacy and safety of firsekibart, a fully human anti-IL-1β monoclonal antibody, in children with active sJIA.MethodsThis multicenter, randomized, open-label, active-controlled phase 2 study (NCT05925452) enrolled participants aged 2– < 18 years with active sJIA across 14 sites in China. Participants were randomized (1:1:1) to firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg or 4.0 mg/kg subcutaneously every 4 weeks, or tocilizumab intravenously every 2 weeks, for 24 weeks. The primary endpoint was modified JIA American College of Rheumatology Pediatric criteria (ACR Pedi) 30 response at day 28. Secondary endpoints included ACR Pedi 30/50/70/90 responses, corticosteroid tapering, immunogenicity, and safety.ResultsFifty participants were randomized (firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg: n = 17; 4.0 mg/kg: n = 16; tocilizumab: n = 17). At day 28, ACR Pedi 30 response rates were 94.1% (95% CI 71.3–99.9), 75.0% (47.6–92.7) and 82.4% (56.6–96.2) in the firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg, 4.0 mg/kg, and tocilizumab groups, respectively. No MAS events or anti-drug or neutralizing antibodies were detected. Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) occurred in 70.6%, 93.8%, and 94.1% of participants in the firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg, 4.0 mg/kg, and tocilizumab groups, respectively. Grade ≥ 3 TEAEs were absent in the 3.0 mg/kg group. Common TEAEs mainly included infections and laboratory abnormalities.ConclusionsFirsekibart demonstrated rapid clinical improvement, steroid-sparing effects, and an acceptable safety profile. Key Summary Points

Why carry out the study? Systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA) is a severe autoinflammatory disease with high morbidity and risk of macrophage activation syndrome (MAS).Although existing IL 1 and IL 6 inhibitors are effective for sJIA, daily injections (anakinra) or intravenous administration (tocilizumab) impacts adherence. Canakinumab is effective, but access and cost remain barriers in many regions, especially in Asia (China).This study evaluated whether firsekibart, a fully human anti-IL 1β monoclonal antibody, administered subcutaneously once-every-4-weeks, could prove effective and is safe in children with active sJIA. What was learned from the study? At day 28, firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg achieved the highest ACR Pedi 30 response (94.1%) and superior steroid-sparing compared with firsekibart 4.0 mg/kg and tocilizumab.No MAS events or immunogenicity were detected, and grade ≥3 TEAEs were absent in the firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg group, supporting a favorable benefit–risk profile. Thus, a once-every-4-week subcutaneous firsekibart regimen may enhance treatment adherence and expand therapeutic options for pediatric patients with sJIA.

Introduction

Systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA) is a distinct and severe subtype of juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA), [1–3] characterized by systemic inflammation alongside chronic arthritis. Clinical manifestations include arthritis, spiking fever, evanescent erythematous rash, lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly and/or serositis, accompanied by elevated inflammatory markers, such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and ferritin levels [4]. Macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) is the most severe and life-threatening complication of sJIA, affecting approximately 10–30% of sJIA patients.

JIA is the most prevalent pediatric rheumatic disease, with an estimated incidence ranging from 1.6 to 23/ per 100,000 [3], and a prevalence of 3.8 to 400 per 100,000 children worldwide. sJIA accounts for 10–20% of JIA cases, with an incidence of 0.4–0.8 per 100,000 [5], and a pooled prevalence estimate up to 8.6 per 100,000 [6], and typically occurs in children aged 1–5 years and shows no clear sex predilection [7].

According to current international treatment guidelines, recommended therapies include corticosteroids and biologics targeting interleukin pathways, particularly IL-6 and IL-1 [2, 8]. Tocilizumab, an IL-6 receptor antagonist, is approved in China and widely used in sJIA. Anakinra, an IL-1 receptor antagonist, has shown efficacy but is limited by its requirement for daily injections, which poses challenges for compliance in children.

Despite these advances, a subset of patients in China continues to experience refractory sJIA or episodes of sJIA-associated MAS, highlighting the unmet need for additional treatment options. In clinical practice, long-term corticosteroid exposure, difficulty in achieving sustained disease control, and the risk of disease flare or MAS recurrence remain major challenges, particularly in young children requiring prolonged treatment. Specifically, there is a pressing need for IL-1 targeted therapies with longer dosing intervals, improved tolerability, and more convenient administration in the pediatric population. Addressing these unmet needs provides a compelling rationale for developing novel IL-1-targeted therapies with optimized dosing regimens.

The IL-1 cytokine family plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of sJIA and MAS, making IL-1 inhibition an established and rational therapeutic target. Among IL-1–targeted strategies, selective and sustained inhibition of IL-1β has been proposed as a promising approach to control systemic inflammation while reducing treatment burden. Firsekibart (formerly known as genakumab) is a high-affinity, fully human monoclonal antibody that selectively binds and neutralizes IL-1β [9]. In June 2025, firsekibart was approved in China for the treatment of acute gout flares in adults who have contraindications, intolerance, or inadequate response to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and/or colchicine, and for whom repeated corticosteroid use is inappropriate [1]. Its potential role in sJIA is currently being investigated in an ongoing phase 3 clinical trial.

The objective of this randomized, open-label phase 2 study was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of firsekibart in participants with active sJIA. In addition, the study aimed to explore its potential to achieve early disease control and support corticosteroid reduction, thereby informing dose selection and guiding the design of future phase 3 trials.

Methods

Study Design

This was a multicenter, randomized, open-label, active-controlled phase 2 study conducted between February 2024 and February 2025 across 14 centers in China (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05925452). A total of 45 participants with active sJIA were planned for enrolment. The study comprised four periods: a 4-week screening period, a 4-week induction phase, a 20-week maintenance phase, and a 4-week follow-up period.

Eligible participants were randomized in a 1:1:1 ratio, stratified by baseline corticosteroid dose (< 0.5 mg/kg/day vs. ≥ 0.5 mg/kg/day prednisone or its equivalent), to receive firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg, firsekibart 4.0 mg/kg, or tocilizumab. Firsekibart was administered subcutaneously once-every-4-weeks, while tocilizumab was administered intravenously once-every-2-weeks (12.0 mg/kg for those with body weight < 30 kg and 8.0 mg/kg for those weighing ≥ 30 kg).

The induction phase included dosing on day 1 (firsekibart groups) or on days 1 and 15 (tocilizumab group). Efficacy and safety assessments were performed on days 3, 15, and 28. Participants achieving an American College of Rheumatology Pediatric criteria (ACR Pedi 30) response at day 28 entered the maintenance phase and continued their assigned regimen, while those who did not meet the response criteria discontinued treatment and entered the follow-up period.

During the maintenance phase, participants receiving concomitant corticosteroids were required to taper their steroid dosage following a predefined tapering schedule once they had achieved an ACR Pedi 50 response and a normal C-reactive protein (CRP) level.

Participants and Eligibility Criteria

Eligible participants were aged ≥ 2 and < 18 years at baseline, with a confirmed diagnosis of sJIA according to the 2001 International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR) classification criteria [2], established at least 2 months before screening. Active sJIA was defined as the presence of arthritis in ≥ 2 joints, a body temperature > 38 °C within 1 week prior to randomization, and elevated CRP (CRP > 30 mg/l). Participants were required to have at least one systemic manifestation, such as an evanescent erythematous rash, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, or serositis. All participants were required to have disease onset before 16 years of age and were on stable background therapy with NSAIDs, methotrexate, and/or corticosteroids as per protocol requirements.

Key exclusion criteria included pregnancy or lactation, hypersensitivity to study drugs or related biologics, active or recent serious infections (including tuberculosis and invasive fungal infection), recent MAS, and any history of severe pulmonary, cardiac, or other systemic disorders that could interfere with study participation. Participants with abnormal liver or renal function tests (> 1.5 × upper limit of normal [ULN]), active hepatitis B or C, or HIV infection were also excluded.

Background Therapy

Participants were required to maintain stable background therapy prior to randomization. Those receiving methotrexate (MTX) must have been treated for ≥ 12 weeks and on a stable dose (≤ 20 mg/m2/week) for at least 8 weeks, with folic or folinic acid supplementation according to local practice. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) were permitted if used as a single agent at a stable dose for ≥ 2 weeks before randomization (excluding temporary antipyretic use within 2 weeks of baseline). The maximum allowable oral corticosteroid dose was ≤ 1.0 mg/kg/day of prednisone or equivalent (up to 30 mg/day for participants weighing ≥ 30 kg for at least 3 days prior to randomization.

Adjustment of background therapy was not permitted unless required for intolerance or rescue management. Participants who completed or discontinued treatment entered a 4-week safety follow-up period.

Assessments

Efficacy

The primary efficacy endpoint was the proportion of participants achieving a modified JIA ACR Pedi 30 (ACR 30) response at day 28. The modified ACR Pedi criteria [10] included six core variables—number of joints with active arthritis, number of joints with limited motion, physician’s global assessment (VAS), parent’s global assessment (VAS), functional ability assessed using the Childhood Health Assessment Questionnaire, CHAQ, and CRP. A participant was considered an ACR 30 responder if at least three of the six core variables improved by ≥ 30% from baseline, no more than one variable worsened by ≥ 30% from baseline, and no fever attributable to sJIA within 7 days prior to the assessment [3].

Secondary efficacy endpoints included the proportions of participants achieving modified ACR Pedi 50, 70, and 90 responses at day 28, as well as the proportions achieving modified ACR Pedi 30/50/70/90 responses at day 15 and at the end of treatment (EOT). Other secondary endpoints included the proportion of participants who achieved successful corticosteroid tapering by EOT and the proportion who discontinued corticosteroids entirely by EOT.

Corticosteroid Tapering and Relapse Definitions

Corticosteroid tapering was evaluated throughout the study. Successful corticosteroid reduction was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: (a) baseline prednisone (or equivalent) ≥ 0.8 mg/kg/day reduced to ≤ 0.5 mg/kg/day; (b) baseline prednisone (or equivalent) dose ≥ 0.5– < 0.8 mg/kg/day reduced by at least 0.3 mg/kg/day; or (c) any participant reached prednisone ≤ 0.2 mg/kg/day. Baseline corticosteroid dose was calculated as the total daily prednisone-equivalent dose divided by the last available body weight prior to randomization, consistent with protocol definitions.

During corticosteroid tapering, disease relapse was assessed and defined as either (a) recurrence of fever (> 38 °C) for ≥ 2 consecutive days (temperature > 38 °C, excluding infection) or (b) worsening of ≥ 3 of the six ACR Pedi core variables by ≥ 30% with ≤ 1 variable improving ≥ 30%. For the purpose of relapse determination, worsening of individual ACR Pedi variables was defined as: CHAQ: increase ≥ 30%; physician or parent global VAS: increase ≥ 30% and ≥ 20 mm; number of active joints or joints with limited motion: increase ≥ 30% and by ≥ 2 joints; CRP increase ≥ 30% and CRP > 30 mg/l.

Participants who met relapse criteria were considered to have tapering failure and resumed the previous corticosteroid dose according to protocol.

Immunogenicity

Immunogenicity endpoints included the incidence of anti-drug antibodies (ADA) and neutralizing antibodies (NAb), ADA titers, and time to ADA onset following treatment.

Safety

Safety endpoints were assessed in terms of incidence, severity, and relationship of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), serious AEs (SAEs), and AEs of special interest (AESIs). In addition, changes in laboratory values, vital signs, and electrocardiographic findings were recorded.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS® software (version 9.4). The full analysis set (FAS), based on the intention-to-treat principle, included all randomized participants who received at least one dose of the study drug and had at least one post-baseline efficacy assessment. The Safety Set (SS) included all participants who received at least one dose of study drug. The anti-drug antibody set (ADAS) included all participants who received firsekibart and had both baseline and post-baseline ADA data.

As this was a phase 2 exploratory study, the sample size was not based on formal power calculations. A total of 45 participants (15 per treatment arm) was considered adequate to provide descriptive estimates of efficacy and safety and to inform dose selection for the subsequent phase 3 study. No formal statistical hypothesis was planned. Accordingly, all analyses were descriptive in nature, and no parametric or nonparametric hypothesis testing was performed.

Efficacy analyses were conducted using the FAS. The proportion of participants achieving a modified ACR Pedi 30 response at day 28 and other categorical efficacy variables were summarized by treatment group using counts, percentages, and two-sided 95% CIs calculated by the Clopper–Pearson method. Continuous variables (e.g., joint counts, VAS scores, CRP levels) were summarized descriptively. Missing ACR Pedi component data were handled according to SAP-specified rules.

Safety analyses were performed in the SS and summarized descriptively. The incidence, severity, and relationship of TEAEs, SAEs, and AESIs were presented by treatment group, and AEs were coded using MedDRA v28.0 and graded per CTCAE v5.0. Laboratory results, vital signs, and electrocardiographic data were summarized using descriptive statistics, and baseline-to-worst post-baseline shifts were tabulated. Immunogenicity analyses were descriptive, summarizing the incidence and titers of ADA and NAb at each assessment time point.

Ethical Approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice, and applicable regulatory requirements in China. The study protocol and informed consent documents were reviewed and approved by the medical ethics committee of Beijing Children’s Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No. [2023]-Y-019-A), as well as by the institutional review boards of all participating centers prior to study initiation (Supplementary Table S1). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants, and assent was obtained from participants as required by local regulations.

Results

Participant Disposition and Baseline Characteristics

Of the 61 participants screened for eligibility, 50 (82.0%) were randomized to receive firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg (n = 17), firsekibart 4.0 mg/kg (n = 16), or tocilizumab (n = 17) in a 1:1:1 ratio. 11 participants (18.0%) who did not meet the eligibility criteria were excluded during the screening. Among the treated participants, 47 (94.0%) completed the induction phase and 43 (86.0%) entered the maintenance phase. Overall, 41 participants (82.0%) completed the treatment. The main reasons for treatment discontinuation were adverse events (n = 4), withdrawal of consent (n = 3), and investigator decision (n = 2).

Across the full study period, 42 participants (84.0%) completed the study, whereas eight participants (16.0%) did not complete the study. The primary reasons for early study termination were withdrawal of participation (n = 3), adverse events for which the investigator deemed continued participation unsafe (n = 3), and investigator decision (n = 2). Efficacy

Primary Efficacy Endpoint

At day 28, the proportion of participants achieving a modified JIA ACR Pedi 30 response was highest in the firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg group at 94.1% (n = 16; 95% CI 71.31–99.85), followed by 75.0% (n = 12; 95% CI 47.62–92.73) in the 4.0 mg/kg group and 82.4% (n = 14; 95% CI 56.57–96.20) in the tocilizumab group (Fig. 2). The response rate in the 3.0 mg/kg group was numerically higher than that of tocilizumab, with a between-group difference of 11.8 percentage points (95% CI –12.80–36.83).

Modified JIA ACR Pedi 30/50/70/90 responses at day 28 in the full analysis set (FAS). The modified JIA ACR Pedi 30/50/70/90 responses were defined as improvements of at least 30%, 50%, 70%, and 90%, respectively, in at least three of the six response variables and a worsening of more than 30% in no more than one of the six variables, as well as an absence of fever. Among those on corticosteroids at baseline, 91.7% (n = 11; 95% CI 61.52–99.79) in the firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg group, 81.8% (n = 9; 95% CI 48.22–97.72) in the 4.0 mg/kg group, and 75.0% (n = 6; 95% CI 34.91–96.81) in the tocilizumab group achieved a modified JIA ACR Pedi 30 response at day 28. Conversely, response rates for individuals not on corticosteroids at baseline were 100% (n = 5; 95% CI 47.82–100; 3.0 mg/kg), 60% (n = 5; 95% CI 14.66–94.73; 4.0 mg/kg) and 88.9% (n = 9; 95% CI 51.75–99.72; tocilizumab).

Secondary Efficacy Endpoints

Modified JIA ACR Pedi 50/70/90 Responses on D28

At day 28, in the 3.0 mg/kg group, 76.5% (n = 13) participants reached ACR Pedi 50, 64.7% (n = 11) reached ACR Pedi 70, and 47.1% (n = 8) achieved ACR Pedi 90. The 4.0 mg/kg group showed comparable efficacy, with 62.5% (n = 10) participants each achieving ACR Pedi 50 and ACR Pedi 70, and 43.8% (n = 7) participants achieving ACR Pedi 90. In the tocilizumab group, 64.7% (n = 11) of the participants achieved ACR Pedi 50, 47.1% (n = 8) reached ACR Pedi 70, and 35.3% (n = 6) attained ACR Pedi 90.

Modified JIA ACR Pedi 30/50/70/90 Responses on D15 and EOT

At day 15, rapid clinical responses were observed across treatment groups. In the firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg group, 76.5% (n = 13) participants achieved a modified JIA ACR Pedi 30 response, 58.8% (n = 10) achieved ACR Pedi 50, 47.1% (n = 8) achieved ACR Pedi 70, and 41.2% (n = 7) achieved ACR Pedi 90. In the firsekibart 4.0 mg/kg group, 75.0% participants (n = 12) achieved ACR Pedi 30, 56.3% (n = 9) achieved ACR Pedi 50, 31.3% (n = 5) achieved ACR Pedi 70, and 25.0% (n = 4) achieved ACR Pedi 90. In the tocilizumab group, 82.4% participants (n = 14) achieved ACR Pedi 30, 76.5% (n = 13) achieved ACR Pedi 50, 47.1% (n = 8) reached ACR Pedi 70, and 35.3% (n = 6) attained ACR Pedi 90.

At EOT, higher-level ACR responses were maintained across all groups. In the firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg group, 82.4% (n = 14) participants each reached ACR Pedi 50 and ACR Pedi 70, and 76.5% (n = 13) achieved ACR Pedi 90. The 4.0 mg/kg group showed comparable efficacy, with 68.8% (n = 11) participants each achieved ACR Pedi 50/70/90. In the tocilizumab group, 76.5% (n = 13) participants each achieved ACR Pedi 50/70, and 70.6% (n = 12) attained ACR Pedi 90.

Glucocorticoid Tapering Outcomes

By the end of treatment, 75.0% participants (n = 9; 95% CI 42.81–94.51) in the firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg group achieved successful glucocorticoid tapering. These rates were higher than those observed in the firsekibart 4.0 mg/kg and tocilizumab groups, with 63.6% (n = 7; 95% CI 30.79–89.07) and 5 (62.5%; 95% CI 24.49–91.48) participants achieving successful glucocorticoid tapering, respectively (Fig. 4).

Complete discontinuation of glucocorticoids by the end of treatment was achieved in 50.0% participants (n = 4; 95% CI 15.70–84.30) in the tocilizumab group.

Only one participant (14.3%) in the 4.0 mg/kg group experienced tapering failure due to elevated CRP (> 30 mg/l), and no sJIA relapses were reported during tapering.

Safety

Median on-treatment exposure was approximately 24 weeks across treatment groups. TEAEs were reported in 70.6% of participants receiving firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg, 93.8% receiving firsekibart 4.0 mg/kg, and 94.1% receiving tocilizumab (Table 2).

No grade ≥ 3 TEAEs or serious adverse events (SAEs) were reported with firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg. In the firsekibart 4.0 mg/kg group, grade ≥ 3 TEAEs and SAEs each occurred in 18.8% of participants, while in the tocilizumab group, grade ≥ 3 TEAEs occurred in 35.3% and SAEs in 23.5% of participants. All grade ≥ 3 TEAEs were assessed as treatment-related. No MAS events nor deaths were reported in any treatment group throughout the study. Incident rates of AEs by system organ class are depicted in Table 2.

Permanent discontinuation due to TEAEs occurred in 0%, 6.3%, and 17.6% of participants in the firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg, firsekibart 4.0 mg/kg, and tocilizumab groups, respectively. One case of acute kidney injury leading to treatment discontinuation occurred in the firsekibart 4.0 mg/kg group and was assessed by the investigator as unrelated to study treatment. Temporary treatment interruption due to TEAEs was more frequent in the tocilizumab group than in the firsekibart groups.

Adverse events of special interest (AESIs) were defined as macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) and infections of grade ≥ 3 severity or tuberculosis requiring anti-tuberculosis therapy, occurred infrequently, and were observed only in the firsekibart 4.0 mg/kg and tocilizumab groups. No AESIs were reported with firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg.

The most common treatment-emergent adverse events (≥ 10% at the preferred term level in any treatment group) were predominantly infections and laboratory abnormalities, consistent with the known safety profiles of IL-1 and IL-6 pathway inhibition. Discussion

In this multicenter, randomized, open-label phase 2 study, firsekibart demonstrated rapid onset of clinical efficacy and an acceptable safety profile in children with active sJIA. Treatment with firsekibart met the primary efficacy endpoint at day 28, was associated with early clinical improvement, and enabled a high proportion of patients entering the maintenance phase to successfully taper and discontinue glucocorticoids. These findings indicate that firsekibart provides effective early disease control, which is a central therapeutic goal in sJIA [11].

Rapid suppression of systemic inflammation is critical in sJIA to facilitate timely glucocorticoid tapering and reduce cumulative steroid exposure. In this study, firsekibart showed early and sustained clinical responses across multiple ACR Pedi thresholds, with consistent improvements observed from day 15 through the end of treatment. Among patients receiving baseline corticosteroids, firsekibart—particularly at the 3.0 mg/kg dose—was associated with higher rates of successful steroid tapering and complete discontinuation by the end of treatment, supporting its steroid-sparing potential.

The rapidity and magnitude of early clinical responses observed with firsekibart are broadly consistent with those reported for canakinumab, another monoclonal antibody targeting IL-1β that is approved for the treatment of sJIA. In pivotal randomized studies, canakinumab demonstrated rapid improvement in adapted ACR responses within the first weeks of treatment, and subsequent real-world data have confirmed durable disease control with meaningful glucocorticoid sparing [3, 12]. In the NEJM study by Ruperto et al., canakinumab achieved adapted ACR Pedi 30 responses in approximately 84% of patients by day 15 and sustained improvements through week 12 [4]. Similarly, firsekibart demonstrated early responses from day 15 and achieved a 94.1% modified ACR Pedi 30 response rate at day 28 in the 3.0 mg/kg group. Higher-level responses (ACR Pedi 50–90) were also comparable between firsekibart and historical canakinumab data, supporting similar efficacy profiles. In canakinumab trials, nearly 33% of patients discontinued steroids by week 12 [4], while firsekibart showed successful tapering and complete discontinuation in a substantial proportion of patients entering the maintenance phase. Real-world evidence from the TURSIS study [13] in Turkey has demonstrated that biologic agents, including IL-1 and IL-6 inhibitors, achieve substantial clinical improvement and steroid-sparing benefits in patients with sJIA, further supporting the clinical relevance of the response patterns observed in our study.

Firsekibart shares a similar once-every-4-weeks subcutaneous dosing regimen, which may offer practical advantages in terms of treatment adherence compared with daily subcutaneous administration of anakinra, particularly in pediatric populations [14, 15].

Although this study was not designed for formal comparative efficacy assessment, the open-label comparison with tocilizumab provides useful clinical context. At early time points, firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg showed numerically higher ACR response rates across multiple thresholds, while the 4.0 mg/kg dose demonstrated broadly comparable efficacy. These observations position the early clinical activity of firsekibart within the expected therapeutic range of cytokine-targeted biologics used in sJIA, including IL-6 receptor blockade [16, 17].

In this study, dose exploration was conducted with firsekibart at 3.0 mg/kg and 4.0 mg/kg. At day 28, firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg achieved a high modified JIA ACR Pedi 30 response rate of 94.1% with comparable ACR Pedi 50–90 response rates between the two dose groups. Collectively, these findings indicate that treatment with firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg is sufficient to achieve the intended clinical efficacy in patients with sJIA.

Across efficacy and safety outcomes, firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg demonstrated the most favorable overall benefit–risk profile in this study. This dose combined robust clinical efficacy with more pronounced corticosteroid-sparing effects and a lower incidence of higher-grade adverse events compared with the 4.0 mg/kg dose. Taken together, these results support the selection of firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg administered subcutaneously every 4 weeks as the recommended dose regimen to be advanced into subsequent phase 3 clinical evaluation.

The safety profile observed in this study was consistent with the known pharmacology of IL-1β and IL-6 pathway inhibition [11, 18]. Long-term observational data with IL-1β inhibitors such as canakinumab have similarly demonstrated a favorable safety profile and sustained clinical benefit in pediatric rheumatic diseases, consistent with the reassuring safety findings reported for firsekibart in the present study [19]. Firsekibart was generally well tolerated, with no macrophage activation syndrome events, no deaths, and no evidence of immunogenicity. Notably, no grade ≥ 3 TEAEs or SAEs were observed in the firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg group, supporting its suitability for longer-term use in a pediatric population.

In addition, as this was a multicenter study, the diagnosis of sJIA and assessment of active systemic features were based on standard ILAR criteria and clinical judgment at each participating site, which may introduce some degree of variability in diagnostic evaluation across centers. Several limitations should be acknowledged. All enrolled participants in this study were required to have active systemic manifestations, such as fever, as required by the eligibility criteria. Therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to patients with sJIA who present with persistent arthritis but no longer exhibit active systemic manifestations. Additional limitations include the open-label design, the modest sample size, and the short-term primary endpoint, which may favor therapies with a faster onset of action. Nevertheless, the internal consistency of responses across ACR levels, the clear steroid-sparing signal, and the reassuring safety profile—particularly at the 3.0 mg/kg dose—provide a strong rationale for further evaluation in a randomized, double-blind phase 3 withdrawal study.

In conclusion, this randomized phase 2 study demonstrates that firsekibart is associated with rapid clinical improvement and an acceptable safety profile in children with active sJIA. The consistent efficacy across ACR response levels, together with meaningful glucocorticoid-sparing effects and a favorable benefit–risk profile, supports further evaluation of firsekibart in larger, randomized phase 3 clinical studies.

Conclusions

Firsekibart demonstrated a favorable overall benefit–risk profile in children with sJIA, with early clinical efficacy, meaningful steroid tapering, and an acceptable safety profile. In this study, no MAS events or anti-drug antibodies were observed. Firsekibart 3.0 mg/kg provided the most balanced efficacy and safety profile and supports further evaluation as a once-every-4-weeks IL-1β-targeted treatment option in phase 3 trials.
