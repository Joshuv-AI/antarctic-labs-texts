MindMInC (mindfulness intervention for insomnia, mood, and cognition): a digital randomized control trial protocol for improving sleep and wellbeing in older adults
Authors: Hemangi Sanjivini, Joshua F Wiley, Giselle Withers, Melinda Jackson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSleep difficulty is prevalent in aging populations but can be challenging to treat due to the barriers to accessing evidence-based treatments. Further, 30–40 per cent of individuals with insomnia do not benefit from first-line treatments, making it important to consider viable alternatives. This protocol details a trial to investigate the feasibility and efficacy of a digital mindfulness intervention in improving sleep and well-being in older adults. Older adults aged 55 and above (n = 106) recruited into the trial will be randomly allocated to either a sleep hygiene program (n = 53) or a mindfulness intervention program (n = 53). Participants in both programs will engage in 6-week, self-directed, digital programs. They will be assessed for their sleep and well-being via self-report outcomes. Primary (Insomnia Severity Index) and secondary outcomes at the baseline, post-intervention, and 3-month follow-up will be compared in linear mixed models to inform efficacy. Feasibility will be evaluated through attrition and participant feedback on the exit questionnaire. Results may help inform the viability of an online, widely disseminable approach to improving older adult sleep health in the community. (Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry #ACTRN12623000839606). Statement of SignificanceNovel features of this protocol are that it is examining a first-of-its-kind digital Mindfulness-Based Therapy for Insomnia program. In doing so it is expanding the limited research on how older adults, specifically, engage with evidence-based digital interventions. This has significant clinical implications for how clinicians and health organizations can deliver services to and engage with older adults in the community.

Introduction

Sleep difficulty is prevalent in aging populations, with 30–48 per cent of older adults presenting with insomnia symptoms such as difficulty falling asleep, maintaining sleep, or waking up too early. [1] The main risk factors that trigger insomnia are stress or disruption to the natural sleep system caused by changes in sleep–wake behaviors, for example, changes to bedtimes which can occur in the context of retirement, caregiving, death of a loved one, medications, or other medical conditions for older adults [2]. Elevated stress is usually accompanied by the activation of the sympathetic nervous system, triggering hyperarousal [3, 4]. Hyperarousal (heightened emotional, physical, and cognitive arousal) can then be maintained by maladaptive behaviors, sleep-related anxieties, and dysfunctional beliefs about sleep [4, 5]. Similarly, dysfunctional beliefs can arise and then be accompanied by hyperarousal, which can cause sleep difficulty [4, 6].

Sleep difficulties arising from insomnia can cause a marked impairment in daytime functioning, such as fatigue [7], disrupted mood [8, 9], poor attention and concentration [10], and longer-term health impacts such as cognitive decline [11] and increased risk of cardiovascular disease [12]. Evidence links subjective cognitive complaints (complaints about poor attention and memory) to future cognitive impairment and dementia [13]. Further, older adults with insomnia are both more likely to experience or develop symptoms of depression [1, 14–17] and anxiety [14, 18]. Altogether, sleep difficulty when left untreated can cause significantly poorer later-life health outcomes and an overall reduced quality of life for older adults. Despite these significant impacts, many older adults often do not seek help for their sleep difficulty, as they are seen as part of the natural aging process [7]. Thus, understanding the factors influencing access and improving older adult access to interventions for their sleep is a critical part of improving public health outcomes.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is considered the current gold-standard treatment for insomnia by the Australasian Sleep Association (ASA), the American College of Physicians, and numerous international medical and sleep associations [19, 20]. Meta-analyses of trials conducted in older adults demonstrate that CBT-I is highly effective in reducing insomnia symptoms and significantly improves sleep efficiency, sleep onset latency, wake after sleep onset, and total sleep time [21]. Further, improvements in sleep observed in older adults are comparable to middle-aged adults [22].

Despite the strong evidence for the efficacy of CBT-I, there is a subset of around 30–40 per cent of insomnia patients who do not achieve full remission in the 6–12 months following CBT-I [23–26]. While few studies have examined the reasons for non-response, one study in pregnant women [27] found that this may relate to residual cognitive arousal after CBT-I. Cognitive arousal refers to heightened mental activity, like repetitive negative thoughts, rumination, and emotions like worry and frustration, which are often associated with a “racing mind” or unhelpful beliefs about sleep and occur at bedtime, interrupting sleep [5, 28–32]. This is important as reducing cognitive arousal may be one of the key mechanisms through which sleep efficiency [33], insomnia severity, and daytime functioning [5] is improved. Further, decreased cognitive arousal following CBT-I has been identified as a mediator for both reduced insomnia [34] and depression symptoms [35]. Older adults, particularly, experience high rates of cognitive arousal [36, 37], possibly heightened by the perceived changes to their sleep as they age [1]. However, it is thought that CBT-I may not directly address this mechanism [35], and evidence from clinical trials has found mixed results for improvements in cognitive arousal following CBT-I [30, 35, 38, 39]. Thus, there is a critical need to explore alternative interventions that can directly address this residual cognitive arousal.

There is good evidence for the use of Mindfulness-Based Therapy for Insomnia (MBT-I) as an alternative therapy for insomnia [40–45]. Mindfulness refers to an intentional, purposeful, and non-judgmental awareness of the present moment [46]. The practice of mindfulness cultivates a mental process of metacognitive shifting by becoming aware of the thoughts and emotions present in the mind as transient events, thereby letting go of attachment toward them [47]. MBT-I merges practice of this mental process with the behavioral strategies used in CBT-I [47]. According to the metacognitive model of insomnia, cognitive arousal is further divided into primary and secondary arousal [29]. Primary arousal encompasses the dysfunctional thoughts related to sleep that maintain insomnia, whereas secondary arousal is defined as the metacognitive processes, such as biases in attention and perception of sleep-related thoughts that perpetuate the primary arousal [26]. As such, Ong et al. [48] propose that MBT-I can directly address cognitive arousal by changing the metacognitive processes associated with secondary arousal with downstream effects on primary arousal. Thus, MBT-I can allow cognitive arousal, maintaining insomnia to subside and normal sleep to re-emerge. Since practicing mindfulness meditation involves making balanced appraisals with greater emotional regulation and cognitive flexibility [48], both dysfunctional beliefs about sleep and the relationship with these thoughts can be changed [49, 50]. Finally, cognitive arousal and dysfunctional beliefs may be directly addressed, which would likely facilitate improvements in both insomnia severity and symptoms of anxiety and depression [35, 51, 52]. There is evidence that mindfulness-based practices lead to improvements in mood and well-being. For example, mindfulness meditation has been linked to enduring improvements in depressive symptoms [7, 53], mood [54], and anxiety [52] in adults from clinical and community settings. However, evidence supporting the effect of improved mental health through MBT-I is still emerging [44].

Studies in middle-aged and older adults demonstrate strong evidence for the efficacy of MBT-I for sleep, highlighted by significant reductions in symptoms of insomnia with moderate to large effect sizes (d = 0.46-0.89) [12, 55]. In particular, our preliminary trial investigating efficacy of digital MBT-I (dMBT-I) for adults showed significant reductions in both insomnia symptoms (d = 1.49) and cognitive arousal (d = 0.35) [40]. Further, a recent trial shows promising evidence for the effectiveness of MBT-I in reducing insomnia symptoms, depressive symptoms, and cognitive arousal in individuals who did not remit from earlier CBT-I intervention [56]. Post-hoc analyses of this trial support that reductions in insomnia, depression, and cognitive arousal are all strongly associated [56]. Notably, these improvements are likely to arise from changes to shared underlying mechanisms of cognitive arousal and dysfunctional beliefs, such that improvements in sleep and mental health are correlated but not necessarily causally driven by one another. Consequently, MBT-I shows high clinical utility for older adults as it proposes a holistic approach to sleep intervention by reducing the cognitive arousal co-occurring with poor sleep and mental health.

There can be significant barriers to accessing psychological interventions for sleep, such as limited number of trained professionals, long waitlists, geographical access, and financial costs [57, 58]. This can be further debilitating for older adults, of whom around 80 per cent have long-standing medical conditions, 29 per cent have comorbidities, and 12 per cent have unmet mobility needs [59]. As such, there is a drive toward creating innovative solutions by digitalizing interventions to enhance accessibility [60].

Digital interventions for insomnia involve pre-recording session video content and summarizing key information through animations, graphics, and readings in a standardized format, which can be later accessed and completed independently anytime and virtually anywhere through a mobile/computer device and internet connection. These interventions, if effective, can be used as standalone treatments that are self-directed but also via a stepped-care model, with clients being streamlined for clinician-guided care based on low treatment gain or adherence [61]. Whilst there are some barriers to digitally delivered interventions for older adults, such as concerns about lack of self-efficacy and support without real-time engagement, there is significant promise about enhancing self-efficacy in technological use and provision of integrated care that addresses comorbidity [62]. For example, utilizing Bowen et al. feasibility framework [63], feasibility can be accessed via a combination of limited efficacy, acceptability, and practicality. This framework allows an investigation of acceptability alongside feasibility to understand the suitability of applying dMBT-I for older adults, which is crucial considering the underrepresentation of older adults in digital sleep intervention research [7, 64].

Current evidence supports the use of dMBT-I for insomnia in the general population [40]. There is also strong support for use of dCBT-I for both insomnia and depression across demographic groups [65–67]. However, there is a lack of research surrounding the use and feasibility of dMBT-I specifically for older adults in the community. Moreover, greater clarity is required to confirm evidence surrounding the benefits of MBT-I for improving overall well-being and mental health. This would allow clinicians to target sleep as a modifiable risk factor to improve health outcomes in aging populations. Ultimately, a feasible, digital intervention could potentially become a widely disseminable, financially accessible, and effective treatment for older adults in the community. This would reduce the pressure on limited face-to-face service providers and provide an alternative avenue for individuals who may not benefit from existing treatments.

Aims

The aim of this study is to investigate the feasibility of a digital MBT-I intervention and its immediate and longer-term effects on the sleep and mental health of older adults in the community. The intervention is being compared against the frequently used sleep hygiene control due to its limited efficacy as a standalone treatment [68]. It is hypothesized that there will be significant improvements in insomnia severity (primary outcome), pre-sleep arousal, dysfunctional beliefs about sleep, and symptoms of anxiety and depression (secondary outcomes) for older adults receiving the digital MBT-I intervention compared to those receiving the digital Sleep Hygiene program after completing the intervention and at the 3-month follow-up.

Methods

Trial design

This study is a prospective, superiority, open-label, parallel-arm, longitudinal randomized control trial (RCT) designed to test the efficacy and feasibility of A Mindful Way for Healthy Sleep (AMW), a digital MBT-I intervention for insomnia, in older adults aged 55 and above. It is registered on the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (#ACTRN12623000839606) and is reported according to the CONSORT, SPIRIT-Outcomes 2022, and TIDieR guidelines (Appendices A, B and C respectively in Supplementary Materials).

Participants will be followed over 12 months, with data collection on self-reported outcomes occurring at screening (T0), baseline (T1, Week 1), post-intervention (T2, Week 6), and 3 months (T3). For the intervention group only, further follow-ups will be conducted 6 months (T4) and 12 months (T5) post-intervention. Follow-ups after 3 months will be ceased in control group because it would not be ethical to withhold treatment for participants for a full year.

After screening, participants will be block randomized to either the MBT-I intervention or sleep hygiene control condition. Neither participants nor researchers will be blinded to the condition they are allocated to. However, participants are blinded to specific study hypotheses and only aware that feasibility of the intervention is being investigated. After the 3-month follow-up, participants in the Sleep Hygiene condition will be invited to cross over to the intervention group if they meet inclusion criteria and will complete all procedures as per the intervention group. At each time point, participants will be asked to complete online questionnaires to assess outcomes. The recruitment, intervention, researcher contact, and follow-ups will all be conducted virtually via website, phone, or video calls.

Amendments to the trial design include clarification of exclusion criteria from “other sleep disorders” to “unmanaged other sleep disorders” to recognize the high comorbidity of sleep disorders in older adults but still control for confounds. Duration of sleep diary was also shortened to 1 week on either end of the program to reduce participant burden and streamline timeframe of study.

Participants

Older adults aged 55 and above, residing in Australia, who report sleep difficulty (indicated by Insomnia Severity Index ≥ 8) will be recruited. Cut-off of eight and above has previously been established to detect cases of insomnia in community-dwelling older populations [69–71]. Participants are required to have consistent access to a stable internet connection and a computer device to complete the questionnaires, sleep diary, and sleep program modules. To preserve the representativeness of the older adult sample, participants on stable medications for health conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, cholesterol, etc., typical in aging populations, will not be excluded. Exclusion criteria include current diagnosis of (1) severe or high-risk psychiatric conditions (depressive, bipolar, schizophrenia/psychotic, substance-related, obsessive-compulsive, trauma-related and stressor-related disorders); (2) neurodevelopmental disorders (dementia, intellectual disability or cognitive disorders such as traumatic brain injury); (3) and other unmanaged sleep disorders (e.g. sleep apnea, restless legs).

Recruitment

Recruitment will be conducted both online and offline. Online recruitment will be undertaken through social media sites and groups, interest group websites and newsletters (e.g. U3A, Sleep Disorders Australia, Probus Clubs), health network advertisement pages (e.g. Sleep Health Foundation), and recruitment databases, including Monash University participant registry and research registries of sleep studies in which participants have consented to be contacted with details for other studies. Offline recruitment will be completed via flyers and posters around university campuses, libraries and community centers and through contacting health professionals such as General Practitioners (GPs).

Screening and consent

Interested individuals will be directed to the study website (https://basemindfulsleep.wixsite.com/mindminc-study). The landing webpage of the website has a “Join the study” button, when clicked, will direct the individual to a Google Form to leave their details so that they can be contacted for further information. On the Google Form, they can review the explanatory statement and select a booking time for an information session. The statement contains a summary of the trial protocol, its aims, implications, and participant requirements as well as their rights when participating in the study. Upon reading the explanatory statement, if interested, potential participants can book the information session and have any questions about the study answered by a researcher. During this phone call, they will be advised of the timeline and study requirements.

After the information session, participants receive an email with a link to the online consent form via RedCap (Research Electronic Data Capture [72, 73]). Informed consent will require ticking “yes” or “no” against study components listed on the form and an electronic signature. Subsequently, online screening surveys to assess for eligibility criteria and plans to travel during the pre-screening and intervention period are completed. Next, a final intake session is conducted via video call with the researcher. Researchers will initiate brief cognitive screening using the mini–Mental State Examination (mMSE), conduct a risk assessment based on the study’s risk protocol to determine risk of harm to self or others, and document the tech devices used to engage with the program.

Once screening has been completed, eligibility to take part in the trial will be assessed. If participants are deemed ineligible under the exclusion criteria, they will be notified. If plans to begin new medication or travel in the upcoming weeks are revealed, intake into the study is rescheduled to a suitable time after the event, e.g. 2–4 weeks after new medication.

Randomization and assessment

Participants will be allocated to either control or intervention arms via block random allocation, stratified by gender, and clinical cut-offs for Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)-9 (<10) and Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) (>14). A randomization script was generated in R software to create and assign a random sequence to participants in each block. Block sizes were set to four, with number of blocks made changeable. All participants maintain a sleep diary (2 minutes/day) for the 1 week prior to beginning either intervention and the final week of the program to evaluate changes to patterns of self-reported sleep. They will then be sent the baseline assessment for completion online via a link to REDCap. The schedule for the administration of outcome measures throughout the trial is in Table 1. Researchers will call participants to evaluate any elevated risk and distress in Week 4 (15 minutes) via phone call. Outcome questionnaires and exit surveys will be administered via REDCap after Week 6 (30–45 minutes). Further follow-up assessments will involve the same questionnaires (30–40 minutes) and be repeated at the 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month timepoints. If a participant decides to withdraw, they will be asked to complete post-intervention questionnaires and an exit survey at that time, and data collected is carried forward as representative values.

Intervention and control

Participants in the intervention group will gain access to a 6-week self-directed digital MBT-I program, called AMW, delivered at the website www.amindfulway.com.au, which can be accessed with any computer or mobile device. The program involves a series of video lessons and six modules teaching formal and informal mindfulness skills, alongside behavioral change using sleep restriction and stimulus control for insomnia based on Ong and Sholtes MBT-I protocol [74] led by Dr Withers, a clinical psychologist and mindfulness teacher with over 20 years of experience. The program has been validated for use in adults [40]. Each module takes 1.5–2 hours each week and builds on the knowledge and homework from the previous week. Table 2 summarizes lesson content. Homework involves 15–30 minutes of daily meditation using audio recordings, making behavioral and cognitive changes, and maintaining a sleep diary as instructed during the course.

Participants in the control group will be given access to a self-directed sleep hygiene package released every 2 weeks for the 6-week duration via the AMW website (session content in Table 2). This information contains videos also presented by Dr Withers and some reading documents with key information on sleep hygiene sourced from factsheets on the Sleep Health Foundation [75]. Ethics and dissemination

This study was approved by the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee (#38104). Verbal assent is provided during a phone call, and written informed consent is provided through REDCap’s online e-consent framework. Any adverse events occurring during the trial will be recorded via self-report at each data collection time point. If these adverse events are related to the trial, participants have been advised to inform researchers immediately and contact their healthcare provider for support. Further, participants are informed of their right to discontinue participation at any time.

All data is secured in encrypted folders hosted on Monash University servers. A participant ID file will be stored separately to responses from questionnaires. Participants will be assigned IDs to link their response files across different time points. Identifiable data will only be available to researchers involved in data collection and analysis. De-identified and aggregated data may be reviewed by other researchers. Data will be retained for 15 years from completion of study before destroyed. Study findings will be disseminated via publications in scientific journals, conferences, talks with stakeholder groups, and brief summaries for participants. Authorship agreements are aligned to the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) Authorship guidelines.

Trial status

This trial began recruiting in September 2023, and data collection is ongoing.

Data monitoring

A Trial Management Group, including project investigators Dr Jackson, Dr Wiley, and Sanjivini, will be responsible for all aspects of the trial, including data monitoring and decisions about trial termination.

Data analysis

Sample size

A priori analysis conducted in G*Power v. 3.1.9.7 determined the sample size required to sufficiently power the analysis. Effects of mindfulness intervention are in the moderate (Cohen’s d = 0.46–0.61) to large (d > 0.8) range in studies with similar protocols using self-report measures and clinician-led intervention [12]. The RCT evaluating this dMBT-I in an adult population also demonstrated a large effect size (d = 1.49) [40]. Conservatively, a moderate value of d = 0.6 (f = 0.3) was set with α = 0.05 powered at β = 0.90. An estimated total sample size of 76 was recommended to detect a significant between-groups effect on the primary outcome measure ISI at post-intervention. As digitally delivered interventions can have higher rates of participant withdrawal, especially in older populations [76, 77], a generous attrition rate of 30 per cent was set, and an aim of recruiting 108 participants (54 per group) was determined.

Statistical analysis plan

As standard practice in analyzing clinical trial data, intent-to-treat (ITT) analysis will be used [78]. Linear mixed models (LMM) will be created to compare the change in outcomes between baseline, post-intervention, and at the 3-month follow-up due to their ability to handle longitudinal data, including missing data and within-subject correlation. For clearer indication of change in outcome, a constrained longitudinal data analysis model will also be used to compare the intervention and control groups [79]. All statistical tests will be conducted two-sided with α = 0.05 and 95% CI. Cohen’s d will be used to estimate effect size.

The primary outcome to assess efficacy is ISI. Secondary and tertiary outcomes include measures of sleep, well-being, cognition, and mindfulness practice. Initially, to test the hypothesis, the model will include the treatment group (MBT-I vs sleep hygiene) as a between-subjects factor, time (dummy coded for pre, post, 3-month follow-up) as a within-subjects factor, and the group-time interaction as fixed effects and participants as the random effect (intercept and slope). Later, further socio-demographic factors will be added to the model to explore interaction and moderation effects. Simple slopes will be calculated and reported on to help understand these effects. Post hoc exploratory analyses will be conducted to examine whether sleep (ISI) or cognitive arousal (Pre-sleep Arousal Scale, PSAS-C) mediates anxiety (Generalized Anxiety Disorder, GAD-7) or depression (PHQ-9).

Discussion

This trial aims to examine the feasibility of a digital MBT-I program in older adults and to determine whether older adults will see improvements in symptoms of insomnia severity, cognitive arousal, and DBAS. Further, is it expected that changes in sleep will be accompanied by improvements to symptoms of anxiety and depression. Support for this hypothesis will build on the current literature supporting dMBT-I as an effective and feasible intervention for addressing insomnia in older adults [40]. These outcomes would also support to the metacognitive model of insomnia and highlight the use of MBT-I in directly addressing metacognitions with flow-on effects on sleep and wellbeing. For stakeholders in the community, it would add to a growing evidence base for digital sleep intervention for managing health outcomes for older adults. Further, it may clarify what barriers exist to delivering sleep interventions to older adults digitally and highlight how programs can overcome these.

The demand to optimize digital intervention for sleep health is rising due to the highly prevalent nature of insomnia and the resulting increased disease burden, leading to poorer health outcomes if left untreated [20, 60]. However, dedicated one-to-one consultation with trained sleep clinicians is not always feasible for such a large population [80]. Due to the limited number of trained professionals, long waitlists, and access issues like geographic distance and cost inherent with specialized clinics, there is a critical need for interventions that can be disseminated faster, bypass access issues, and help individuals in earlier stages of their disorder [81]. If results for this study support that older adults find digitally delivered interventions acceptable, then this study will add evidence to using digital sleep interventions as an avenue to address these unmet needs.

Existing trials for digital sleep intervention commonly evaluate digital CBT-I in adult populations. This study is novel as it is investigating digital MBT-I in older adults, offering a unique perspective to the current gap in literature regarding this approach. Further, existing MBT-I studies are limited in their conclusions due to lack of longitudinal follow-up [48]. A strength of this trial is following the intervention group for 12 months post their completion of the intervention. Moreover, due to the exploratory approach of this trial, a variety of measures corresponding to wellbeing, mental health, and sleep are being analyzed, thus helping clarify the mixed results surrounding links between improved sleep and wellbeing in literature. Recruiting from a community sample helps mirror the heterogeneity present in real-world settings. However, it may also act as a limitation, considering benefits from intervention are more clearly captured via large effect sizes in purely clinical samples. Further, due to the highly self-directed nature of the intervention, it is possible that we are not able to fully reflect experiences of older adults living in assisted care settings. Given the nature of the digital intervention, which is delivered directly to participant homes across Australia, only self-report outcomes will be used and will not be paired with objective measures like actigraphy due to logistical challenges and to increase the feasibility of recruitment and data collection. This limits the ability to determine if sleep patterns are objectively changing or if only individual perceptions of sleep patterns are changing. However, self-report measures are typically used in evaluating dCBT-I in other randomized controlled trials [82]. Thus, if the intervention is found feasible and effective in this trial, it could represent significant development in the space of digital sleep health for older adults in the community. It may provide inroads to digital sleep intervention being used as a standalone treatment or even a model of stepped intervention. This could benefit both clinicians and older adults by promoting increased access and viable alternative for people on waitlists or individuals who do not fully qualify for clinical intervention at their current presentation of symptoms.
