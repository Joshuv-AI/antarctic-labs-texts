Physiological quotients and mortality: redefining lung function interpretation beyond FEV1

Introduction

How best to interpret lung function results is a contentious topic. In 2012, the Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) published multi-ethnic reference equations for spirometry [1]. These provide ethnicity-specific equations that account for the variation in “normal” values seen in different populations around the globe. In recent years, inspired by the seminal paper by Burney and Hooper [2], an ethnicity-neutral approach to lung function interpretation has been implemented, using a single global standard, specific to age, sex and height, to which all individuals are compared. This culminated in the publication of the GLI global reference equations in 2023 [3]. The rationale behind this move is that there is minimal variation in the lower limit of normal (5th percentile of a normal non-smoking population) for the forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio across different world regions, and that variation seen in FVC across world regions is likely due to impaired lung function, rather than lower values in certain regions being normal [4]. Despite the move towards ethnicity-neutral reference equations, there are concerns about reclassification and the impact this could have on diagnosis and treatment decisions [5–7].

Other measures of lung function, including diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) and total lung capacity (TLC), are vital for clinical decision making, especially when diagnosing and establishing the severity of interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) and COPD [8]. Debate around how best to interpret these measurements is limited, largely because reference equations are restricted to those derived from healthy European populations and are almost universally adopted irrespective of ethnicity [9, 10]. A major limitation of these equations is that they do not include predicted values for those >85 years old for DLCO and >80 years old for TLC, which for an ageing population, with increasing multimorbidity, is a major concern [11].

A different approach to interpreting lung function was proposed by Miller et al. [12] in 2010, who derived the FEV1 quotient (FEV1Q), a physiological quotient that describes how far an individual is from the 1st percentile (minimally survivable value) for FEV1. Miller et al. [12] used hospital-based data and found that the 1st percentile for FEV1 was different between males (0.50 L) and females (0.40 L), but stable across age. They defined FEV1Q as the measured FEV1 divided by the 1st percentile for FEV1. Miller et al. [12] further found that FEV1Q was a better predictor of mortality than FEV1 expressed as percent predicted or z-score. These results were recently replicated using data from the NHANES and UNOS databases, where it was further shown that the 1st percentile for FEV1 did not vary by ethnicity [13].

Due to the limitations of reference equations, which include the under-representation of older adults and changes to diagnostic classifications when switching between equations, alternative methods of interpreting lung function results are urgently needed. However, to date, physiological quotients have only been derived for FEV1. We used data from two separate UK hospitals to explore the feasibility and clinical utility of deriving physiological quotients for other important lung function measures, including FEV1/FVC, FVC, DLCO, transfer coefficient of the lung for carbon monoxide (KCO), alveolar volume (VA) and TLC.

Methods

Study population

We analysed data from adults (aged ≥18 years) referred for lung function testing at Cambridge University Hospital (CUH) and Royal Papworth Hospital (RPH) NHS Foundation Trusts (Cambridge, UK), for any referral reason, between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2023. Demographic data included age at time of test, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), self-reported ethnicity (Southeast Asian, Northeast Asian, Black, White or Other/mixed), smoking history (never or ever) and referral reason. Data were included in this study from the first instance a patient performed spirometry, single-breath gas transfer and body plethysmography in a single testing session, and had acceptable and reproducible lung function measurements according to the most up-to-date American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) standards [14–16]. Data were excluded if the patient had opted out of their data being used for research purposes. Approval to use the data was provided by the EHR Research and Innovation (ERIN) database data access committee at CUH (IRAS: 318784; study reference: A097236) and the Papworth Cardiorespiratory Physiology Research database (PCRPRD) data access committee at RPH (IRAS: 346834; study reference: PCRPRD0008). Research was conducted according to the principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Lung function

At CUH and RPH, spirometry, single-breath gas transfer and body plethysmography were performed on the MasterScreen PFT Pro (Vyaire Medical, Mettawa, IL, USA). Trained respiratory physiologists instructed all tests, and only those equivalent to grades A or B according to ATS/ERS quality standards were included in our analysis [14–16]. Spirometry measures included FVC (L), FEV1 (L) and FEV1/FVC; single-breath gas transfer DLCO (mmol·min−1·kPa−1), KCO (mmol·min−1·kPa−1·L−1) and VA (L); and plethysmography TLC (L). For spirometry, raw values were converted into z-scores and percent predicted using GLI global reference equations [3]. For single-breath gas transfer and plethysmography, GLI reference equations were used [9, 10]. The lower limit of normal for lung function measures was defined as a z-score of −1.645 or as the 5th percentile of a healthy non-smoking population.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the ability of the derived quotients to discriminate all-cause mortality in comparison to lung function measures expressed in raw units, z-scores and percent predicted. Data on all-cause mortality were extracted from the electronic health records of patients at both sites. Mortality status from electronic health records was cross-checked with the NHS Personal Demographics Service, which receives and stores mortality data reported by general practices and acute trusts within 1 working day of a death occurring.

Statistics

Analysis was performed separately for data from CUH and RPH. Normality of the data was determined through inspection of Q–Q plots and histograms. For normally distributed data, the mean and standard deviation were reported, while for non-normal data, median and interquartile range (IQR) were reported. Categorical variables were summarised as the number and percentage in each group. For each lung function measure, we calculated the 1st percentile using linear interpolation, which has no distributional assumptions. We then computed the 95% confidence intervals using the exact binomial method. We stratified this by sex (male/female) and by age group (<40, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69 and ≥70 years). We plotted results graphically for CUH and RPH, and as a consensus among the research team decided on a 1st percentile value that was representative of any variation seen across sex and age. We fitted graphs with polynomial trend lines to show variation in the 1st percentile across age groups. We separately calculated 1st percentile values for non-White individuals and compared them to the consensus values. Finally, we calculated the physiological quotients (Q=measured value/1st percentile value) and used descriptive statistics to demonstrate how they varied according to sex, age group and referral reason.

Differences in physiological quotient values between males and females were compared by the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test. Differences across age groups and referral reasons were compared by ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test as appropriate.

For the survival analyses, we summarised the characteristics of those patients alive and deceased for each site. We used the independent samples t-test to investigate the differences in mean values and the Mann–Whitney U-test for differences in medians. We used the Chi-squared test to evaluate differences in categorical variables. We used univariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to investigate the association of the lung function measures (per 1-unit increase) expressed in raw units, physiological quotients, z-scores and percent predicted with all-cause mortality. We compared the discriminative ability of the models using Harrell's C-index, and presented these graphically for each site with 95% confidence intervals. We performed sensitivity analyses expressing spirometry parameters as percent predicted and z-scores using the GLI ethnicity-specific reference equations [1]. We then generated Kaplan–Meier survival curves for different cut-offs of the physiological quotients, ranging from one to six turnovers from the 1st percentile value. To show clinical application of the quotients, we constructed curves for key lung function measures for patients referred for COPD at CUH and ILD RPH, the two largest cohorts. Finally, for each site, we used multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to evaluate the association of the physiological quotients with all-cause mortality. In model 1 we adjusted for age (years), sex (male/female), height (cm), smoking history (never/ever) and referral reason. In model 2 we adjusted for the same as model 1 but also included ethnicity (White/non-White) as a main effect and interaction term to check for confounding and effect modification. Results were considered significant if p<0.05. Analyses were performed using Stata version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

1st percentile values

Consensus 1st percentile values for spirometry measures are presented in table 2 and supplementary figures S1–S3. For FEV1 we found that the 1st percentiles had overlapping 95% confidence intervals across age groups but were different for males (0.50 L) and females (0.40 L) (supplementary figure S1). The same was seen for FVC, with the 1st percentile being ∼1.50 L for males and ∼1.20 L for females (supplementary figure S2). Polynomial trendlines for both parameters demonstrated 1st percentile values were generally higher in younger age groups. Interestingly for FEV1/FVC, the 1st percentile was stable across age groups and sex, with a universal 1st percentile of 0.15 (15%) (supplementary figure S3).

For single-breath gas transfer, consensus 1st percentile values are presented in table 2 and supplementary figures S4–S6. The 1st percentile for DLCO was relatively consistent across age and sex, at 1.60 mmol·min−1·kPa−1 (supplementary figure S4). KCO was similar, with a 1st percentile of 0.40 mmol·min−1·kPa·L−1 regardless of age or sex (supplementary figure S5). For VA, the 1st percentile appeared stable across age but was different for males (2.40 L) and females (2.00 L) (supplementary figure S6). Consensus 1st percentile values for TLC were stable across sex but different for males (2.60 L) and females (2.30 L) (table 2 and supplementary figure S7). Data points for TLC became more concentrated around the consensus 1st percentile when those with severe or very severe airflow obstruction according to Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease criteria were excluded, likely due to greater precision once those with significant hyperinflation were excluded (supplementary figure S8) [17].

The sample size was not large enough to perform stratified analyses by age for those of a non-White background; however, the overall 1st percentile values for non-White males and females at CUH were similar to those of the consensus 1st percentile for all lung function measurements (table 2).

The derived physiological quotients FEV1Q, FVCQ, FEV1/FVCQ, DLCOQ, KCOQ, VAQ and TLCQ decreased with age and varied by referral reason (supplementary tables S1 and S2).

The results of the univariable Cox regression analyses are presented in supplementary table S3. A 1-unit increase in lung function, whether expressed as a raw value, physiological quotient, z-score or percent predicted, was associated with lower all-cause mortality risk, with the magnitude of the estimates similar for CUH and RPH. For spirometry, the physiological quotients FEV1Q, FVCQ and FEV1/FVCQ had better discriminative ability with a higher Harrell's C-index compared with the other expressions, with z-score and percent predicted performing worst (figure 2 and supplementary table S3). Using GLI ethnicity-specific reference equations to derive z-score and percent predicted values did not materially change results (supplementary table S3). Similar was seen for single-breath gas transfer and plethysmography, where DLCOQ, KCOQ, VAQ and TLCQ had higher Harrell's C-indexes than other expressions of lung function, although 95% confidence intervals were closer (figure 2 and supplementary table S3). For patients referred to CUH with COPD, 388/1383 (28%) died during follow-up. For COPD patients, 1-unit increase in FEV1Q was associated with a 30% lower risk of all-cause mortality (hazard ratio (HR) 0.70, 95% CI 0.65–0.75), DLCOQ a 49% lower risk (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.46–0.56) and VAQ a 68% lower risk (HR 0.32, 95% CI 0.25–0.42). Figure 3 presents Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrating the dose–response relationships. Of patients referred to RPH with ILD, 2024/4681 (43%) died during follow-up. A 1-unit increase in FVCQ was associated with a 59% lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR 0.41, 95% CI 0.38–0.44), DLCOQ a 49% lower risk (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.49–0.53), VAQ a 67% lower risk (HR 0.33, 95% CI 0.30–0.36) and TLCQ a 45% lower risk (HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.51–0.60). Table 4 shows the results of the multivariable Cox regression analyses. After adjustment for potential confounders, all physiological quotients were associated with all-cause mortality, except for FEV1/FVCQ. The magnitude of the effects was similar for estimates from CUH and RPH, and the associations were not confounded or modified by ethnicity. The strongest association was seen for VAQ, with a 1-unit increment being associated with a 69% lower risk of all-cause mortality.

Discussion

Using data from two different hospitals, we show the feasibility of generating physiological quotients for a range of lung function measures, based on 1st percentile values that are stable across age and sex. We found that these physiological quotients generally have greater discriminative ability in the prediction of all-cause mortality than lung function expressed as raw units, z-scores and percent predicted, offering an alternative means of interpreting lung function independent of reference equations.

For FEV1, we found that the 1st percentile was different for males (0.50 L) and females (0.40 L), but was relatively stable across age, similar to the results of Miller et al. [12]. More recent studies have found slightly lower values for the 1st percentile for males and females [13, 18]; however, confidence intervals overlapped with the 1st percentile values identified in our study. Balasubramanian et al. [13] used data from the UNOS lung transplant database, while Björklund et al. [18] used data from elderly COPD patients on long-term oxygen therapy. Both patient populations are highly specific, likely terminally ill and not representative of the wide range of referrals made to general respiratory clinics. Like Miller et al. [12], our cohorts reflected a broad range of referral reasons, and as stated by Björklund et al. [18], changes to the original values of 0.50 L for males and 0.40 L for females should not be made based on highly selective cohorts. Like previous studies, we found that FEV1Q was associated with all-cause mortality [12, 13, 18–20], with higher values being protective. We also found that FEV1Q performed better than z-scores and percent predicted, in line with previous publications [12, 13]. Like for FEV1, we found that the 1st percentile for FVC was different for males (1.50 L) and females (1.20 L) but relatively stable across age. We found that the derived FVCQ was associated with all-cause mortality, and was a better predictor than FVC expressed using z-scores and percent predicted. The association of FVC with mortality is well documented in general and patient populations [21–23]. Our data suggest that FVCQ may offer greater predictive ability; however, our results need to be replicated in different cohorts, particularly those with greater ethnic diversity. Interestingly, we found that the 1st percentile for FEV1/FVC was the same for males and females at 0.15 (15%). Like other expressions of FEV1/FVC, FEV1/FVCQ was weakly associated with mortality in unadjusted models, and not associated with mortality after adjustment for confounding. Therefore, it is likely that expressing FEV1/FVC as a physiological quotient offers little benefit clinically, over the use of raw units or z-scores. Other studies have shown that FEV1/FVC alone is a poor predictor of mortality [23] or that it has a complex U-shaped association, where the association is mainly evident at extreme values [24]. Therefore, FEV1/FVC is better placed to define airflow obstruction, with other lung function measures used to monitor disease severity and mortality risk, as is currently recommended [8, 17].

We found that the 1st percentile values for DLCO (1.60 mmol·min−1·kPa−1) and KCO (0.40 mmol·min−1·kPa−1·L−1) were relatively consistent regardless of age or sex. The derived DLCOQ and KCOQ were strongly associated with mortality in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses, and discriminated better than z-scores and percent predicted values, although the difference was not as clear as it was for spirometry measures. Ours is the first study to derive physiological quotients for DLCO and KCO, and it is important that these 1st percentile values are replicated in other cohorts. The association of DLCO with mortality is seen in many respiratory conditions and is a key outcome measure in clinical trials [25–27]. The advantage of DLCOQ and KCOQ is that they do not rely on reference equations, which are based on data from individuals of White European origin and restricted to those aged <85 years [10].

We assessed lung volumes using both VA and TLC. In both instances, 1st percentile values were stable across age but different for males and females, reflecting the known anatomical differences between sexes. The 1st percentile for VA was 2.40 L for males and 2.0 L for females, while TLC was 2.60 and 2.30 L, respectively. Regardless of the metric used, VA was more strongly associated with mortality than TLC, with VAQ and TLCQ performing best. We found that VAQ had the strongest association with all-cause mortality of all the physiological quotients after adjustment for confounding. This is likely explained by the fact VA declines in both obstructive lung disease due to gas trapping and restrictive disease due to volume loss, while the association of TLC with mortality is inverse in COPD [28]. In a recent study we demonstrated that VA is just as good as TLC in identifying restriction [29], and the results of this study add further weight to the utility of VA over TLC when assessing lung volume, especially if mortality is the outcome of interest.

Following on from the concept of Miller et al. [12], we have derived physiological quotients for a range of lung function measures which can be applied universally. These quotients are at least as good, if not better than currently available metrics when discriminating all-cause mortality, providing an alternative means of interpreting lung function results, previously restricted to FEV1. When applying this to patients with COPD and ILD specifically, we have provided estimates of survival probability at different quotient thresholds, which can be used to inform practice. For example, in COPD, a FEV1Q, DLCOQ or VAQ of 2 is associated with a 5-year survival probability of ∼70%, while in ILD, an FVCQ or DLCOQ of 2 is associated with a 5-year survival probability of ∼60% and ∼50%, respectively. Improving discrimination of mortality is important when considering that heart disease and COPD are the 1st and 4th leading causes of death globally (World Health Organization) and lung function is routinely assessed in these populations to determine pre-operative fitness and disease severity [17, 30]. However, we acknowledge that further work is needed to replicate our findings and to investigate associations with other clinical end-points, such as symptoms, quality of life and disease classification. Until this work is done, physiological quotients cannot replace reference equation-based metrics.

The strengths of our study include the large sample of lung function data from two different hospitals and the diversity of different referral reasons, which included patients typical of a general respiratory clinic (e.g. COPD and asthma) and a specialist transplant centre (e.g. ILDs, heart failure and pulmonary hypertension), which make our findings widely applicable. The limitations of our study include the lack of ethnic diversity, with the majority of patients being of White European origin. To counteract this, we investigated the utility of the physiological quotients in non-White individuals and controlled for ethnicity in our statistical models, which demonstrated that the association with mortality did not vary by ethnicity. However, further research is needed to directly replicate our findings in ethnically diverse populations. We also did not have sufficient sample to calculate 1st percentiles for those aged <30 years separately, likely due to a lower burden of chronic respiratory disease in this age group. Nor did we have sufficient data on patients at extremes of height (<130 or >190 cm); however, we adjusted our regression models for height and the strong association between the physiological quotients and mortality remained. A further limitation is that we did not have information on other causes of mortality, which would have provided more granular data on the association with survival. Additionally, mean follow-up was 5.5 years, so it is difficult to predict how the physiological quotients would perform when looking at long-term survival. We also did not have data on other outcome metrics such as symptoms, quality of life and clinical decline, which will be necessary if the concept of physiological quotients is to be widely adopted. Finally, we acknowledge that consensus 1st percentile values do not precisely fit all age groupings for some measures, and a degree of subjective clinical judgement was required based on the overlap of 95% confidence intervals. We felt this approach was important to preserve simplicity and offer a genuine alternative to reference equations.

In conclusion, we have shown that it is feasible to derive physiological quotients for lung function measures that are largely independent of age and in some cases even sex. These quotients have greater discriminative ability in the prediction of mortality than existing metrics such as z-scores and percent predicted, providing an alternative standard for lung function interpretation. Future studies should replicate our findings in diverse cohorts and focus on cause-specific mortality and other clinically important outcomes.
