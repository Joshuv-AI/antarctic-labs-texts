Reducing Rates and Risk of Stroke in Adults With Congenital Heart Disease: What Can We Do Now, and What Should We Do Next?
Authors: Meshari Alsaeed, Thalia S Field
Published: 2024

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common birth defect, affecting approximately 1% of all live births. 1 Importantly, due to medical and surgical advances over the past several decades, it is also now the fastest‐growing area of adult cardiology. Even for complex congenital heart lesions, which comprise approximately 10% of CHD, survival well into adulthood is now commonplace, and adults with CHD now outnumber children living with CHD. 2

A longer lifespan with CHD, however, is also associated with a longer exposure to health risks, both later‐stage CHD‐related complications as well as general aging‐related conditions, 3 and potentially in synergy. 4 In particular, strategies to optimize brain health in this population remain a priority for further research. People with CHD may experience baseline susceptibilities to their brain health from neurodevelopmental and acquired insults beginning in early life 5 and may be at increased risk for dementia, particularly earlier‐onset disease. 6 For individuals with CHD, who have an overall increased risk for stroke, it is therefore critical to have a better understanding as to which individuals with CHD are at highest risk, when in their lives they are most susceptible, and why. Neurocognitive trajectories in people with CHD in particular may be affected by stroke, which in the general population is the leading cause of adult‐acquired disability and a major risk factor for cognitive impairment and dementia. 7

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Sodhi‐Berry and colleagues describe incidence and risk factors for ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke in adults with CHD (ACHD) using administrative data from Western Australia from 2000 to 2017. 8 The authors calculated estimates for age‐ and sex‐stratified incidence rate ratios for stroke in ACHD versus the general population and assessed ACHD‐specific risk factors for stroke using a nested case–control design. The study contributes additional knowledge regarding rates of stroke in ACHD, described in only a handful of population‐based studies to date. It also contributes data from Australasia; previous estimates are from North American and Northern European data. 9 , 10 , 11 Information capturing Indigeneity status and rurality are additional strengths. Identifying CHD within administrative data can be a challenge in and of itself due to misclassification alongside codes for acquired cardiac disease. 12 The authors used methods that were previously validated outside of Australia, but their estimated prevalence of CHD in Western Australia resembles previously published rates and provides additional reassurance regarding the robustness of their approach.

The study population was limited to first‐ever stroke in individuals ages 18 to 64. Over a mean follow‐up of 11 (SD 6) years in 7916 individuals with CHD, cumulative incidence of stroke was 3.15% overall. This equated to a rate of 258 (95% CI, 220–295)/100 000 person‐years for ischemic stroke (rates were 299 [95% CI, 259–340] when combined with the 11.7% of strokes that could not be specified by type) and 46 (95% CI, 31–62) for hemorrhagic stroke (~61% intracerebral hemorrhage, 39% subarachnoid hemorrhage). In the small cohort with intracerebral brain hemorrhage, rates were 4‐fold higher (though not significantly so) in older versus younger individuals. Overall stroke rates in the ACHD cohort were 6.5 (95% CI, 6.4–6.6) times higher than in the general population, with a larger differential in the younger (incidence rate ratio [IRR] in ages 18–44, 13.4 [95% CI, 13.2–13.6]) compared with the older subgroup (IRR in ages 45–64 years, 4.8 [95% CI, 4.6–5.0]). This differential risk by age is larger than those reported in previous population‐based cohorts. As the authors suggest, this may be due to differing methodology and definitions between studies. In particular, overall stroke rates in the general population have decreased significantly in the past 2 decades, particularly in older relative to younger adults. 13 Other population‐based cohorts are either inclusive of several decades predating contemporary vascular risk factor control 10 , 11 or were published prior having data from the most recent decade of advances in applied primary prevention. 9

Acquired risk factors associated with stroke within the ACHD population included recent surgery, endocarditis, and conventional vascular risk factors, including hypertension, tobacco use, and peripheral vascular disease. Recent surgery in particular was associated with heightened risk. Burden of comorbid illness, and mental health disorders, were also associated with increased risk of both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke. With respect to lesion types, the highest risk involved shunt lesions and left‐sided valvular repair/replacement. Although at least 1 other population‐based study identified atrial shunts as being a high‐risk lesion for stroke in ACHD, 10 different approaches to lesion classification between studies may play some role in variable results. A more consistent approach to lesion classification in future work may prove challenging given the additional axis of varying physiology. Additionally, classification efforts may also be stymied by changing surgical approaches over time and their later complications, such as the shift from atrial to arterial switch for dextro‐transposition of the great arteries. 14 Still, efforts toward consensus‐based classification will enhance applicability of future work to clinical care.

Relevant takeaways for current clinical practice include an emphasis on aggressive control of conventional vascular risk factors. Similar to non‐ACHD cohorts of younger adults with stroke, modifiable risk factors, including hypertension and tobacco use, are important risk predictors. 15 The association between cardiac surgery and ischemic stroke is also well described in the general population. For ACHD in particular, however, dedicated multidisciplinary CHD‐related expertise is key to ensuring optimal perioperative and postoperative monitoring and management. 16 The high association in this study between recent surgery and intracranial hemorrhage in particular is unexpected and mechanistically unclear. Whether this finding may be due to chance in the context of a low number of cases overall, from misclassification of ischemic strokes with secondary hemorrhagic transformation, or from other factors is unknown and should be reassessed in future work. Further, despite the broad definition for mental health disorders in this study, their association with heightened stroke risk highlights the need for improved diagnosis and management of psychiatric disease, the most common comorbidity in people with CHD. 17 The relationships between psychiatric disease and CHD, and their impacts on prognosis, are complex. Further study is warranted to assess optimal leverage points for interventions and their timing. Still, this should not delay improved access to, and integration of, mental health supports within CHD multidisciplinary care. 18

It is also important to note that the overall stroke‐associated mortality rate in this study of 5% is low, both in comparison to general as well as ACHD cohorts poststroke. This may reflect increased ascertainment of minor or subclinical strokes, for example, through use of postoperative diffusion‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging, compared with other cohorts. It is also possible that, in some cases, neuroimaging done for a nonstroke indication such as headache may have revealed old, but previously unknown, infarcts that were misclassified as new incident strokes in administrative data. Although improved treatments for acute ischemic stroke have contributed to better functional outcomes, they have generally not reduced mortality 19 , 20 and thus are less likely to have contributed to lower rates of death in this study.

The study team opted to focus on incident stroke only and restricted their analysis to adults under the age of 65. The former is an accepted approach in health services stroke research to reduce misclassification of readmissions or late sequelae related to an index event. However, we would encourage future work examining rates and risk factors for recurrent stroke in ACHD for multiple reasons. First, previous stroke is an important risk factor for stroke recurrence that bears inclusion in risk‐stratification models. Second, recurrent strokes compound risk of functional and cognitive impairment. Third, for the ACHD population in particular, mechanisms for stroke may modulate over time, with different implications for secondary prevention. An individual with a perioperative stroke as a younger adult may, in later life, have another stroke in the context of an atrial arrhythmia, intracardiac or conduit thrombosis, or other acquired issue, such as atherosclerotic disease. It is also highly relevant to people with living experience of CHD, as well as their health care providers, to understand factors associated with late‐life risks of stroke. Numbers of older adults living with CHD are growing. It is increasingly important for personalized, as well as system‐level, planning to know whether their risk profiles begin to resemble those of the general population in healthier surviving older individuals with CHD, or whether there are unique considerations compounding the effects of age‐related and CHD‐specific factors on risks of stroke or its implications for functional independence and cognition.

A final consideration for future related work would be to examine incidence trends and demographic characteristics in ACHD‐related stroke over time. A recent study using US‐based data from the National Inpatient Sample shows a dramatic increase in hospitalizations related to stroke and transient ischemic attack between 2000 and 2018 in ACHD. 21 Reasons are undetermined as of yet. It is important to know if this finding reflects a broader trend, and if it reflects the relative potential contribution from a progressively aging cohort with ACHD, growing numbers of surviving individuals with more complex and higher‐risk disease, or other factors.

To summarize, improving our understanding of the risks of stroke in ACHD, and how they evolve throughout the lifespan, is an important step towards optimizing neurocognitive trajectories in this growing population. The contribution from Sodhi‐Berry and colleagues highlights priorities for future prevention‐related research and opportunities for improved risk optimization in the here and now.
