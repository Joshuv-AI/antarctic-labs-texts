The effect of fatigue on static and dynamic balance in healthy young male athletes: a randomized trial
Authors: Anwar B Almutairi, Abdullah Alabdulwahab, Maraheb Alkhalidi, Saleh Alnouri, Mohammed Alkatan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of the study is to assess the effect of a running fatigue protocol on static (SB) and dynamic balance (DB) abilities using Wii balance board (WBB) and Y-balance (YB) tests, respectively, in young male recreational athletes.Materials and MethodsParticipants were randomized into control and experimental groups. They were examined on static and dynamic balance (pre- & post-tests). Then, the experimental group underwent a fatigue protocol, in which speed and incline increased one unit for each passing minute, and stopped when rate of perceived exertion level reached ≥ 18, and the plasma lactate level differed from the baseline by ≥ 4mmol/L.ResultsForty male athletes aged between 18–22 years old participated with 20 participants in each group. Between groups analysis showed that the control group scored significantly higher score on YB, however, the experimental group scored significantly lower on WBB in all conditions. Regression analysis revealed that, for every one unit increase in SB score, plasma lactate levels decrease by 0.051 (5.1%).ConclusionsReduced performance on both static and dynamic balance tests after a treadmill running fatigue protocol was observed in recreational young male athletes, compared to a randomly-assigned control group of their peers.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06901219, Retrospectively registered on 16/3/2025.Keywords: Static Balance, Fatigue, Athletes, Lactate, Dynamic Balance

Introduction

Fatigue is a complex phenomenon that lacks a consistent definition [1–3]. This could be caused by the variety of the dimensions of this phenomenon, including mental, physical, emotional, and cognitive. The newly proposed taxonomy by Enoka et. al [1, 3]. suggests that fatigue is a self-reported disabling symptom developed from perceived fatigability and performance fatigability [1, 3]. Perceived fatigability is defined as alterations in the sensation that adjusts the integrity of the performer [1]. However, performance fatigability is defined as a deterioration in an objective measure of performance over a distinct period of time [1]. Each attribute is further divided into 2 domains, perceived fatigability into homeostasis and psychological state, while performance fatigability into contractile function and muscle activation [1–3]. It is important to understand that many aspects contribute to fatigue and are affected by it. One of the main aspects affected by fatigue is balance abilities which impact daily activities and participation [4].

Balance is defined as the ability to maintain center of mass (COM) within the base of support (BOS), either during stability (i.e. static balance) or mobility (i.e., dynamic balance) [5]. Static balance is the ability to maintain postural stability with minimal movement, whereas dynamic balance is the ability to execute a task while preserving a stable posture [5–7]. This process relies on constant feedback from visual, somatosensory, and vestibular systems along with neuromuscular actions [4, 5]. The influence of fatigue on balance abilities is still not fully understood which is due to inconsistent methodology [1, 3, 8]. Furthermore, the majority of studies that examine fatigue in healthy participants or participants with different diseases, employ perceived fatigability only to ensure the reach of fatigue (e.g. psychological) [2, 9]. The incorporation of both perceived fatigability and performance fatigability ensures consistency and reproducibility of the methods used. This method was attempted by Huang and colleagues [4] where they used maximum heart rate with rating of perceived exertion using the Borg scale. Using squatting as their fatigue-inducing activities, they asked their participants to perform the y-balance test which was captured with motion analysis system [4]. They found that there were significant changes in terms of distance, range of motion (ROM), torque, and center of pressure (COP) displacement. This shows that fatigue has effects on dynamic balance and biomechanical abilities during day to day tasks [4]. Nevertheless, their attempt at including 2 types of assessment to ensure fatigue did not take into consideration the fatigue taxonomy when selecting their assessment, which led to the lack of diversity in the outcomes used.

Abdelkader et. al [10]. conducted a systematic review to examine the effect of fatigue on dynamic balance in athletes [10]. They identified 15 studies that had a low risk of bias, in which mostly recreational and competitive athletes participated. The review found a lack of consistency in terms of athletic populations and fatigue protocols, and the majority of studies incorporated pre-post designs without a control group comparison. It was also mentioned that there were observed differences between recreational and competitive athletes. The rationale provided was related to the focused training received by competitive athletes which would delay the effects of fatigue [10]. Although their review provided enormous insight, they did not mention the type of outcomes that would ensure the success of the fatigue protocol. Also, they only focused on dynamic balance, which did not encapsulate both types of balance [5].

Fatigue causes the motor unit to be disrupted and affects the shear and torque forces it applies, which leads to joint stability to become compromised [11, 12]. Proprioception has also been found to affect postural sway, reaction time, and balance [12]. It was reported that Taekwondo athletes had significantly decrease joint position sense after muscle fatigue [13]. Lack of joint position sense could be compromised during fatigue which could lead to balance issues. This is supported by reports of ankle sprains occurring in the last one-third or two-thirds of a race, which would suggest that altered ankle stability could be induced by the neuromuscular fatigue [11, 14].

Recreational athletes, alternatively termed as amateur athletes, differ from competitive athletes in terms of their commitment to the practiced sport. Competitive athletes are defined as athletes that receive a written contract with an institution, and entails receiving regular monetary compensation for full-time participation in a certain sport [15]. Recreational athletes have lower intensities of training, 2–3 times per week versus daily training of professional athletes [16, 17]. The types of athletes would have different motives and therefore different training styles, along with differences in professional coaching. Despite differences in training and coaching styles, it is reported that recreational soccer players sustain significantly more injuries during training compared to their professional peers [17, 18]. This demonstrates that this population may not have the necessary targeted training to overcome the injuries experienced, in spite of having a 17% higher rate of match exposure per player compared to professional athletes [16, 17].

Considering the evidence in the literature, the aim of the current study is to assess the effect of a running fatigue protocol, measured by both objective and subjective tools, on static and dynamic balance abilities using Wii balance board (WBB) and Y-balance (YB) tests, respectively, in young male recreational athletes. Our hypothesis is that fatigue would affect both static and dynamic abilities in the experimental group.

Materials and Methods

A randomized parallel trial utilizing a pre-test/post-test design was used to assess the effect of running fatigue on static and dynamic balance abilities in male recreational athletes. Only male athletes were recruited to avoid gender-based physiological differences. Recreational athletes were operationally defined as athletes that are not associated with an institution and do not receive an compensation for their participation in any sport [15]. To be included, participants had to be: a) male and aged between 18–22 years, and b) physically active (i.e. exercised ≥ 3 times weekly during the past three months). Participants were excluded if they reported a minor injury in the past two months, major injury in the past 12 months, or underwent a major primary surgery on their lower limb. Sample size analyses revealed that a total of 30 participants (15 per each group) were needed to have sufficient statistical power. To account for a 20% potential attrition rate, the final recruitment was adjusted to 40 participants. More details on the sample size analysis are provided in the statistical analysis section. The study adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines [19].

Ethical approval, consent to participate, and Trial registration

Ethical approval for the current study was obtained from the ethical committee in the college of Basic Education, Public Authority for Applied Education & Training (no. BE-17–14). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Signed written informed consent forms were obtained from all participants. ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06901219, retrospectively registered.

Procedure

Class announcements were used as the recruitment method at the physical education college to inform participants about the study. Afterwards, all participants provided demographic data (i.e. age in years and the number of times they exercised per week in the last 3 months) and anthropometric properties (i.e. Body mass index (BMI), fat%, visceral fat, and muscle mass) using Omron HBF-514C [Omron Healthcare, Inc., Illinois, USA] [20]. Participants were then randomly assigned to either an experimental group or control group. Randomization was completed using computer-generated sets of numbers 1–40 assigned to each participant randomly to indicate their group. Then, both groups underwent dynamic and static examination. Dynamic postural balance is a test designed to examine the ability to maintain postural stability in dynamic conditions [21] and was measured using Y-balance. In this test, participants were asked to single-leg stand on a block (starting with the left) and were instructed to push a block with their other leg as far as they can without losing their balance (i.e. lifting their leg of the moving block and placing it on the ground), while having their hands on their hips. The free blocks are placed anteriorly and posterolaterally of the participant in both directions, creating a Y shape with the stable block on the intersection of these lines. Distance reached was recorded for each direction. The directions of the test were anterior, posteromedial, posterolateral (pushing on the contralateral side). Participants had a practice trial before scores were recorded for the study [21]. The participants were then asked to switch their feet and preform the task again, with six trials recorded in total (i.e. successfully moving the block without losing balance) to minimize learning effect [21].

Static balance testing was performed using virtual reality interactive gaming system (Wii balance board [Nintendo, Kyoto, Japan]). The WBB contains four strain gauge-based load sensors, qualified to obtain center of pressure movement, which makes it valid and reliable to measure posture stability [6]. The participants were asked to stand still for 30 s in each condition, first with their hands on their sides with their feet shoulder-width apart together bilaterally weightbearing, then with unilateral support on the right, and finally with unilateral support on the left. All postures were performed under two conditions: eyes open and eyes closed. Balance score were collected as a percentage of the movement of COP (%) with higher scores indicating better balance.

After balance testing, a running fatigue protocol was implemented for the experimental group. Prior to the start of the fatigue protocol, lactate plasma level was measured using a portable blood lactate analyser (The Lactate Plus [Nova Biomedical, USA]) as a baseline. Blood collection was standardized for all participants starting with the tip of the right index finger and moving toward the right little finger with each testing. Alcohol wipes were used before each testing to ensure testing site hygiene, and an adhesive bandage was used to cover the site after the testing. Participants were requested to walk for five minutes on a treadmill (life fitness, T5 TREADMILL, Momentum, Ireland) with a speed of 5 kms/hour as a warm-up, the speed and incline were then increased one unit for each minute that passed. The participants had a printed version of the 6–20 Borg scale taped on the wall in front of the treadmill for ease of remembrance for the participants. Participants were asked to provide their rate of perceived exertion (RPE) every 5 min. If the participants reported RPE level of ≥ 18, the task would be stopped, and the plasma lactate level would be measured. If the plasma lactate level differed from the baseline level by ≥ 4mmol/L [8], the protocol would be terminated. Otherwise, the participants continued with the fatigue protocol until a difference of ≥ 4 mmol/L with plasma lactate level was achieved. Final RPE and plasma lactate level were recorded. Both groups had a re-assessment of dynamic postural balance (Y-balance), and static postural balance (WBB). The control group had the re-assessment after 20 min break (Fig. 1), which is the average time for a running fatigue protocol [22].

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM® SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The statistical significance level was accepted as α = 0.05. All the variables were evaluated by the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests to assess the normality distribution. Categorical variables were presented as count (n) and percentage (%), while continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation (mean ± SD), median and interquartile range [Median(IQR)], and standard error difference (mean ± SED). The sample size calculation was based on findings of a prior study that investigated differences in postural balance performance assessed by the Wii Balance Board System in sway area measurements among fallers and non-fallers with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.8) [23]. Although no studies to date have directly evaluated the effect of fatigue on balance, the Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT) has been noted to be a reliable and valid measure of dynamic balance in healthy adult populations [24]. For the proposed study, sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9 [25]. Determination of sample size for comparisons within-group (pre- vs. post-intervention of fatigue protocol) was completed using a paired samples t-test with the following parameters: two-sided test with significance level (α) = 0.05, power (1 – β) = 0.80, and a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.8). Based on the previous assumptions, a total of 30 participants (15 per each group) would need to be enrolled to have sufficient statistical power. To account for a 15% potential attrition rate, the final recruitment was adjusted to 34 participants. The YB tested were presented as unadjusted and adjusted values for leg length, which was calculated as distance reached divided by leg length then multiplied by 100 [21].

Paired samples t-test was utilized to determine if there was a significant difference between the pre and post-tests within each group. Once changes within the groups were analyzed, the independent t-tests were used to compare the mean differences between the experimental and control groups at the post-test stage. With regards to the plasma lactate levels, the paired samples t-test was used to determine if there was a significant difference in fatigue scores before and after the fatigue protocol. Furthermore, a two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to examine the effects of the intervention on dynamic postural stability (YB), and static balance (WBB) outcomes. In these models, within-subject factors included Time (two levels: pre-intervention and post-intervention), and between-subject factors included Group (two levels: experimental vs. control). The Time × Group interaction term represents the differential changes over time between groups and is therefore the primary effect of interest. For dynamic postural stability (YB), separate ANOVA models were implemented for each reach direction (anterior, posteromedial, posterolateral) and for each leg (right and left), with reach distances normalized to limb length to control for anthropometric variabilities. For static balance performance (WBB), separate ANOVAs were conducted for each condition: unilateral stance (right and left; eyes open and eyes closed) and bilateral stance (eyes open and eyes closed). For each ANOVA test, effect sizes for F-statistics were presented as partial eta-squared (η2p) and interpreted according to Cohen’s guidelines: as small (0.01), medium (0.06), and large (0.14) [26]. Additionally, multivariable linear regressions analyses were performed to explore was used to explore the relationship between the plasma lactate levels (fatigue score) and demographic, anthropometric variables, static postural balance, and/or dynamic postural stability. The primary outcome was defined as a continuous variable (fatigue score as plasma lactate concentration measured post-fatigue protocol). Predictors variables was defined as a continuous variable including age, weekly exercise frequency, body mass index (BMI), muscle mass, body fat percentage, visceral fat, YB score, and WBB score. A linear regression model with both an unadjusted and an adjusted regression coefficient was conducted. Adjusted models controlled for the potential confounding effects of BMI and body fat percentage, and separately for BMI and visceral fat. All assumptions of linear regressions were evaluated prior to analysis. The models were evaluated for linearity between predictors and outcomes using visualization of the standardized residuals against their normal scores by graphing Q-Q plots. In addition, independence of errors was evaluated using the Durbin–Watson test statistic. Furthermore, the variance of the residuals (homoscedasticity) was assessed by plots of standardized residuals versus predicted values. Moreover, multicollinearity was tested prior to model building using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) index,and values greater than 5 indicated multicollinearity [27]. For each model, results were presented as unstandardized coefficients (B), standard errors (SE), t-statistics, p-values, and model fit indices (R, R2, and F).

Results

Forty physically active male students aged between 18–22 years old participated in the current study (experimental group, n = 20; control group, n = 20). The median BMI of the sample was 28.2 kg/m2. There were no significant differences between groups based of the demographic and anthropometric variables (Table 1). The plasma lactate levels were significantly different pre- and post-fatigue protocol (pre- = 2.45 ± 1.05 vs. post- = 11.15 ± 4.99, p = 0.001).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to assess the effect of a running fatigue protocol, measured using both objective and subjective tools for static (WBB) and dynamic (YB) balance abilities in young male recreational athletes.

The experimental group had significantly lower scores post-fatigue protocol on static balance with single support on each side in the open eyes condition only, and on bilaterally weightbearing support in both visual conditions (eyes open and closed). Although, the closed eyes condition scores did not reach statistical significance, they were on average almost half the pre-test scores. This shows that all of SB test conditions were affected by the fatigue phenomena. Between groups comparisons revealed that the experimental group had significantly lower scores on one condition in YB (i.e. posterolateral, right side), and all conditions of WBB test (see Table 4). This shows that the static balance abilities were mostly affected which may be explained by fiber type distribution in these athletes. In the current study, participants were not identified or stratified based on their sport, but instead, the fact that they are athletes only. Power athletes are known to have higher proportions of fast-twitch fibers, whereas, endurance athletes are known to have higher proportions of slow-twitch fibers [28]. Given that the majority of athletes in this study were recreational athletes, the intensity and type of training they receive is known to be less frequent and less intense compared to competitive athletes, which may also explain differences noted in static balance observed in this study [16, 17].

Another interpretation of the effects of fatigue on static balance could be its influence on proprioception [12, 13]. Balance strategies would be altered by impairment in mechanoreceptors which may lead to an inability to be utilized by the individual when needing to regain balance [1, 3, 11]. In athletes, these senses are essential for the integrity of their performance, and if impaired would lead to injuries [11]. In a systematic review, Sayyadi and colleagues [11] reported that fatigue caused a significant increase in active angular error of the knees, ankles and hips, which would impair sensation of the joint position. This shows that these senses when trained, help with balance abilities during fatigue and exhaustion periods [12, 29].

Interestingly, the control group had significantly high scores in the YB post-test, and within-session balance assessment was found to be similar if done 1–3 times, which was attributed to a learning effect [30, 31]. Conversely, the experimental group had lower scores on the YB post-test with only the anterior right-side condition reaching statistical significance. This is aligned with our study hypothesis despite that other conditions did not reach statistical significance. Notability, the participants had several failed attempts before achieving success in the tasks, which were not recorded by the assessors. It is also worth noting that the anterior right-side condition was the first to be tested in each participant, which could be showing the true state of the participant immediately following the fatigue protocol.

Several studies have explored the effects of balance and fatigue. Springer and Pincivero [32] conducted a study in which they only measured static single leg balance in 3 groups: control, heel raises fatigue and rowing machine fatigue protocols [32]. They showed that the localized muscle fatigue group scored significantly lower in the balance test, compared to the other 2 groups. Although these results were consistent with the results of the current study, they only had a measure for static balance and did not measure dynamic balance. Additionally, they did not use consistent measures to ensure fatigue achievement (i.e. concentric contraction failure for heel raises and reduction of cadence in rowing protocol). Conversely, Abutaleb and colleagues [33] conducted a treadmill walking-based fatigue protocol, where they used the participant report of exhaustion to terminate the protocol. They measured dynamic balance using Biodex Stability System. They demonstrated that all indices of dynamic balance were significantly lower post-fatigue protocol, which is not consistent with our results. The lack of consistency may be explained by the fact that their participants were not athletes, and athletic populations would have on average improved balance abilities.

Several limitations were identified in this study. The inclusion of only male participants limit the generalizability of the results of the study to all athletes regardless of biological sex. Moreover, the time spent until fatigue reached was not recorded which would have been another variable that would explain the differences observed between participants. The current study only included recreational athletes, who are recognized to have more injuries during training. In contrast competitive athletes are known to have significantly higher injuries during games [17]. Future studies should include competitive athletes to determine whether any true differences are observed between them and recreational athletes.

Additionally, the static and dynamic balance post-fatigue protocol were conducted on the same day, which hindered a more accurate recording of the scores. The pre- and post- fatigue for the static and dynamic balance are better suited to be completed on 2 different days to better capture the effect of fatigue. An additional time component that proved to be a limitation was the short waiting period of the control group (i.e. 20 min), which may have led to a learning effect for this group. This could have been solved by providing one to two days separation between practice, pre-, and post-trials. Finally, failed trials were observed by the assessors but were not recorded. Recording these failed attempts in both static and dynamic balance testing would have better supported the interpretation of results given that the number of attempts would also impact fatigue in these participants. Furthermore, a number of demographic variables may have proved useful for secondary analyses exploring differences in these groups, including the distribution of fiber types, sport activities’ type, and training level of these participants.

In conclusion, randomly assigned recreational young male athletes had a reduced performance in both static and dynamic balance tests (anterior only) after a treadmill running fatigue protocol, measured by RPE and plasma lactate level, compared to a control group of their peers. The interaction between time and group showed significant effects in dynamic (only right posterolateral) and all static balance conditions (except for left unilateral eyes closed). This shows that many aspects of balance were compromised prior to a fatigue protocol. This indicates that fatigued athletes could face a serious injury in the field if their balance was impaired. Proprioception-focused training would also be beneficial to improve athletes’ skills in controlling their balance while fatigued. As a recommendation, trainers should focus on balance training during limits of fatigue to enhance athlete balance abilities in this period. This could be initiated with both static and dynamic balance training alone, then training balance following training while the athletes are fatigued. It can be at the midpoint of the training or the last one-third of the training. Fatigue can seriously impact the balance skills of athletes, leaving them at risk for serious injuries while participating in their sport. A strong training focus on static and dynamic balance is recommended while these athletes are fatigued to ensure that risk of future injuries is minimized.
