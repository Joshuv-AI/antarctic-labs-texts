Introduction

Persistent left superior vena cava (PLSVC) is a rare vascular anomaly within the systemic venous circulation. Its estimated prevalence in the general population ranges between 0.3 % and 0.5 % [1,2]. However, the incidence of PLSVC is higher in patients with congenital heart disease, estimated to be between 1.3 % and 4.5 % [1].

PLSVC typically does not result in significant clinical consequences as the return of systemic venous blood to the right atrium occurs through the coronary sinus, and a normal right superior vena cava is usually present [3]. Most individuals affected by PLSVC, who do not have a prior history of congenital heart disease, are asymptomatic. This anomaly can be encountered during various cardiovascular imaging procedures, cardiothoracic surgeries, or central venous catheter-based interventions, including cardioverter-defibrillator implantation, electrophysiological procedures, and right heart catheterization [2,4,5]. Nevertheless, the use of a left subclavian approach for vascular access may pose a challenge in the presence of a PLSVC due to the fact that it may make placing a catheter on the right side of the heart challenging [5].

Approximately 92 % of PLSVC cases result in drainage into the coronary sinus, while the remaining 8 % lead to drainage into the left atrium, causing a right-left shunt that is usually clinically insignificant [1]. Despite this, central catheters may cause a risk of systemic embolization for those in the latter group and should be taken out if the patient's heart and chest anatomy has not been thoroughly evaluated. If there is no shunt, catheters can stay in place, but caution should be taken to avoid the nearby coronary sinus and arrhythmias caused by a mispositioned catheter tip [1,5].

This case report describes the unexpected discovery of a malformation during venous port placement while also offering a comprehensive review of existing literature on this anomaly. Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained, and this work adheres to the SCARE criteria [6].

Case presentation

A 59-year-old female patient presented at the medical service with abdominal distention. A physical exam showed increased abdominal perimeter and ascites. Therefore, subsequent studies were performed, revealing peritoneal carcinomatosis of unknown origin as the cause of the ascites. Subsequently, the patient underwent staging laparoscopy with biopsy sampling, retroperitoneal tumor resection, hysterectomy, and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, unveiling the presence of bilateral high-grade serous carcinoma of the ovary.

Therefore, the patient required the placement of an implantable catheter for chemotherapy, which was successfully performed. In accordance with institutional protocol, the catheter is positioned in the contralateral extremity to their dominant side, in this case was placed at the left side. However, a portable chest X-ray (Fig. 1, Panel A) was obtained in the operating room to confirm the appropriate positioning of the device, revealing the catheter extending along the left subclavian vein and progressing through the left paramediastinal region. Due to this finding, potential causes of catheter misplacement were suspected. After reviewing the preoperative studies, a prior chest Computed Tomography Angiography (Fig. 1, Panels B and C) revealed the presence of an anomalous vessel coursing inferiorly to the left of the arch of the aorta and anterior to the left hilum. This vessel was found to be in direct continuation of the confluence of the left internal jugular vein and the left subclavian vein, confirming the diagnosis of persistent left-sided superior vena cava, even though it had not been reported in the initial report. To better characterize the findings, a 3D reconstruction was performed (Fig. 2). Discussion

The most common abnormality of the venous drainage system is PLSVC and there are several anatomic variants reported in literature as: double superior vena cava without any anastomosis, double superior vena cava with a transverse anastomosis between two venae cavae and left superior vena cava with absent right superior vena cava, being the last one the rarest of these conditions [7]. During normal development, the anterior cardinal veins, which drain the head, neck, and arm, unite with the posterior cardinal vein. Most of the left-sided cardinal system disappears, a failure of obliteration of the left anterior cardinal vein results in the persistence of the left superior vena cava [8]. This case report shows a 59-year-old female patient with a double superior vena cava without any anastomosis.

Gonzalez et al. established that almost 40 % of cases have associated significant cardiovascular anomalies, septal defects being the most common [9]. In their 10 cases report all patients had associated heart disease, namely, congenital heart disease, ischemic heart disease, hypertensive cardiomyopathy, and arrhythmic heart disease [9]. Lendzian et al. established in their article Are Anomalies of the Caval Veins More Common in Complex Congenital Heart Disease? that the most common congenital diseases associated with this anomaly are: atrioventricular septal defects, tetralogy of Fallot, double outlet left ventricle, double outlet right ventricle, hypoplastic left heart, hypoplastic right heart, pulmonary atresia, single atrium, single ventricle or total anomalous pulmonary venous return [10].

However, PLSVC is present in 0.5–2 % of the general population (without any congenital heart diseases) which leads to most commonly asymptomatic patients [7,11]. This case report shows a patient with an incidental diagnosis who had no present or history of previous symptoms which is consistent with literature reports.

Nevertheless, this anatomical anomaly can lead to various clinical implications and present challenges during central venous catheterization. Access difficulties can result in hypotension, angina, heart perforation, tamponade, and cardiac arrest. Moreover, during pacemaker implantation, the circuitous path taken by the electrode can make it challenging to achieve a stable electrode position and sustain capture. Additionally, in the context of cardiopulmonary bypass, an isolated PLSVC hinders the utilization of retrograde cardioplegia, thereby impairing its effectiveness [9]. Even though our patient did not present any complications during catheterization it was during a venous port placement that this variant was diagnosed. Recognizing anomalies and anatomical variants with the involvement of a multidisciplinary team is critical to prevent vascular complications during endovascular procedures and to tailor techniques for patients in need of cardiac endovascular procedures or cardiac devices.

Conclusion

The discovery of an PLSVC during central venous catheterization is a rare but significant event that must be recognized promptly as possible. Even though PLSVC is often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally, it is crucial to possess a comprehensive understanding of this anomaly and conduct meticulous imaging workups to prevent potential complications.
