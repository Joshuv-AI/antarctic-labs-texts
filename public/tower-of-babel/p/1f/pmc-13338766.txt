Changes in hand hygiene compliance and technique among non-healthcare professionals in a tertiary-care hospital: Before and during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Philipp Warnke, Hagen Frickmann, Hannah Charlotte Schumann, Andreas Hahn, Andreas Podbielski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe assessed changes associated with the SARS-CoV-2 (severe acute respiratory coronavirus 2) pandemic on hand disinfection compliance and technique among inpatients and visitors in a German tertiary-care hospital.MethodsHand disinfection compliance and technique were directly observed. Approximately 10% of observed individuals were included in a questionnaire-based study on motivations and knowledge (voluntary participation). A pre-pandemic period of 5 months was compared with a pandemic period of 10 months during the early phase of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic in 2020.ResultsA total of 5,517 individuals observed as well as 456 observed and questioned were included in the assessment. Among observed individuals, hand disinfection compliance increased from 8.7% (181/2,087) to 24.0% (823/3,430) (P < 0.0001), and correct technique increased from 4.4% (91/2,087) to 10.0% (343/3,430) (P < 0.0001) during the pandemic period. Among volunteers participating in the questionnaire arm, compliance was high pre-pandemic (47.2%; 58/123) and remained stable during the pandemic period (45.6%; 152/333) (P = 0.8325). In this subpopulation, correct technique declined from 28.5% (35/123) to 17.7% (59/333) (P = 0.0136).ConclusionsModerate pandemic-period-associated improvements in hand disinfection compliance and technique among non-healthcare professionals in healthcare settings may not increase indefinitely, but may reach a saturation level in individuals who already show comparatively high adherence.

Introduction

During the COVID-19 (coronavirus disease 2019) pandemic, which was caused by SARS-CoV-2 (severe acute respiratory coronavirus 2), hand hygiene was among the recommended preventive approaches. SARS-CoV-2 can remain viable for days on various surfaces [1] but can be reliably inactivated by alcohol-based hand-rub formulations [2]. While compliance with hand disinfection can be enforced through disciplinary measures for employees of healthcare facilities, far fewer enforcement options exist for inpatients and visitors. Therefore, their prevention-related behaviour, including compliance with hand disinfection, may more closely reflect the population's perceived risk, consistent with Peltzman's risk-compensation hypothesis [3]. By assessing it, the results can guide infection prevention and control decision makers.

Typically, compliance of hospital visitors with hand hygiene standards is low. In a pre-pandemic US assessment, even hand hygiene–facilitating strategies increased compliance only moderately, from <1% to approximately 10% [4]. Slightly higher proportions can be achieved by targeted nudging strategies [5]. In intensive care units, the situation is not much better [6]. Time-consuming approaches such as educational speech interventions can raise compliance rates, but are difficult to sustain in routine practice [7]. An innovative organisational approach connected an automated door sensor to a disinfectant dispenser, allowing entry only after dispenser use, thereby substantially increasing visitor hand hygiene compliance [8]. Notably, self-protection was a stronger motivator for hand cleaning than patient protection among hospital visitors in a Turkish assessment [9]. Furthermore, medical non-healthcare professionals tend to prefer soap-and-water handwashing over hand disinfection [10].

During the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, disinfection-based hand hygiene was actively promoted in German hospitals [11]. Only temporary changes in hand hygiene compliance rates were reported over the course of the pandemic [12], a finding that contrasts with self-reported high motivation for hand hygiene and initial enthusiasm at the beginning of the pandemic [13–15]. In outpatient medical facilities, a relatively low hand hygiene compliance in the 10% range was observed even among outpatients, with slightly higher values in individuals with a more approving attitude towards pandemic countermeasures [16]. Even in healthcare professionals, a significant but only moderate increase in compliance (from 54.5 to 70.1%) was observed in an European assessment [17].

Building on existing evidence on hand hygiene compliance during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, the present study focuses on hand hygiene practices of inpatients and hospital visitors in a German tertiary hospital using a single-centre approach. Direct observation of hand hygiene compliance was combined with a questionnaire-based assessment to explore reasons for compliant and non-compliant behaviour.

Methods

Study type and sites

The study was conducted as a single-centre cross-sectional assessment at the clinics for dentistry as well as oral and maxillofacial surgery (DOMS), the surgical clinics (SC), the medical clinics (MC), and the paediatrics department (Ped) of a German tertiary-care hospital. Notably, the availability and visibility of disinfectant dispensers were expanded at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic (Fig. 1). Visitors and inpatients were assessed.

Observation strategy

The observation period was divided into a pre-pandemic period (19 September 2019 to 9 February 2020) and a pandemic period, which started with the diagnosis of the first COVID-19 patient in the study region (16 February 2020) and continued until 20 December 2020. Hand disinfection compliance and technique quality of the “observed individuals” were monitored when entering or leaving the clinics using a standardised observation form. For these “observed and questioned individuals”, age, sex, and status (visitor vs inpatient) were systematically recorded. Details of the questionnaire items are provided in Table 2. Notably, at MC only the “observed and questioned” study arm was implemented.

Statistics

The counts of observations and observed and questioned individuals were determined by the duration of the assessment periods. We aimed for a pre-pandemic-to-pandemic distribution no higher than 1:2. Odds ratios were calculated, and categorial variables were compared using Fisher's exact test or, for larger samples sizes, the chi-square test. Chi-squared tests for independence were used to explore associations with selected covariates. Analyses were performed using GraphPad InStat version 3.06 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No correction for multiple testing [18] was applied because analyses were exploratory, hypothesis-generating design.

Ethics

Ethical approval was obtained from the University Medicine Rostock, Germany, ethics committee (reference number: A2019-075). Informed consent was obtained from all individuals participating in the questionnaire-based study arm in which participant data were collected. The assessment was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with German law.

Results

Study populations

With 5,517 observed individuals and 456 observed and questioned individuals, the intended approximately 10:1 distribution was achieved, as was the targeted period distribution (pre-pandemic vs pandemic not exceeding 1:2). Study populations in the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods were homogeneously distributed with the following exceptions. Among observed individuals, proportions were lower at DOMS and higher at SC and Ped in the pre-pandemic period compared with the pandemic period. Among observed and questioned individuals, proportions were higher at Ped in the pre-pandemic period and higher at MC in the pandemic period. In addition, a higher proportion of observed and questioned individuals was recorded in the pre-pandemic period (Table 3).

Observations

Details on observed hand hygiene compliance and technique quality for both assessed populations, stratified by pre-pandemic and pandemic periods, are provided in Table 4. In summary, the observed individuals started from a very low level with regard to hand hygiene compliance and technique quality, but demonstrated a moderate increase in both outcomes at the beginning of the pandemic (Fig. 2). The observed and questioned individuals showed a comparatively high level with regard to both outcomes already at the onset of the study. Subsequently, the pandemic situation did not lead to any further increase in compliance, and technique quality could not be maintained. Although this study group still performed quantitatively better than the observed individuals, trends during the first year of the pandemic pointed in opposite directions (Fig. 3). Among those who did not perform hand disinfection correctly, a significant trend towards an increase in failures of the movement-related components of the technique was evident in both groups at the beginning of the pandemic. For the observed and questioned individuals, there was also a trend towards use of reduced amounts of disinfectant during the pandemic period.

Questionnaire-based assessments

When comparing the pre-pandemic and the pandemic periods, Table 6 shows that fear of infection decreased as a motivator for disinfection among the observed and questioned individuals during the assessed early phase of the pandemic and, similarly, the proportion of individuals reporting regular use of disinfectant after potentially infective contacts decreased. Information on the requirement for hand disinfection became more likely to come from the media rather than from individuals' personal environment at the beginning of the pandemic. During the pandemic period, there were fewer requests to reposition disinfectant dispensers, and—as shown in Table 7—unidentifiable dispensers or a need to improve visibility were cited less frequently as reasons for lacking disinfection. Furthermore, Table 7 indicates that, among participants with insufficient hand disinfection compliance in particular, disinfection was less often considered unnecessary than before the pandemic. Notably, there was a pandemic-associated shift in proposals for better enforcement of hand hygiene compliance from promotion- and information-based strategies to pressure-based approaches. Other significant associations were not recorded.

Discussion

This study evaluated the effects of the early COVID-19 pandemic on hand disinfection compliance and technique quality among inpatients and visitors in inpatient healthcare settings. The investigation indicated substantial differences between the general observed population and a subpopulation volunteering to participate in a questionnaire-based assessment.

The general observed population showed very low hand disinfection compliance and technique quality in the one-digit-percentage range in the pre-pandemic period. This observation is consistent with historic results [16]. In line with previous investigations during the COVID-19 pandemic [12, 16], this population showed a moderate increase of factor 2–3 in hand hygiene compliance and technique quality during the pandemic period. This increase may reflect heightened hygiene awareness in response to an international pandemic with a quantitatively relevant case fatality rate, particularly among older individuals [19]. In marked contrast, the subpopulation volunteering for the questionnaire-based assessment showed high compliance rates, comparable to those historically observed among healthcare professionals [17], even prior to the start of the pandemic; this level did not increase further during the pandemic. Although hand hygiene compliance remained stable in these individuals, there was a decline in technique quality. Interestingly, there were parallel reductions in fear of infection and in regular use of hand disinfection for self-protection after contact with potentially infectious materials in the same subpopulation. This may partly account for the reduction in technique quality, given the known importance of perceived self-risk as a compliance motivator [9]. We speculate that the observed shift from personal environment to media as the main information source regarding the need for hand hygiene during the pandemic may have contributed; however, this observation is hypothesis-generating and requires confirmation. Notably, absolute compliance and technique quality remained higher in the subpopulation volunteering for the questionnaire-based study.

For infection prevention and control decision makers, the study indicates that pandemic-associated awareness may be associated with increased hand hygiene compliance and technique quality in the general population attending healthcare facilities; however, the effect size appears moderate as indicated by the factor 2–3. In turn, data from the observed and questioned subpopulation suggest possible saturation effects, such that pandemic countermeasures may not yield additional improvements among individuals with already high baseline adherence. Even in the questioned subpopulation, however, the mode of information transfer affected compliance rates, with the best effects observed for communication by healthcare employees, consistent with previous findings [7].

Several additional findings are noteworthy. Even in the general observed population, which showed improved technique quality during the pandemic period, there was an increase in movement-related errors. This finding suggests that increased motivation for hand hygiene still requires instruction in appropriate technique for lay persons not accustomed to it. The hospital's strategy of improving the visibility of disinfectant dispensers also appears to have been successful, as requests to reposition dispensers and concerns regarding their visibility were less frequently reported during the pandemic period, and “not having seen the dispenser” was less often cited as a reason for omitted disinfection. Furthermore, fewer questioned individuals considered hand disinfection unnecessary, and more reported improved attitudes and compliance in response to COVID-19; however, these self-estimates were not reflected by observed hand disinfection behaviour within this subpopulation. While lower compliance among visitors compared with typically more vulnerable patients was expected given the importance of self-protection as a motivator [9], reasons for poorer technique among females and among observed and questioned individuals in the medical clinics remain speculative. The general aggression model, linking stress to dispositions for aggressive behaviour [20], might partially explain the observed pandemic-associated preference for pressure-based motivation strategies over promotion- and information-based strategies as a response to pandemic-associated stressors [21].

This study has limitations. Due to the exploratory, hypothesis-generating design, there were no a priori effect-size expectations; therefore, no formal sample-size calculation was performed. Accordingly, small differences may have been missed. Second, for organisational reasons during the first year of the pandemic, the medical clinics could only be included in the observation-and-questionnaire arm but not in the general observation arm. Third, the lack of adjustment for multiple testing may reduce the robustness of findings with P values close to 0.05; these should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Fourth, the single-centre design may limit generalisability.

Conclusions

Despite the above-mentioned study limitations, the assessment confirmed moderate pandemic-period-associated increases in hand disinfection compliance and technique quality in the factor 2–3 range among non-healthcare professionals in healthcare settings. However, it also suggested that such effects may not increase indefinitely, but may reach a saturation level in individuals already showing comparatively good adherence, consistent with risk-compensation considerations [3]. If increases above such saturation levels are desired, evidence from other studies [7, 8] suggests that either technology-based organisational approaches [8] or resource-intensive individual guidance [7] may be required to address residual non-compliance among non-healthcare professionals.
