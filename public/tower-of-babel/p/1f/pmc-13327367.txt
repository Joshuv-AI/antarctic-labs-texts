Apnea Testing for Brain Death/Death by Neurologic Criteria in Adults: A Prospective Multicenter Observational Study Assessing the Relationship Between Methodology, Safety, and Duration
Authors: Sean Cuninghame, John Basmaji, James Duffin, Andrew Healey, Beth Paltser, Michelle Snyder-Salehi, Alexander Yoshiki, J. Gordon Boyd, Michael Hartwick, Karim Soliman, Jeffrey M. Singh, David Katz, Janice Beitel, Ebenezer Ogunsakin, Marat Slessarev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IMPORTANCE:Apnea testing is an integral component of brain death (BD) assessments. The optimal method of Apnea Testing is currently not known.OBJECTIVES:To describe the current use of apnea test methods, and compare the effect of the chosen apnea test method (passive oxygenation, continuous positive airway pressure [CPAP], or exogenous Co2) on safety and duration of apnea test in adult BD/death by neurologic criteria (DNC) organ donors.DESIGN:A prospective multicenter observational study.SETTING:Seventy-four ICUs in Ontario, Canada, who participate in organ donation practices.PATIENTS:All BD/DNC assessments in Ontario, Canada, from July 2023 to August 2024.INTERVENTIONS:None.MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES:Outcomes included the rate of apnea test attempts, completions, and adverse events. We also measured the incidence of each method used and the duration of the apnea test.RESULTS:During the 1-year study period, a total of 368 BD/DNC assessments were performed, of which 361 (98%) attempted an apnea test. Of the 361 apnea tests attempted, 236 (65%) used passive oxygenation (tracheal insufflation), 103 (29%) used CPAP-based methods, and 22 (6%) used exogenous Co2. Nine tests were not completed: seven were terminated due to adverse events (all with passive oxygenation; five hypoxemia, two hemodynamic instability) and two due to observed respiratory effort. All adverse events occurred with the passive oxygenation method (p = 0.20). No statistically significant differences were seen in apnea test duration between methods. The median test durations were 12 minutes (interquartile range [IQR], 10–17 min), 12 minutes (IQR, 8–15 min), and 15 minutes (IQR, 11–25 min) for passive oxygenation, positive pressure, and exogenous Co2, respectively.CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE:In this large multicenter study, passive oxygenation was associated with a nonsignificant increase in adverse events compared with CPAP-based and exogenous Co2 methods. Exogenous Co2 did not shorten apnea test time. These findings support further investigation of CPAP-based methods as an alternative approach to apnea testing and refinement of Co2 delivery parameters.

KEY POINTS

Question: What proportion of brain death/death by neurologic criteria (BD/DNC) assessments have an apnea test attempted and completed, how frequently is each apnea test method used, and does the method use affect the rate of test completion, adverse events, or test duration?

Findings: In a prospective multicenter study across 74 ICUs, nearly all BD/DNC assessments had an apnea test attempted (98%) and completed (98% of all attempted). Most attempts used a passive oxygenation (tracheal insufflation) method (65%). All adverse events during apnea testing occurred with passive oxygenation (tracheal insufflation) that was nonsignificant. The use of exogenous Co2 did not shorten test duration as initially hypothesized.

Meaning: These findings suggest that continuous positive airway pressure-based methods are safe approaches for apnea testing during BD/DNC assessments and warrant further investigation.

Apnea testing is an integral component of the clinical examination for brain death/death determination by neurologic criteria (BD/DNC) (1). The standard apnea test involves disconnection of the endotracheal tube (ETT) from the ventilator circuit, followed by administration of oxygen via a cannula placed inside the ETT or T-piece (Fig. 1A). This allows for gradual increase in Paco2, with a consequent fall in pH. In patients with preserved brainstem function, this would result in spontaneous respiratory effort via stimulation of medullary chemoreceptors (2, 3), while in patients meeting BD/DNC criteria, there is no respiratory effort due to loss of brainstem respiratory drive centers. Clinicians monitor serial blood gases until predefined Co2 and pH targets are met, observing for respiratory effort throughout. This “passive oxygenation” apnea test (also referred to tracheal insufflation) method can be lengthy to complete (5–20 min) and result in lung de-recruitment from the loss of positive airway pressure, leading to hypoxemia, hemodynamic instability, or cardiac arrest (4, 5). The incidence of adverse events with passive oxygenation method are estimated to range from 2% to 10% (6–9).

B, Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP; positive pressure) method using: i) an independent CPAP circuit, ii) a T-piece with positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) valve, iii) a flow-inflating resuscitation bag with PEEP valve, or iv) a CPAP mode on the ventilator with apnea alarms disabled. Modifications to the passive oxygenation (tracheal insufflation) method have been proposed to improve the safety and efficiency of the apnea test (10). Maintenance of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) may be achieved via attaching the ETT to an independent CPAP device, a T-piece with positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) valve, a PEEP valve attached to a flow-inflating resuscitation bag, or switching to CPAP on the ventilator (Fig. 1B). These methods may prevent alveolar de-recruitment and avoid hypoxemia and are now considered standard practice in some jurisdictions (11). Exogenous Co2 supplementation is an alternative modality that may allow for reaching Co2/pH targets faster. Whether this reduces the risk of complications remains uncertain (10, 12) (Fig. 1C).

The 2020 World Brain Death Project provided an international best practice statement on the minimal requirements for apnea testing. These guidelines suggested an apnea test be included as part of routine BD/DNC assessments after careful consideration of the patient’s ventilatory requirements and hemodynamic status, given the instability that apnea may induce (13). Since the World Brain Death Project was published, several groups have published guidelines pertaining to their jurisdiction, including the Pediatric and Adult BD/DNC Consensus Guideline co-developed by the American Academy of Neurology, American Academy of Pediatrics, Congress of Neurological Surgeons, and Society of Critical Care Medicine (14), the Canadian Clinical Practice Guideline (15), and the U.K. Code of Practice for Brain Death (16). While each of these guidelines recommend the routine use of the apnea test as part of BD/DNC assessments, there is no universally accepted method for apnea testing. Furthermore, the potential risks of the apnea test have raised ethical concerns as some argue the test may induce harm without providing benefit to the patient (17–19). Therefore, prospective studies are necessary to determine the optimal method of performing the apnea test to ensure patient safety and optimize health systems outcomes (6, 15).

The objective of the present study was to evaluate apnea test practices within Canada’s largest organ donation organization, the Ontario Health-Trillium Gift of Life Network (OH-TGLN), where passive oxygenation, CPAP and exogenous Co2 are all permitted methods for the apnea test given the limited comparative data available. We compared adverse event rates associated with each method to assess their relative safety and to determine whether exogenous Co2 supplementation shortens the duration of apnea test. Our goal was to support clinicians in selecting the most appropriate and safe approach during BD/DNC assessments and inform best practices.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics and Consent

The study protocol, “Variability in Apnea Testing Method and their Effect on Organ Donation Outcomes in Ontario” received Western University Health Science Research Ethics Board approval on March 21, 2023 (Project Identifier: 124563). Procedures complied with the ethical standards of the Research Ethics Board for projects involving human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. Consent was obtained by Specialists in Organ and Tissue Donation (SOTD) with the OH-TGLN under its legislated mandate pursuant to Ontario’s Gift of Life Act, R.S.O. 1990, c. H.20.

Study Design and Participants

This prospective cohort study was conducted between July 2023 and August 2024 in the 74 ICUs in Ontario, Canada, that participate in organ donation with support from Trillium Gift of Life Network. Participants were adult patients (> 18 yr old) who underwent BD/DNC assessment. We adopted a pragmatic study design to capture apnea testing practices across a broad range of ICUs, including smaller community centers. This approach prioritized generalizability and feasibility of data collection over the granularity of an explanatory trial, with abbreviated case report forms to minimize burden during routine clinical care. Patients on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) were not excluded.

BD/DNC Assessment and Documentation Procedure

All BD/DNCs performed in this study were done by two independent Critical Care Physicians following existing institutional BD/DNC policies. Outcomes were documented by ICU providers performing the BD/DNC. Individual hospitals in Ontario use the Canadian BD/DNC guidelines (15) to develop their own institution-specific BD/DNC protocols. These national guidelines mirror those of others for the requirements for BD/DNC around the world (13). Institutional protocols are developed and approved by OH-TGLN to ensure adherence to international standards. Ontario hospitals may use any of three approved apnea test methods. OH-TGLN donation support physicians offer consultation to clinicians performing BD/DNC, although responsibility for testing, documentation, and pronouncing death remains with the treating clinician(s). Documentation is reviewed by OH-TGLN SOTDs to confirm BD/DNC criteria are met.

Apnea Test Methods

Three apnea test methods were permitted in this study. If study participants were determined to be comatose with absent brainstem reflexes, the apnea test followed provided the clinician felt it was safe. All patients were pre-oxygenated with Fio2 of 1.0 before apnea testing. The choice of test used was based on clinician discretion and institutional protocol:

1) Passive oxygenation method: Disconnecting the ventilator circuit from the ETT with administration of oxygen through a cannula placed inside the ETT or via a T-piece connector (Fig. 1A).

1) Passive oxygenation method: Disconnecting the ventilator circuit from the ETT with administration of oxygen through a cannula placed inside the ETT or via a T-piece connector (Fig. 1A).

2) CPAP alone: Disconnecting the ventilator circuit and attaching the ETT to an independent CPAP circuit, a T-piece with a PEEP valve, a flow-inflating resuscitation bag with PEEP valve, or leaving the patient on the ventilator in a CPAP mode with apnea alarms turned off (Fig. 1B).

2) CPAP alone: Disconnecting the ventilator circuit and attaching the ETT to an independent CPAP circuit, a T-piece with a PEEP valve, a flow-inflating resuscitation bag with PEEP valve, or leaving the patient on the ventilator in a CPAP mode with apnea alarms turned off (Fig. 1B).

3) Exogenous Co2 method: Leaving the patient on the ventilator in a spontaneous intermittent mandatory ventilation mode of 2–4 breaths/min with a 5% Co2/95% O2 as the gas source. Low respiratory rate (RR) of 2–4 breaths/min is required to deliver exogenous Co2 to alveoli and permit Co2 diffusion as previously described (20). Confirmation of absent respiratory effort was performed by disconnecting the patient once Paco2/pH targets were reached and observing for a minimum of 1 minute (Fig. 1C).

Low respiratory rate (RR) of 2–4 breaths/min is required to deliver exogenous Co2 to alveoli and permit Co2 diffusion as previously described (20). Confirmation of absent respiratory effort was performed by disconnecting the patient once Paco2/pH targets were reached and observing for a minimum of 1 minute (Fig. 1C).

Apnea alarms were temporarily disabled during the test. A baseline arterial blood gas (ABG) was obtained at the start of the apnea test and at least one confirmatory ABG demonstrating that targets were met (Paco2 ≥ 60 mm Hg and ≥ 20 mm Hg above baseline with a pH < 7.28 per prespecified guideline criteria) (15). ABGs were generally obtained via arterial line using point-of-care analyzers; however, the number and timing of intermediate ABGs were not prescribed. Point-of-care blood gas analyzers were subject to each hospital’s calibration and quality assurance protocols.

Data Variables and Data Collection

We developed a Case Report Form (CRF) a priori in consultation with OH-TGLN coordinators and medical leads that included variables and outcomes of interest (Supplemental Materials, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B647).

The following variables were collected: demographic data (age, sex, height, weight); comorbidities (cardiovascular, respiratory, other); start date/time of BD/DNC assessment; start date/time of apnea test; end date/time of apnea test; method of apnea test used; adverse event (yes/no) during apnea test; if not completed, why?; was an ancillary test performed (yes/no; if yes, what type); and total time from BD/DNC assessment to organ allocation (days:hours).

SOTDs collected data during BD/DNC assessments performed by ICU clinicians. A baseline ABG was done at the time of apnea test initiation and subsequently at intervals based on clinician request. End-tidal co2-monitoring was optional based on local practices. ICU clinicians were not bound by specific criteria for apnea test discontinuation or when to seek an ancillary test, both of which were subject to equipoise and advice from OH-TGLN’s medical leads. The CRF data were then uploaded to a centralized database and analyzed prospectively by two reviewers (S.C., M.S.).

Exogenous Co2 Modeling

While not part of initial study protocol, exogenous Co2 modeling was performed post hoc, after the apnea test duration findings were available, to explore the conditions under which exogenous Co2 supplementation would be expected to shorten the apnea test. We used LabVIEW (National Instruments, Austin, TX) to model the effect of exogenous Co2 on duration of apnea testing. We assumed a steady state of resting parameters including Co2 production of 200 mL/min and cardiac output of 5 L/min, with a functional residual capacity of 3000 mL, and then alveolar ventilation and inspired Co2 was adjusted to observe the changes produced.

To simulate the effects of discontinuing lung ventilation, a simple three compartment model was used, consisting of lung gas exchange, brain, and all other tissues each with its own material balance equation for Co2. In the normal brain, brain compartment blood flow increases sigmoidally with Paco2 (21), while cardiac output increases linearly (22), and brain compartment Co2 production decreases linearly with Paco2 (23) with a standard Co2 dissociation curve (24). The chemoreflex control of lung compartment alveolar ventilation was modeled based on established understanding of central chemoreceptors (2, 3).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the R Computing Language (RStudio Version 4.4.2; Integrated Development Environment for R, Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA). Normality was assessed graphically and with Shapiro-Wilk test. One-way analysis of variance was used in testing where data were determined to be parametric. The Kruskal-Wallis test used to assess for differences in apnea test duration times, as the data were nonparametric. A p value of less than 0.05 was selected a priori to determine significance.

RESULTS

Apnea Test Completion

During the 1-year study period from July 2023 to August 2024, a total of 368 BD/DNC assessments were performed. Of these, 361 (98%) had an apnea test attempted (Fig. 2A). Baseline characteristics and preexisting comorbidities were similar between groups (Table 1). No patients on ECMO underwent BD/DNC assessment during the study. Of the seven BD/DNC assessments that did not attempt an apnea test, six were not attempted due to the clinician’s concern for the safety of the patient to tolerate an apnea test. One BD/DNC assessment did not include an apnea test due to difficulty obtaining arterial access for ABGs. In lieu of an apnea test attempt, all seven of these cases went on to an ancillary test. Of the 361 BD/DNC assessments that did include an apnea test, 352 (97%) safely completed the apnea test, with nine apnea tests aborted. Of these nine, seven were due to an adverse event that arose during the test, and two were due to observed respiratory effort. Of the nine that did not complete an apnea test, eight were without PEEP and one with PEEP (RR, 0.23; 95% CI, 0.03–1.86; p = 0.17). Apnea test ABG parameters at baseline and completion of the test were similar between groups (Table 2).

Apnea Test Methods

Of the 361 apnea tests attempted, 236 tests (65%) were performed using passive oxygenation, 103 (29%) used some form of continuous positive pressure (either an independent CPAP circuit, CPAP applied while on the ventilator, or a PEEP valve attached to a resuscitation bag), and 22 (6%) used exogenous Co2 while on a ventilator circuit (Fig. 2B). All 22 exogenous Co2 cases were done at a single center.

Apnea Test Method Duration

Of the 361 apnea tests completed, test duration was recorded for 314 apnea tests. These 314 apnea tests included 209 (66%) performed with passive oxygenation, 87 (28%) with positive pressure, and 18 (6%) with exogenous Co2. The median test length durations were 12 minutes (interquartile range [IQR], 10–17 min), 12 minutes (IQR, 8–15 min), and 15 minutes (IQR, 11–25) for passive oxygenation, positive pressure, and exogenous Co2, respectively (Fig. 3). There was no statistically significant difference between methods for apnea test time (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.09).

Apnea Test Adverse Events

Of the seven adverse events that necessitated discontinuing the apnea test, all seven used the passive oxygenation method (Table 3; p = 0.20). All seven cases did not attempt a second apnea test and had ancillary tests done confirming BD/DNC. The adverse events noted were hypoxemia (5/7) and hemodynamic instability (2/7). No adverse events were detected with the use of positive pressure (with or without Co2). No cardiac arrests occurred during any apnea tests during the study period.

Modeling Changes in Paco2

Our post hoc physiologic model predicted a test duration of 5 minutes using a RR and Fico2 of zero—values that would be expected during passive oxygenation method (where Paco2 would rise from zero as a function of metabolic Co2 production; Fig. 4). The model further suggested that Fico2 of at least 10% would be required during exogenous Co2 method to shorten apnea test duration relative to passive oxygenation (Fig. 4). Apnea test duration can be further shortened by increasing either Fico2 or the ventilator RR if Fico2 greater than or equal to 10% Co2 is used.

DISCUSSION

Passive oxygenation remains the simplest method for apnea testing; however, CPAP-based techniques have recently been proposed to enhance safety, and exogenous Co2 supplementation has been suggested to accelerate test completion (20). Objective data validating these proposed advantages have been limited. In this large, prospective multicenter study across 74 ICUs in Ontario, we describe that most (98%) BD/DNC assessments include an apnea test attempt. We also document heterogeneity in the apnea test method used, with passive oxygenation predominating. Second, we demonstrate that adverse events during apnea testing are uncommon overall but only occurred during passive oxygenation. Third, exogenous Co2 supplementation did not shorten apnea test duration; however, we provide physiologic modeling data to explain how Co2-based approaches could be modified to achieve their intended purpose. Together, these findings provide much-needed empirical data informing clinicians, policy, and future research aimed at identifying the optimal apnea testing strategy.

While the rate of apnea test attempts described herein is higher than historical rates (8), we hypothesize this may be due to recent guidelines emphasizing the need for apnea testing as a central component of BD/DNC (15). Furthermore, practitioners may now be more comfortable attempting apnea testing in physiologically complex patients, particularly when modifications described herein are used (25). The high rate of attempts overall in this study did not lead to higher rates of complications than previously reported, highlighting that the apnea test is almost always safe and complications are increasingly rare (6–9).

The occurrence of adverse events exclusively during passive oxygenation in our cohort provides important mechanistic insight into apnea test performance. While not statistically significant, our data suggest that CPAP-based testing warrants further investigation to assess if it provides safety advantages and, if so, in which populations. Because exogenous Co2 testing in prior studies has been performed in conjunction with positive airway pressure, the safety profile attributed to Co2, methods in existing literature (20, 26) may instead reflect the contribution of positive airway pressure, rather than Co2 itself. This hypothesis is supported by existing evidence of higher adverse events with Co2 insufflation methods where the patient is disconnected from positive pressure (12). These observations align with the recent 2025 U.K. guidelines, which suggest the use of an independent CPAP circuit for all patients undergoing an apnea test (16), as well as smaller previous observational studies and case reports (27, 28). The use of an independent CPAP circuit or PEEP valve attached to a resuscitation bag may also reduce auto-triggering and allow the bedside examiner clearer observation of absent respiratory effort (29–31). Our data suggest that CPAP-based apnea testing warrants further investigation, particularly in patients perceived to be at higher risk for instability. In cases where complications occur during an apnea test with passive oxygenation, repeating the test using a CPAP-based approach may allow safe completion and avoid the need for ancillary testing. Safety of apnea testing may be most improved in patients with respiratory pathology, which often complicates the course of critically ill brain injured patients (32).

Apnea test duration was similar between passive oxygenation and CPAP methods, whereas there was a trend observed that exogenous Co2 testing took longer, but this was not statistically significant. The longer duration may be attributable to the low Fico2 and RR used, as explored in our modeling data. The exogenous Co2 method used herein took a median of 15 minutes, whereas an early study describing passive tracheal Co2 insufflation without positive pressure reported a mean of 2 minutes (33). However, criteria for test completion in that study solely used a threshold of 60 mm Hg Paco2, without pH or rise from baseline Paco2 thresholds that have been used in clinical practice for many years (14, 15). Contemporary criteria for what constitutes a completed apnea test may partially explain why our and more recent data describe longer test times with Co2 insufflation compared with early descriptions of Co2 methods (33). In order to shorten test time while maintaining the improved safety of positive pressure, we propose the use of a standardized “Co2 bolus method”—a brief period of high Fico2 (Fico2 of 20%) with high RR (e.g., 12 breaths/min—for a period of 1-min) followed by confirmation of target Pco2 levels, with a 1-minute observation period after disconnecting the patient from the ventilator to confirm apnea. We hypothesize this method could be more efficient than the protocol used herein, while also reducing adverse events by adding positive pressure to traditional Co2 insufflation methods (12). This approach may prevent the hemodynamic complications that may arise from overshooting Paco2 for prolonged periods of time (10). The impact of apnea test duration on transplantable organ quality is expected to be minimal; the primary rationale for shortening test duration is workflow considerations.

Our study has several strengths. First, this was the largest prospective study to date in terms of both participants and study sites examining apnea test outcomes. It was a multicenter trial that incorporated 74 ICUs and many ICU practitioners across Ontario performing BD/DNC assessments, which captures variability that may be present in the way practitioners and institutions perform BD/DNC assessments, increasing generalizability. The inclusion of various CPAP delivery devices captures the existing variation in clinical practice. Data were also collected prospectively, which minimized the degree of missing data and patients lost to follow-up. Last, data were analyzed centrally by a statistician blinded to treatment group, limiting bias where possible.

Our study also has some limitations. First, we intentionally did not define specific criteria for test termination or choice of apnea test type. Future work should document baseline ventilator settings, hemodynamic support, and document test center method preferences. Data in this regard would further refine which patient populations may benefit from CPAP-based techniques. Our aim was to pragmatically capture the variability that currently exists in practitioner tolerance while performing the apnea test. As a pragmatic study including many ICUs without dedicated research infrastructure, we relied on coordinators to collect data during routine care, resulting in some missing data (test duration for 47 tests). Apnea test methodology was not randomized and ABG intervals were not standardized, both inherent to the pragmatic design. Second, the practitioner involved in assessing for test safety was not blinded to the apnea test type, as the choice of apnea test was determined by the testing clinician, which was informed by institution protocol. Future studies should elucidate the optimal device for positive pressure application during apnea testing (Fig 1B), as we did not collect outcomes based on the specific device used. In addition, although exogenous Co2 supplementation is permitted within many guidelines, it was used at a single center. As a result, conclusions regarding the effects of exogenous Co2 on apnea test duration and performance are limited and should be explored at additional sites in the future. Future research should build on our physiologic modeling to refine Co2-based apnea testing and evaluate its impact on test duration and donor outcomes through randomized controlled trials. Additionally, the availability and use of point-of-care ABG analyzers and end-tidal co2 monitoring were based on local practices and may have affected measured test duration. These sources of variability are inherent to the pragmatic design of this study, but should be accounted for in future explanatory studies.

CONCLUSIONS

In this large multicenter study, all adverse events occurred with passive oxygenation, and positive airway pressure maintenance was associated with higher test completion rates, although differences did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest CPAP-based apnea testing warrants further consideration as a safe initial approach and are intended to clarify current practice rather than establish definitive best practice. Identifying clinical features of patients who experience complications during passive oxygenation would help target populations likely to benefit from CPAP. Exogenous Co2 supplementation did not shorten test duration; future studies should refine delivery parameters and evaluate safety and efficacy in controlled trials.
