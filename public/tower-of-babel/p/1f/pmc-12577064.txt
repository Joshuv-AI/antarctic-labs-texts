Measuring what matters: key indicators for performance and resilience in fragile, low-income contexts. A scoping review
Authors: Maisoon Elbukhari Ibrahim, Saad El-Din Hussein Hassan, Dell D. Saulnier, Karl Blanchet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMeasuring and monitoring health system performance and resilience is key for planning and managing resilience-building activities. Recurrent simultaneous shocks, particularly in fragile contexts which are home to nearly one quarter of the world’s population, underscore the need for resilient health systems able to provide the needed health care. This scoping review aims to examine how the performance and resilience of the health system have been assessed and measured in fragile, low-income contexts, identify gaps and provide recommendations to improve resilience measurement.MethodsA scoping review of peer-reviewed literature on health systems’ performance or resilience indicators was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. Only studies that were set in countries classified by the OECD as fragile and low-income and that took a whole-of-system approach were included. Of 2175 articles identified 18 met the inclusion criteria. Indicators were classified against the WHO’s building blocks and four resilience dimensions, then assessed for comparability, feasibility and relevance to resilience.ResultsThe studies covered 23 of the 24 countries classified as fragile and low-income by the OECD. A total of 466 indicators were identified. Among the four dimensions of resilience, 39% of the indicators assessed the system’s capacity to manage multiple- and cross-scale dynamics and feedback (interdependence), and only 5% of the indicators assessed the capacity to anticipate and cope with shocks (uncertainty). Less than half of the indicators (n = 230) rely on data from routine health information systems or global datasets, those were classified as feasible. In total, 58% of indicators enable comparison across countries and subnational entities (comparable). Of the 218 indicators classified as both feasible to measure and comparable, 61 indicators were identified as relevant for assessing health system resilience and hence categorized as core indicators.ConclusionsSignificant gaps remain in measuring health system resilience. Qualitative indicators lacked standardization, quantitative indicators did not track health system’s status over time, and few assessed the capacity to anticipate shocks. Improving resilience measurement in fragile low-income contexts requires a whole-of-system approach, using indicators that assess the health system’s capacity to respond to shocks, track performance over time, and provide feedback to guide policy decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12961-025-01410-z.

Background

With the growing demand for health systems that can withstand stressors and crises while sustaining functionality, health system resilience has become a central focus in health system research. [1]. Health system resilience can be defined as “the system’s capacity to absorb, adapt and transform when exposed to a shock such as pandemics, natural disasters or armed conflict and still retain the same control over its structure and functions”[2]. The health system resilience framework by Blanchet et.al, defines three resilience capacities: absorptive capacity as the ability to maintain basic healthcare services despite shocks using existing resources; adaptive capacity as the ability to sustain services by reorganizing and using fewer or alternative resources; and transformative capacity as the ability to restructure the health system to a changing environment [2]. The framework also defines four interlinked dimensions essential to managing resilience: (i) Knowledge defined as the capacity to collect, integrate and analyse different forms of information; (ii) Uncertainty defined as the ability to anticipate and cope with shocks; (iii) Interdependence defined as the ability to engage effectively with and handle multiple- and cross-scale dynamics and feedbacks; and finally (iv) Legitimacy defined as the ability to govern, coordinate and develop institutions and mechanisms that are socially accepted and contextually adapted [2].

Managing and enhancing health system resilience in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) require context-specific approaches and tools that are distinct from those employed in high-income countries [3]. Within LMICs, one key classification is the country’s state of fragility. Fragility is defined by the Organization for Economic Co-Operation and Development (OECD), as the combination of exposure to risk and insufficient coping capacities of the state, system and/or communities to manage, absorb or mitigate those risks [4]. In 2022, 60 contexts were classified as fragile by OECD, of which twenty-four were low-income countries. The 60 contexts are home to 23% of the world’s population and 73% of people living in extreme poverty worldwide. The health systems in these contexts – which face multiple simultaneous shocks, inherent performance issues, chronic systemic challenges and severe resource constraints – experience unique challenges that necessitate context-specific approaches to building resilience [4].

Measuring and monitoring health system resilience is key for guiding resilience-building activities [5, 6]. Despite the critical need for better understanding of health system resilience in fragile low-income contexts, a significant knowledge gap remains regarding the methods for assessing and measuring health system resilience in these contexts. Addressing this gap by developing and applying measurement tools is essential for guiding investment in resilience-building activities as what gets measured gets managed [7]. Resilience of the health system is not as frequently measured as performance. Performance indicators assess how well a health system functions [8], whereas resilience indicators capture its ability to maintain that function under chronic stressors and acute shocks; the two are closely interlinked [2, 9]. A resilient health system must be performing, but a performing health system is not necessarily resilient. Accordingly, this scoping review aims to identify the indicators used to assess health system performance and resilience in fragile low-income countries.

Methods

The aim of this scoping review is to examine how the performance and resilience of the health system has been assessed and measured in fragile, low-income contexts, identify gaps and provide recommendations to improve resilience measurement. A scoping review was chosen to assess the extent of the available evidence and then to highlight the gaps in measuring resilience of health systems in fragile low-income contexts [10].

The review included studies that proposed, collected, analysed and reported indicators to assess and measure health system performance or resilience at any level of the system (national, subnational, or facility levels). Only countries classified by OECD as fragile and also low-income in 2022 were included [4]. Studies also had to take a whole-of-system approach, defined as looking across the health system building blocks [11] or functions [12]; therefore, studies that focused on the resilience of a single building block [11] or function [12] were excluded. Peer-reviewed original research articles published in a scientific journal between January 2014 and April 2024 in English or Arabic were included. The 10 year search period started in January 2014, aligning with a milestone on health system resilience research [13–15]. There was no limitation on study design. The three databases EMBASE, PubMed and Web of Science were searched in July 2024. Database-specific search strings were developed using key words for health system performance, resilience and fragility (Additional file 1). After removing duplicates, 1847 articles were identified, 267 reviewed for full text and 18 included (Fig. 1).Fig. 1PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for new systematic reviews which included searches of databases and registers only. The studies were screened by title, abstract and full text by the first author in Covidence Systematic Review Software (Covidence) [17]. Questions of eligibility were discussed with the other authors when they arose. A data extraction table was developed and agreed upon by all authors. Data was extracted by the first author, in consultation with the other authors when required. Data extraction was completed in Covidence and was based on descriptive study data, including title, authors, article year, country, study aim, study methods, study design, types of shocks, level of assessment (national, subnational, facility), framework(s) used, whether the study focused on performance, resilience or both and whether the study proposed an assessment tool or applied one and the list of indicators used in the article. Full details of extracted data can be seen in the Supplementary Materials (Additional file 2).

Although quality assessment is not required for scoping reviews, one was conducted for the included studies to ensure transparency and strengthen confidence in the included evidence. The quality assessment tool was developed as a synthesis of established checklists, using the Critical Appraisal Skills Program Qualitative Checklist [18], the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies [19], the National Institute of Health checklist [20] and the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool [21]. The questions and the scores are included in the supplementary material (Additional file 1).

Data processing and analysis of the CSV file extracted from Covidence, and the secondary analysis were conducted using Python (version 3.12.4). The Pandas library was utilized for data cleaning, organization and descriptive analysis [22].

We conceptualize resilience as a process, whereby each health system building block demonstrates resilience. Indicators were classified using two frameworks: the WHO’s six building blocks – leadership, information, health workforce, medical products vaccines and technologies, financing and service delivery [11]- and Blanchet’s framework which examines resilience through three capacities and four dimensions [2]. As our focus was on system capacities, we did not extend the analysis to overall health system goals [23]. Indicators were classified according to the definitions and categories used in the original studies. Where an indicator could relate to multiple building blocks, it was assigned to the category used in the source publication. Indicators were also classified as input, output, outcome and impact [24] and categorized as qualitative or quantitative as applied in each study.

Each indicator was categorized according to Blanchet’s four dimensions of health system resilience also known as capacities to manage resilience. Indicators assessing the system’s capacity to collect, integrate and analyse diverse forms of information were classified under knowledge. Those evaluating the system’s ability to anticipate and respond to shocks were classified under uncertainty. Indicators examining the system’s capacity to manage multiple, cross-scale dynamics and feedback were grouped under interdependence. Finally, indicators related to the governance, coordination and development of institutions and mechanisms that are socially accepted and contextually appropriate were classified under legitimacy [2]. Some indicators might overlap across categories reflecting the complex and interconnected nature of health systems as complex adaptive systems [2].

Each indicator was assessed for feasibility, comparability and being relevant to assess health system resilience. This method aligns to established protocols for indicator selection, wherein indicators are assessed on the basis of their ability to be measured using existing datasets or routinely collected data, alignment with established metrics for comparability and relevance for program design and improvement [25, 26].

Feasibility is defined by WHO as the availability of data to measure the indicator, specifically whether the data could be sourced from existing datasets without imposing additional data collection burden [24]. To assess feasibility, we reviewed the data sources cited in studies and considered whether the data required by the indicator might be available from national or global datasets. Global datasets such as the Global Health Observatory, World Bank and UNICEF databases, as well as the national health information systems, such as the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2)[27], were classified as feasible, and given a score of 2. The System of Health Accounts [28] was considered less feasible, as it relies on validated expenditure data that are often incomplete or delayed in fragile contexts owing to limited capacity and weak tracking systems [29], and was therefore given a score of 1.5. National, health facility and household surveys, despite their differing scope and purpose, were considered the least feasible, and given a score of 1, as they are not routinely implemented, and require substantial human, financial and technical resources [30].

Comparability in public health refers to the extent to which health data and indicators from different countries, regions, or time periods can be reliably compared. Achieving comparability requires alignment in indicators definitions, data collection methods, population coverage and measurement techniques to ensure that variations in results reflect actual differences rather than methodological inconsistencies. In the context of health system performance and resilience, comparability is essential for cross-country learning and global monitoring [8]. International institutions, including the WHO, UNICEF and the World Bank, consistently collect, standardize and disseminate health data via platforms, such as the Global Health Observatory [31], to facilitate cross-country comparison. Similarly, global indicator frameworks – including the System of Health Accounts [28], Universal Health Coverage indicators and International Health Regulations – are designed to ensure alignment in definitions and consistency in methodology. Standardized national systems such as the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2) also support data comparability within and across countries [27]. Benchmarking is inherently reliant on systematic comparison of performance against established standards or peer systems [8]. Benchmarking helps organizations learn from others, adopt best practices and improve their own performance. Governments may use benchmarking to evaluate progress, identify gaps and guide policy and investment in health system resilience [8]. In this review, comparability and benchmarking were assessed using two criteria: (1) whether the indicator is included in datasets used for global reporting or national health information systems, and (2) if so, whether its formulation in the source document aligns with the standard definitions used within those datasets. Indicators meeting both criteria were assigned a benchmarking score of two out of two and indicators meeting only the first criteria were assigned a score of one.

Relevance as defined by WHO, refers to the degree to which data meet the information demands of decision-makers, supporting the design, implementation and evaluation of health policies and interventions [24]. It captures the extent to which an indicator is appropriate, actionable and aligned with user needs for program improvement and policy decision-making. In this Review, an indicator is considered relevant if it meets at least one of three criteria: (a) it reflects the system’s capacity to adapt and learn in response to changes or shocks, (b) it serves as a feedback mechanism that informs managers about the consequences of their policies or guides future decisions or (c) it tracks the system’s state, performance or behaviour over time [32].

All indicators, whether for performance or resilience, were assessed using a structured scoring framework, assigning a maximum of 2 points each for feasibility and comparability, and 1 point for relevance, yielding a total possible score of 5. Each indicator was systematically evaluated against these three criteria, with overall scores calculated to inform prioritization.

Study limitations

The requirement for studies to adopt a whole-of-system approach for assessing health system performance or resilience may have excluded studies that offer valuable insights into the resilience of a specific building block or function. Additionally, by focusing exclusively on fragile, low-income contexts, the study may have missed relevant lessons from comparable contexts in middle-income countries; however, this focus was scientifically justified to ensure contextual relevance and avoid drawing conclusions from settings outside the study’s objectives.

Restricting the search to studies published from January 2014 may have excluded analyses of the 2008–2009 global financial crisis. However, this timeframe aligns with the study’s scope, capturing the surge in resilience literature following the West Africa Ebola outbreak [13–15].

Blanchet’s framework provides a structured approach to categorizing indicators across the four dimensions of resilience; however, subjectivity in classification remains a limitation. The multidimensional and interconnected nature of health systems means that many indicators span multiple categories, reflecting the characteristics of complex adaptive systems, where the four dimensions are interdependent. Categorization decisions were made systematically and transparently, but interpretation may vary according to the perspective of the assessor. It is important to note that this classification was applied only as an additional layer of qualitative analysis and did not influence the scoring of indicators.

The dynamic nature of fragility and income level represents a limitation, as shifts in this classification may affect the contextual relevance of included studies. In this review, however, such changes did not affect the inclusion criteria: no contexts have exited the OECD framework in 2022 compared with 2020, and no new States of Fragility report has been released as of December 2024 [4, 33].

Results

Of the eighteen articles, eight applied Kruk’s framework, four used the Blanchet’s framework [13, 34–36], one drew on the Primary Health Care Performance Framework, and one employed a resilience framework adapted from an economic vulnerability model [6, 37]. Details of these frameworks are included in Additional File 1, Table 4.

In total, 14 studies explicitly referred to resilience in their aim. Regarding the focus of the studies, eight focused on health system resilience without reference to performance, seven examined both resilience and performance and three addressed performance alone. For assessment approaches, nine studies applied WHO’s 2007 building blocks framework [11], two used WHO’s 2000 health system framework [12] and seven adopted alternative approaches, as detailed in Additional File 1, Table 5. The administrative levels studied were health facilities (12 studies), the subnational level (six studies: state, district, or province) and the national level (11 studies), with some spanning multiple levels (see Additional File 2 for details).

The studies covered various types of shocks, with armed conflict being the most frequently reported (n = 11 studies). Other shocks included disease outbreaks other than coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)) (n = 6), COVID-19 (n = 6), population movement (n = 2), extreme weather events (n = 1) and financial crises (n = 1), with several countries experiencing multiple simultaneous shocks. In terms of resilience capacities, most studies addressed more than one: 14 studies illustrated adaptive capacity, 9 assessed absorptive capacity and 7 demonstrated transformative capacities.

Indicators descriptive analysis

Service delivery accounted for the largest number of indicators, followed by governance and health workforce (Table 2). As an output that depends on multiple building blocks [38], service delivery indicators captured the capacity and resources required to provide care [6, 34, 39], as well as service availability, accessibility and quality [6, 34, 37, 40].

An important finding is the prominent focus on the Leadership and Governance building block, which accounted for the second highest number of reported indicators (n = 94, 20%). Importantly, 65 of these were quantitative, addressing the persistent challenge of measuring this building block. This differs from reviews in high-income countries, where governance is predominantly assessed qualitatively [3]. In our review, governance indicators assessed aspects such as the presence and quality of strategic direction, the capacity and resources to implement health strategies and the degree of community engagement [5, 40–45].

The health workforce was assessed through 45 indicators (10%), including workforce density and distribution, systems for tracking availability and measures to ensure safety and support to during emergencies [5, 37, 40–45]. No indicators addressed workforce production.

Health system financing, health information and medical products had 9%, 8% and 4% of the identified indicators respectively. This review showed that even within studies adopting a whole-of system approach, supply chain management and availability of health products during crisis were the least examined domain. The identified indicators primarily assessed stock of essential equipment, supplies, drugs and vaccines; the proportion of facilities not reporting major supply challenges; and the capacity of regulatory system [34, 37, 40, 42]. However, little is known about how supply chains perform under chronic stressors and shocks. Metrics are needed that capture the frequency of stock outs at national and subnational levels, the storage conditions, timeliness of distribution and the ability of the system to monitor the quality of the health products. In the health information system, data for decision making was the most studied (n = 25), with fewer examining governance and reporting functions (n = 12). No indicator addressed data and cyber security, despite its importance for protecting patients’ privacy and sustaining trust in the health system.

Indicators related to community participation and engagement were reported across governance, health workforce, health information systems and service delivery. They assessed trust, communication, community-level mapping of health risks, integrated community care and home-based services [34, 37, 39–41, 44, 46, 47].

Overall, 51% of indicators measured outputs, 36% inputs, 9% outcomes and 4% impacts. By study application, most indicators were quantitative (n = 351; 75%).

By resilience dimension, 181 indicators (39%) assessed the capacity of health system to engage with and manage multi-level dynamics and feedback loops (interdependence), and 180 indicators (39%) evaluated the ability to collect, integrate and analyse diverse forms of information (knowledge) (Table 3). Together, these two dimensions accounted for 78% of all indicators reviewed. Examples include mechanisms for ongoing analysis of case reports [46], active and/or passive surveillance systems, availability of essential equipment and supplies [34] and laboratory capacity to test for priority health threats [44]. Legitimacy defined as the system’s ability to govern, coordinate and develop institutions and mechanisms was assessed by 84 indicators (18%). Examples include the overall level of preparedness [41], availability of up-to-date health assets mapping, implementation of simulation exercises for high-risk shock events and the presences of public health emergency response mechanisms [5]. Only 21 indicators (5%) addressed the ability to anticipate and respond to shocks (uncertainty). Examples include plans for providing security for healthcare workers during emergencies, established evacuations protocols [46], the proportion of facilities reporting no moderate to severe human resource challenges, and the proportion reporting no significant supply shortages during the pandemic [40]. Several indicators were conceptually linked to more than one resilience dimension, reflecting the interconnected nature of health system functions and the overlapping roles that specific capacities play in supporting resilience.

Critical analysis of indicators

All indicators were assessed for feasibility, comparability and relevance. Overall, 230 indicators (49%) were classified as feasible. Of these, 122 were sourced from global databases and 58 from national health information systems, including DHIS2; all were classified as highly feasible (score = 2). Four indicators derived from administrative sources, such as health facility records and Ministry of Health data, were considered moderately feasible (score = 1.5) [43]. Although national surveys were generally deemed less feasible, 46 indicators implemented through special surveys were nonetheless assessed as feasible, as they were part of global reporting mechanisms [34, 43, 44], conducted via phone-based methods [40] or integrated into routine surveillance systems [5].

272 indicators (58%) were classified as comparable, with benchmarking scores ranging from one to two. Lower scores were primarily attributed to indicators that had been adapted or customized from standard definitions. A total of 190 indicators (41%), drawn from ten studies, received the maximum score of 2, indicating their strong potential to support comparability and benchmarking across settings [5, 6, 34, 40–43, 46, 48, 49]. Overall, 218 indicators (46%), drawn from 11 studies, met the criteria for both feasibility and comparability.

Among all assessed indicators, 161 (34%) were classified as relevant for assessing or measuring resilience. Of these, 61 (13%) also met the criteria for feasibility and comparability and are identified here as the core resilience indicators (Table 3). Of the 61 core indicators, 50 assessed the health system’s capacity to respond and adapt to shocks (criterion a), 10 looked at feedback mechanisms (criterion b) and 1 assessed performance over time (criterion c). Examples of criterion a include assessment of vulnerabilities to natural hazards [46], the ability to identify and isolate health threats [47] and the proportion of facilities reporting no financing challenges during the pandemic [40]. Examples of criterion b include systems for collecting and analysing real-time data [43], and formal processes for reviewing and responding to performance issues [5]. The sole indicator for criterion c captured outpatient service volumes during COVID 19 between March 2020 and December 2021, and compared this with the expected levels based on pre-pandemic seasonality and trends [40].

Discussion

This scoping review examined indicators used to assess health performance and resilience in fragile, low-income contexts. This review focused on studies that adopted a whole-of-system approach excluding those limited to individual building blocks or functions. As a result, 67% of studies eligible for full-text review (n = 179/267) did not meet the inclusion criteria. The paucity of evidence adopting a whole-of-system approach highlights a knowledge gap in assessing health system performance and resilience in fragile settings. One explanation for this gap could be the predominance of research approach that deconstructs health system challenges into smaller, specific components – such as individual building blocks or functions – that can be examined in detail. While this approach provides detailed insights into specific issues, it does not generate operationally actionable evidence. Policymakers require system-wide assessments to guide the allocation of scarce resources across competing priorities. Adopting a whole-of-system approach could enable researchers to act as knowledge brokers, supporting the translation of evidence into actionable policy recommendations bridging the gap between evidence and decision-making [50].

Our findings underscore the importance of assessing health system resilience across multiple administrative levels. While each study had a primary focus, many indicators spanned levels- for instance, 143 indicators were relevant to both national and subnational assessments. This is particularly salient in fragile settings, where crises often accelerate de facto decentralized and shift responsibilities to subnational actors [51]. Even in non-federal systems, decentralization during shocks was described as a strategy to enhance responsiveness and maintain service continuity [52]. Resilience assessment tools should therefore capture status across levels and consider how coordination and resource allocation between them shape health systems functioning.

This analysis identified a range of indicators drawn from global reporting frameworks, including the International Health Regulations, the Sustainable Development Goals, WHO-endorsed frameworks, as well as national health information systems. Nevertheless, standardized metrics accounted for 58% of indicators, while 42% were customized to the specific objectives of the study, limiting comparability across contexts. The lack of standardization remains a central challenge to assessing health system performance and resilience in fragile contexts. Furthermore, the diversity of indicators highlights the absence of consistent resilience measurement frameworks and tools. Most indicators were quantitative (75%), marking progress in operationalizing resilience measurements in fragile, low-income contexts. While measurement of the health system resilience is an essential step in the process to improve health system resilience, as “what gets measured gets managed” – it also raises important questions about what remains unmeasured. Quantitative indicators may overlook qualitative dimensions of resilience, and efforts to standardize measurement risk oversimplifying dynamic systems and obscuring local realities. Addressing this gap is critical: investment in resilience-building activities, monitoring progress, reporting successes and learning from failures all require a balanced approach that integrates both quantitative and qualitative measures.

Feasibility is especially important in resource-limited settings, where data collection efforts must maximize the use of available resources. Valuable information already exists within international datasets, national health information systems and administrative records, and leveraging these sources is essential for assessing health system performance and resilience. Where new indicators are required to address gaps, feasibility should remain a priority. Given the financing limitations, large-scale household and facility surveys are unlikely to be sustained. Instead, national health information systems, such as DHIS2, offer a practical opportunity as their metadata and reporting frameworks can be adapted to incorporate modules for resilience measurement [53, 54].

The relevance of any resilience assessment tool lies in its capacity to capture the multifaceted nature of resilience and to fulfil at least one of three functions: reflects the system’s ability to adapt and learn in response to changes or shocks, provides feedback to inform decision-making, or tracks system performance and behaviour over time. Yet major gaps remain in these areas. To address these challenges this analysis emphasized feasibility, comparability and relevance in selecting the Core Resilience Indicators. Future work should refine and prioritize a smaller, standardized set of relevant indicators to enhance usability and appeal for decision-makers. For future research, this underscores the need for developing fit-for-purpose assessment tools that balance quantitative and qualitative methods, capture adaptive capacity and learning and remain sensitive to contextual complexity, while also ensuring feasibility and comparability across fragile and low-income settings.

Although the complexity of resilience has often been cited as the barrier to measurement [23], advances in resilience research, incremental progress in resilience measurement and new opportunities through connectivity and machine learning suggest that meaningful measurement is increasingly possible. What is required now is deliberate investment in critically reviewing and leveraging existing data, while cocreating contextually relevant frameworks and tools to measure resilience. The knowledge base exists; what remains is the commitment to act.

Conclusions

This scoping review highlights the urgent need for more standardization in assessing and measuring health system resilience in fragile, low-income settings. Although existing frameworks and indicators provide valuable insights, their fragmentation challenges comparability and limits the ability to identify effective, scalable resilience building strategies. Future work should focus on refining and prioritizing a manageable set of relevant, feasible and standardized indicators. Establishing minimum standards for resilience assessment, underpinned by a set of Core Resilience Indicators, would enable systematic measurement and monitoring while allowing for flexibility to adapt to local contexts. Advancing a contextually appropriate, whole-of-system framework – paired with a pragmatic and adaptable assessment tool – represents a critical next step. Measurement must not be seen as an end, but as a means to guide investment, strengthen policy and ultimately build resilient health systems where they are needed most. Looking ahead, the increasing frequency of health shocks makes this agenda essential for global health security, and most importantly, for the 23% of the world’s population living in fragile and conflict-affected contexts.
