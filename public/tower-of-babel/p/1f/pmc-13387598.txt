From awareness to action: building monitoring capacity for tobacco and nicotine industry interference—experience from the JA PreventNCD project
Authors: Taija Voutilainen, Silke Cnockaert, Viðar Jensson, Zala Jeras, Helena Koprivnikar, Anneli Sammel, Stefanie Vandevijvere, Hanna Ollila
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndustry interference remains a major barrier to effective tobacco control. WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC) Article 5.3 obliges Parties to protect public health policies from industry influence, yet systematic monitoring of interference is often lacking. The emergence of new nicotine products and actors further complicate implementation and monitoring. Within Joint Action PreventNCD, we assessed monitoring and information exchange practices related to tobacco and nicotine industry interference in five European countries (Belgium, Estonia, Finland, Iceland, and Slovenia). Partner organizations (n = 5) completed an online questionnaire (July–September 2024) and the responses were descriptively analysed. To supplement the questionnaire data, we continued and expanded the search for recent interference examples until January 2025, covering examples from 2019 to 2024 through a review of publicly available sources; these were categorized by known interference tactics. Although all partners acknowledged the importance of monitoring, most countries lacked protocols, designated entities, or registries for industry interference. While some disclosure measures existed, such as policies requiring disclosure of meetings with industry and potential conflicts of interest, comprehensive safeguards were rare. Interference examples underscored the need for stronger monitoring, as known interference tactics—lobbying, harm-reduction narratives, corporate social responsibility campaigns, and use of front groups—were consistently employed, particularly around regulatory blind spots and new nicotine products. Systematic monitoring of tobacco and nicotine industry interference is essential to safeguard public health policies under WHO FCTC Article 5.3. Building national capacity, fostering collaboration between governments and civil society, and using international benchmarking tools are important steps toward this goal.

Introduction

Tobacco use causes more than 8 million preventable deaths each year worldwide [1]. Efforts to reduce this burden, however, are consistently undermined by tobacco industry interference, which remains one of the most significant barriers to effective tobacco control [2]. Article 5.3 of the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC) obliges Parties to protect public health policies from the commercial and other vested interests of the tobacco industry [3, 4]. Despite widespread ratification of the Convention, implementation of Article 5.3 remains insufficient, particularly in terms of systematic monitoring and transparency [5]. Without robust mechanisms to detect and document interference, policy processes risk being undermined by hidden influence.

From the historical perspective, the tobacco industry has long employed a well-documented set of strategies to interfere with public health policy. These include, e.g. lobbying against regulation, funding biased or misleading research, deploying front groups, promoting voluntary regulation over binding legislation, and corporate social responsibility (CSR) campaigns [2, 5–9]. CSR activities–such as clean-up campaigns and environmental partnerships–are often used to greenwash the industry’s image and gain access to policymakers, despite the inherent contradiction between these activities and the health and environmental harms caused by their products [10–12]. To counteract these tactics, it is essential to expose how the tobacco and nicotine industry manipulates public perception and downplays the risks of its products [7–9, 13]. Promoting transparency around these practices not only empowers policymakers to make evidence-based decisions, but also strengthens regulatory efforts and contributes to reducing tobacco-related harm [9]. Therefore, systematic monitoring of industry interference is crucial to safeguard public health and ensure accountability.

In recent years, the emergence of new tobacco and nicotine products–including electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes), heated tobacco products (HTPs), and nicotine pouches–has significantly altered the tobacco industry landscape. Tobacco companies increasingly describe their move into new nicotine products as part of a harm-reduction agenda. This framing can co-opt public health language by portraying commercial diversification as health driven. However, research shows this narrative often strategically misrepresents the fact that tobacco companies continue to rely on cigarette sales and actively seek to shape regulatory environments to protect their commercial interests [13, 14]. Thus, rather than signalling a shift in priorities, the expansion into new nicotine products has allowed major tobacco companies to diversify their portfolios and maintain influence in the evolving nicotine market, while other industry actors have also gained prominence, creating opportunities to exploit regulatory gaps [13, 15, 16]. Moreover, the tobacco industry has not abandoned its historical tactics; rather, it has adapted them to promote new tobacco and nicotine products–often targeting youth and non-smokers through appealing designs, flavours, and digital marketing–while simultaneously influencing regulatory environments [8, 17]. The growing popularity of these products among young people who have never smoked has raised concerns about nicotine addiction [18, 19] and prompted WHO to call for precautionary regulation and international coordination to prevent these products from undermining public health [20]. In this manuscript, we use the term “tobacco and nicotine industry” or simply “industry” to refer collectively to actors involved in traditional tobacco products and/or newer nicotine products, as many major tobacco companies now operate across multiple product segments, blurring boundaries between otherwise separate industries and employing similar political and commercial strategies.

Tools such as the Global Tobacco Industry Interference Index (GTI) benchmark government implementation of WHO FCTC Article 5.3 by assessing policies and practices across indicators derived directly from the Article 5.3 implementation guidelines [21]. The GTI is produced by the Global Center for Good Governance in Tobacco Control (GGTC), using a standardized questionnaire originally developed by Southeast Asia Tobacco Control Alliance (SEATCA) and implemented by civil society partners, who compile publicly available evidence following uniform search methods [21–23]. Despite its expansion, GTI coverage in the WHO European Region remained limited in 2023, with the European index assessing 20 of the Region’s 53 Member States [22]. While GTI helps identify gaps, strengthen monitoring systems, and encourage good practices globally, the evolving interference in connection with the newer nicotine products challenges existing monitoring frameworks. Although Article 5.3 provides a legal obligation for the Parties to protect public health policies from tobacco industry interference [4], it does not explicitly address the producers of newer nicotine products (containing synthetic nicotine). However, the implementation guidelines extend protection against organizations and individuals that work to further the interests of the tobacco industry. The lack of clarity, may allow emerging actors to exert influence with limited oversight, reinforcing the need for anticipatory and comprehensive regulation. Despite growing evidence of such involvement in policymaking [24–26], there is limited documentation on how countries monitor interference from actors involved in tobacco and nicotine industry.

Therefore, as part of the Joint Action PreventNCD (JAPreventNCD)—an European Union–co-funded collaboration aimed at strengthening the prevention of non-communicable diseases and cancer across Europe [27, 28]—our aim was to examine the existence and scope of national monitoring systems and information exchange practices related to tobacco and nicotine industry interference in five EU and/or EEA Member States that had not previously participated in the GTI (Belgium, Estonia, Finland, Iceland, and Slovenia). In addition, we set out to identify recent examples of interference, with a particular focus on new nicotine products, to inform future regulatory responses and strengthen the implementation of Article 5.3.

Methods

Study design and sampling

We conducted a cross-sectional survey in five countries—Belgium, Estonia, Finland, Iceland, and Slovenia—that represent diverse tobacco control contexts and had a shared interest in strengthening the implementation of Article 5.3 through their national public health institution’s involvement in the subtask 9.3.5 within the JAPreventNCD (Supplementary File S1, Supplementary Table S1; Table 1). For the purposes of this study, we defined “monitoring” in line with Article 5.3 guidelines and the broader literature on commercial determinants of health, referring to regular and systematic practices for collecting, documenting, and analysing information on industry activities that may influence public health policymaking [4, 29]. This encompasses both formal mechanisms, such as registers, protocols, and designated bodies, and informal practices, including information exchange, documentation of meetings, and ad hoc reporting.

Questionnaire development

To identify challenges and good practices in the implementation of measures that prevent influence and vested interests of the industry (the WHO FCTC, Article 5.3) and to assess the monitoring and information exchange practices related to industry interference, we developed an online questionnaire informed by a literature review [21, 30] (Supplementary File S2). Most questions were closed using either 7-point Likert scale (from 1, strongly disagree to 7, strongly agree), or three options (no/do not know/yes) with optional open-text fields for further elaboration. Before its launch, the questionnaire was created in Webropol online platform and reviewed and pilot-tested by partners. Necessary adjustments were made to improve clarity and functionality.

Data collection

Data collection via Webropol took place from July to September 2024, during which each national public health institutions submitted one response. To support accurate reporting, respondents could consult colleagues or relevant external organizations, including governmental organizations and civil society organizations (CSOs), such as NGOs, when preparing the institutional survey response.

To supplement the questionnaire data and potentially identify additional examples of industry interference, we continued the search after the questionnaire data collection period, reviewing publicly available sources until January 2025. Searches were conducted in the relevant local languages using structured but flexible keywords related to tobacco and nicotine products, industry actors, consultation processes, lobbying, and regulatory processes. The search aimed to identify illustrative interference cases between 2019 and 2024 in openly accessible sources, such as national newspapers, legislative consultation registries, and other publicly available documents. The identified cases were extracted into a shared dataset for analysis.

Data analysis

To summarize how many participating countries reported to have each monitoring practice and mechanism in place, we conducted descriptive analysis of the questionnaire responses using Microsoft Excel by calculating frequencies and percentages. The identified interference examples were analysed using a qualitative descriptive approach. We categorized the examples according to commonly recognized industry tactics (Influencing policies, e.g. lobbying; misleading research and misinformation; CSR; front groups and astroturfing; and legal challenges) [7–9, 30] and organized them by country to support cross-country comparison and contextualization of questionnaire findings regarding monitoring.

Results

Descriptive information of the respondents

All organizations in the five participating countries gave an institutional response to the survey (n = 5, 100%). Most respondents in these organizations were experts in the field of tobacco control, with varying degrees of prior involvement in policy development, but in one country, the partners had not followed or participated in tobacco control policy-related work before this project. However, all respondents at least somewhat agreed that they are well aware of the WHO FCTC and the Art. 5.3 implementation guidelines.

Monitoring and information exchange

Although all respondents recognized the need for and importance of regularly collecting and monitoring tobacco industry interference, assessment of national mechanisms for monitoring industry influence revealed significant gaps in institutional frameworks and regulatory practices across the participating countries (Fig. 1). Most lacked comprehensive systems for tracking interference. Only one country (Slovenia) reported having a designated entity and protocol for monitoring; however, this referred to an existing national public authority (Commission for the Prevention of Corruption) tasked with enforcing general lobbying transparency rules, rather than a body specifically mandated to monitor tobacco and nicotine industry interference. Two countries had national registers for lobbyists: In Finland, the newly established Transparency Register requires lobbying actors and consultancies to report their activities to Parliament and the ministries, while in Slovenia the Commission for the Prevention of Corruption maintains a legally mandated public register in which lobbied officials must report all lobbying contacts. Two countries also had general policies requiring public disclosure of meetings with the industry and potential conflicts of interest (COI): in Estonia these obligations stem from broad government transparency and COI rules, whereas in Slovenia, the Integrity and Prevention of Corruption Act mandates that lobbied officials publicly report all lobbying contacts and comply with general conflict of interest provisions.

Heatmap of perceived needs, benefits, and existing practices for monitoring tobacco industry interference across five European countries [Belgium (BE), Estonia (EE), Finland (FI), Iceland (IS), and Slovenia (SI)].

Only Slovenia had established consequences for misleading or false information provided by the industry and required disclosure of CSR and lobbying expenditures. In Slovenia, violations involving lobbying contacts are primarily sanctioned through minor-offense fines and administrative measures under the Slovenian Integrity and Prevention of Corruption Act. Individual lobbyists and public officials targeted by lobbying may face fines, depending on the type of violation. These violations may include unregistered lobbying, providing misleading information, or failing to report or reject prohibited lobbying contacts. Legal entities may face substantially higher fines, potentially reaching tens of thousands of euros in more serious cases. In addition to financial penalties, the Commission for the Prevention of Corruption may impose administrative measures, such as warnings, temporary bans, or removal from the lobbying register. Overall, enforcement relies primarily on financial and administrative mechanisms rather than criminal sanctions.

Despite the gaps in practices and protocols in these countries, most reported there was international collaboration, primarily through Nordic networks and EU joint actions (e.g. JAPreventNCD, JATC-2). Moreover, while all countries identified a CSO working in tobacco control, four out of five were unaware whether these organizations would have the capacity to monitor industry interference, and one explicitly reported insufficient resources. After further inquiry, the participants in three countries (Belgium, Finland, and Slovenia) were able to identify a potential CSO with the capacity to take part in monitoring needed for GTI.

Identified cases of industry influence

A consistent pattern of tobacco and nicotine industry interference, particularly concerning newer tobacco and nicotine products, was found across the participating countries (Table 2). Industry actors had employed a range of tactics to influence legislation, including lobbying during key regulatory processes, promoting alternative products as less harmful, and challenging proposed restrictions such as flavour bans and plain packaging. Legislative changes and tax policies were consistently focal points for industry influence, often accompanied by coordinated public relations campaigns and the strategic set of front groups to sway public opinion and shape political narratives. In several cases, companies bypassed formal consultation procedures, engaged directly with policymakers, or relied on selective scientific claims to advance their interests. Regulatory gaps related to the treatment of newer nicotine products further enabled these activities, allowing emerging actors to operate with less scrutiny than traditional tobacco companies. For example, in Finland, uncertainty around the classification enabled a nicotine pouch company to bypass transparency and disclosure requirements and attempt to influence outside the formal consultation period. Because the examples were identified through questionnaire responses and supplementary open‑source searches conducted in different national contexts, and without predefined inclusion criteria, the number of potentially relevant records could not be reliably quantified or compared across countries; the examples presented should, therefore, be interpreted as illustrative rather than exhaustive.

Discussion

This study examined national monitoring and information exchange practices related to tobacco and nicotine industry interference in five European countries (Belgium, Estonia, Finland, Iceland, and Slovenia) and found that systematic mechanisms for monitoring or responding to industry interference were largely absent. Existing practices and regulations were mostly limited to general transparency or lobbying frameworks, with no Article 5.3 specific safeguards in place. Together with the identified examples of industry influencing, illustrating notable gaps and blind spots around newer nicotine products, the results highlight that establishing comprehensive monitoring systems for industry interference and expanding the interpretation of WHO FCTC Article 5.3 to cover also entities and actors involved in newer nicotine product business are needed to safeguard the public health policymaking.

Although, all five countries have performed well on other tobacco control indicators [31, 32], they still exhibited significant gaps in national mechanisms for monitoring and regulating tobacco and nicotine industry interference, particularly in relation to newer nicotine products. Only Slovenia reported to have both a designated entity and protocol, but these were limited to general lobbying oversight. While general lobbying registries contribute to overall governance transparency, they may remain insufficient for capturing the full spectrum of interference tactics used by the tobacco and nicotine industry—especially in the case where these registries depend on lobbyists to declare their own activities. This limitation has been demonstrated in Ireland, where a well‑established transparency regime has not prevented sustained and wide‑ranging tobacco and vaping industry influence [24]. Together these studies highlight the need for dedicated Article 5.3-aligned approaches for documenting and responding to industry interference to strengthen transparency and accountability in policymaking.

The identified examples of industry influence, mirroring tactics observed in previous research [8, 25, 26, 30, 33], and the rapid emergence of new nicotine products and industry actors, not consistently covered by existing monitoring frameworks [16], also emphasize the need to establish comprehensive monitoring systems to protect public health. Example in Finland, where a nicotine pouch company attempted to influence regulation outside formal consultation processes, illustrate how regulatory blind spots enable interference. Another challenge is the product classification debates–such as those in Iceland, where PMI and JTI opposed the classification of HTPs as combustible using similar tactics seen in Australia [26] and Latin America [25]—which create loopholes the industry exploits to delay and weaken regulation [20, 34]. In the EU, the legislation defines HTPs as either combustible or smokeless [35], enabling the industry to contest national decisions to regulate them as combustible and thereby promoting industry-friendly harm-reduction narratives. Thus, there is a need to broaden the interpretation of Article 5.3 to include all entities involved in the production, marketing, and promotion of new nicotine products, ensuring that emerging forms of interference are systematically addressed. This would support also the implementation of Article 5.2b which requires effectives measure to prevent nicotine addiction. The recent GTI report and guidance from the JATC-2 project, provide practical tools for governments and civil society to counter interference, including in the context of new nicotine products [23, 36], stressing the need for proactive, transparent, and well-resourced systems to detect and respond to industry tactics.

Monitoring tobacco and nicotine industry interference is crucial for protecting public health. Effective monitoring helps shift the policy focus back to structural protections rather than individual consumer choice, which is often manipulated through these industry-led narratives increases transparency [37]. Monitoring also supports policy evaluation and acts as an early warning system for emerging products and tactics. Thus, it is not surprising that all participants not only acknowledged the importance of monitoring but also agreed that collecting evidence on industry interference can inform national priorities and help inform challenges and good practices in the implementation of the measures to prevent industry interference.

However, several factors may explain the current insufficient oversight. In our data, resource constraints appeared more relevant than lack of awareness, whereas GTI reports indicate many governments still lack awareness of the need for monitoring, fail to recognize interference as a policy issue, and remain unfamiliar with available tools—showing little progress in addressing these gaps [22, 23]. Moreover, only a few countries have programs to raise awareness on Article 5.3 within government departments or provide publicly available tools to support monitoring. Thus, as public health political science shows, implementation gaps are not merely technical deficits but reflect government priorities, institutional incentives, and evidence circulation [38]. This reinforces the need for multi-sectoral collaboration and capacity-building.

Thus, supporting countries in establishing or joining existing monitoring systems can advance the implementation of WHO FCTC Article 5.3. Previous examples in the European contexts show that participating in existing monitoring system drives meaningful policy reform: In Lithuania, monitoring led to national transparency framework, including mandatory disclosure of meetings with the tobacco industry [39]; Ukraine enhanced the implementation of Article 5.3, despite operating under wartime conditions [40]; and France and the Netherlands demonstrated how strong civil society engagement and institutional safeguards can effectively protect public health policies [22]. As the active role of CSOs–through monitoring, exposing, mobilizing, engaging, and litigating [5][mdash]is crucial for countering tobacco industry interference, it is essential to empower them to take on this role [37]. Hence, building on our investigation on identifying CSOs with capacity to participate in monitoring, three participating countries joined the GTI that in 2025 covers 100 countries, including 25 countries from WHO European region [23].

This study has some limitations. First, our dataset was small, with only one institution from five countries participating. Hence, the findings may not fully capture the range of perspectives and experiences in other countries. To enhance the depth of the in national context, several experts contributed to the formulation of the responses and consulted additional organizations, including governmental bodies and CSOs. In the future, direct engagement with government departments should be established to verify how monitoring policies are implemented in practice, ensuring accurate and comprehensive assessments. Second, monitoring remains challenging because many industry activities are deliberately hidden, making them difficult to detect. Thus, the examples on industry influencing on open‑source materials do not capture covert, informal, or undocumented interference, and should be interpreted as illustrative rather than comprehensive. Despite this, several concrete interference examples across the five countries were identified, demonstrating its value for initial assessments. Future research should consider broader respondent pools and more structured document searches to uncover covert interference and complement open-source searches.

The findings point to the need for dedicated, Article 5.3-aligned monitoring mechanisms that move beyond general transparency and lobbying frameworks and cover actors across the evolving tobacco and nicotine landscape. Effective monitoring should be independent of industry involvement and supported by coordinated action from public health authorities, civil society, and independent research organizations. International benchmarking tools, such as the GTI, can support countries in identifying gaps, sharing good practices, and strengthening accountability.

In conclusion, this study shows that monitoring systems for tobacco and nicotine industry interference remain fragmented and under-resourced in the five participating European countries. Strengthening independent, systematic monitoring is essential to protect public health policymaking as industry tactics evolve, particularly around newer nicotine products. Without stronger implementation of Article 5.3, governments will remain vulnerable to persistent industry influence.
