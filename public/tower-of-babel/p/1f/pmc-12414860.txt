Right ventricular myocardial blood flow estimated by 13N-ammonia positron emission tomography in patients with coronary artery disease
Authors: Masateru Kawakubo, Sana Tezuka, Michinobu Nagao, Atsushi Yamamoto, Yoko Kaimoto, Risako Nakao, Yuji Tsutsui, Yuka Matsuo, Koichiro Kaneko, Akiko Sakai, Shuji Sakai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBecause the myocardium thickness and blood flow of the right ventricular (RV) are lower than those of the left ventricle, it is challenging to perceive the RV myocardium in normal individuals. This study aimed to measure the myocardial perfusion in the RV (myocardial blood flow [MBF]RV, myocardial flow reserve [MFR]RV) from 13N-ammonia PET images and investigate the associations between the MBFRV and MFRRV in patients with and without coronary artery disease (CAD) in the right coronary artery (RCA) region. A total 121 MBFRV and MFRRV were retrospectively measured from PET images by referring to the radioactivity and clinical blood flow values of the left ventricle. Adenosine-stressed and resting MBFRV and MFRRV were statistically compared among patients with RCA-MFR < 2.0 (group 1 [n = 61]), without RCA-MFR < 2.0 but with reduced MBF (group 2 [n = 16]), and without MFR < 2.0 (group 3 [n = 44]) using one-way analysis of variance with Tukey or Dunn post hoc analyses.ResultsStress MBFRV was lower in Group 1 than in Group 3, whereas resting MBFRV was higher in Group 1 than in Group 3. MFRRV gradually decreased from Group 3 to Group 1.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated the potential clinical applications of RV myocardial perfusion imaging using PET data. Our results highlight the importance of evaluating the MBFRV, particularly in patients with CAD. In CAD cases, the MFRRV was significantly reduced, with a more pronounced decrease, reflecting the severity of the disease. Additionally, increased resting MBFRV in patients with CAD may indicate compensatory mechanisms or microcirculatory disorders. These findings provide a foundation for further exploration of MBFRV and support the development of robust automated processing techniques to enhance clinical applicability.Keywords: Right ventricle, Myocardial blood flow, Myocardial flow reserve, Positron emission tomography

Introduction

Contemporary consensus statements and guidelines direct the assessment and treatment of epicardial coronary artery disease (CAD) and the resultant myocardial ischemia in various clinical situations (Writing Committee Members et al. 2023; Collet et al. 2021; Amsterdam et al. 2014; O’Gara et al. 2013). However, among the multiple imaging modalities that can be used to assess coronary microvascular dysfunction in the absence of obstructive epicardial disease, positron emission tomography (PET) is the gold standard for measuring myocardial blood flow (MBF) (Mathew et al. 2020). PET is essential for quantifying MBF at rest, hyperemia-induced adenosine stress, and myocardial flow reserve (MFR), which is defined as the ratio of MBF during stress to that at rest (Slomka et al. 2014; Choi et al. 1999; Fakhri et al. 2009; Gulati et al. 2009; Ziadi et al. 2011). The MBF and MFR can be modeled as the 17 segments of the left ventricular (LV) myocardium and displayed as a color map to obtain the regional MFR for each coronary artery territory, including the right coronary artery (RCA), left anterior descending artery (LAD), and left circumflex coronary artery (LCX). The RCA also nourishes the right ventricular (RV) myocardium (Spigel et al. 2020); however, PET measurements of MBFs and MFR have focused on the LV myocardium. LV dysfunction has long been recognized as a major determinant of adverse outcomes, including heart failure and mortality, and remains a central focus of cardiovascular medicine. In contrast, the RV has historically received less attention, partly due to its complex anatomy and the technical challenges of assessment. However, interest in the physiology and pathophysiology of the right heart has grown, as accumulating evidence demonstrates that RV size and function are strongly associated with outcomes across a variety of cardiovascular diseases, including pulmonary hypertension, left heart failure, and valvular heart disease (Hahn et al. 2023). Additionally, low-risk myocardial perfusion imaging studies have demonstrated that patients with incident RV abnormalities may be more susceptible to adverse clinical outcomes (Jose et al. 2022); however, there are limited reports measuring RV MBF using PET images (Harms et al. 2023). Although metabolic imaging using 18F-labeled tracers and myocardial blood flow assessment using 15O-water have been performed (Han et al. 2023; Kudomi et al. 2019), 18F agents are still in the process of becoming widely adopted (Al-Mallah et al. 2025). While 15O-water provides excellent flow-tracking capabilities, its inherently low image quality makes it less suitable for visual assessment. In contrast, 13N-ammonia offers a favorable balance between image quality and quantitative accuracy, making it a suitable candidate for image processing-based evaluation of right ventricular myocardial blood flow. Furthermore, automatic RV segmentation in myocardial perfusion imaging using 13N-ammonia PET is yet to be developed. The myocardium thickness and blood flow of the RV are lower than those of the LV; therefore, it is challenging to perceive the RV myocardium in normal individuals (Entezarmahdi et al. 2023; Germano et al. 2007). Thus, in the present study, we calculated the MBF images by referencing the LV global MBF values measured from 17 segments of PET radioactivity accumulation images. We then measured the MBFRV and MFRRV using analyses based on the region of interest of the images. We also investigated the associations of the MBFRV and MFRRV among the patient groups with RCA disease, CAD without RCA region, and non-CAD.

Methods

Study population

This study enrolled a subset of patients who had undergone myocardial perfusion imaging with single photon emission tomography (SPECT) and PET in a previous study (Kawakubo et al. 2024). Notably, only PET image data, excluding the SPECT image data, were analyzed in this study. Patients who underwent resting and stress myocardial 13N-ammonia PET perfusion imaging for known or suspected CAD during a previous study (January 2014 to August 2023) were included in this study. The retrospective protocol was approved by the institutional research ethics boards of the two centers, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) age < 18 years, (b) congenital heart disease, (c) transplanted heart, and (d) lack of information about cardiovascular risk factors that were retrieved from patients’ medical records at the time of 13N-ammonia PET.

13N-ammonia PET imaging protocol

Patients refrained from caffeine consumption for at least 12 h before the 13N-ammonia PET examination and did not take any antianginal medications on the morning of the examination. Furthermore, 13N-ammonia myocardial perfusion PET was performed using a three-dimensional PET system (Biograph mCT; Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). Repeatedly upgraded Syngo VA30A_HF07 software was used for dose correction (the difference in residual 13N-ammonia activity between the resting and stressed images). Sequential CT scans (120 kV, 20 mA, and 3-mm slice collimation) were acquired for attenuation correction. Immediately after the intravenous administration of 185 MBq of 13N-ammonia (5 mCi), electrocardiography-gated acquisition was performed for 10 min with 16 frames per cardiac cycle using parallel list-mode acquisition. Perfusion images were visualized for the initial 2 min to obtain the arterial input function at the LV cavity, and myocardial perfusion images for the next 8 min. After the PET scan at the resting state, the adenosine stress test was performed (0.12 mg/kg/min for 6 min). Furthermore, 3 min after vasodilator administration, 13N-ammonia was infused (approximately 555 MBq, 15 mCi), and image acquisition was performed for 10 min, similar to that in the resting state. Images were reconstructed using Fourier rebinning and filtered back-projection with a 12-mm three-dimensional Hann window for the ramp filter. The reconstructed images were automatically reoriented into short-, horizontal long-, and vertical long-axis images using a 128 × 128 matrix. The pixel size of the static images was 1.6 × 1.6 mm, with a 1.6-mm slice thickness (resolution: 0.6 pixels per mm). Automatic reorientation of the images, automatic extraction of the mean myocardial and cavity time-activity curves, and generation of polar maps of the absolute MBF and MFR were performed using dedicated software (Syngo MI Cardiology, Siemens Healthineers). The MBF was estimated using a time-activity curve of the LV input and myocardial uptake using a three-compartment model and a dataset of list-mode images obtained in the first 2 min. The MFR was determined as the ratio of the hyperemic MBF to the resting MBF.

RV MBF and MFR measurements

We obtained images that showed the accumulation of 13N-ammonia in myocardial tissue. The MBF and MFR values were calculated for 17 myocardial segments. The mean values of the MBF and MFR represented the global LV myocardium. Global MBF values were quantified using a three-compartment model. Due to the high uptake rate of 13N-ammonia, MBF derived from the ratio of ammonia radioactivity to the vascular input function in the LV cavity correlates well with results from compartmental analysis using microsphere values (Alessio et al. 2013). We assumed a linear relationship between the mean radioactivity values of the entire LV myocardium and the calculated global MBF values. We processed the images to obtain global radioactivity values to segment the LV myocardial region. We then converted each image into an MBF image through a proportional calculation based on the global MBF value. Notably, all image-processing algorithms for stressed and resting PET were implemented using MATLAB R2020a (version 9.8; MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). First, an approximate region covering the LV was manually defined on the basal short-axis image (Fig. 1a). The extracted LV region was then over-rayed onto other slices of the short-axis image, and all LV short-axis images were masked with the extraction region. The threshold of the masked region was then determined using the Otsu method (Otsu 1979), and pixels indicating radioactivity smaller than the threshold were replaced with zero. This process resulted in the extraction of LV myocardial regions for all short-axis slices. Second, the images at all slices were polar-expanded counterclockwise from the centroid of the non-zero pixels in each slice (Fig. 1b), starting from − 30° in 1° increments. Therefore, three-dimensional image data were obtained (64-pixel × 360° × slices) as the image was polar-expanding. Moreover, to define the myocardial regions in each slice, the mean values of radioactivity at full width at half maximum were calculated (360° × slices). Third, the number of slices from the base to the apex was taken as 100%, and 6–45%, 46–65%, 66–85%, and 86–95% were merged into one slice using bicubic interpolation. Notably, the basal septal region, where there is no myocardium and where ammonia does not accumulate, was replaced with a merged value of 26–45% of the basal septal region. In addition, the upper and lower 5% areas of the base and apex were excluded from the calculation because the accumulation values were specifically low owing to partial volume effects with the extra myocardial region (360° × base-to-apex 4-slice) at the left of Fig. 1c. Finally, according to the 17-segment model of the American Heart Association, the radioactivity value in 360° × base-to-apex 4-slice was averaged corresponding to polar coordinates, resulting in a colored polar map. As shown in Fig. 2, radioactivity images on the horizontal long axis were converted to MBF images by multiplying their values by the ratio of global MBF to global radioactivity ​​(mean of 17 segments). The mean MBF value of the non-zero region in the manually drawn area was defined as the MBFRV in the middle slice of the horizontal long-axis images. However, the LV MFR in the 17-segment and MFRRV were calculated by dividing the MBF values at stress by those at rest.

Statistical analysis

The study cohort was divided into three groups based on the regional MFR with a cutoff value of 2.0 corresponding to the LAD (segments #1, #2, #7, #8, #13, #14, and #17), RCA (segments #3, #4, #9, #10, and #15), and LCX (segments #5, #6, #11, #12, and #16) (Ziadi et al. 2011; Fiechter et al. 2012). An MFR of < 2.0 is the threshold commonly used by clinical centers to define coronary microvascular dysfunction (Schindler et al. 2023). Groups 1, 2, and 3 consisted of patients with RCA-MFR < 2.0, patients without RCA-MFR < 2.0 but with LAD-MFR < 2.0, LCX-MFR < 2.0, or both, and patients without MFR < 2.0 in any coronary region. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.3 Mac; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical significance was set at a P-value < 0.05. Furthermore, based on the data distributions provided by the Shapiro–Wilk test, normally distributed data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, and non-normally distributed data are presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR, 25th–75th percentile). Stressed and resting MBFRV and MFRRV were compared among the three groups using the one-way analysis of variance with the Tukey or Dunn post hoc analysis. Similarly, the stressed and resting MBF and MFR in the LV were compared. Furthermore, MBFs and MFRs between the RV and LV were compared using the paired t-test or Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test for the three groups.

Intra- and inter-observer reproducibility

We analyzed the reproducibility of the semi-automated imaging process that was used to ensure the precise extraction of the myocardium for the linear conversion of radioactivity to MBF. For the LV myocardium, we determined the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with one-way (ICC (1, z)) or two-way (ICC (2, 1)) measurements of the mean radioactivity values for both the stressed and resting states. For the RV myocardium, we analyzed the ICCs of the stress and resting states of MBFRV and MFRRV. The intra-observer reproducibility of the LV and RV myocardial extractions was evaluated by using a blinded observer to perform extractions in all subjects. The observer performed a second extraction after waiting a minimum of one month. The inter-observer reproducibility was evaluated by using a second blind observer to perform measurements in all subjects. The ICCs were defined as excellent (ICC ≥ 0.75), good (ICC, 0.60–0.74), moderate (ICC, 0.40–0.59), or poor (ICC ≤ 0.39).

Results

Overall, 154 patients underwent 13-N ammonia PET between January 2014 and August 2023. Among them, one case of congenital heart disease was excluded, and 32 were excluded because of a lack of information about cardiovascular risk factors retrieved from patients’ medical records at the time of 13N-ammonia PET. Finally, 121 patients were included in this study. Sixty-one patients were assigned to group 1. Four patients had an MFR < 2.0 in only the RCA territory, five had an MFR < 2.0 in coronary territories in the RCA, LAD, or LCX, and 52 patients had an MFR < 2.0 in all coronary territories. Furthermore, 16 patients were assigned to group 2. Nine patients had an MFR < 2.0 in the LAD or LCX territory, and seven had an MFR < 2.0 in the LAD and LCX territories. Forty-four patients with MFR ≥ 2.0 were assigned to group 3. The detailed characteristics of the overall study population and each of the three groups are shown in Table 1.

Discussion

In clinical PET myocardial perfusion imaging, polar maps of the MBF and MFR based on the 17-segment model are typically provided. However, the PET-derived radioactivity images used to calculate these polar maps visualized the RV myocardium. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to propose a method for estimating MBFRV and MFRRV based on post-processed 13N-ammonia PET images. Although the evaluation was limited to a simplified assessment using a horizontal long-axis plane, the results suggest that this approach holds a certain level of validity. In the non-CAD group, the MFR in the RV and LV was consistently > 2.0. This finding aligns with the well-established MFR > 2.0 cutoff for ischemia diagnosis (Ziadi et al. 2011; Fiechter et al. 2012). Conversely, the MFR was reduced in the CAD groups originating from the LAD and LCX and further decreased in the RCA-CAD group. Given that 84% (51/61) of patients in the RCA-CAD group had triple-vessel disease, the decreased MFR likely reflected the severity of coronary artery disease. Notably, this study revealed a decrease in MFR in the LV and RV myocardium in patients with CAD. Additionally, the MFRRV was higher in the CAD group than in the CAD group. This difference likely reflects the greater absolute MBF in the LV compared with that in the RV, thus highlighting the pronounced decrease in MBF due to CAD. As evidence for this theory, MBF was consistently higher in the LV than in the RV across all groups, irrespective of the stress or rest conditions. This result is reasonable because the RV is predominantly supplied by the RCA and has a smaller myocardial mass than the LV. Some high outliers in resting LV MBF were observed. In our PET protocol, 5 mCi of 13N-ammonia was administered at rest and 15 mCi at stress. Therefore, the radioactivity counts at rest were lower than those at stress, resulting in noisier images during the resting phase. We believe that this increased noise led to greater errors in the calculation of MBF and MFR, particularly at rest. Although statistical noise can affect both stress and rest measurements, the fact that the outliers were more prominent at rest suggests that the lower dose of the radiotracer at rest may have contributed to these outliers.

Furthermore, increased resting MBFRV is an interesting finding in patients with CAD. We hypothesised that cardiomyocytes with CAD might compensate by increasing MBFRV to improve oxygen transport, and elevated resting MBF may be evidence of these structural changes. Guerraty et al. have verified this hypothesis in the LV (Guerraty et al. 2020). They reported that resting MBF was also associated with adverse outcomes because the decreased MFR often observed in patients with coronary microvascular disease may be due to increased resting MBF rather than decreased stress MBF (Kato et al. 2017). They also discussed why resting MBF increased as follows: One potential explanation is that patients with increased resting MBF had lower hemoglobin levels, which is associated with poor outcomes. A second potential hypothesis is that microvascular disease, which includes luminal obstruction and basement membrane thickening (Camici and Crea 2007), impairs adequate oxygen transport from the vessel lumen to cardiomyocytes, even under basal conditions. Therefore, the tendency of the LV resting MBF to increase in the CAD group, which was also observed in our cohort, was consistent with these reports. Thus, an increase in resting MBFRV reflects microcirculatory disorders of the RV myocardium. Consequently, we should also consider other points. Cho et al. reported that increased resting MBF was associated with a lower rate of smoking, younger age, less coronary calcium burden, and less coronary stenosis than low resting MBF in patients with angina (Cho et al. 2015). The resting MBFRV is smaller than that of the LV, and the changes are slight; therefore, it is debatable whether to discuss the risk of RV cardiomyopathy based on it alone. It is desirable to comprehensively assess RV myocardial perfusion by referring to the MBFRV and MFRRV in clinical situations. Sakai et al. reported that resting MBF increased in patients with ischemia without obstructive CAD but remained unchanged in those with CAD (Sakai et al. 2024). Ischemia without obstructive CAD is a newly recognized condition with an ongoing exploration of its underlying mechanisms (Bairey Merz et al. 2017); therefore, our proposed RV myocardial blood flow measurement holds promise for advancing its noninvasive understanding using nuclear cardiology. Based on these findings, assessing RV myocardial perfusion using MBFRV and MFRRV could help identify patients with microvascular dysfunction and potentially guide risk stratification and management. In particular, noninvasive evaluation of RV perfusion may contribute to better understanding and monitoring of conditions such as ischemia with non-obstructive CAD or RV involvement in CAD, both of which are associated with adverse outcomes.

This study has some limitations. First, it has a single-center retrospective design, which did not allow for an exhaustive evaluation of cases with isolated RCA ischemia or for adjustment of potential confounding factors, such as differences in patient characteristics (e.g., age, sex, clinical history). In addition, the lack of clinical or prognostic validation limits the ability to directly relate our findings to patient outcomes. However, the observed RV blood flow patterns were consistent with the established behaviors of the LV MBF and MFR, supporting the validity of our findings. Therefore, this investigation is the first significant noninvasive assessment of RV myocardial blood flow derived from imaging data. Furthermore, the clinical application of 13N-ammonia PET is limited by the requirement for an on-site cyclotron, which restricts its broader use. In this context, newer PET tracers such as 18F-flurpiridaz, with a longer half-life and the potential for wider distribution, are gaining attention and may facilitate larger (Al-Mallah et al. 2025), multicenter studies to further validate our proposed method. Excellent intra- and inter-observer reproducibility was observed in our proposed methodology for RV blood flow measurement. Our method is based on a semi-automated process that will ensure the precise extraction of the LV and RV regions. The inter-observer reproducibility in RV was slightly lower than that of other image processing techniques because of the complex structure and small size of the RV myocardium. Previous analyses have confirmed the reliability of PET imaging for manual LV and RV myocardium delineation with minimal intra- and inter-observer variability (Kawakubo et al. 2022a, 2022b); however, the development of automated processing techniques is crucial for the development of broader clinical applications and the design of further prospective studies. Therefore, future efforts should focus on refining these automated tools to enhance their accuracy and reproducibility in both research and clinical settings, potentially incorporating advanced image processing algorithms and machine learning techniques. The estimation of MBFRV and MFRRV in this study assumed linearity between 13N-ammonia radioactivity and global MBF. As a result, these values do not strictly reflect blood flow to the RV myocardial tissue. However, in the study cohort, MBFRV and MFRRV demonstrated a reasonable association with symptoms of CAD. Future advancements in the direct calculation of RV myocardial blood flow are needed. Nevertheless, our proposed approach offers a simple and practical method for analyzing RV myocardial blood flow using conventional imaging data.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this is the first study to demonstrate the potential clinical application of RV myocardial perfusion imaging using PET data. Our results highlight the importance of evaluating the RV and LV myocardial blood flow, particularly in patients with CAD. The findings revealed that the MFRRV, like in the LV, was consistently > 2.0 in non-CAD cases, aligning with the established threshold for ischemia diagnosis. However, in the CAD cases, the MFR was notably reduced in both ventricles, with a more pronounced decrease reflecting the severity of the disease. Additionally, increased resting MBFRV in patients with CAD may indicate compensatory mechanisms or microcirculatory disorders. However, despite the study’s single-center retrospective design, it establishes a foundation for further exploration of RV myocardial blood flow. It also initiates the development of robust automated processing techniques to enhance clinical applicability.
