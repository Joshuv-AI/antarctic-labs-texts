Live-stream infrared perception for lithium-ion battery thermal diagnosis
Authors: Luyu Tian, Chaoyu Dong, Huiqing Qi, Guoyu Fu, Hongjie Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAccurate thermal fault localization in densely packed lithium-ion battery packs is significantly challenged by mutual cell occlusion, hindering traditional single-perspective monitoring and thermal runaway prevention. To overcome this, we propose a novel two-stage thermal characterization and localization framework. Its core innovation uses real-time camera rotation to capture multi-perspective surface thermal images, effectively circumventing occlusion. Stage 1 uses a detection transformer (DETR) for coarse target characterization via video instance query and frame tracking. Stage 2 achieves precise cell-level fault localization using a 3D incoherent region detector combined with point quadtree-based mask fine-adjustment. Tested on Ansys Fluent-generated battery models under varying ambient temperatures, the system provides robust, accurate end-to-end thermal fault cell positioning under occlusion, achieving 0.78 mAP, 0.75 mAR, and 8.0 FPS inference speed with a 4.5G-parameter model. The refinement of the segmentation boundary in this work makes it clearer and provides a new paradigm for battery fault location using thermal imaging.

Introduction

The development of renewable-energy technology has been aided by global efforts to reduce carbon emissions and combat the fast pace of climate change.1 Energy storage is an essential component of renewable energy technology, and a variety of energy storage product deployments are necessary to achieve industrial transformation.2 Lithium batteries, such as ternary lithium batteries and lithium iron phosphate batteries, have become one of the most important forms of energy storage currently. It is widely used in energy storage power plants, electric vehicles, aerospace, and medical equipment. Lithium-ion battery faults frequently result from a reduction in battery capacity brought on by thermal, electrical, and mechanical abuse.3 Users must troubleshoot immediately to prevent thermal runaway.4 Thus, one of the biggest challenges is to prevent thermal runaway under different working conditions.

From a technical point of view, battery diagnosis is now developing in two major directions. First and foremost, new sensors such as mechanical or optical fibers had to be employed for monitoring.5 These techniques frequently reflect external battery faults by characterizing the internal structure of the battery’s damage, deformation, pressure, or temperature using impedance and ultrasound techniques. For example, Cai proposed a method,6 which diagnosed battery faults by monitoring the emission of CO2 from batteries during thermal runaway events through sensors. Due to its high cost and lack of technical maturity, this kind of approach is not commonly utilized.

Secondly, another type of direction is fault diagnosis methods designed based on existing electrical and thermal sensor signals.7 There are two types of methods for this kind of direction: model-based and signal processing-based.8 Model-based methods restore the internal chemical reactions of batteries through modeling.9 For instance, a second-order RC circuit model that is especially enhanced for lithium-ion battery external short-circuit fault states was proposed.10 This model can precisely identify battery problems during extreme events. Although modeling the battery can provide a more accurate estimation of its state and make warnings, fully simulating the internal chemical reactions of batteries and performing complex calculations requires a significant amount of computing power, which is not common in practical applications. The signal processing method typically computes parameters such as state of charge (SOC) and state of health (SOH) by analyzing signals gathered from sensors during battery operation, primarily voltage and current, to extract fault characteristics and enable reliable fault detection.11 Yang et al. employed a particle swarm optimization-simulated annealing-density based spatial clustering of applications with noise (PSO-SA-DBSCAN) algorithm for the online diagnosis of battery packs.12 Wang et al. present an early fault diagnosis method that integrates multi-scale features to facilitate early warning of batteries.13

The advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) technology has led to an increasing studies applying deep learning techniques in the field of batteries.14 For instance, Li et al. have developed a thermal diagnosis network for lithium-ion batteries utilizing the Transformer model, which predicts battery temperature based on voltage and current measurements.15 Tian et al. employ instance segmentation to identify thermal faults in battery packs.7 Deep learning algorithms exhibit enhanced feature extraction and learning capabilities, providing significant advantages in processing large datasets.16 This is precisely what is required to analyze battery characteristic signals. Consequently, the precision of battery fault diagnosis is increasingly dependent on the strengths and weaknesses of various algorithms. The signal processing method is ineffective in differentiating progressive small faults. To identify the fault area, it is essential to refine decomposition parameters or gather additional sensor data, resulting in inevitable redundancy.17

Consequently, we derived insights from infrared thermography. The application of infrared thermography for fault diagnosis in power equipment has developed significantly.18 It can lower the volume of data handled by the system because it is a non-contact temperature measurement technique. The integration of corresponding image processing methods can diminish reliance on technical personnel in the detection process.19 Thus, obtaining a surface temperature map of lithium-ion batteries with a tiny thermal imaging device can offer worldwide information while the battery pack is operating. Surface thermal imaging has been employed to identify defective lithium-ion battery cells.20 For lithium-ion batteries, this article suggests a two-stage live-stream thermal fault localization model that identifies faulty battery cells. Results

Training results

Pytorch 2.0 is chosen to build our diagnostic model. The initial learning rate is set to 0.002. To prevent model oscillation during training, the learning rate will decay to one-tenth of its original value every 10 epochs in the later stages. Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)21 is used to update weight parameters. The optimizer momentum and weight decay are 0.9 and 0.0001, respectively.

ResNet50 is used as the backbone during training. As shown in Figure 1, the total loss decreases as the training progresses and eventually stabilizes at around 1.1. The classification loss, bbox loss, and segmentation loss all decrease. The classification loss decreases to 0 after 65 epochs, while the bounding box loss oscillates in the first 25 epochs but also significantly decreases on the 65th epoch, ultimately stabilizing at 0.08. The largest proportion of the total loss is the loss of 3D incoherent region detection in yellow, which ultimately decreases to around 0.75. The reason might be the need to integrate different scales of FM for incoherent detection. The remaining loss mask, loss incoherent, and loss mask refine all rapidly decrease at the beginning of training, eventually approaching 0.05.

The evaluation for video segmentation is different from that for images.22 The metric for video instance segmentation is:(Equation 1)VISIOU=∑t=1T|Pt∩Yt|∑t=1T|Pt∪Yt|where VISIOU stands for the intersection over union ratio in video instance segmentation. The result of video instance segmentation is a sequence of masks. Pt represents the predicted value of the current frame. Yt represents the true value of the current frame. VISIOU calculates the intersection and union (IOU) ratio of each frame and then superimposes it, finally calculating the IOU of a video.(Equation 2)Precision=TP/(FP+TP)(Equation 3)Recall=TP/(TP+FN)

Equations 2 and 3 shows the calculation method of precision and recall, where true positive (TP) represents the positive sample predicted as a positive class by the model; true negative (TN) represents the negative sample predicted as negative by the model; false positive (FP) represents the negative samples predicted as positive by the model; false negative (FN) represents the positive sample predicted as negative by the model.23 The division of positive and negative samples is based on VISIOU in Equation 1. The predicted mask with VISIOU exceeding the specified value will be marked as TP.(Equation 4)mAP=1C∑c=1C∑n=1NP(n)Δr(n)(Equation 5)mAR=2C∑c=1C∑o=0.51R(o)Δ(o)

The mean average precision (mAP) and mean average recall (mAR) are metrics calculated based on the VISIOU. mAP is the average accuracy of each type of instance, used to measure the accuracy and precision of the model. mAR is the average recall rate of each type instance, used to measure the comprehensiveness of model detection.

In Equations 4 and 5, C represents the total number of categories. In Equation 4, n represents the current sampling point, and N is the predicted total instance number. The P-R curve can be drawn with recall as the horizontal axis and precision as the vertical axis. P(n) represents the value of the P-R curve at the current sampling point, and Δr(n) is the horizontal axis change. Therefore, mAP mathematically represents the area of the P-R curve and the horizontal axis, and then takes the average in the category. VISIOU as the horizontal axis and recall as the vertical axis can draw the Recall-IOU curve of recall changing with VISIOU. In Equation 5, R(o) represents the value of the Recall-IOU curve at the current VISIOU. Δ(o) represents the VISIOU change in the horizontal axis. Therefore, mAR mathematically represents twice the area of the Recall-IOU curve and the horizontal axis, and then takes the average in the category.

The mAP and mAR of the diagnostic model are calculated on the validation dataset during the training process. The training model with the highest mAP and mAR is selected as the final model for testing.

The improvement of model performance through two-stage optimization

We compared the segmentation results of the image-based thermal fault diagnosis network, the video-based one-stage battery thermal diagnosis network, and the model proposed in this article on the testing dataset. From the results, it can be seen that the developed two-stage network significantly improves mAP values, which is nearly 0.1 higher than the one-stage network. Because the two-stage network performs secondary refinement on the mask regions generated in Stage 1, the improvement in segmentation tasks is more significant. For example, in the segmentation task, the mAP improvement exceeds 0.08 compared to the one-stage model and exceeds 0.13 compared to the image-based model. This proves that the two-stage model is more precise in localizing thermal faulty batteries compared to the one-stage model. Compared to image-based networks, two-stage networks have a higher recall rate. Diagnostic results of battery thermal faults at different initial ambient temperatures

The initial ambient temperature influences the battery’s performance and safety. Elevated initial ambient temperatures exacerbate the challenges of battery heat dissipation, rendering it more susceptible to thermal buildup. The reaction of active substances within the battery intensifies, and the self-discharge rate escalates, as well as the growing likelihood of thermal malfunctions and the rate of fault progression. Consequently, we evaluated the model’s segmentation performance at various initial ambient temperatures. Most commercial lithium-ion batteries adopt aluminum as casing material (aluminium-plastic films for pouch batteries, aluminum shells for cylindrical/square batteries). The emissivity of factory-fresh smooth aluminum in the 8–14 μm infrared band is typically 0.1–0.3, while that of oxidized Al2O3 reaches 0.6–0.9. Non-metallic substances such as leaked electrolyte and thermal event residues during battery service, also show high emissivity, as do surface contaminants (dust, oil stains, fingerprints, electrolyte deposits). To simulate battery aging, oxidation, and surface contamination, we adjusted the surface material’s emissivity. An increase in emissivity causes the actual surface temperature of lithium-ion batteries to be lower than the temperature measured by an infrared thermal imager. Discussion

The article presents a two-stage lithium-ion battery thermal fault diagnosis system based on live-stream infrared videos. A one-stage detector based on DETR architecture can quickly identify thermal fault battery cells and locate faults. In the second stage, the boundary is made clearer through the secondary refinement of the mask, avoiding segmentation misjudgment caused by heat accumulation and the tight arrangement of individual cells between batteries. Ansys Fluent is used to simulate the heat generation of a 1P3S battery pack during single-cell faults, and real-time surface thermal imaging videos of the battery are obtained. The diagnostic performance of the model has been proven through testing, and the following conclusions have been drawn.1.A two-stage lithium-ion battery thermal fault diagnosis system is built based on live-stream infrared videos. By monitoring the thermal imaging of the battery surface, the end-to-end detection of video targets has been achieved. It is especially possible to locate the fault location in the battery pack, obtain the fault label, fault area, and confidence level.2.Stage 1 detector is designed based on deformable DETR, which encodes and decodes the feature maps output by the backbone network to obtain video features. The video query tracks the target of each frame before and after the video. In the second stage, a 3D incoherent detector was set up to recognize the mask boundary, and the detection results were stored using a point quadtree. The final Transformer outputs finely segmented faulty regions.3.The rotating camera positions cover 3D position information, which can obtain the global surface temperature of the battery pack during operation from different angles, completing real-time monitoring. At the same time, more precise segmentation boundaries also meet the segmentation requirements under the conditions of battery heat accumulation and tight arrangement of battery packs, avoiding model misjudgment and achieving better localization of thermal fault cells.4.Ansys Fluent was used to model and simulate lithium-ion batteries, generating a surface thermal video dataset of 1P3S batteries. The diagnostic system proposed in this article performs well in identifying faults in obstructed and tightly arranged batteries. The system mAP and mAR on the test set exceed 0.7, FPS exceed 8.0, and the confidence score exceeds 90%.

At present, this article verifies the 1P3S battery pack, including the performance improvement of the two-stage detector and the segmentation effect under different initial environmental temperatures. In future research, the application scenarios will be enriched to achieve the identification and localization of thermal faults in more complex battery pack structures and scenarios. At the same time, we will pay more attention to the application in practical scenarios.

Instead of replacing current diagnostic techniques, two-stage lithium-ion battery intelligent thermal runaway positioning efficiently achieves end-to-end diagnostics under a range of initial ambient temperatures and high arrangement density situations, which is a valuable supplement. Our approach can enhance diagnostic data by integrating signals such as voltage, current, SOC, SOH, and so forth. Once a thermal fault is detected, the system promptly transmits the relevant information to the subsequent safety management module, thereby triggering an alarm and preventing the fault from worsening.

Similarly, the model focuses on surface thermal imaging, which is not limited by electrochemical composition or cell size. Although the prismatic lithium-ion phosphate battery is used as an example, the method is also applicable to other batteries. Our detection approach may also identify surface temperature changes caused by thermal flaws, independent of battery parameters. For EV producers and commercial stakeholders in battery manufacturing, this technology is capable of reducing safety risks and operating costs in early detection, especially within battery packs with high alignment densities.

In addition, our method chooses a video-based instance segmentation algorithm instead of image-based segmentation. Algorithms for image-based segmentation can only identify objects in a single frame, whereas video algorithms can track objects within many frames, resolving issues such as motion blur, occlusion, and inter-frame offset, particularly when battery cells are arranged closely. As mentioned in Data acquisition, we set up thermal imaging monitoring with 8 perspectives as the training set perspective, so the system can locate faulty units from any perspective during actual deployment. Video-based techniques offer important application value in dynamic lithium-ion battery thermal fault scenarios and are able to track targets in real-time, including changes in location and shape.

In conclusion, this technique allows for real-time diagnostics and is a useful addition to current techniques that work with different kinds of batteries. Future research will concentrate on enhancing the experimental setting to allow for more thorough laboratory tests, given the significant safety risks associated with battery trials. Furthermore, our approach of fusing cutting-edge deep learning techniques with real battery thermal runaway diagnostics might further enhance the system’s real-time processing power and create new useful application scenarios for deep learning algorithms. The technical and financial viability of this strategy could also be investigated in future research. For instance, the cost-effectiveness of integrating thermal imaging into current battery management systems (BMSs), its potential for industry standardization, and its potential integration into international safety regulations for lithium-ion batteries could all be examined.

Limitations of the study

Although this study proposes a novel two-stage system for thermal fault identification and localization in lithium-ion batteries, the current work presents several limitations.

First, validation remains confined to simulation, lacking empirical verification through physical battery pack experiments, primarily due to significant safety risks associated with destructive thermal testing in laboratory environments. Consequently, the research currently only demonstrates the method’s feasibility in simulated settings, necessitating subsequent testing on actual battery systems.

Second, the computational complexity is notably high. While the 3D point quadtree effectively manages storage for multi-scale thermal feature map (FM) data, it introduces considerable computational overhead. Experimental results indicate that the parameter count of the two-stage model is nearly quadruple that of the single-stage model, inevitably compromising computational speed and imposing stringent hardware requirements. Future efforts will focus on reducing model complexity and optimizing data structures to lower the hardware barriers for industrial deployment.

Third, the impact of noise sensitivity remains unexplored. Actual battery operation involves environmental noise interference, while potential gas release during thermal events further challenges thermal imaging recognition. Additionally, the inherent noise in thermal imaging itself constitutes a significant interference factor. Future research will investigate diverse operating conditions and incorporate noise environment simulations to evaluate the diagnostic system’s robustness under more realistic scenarios.

Finally, the approach exhibits a complementary relationship with BMS. Being inherently reliant on surface thermal imaging analysis, it cannot replace existing thermal management functions within BMS but serves as a supplementary tool. This complementary nature constitutes a core limitation: the solution is not designed for fully independent industrial deployment. Its effective implementation requires deep integration with voltage, current, and other diagnostic signals to achieve comprehensive battery state assessment.

Experimental model and study participant details

Two-stage end-to-end multi-view video diagnostic network

Figure S1 shows the proposed structure of a two-stage end-to-end multi-view video diagnostic network, which obtains live-stream infrared videos of battery surfaces from different perspectives by setting the point position of the thermal imager as system input. The diagnostic system is improved by the DETR and is segmented into two stages. The input video is initially processed by the backbone, which is a feature extraction network, to generate multi-scale FMs. FMs serve as input for stage 1.

The stage 1 network employs a Transformer to do an initial assessment of the defective battery cells. The multi-head deformable attention (DeformAttn) in the Transformer encoder emphasises critical sampling sites in the FM and computes offsets to collect global information. The Transformer decoder calculates deformable attention across video frames using instance query decomposition. The decoder produces coarse segmentation regions via three distinct task heads: the class head, the bounding box (bbox) head, and the mask head.

To ensure localization accuracy, the coarse mask must be secondarily improved. Stage 2 enhances the imprecise mask. Initially, the 3D incoherent detector identifies incoherent loss points in the coarse mask and records the detected points in a quadtree. Subsequently, the encoder node, sequence encoder, and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) rectify the labels of ambiguous mask regions, ultimately producing the evaluation finding as a heatmap.

Infrared thermal imaging and Li-ion modeling

According to the theory of blackbody radiation, all objects with temperatures above absolute zero (−273.15 °C) emit electromagnetic waves.25 Therefore, the infrared thermal imager collects the infrared radiation of the target through the infrared lens, converts the radiation into an electrical signal, and finally generates a temperature distribution image (pseudo-colour heatmap) through the signal processing and display unit. Infrared imaging technology can measure surface temperature without touching objects, which is suitable for high temperatures and dangerous scenarios.

Figure S2 shows the case of equipping a lithium-ion battery pack with thermal imaging monitoring. Since the battery pack often works in series and parallel with multiple battery cells, the voltage and current of each battery cell in the battery pack are balanced during normal charging and discharging (assuming that the internal resistance of each battery cell is equal), so the surface temperature is also balanced. If an internal short-circuit fault occurs in a certain battery cell at this time, the short-circuit current of the battery cell rises sharply, and the surface temperature rises as a result, and a different surface heatmap from the surrounding battery cells. Since passive infrared thermal imaging relies on the physical thermal radiation, which penetrates conditions such as smoke or dust. It is not affected by the release of battery pack gas during thermal runaway. Therefore, the diagnostic model localizes the internal temperature of the abnormal battery cell according to the surface infrared thermal imaging map.

Ansys Fluent software is utilized to construct a 1P3S lithium-ion phosphate battery model and perform finite volume modeling (FVM) on the battery. FVM divides the physical domain into “control volumes” and forces equilibrium on each cell through an integral conservation equation. The individual battery capacity of the pack is 14.6Ah, the system power is 200W, the anode material is LiFePO4, and the cathode material is graphite. The minimum stopping voltage of a single unit is 3V, while the highest stopping voltage is 4.3V. The measurements of an individual battery cell are in Figure S3 and Table S1:

Method details

Stage 1: Transformer-based video characterization

Transformer architecture

Stage 1 is improved on DETR.28 The input video features obtained by the backbone are passed into the Transformer encoder. Since each frame of the video is continuous, the detected target also exhibits continuous shape and position changes in the video. However, Transformers do not have location awareness. Therefore, the input video FMs are divided into N patches. After passing through the embedding layer, sine-cosine position encoding is added,29 in Equation 17.(Equation 17)PE(pos,i)={sin(pos/100002i/d),i=2kcos(pos/100002i/d),i=2k+1,i∈[1,d2]where pos is the patch position of the input FM; d is the dimension output after position encoding; i is used for traversing in dimensions. The odd positions in FM are transformed using the cosine function, while the even positions are transformed using the sine function. The size of FM after adding position encoding is {ft∈R256×H×W}t=1T,t∈[1,T], where T is the number of frames in the input video, and the size of each frame of video is H × W.

The encoder and decoder of the Transformer are both 6 layers, the same as DETR. In the encoder, the entire video is first processed to ensure that the network obtains all the temporal and spatial information of the video from the first frame to the last frame. The multi-level multi-head deformable attention (MSDeformAttn) mechanism is calculated as (Equation 18):(Equation 18)MSDeformAttn(zq,{xl}l=1L)=∑m=1MWm[∑l=1L∑k=1KAmlqk·Wm′xl(ϕl(pq)+Δpmlqk)]where m is the number of heads in the multi-head attention, l is the number of layers in the multi-level FM, and k represents the number of sampling points. Δpmlqk and Amlqk represent the sampling offset of the k-th sampling point of the m-th attention head relative to the reference point. The attention weights of the k-th sampling point on the l-th layer FM and m-th attention head, which satisfies ∑l=1L∑k=1KAmlqk=1. ϕl(pq), rescales the standardized sampling point coordinates to the corresponding l-level.

Afterward, residual connections30 are made, and layer normalization is performed to accelerate the convergence speed during training. Finally, the data is linearly transformed through two linear layers to obtain the output of the encoder.31

To track targets within the video, the decoder independently executes a DeformAttn32 on each frame t, in Equation 19:(Equation 19)DeformAttn(zq,x)=∑m=1MWm[∑k=1KAmqk·Wm′x(pq+Δpmqk)]where x is the input FM (including key and value, i.e., K′ and V′ in Figure S4); zq is the input feature of Query, which is Q′ in Figure S4. M represents the number of multi-head attention. Wm and Wm’ are both trainable weight matrices in the m-th attention head; Wm′x represents Value. K is the number of sampling points in FM. The attention weight Amqk in the m-th attention head is a scalar. pq represents the position coordinates of zq; Δpmqk represents the positional offset of the k-th sampling point of the m-th attention head relative to the reference point.

At the same time, the decoder implemented query decomposition. Performing instance queries in the first layer decoder, ft obtained after two embeddings in the encoder is subjected to deformable attention with the initial instance query Iq to obtain the box query Bt1,t∈[1,T] for the first layer, as shown in Equation 20:(Equation 20)Bt1=DeformAttn(Iq,ft)

Afterward, the input of the l-th (l > 1) layer of the decoder is the box query output of the previous layer’s decoder, with DeformAttn performed in this layer, as shown in Equation 21:(Equation 21)Btl=DeformAttn(Btl−1,ft)

Finally, as Iql in the l-th layer is the superposition of Iql−1 in the previous layer and the box query, the instance query is updated in Equation 22:(Equation 22)Iql=∑t=1TBtl×FC(Btl)∑t=1TFC(Btl)+Iql−1where FC represents the output of Btl after passing through the fully connected layer.

Multi-task output head

The bbox head and class head in Stage 1 jointly output coarse fault battery labels, confidence levels, and bbox. The bbox head is composed of a three-layer feedforward neural network (FFN). For each frame of input video, the bbox head output is the predicted normalized center point coordinates with the height and width (x, y, w, h) of the box. The class head is composed of a linear layer. For each frame of the input video, its output is the probability that the battery cell may be faulty.

Stage 2: Live-stream infrared video localization adjustment

The proximity of cells in a battery pack may cause a thermal problem in one unit to propagate to others. The direct application of coarse masks may lead to ambiguous boundary segmentation and erroneous assessments of defective batteries. Consequently, Stage 2 is responsible for the refinement of the coarse mask.

Dynamic convolution generates coarse masks

Firstly, in the process of generating a coarse mask, the mask head consists of four layers of CNN and one layer of dynamic convolution. The dynamic convolution layer can identify more appropriate convolution parameters based on varying inputs, enhancing the expressive capacity of convolution while minimizing computational resources. The encoder’s output will serve as a mask branch, which will be combined with the mask head’s output to predict the mask for each frame in the video, ultimately resulting in the predicted coarse mask sequence.

However, the boundary area of the coarse mask is the most crucial place to concentrate on. The generation of coarse masks requires the backbone to extract features from the input video, followed by up-sampling and down-sampling processes. Upsampling and downsampling result in resolution loss, which is the cause of the inaccuracy at the segmentation boundary pixels. Therefore, to monitor the areas of resolution loss, we set up an incoherent region detector. The system inputs various scales of FMs from the coarse mask and backbone into a three-layer fully connected network (FCN) with ReLU activation to restore the corresponding scale of incoherent masks. Figure S5 illustrates the structure for generating a fine mask.

In Stage 2, we built a 3D point quadtree structure to store the origin nodes in FMs,33 which helps to better retain the various-sized FM’s incoherent points. The incoherent node at the highest scale of the FM serves as the root node, subsequently extending to four nodes at the next scale, as shown in Figure S6. Only nodes that have been identified as incoherent points can be further subdivided. The stored incoherent regions enable data fusion across various scales, thereby enhancing the processing capabilities of Transformers.

The data contained in the 3D point quadtree is initially processed by the node encoder and subsequently processed hierarchically. In contrast to images, videos encompass more comprehensive information and enhance the frame rate dimension. In Stage 2, the sequence encoder first employs attention processing on the quadtree nodes, namely the node encoder layer (NEL). Following each NEL, the instance encoder layer (IEL) incorporates a video instance query as both the key and value, whereas incoherent points serve solely as the query.34 Consequently, the sequence encoder can comprehend the boundary specifics of the video and the boundary particulars of each frame instance, which improves data management in quadtrees. The decoder employs a three-layer multi-layer perceptron (MLP) network and ultimately produces the correction labels for incoherent nodes. The finalized, rectified label values will be stored in reverse order within the quadtree and output as fine masks.

Loss function

It first needs to use the output of the class head and bbox head to find a bipartite graph that matches the prediction and ground-truth (gt), as shown in Equation 23.(Equation 23)Lmatch(yi,yˆσ(i))=−pˆσ(i)(ci)+Lbox(bi,bˆσ(i))where yi = {ci,(bi,1,bi,2,bi,3, …,bi,T)} yi = {ci,(bi,1,bi,2,bi,3, …,bi,T)} represents the gt of the video, and where i is the number of categories. yˆσ(i) represents the predicted value, indexed with σ(i) represents the value computed by the sigmoid function. ci is the label of the target category, bi is the gt bbox from l-th frame to the T-th frame. pˆσ(i) is the output of class head, bˆσ(i) is the output of bbox head.

To match a prediction to the best gt, we need to search for the smallest Lmatch regarding each predicted value, in Equation 24:(Equation 24)σˆ=argmin∑iNLmatch(yi,yˆσ(i))

After finding the best gt for each predicted value, the total loss is calculated as (Equation 20). The total loss sums the classification loss, bbox loss, and mask loss.(Equation 25)Loss=Lce+Lbbox+Lmask

The classification task uses binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss in Equation 26:(Equation 26)Lce=−1N∑i=1N[yilog(yˆσ(i))+(1−yi)log(1−yˆσ(i))]where, yi is the true value of i samples after a bipartite graph match, which satisfies yi∈{0,1}, and yˆσ(i) is the predicted value with a sigmoid for that sample. The loss is averaged over N samples.

The bbox task selects L1 loss and GIoU loss to measure the differences in position and size between the predicted box and the true value, as well as the relationship between the overlapping areas of the predicted box and the true value, as shown in Equation 27.(Equation 27)Lbbox=1N∑i=1N|bˆi−bi|+1N∑i=1N(1−GIoU(Bˆi,Bi))where bˆi and bi are the predicted value after bipartite graph match and gt of the i-th sample’s bbox, and Bˆi and Bi are their bbox regions, respectively. The loss is averaged over N samples.

The thermal diagnosis model is further improved on coarse mask segmentation with the loss function in Equation 28:(Equation 28)Lmask=Lcmask+Lrmask+Ldec+Linc

The total mask loss includes coarse mask loss, refine mask loss, 3D incoherent region detector loss, and incoherent region loss. The coarse and the fine mask loss are the same, both being the superposition of BCE loss and dice loss (DICE), as shown in Equation 29.(Equation 29)Lrmask=−1N∑i=1N[milog(mˆi)+(1−mi)log(1−mˆi)]+DICE(mi,mˆi)where mi and mˆi represent the predicted and true values of the mask region of the i-th sample, respectively.

Ldec reflects the loss of whether the pixel is a resolution loss pixel, that is, the superposition of BCE loss and DICE loss on the predicted mask boundary area and the true value. Linc selects L1 loss to determine whether the model’s judgment is correct on resolution loss pixels. The loss weights of these four parts are [1.0, 1.0, 0.5, 0.5].

Data acquisition

We simulated the heat generation of three battery cells when they suddenly experienced internal short circuit faults within 300s of normal discharge at 1C. During the simulation in Ansys Fluent, the 1P3S battery shifts from a fault-free condition to a state where cell 1, cell 2, and cell 3 encounter faults at distinct intervals. The thermal imager is positioned on the surface of the 1P3S battery pack, with the camera altering its location every second. The camera is capable of movement in eight directions: up, down, left, right, left-up, left-down, right-up, and right-down, relative to the front of the battery pack. The thermal imager captures the current thermal image as a frame with each movement, subsequently compiling these frames into a video from various views for the diagnostic model’s input. The battery cell malfunction and video frame rate in the final thermal videos are listed in Table S2:

The obtained videos from different perspectives are used as the dataset and divided according to the corresponding proportions (4:1:1) of the training dataset, validation dataset, and testing dataset. The number of videos in the dataset is shown in Table S3:

Among them, 32 videos in the training dataset include all perspectives, with a total of 1118 frames. In 8 of the videos, the battery is running normally without any thermal faults. The validation dataset and test dataset consist of 8 videos with a total of 617 frames, including only four perspectives: up, down, left, and right. The videos in the dataset are generated and recorded during the Fluent simulation process, without human intervention, to ensure the universality of the dataset.
