A novel design conception of foot orthoses promotes normal walking in patients with pronated feet
Authors: Fuhao Mo, Ke Wu, Ye Tian, Tengfei Tian, Michel Behr, Qi Wang, Chunna Lan, Tang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFoot orthosis (FO) is one of the most important treatments for pressure relief and gait rehabilitation in the foot misalignment population. The previous design conception was primarily focused on arch support and pressure reduction, but recent studies also questioned their efficiency.MethodsThree bionic fish bone FOs based on the COP characteristics of healthy people were designed and manufactured. The orthoses were tested in 30 volunteers through a series of experiments including both static standing and dynamic gait, and evaluated through finite element (FE) simulations with a detailed ankle-foot model considering deep tissue deformation.ResultsIn the static standing experiments, the calcaneus eversion angle was corrected with average ratios of 56%, 43% and 37% through three FO interventions, while the navicular drop height was reduced by average ratios of 65%, 54% and 46%. In dynamic gait experiments, obvious corrections for the COP curves of the volunteers were noted when comparing the COP corridor of healthy people, while COP excursion index values also increased by average ratios of 34%, 32% and 21% through 3 FO interventions. By the way, the peak plantar pressure was spontaneously reduced with an average ratio from 3% to 10%. Dynamic gait analysis with FE models also demonstrated that the foot orthoses can significantly reduce strain levels in the deep plantar soft tissues of flat feet from 1.6% to 28.9%.ConclusionThe change of evaluation indexes indicated significant effects of the present design on the correction of abnormal foot functions. We also believe that the present design can be extended for injury prevention or rehabilitation of other foot function-related diseases, such as diabetes or foot ligament laxity.Keywords: Foot, Gait, Biomechanics, Center of pressure, Foot orthosis

Introduction

Compensatory pronation, which is characterized by the falling of the medial longitudinal arch, eversion of the hindfoot and abduction of the loaded forefoot, may occur for anatomical reasons [1]. It can cause malalignment of the lower extremities, frequently leading to structural and functional deficits both in standing and walking [2, 3]. Recent systematic reviews have found that nonnormal foot postures, such as pes planus (low medial longitudinal arch) and pes cavus (high medial longitudinal arch), are associated with increased odds of injury to the lower extremities [4, 5], such as metatarsal stress fracture, plantar fasciitis, and Achilles tendonitis. In addition, a pronated foot is also associated with an increased risk of medial tibial stress syndrome and patellofemoral pain [6, 7]. Therefore, research and engineering developments on the treatment of pronation feet are currently increasing [8].

Different strategies have been developed for controlling foot pronation. Among them, foot orthosis (FO) is one treatment for pressure relief and gait rehabilitation in the foot misalignment population. FO has been advocated and successfully used for improving lower extremity function and pain control in patients with various foot problems and physically intensive activities [9–12]. It can improve foot pain in a variety of ways, such as by providing better cushioning to the plantar [13], improving sensory feedback [14, 15], adjusting skeleton alignment [16], reducing joint sprains [17] that may result from exercise, and modifying center of pressure (COP) pathways [18].

Recently, research on FO has rapidly emerged, and several novel foot orthoses have been proposed for pronation patients [19]. Some are custom-made based on the scan or cast of the patient’s foot [20, 21], and some are mass-produced. The design mechanism of most existing foot orthoses is to reasonably distribute plantar pressure by providing foot arc support to increase the contact area and reduce concentrated pressure. Reina-Bueno et al. found that, compared with placebo flat cushioning insoles, custom-made foot orthoses significantly reduced the foot pain of patients with rheumatoid arthritis [20]. Burns et al. found that foot pain scores of patients with a painful cavus or high-arched foot improved by 74% with custom foot orthoses compared with the scores of a control group who received simple sham insoles [12]. On the other hand, there are also several previous studies indicating that currently there is no evidence to suggest that prescribing foot orthoses individually offers any benefits over standardized devices [11, 22, 23]. However, conventional orthoses that focus solely on supporting the medial longitudinal arch provide mainly static structural assistance and fail to address the abnormal dynamic loading trajectory of the COP that characterizes pronated feet. Excessive medial deviation of the COP has been shown to induce compensatory tibial rotation and inefficient energy transfer during gait, suggesting that arch-support–based designs may alleviate symptoms without restoring normal biomechanical function [24, 25]. This proposed the question of whether the current mainstream design mechanism and customization method can be adjusted to achieve a more efficient FO design.

Then, the purpose of the present research was to propose a new design conception foot orthoses for pronated foot rehabilitation, in which the critical focus is on adjusting the COP pathway rather than merely supporting the medial longitudinal arch. In contrast to conventional orthoses that mainly provide static arch support to redistribute plantar pressure, a COP pathway–modifying orthosis aims to guide the progression of the COP along a more physiological mediolateral trajectory, as observed in healthy individuals. By restoring a balanced COP transition from heel strike to toe-off, this approach may improve hindfoot alignment, reduce abnormal medial loading, and normalize lower-limb kinematics. Previous studies indicated that different COP characteristics can represent different mechanisms for generating force to facilitate the forward progression of the body during propulsion [23, 26–28], which further underscores the clinical relevance of COP modulation as a biomechanical corrective strategy. Specifically, we hypothesized that guiding the COP pathway of individuals with pronated feet toward the physiological pattern observed in healthy controls will improve hindfoot alignment, reduce excessive medial loading, alleviate abnormal strain in plantar soft tissues, and normalize gait kinematics. To test this hypothesis, three novel bionic foot orthoses based on the COP characteristics of healthy people were designed, manufactured, and evaluated through finite element simulations and a series of experiments including static standing and dynamic gait tests. Furthermore, a balanced customized and mass-production strategy is discussed based on the structural features of the present design conception.

Materials and methods

Design of bionic fishbone foot orthosis

The design conception and prototypes of a novel FO are shown in Fig. 1. The FO consists of six parts: a breathable covering layer, a soft buffer layer, a hard buffer layer, a key supporting layer, a forefoot pad and a heel pad (Fig. 1A). The corresponding materials for different parts are also listed in the figure. The key supporting layer, which was manufactured like a fishbone, was designed based on the COP curve of 230 healthy volunteers determined in a previous study [29]. The backbone was designed by offsetting the mean COP corridor 5 mm toward the two sides, while the new offset corridor is also in good agreement with the experimental COP corridor of the healthy volunteers, as shown in Fig. 1B. Then, spurs were cut on the two sides of the backbone with different widths and numbers that were used to adjust the stiffness on the two sides of the backbone. In this case, the orthosis can adjust the patient’s gait with pronation to achieve a reasonable COP status fitting healthy people and reduce the plantar pressure at the same time.

Overall introduction of the novel FO. (A) Design conception based on the mean COP curve of healthy people. (B) Multilayer structure of the FO. (C) Fishbone orthoses prototypes with different key supporting layers

Three different types of orthoses were manufactured considering different stiffness arrangements, as shown in Fig. 1C. The fishbone-like supporting layer was specifically designed to permit regional modulation of stiffness along the medial–lateral sides of the COP pathway. This structural strategy not only enables the COP pathway of individuals with pronated feet to be guided toward the physiological COP corridor observed in healthy controls, but also reduces localized plantar pressure while maintaining adequate overall support. Moreover, the bilateral cut-out spurs of the fish-bone structure provide localized cushioning effects on both sides of the plantar surface, which alleviate concentrated pressure, improve perceived comfort, and enhance the likelihood of long-term patient adherence compared with fully rigid orthoses. By varying the number and width of cut-out spurs in the hard nylon layer, the medial–lateral stiffness distribution of the orthosis could be finely tuned. FO1 incorporated the largest asymmetry in stiffness, thereby providing the strongest corrective force and being most suitable for highly pronated feet. FO2 applied a moderate stiffness difference and was intended for individuals with moderate pronation. FO3 demonstrated the smallest asymmetry, offering milder correction while emphasizing comfort, and was considered appropriate for subjects with slight pronation. Collectively, these three prototypes covered a spectrum of correction levels corresponding to different severities of pronation.

Study subjects

A group of volunteers were recruited to participate in this study. The inclusion criteria were defined as follows: age less than 50 years, foot length between 235 mm and 255 mm, and pronation feet with Foot Posture Index (FPI) scores more than 6. The exclusion criteria were defined as follows: emerging pronation caused by trauma or other musculoskeletal diseases, skeletal musculoskeletal system diseases and central or peripheral nerve injuries affecting standing and walking; unequal lengths of the lower limbs; an absolute length of more than 2 cm as confirmed by X-ray measurement. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of Xiangya Second Hospital. The subjects signed the informed consent form approved by the ethics committee before participating in the study.

Classification

An approach to evaluating standing foot posture is offered by the Foot Posture Index (FPI) [30–32]. This methodology is valid, reliable, multidimensional, and easily accessible for health professionals in a clinical context. And the FPI criteria were used to quantify the volunteer’s pronation levels, each of which was graded from 0 (neutral) to +1 or +2 (pronated) and −1 or −2 (supinated). The FPI assessment was conducted by two previously trained physical therapists. Total scores from 0 to +5 were ranked as normal, scores of +6 to +9 were ranked as pronated, a score of 10 + was ranked as highly pronated, scores of −1 to −4 were ranked as supinated, and scores of −5 to −12 were ranked as highly supinated.

Assessment of the foot orthosis

Assessment by experiment

To evaluate the performance of the foot orthoses, 3 different experiments were designed, including static standing, navicular drop and gait experiments (Fig. 2). The correction of foot orthoses on foot function was evaluated by indexes, such as eversion angle, navicular drop height, plantar pressures and COP curves of the gait. In total, 30 males with pronated feet were recruited for the experiments. Only male participants were included to reduce potential sex-related variability in foot morphology and flexibility in this initial evaluation. Moreover, as the initial orthosis prototypes were designed based on plantar scan data obtained from male subjects, restricting the participants to men ensured consistency with the design foundation. All volunteers needed to try the three abovementioned foot orthoses. To avoid sequential biases on subjective judgments, the three foot orthoses were randomly provided to the volunteers in all the tests, and a five-minute gap was required between each test.

Overview of the experimental setup for evaluating FO performance: (A) Static standing for testing eversion angle correction and navicular drop test for measuring the navicular height change and (B) a normal gait test to evaluate plantar pressure and COP curve changes

In the static stance test, all subjects were asked to subjectively assess the three foot orthoses by finishing a questionnaire, while the foot alignment was examined as an objective contributing factor. Foot alignment was evaluated according to the calcaneal eversion angle (Fig. 2A), which was measured as the angle between the bisector of the posterior surface of the calcaneus and a vertical line in the relaxed calcaneal stance position [33]. All subjects were instructed to stand still for 60 s and relax with their feet shoulder-width apart on the floor and their eyes directed straight ahead without any movement, such as inclinations and rotations, that may interfere with the measurement. The calcaneal eversion angle was captured by cameras and calculated by marker points.

Navicular drop was assessed using the standard navicular drop test, and all measurements were conducted by the same experienced clinician to ensure consistency. In the barefoot control condition, the vertical distance from the navicular tuberosity to the floor was measured first in a seated position with the feet relaxed, and then again in a standing position. The navicular drop value was defined as the difference between the seated and standing heights. In the orthosis conditions, the same procedure was followed while participants wore the test orthoses. Because the presence of the orthosis could artificially increase the measured standing height of the navicular tuberosity, the local thickness of the orthosis beneath the navicular region was measured and subtracted from the recorded standing height. In this way, the resulting navicular drop values reflected true postural changes of the foot, rather than being influenced by the material thickness of the orthoses.

In the gait experiment, plantar pressures and COP curves were examined as contributing factors. Scan (Tekscan Inc., Boston MA) pressure measurement system inside the shoe was used to record plantar pressures in the complete gait cycle. The thickness of the F-Scan pressure insole was 0.15 mm, with an embedded gridwork of 960 contact points. The disposable sensors were trimmed to fit into shoes before the test, with the resultant loss of approximately 5% to 10% of the contact points. The data recording frequency was 50 frames per second. The recorded data were transferred to the computer via the VersaTek Cuff, and the data cable was connected to the pressure insole and processed by F-Scan Research 7.50 software. Available data for analysis were extracted when the subjects indicated that they were familiarized with the experimental procedure and the foot orthoses.

Statistical analysis was conducted to evaluate the test results by SPSS 2.0. Significant difference of the calcaneal eversion angle, navicular height and center of pressure excursion index were evaluated based on P-value.

Assessment by finite element simulation

As the effectiveness of the foot orthosis on deep soft tissue deformation is hard to be measured in the experiment, the finite element simulation with a detailed ankle-foot model was adopted to evaluated it [40]. The ankle-foot model was developed in the previous study and reconstructed based on medical images of a 50th percentile Chinese male volunteer (height 173.0 cm, weight 71.2 kg). The volunteer’s foot type index was ascertained to be +6, concurrently manifesting a diminished arch with a degree of flattening in the central position. This observational outcome resulted in the classification of the volunteer’s foot type as flatfoot. After ensuring a snug fit between the foot and the FOs, the keyword *CONTACT_TIED_SURFACE_TO_SURFACE is configured to simulate the interaction between the ankle-foot model and the orthosis during the gait process. Then, continuous dynamic gait loading was simulated at a standard working speed of 1.2 m/s (Fig. 3). As presented in the previous study [40], the models boundary conditions for dynamic gait loading are shown in Fig. 3. A rigid plate with all degrees of freedom fixed was employed to represent the ground. The initial angle between the foot and the ground was set to 18°. The time series of rotational angles at the talus and time series of ground reaction forces were applied, which were derived from standardized experimental data referenced in [47]. The friction coefficient between the ground, the foot orthoses and the foot soft tissues of the foot is 0.6 [48].

Results

Participants characteristics

Following the exclusion criteria and FPI-6 scores, a total of 30 male volunteers (age: 24.9 ± 7.3 years, BMI: 23.5 ± 2.4 kg/m2) with a foot length of approximately 248 ± 7.68 mm, a foot width of approximately 96 ± 5.8 mm and FPI scores of more than 6 (FPI: 9.4 ± 1.5) were recruited in the present study. The participants were divided into 3 groups according to FPI scores: slight pronation (FPI scores from 7 to 8), middle pronation (FPI scores from 9 to 10), and high pronation (FPI scores from 11 to 12). Eight subjects were assigned to the slight pronation group, fifteen subjects were placed in the middle pronation group, and seven subjects were assigned to the high pronation group. The data were normally distributed. Participants characteristics are shown in Table 1, and the more details of the participants are shown in Appendix 1.

Calcaneus eversion angle and navicular drop height

Wearing fishbone foot orthoses obviously corrected both calcaneus eversion angles and reduced the navicular dropped heights of the volunteers, as shown in Fig. 3. All data were consistent with a normal distribution. For all subjects, the corrective calcaneal eversion angles obtained by wearing the orthoses were lower than their initial eversion angles (Fig. 4A). The correction results of the calcaneus eversion angles with 3 FOs revealed a significant difference before and after prescription (p < 0.01) according to the paired sample test. The initial calcaneal eversion angle of the subjects ranged from 5 to 14 degrees with a mean value of 9.9 degrees. The corrective angle ratio achieved by wearing the foot orthoses ranges from 10% to 100%. Both FO1 and FO2 presented a maximum correction percentage of 100% with a maximum correction angle of 12 degrees, and FO3 presented a maximum correction percentage of 60% with a maximum correction angle of 7 degrees. According to the paired sample test, a nonsignificant correlation was noted between the pronation levels and the foot orthoses types concerning the correction of the calcaneus eversion angle (Table 2).

For all subjects, the navicular drop height was reduced after foot orthosis prescription (Fig. 4B). The correction results of the navicular drop height with 3 FOs revealed a significant difference before and after prescription (p < 0.01), according to the paired sample test. The initial navicular drop height of the subjects ranged from 3 to 13 mm with a mean value of 6.4 mm. The reduced ratio of the navicular drop height by wearing the foot orthoses ranged from 17% to 100%, while a maximum reduction in the drop height reached 9 mm by wearing FO1. A nonsignificant correlation was noted between pronation levels and foot orthoses concerning the change in navicular drop height (Table 2).

The results of the subjective assessment questionnaire (Appendix 1) indicated that the use of orthoses induces a significant improvement in the comfort scores. Among the 3 FOs, higher comfort scores were noted for the subjects with slight pronation (FPI scores from 7 to 8) when using FO3. The subjects with moderate pronation (FPI scores from 9 to 10) and high pronation (FPI scores from 11 to 12) generally had higher comfort scores to FO1. Most participants wore all 3 orthoses without significant pain, except two participants with slight pronation who felt low pain when wearing FO1.

Peak pressure in the stance phase

Three separate plantar regions were selected and their regional peak pressure records were used to evaluate the performance of the foot orthoses, which included the lateral side of the first metatarsal, the plantar foot and the lateral heel. The statistical results are shown in Fig. 5A - C. According to the paired sample test, the peak pressure of the plantar foot drop was significantly reduced by wearing FO1 (p < 0.05), while a nonsignificant reduction was noted by wearing FO2 and FO3. The average peak pressure of the plantar foot was reduced by approximately 50 kPa with FO1 corresponding to 10% of the initial value, followed by a reduction of 25 kPa with FO2 corresponding to 4% of the initial value, and a reduction of 18 kPa with FO3 corresponding to 3% of the initial value. The average peak pressure of the lateral side of the first metatarsal was reduced by approximately 43 kPa or 18% of the initial value when using FO1, followed by a reduction of 25 kPa and 10% with FO2 and a reduction of 5 kPa and 2% with FO3. A significant correlation was still not noted between the pronation levels and the foot orthoses concerning the peak pressures of different regions (Table 2).

Biomechanical response of gait simulation

Based on the contact interactions between the foot-orthosis system and the ground, the gait process was partitioned into three distinct phases. The first phase involved exclusive contact between the heel of the foot and the insole of the orthosis with the ground. The second phase encompassed full-foot contact between the foot and the orthosis with the ground. Finally, the third phase consisted of sole interaction between the forefoot and the orthosis with the ground.

Strain data for the soft tissues of the foot were extracted for each of these gait phases (Fig. 8). In comparison to walking without FOs, all three types of FOs led to a reduction in plantar soft tissue strain throughout the gait cycle from 1.6% to 28.9%. The largest strain levels always appear in the deep soft tissue around bone structure. To absorb the energy of the heel hitting the ground, FOs has a buffer EVA (ethyl vinyl acetate) in the heel area. As a result, the soft tissue strain at the heel experienced a reduction ranging from 15.1% to 16.1% by using FOs. In the midfoot upright grounding phase, deep tissue strain reduction reached 16.9%~28.9%. Notably, FO1 showed the most significant effect, with a 28.9% reduction in plantar soft tissue strain. During the forefoot landing phase, the EVA material functioned to propel the forefoot while alleviating compression of the metatarsal bone against the soft tissue, resulting in strain relief of 1.6% to 4.0%. The finite element simulation results demonstrated a significant decrease in soft tissue strain when the FOs was used. Hence, considering both experimental and simulation results, the utilization of FOs can lead to a dual benefit of not only diminishing average plantar pressure but also mitigating strain within the deep plantar tissues.

Discussion

Foot formation designs for different foot function diseases are attracting considerable attention from manufacturers and researchers. However, through many years of involution, the previous designs of FOs were still primarily focused on how to support the arc of the foot and distribute pressure [21, 34, 35]. Classic biomechanical and anatomical studies have long emphasized the central role of the COP in foot mechanics. The theoretical framework of subtalar joint motion originally described by Manter [36] and detailed in Cunningham’s Textbook of Anatomy [37] has been extended by modern gait analyses, which confirmed that mediolateral shifts in COP strongly influence hindfoot alignment and stability. Borelli’s early principles of dynamic force transmission have been revisited in recent years using finite element models and motion capture techniques [38, 39], demonstrating that modulation of COP directly affects plantar tissue stress distribution. More recently, Fuller [24, 42] highlighted that COP progression reflects the functional engagement of the windlass mechanism and proposed its relevance as a clinical target for orthotic intervention. Additionally, previous studies have indicated that conventional orthoses focusing solely on static arch support often fail to normalize dynamic COP progression and subtalar motion, leading to incomplete biomechanical correction [43, 44]. Together, these theoretical foundations and modern findings support our rationale that targeting the COP pathway offers a more physiological and potentially more effective corrective approach than conventional arch-support–based orthoses.

Building on this perspective, the present study introduced and evaluated a novel COP-based foot orthosis design for patients with pronated feet. The experimental results from both static and dynamic evaluation indexes all suggested that the present design has a significant effect on correcting foot function in pronation patients. The finite element simulation results show that the deep tissue strain of plantar is also significantly reduced by FO intervention. In addition, the increased plantar pressure of diabetic patients, especially those with diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), was indicated largely due to the abnormal COP caused by neuromuscular pathological changes, which would increase the ulcer risk in diabetic patients [45, 46]. Given that deep soft tissue strain is acknowledged as a contributing factor to the development of diabetic foot complications, we believe that the present design conception can also be valuable for diabetic patients, while obvious plantar pressure reduction in diabetic patients was already noted in several experimental trials in our continuous research. In summary, this design conception provides an alternative foundation for the prescription of pronation correction and could also be extended to treat different foot function diseases in the future.

In the present study, 3 types of COP-based FOs were designed concerning the stiffness differences of the two sides of the standard COP line. We aimed to correlate 3 FO types with different pronation levels and reach a custom design: FO1 with a maximum stiffness difference corresponded to high pronation, FO2 corresponded to moderate pronation, and FO3 with a minimum stiffness difference corresponded to slight pronation. However, a nonsignificant correlation was noted between the correction levels of different pronation groups and 3 FO types. Only the results of the subjective assessment questionnaire showed that the FO3 showed higher comfort scores from the slight pronation patient group, while the FO1 prompted higher comfort scores from the moderate and high pronation groups. This may be a limitation of the present study due to the small sample size of the recruited patients. On the other hand, although many previous studies discussed that custom design is important for patients [12, 20], our recent study indicated that custom designs of FOs or insoles concerning foot shapes can be unnecessary as large forces of the whole human body were applied to them [40].

Compared with conventional orthoses that primarily aim to support the medial arch and redistribute plantar pressure, the COP-based design tested in this study provides a distinct dynamic correction mechanism by directly guiding the COP pathway toward a physiological mediolateral trajectory. Previous studies have demonstrated that excessive medial COP deviation is associated with increased tibial internal rotation, altered subtalar joint kinematics, and elevated plantar tissue stress—all of which contribute to functional inefficiency and discomfort in pronated feet [25, 43]. By restoring a balanced COP progression from heel strike to toe-off, the present design theoretically improves energy transfer efficiency, enhances hindfoot alignment, and reduces medial overloading, thereby addressing functional deficiencies that static arch support alone cannot correct. Although this study did not include a direct comparison with commercially available prefabricated or custom-made devices, the observed improvements in hindfoot alignment, navicular drop, COP excursion, and plantar tissue strain suggest that COP-based orthoses may offer additional corrective benefits. Several potential advantages can be highlighted. First, the design reduces reliance on labor-intensive and costly customization due to the complex design and manufacture of patients’ individual characteristics and large body weights, yet remains adaptable to different severity of flat feet. Second, its relatively simple structural concept facilitates mass production and broader clinical accessibility. Third, the fish-bone supporting layer with cut-out spurs on both sides provides graded stiffness and localized cushioning effects, improving comfort and reducing medial–lateral pressure imbalance compared with the uniformly rigid support of traditional custom-made orthoses. Finally, subjective assessments indicated that the devices were generally well accepted in terms of comfort across different pronation levels. Taken together, these features suggest that the COP-based design could represent a cost-effective and generalizable alternative to conventional custom-made orthoses, while future direct comparative studies are warranted to confirm its relative clinical value.

A limitation of this study is that only male participants were recruited. While this approach reduced sex-related variability in this initial evaluation, it limits the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the absence of a direct comparative experiment with commercially available or custom-made orthoses represents another limitation. Nevertheless, the present findings—demonstrating improvements in hindfoot alignment, COP excursion, and plantar tissue strain—provide preliminary evidence supporting the potential clinical value of the proposed COP-based design. Future research will expand on these results by including female participants, conducting direct comparisons with conventional orthotic devices, and implementing larger-scale clinical trials to further validate and optimize the design’s corrective efficacy and practical applicability.

Conclusion

A COP-based design of bionic fishbone foot orthoses was proposed for the rehabilitation of pronation patients. Both static standing and dynamic gait evaluation indexes in the experiments indicated significant effects of the present design on the correction of abnormal foot functions, which included calcaneus eversion angle, navicular drop height, peak plantar pressure, gait COP curve and plantar tissue strain. The simulation results also indicated that the foot orthoses can significantly reduce the deep soft tissue strain levels in the gait cycle. Based on the experimental and simulation results, it can be also concluded that the present design can be used or extended for injury prevention or rehabilitation of other foot function-related diseases, such as diabetes or foot ligament laxity.
