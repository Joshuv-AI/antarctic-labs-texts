Effects of a generative AI-assisted ADPIE Framework on critical thinking, learning self-efficacy, and clinical reasoning in nursing education: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Muafiqoh Dwiarini, Gwo-Jen Hwang, Ching-Yi Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe nursing process is a systematic, patient-centered framework essential for clinical reasoning and decision-making in nursing education. It is commonly operationalized through the ADPIE (Assessment, Diagnosis, Planning, Implementation, and Evaluation) model, which guides nurses in identifying patient needs, developing care plans, implementing interventions, and evaluating outcomes in a structured and iterative manner. Although the nursing process is fundamental to nursing education and practice, some students may experience difficulties in applying ADPIE effectively, particularly the aspects of clinical reasoning and reflective practice.MethodsThis was a quasi-experimental study conducted to compare the effects of a Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI)-assisted nursing process approach with traditional instruction on nursing students’ evidence-based critical thinking, learning self-efficacy, and self-reported clinical reasoning. Moreover, the study integrated the intervention into a second-year nursing course (i.e., nursing process via obstetric nursing internship) as part of formal curriculum activities.ResultsStudents who participated in the GenAI-assisted nursing process approach demonstrated higher levels of evidence-based critical thinking, improved learning self-efficacy, and enhanced self-reported clinical reasoning ability compared with those in the traditional instruction group. The GenAI system provided adaptive feedback, real-time scaffolding, and iterative case refinement beyond traditional case-based learning.ConclusionsIntegrating GenAI into the ADPIE framework within nursing education may enhance students’ critical thinking, learning self-efficacy, and self-reported clinical reasoning by supporting structured and reflective learning processes, offering a promising pedagogical approach for nursing education.

Introduction

The Assessment, Diagnosis, Planning, Implementation, and Evaluation (ADPIE) framework is fundamental to nursing education, as it provides a structured approach to delivering safe and effective patient care, and underpins the development of nursing care competencies. This cyclic and dynamic process guides nurses in identifying patient needs, planning appropriate interventions, implementing care, and evaluating outcomes to promote health and well-being. However, some students may experience difficulties when applying ADPIE in practice, particularly when it comes to interpreting patients’ admission health problems and making clinical judgments. These challenges are further compounded by limited clinical experience and insufficient opportunities for guided reflection, making it difficult for learners to fully engage in the iterative and reflective processes required for effective clinical judgment.

However, scholars have indicated that students often encounter several learning challenges during the traditional nursing process [1]. These include high cognitive load, limited ability to integrate knowledge across multiple databases, and unfamiliarity with database navigation and interface operations [2]. Moreover, many students struggle to determine whether the medical or patient-related evidence they retrieve is sufficiently relevant and reliable to support clinical decision-making [3]. The challenges could be due to the fact that learners generally do not have sufficient time to find evidence-based references to continuously refine their understanding of the nursing process for patient care decisions [4]; consequently, they may lack a comprehensive grasp of the key aspects of clinical problems, and demonstrate insufficient proficiency in patient history taking and physical assessment [5]. This implies the need to provide instant guidance or support to individual students during the learning process.

Numerous researchers have pointed out the potential of employing technology-enhanced nursing process learning strategies by referring to the social learning theory, positing that knowledge construction and meaning-making occur through learners’ experiential interactions within social and contextualized learning environments [6]. Following this theory, various educational software programs have been developed to improve learners’ efficiency in practicing patient care [7], performing accurate nursing diagnoses [8], and identifying patient conditions [9]. Several previous studies have further reported the important role of critical thinking in fostering nursing knowledge [10]. To engage learners in critical thinking, the ADPIE process functions as a structured reasoning pathway that guides learners from data interpretation to decision justification, thereby operationalizing clinical reasoning in a stepwise and cognitively transparent manner [11–13], expanding their medical knowledge [14], and strengthening their critical thinking [15] as well as self-efficacy [16], communication, and interpersonal collaboration within patient care contexts [17]. Moreover, the use of computer technologies could be a potential means to accomplish the aim of ADPIE [18, 19]. That is, using computer-based applications throughout the ADPIE nursing process to provide personalized feedback and guidance could be the key to improving nursing learning outcomes [20].

Among various computer technologies, Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) has been widely recognized as an effective application for providing personalized learning supports with the potential to improve learners’ learning outcomes [21, 22]. Compared with conventional virtual simulation or digital case-based learning environments, which often rely on pre-scripted scenarios and limited feedback loops, GenAI-enabled systems provide real-time, dialogic learning support to individual learners. Traditional virtual simulations typically constrain learners to fixed pathways, restricting opportunities for iterative reasoning and personalized feedback. In contrast, GenAI dynamically generates responses, scaffolds reasoning processes, and allows learners to engage in continuous refinement of their clinical judgments, thereby enhancing both cognitive flexibility and depth of learning. Nevertheless, there remains a scarcity of research applying this approach within the nursing discipline, particularly research that integrates the ADPIE nursing process into evidence-based learning activities.

To address this gap, the present study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a GenAI-assisted nursing process (GAANS) approach integrated into case-based ADPIE training. This approach incorporates an application and online learning system to foster deeper cognitive engagement and promote flexible, self-regulated learning within authentic clinical contexts. Specifically, this study examined its effects on nursing students’ critical thinking, learning self-efficacy, and clinical reasoning. Accordingly, the study was guided by the following three research questions:

Literature review

Nursing process education

The nursing process is a systematic, evidence-based, and patient-centered framework used by nurses to deliver individualized care. According to scholars, the nursing process involves five interrelated steps: Assessment, Diagnosis, Planning, Implementation, and Evaluation (ADPIE) [23, 24]. Nursing process training is fundamental to high-quality nursing education and clinical practice. It is not merely a procedural requirement but rather the foundation of professional nursing judgment and patient-centered care [25]. Integrating this training into clinical education helps ensure that future nurses become safe, skilled, compassionate, and critically reflective practitioners [26]. Furthermore, the nursing process serves as a problem-solving approach that enables nurses to organize and prioritize patient care processes. It encompasses data collection, clinical judgment, goal setting, intervention, and continuous evaluation, ensuring safe, efficient, and holistic care [26, 27].

According to previous studies, teaching professional students the nursing process is crucial because it promotes students’ critical thinking and clinical reasoning [10, 13]. Lee et al. defined clinical reasoning as the cognitive processes underlying professional judgment, evaluating evidence quality, solving problems, and formulating diagnostic and management decisions [28]. In real clinical settings, nurses need to employ patient-centered mental models that enable efficient cognitive shortcuts, drawing on established schemas, prior experiences, and intuitive insights to reach conclusions [29]. Thus, nursing process training also ensures that students can deliver individualized, patient-centered care, equipping them to meet patients’ physical, psychological, and social needs [24]. Hence, the nursing process encourages students to think systematically. Moreover, from a theoretical perspective, the nursing process can be conceptualized as a structured cognitive scaffold that supports learners’ clinical reasoning development through iterative cycles of information processing, decision-making, and reflective evaluation. This aligns with constructivist learning theory, which emphasizes that knowledge is actively constructed through experience and reflection rather than being passively received.

Agazzi et al. stated that the nursing process teaches students to assess, diagnose, plan, implement, and evaluate care while developing soft skills to analyze patient data, prioritize care needs, and make evidence-informed decisions [30]. It emphasizes holistic assessment. Löfgren et al. proposed that it fosters students’ empathy and cultural sensitivity [31]. Lee and Son found that training students in the nursing process enhances communication and documentation skills. Standardized documentation (e.g., SOAP notes) improves interdisciplinary collaboration and continuity of care [32]. Zhang noted that the reflective, cyclical nature of the nursing process (especially the evaluation phase) facilitates students’ lifelong learning and adaptability [33]. It helps students learn from practice, and fosters adaptability in responding to complex or changing patient conditions. Lee and Son further emphasized that it strengthens students’ professional identity and accountability by encouraging autonomy and responsibility in nursing practice [32]. It reinforces the nurse’s role as a clinical decision-maker rather than as a passive assistant to physicians. Furthermore, Oreofe et al. highlighted that the nursing process provides students with a standardized framework for care delivery. It helps reduce variability in care by guiding students through a step-by-step approach to problem solving and decision-making [34]. Nemati-Vakilabad et al. also found that thorough education in the assessment and evaluation steps reduces the likelihood of errors or omissions [35]. Regular evaluation allows for early detection of complications and timely interventions.

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) in nursing education

GenAI has recently emerged as a transformative tool in health profession education, offering new opportunities to enhance learning and clinical reasoning [36, 37]. GenAI can provide real-time feedback, generate patient scenarios, and support learners in synthesizing complex clinical information, thereby facilitating deeper cognitive engagement and active learning [38]. In nursing education, these capabilities are particularly relevant to the application of the ADPIE framework, which requires iterative reasoning, data interpretation, and decision-making. By scaffolding each stage of the nursing process, GenAI may help students organize patient information, formulate accurate nursing diagnoses, and evaluate intervention outcomes more systematically [39].

Moreover, GenAI has been shown to promote self-regulated learning by enabling learners to interact with adaptive content and receive immediate, personalized guidance [40]. This may enhance learners’ confidence and learning self-efficacy while encouraging reflective thinking and critical analysis. Such features are closely aligned with the development of nursing process competencies and clinical reasoning, which are essential for safe and effective patient care. Therefore, integrating GenAI into ADPIE-based instruction holds promise for addressing common learning challenges and strengthening students’ ability to apply the nursing process in clinical contexts.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a quasi-experimental design conducted within a second-year undergraduate nursing program at a university in Taiwan. The intervention was implemented in a required course, “nursing process via obstetric nursing internship,” which integrates classroom instruction with clinical practica in obstetric care settings. The course was designed to develop students’ competencies in applying the nursing process to real-world maternal and neonatal care scenarios.

The quasi-experimental design aimed to examine the effectiveness of the GAANS approach within a formal course-based instructional context, ensuring that the intervention was fully integrated into routine teaching practices rather than delivered as an isolated experimental activity. The course spanned one academic semester, combining lectures, case-based discussions, and supervised clinical practice in affiliated hospitals. Participants were assigned according to existing intact class cohorts rather than through randomization, matching, or stratification procedures. Two intact cohorts of second-year nursing students were assigned as the experimental group (n = 21), which received GAANS-based instruction, and the control group (n = 21), which received conventional instruction. Baseline demographic characteristics and pretest scores were compared between groups prior to the intervention, and no statistically significant differences were identified. In addition, both groups were taught during the same internship period and received comparable clinical exposure, learning environments, instructional content, course materials, and learning objectives. The same course instructor and clinical teaching framework were used for both groups so as to reduce instructional variability. However, because random assignment was not feasible within the educational setting, the possibility of selection bias and potential instructor or cohort effects cannot be completely excluded.

Participants

This study used a convenience sample of students enrolled in the course during the study semester. Inclusion criteria included enrollment in the nursing course during the study period, willingness to participate in the study, and completion of both pretest and posttest assessments. In addition, participants had no prior formal training or structured learning experience using the GAANS or other AI-supported nursing learning systems before the intervention. Of 52 eligible students, 42 participated and completed the study (80.8%). The remaining 10 students did not participate due to absence during data collection sessions, incomplete participation, or unwillingness to complete all study procedures. The final sample consisted of 42 students, with 21 in the experimental group and 21 in the control group.

Ethical considerations

This study received ethical approval from the university’s institutional review board. Participation was voluntary and unrelated to course evaluation, and recruitment was conducted by a research assistant independent of course instruction, who invited all eligible students to participate, and obtained informed consent prior to data collection. Students were assigned to groups based on their existing class cohorts, with one cohort designated as the experimental group and the other as the control group; no randomization, matching, or stratification was performed. Measures were taken to reduce potential power dynamics between instructors and students, including anonymous questionnaire responses and separation of teaching and research roles. Students who chose not to take part in the experimental procedures continued with the traditional learning method and were excluded from questionnaire administration.

Maternal scenario

Pregnancy-Induced Hypertension (PIH) was selected as the maternal scenario due to its high clinical relevance, complexity in decision-making, and alignment with core nursing competencies in obstetric care. PIH requires continuous assessment, timely intervention, and accurate clinical judgment, making it an appropriate context for fostering students’ critical thinking and nursing process competencies. Additionally, maternal care scenarios are commonly encountered in clinical training, enhancing the ecological validity and transferability of learning outcomes.

The class was provided with a maternal scenario activity case: Labor in a Woman with Pregnancy-Induced Hypertension (PIH), as follows: Mrs. Lin, a 32-year-old woman, gravida 2, para 0, abortus 1, at 37 weeks of gestation, was admitted to the labor and delivery unit for induction of labor due to pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH). Her prenatal care was consistent, and she had been taking Methyldopa (Aldomet) 250 mg three times daily to control blood pressure and Bokey EM 100 mg daily until 36 + 6 weeks to prevent preeclampsia. She lives in Zhonghe District with her husband, who accompanied her to the hospital. The patient is currently unemployed, while her husband works as an interior designer. Both are cooperative and supportive of each other throughout the admission process. The patient walked into the labor room with her husband’s assistance. She was alert, oriented, and calm but expressed mild anxiety about the induction process. On admission, continuous fetal heart rate monitoring was initiated and showed a reassuring pattern. Her vital signs were stable, and no abnormal findings were noted. Prenatal laboratory tests, including VDRL, HIV, HBsAg, HBeAg, and GBS, were all negative. She denied experiencing fever, chills, or any respiratory symptoms. Her previous mild cough had resolved. The patient had received two doses of BNT COVID-19 vaccine and two doses of Moderna, and reported past COVID-19 infections.

Intervention

This study developed a GAANS, which was integrated within the Edcafe AI platform [41], an e-learning platform that allows students to access and analyze ADPIE-based clinical cases flexibly. The platform utilized a GenAI model to provide interactive responses based on standardized ADPIE-guided prompts designed by the research team. To enhance consistency and educational relevance, participants used the same structured prompt framework during case analysis activities. In addition, AI-generated clinical content was reviewed and monitored by nursing instructors to ensure its accuracy, appropriateness, and alignment with nursing education objectives. Using a structured ADPIE search template, students organized and applied nursing process knowledge from case-based materials. The design of GAANS was informed by Bandura’s Social Learning Theory (SLT), together with constructivist and self-regulated learning principles, to support the development of clinical reasoning, as shown in Fig. 1. Specifically, the system facilitates observational learning, guided practice, and reflective feedback across the ADPIE phases (Assessment, Diagnosis, Planning, Implementation, and Evaluation). Within this framework, GenAI functions as a learning mediator by providing cognitive support, interactive feedback, and metacognitive prompts [42]. These features were expected to enhance students’ clinical reasoning, engagement, and self-regulation, corresponding directly to the study’s measured learning outcomes.

GenAI-assisted social learning theory model

The GAANS intervention was implemented over a 4-week period, with one 120-minute session per week, following a structured sequence aligned with the ADPIE framework and SLT. Each session corresponded to a specific learning phase: (1) observational learning, (2) modeling and imitation, (3) social reinforcement, and (4) self-reflection and evaluation. The instructional sequence was designed to progressively guide learners from knowledge acquisition to application and metacognitive reflection. All participants completed the phases in a fixed order to ensure consistency in learning exposure and to reduce instructional variability.

In the observational learning phase (Week 1, 120 min), students explored realistic nursing scenarios generated by Edcafe, where maternal virtual patient cases demonstrated assessment cues and evidence-based decision-making processes, as shown in Fig. 2. At this stage, the GenAI system functioned by generating structured patient cases, highlighting key clinical cues (e.g., vital signs abnormalities), and providing comparative examples of correct and incorrect nursing judgments. Each scenario simulated authentic patient data, such as vital signs, nursing documentation, and progress reports, to help learners recognize clinical problems and identify relevant nursing diagnoses. The GenAI system produced diverse patient narratives to allow comparison of correct and incorrect nursing responses, thereby fostering analytical thinking. From a theoretical standpoint, this phase operationalizes observational learning by enabling learners to acquire cognitive schemas through exposure to modeled expert reasoning and contrasting cases, which is critical for developing clinical judgment.

The maternal virtual patient system

In the modeling and imitation phase(Week 2, 120 min), as shown in Fig. 3, students practiced formulating nursing diagnoses, developing care plans, and implementing nursing interventions. During this phase, GenAI provided real-time prompts, exemplar responses, and step-by-step reasoning demonstrations based on student inputs. Guided prompts from GenAI illustrated how expert nurses prioritize care, set measurable goals, and deliver interventions. Students were encouraged to replicate these reasoning steps, supported by peer collaboration and teacher feedback. This phase aimed to strengthen students’ ability to translate theoretical knowledge into practical decision-making skills. Importantly, GenAI enhances this phase by dynamically generating context-sensitive prompts and exemplars, allowing learners to engage in adaptive imitation rather than static replication. This distinguishes the approach from traditional simulation, where modeling is often fixed and lacks real-time responsiveness.

Modeling and imitation phase example

During the social reinforcement phase (Week 3, 120 min), as shown in Fig. 4, students had discussions, and immediate feedback sessions were integrated to support the SLT mechanism of vicarious reinforcement within the interactive maternal virtual patient environment. GenAI operated as an interactive feedback agent, analyzing student responses and providing instant, individualized feedback on accuracy, completeness, and clinical reasoning logic. It also simulated peer-like dialogue to support social interaction and vicarious learning.

Fig. 4Social reinforcement example

Social reinforcement example

From a theoretical perspective, this feedback loop functions as both external reinforcement and internal cognitive regulation, enabling learners to recalibrate their reasoning processes through iterative dialogue with the AI system. The Maternal virtual patient created in the Edcafe AI system was used as an interactive facilitator, providing immediate feedback on the accuracy, clarity, and comprehensiveness of each student’s nursing process documentation (e.g., SOAP notes, including Subjective data, Objective data, Assessment, Plan). This iterative dialogue with the AI enhanced communication and promoted evidence-informed care planning.

In the self-reflection and evaluation phase (Week 4, 120 min), as shown in Fig. 5, students engaged in metacognitive review through AI-generated reflection prompts. At this stage, GenAI functioned as a metacognitive facilitator by generating reflective questions, identifying discrepancies in clinical reasoning, and suggesting improvement strategies based on students’ prior responses. These prompts guided them to evaluate the effectiveness of their nursing interventions, identify potential errors, and suggest improvements for future practice. The cyclic reflection process aligned with the evaluation stage of ADPIE, fostering continuous professional growth and lifelong learning. This phase also aligns with self-regulated learning theory, as students engage in goal setting, self-monitoring, and reflective evaluation. The AI-generated prompts act as metacognitive scaffolds, supporting learners in identifying discrepancies between expected and actual outcomes, thereby fostering deeper clinical insight and adaptive expertise.

Self-reflection and evaluation phase example

To support multimodal engagement, students used notebooklm to construct concept maps summarizing their nursing process reasoning. They integrated AI-generated content and self-created notes into structured visual diagrams, exported as PDF or PNG files for submission and peer review from the interactive results of the maternal virtual patient cases, as shown in Fig. 6. In addition to GenAI-generated feedback, the intervention also incorporated concept mapping, peer collaboration, reflective activities, and e-learning interactions to support students’ learning processes. This process enabled students to visualize relationships among assessment data, nursing diagnoses, interventions, and outcomes, reinforcing cognitive organization through both individual and social learning. This visualization process is grounded in cognitive theory, where concept mapping supports knowledge organization and schema construction. By externalizing internal reasoning processes, learners can more effectively integrate fragmented clinical information into coherent mental models.

Experimental procedure

During the initial phase, both groups received instruction on the ADPIE framework and training in evidence-searching skills using the GAANS academic database over a 5-week period. Subsequently, participants completed pretests measuring critical thinking, learning self-efficacy, and clinical reasoning, each lasting approximately 30 min.

In the second phase, the control group followed a conventional instructional approach, which involved using the e-learning system to locate nursing process materials and review related e-books. In contrast, the experimental group engaged with the GAANS system, where each student independently searched for evidence-based nursing resources through the university library database, and collaboratively evaluated ADPIE-related articles on the e-learning platform via their mobile devices (Fig. 3).

This process enabled students to access, analyze, and critique ADPIE articles aligned with the American Nurses Association standards [27]. Furthermore, the Nursing Process Evaluation Form was employed to assess students’ performance across five key dimensions of the nursing process, adapted from the Taiwan Nurses Association case report rubric, which also served as a self-assessment reference via the Nursing Process Evaluation Form for learners (Table 1).

To minimize a potential Hawthorne effect, students assigned to the control group were invited to participate in the GAANS-based learning activity after completion of the instructional sessions. At the conclusion of the intervention, participants completed posttests assessing their critical thinking, learning self-efficacy, and clinical reasoning, each with a duration of approximately 30 min.

Measuring tools

Based on the purpose of the study, three measuring instruments were used in the present study. All instruments underwent linguistic translation and cultural adaptation to ensure suitability for the study context, including forward–backward translation procedures and expert review to establish content validity. The questionnaire for critical thinking ability was designed on the basis of the questionnaire proposed by Chang et al. and included six items with a 5-point scale, with a Cronbach’s α value of 0.85 [43]. In the current study, the internal consistency reliability of this scale was re-examined, yielding a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.87, indicating satisfactory reliability for the present sample. In addition, content validity was reviewed by three experts in nursing education and educational technology to ensure the appropriateness of the items for the study context. Minor wording adjustments were made to enhance clarity and contextual relevance. The questionnaire for learning self-efficacy was designed on the basis of the questionnaire proposed by Li et al. and included six items with a 5-point scale, with a Cronbach’s α value of 0.83 [44]. For the current sample, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.86, demonstrating good internal consistency. To ensure contextual suitability, the instrument was slightly adapted in wording to align with the GenAI-assisted learning environment, and its face validity was confirmed through expert review.

Clinical reasoning was measured using the Nurses’ Clinical Reasoning Scale (NCRS), a self-report questionnaire originally developed by Liou et al. [45]; its validity was established for use with undergraduate nursing students. The instrument contains 15 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale self-report questionnaire (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), where higher scores reflect stronger clinical reasoning ability. In the original validation study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for internal consistency was reported as 0.94. In this study, the NCRS demonstrated excellent reliability, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.92 for the current sample. Prior to administration, the instrument underwent a contextual adaptation process, including expert panel review and minor linguistic refinement to ensure alignment with the learning activities and clinical scenarios used in this study. The construct validity of the scale has been supported in previous studies, and its applicability to the present sample was deemed appropriate.

Results

Critical thinking

An analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted to examine differences in the critical thinking scores of the two groups. In this analysis, the pretest scores served as the covariate, while the posttest scores were treated as the dependent variable. The assumption of homogeneity of regression slopes was satisfied, as indicated by F(1, 40) = 0.83, p = .369 (> 0.05). The ANCOVA results are presented in Table 2. Findings revealed that students in the experimental group using the GAANS approach demonstrated higher critical thinking than those in the control group, F(1, 39) = 11.48, p < .01, with a partial η² of 0.227, indicating a moderate effect size.

Discussion

This study introduced an AI-supported nursing process learning approach aimed at enhancing students’ performance in applying the nursing process. Results from the ANCOVA analysis indicated that participants in the GAANS group achieved higher levels of critical thinking, learning self-efficacy, and self-reported clinical reasoning compared to those who received traditional instruction during the nursing process activity. These findings suggest that the integration of GenAI into nursing education may provide meaningful support for developing higher-order cognitive skills and improving learning outcomes. The observed improvements may not be attributable solely to the GenAI component, as the intervention incorporated multiple instructional strategies, including virtual patient simulation, concept mapping, peer discussion, reflective learning, and e-learning activities. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting the combined effect of an integrated instructional design rather than the isolated impact of GenAI alone.

The findings of this study further highlight the potential advantages of GenAI-assisted learning over traditional virtual simulation approaches. While conventional simulations provide structured experiential learning, they are generally unable to provide guidance or support based on individual learners’ learning status or needs. In contrast, the integration of GenAI within the ADPIE framework enables real-time interaction, supports iterative reasoning, and enhances reflective thinking processes.

These results are consistent with the findings of Xu et al., who reported that incorporating the GAANS framework into AI-based learning activities can foster improvements in students’ learning performance and higher-order thinking skills [46]. It can be inferred that the use of AI-supported tasks provides opportunities for reflective learning, enabling students to engage in metacognitive processes that promote the construction of nursing process knowledge and competencies through ADPIE-centered activities. Similarly, Gupta found that AI-assisted strategies serve as effective tools for facilitating individualized learning and the development of professional skills [47]. By reviewing and evaluating their peers’ work, students also gain insights into alternative thought processes and reasoning strategies. In line with this, Ali and Shaban highlighted that AI applications can enhance nursing students’ engagement in clinical practice, deepening their reflective thinking and professional communication [48]. Future studies using controlled designs are needed to isolate the specific contribution of GenAI within multifaceted nursing education interventions. The relatively high posttest scores should be interpreted cautiously, as ceiling effects or response tendencies associated with self-report measures may have influenced the observed outcomes. Nonetheless, integrating AI into clinical education remains challenging, as ethical considerations, such as data privacy, academic integrity, and the responsible use of AI in clinical decision-making must be carefully addressed alongside contextual factors. In addition, potential risks associated with GenAI, including overreliance on AI-generated reasoning, inaccuracies or hallucinations in generated responses, and the possible influence on students’ independent clinical judgment, should also be critically considered when integrating AI into nursing education.

Limitations

Although the nursing process is a fundamental component of nursing education, this study explored whether GenAI could serve as a supportive learning tool to facilitate students’ application of ADPIE and enhance clinical reasoning during the learning process. In addition, this study demonstrated the effectiveness of a GenAI-assisted nursing process approach for assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation, and evaluation (ADPIE) in terms of enhancing nursing students’ critical thinking, learning self-efficacy, and clinical reasoning. However, a potential instructor effect and lack of randomization may have influenced the study’s internal validity. Several limitations should thus be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size reduces statistical power and may increase the likelihood of overestimating effect sizes, thereby limiting the precision and stability of the findings. In particular, small samples are more susceptible to sampling variability, which may result in inflated or unstable effect size estimates and reduce the reproducibility of the findings across different populations. Therefore, the magnitude of the reported effects should be interpreted with caution, as they may not fully reflect the true population-level impact of the intervention.

Second, the relatively high posttest scores observed across groups may indicate potential ceiling effects, which could limit the sensitivity of the instruments to detect further improvements. The reliance on self-reported measures may also have introduced social desirability bias, as participants may have tended to provide favorable responses regarding their learning experiences and perceived competencies. No objective performance-based assessments, such as Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs), blinded evaluation of nursing care plans, or external clinical performance assessments, were included. Therefore, the reported improvements may reflect students’ perceived competence rather than objectively verified clinical reasoning. Additionally, while effect sizes provide useful information regarding the magnitude of observed differences, they should not be interpreted in isolation. In small-scale educational studies, effect sizes may be influenced by contextual factors such as instructional design, participant characteristics, and measurement sensitivity. As such, large effect sizes do not necessarily equate to strong practical or clinical significance, and should be considered alongside study design limitations and external validity concerns. Furthermore, confidence intervals for effect size estimates were not reported, which limits the ability to assess the precision and uncertainty of these estimates. Furthermore, because the intervention integrated several educational components simultaneously, it was difficult to isolate the independent effect of the GenAI component on learning outcomes. Therefore, the study’s findings should be interpreted cautiously. Additionally, limited transparency regarding the architecture, prompting strategies, and operational mechanisms of the GenAI system may affect the reproducibility and interpretability of the intervention. Future studies should provide more detailed descriptions of the GenAI system and its implementation process to strengthen the methodological transparency and scientific rigor. Future research is encouraged to report effect size confidence intervals to enhance transparency and interpretability. Future investigations could integrate qualitative methods, such as semi-structured interviews or focus group discussions, to capture richer insights into students’ perceptions and experiences with this GenAI-assisted nursing process approach.

Conclusion

This study highlights the potential of the GAANS system to support nursing students’ learning through structured engagement in observation, reflection, and iterative learning. The findings suggest that integrating GenAI into nursing education may enhance students’ critical thinking, learning self-efficacy, and self-reported clinical reasoning within online learning environments. As suggested by Kamlah et al., educators and practitioners are encouraged to recognize the pedagogical advantages of GAANS and to integrate it with other instructional strategies in nursing education [49]. Their findings not only demonstrated the system’s potential to enhance learners’ performance, but also advocated for comparing different AI-assisted approaches by employing GAANS in both experimental and control groups in future investigations. Future research should further examine the application of AI-assisted tools in clinical education, and explore their integration with established pedagogical frameworks to optimize learning outcomes.
