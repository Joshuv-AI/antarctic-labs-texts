Guideline-directed medical therapy and in-hospital mortality in acute coronary syndrome patients with advanced renal dysfunction: analysis of two nationwide retrospective cohort studies
Authors: Zhengyang Jin, Geru Aa, Yifan Guo, Hangkuan Liu, Haonan Sun, Zhi Li, Min Ma, Gregg Fonarow, Yuxia Gao, Dong Zhao, Qing Yang, Xin Zhou, CCC-AF investigators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to verify whether guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT, ie, the combined use of β-blocker, ACE inhibitor/angiotensin receptor blocker, dual antiplatelet drugs and statin) could improve in-hospital mortality in acute coronary syndrome (ACS) patients with advanced renal dysfunction (estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2).DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingThis study used data from two large, multicentre, observational cohorts: the Chinese National Electronic Disease Surveillance System Platform (CNEDSSP) and the Improving Care for Cardiovascular Disease in China-ACS (CCC-ACS) project. These cohorts consist of ACS patients admitted to 47 and 241 hospitals, respectively, across China from 2014 to 2019.ParticipantsA total of 6260 patients diagnosed with advanced renal dysfunction (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m²) on admission were included in the analysis. Among these, 3013 patients were from the CNEDSSP cohort and 3247 from the CCC-ACS cohort. Patients were categorised based on receipt of GDMT within 24 hours of admission.Main exposure measureThe main exposure measure was the early use of GDMT, defined as the administration of all four components of GDMT within 24 hours of hospital admission.Main outcome measureThe primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, assessed within the study cohort.ResultsIn a pooled analysis of the CNEDSSP and CCC-ACS cohorts, early GDMT was associated with a significant reduction in in-hospital mortality (relative risk (RR): 0.62, 95% CI: 0.47 to 0.81). More pronounced reductions were observed in myocardial infarction (MI) patients, with HRs of 0.31 (95% CI: 0.17 to 0.55) in the CNEDSSP cohort and 0.47 (95% CI: 0.28 to 0.77) in the CCC-ACS cohort. A random-effects pooled analysis of MI patients with advanced renal dysfunction at admission showed a 61% reduction in in-hospital mortality among GDMT users (RR: 0.39, 95% CI: 0.27 to 0.58).ConclusionsThis nationwide study highlights that early GDMT is associated with a reduced risk of in-hospital mortality in ACS patients (particularly in MI patients) with advanced renal dysfunction.Keywords: Coronary heart disease, Kidney & urinary tract disorders, Myocardial infarction, Protocols & guidelines

Introduction

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is a leading cause of mortality worldwide and poses a significant health and economic burden, with an in-hospital mortality rate of approximately 5%.1 2 European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommend the use of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) including β-blockers, statins, ACE inhibitor/angiotensin receptor blocker (ACEI/ARB) and dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) in patients with ACS.3 4 But for advanced renal dysfunction (estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)<30 mL/min/1.73 m2), the early application of GDMT has always been controversial. Some studies showed that statin,5,8 ACEI/ARB and9 DAPT10 11 might reduce cardiovascular benefit in patients with advanced renal dysfunction, but some other studies have different conclusions.12,16

Overall, the proportion of GDMT application in ACS patients with advanced renal dysfunction is insufficient due to inconsistent results of clinical trials.17,20 Some studies showed that insufficient use of GDMT may be related to poorer short-term and long-term prognosis in ACS patients with advanced renal dysfunction.17,19 Lower prescriptions for evidence-based medications in advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) reportedly relate to concerns about drug toxicity, deterioration in renal function and bleeding. Based on the current situation, there is a lack of randomised controlled trials in the application of β-blockers, DAPT, statins and ACEI/ARB in ACS patients with advanced renal dysfunction. Although some expert clinicians and researchers advocate for an individualised treatment regimen with careful assessment of risks and benefits for advanced renal dysfunction,21 real-world evidence is still urgently needed to guide clinical practice.

In the present study, we therefore used the Chinese National Electronic Disease Surveillance System Platform (CNEDSSP) database and the Improving Care for Cardiovascular Disease in China-ACS (CCC-ACS) project to explore the association between the use of GDMT and in-hospital mortality in ACS patients with advanced renal dysfunction.

Methods

Data sources and study design

The present analysis was a retrospective, multicentre, observational study based on the CNEDSSP database and CCC-ACS project.

The CNEDSSP was supported by the National Science and Technology Support Program (approval number 2005BA114A01) and was initiated by the Statistical Information Center of the National Health Commission of China in 2005. It aims to improve medical quality management by constructing an integrated platform of electronic health record (EHR) from nationwide, representative hospitals in China. According to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision diagnostic codes, 184 850 ACS patients hospitalised in 47 hospitals in China from July 2014 to December 2018 were included. Information on demographics, clinical variables, procedures and medications was collected for this study. The data cleaning and management service was provided by Beijing 1 M Data Technology Corporation. This study protocol was approved by the medical ethics committee of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (approval number: IRB2022-YX-045–01) and exempted from informed consent. This study used the same two national cohorts—CNEDSSP and CCC-ACS—as those reported in our previously published study.15 The study design and baseline variable definitions are similar to those in the prior work.

The CCC-ACS project is an ongoing nationwide registry jointly initiated by the American Heart Association and the Chinese Society of Cardiology from 2014, aiming to improve the quality of care for ACS patients in China, which included 104 516 ACS patients enrolled in 241 hospitals from 1 November 2014 to 31 December 2019 in China. Details regarding the study design and methodology have been published previously.22 The CCC-ACS project was approved by the institutional review board of Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University, with a waiver for informed consent. This study is registered at URL: https://clinicaltrial.gov (unique ID: NCT02306616).

In this study, advanced renal dysfunction was defined as an eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 on the first laboratory test after admission, which was calculated according to the equation by the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration.23 GDMT users were defined as patients who received all of β-blockers, DAPT, statins, ACEI/ARB treatment within 24 hours of admission. Non-GDMT users were defined as patients who did not receive all treatment of β-blockers, DAPT, statins, ACEI/ARB within 24 hours of admission. For further analysis, we defined patients who received dual-combination therapy as those receiving any two GDMT components within 24 hours after admission, and patients who received three-drug therapy as those receiving any three GDMT components within the same time frame.

Study outcome and covariates

The study outcome was in-hospital mortality. The following variables were treated as covariates for multivariable adjustment and propensity score matching (PSM): (1) demographics: age and sex; (2) previous history: myocardial infarction (MI), percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), coronary bypass grafting (CABG), diabetes, dyslipidaemia, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, heart failure (HF), ischaemic stroke, haemorrhagic stroke, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and peripheral vascular disease; (3) clinical characteristics on admission: systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate and Killip class, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, eGFR and myocardial enzymes (creatine kinase-myocardial band (CK-MB) for CCC-ACS cohort and the proportion of increased myocardial enzymes for CNEDSSP cohort (as an EHR-based study, due to the existence of missing value of a certain cardiac enzymes (troponin I, T or CK-MB), we used the proportion of higher than the upper reference limit of a certain maker)); (4) PCI or not on the index admission and (5) ACS subtypes: ST-elevation MI (STEMI), non-STEMI (NSTEMI) and unstable angina (UA); (6) use of GDMT medications within 2 weeks prior to admission, fasting blood glucose, haemoglobin levels and in-hospital intravenous anticoagulant therapy.

Statistical analysis

We used PSM of a ratio of 1:1 to balance the differences in baseline characteristics between GDMT users and patients not receiving early GDMT using a calliper of 0.005 of the propensity (Stata command ‘calipmatch’) for CNEDSSP cohort and CCC-ACS cohort, respectively. Continuous data with normal distribution are presented as means and SD. Non-parametric continuous data are presented as medians with the 25th–75th percentiles and categorical data are presented as number and percentage. The absolute standardised difference (ASD) was used to assess the differences between groups, with an ASD of <10% considered ideal to indicate balanced groups.24 The Cox regression model will be used to assess the association between the use of GDMT and in-hospital mortality in the matched groups. Because the CCC-ACS cohort and the CNEDSSP cohort were two independent cohorts, we performed pooled analysis based on the random effect model to combine the results derived from two matched cohorts to verify our main results. We also tested the effect of non-GDMT on in-hospital mortality by analysing the combination of two (β-blocker and statin, β-blocker and DAPT, β-blocker and ACEI/ARB, statin and DAPT, statin and ACEI/ARB, DAPT and ACEI/ARB) or three drugs (non-β-blocker, non-statin, non-DAPT, non-ACEI/ARB) from GDMT. Additionally, we performed the following sensitivity analyses based on the matched cohort: (1) excluded patients who died within 24 hours after admission; (2) excluded patients with serious conditions on admission (cardiogenic shock, HF or cardiac arrest); (3) excluded patients with a previous history of PCI or CABG and (4) E-value to assess the robustness of the associations to unmeasured/uncontrolled confounders.25 Finally, we performed the following subgroup analyses based on matched cohort, including age (<75 years or ≥75 years), sex, ACS subtypes (MI or UA during the index admission), medical history (MI, diabetes and HF) and PCI or not during the index admission. Stata V.15.1 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) was used for the analysis, and a two-tailed p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics between CNEDSSP and CCC-ACS cohorts

There were 184 850 and 104 516 ACS patients enrolled in the CNEDSSP cohort and CCC-ACS cohort, respectively. Finally, 6260 patients diagnosed with advanced renal dysfunction on admission (3013 patients in the CNEDSSP cohort and 3247 patients in the CCC-ACS cohort) were included for analysis (figure 1). There were 1239 (19.8%) patients who received early GDMT and 5021 (80.2%) patients without GDMT. During the hospital stay, a total of 542 in-hospital deaths (8.7%) were recorded in the two cohorts, including 62 patients receiving early GDMT (5.0%) and 480 patients not receiving early GDMT (9.6%). As shown in table 1, the average age of the two total cohorts was 72.7 years, with 55.2% being men. Compared with patients on non-early GDMT, patients on early GDMT were more likely to have a high level of triglycerides, blood pressure and eGFR. Moreover, patients on early GDMT were more likely to have a history of MI and PCI and less likely to have a previous history of HF, AF and stroke. Patients with a diagnosis of STEMI, NSTEMI and UA accounted for 21.1%, 24.0% and 54.8% in the CNEDSSP cohort. Accordingly, 47.7%, 38.4% and 13.9% corresponding patients in the CCC-ACS cohort (online supplemental figures S1A). In addition, patients in the CNEDSSP cohort had a higher proportion of medical history of MI or HF and had a higher Killip class compared with the patients in the CCC-ACS cohort (online supplemental figures S1B and S1C).

Association between GDMT early use and in-hospital mortality

In multivariable analyses, GDMT users exhibited significantly lower in-hospital mortality compared with non-GDMT users in both the CNEDSSP cohort (HR=0.68, 95% CI: 0.49 to 0.96) and the CCC-ACS cohort (HR=0.51, 95% CI: 0.32 to 0.82). Consistently, a pooled analysis of the two cohorts demonstrated a significant reduction in mortality among GDMT users (relative risk (RR)=0.62, 95% CI: 0.47 to 0.81; figure 2). Furthermore, Kaplan-Meier survival curves (figure 3) showed a clear separation between GDMT users and non-GDMT users, with higher in-hospital survival observed in the GDMT group across both cohorts.

Further analysis of GDMT components revealed that patients receiving β-blockers, DAPT and statins, but not ACEI/ARB, had significantly lower in-hospital mortality. The analysis showed that the absence of ACEI/ARB therapy, alongside the use of β-blockers, DAPT and statins, was linked to significantly lower in-hospital mortality compared with patients who did not receive all of these medications (HR: 0.55, 95% CI: 0.44 to 0.68). Similarly, the absence of DAPT (HR: 0.59, 95% CI: 0.45 to 0.77) or statins (HR: 0.58, 95% CI: 0.44 to 0.78) was also associated with a reduced risk of in-hospital mortality. In contrast, the combination of β-blockers and ACEI/ARB significantly improved in-hospital mortality (HR: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.40 to 0.67), while the combination of statins and DAPT showed no mortality benefit in ACS patients with advanced renal dysfunction (HR: 1.10, 95% CI: 0.89 to 1.35; online supplemental figure S2).

Similar findings were confirmed using PSM. The matched CNEDSSP cohort included 1220 patients, and the matched CCC-ACS cohort included 1210 patients, with well-balanced baseline characteristics (online supplemental table S1 and figure S3). In the matched cohorts, a total of 97 in-hospital deaths (8.0%) were recorded in the CNEDSSP cohort and 55 (4.5%) in the CCC-ACS cohort. The Kaplan-Meier curves for GDMT and non-GDMT users in the matched cohorts showed significant heterogeneity between the two cohorts due to differing proportions of MI (online supplemental figure S4). Pooled analysis showed a trend towards reduced in-hospital mortality with GDMT use in ACS patients with advanced renal dysfunction, consistent with findings from the overall cohorts (RR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.45 to 0.86; online supplemental figure S5). Among MI patients with advanced renal dysfunction, those receiving early GDMT were younger, had higher admission blood pressure and LDL cholesterol levels, and were more likely to undergo PCI during the index admission (online supplemental table S2). In MI patients with advanced renal dysfunction, early GDMT use was associated with a 61% reduction in in-hospital mortality (RR: 0.39, 95% CI: 0.27 to 0.58; online supplemental table S2 and figure S6).

Sensitivity and subgroup analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the association between early GDMT use and in-hospital mortality. Patients who died within 24 hours of admission, had severe conditions at admission (cardiogenic shock, HF or cardiac arrest), or had a history of PCI or CABG were excluded. Notably, after excluding patients with a history of PCI or CABG, early GDMT use remained significantly associated with reduced in-hospital mortality (figure 4). Subgroup analyses were conducted to further validate and generalise these findings across key clinical and demographic subgroups. Consistent trends were observed across age groups (≥75 or <75 years), sex, ACS subtypes (STEMI or NSTE-ACS), diabetes and PCI status during the index admission. While similar protective trends were observed across all subgroups in the CCC-ACS cohort, larger differences were noted in the CNEDSSP cohort (figure 5).

Discussion

In two nationwide ACS cohorts in China, this study demonstrated that early GDMT (initiated within the first 24 hours of admission) was associated with a reduction in in-hospital mortality in ACS patients with advanced renal dysfunction (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2), and these findings were consistent in multiple statistical analyses. The same trend was demonstrated in PSM-derived cohorts, although no statistical significance was present. After excluding patients diagnosed with UA on admission, we found that early GDMT-related reduction in in-hospital mortality was mainly driven by the MI cohort. On the basis of these findings, our findings provide evidence suggesting the clinical effectiveness of early initiation of GDMT in MI patients with an admission of advanced renal dysfunction.

Feasibility of early GDMT use

In this study, ACS patients with advanced renal dysfunction have more comorbidities (such as hypertension accounting for 73.5%, diabetes 42.1%), which is similar to other studies.17 26 In fact, CKD patients have a significantly increased risk of cardiovascular and thromboembolic complications, as well as a higher risk of bleeding.27,29 Due to concerns about the risk of in-hospital adverse outcomes (such as metabolic changes and bleeding risks), the early use of GDMT in high-risk ACS patients was often conservative. A secondary analysis study from the CPACS-2 Programme showed that there was a lower percentage of adherence to guideline-recommended treatments and worse clinical prognosis among ACS patients with renal dysfunction in China.30 31 About 19.8% of ACS patients with advanced renal dysfunction have received GDMT on admission in our study, which is similar to other previous studies.19 In relation to previous studies, our findings provide further insights into DAPT use among patients with advanced CKD. Prior registries such as the IN-ACS Outcome Registry reported an association between aspirin use and improved mortality outcomes in CKD patients, while the CREDO Trial did not demonstrate a significant benefit of prolonged DAPT.32,34 It is noteworthy that these studies primarily enrolled patients with moderate CKD (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m²), with an average eGFR around 47 mL/min/1.73 m², suggesting a relatively preserved renal function compared with our cohort (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m²). In addition, our study population exhibited a more adverse clinical profile, characterised by a higher burden of comorbidities, reduced cardiac function, lower PCI rates and a higher in-hospital mortality rate (~9%), compared with IN-ACS (~6%) and the CREDO Trial (~9% for the 28-day major adverse cardiovascular events composite endpoint). These differences may partly explain the observed inconsistencies. Given the elevated bleeding risk and overall frailty in patients with severe CKD,35,37 our results emphasise the need for a more individualised approach to antiplatelet therapy, carefully balancing ischaemic protection against bleeding complications. Future research should aim to refine DAPT strategies tailored to the heterogeneous risk profiles within the CKD population.

In addition, some observational studies have shown that the use of ACEI/ARB can further worsen renal function in patients with advanced renal dysfunction, so the use of ACEI/ARB is still cautious in patients with advanced CKD.38 39 This phenomenon has also been observed in our research, in which the proportion of ACEI/ARB accounts for 39.0%. However, recent randomised controlled trials and large cohort studies have found that ACEI/ARB use could improve cardiovascular outcomes,9 40 41 and application of ACEI/ARB combined with other certain medications of GDMT is associated with reduction of in-hospital mortality.

Due to the proved clinical efficacy in the secondary prevention of ACS, GDMT is currently recognised as the standard treatment for these patients. However, it is difficult to establish a causal relationship between GDMT and reduction of in-hospital mortality in randomised controlled trials because of these trials’ exclusion criteria (most trials exclude advanced renal dysfunction). To our knowledge, we may be the first study to examine the effect of early GDMT use on in-hospital mortality for patients with admission eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 in the acute phase of ACS. Notably, we observed that patients with a diagnosis of MI on indexed admission were more likely to benefit from early GDMT, whereas for UA patients, this survival benefit was attenuated. In stratified analyses, we found that early GDMT use was similar to the combination of β-blocker and statin or the combination of β-blocker, statin and DAPT regardless of the use of other drugs in improving in-hospital mortality, which may indeed present a new individualised approach to clinical decision-making in terms of drug selection. By a sensitivity analysis, we hypothesised that the survival-associated benefit of early GDMT in the UA patients was offset by bleeding events or further deterioration of renal function. Given the relatively low-risk characteristics of UA patients, the prescription would be individualised according to each patient’s characteristics. Subsequent large clinical trials are needed to examine the effect of treatment on death in patients with UA and severe renal dysfunction.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths: (1) it is a large, retrospective cohort study based on two national databases, enhancing the generalisability and robustness of the findings; (2) multiple traditional risk factors were adjusted for, minimising confounding variables. However, there are several limitations: (1) as an observational study, it can establish only associations, not causality and randomised controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings; (2) the study could not account for unmeasured confounders, such as metabolic or electrolyte disturbances, which may influence both treatment decisions and patient outcomes; (3) the focus on a specific population and the lack of long-term follow-up limit the generalisability of the findings to other populations and the assessment of long-term outcomes. Further research with longer follow-up periods is needed to evaluate the lasting effects of early GDMT use.

Conclusion

Based on a nationwide EHR-based cohort and on a nationwide ACS registry in contemporary practice, this study demonstrated that GDMT initiated within 24 hours of admission is associated with a reduced risk of in-hospital mortality in ACS patients with admission eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, and this associated benefit is primarily observed in patients with MI.
