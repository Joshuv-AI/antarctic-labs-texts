Metagenomics comparison identifies shared pathogenic microbiome in humans, pigs and chickens
Authors: Sung J. Yu, Dragana Stanley, Thi T. H. Van, Jason C. Steel, Yadav S. Bajagai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntegrating human, animal, and environmental health is crucial for combating infectious diseases, as an estimated 60 to 75% of emerging infectious diseases originate from zoonotic sources globally. In this study, we analysed 1274 shotgun metagenomic faecal samples of humans, pigs, and chickens collected across multiple countries to estimate levels of microbial sharing at the species-level genome bins (SGBs) resolution. We confirm that host species, rather than geography, significantly structures the gut microbial community, as shown by alpha and beta diversity analyses. Despite this high host specificity, we identified substantial cross-host sharing of SGBs, including taxa recognised as pathogens such as Escherichia coli, Clostridium perfringens, Clostridium innocuum, Clostridium disporicum, Enterococcus species, and Streptococcus alactolyticus. Core taxa were predominantly host-specific, while non-core taxa were more frequently shared across humans, pigs, and chickens. LEfSe analysis identified distinctive microbial signatures for each host, further supporting differences in community composition. These findings demonstrate that unrelated and geographically distant humans and livestock can harbour highly similar microbial populations with pathogenic potential. This work provides molecular evidence supporting the need for integrated One Health surveillance to better detect, manage, and prevent zoonotic and reverse zoonotic transmission events across interconnected human, animal, and environmental systems.Key points• There is substantial cross-host sharing of species-level genome bins, including potential pathogens• Core taxa are predominantly host-specific• Non-core taxa are more likely to be shared across humans, pigs, and chickensSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00253-026-13948-1.

Introduction

Livestock industries are undergoing rapid technological advancement driven by developments in metagenomics, microbiota, improved breeding and genomics, enhanced animal welfare, and increased automation and robotics. Precision feeding and sensor technologies now support optimised nutrition and continuous animal health monitoring. Together, these innovations increase productivity and sustainability and enhance global food security in countries that can afford to keep pace with research and development.

Global meat consumption continues to increase, driven by rising incomes in middle- and lower-income countries, while more developed countries appear to have reached their peak consumption (Henchion et al. 2021). Although livestock production supports global nutrition and contributes to food safety, it is increasingly scrutinised for its environmental impacts. Major concerns include greenhouse gas emissions, water and land use, deforestation, water and soil pollution, increased antibiotic resistance, and zoonotic disease risk (Lee et al. 2025; Takacs et al. 2025).

The One Health paradigm is an integrated approach to balancing and optimising the health of people, animals, and the environment. This holistic approach recognises the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health, where changes within one system influence the others. The importance of One Health is underscored by the global health threat posed by emerging infectious diseases (EIDs), with an estimated 60 to 75% of all EIDs having a zoonotic origin (Borham et al. 2025; Horefti 2023). The spread of antimicrobial resistance illustrates these connections, with resistance factors originating from livestock manure or human waste entering water systems, spreading to humans and animals, and contributing to disease outbreaks and environmental pollution (Abeles 2024; Stanley et al. 2022).

Livestock production is associated with a high pathogen load, faecal contamination, and numerous zoonotic threats. Poultry-associated diseases, including salmonellosis and campylobacteriosis, are commonly transmitted through contaminated food and faecal material (Agunos et al. 2016; Varela et al. 2022). Critical pathogens, such as avian influenza and Newcastle disease, also pose substantial risks to both animal and human health (Agunos et al. 2016; Varela et al. 2022).

Reverse zoonosis, or zooanthroponosis, involves the transfer of pathogens from humans to animals, and is rising due to urbanisation, globalisation, and climate change. A growing body of research documents viral, bacterial, parasitic, fungal, and protozoal transmission from humans to animals (Al Noman et al. 2024). Despite its importance, reverse zoonosis remains less recognised than traditional zoonotic transmission. The potential for human pathogens to infect large numbers of farmed animals raises concerns about animal welfare, economic losses, and the amplification of pathogens that may subsequently return to humans. Kibenge (2023) suggested a One Health approach to addressing these issues, including active surveillance and vaccination of humans, especially those exposed to susceptible livestock.

Recent advances in sequencing technologies enable the culture-free identification of pathogens and can facilitate the reconstruction of microbial genomes from metagenomic data. Such approaches enable the identification of strains transmitted from livestock to farmers and vice versa, even when strains are not overtly pathogenic. Although awareness of zoonotic transmission is common among farmers, understanding of reverse zoonosis remains limited (Al Noman et al. 2024; Kibenge 2023).

Currently, most journals and funding agencies require deposition of raw sequencing data in public repositories, allowing large cross-species metagenomic datasets to be retrieved and reanalysed. This enables comparative investigation of reconstructed species-level genome bins (SGBs) to identify microbial lineages shared among host species (Borderes et al. 2021).

Multiple strain-level genome bins can be assigned to the same species but map to distinct metagenomes and are considered approximations of strain-level. Once a species-level genome bin is identified, strain-level analysis goes deeper into genetic differences, identifying single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) or the presence/absence of specific genes (e.g. virulence factors or antibiotic resistance genes) within that species SGB, creating so-called sub-bins. Although exact strain-level resolution remains technically challenging, comparing these unique metagenomic SGBs provides insight into the likelihood of shared microorganisms and potential cross-host transmission risks.

In this study, we downloaded publicly available shotgun metagenomic sequencing studies on chicken, pigs, and humans from studies originating from Belgium, Bulgaria, Canada, China, Denmark, France, Germany, India, Italy, Malawi, Netherlands, Poland, South Korea, Spain, the UK, and the USA, ensuring multicontinental coverage. After quality filtering, 1274 faecal metagenomic samples were analysed; pigs and humans were well represented with 537 and 539 samples respectively, whereas only 198 chicken samples were included because faecal samples are less commonly available in poultry research, where cloacal swabs and caecal samples are more frequently used. Faecal samples were selected to enable a consistent cross-host comparison and because faecal material is a major route through which many enteric microorganisms, including zoonotic and opportunistic pathogens, can be shed into farm, food-chain and environmental interfaces (Delahoy et al. 2018; Penakalapati et al. 2017). Our objectives were to quantify the magnitude of shared SGBs among the three hosts, evaluate the occurrence of taxa with known pathogenic potential, and interpret these data within a One Health framework relevant to zoonotic and reverse-zoonotic risk assessment.

Materials and methods

Study design and data sources

We conducted a cross-host species comparative analysis of the faecal microbiomes from humans, chickens, and pigs using publicly available shotgun metagenomic datasets. To ensure a like-for-like comparison, only faecal samples were included, resulting in a smaller number of chicken samples. Studies were selected to create balanced cohorts spanning multiple countries and independent research groups. Raw sequencing data and associated metadata were retrieved from public repositories (primarily the NCBI Sequence Read Archive, SRA) using the SRA Toolkit.

A total of 1274 samples were analysed: human (n = 539), pig (n = 537) and chicken (n = 198) (Table 1). Countries represented were China, Italy, and the UK for chicken; Germany, India, Italy, Malawi, the Netherlands, Poland, South Korea, and the USA for human; and Belgium, Bulgaria, Canada, China, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Poland, Spain, and the UK for pig. Datasets were selected based on the quality of sequences and annotations. Read processing and quality control

Initial quality control of paired-end FASTQ files was performed using FastQC v0.11.2 (Andrews 2010) and MultiQC v1.11 (Ewels et al. 2016), followed by adapter removal and quality trimming with fastp using default parameters, including automatic adapter detection and the default quality and length filtering thresholds (Chen et al. 2018). Host-derived reads were removed using KneadData v0.7.10 (https://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/kneaddata) employing Bowtie2 v2.5.1 (Langmead and Salzberg 2012) to align reads against species-specific host genomes for human (GRCh38.p14, Accession: PRJNA31257), chicken (bGalGal1, Accession: PRJNA660758), and pig (Sscrofa11.1, Accession: PRJNA13421) to retain predominantly microbial reads for downstream profiling. After processing, approximately 5.9 × 101 high-quality, host-filtered reads were retained across all samples, with an average of ≈ 46.30 million reads per sample. Sample-level accession numbers with the number of quality-filtered reads for each sample are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Bioinformatics and statistical analyses

The KneadData trimmed clean sequences were analysed for microbial taxonomic profile using MetaPhlAn 3 (Beghini et al. 2021) with default parameters and mpa_vJan21 ChocoPhlAn database. The downstream statistical analysis and visualisation were done with the R program v4.4.2 using the packages Phyloseq v1.50.0 (McMurdie and Holmes 2013) and Microeco v1.15.0 (Liu et al. 2021) and Microbiome v1.28.0 (Lahti and Shetty 2018).

Within each host group and at the selected taxonomic rank, taxa were classified as core if they reached ≥ 0.1% relative abundance in ≥ 50% of samples. Taxa that did not meet this prevalence at the same detection threshold were classified as non-core. The same thresholds were applied across the host groups to enable like-for-like comparisons and subsequent overlap analyses. Host-specific lists of taxa classified as core and non-core, and the overlap among humans, chickens, and pigs, were quantified by computing pairwise and three-way intersections. Shared taxa were summarised numerically and visualised with Venn diagrams.

Results

Recent advances in our understanding of the roles of intestinal microbiota suggest that differences in gut anatomy and environmental factors influence alpha and beta diversity of intestinal microbiomes. Although the samples used in this study originated from different countries and studies, consistent patterns emerged when comparing the three host groups. Alpha diversity analysis showed that richness, measured using the Chao1 index, and diversity, measured using the Shannon index, varied among chickens, humans, and pigs (Fig. 1). Chicken samples in our study had the lowest richness and diversity, significantly lower than those of humans and pigs. In contrast, pig samples showed the highest levels of both richness and diversity, but also the greatest dispersion among samples, indicating substantial heterogeneity across pig datasets. Supplementary Figs S1 and S2 show the overall taxonomic composition of the faecal microbiota of chickens, humans, and pigs at the phylum and genus levels, respectively. Firmicutes was the dominant phylum in all three host species, but the abundance of other major phyla differed between hosts. Chicken microbiota had higher levels of Proteobacteria and higher abundance of genera such as Escherichia and Lactobacillus. Human microbiota had a higher representation of Bacteroidetes and Actinobacteria, with greater abundance of genera involved in complex carbohydrate fermentation and short-chain fatty acid production, including Bacteroides, Phocaeicola, Alistipes, Faecalibacterium, and Bifidobacterium. In contrast, the pig microbiota were dominated by Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes and showed higher abundances of Prevotella, Lactobacillus, and Streptococcus. Overall, although the major phyla were broadly similar across hosts, clear differences at both phylum and genus levels support strong host-associated structuring of the gut microbiota across the geographically diverse datasets analysed in this study.

Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and Jaccard distance were used to assess beta diversity. Bray–Curtis considers differences in taxon abundance and is more influenced by dominant taxa, whereas Jaccard distance is based on presence-absence patterns and is more sensitive to rare taxa. Chickens had characteristic high levels of Proteobacteria and Fusobacteria phyla, as well as the genera Escherichia, Lactobacillus, Acinetobacter, and Ligilactobacillus (Fig. 5). Human characteristic phyla were Actinobacteria and Verrucomicrobia, with Alistipes, Bacteroides, Bifidobacterium, Phocaeicola, and Faecalibacterium as distinct genera, while in pigs, characteristic phyla include Spirochaetes and Chlamydiae, with Prevotella genus as the only identified characteristic pig genus.

Discussion

This study provides a multicontinental comparison of faecal metagenomic datasets from humans, pigs, and chickens. It shows that host species strongly shapes gut microbial communities, even when samples come from different countries and independent studies. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that host physiology, diet, intestinal anatomy, and immune selection influence gut microbiome composition (Degregori et al. 2025; Wilde et al. 2024). However, the detection of shared species-level genome bins across species suggests that host-associated structuring does not fully prevent some microbial lineages from occurring across host boundaries.

The difference between core and non-core taxa was important in this study. Core species-level genome bins were mainly linked with specific host species. This suggests that common and dominant faecal microorganisms are strongly influenced by host ecology. In contrast, non-core taxa were more often shared among humans, pigs, and chickens. This pattern suggests that taxa with low or moderate prevalence may play a larger role in microbial overlap between hosts. These taxa may include transient microorganisms, environmentally acquired microorganisms, food-chain-associated taxa, or opportunistic lineages that can colonise more than one host species.

The livestock industry encompasses multiple animal species, including poultry, pigs, and ruminants, and provides meat, eggs, milk, and other products essential for human health and the global economy. Despite these contributions, the industry faces continually evolving challenges that demand improvements in productivity, sustainability, and biosecurity. Biosecurity has always been at the forefront of livestock management, and new threats continue to arise through rapid globalisation, increased movement of people and goods, and the introduction of exotic diseases. The industry addresses these challenges by maintaining strict hygiene for visitors and equipment, conducting ongoing monitoring of animal health, managing feral animals, especially in free-range settings, and ensuring that response plans are in place for any suspected disease outbreaks.

Human, ecological, and pathogen-related factors all contribute to the rise of emerging and reemerging diseases, with climate change increasingly recognised as an accelerator of these events. Emerging infectious diseases are not limited to humans or human-associated animals. Evidence now demonstrates that novel pathogens can arise in a wide range of wild species, including snakes (Liu et al. 2023), birds (Robinson et al. 2010), and insects (Damian et al. 2020). These observations highlight the need for integrated approaches that recognise the interconnectedness of environmental and biological systems.

Livestock, including pigs, poultry, and cattle (Astill et al. 2018; Grout et al. 2022; McLean and Graham 2022), have been identified as reservoirs for the amplification of emerging pathogens that can pose a risk to humans. Traditional assumptions suggesting that most microbial species evolved toward a single preferred host are being challenged by modern genomic technologies, which reveal that many pathogens can infect multiple hosts or undergo host switching. Host switching is a significant factor in the emergence of new infectious diseases. Chavez-Dozal et al. (2014) examined gene exchange between marine pathogen strains isolated from different host species on different continents. They showed that gene complementation between strains increased the fitness of Vibrio fischeri, enabling it to achieve competitive dominance in a non-native host. Similarly, humans have been identified as a central reservoir for the multi-host bacterial pathogen, Staphylococcus aureus, in which gene exchange between ancient and contemporary strains has facilitated host-switching events that contributed to the emergence of livestock-adapted lineages (Richardson et al. 2018). These examples highlight the potential for both zoonoses and zooanthroponosis to occur within farm environments.

Other factors that increase the ability of pathogens to jump from one host to another include the presence of multi-host pathogens, the presence of mutagens in feed and the environment, and the innate adaptability of microbial pathogens (Richardson et al. 2018). Environmental mutagens are not decreasing, and climate change may increase mutation rates by elevating temperatures and influencing molecular processes related to DNA stability (Jacobs 2021; Liao et al. 2024). These conditions heighten the risk that human or livestock outbreaks may provide opportunities for pathogen adaptation and cross-species transmission.

Because this analysis cannot distinguish pathogenic from benign SGBs, this study focused specifically on shared SGBs associated with known or emerging pathogens (Table 2, and Supplementary Table S2). The sharing of species-level genome bins for E. coli, C. perfringens, Clostridium difficile, C. innocuum, E. cecorum, E. gallinarum, and S. alactolyticus between humans and livestock raises substantial animal and public health concerns that require further pathogenic strain-resolved investigation. E. coli is well known for causing urinary tract infections (UTIs) in humans, and represents a considerable health burden that requires prolonged and repetitive use of antibiotics. Broiler meat, broiler chickens, pork, and pigs have been identified as probable sources of UTI strains in humans, with evidence implicating chickens as a significant source of the pathogen (Jakobsen et al. 2010a, 2010b; Schaeffer 2013).

The toxin-producing C. perfringens infects humans, pigs, and chickens (Camargo et al. 2024). Various strains can cause significant necrotic enteritis or similar disease in multiple hosts, including humans, pigs, and chickens (Lu et al. 2021). However, the versatility of these strains extends beyond typical enteric diseases to frequently cause fatal neurotoxic conditions in ruminants (Finnie and Uzal 2022). The potential adaptation of certain strains to human hosts represents a growing concern and remains a topic of active scientific debate (Dorca-Arevalo et al. 2008; Finnie and Uzal 2022). Of particular concern is the epsilon toxin (ETX)-producing C. perfringens, which is classified as a bioterrorism agent due to its potency. Adaptation of this strain to human hosts could cause enterotoxemia similar to that observed in animals, with devastating implications for public health. Notably, C. difficile, a well-characterised and devastating pathogen (Wang et al. 2025), was not identified as a shared SGB in this study, which may reflect its lower prevalence or abundance in faecal metagenomes from the datasets analysed, rather than a true absence from these host species. Additional concerns arise from the emerging pathogens, C. disporicum and C. innocuum. C. disporicum was recently implicated in the first reported cases of joint and abdominal infections (McBride et al. 2017; Plassart et al. 2013), while C. innocuum has only recently been recognised as an emerging pathogen (Cherny et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2023).

Interestingly, our study identified no shared SGBs for several well-known pathogen groups, including S. aureus, Streptococcus suis, nor SGBs from genera often associated with respiratory infections such as Haemophilus, Moraxella, Mycobacterium, Bordetella, Pseudomonas, Neisseria, Chlamydia, and Legionella. The absence of shared SGBs across these groups may reflect host specificity but could also be due to insufficient representation in the included datasets.

The LEfSe analysis indicated that chickens had a higher representation of Escherichia species, suggesting a higher abundance of these taxa in faecal material. However, it is important to acknowledge that microbiome profiles differ across intestinal regions in each host, and faecal abundance does not necessarily reflect overall intestinal colonisation.

The broader implication of these findings is that One Health surveillance should not be limited to known outbreak pathogens. Shared non-core taxa may give early signals of microbial exchange between humans, livestock, and environments, especially when combined with virulence-gene, antimicrobial-resistance, and strain-level analyses. To translate the One Health framework into action, greater emphasis is needed on implementation science. Implementation science examines the gap between controlled research outcomes and real-world application, which can be influenced by socio-economic and political contexts (Ogunseitan 2022). A lack of multisectoral coordination between veterinary and medical sectors remains a significant challenge (Shafique et al. 2024). Examples from integrated approaches, such as those used in Tick-Borne Disease surveillance in the Netherlands, demonstrate that high transdisciplinary collaboration and public engagement can lead to measurable improvements in health outcomes and early detection of emerging pathogens (Garcia-Vozmediano et al. 2022).

In conclusion, our findings provide clear molecular evidence that humans, pigs, and chickens share multiple microbial taxa, including species with pathogenic potential. These results reinforce the importance of integrated surveillance systems that monitor human, animal, and environmental health simultaneously. Capacity building at local and regional levels, combined with cross-sector collaboration, is essential to support early detection, prevention, and containment of emerging health threats within the One Health framework.
