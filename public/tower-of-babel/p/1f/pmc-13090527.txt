Age‐Dependent Differences in Canakinumab Safety: A Comprehensive Pharmacovigilance Analysis Using the FAERS Database
Authors: Youyang Wang, Ngasin Lam, Xiaoming Huang, Yang Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile the efficacy of canakinumab, an anti‐interleukin‐1β monoclonal antibody, is well‐established, its safety profile, particularly across different age groups, remains inadequately explored. Using the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database, this study evaluated postmarketing safety by analyzing adverse event (AE) reports from 2010 onward, with canakinumab as the primary suspected drug. Disproportionality analysis employed four algorithms—reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio, multi‐item gamma Poisson shrinker, and Bayesian confidence propagation neural network—to detect safety signals, and the Weibull distribution was used to model the temporal risk of AEs. 9262 canakinumab‐related AE reports were analyzed. Significant signals were detected across multiple system organ classes (SOCs), most prominently general disorders and administration site conditions (n = 6765; ROR 1.47) and infections and infestations (n = 3532; ROR 2.54). Age‐stratified analyses revealed distinct AE profiles: pyrexia, inappropriate schedule or dose of administration, and gastrointestinal and hepatobiliary disorders (e.g., abdominal pain, hepatitis) were most frequently reported in individuals < 12 years, as well as leukopenia in adolescents (12–17 years). Older patients (≥ 65 years) most frequently reported pneumonia, sepsis, and cellulitis, with strong signals for neoplasms. Temporal analysis revealed an early failure pattern, but ~40% of AEs occurred after 1 year. These findings underscore significant safety signals associated with canakinumab, including infections, gastrointestinal/hepatobiliary disorders in children, and neoplasms in older patients. The dual temporal pattern of AEs underscores the need for both short‐ and long‐term surveillance.Keywords: adverse drug events, canakinumab, Ilaris, pharmacovigilance

Introduction

The interleukin‐1 (IL‐1) family of 11 pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory cytokines is a central mediator of innate immunity and inflammatory responses. IL‐1β, the most extensively studied family member, is primarily produced by monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells (DC). IL‐1β is activated through both inflammasome‐dependent and ‐independent mechanisms [1] and binds to the type 1 IL‐1 receptor (IL‐1R1), triggering inflammatory cascades and intracellular signaling pathways involving IRAK4, MK2, and NF‐κB, ultimately leading to further expression of inflammatory cytokines and inflammasome proteins [2]. Under physiological conditions, IL‐1β is either not expressed or maintained at minimal basal levels. Dysregulated IL‐1β activity is associated with several autoinflammatory diseases. Given that many chronic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases and cancer, share similarities with dysregulated inflammatory responses, blocking IL‐1β may offer benefits in these conditions [3].

Canakinumab is a fully human monoclonal antibody that targets IL‐1β. By specifically binding to IL‐1β, canakinumab competitively blocks ligand‐receptor engagement with IL‐1R1, thereby suppressing subsequent inflammatory responses. Compared to other IL‐1 blockers (e.g., anakinra, rilonacept), canakinumab has greater specificity for IL‐1β, does not interfere with other IL‐1‐activated pathways, and has a longer half‐life, thereby reducing the need for frequent injections or high doses. FDA‐approved indications for canakinumab include cryopyrin‐associated periodic syndrome (CAPS), tumor necrosis factor receptor‐associated periodic syndrome (TRAPS), hyperimmunoglobulin D syndrome (HIDS)/mevalonate kinase deficiency (MKD), familial Mediterranean fever (FMF), Still's disease (including adult‐onset Still's disease and systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis), and gout flares.

Several previous studies have demonstrated the clinical efficacy, tolerability, and safety of canakinumab. However, due to the rarity of its approved indications, most adverse event (AE) data are derived from small clinical trials or observational cohorts, so safety data are limited. Commonly reported AEs include headache, nasopharyngitis, upper respiratory tract infections, and abdominal pain. Serious AEs are often related to infections such as tonsillitis, methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus abscess, pericarditis, and acute peritonitis [4]. However, long‐term, large‐scale safety evaluations in clinical practice are still lacking. Additionally, canakinumab is approved for pediatric and adult use in all indications except gout, and it has been tested in various other diseases. Given the wide age range of target patients, a comprehensive analysis of risk factors and AEs across different age groups is essential.

The FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database serves as a critical pharmacovigilance platform, integrating spontaneous reports from healthcare professionals (e.g., physicians, pharmacists), consumers, and other reporters to systematically detect postmarketing safety signals. Here we aimed to analyze canakinumab‐related data from the FAERS database to investigate the types, relative risks, and timing of AEs associated with canakinumab across different age groups. These findings provide comprehensive and specific safety information to inform and enhance patient adherence and tolerance, while offering more accurate and personalized guidance for clinical use.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

This was a disproportionality analysis using FAERS data to explore associations between canakinumab and its AEs, adhering to the latest Reporting of A Disproportionality Analysis for Drug Safety Signal Detection Using Individual Case Safety Reports in Pharmacovigilance (READUS‐PV) guidelines [5]. Data were retrieved using both the brand and generic names of canakinumab as search keywords, covering the period from the first quarter of 2010 (the year of canakinumab's market approval) to the third quarter of 2024. AE‐related information in the FAERS database was structured into seven principal data files: demographic information (DEMO), drug details (DRUG), AE descriptions (REAC), patient outcomes (OUTC), reporting sources (RPSR), medication dates (THER), and indications for medication use (INDI). To improve accuracy, only AE reports in which canakinumab was classified as the primary suspect in the DRUG file were included. Duplicates were processed according to FDA‐recommended practices: for reports with the same case identifier (CASEID), the report with the most recent FDA receipt date (FDA_DT) was retained; for reports with the same CASEID and FDA_DT, the report with the highest PRIMARYID (the unique identifier assigned to each report) was retained. AEs reported in the REAC file were coded by preferred terms (PTs) and organized into system organ classes (SOCs) using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities v27.1.

As this study analyzed publicly available data from the FAERS database, neither ethical approval nor patient consent was required.

Statistical Analysis

The clinical characteristics of canakinumab‐related AE reports are reported descriptively. The chi‐squared test was used to compare the distributions of patients across age groups. Four disproportionality analysis methods were used to detect potential AE signals: reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), multi‐item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS), and Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN). Detailed two‐by‐two contingency tables, along with the formulas and thresholds for these disproportionality analyses, are provided in Tables S1 and S2. The interval between the occurrence of AEs (as EVENT_DT in the DEMO file) and the initiation of canakinumab treatment (as START_DT in the THER file) defined the onset time of canakinumab‐related AEs. Weibull distributions were utilized to model temporal changes in the incidence of AEs. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.1.

Results

Characteristics of AE Reports

From the first quarter of 2010 to the third quarter of 2024, this investigation analyzed 9262 reports of AEs (27 954 total AEs) in which canakinumab was the suspected primary drug. Significant differences in demographic characteristics were observed across various age groups. Overall, females accounted for 51.9% (n = 4806) of reports, while males represented 37.3% (n = 3455). The median age of cases was 18 years (interquartile range [IQR] 9–51). Geographically, the United States contributed the highest proportion of reports (39.5%), followed by Japan (5.3%) and Germany (3.7%). Healthcare professionals submitted 50.6% of reports. Among known indications, canakinumab was primarily prescribed for Still's disease, CAPS, and juvenile idiopathic arthritis. However, in patients ≥ 65 years, cardiovascular event prophylaxis (46.0%, n = 258) and nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (10.3%, n = 58) were the most common indications. The most frequently reported outcomes were hospitalization—initial or prolonged (18.4%, n = 1702), other serious (important medical event) (16.8%, n = 1554), and death (4.0%, n = 369). Distribution of AEs at the SOCs Level

AEs associated with canakinumab spanned all 27 SOCs. Nine SOCs were identified as significant based on meeting the threshold for at least one of the four disproportionality analysis algorithms (ROR, PRR, MGPS, or BCPNN). Based on the number of AEs, the three most frequently reported SOCs were: (i) general disorders and administration site conditions (n = 6765; ROR = 1.47), (ii) infections and infestations (n = 3532; ROR = 2.54), and (iii) musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders (n = 2066; ROR = 1.41), as detailed in Table 2.

Distribution of AEs at the PT Level

A total of 217 canakinumab‐induced AE signals were detected at the PT level using all four algorithms. Among the 30 most frequently reported AEs, known reactions included pyrexia, arthralgia, rash, pneumonia, cough, abdominal pain, nasopharyngitis, influenza, infection, oropharyngeal pain, joint swelling, and rhinorrhoea. Additionally, potential AEs not listed on the label, such as inappropriate schedule of product administration, incorrect dose administered, COVID‐19, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, cellulitis, lymphadenopathy, and diverticulitis, were identified (Table 3).

Sensitivity Analysis

Based on clinical practice and FAERS data, commonly co‐administered or prior medications with canakinumab include prednisone, methotrexate, tocilizumab, and aspirin. After excluding reports involving concurrent use of other drugs, 8334 reports involving 2238 signals at the PT level were identified. Among these, 156 signals met all four algorithm criteria. Persistent potential AEs associated with canakinumab monotherapy were consistent with previous analyses and included pyrexia, inappropriate schedule of product administration, malaise, condition aggravated, and incorrect dose administered (Table S3).

Discussion

Although AEs associated with canakinumab have been reported in clinical trials, comprehensive studies, particularly in specific populations, remain limited. This study, based on real‐world data from the FAERS database, used disproportionality analysis to identify AEs significantly associated with canakinumab therapy and investigated differences across age groups.

Analysis of Signals in the Overall Population

Among all canakinumab users, the risks of infections and general disorders warrant particular attention. Infections and infestations, as well as respiratory, thoracic, and mediastinal disorders, were identified as significant SOCs across all age groups. PTs related to upper respiratory and pulmonary infections accounted for six of the top 20 PTs (pneumonia, cough, COVID‐19, nasopharyngitis, influenza, rhinorrhoea), aligning with the FDA black box warning for canakinumab. General disorders and administration site conditions represented the most frequently reported SOC and were identified as significant in three of the four age groups. Related PTs included pyrexia, malaise, illness, and arthralgia. Patients with autoinflammatory diseases experience significant impairment in their quality of life (QoL), and canakinumab therapy has been shown to improve QoL in these patients [7, 8]. However, the presence of these AEs may further exacerbate already compromised QoL, necessitating greater attention and comprehensive management. Notably, injection site reactions were not identified as significant AEs, consistent with previous retrospective studies. Compared to anakinra, injection‐site reactions are less frequent in patients receiving canakinumab, which, combined with less frequent dosing, may improve adherence.

Through sensitivity analysis, we reduced the influence of glucocorticoids, methotrexate, tocilizumab, and aspirin on the outcomes, all of which are commonly administered concurrently with or prior to canakinumab. The analysis showed that concomitant medication use did not affect the analysis of canakinumab‐induced AEs, supporting the robustness of the findings.

Temporal analysis and Weibull distribution modeling helped establish effective timelines for monitoring drug‐related AEs. The early failure pattern for canakinumab‐related AEs suggests that these events are more likely to occur soon after treatment initiation, rather than later in the treatment course, emphasizing the need for close monitoring during the initial phase. However, a considerable proportion (~40%) of AEs occurred 1 year or more after the initiation of canakinumab therapy. A prospective CAPS cohort study also reported an increase in severe AEs with prolonged canakinumab exposure [9], highlighting the need for further evaluation of long‐term safety.

Analysis of Signals in Children

A significant proportion of canakinumab users are children or adolescents, with individuals under 18 years of age representing 48.3% of cases with documented age information in this analysis. Pediatric patients demonstrated distinct AE profiles compared with adults, and differences were also observed between those aged < 12 years and 12–17 years.

At the SOC level, two specific signals were identified among patients younger than 12 years: gastrointestinal disorders (e.g., abdominal pain, mouth ulceration, gastritis, paralytic ileus, inflammatory bowel disease, parotid gland enlargement) and hepatobiliary disorders (e.g., hepatomegaly, hepatitis, hepatic steatosis, acute hepatitis). Studies on anakinra, another IL‐1 inhibitor, have identified pediatric age group and treatment duration as risk factors for liver toxicity [10]. The canakinumab prescribing information includes increased liver enzymes as an AE and advises against its use in patients with hepatitis B or C. However, other hepatobiliary disorders are not explicitly mentioned, and previous studies have rarely reported them. A clinical trial investigating canakinumab in alcohol‐related hepatitis reported similar AEs but only enrolled patients > 18 years [11]. These findings suggest that gastrointestinal and hepatobiliary disorders associated with canakinumab are underrecognized and warrant clinical attention.

At the PT level, inappropriate schedule or dose of administration was among the most frequently reported AEs in pediatric patients. Previous studies have demonstrated that intensified dosing of canakinumab may enhance disease control in patients with CAPS, particularly in children [12]. Data from the German RELIANCE registry indicated that over 60% of pediatric CAPS, TRAPS, and MKD/HIDS patients under 12 years of age received doses exceeding the standard, while 27.8% of patients aged 12–17 years were administered higher‐than‐standard doses [12]. Multiple infection‐related AEs were also identified in pediatric patients, including COVID‐19, influenza, upper respiratory tract infections (e.g., nasopharyngitis, pharyngitis, tonsillitis), gastroenteritis, bronchitis, urinary tract infections, ear infections, impetigo, and varicella or herpes zoster. Although previous studies have not consistently concluded that children are more susceptible to serious AEs [9, 13], infections were reported more frequently in pediatric patients compared to adults [13]. Notably, ear infections and impetigo were not explicitly mentioned in the canakinumab prescribing information. Further stratification of pediatric patients revealed that bronchitis, scarlet fever, and paronychia were significant only in patients under 12 years of age, while urinary tract infections and sinusitis were significant only in patients aged 12–17 years. These findings suggest that clinicians should closely monitor infection risks tailored to different pediatric age groups.

Additionally, Leukopenia emerged as a unique signal in patients aged 12–17 years. Previous clinical trials and observational studies have reported canakinumab‐related leukopenia, which led to treatment discontinuation in some cases [14, 15, 16]. This may be attributed to canakinumab's direct effects on neutrophil production or destruction [17], or it may be related to concomitant viral infections or disease activity causing increased apoptosis of inflammatory cells [18]. Regardless of the underlying mechanism, our study underscores the importance of monitoring leukocyte counts in patients aged 12–17 years and further investigating the mechanisms involved.

Analysis of Signals in Elderly

There were fewer AE reports from patients aged ≥ 65 years in the FAERS database. The primary indications for canakinumab in this group were not its approved indications (autoinflammatory diseases and gout flares) but rather cardiovascular event prophylaxis and NSCLC. The phase III Canakinumab Anti‐Inflammatory Thrombosis Outcomes Study (CANTOS) demonstrated that canakinumab reduced recurrent cardiovascular events in patients with prior myocardial infarction and elevated high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein, independent of lipid‐lowering effects [19]. An exploratory analysis of CANTOS suggested potential benefits in reducing lung cancer incidence and mortality [19, 20]. Blocking IL‐1β activity may control the growth of various cancers by reprogramming and antagonizing tumor cell‐intrinsic and ‐extrinsic inflammation, as observed in preclinical mouse models [21, 22]. However, the subsequent trial failed to show benefit with adding canakinumab to standard treatment for NSCLC [16]. Our study identified strong signals for various tumors in elderly patients, including basal cell carcinoma, colorectal cancer, and transitional cell carcinoma. CANTOS also reported a higher incidence of basal cell carcinoma in canakinumab groups compared with placebo, although the difference was not statistically significant [19]. Given the complex pathophysiology, the observed overproduction of IL‐1β in tumors and its clinical manifestations may not have a direct causal relationship in humans [23]. Therefore, the impact of canakinumab on cancer risk requires further exploration, and the potential risks of canakinumab in elderly patients at high risk of tumor formation should be carefully considered.

The most frequently reported AEs in elderly patients were pneumonia, sepsis, and cellulitis, which differed significantly from those seen in younger patients. Febrile neutropenia and septic shock were also among the top 10 AEs in this population. Skin and soft tissue infections (e.g., cellulitis, erysipelas) and abdominal and gastrointestinal infections (e.g., diverticulitis, peritonitis, cholecystitis, appendicitis) are not explicitly mentioned in the canakinumab prescribing information. Susceptibility, morbidity, and mortality to infectious diseases increase with age [24]. Compared to younger patients, elderly patients with infections tend to have more severe disease and require more treatment resources [25]. Elderly patients using biologic agents have a 2‐ to 3‐fold higher risk of serious infections after age 65 [26]. Previous canakinumab clinical trials also reported that participants who died from infections tended to be older [27].

As canakinumab continues to be explored for cardiovascular diseases and cancer, more elderly patients are expected to use the drug. Systematic prophylaxis and continuous monitoring of infections, as well as a clearer understanding of its effects on tumor growth and inhibition, are crucial.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the FAERS database is a global spontaneous reporting system with inherent limitations including underreporting, overreporting, misreporting, incomplete information, and nonstandardized data. The number of patients receiving canakinumab treatment who did not report AEs is unknown, and denominator data are lacking. Some AEs might be attributed to underlying diseases, making it challenging to establish a causal relationship between canakinumab and AEs or to calculate the true incidence of canakinumab‐related AEs. Moreover, this study relied solely on the FAERS database, and using a single data source inevitably limits the comprehensiveness and generalizability of the findings. Ideally, complementary data sources should be integrated to minimize the biases and reporting deficiencies inherent to any individual database. Future research should combine data from multiple pharmacovigilance systems, clinical pharmacology studies, and real‐world evidence to more thoroughly evaluate canakinumab‐related AEs and provide robust evidence to inform clinical practice and potential regulatory actions [28, 29]. Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable insights into the basic aspects of canakinumab‐related AEs across different age groups and may serve as a foundation for future rigorous prospective studies.

Conclusion

This comprehensive analysis of canakinumab‐related AEs using the FAERS database provides critical insights into the safety profile of canakinumab across different age groups. Patients in all age groups exhibited a high burden of infections and infestations, as well as respiratory, thoracic, and mediastinal disorders, highlighting the pervasive risk of these AEs regardless of age. Pediatric patients demonstrated additional risks, including dose‐related AEs and unique signals such as gastrointestinal and hepatobiliary disorders in patients < 12 years and leukopenia in adolescents (12–17 years), underscoring the need for vigilant monitoring and dose optimization in this population. Elderly patients (≥ 65 years), often prescribed canakinumab for off‐label indications like cardiovascular prophylaxis and NSCLC, showed significant risks of tumor formation and severe infections, emphasizing the importance of balancing therapeutic benefits with potential risks in this vulnerable group.

Temporal analysis revealed an early failure pattern in Weibull distribution modeling, indicating that many AEs occur soon after treatment initiation. However, a substantial proportion of AEs (approximately 40%) occurred ≥ 1 year after treatment initiation. This dual temporal pattern underscores the necessity for both short‐ and long‐term surveillance and proactive management of AEs throughout the treatment course.

Despite the inherent limitations of spontaneous reporting systems, this study identifies several underrecognized AEs, such as hepatobiliary disorders in children and tumors in elderly patients, which warrant further investigation. The signals detected using multiple disproportionality analysis methods provide a foundation for future prospective studies to validate these findings and refine risk mitigation strategies.

In conclusion, this study highlights the importance of age‐specific safety monitoring and long‐term pharmacovigilance for canakinumab. By addressing the unique risks associated with different age groups and recognizing the persistent risk of AEs during treatment, clinicians can optimize canakinumab use, improve patient outcomes, and enhance adherence. Future research should focus on elucidating the mechanisms underlying these AEs and developing targeted interventions to minimize risks while maximizing therapeutic benefits.
