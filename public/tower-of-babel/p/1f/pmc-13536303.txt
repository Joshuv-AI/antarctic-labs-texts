Self-perceived attractiveness of labiomental angles in Taiwanese and Caucasian males: A comparative study before and after orthodontic education among dental students
Authors: Shih-Chieh Chen, Chun-Ming Chen, Szu-Ting Chou, Fu-Jia Syu, HisHam Mamoon, Yu-Chuan Tseng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeThe labiomental angle plays a critical role in determining facial profile attractiveness, with aesthetic preferences influenced by ethnic background. This study investigated how variations in the labiomental angle influenced facial profile aesthetics and examined changes in dental students’ understanding of facial aesthetics before and after orthodontic lectures.Materials and methodsOne adult Taiwanese male and one adult Caucasian male were selected. Three-dimensional facial images were captured using a facial scanner and digitally modified to create labiomental angles ranging from 117° to 152° at 5° intervals. A total of 16 image sets, each consisting of five static images and one video, were generated. Eighty-eight dental students evaluated the images before and after orthodontic education. Attractiveness was assessed using a visual analogue scale, while acceptance was rated on a 3-point Likert scale.ResultsBefore the lectures, the most aesthetically preferred labiomental angle was 132° in Taiwanese males and 122° in Caucasian males. After the lectures, preferences shifted to 137° for the Caucasian male, while the Taiwanese male’s preference remained at 132°. Acceptance ratings followed a similar trend, with the most accepted angle shifting from 122° to 127° for the Caucasian male, while remaining at 132° for the Taiwanese male. Dental students demonstrated a statistically significant difference in their preferences for the labiomental angle when comparing Taiwanese and Caucasian males.ConclusionLabiomental angle preferences vary by cultural background. Orthodontic lectures can alter dental students’ aesthetic preferences for Caucasian male profiles but have minimal impact on preferences for Taiwanese male profiles.Keywords: Facial profile, Attractiveness, Acceptances, Labiomental angle, Orthodontic education

Introduction

Facial aesthetics is a multifaceted concept encompassing several factors, including facial symmetry, proportions, and overall harmony. In dentistry, aesthetics plays a critical role in both diagnosis and treatment planning. Orthodontics involves more than just correcting dental misalignment and malocclusion; it also strives to achieve facial balance and harmony.1,2 As a result, the desire to improve both facial and dental aesthetics is often a key motivation for patients seeking orthodontic treatment. Konstantonis et al.3 reported that dental alignment and occlusion significantly impact an individual’s facial aesthetics, reinforcing the role of orthodontic treatment as an aesthetic intervention. Therefore, understanding patients’ aesthetic expectations is essential in orthodontic treatment to ensure that treatment outcomes align with their attractiveness, ultimately enhancing patient satisfaction.

Research indicates that aesthetic standards vary between Asian and non-Asian populations.4,5 Furthermore, even within Asian communities, attractiveness can vary regarding appearances of both Asian and non-Asian individuals. 6 These findings highlight the importance of considering cultural differences in facial aesthetics when planning cosmetic procedures. In recent years, the use of 3D reconstructed imaging technology in dentistry has gained momentum.7 This technology enables more precise analysis and evaluation of facial structures, allowing clinicians to simulate and predict treatment outcomes in a virtual environment. Moreover, 3D imaging has become an essential tool for aesthetic assessments. By employing this technology, clinicians can effectively demonstrate changes in facial structures throughout the treatment process, improving patients’ understanding of treatment objectives and expected outcomes. The use of facial scanners in dentistry and orthodontics has also expanded,8–10 facilitating more accurate analysis and modification of labiomental fold dimensions. Nonetheless, there is a lack of research investigating the attractiveness of dental students in Taiwan regarding the labiomental fold.

This study investigated the relationship between facial aesthetics and the labiomental fold using facial scanning technology to reconstruct 3D facial images with varying labiomental angles, which were assessed for perceived attractiveness. By comparing adult Taiwanese and Caucasian male facial profiles, the study evaluated whether ethnic differences influenced aesthetic preferences among Taiwanese dental students. In addition, it examined whether orthodontic lectures enhanced dental students’ understanding of facial aesthetic evaluation across different ethnic groups.

Materials and methods

This study enrolled one adult Taiwanese male (Fig. 1) and one adult Caucasian male (Fig. 2), both with no history of facial trauma, plastic surgery, or orthognathic surgery. To assess changes in aesthetic perception and evaluation criteria, fourth-year dental students completed questionnaires at two time points—before and after receiving orthodontic education. This study design enabled the analysis of how orthodontic education influences students’ aesthetic concepts and assessment standards.

Taiwanese male: 5 images (117°), from right to left 90-degree profiles. Labiomental fold angle: 122°, 127°, 132°, 137°, 142°, 147°, and 152°.

Caucasian male: 5 images (117°), from right to left 90-degree profiles. Labiomental fold angle: 122°, 127°, 132°, 137°, 142°, 147°, and 152°.

The Accu3D face scanner and Accu3Dx pro software (Digident Image Technology Co., Taichung, Taiwan) were used to capture 3D facial data and facilitated data processing, analysis, and facial measurements. For both study subjects, the labiomental angle was adjusted to eight different values, ranging from 117° to 152°, with a new 3D facial model generated at 5° increments while maintaining the spatial relationship between the upper and lower lips and Rickett’s aesthetic line.11 The labiomental angle was decreased through mandibular retraction and chin advancement, whereas mandibular advancement and chin retraction increased the angle.

Additionally, screenshots were captured to generate images from five different angles: frontal view, left and right 90-degree lateral views, and left and right 45-degree oblique views. Following the methodology established by Tan et al.,12 screen recording was employed to create 10-s video clips for each 3D model. These clips consisted of rotations from the right 90-degree lateral view to the frontal view, and then to the left 90-degree lateral view. These images and video clips were provided to participants during the questionnaire assessment to aid in evaluating their aesthetic attractiveness. They completed the questionnaire before and after attending the orthodontics course. The questionnaire was time-limited to 30 min to allow for thoughtful consideration of responses and ensure timely completion. In order to reduce the risk of bias, participants were not provided with any detailed information regarding the alterations made to the facial features of the 3D models, both prior to and following the completion of the questionnaire.

The questionnaire consisted of three sections: demographic details, facial aesthetic attractiveness, and attitudes toward facial aesthetic acceptance.

Demographic Information: This section gathered background details on participants, including gender, age, and responses before and after attending the orthodontic lecture.

Demographic Information: This section gathered background details on participants, including gender, age, and responses before and after attending the orthodontic lecture.

Visual analogue scale (VAS): Participants viewed 3D images and videos featuring varying labiomental angles and rated the attractiveness of each image using a VAS ranging from 0 to 100.

Visual analogue scale (VAS): Participants viewed 3D images and videos featuring varying labiomental angles and rated the attractiveness of each image using a VAS ranging from 0 to 100.

Perceptions of abnormality and acceptability: For each image, dental students evaluated the perceived abnormality of the labiomental angle and their acceptance of the deviation. Responses were recorded using a Likert scale ranging from 1 (completely normal/fully acceptable) to 3 (highly abnormal/completely unacceptable), with lower scores indicating greater attractiveness.

Perceptions of abnormality and acceptability: For each image, dental students evaluated the perceived abnormality of the labiomental angle and their acceptance of the deviation. Responses were recorded using a Likert scale ranging from 1 (completely normal/fully acceptable) to 3 (highly abnormal/completely unacceptable), with lower scores indicating greater attractiveness.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to calculate the mean age of participants, VAS scores, and Likert scale scores. Comparisons of attractiveness and acceptance levels before and after the orthodontics course were analyzed using a paired-sample t-test. The null hypothesis stated that no significant difference existed in participants’ attractiveness and acceptance of the labiomental angle for Taiwanese and Caucasian males. A 95 % confidence interval was applied, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kaohsiung Medical University Hospital (KMUHIRB-SV(II)-20220053).

Results

A total of 88 valid questionnaires were collected in this study. Among the dental students, 55 were male with an average age of 21.8 years, and 33 were female with an average age of 22.2 years. Before attending the orthodontics course (Table 1), the VAS scores for the Taiwanese male subject ranged from 40.19 (152°) to 74.03 (122°). The 122° profile received the highest VAS score (72.48), while the 152° profile had the lowest. After completing the orthodontics course (Table 2), VAS scores ranged from 51.52 (117°) to 72.48 (132°), with the 132° profile receiving the highest score and the 117° profile the lowest. Before attending the orthodontics course (Table 3), the average Likert scale scores for the Taiwanese male subject ranged from 1.39 (132°) to 2.81 (152°). After completing the course (Table 4), the scores ranged from 1.47 (132°) to 2.72 (152°). This indicates that preference and acceptance coincided at 132° after the orthodontics course.

Before the orthodontics course, the 117° profile was the most preferred for the Caucasian male subject, whereas after the course, the preference shifted to the 137° profile. Meanwhile, the 152° profile consistently received the lowest VAS scores. Similarly, the Likert scale scores indicated that the 122° profile was perceived as the least abnormal before the course, whereas after the course, the 127° profile held this distinction, while the 152° profile remained the most abnormal in both instances. Before and after receiving orthodontic education, significant differences were observed in the VAS scores for the Taiwanese male subject at 122°, 127°, 137°, 147°, and 152°, with a significant difference in the Likert scale at 127°. For the Caucasian male subject, significant differences were found in the VAS scores at 117°, 132°, and 142°, while no significant differences were noted in the Likert scale scores. At the 127° angle in the Caucasian male profile (Table 4), the number of students showing increased acceptance (19 dental students) was approximately equal to those showing decreased acceptance (18 dental students), and many (51 dental students) show no change. Statistically, this indicates no overall directional change, resulting in a P-value of 1, which is reasonable and commonly observed in pre–post analyses using Likert scale data.

A comparison of aesthetic attractiveness and acceptance for specific labiomental angles in the Taiwanese and Caucasian male models is presented in Table 5. Before the orthodontic lectures, the VAS scores at 117°, 147°, and 152° were significantly higher for the Caucasian male than for the Taiwanese male. The Likert scale scores for the Caucasian male with angles of 122°, 132°, and 137° were significantly higher than those for the Taiwanese male. After the orthodontic lectures, the VAS score at 117° was significantly higher for the Caucasian male than for the Taiwanese male. The Likert scale scores for the Caucasian male with angles of 122°, 132°, and 137° remained significantly higher than those for the Taiwanese male. Given the significant differences observed in both the VAS and Likert scale scores between the Taiwanese and Caucasian male models, the null hypothesis was rejected.

Discussion

In order to establish population norms of angular and linear facial measurements across ethnicities, Wen et al.13 reviewed photogrammetric studies of Asian, African, and Caucasian populations who were college students or young adult volunteers. They reported that the mean labiomental angle in males was 134.8° (95 % confidence interval [CI]: 128.8°–140.4°) in Asian populations, 130.2° (95 % CI: 122.0°–138.4°) in African populations, and 128.6° (95 % CI: 124.3°–133.3°) in Caucasian populations. Overall, Caucasian males exhibited a smaller labiomental angle compared with African and Asian males. Therefore, cultural considerations and study populations are the importance to concern the ethnicity on aesthetic attractiveness of profile.

In our prior study,14 dental students identified the most appealing labiomental angle for a Taiwanese female model as 137°, according to VAS scores, which increased to 147° following lectures. For the Caucasian female model, the most favored angle remained at 117° before and after the lectures. Regarding acceptance measured by the Likert scale, the labiomental angle of 137° was consistently the most accepted for the Taiwanese female model, while the angle of 117° was preferred for the Caucasian female model. A significant difference in preferred labiomental angles was noted between Taiwanese and Caucasian female models. Taiwanese participants preferred a larger labiomental angle, as smaller angles were perceived to create an pronounced chin, while Caucasian females favored a smaller angle to enhance facial definition. Overall, the perceptions of attractiveness and acceptance regarding labiomental angles were significantly influenced by cultural background and the ethnicity of the assessed individual, and orthodontic lectures significantly influenced aesthetic perceptions.

Naini et al.15 conducted a study using the silhouette male Caucasian profile image and determined that the most attractive labiomental angle ranged from approximately 107°–118°. In present study, prior to orthodontic education, the highest VAS scores were 122° for the Taiwanese male and 117° for the Caucasian male. This may be attributable to media representations that often emphasize certain features associated with prevailing beauty standards, which can unconsciously shape individuals’ perceptions of attractiveness. In particular, a more protrusive lower chin may reflect a response to the frequent exposure to idealized images of Western celebrities or characters exhibiting similar traits, thereby influencing perceptions of attractiveness. Such media portrayals play a significant role in defining aesthetic ideals across various cultural demographics, serving as benchmarks for what is classified as “ideal” or “desirable.” Following the orthodontics lecture, the highest VAS scores increased to 132° for the Taiwanese male and 137° for the Caucasian male indicating the impact of formal education on students’ perceptions.

Before taking the orthodontics lecture, the lowest Likert scores were 132° for the Taiwanese male and 122° for the Caucasian male, suggesting a preference for a protrusive lower chin in Caucasian male. However, after orthodontic lectures, the lowest Likert score for the Taiwanese male subject remained at 132°, while for the Caucasian male subject, it increased to 127°. For Taiwanese male, VAS and Likert scores are consistent, indicating that their preference and acceptance are aligned. In contrast, for Caucasian male, the VAS score is 10° higher than the Likert score, suggesting a slight difference between their preference and acceptance. This may be attributed to the Likert scale’s limited three-response options, which could result in a tendency toward neutral responses. These findings suggest that a smaller labiomental angle in Caucasian males is still perceived as acceptable and normal. A possible explanation is that Caucasian facial features are generally more defined, with a prominent nose and pronounced chin, making a deeper labiomental fold appear more harmonious within their overall facial structure.

When comparing the same labiomental angle, from the perspective of VAS preference, before the lecture, students still significantly preferred Caucasian male at angles of 117°, 147°, and 152°. This suggests that both very small and very large jaw angles for Caucasian male are considered within the normal and acceptable range. This preference may be attributed to the more prominent facial contours of Caucasian male, including straighter noses and more pronounced or straighter chins, which may be particularly appealing to Asian students. However, after the lecture, there was no significant difference between Taiwanese and Caucasian males at angles of 147° and 152°, and students no longer significantly preferred Caucasian male. From the perspective of the Likert scale, the acceptance level was significantly lower among Taiwanese male compared to Caucasian male at angles of 117° and 152° both before and after the lecture. Based on the VAS and Likert results, it was found that Taiwanese male showed significantly greater preference and acceptance for appearance at angles of 122°, 132°, and 137° compared to Caucasian male. Meanwhile, Caucasian male had significantly higher preference and acceptance at 117°, which was greater than that of Taiwanese male.

Studies16–18 across various disciplines have directly compared the VAS and Likert scales, with results varying likely due to differences in study design and context. The VAS consists of a 10-cm straight line with two endpoints representing the minimum and maximum levels. This method16 allows for a continuous scale to assess subjective experiences, such as pain or aesthetic attractiveness, and provides a reliable way to measure varying degrees of intensity or preference. The Likert scale is widely used in health research17 as a psychometric response scale, typically ranging from 3 to 7 levels of agreement or disagreement. It is one of the most commonly applied scales in survey research, often utilized in questionnaires to assess respondents’ levels of agreement with given statements. However, the Likert scale may be influenced by various biases that can distort responses. For example, respondents may avoid selecting extreme options (central tendency bias) or habitually agree with statements regardless of their content (acquiescence bias).

When considering the advantages and limitations of both scales, the Likert scale is preferred for its ease of application and interpretation, whereas the VAS, with its more refined response levels, provides greater sensitivity in detecting small but significant changes. Nonetheless, when participants encountered challenges in providing precise scores on the VAS, they tended to align their responses more closely with the straightforward ratings on the Likert scale. In a study by Dourado et al.,18 both the VAS and the Likert scale were found effective in evaluating facial pleasantness, yielding similar results across evaluators from diverse professional backgrounds and genders. The Likert scale was favored by the general public, orthodontists, and surgeons, potentially due to its relative ease of use in recording responses and expressing opinions. Similar to report of Dourado et al.,18 it appears that our students generally prefer the Likert scale over the VAS for rating their preferences regarding facial profiles.

Choosing dental students as participants for this study offered both advantages and challenges relative to selecting laypeople.19 The advantages include the following: 1. Professional Expertise: Dental students possess relevant professional knowledge, enabling them to have a more nuanced understanding of facial aesthetics and orthodontic treatment. This proficiency enhances both the validity and reliability of the questionnaire, and their insights can offer valuable perspectives for orthodontic professionals. 2. Heightened Sensitivity: Dental students are often more adept at noticing subtle changes in facial features, allowing for a more accurate assessment of the aesthetic implications associated with varying angles of the labiomental fold. 3. Educational Motivation: Their specialized training fosters a greater interest and motivation towards the research topic among dental students. This enthusiasm is likely to improve both participation rates and the quality of responses in the questionnaire survey. 4. Controlled Variability: Operating within a common educational framework, dental students receive a relatively uniform level of instruction regarding orthodontic treatment. This consistency helps mitigate potential confounding variables, thereby bolstering the reliability of the study outcomes. There are two primary drawbacks to consider: 1. Potential for Bias: The educational backgrounds of dental students may foster preconceived notions regarding aesthetic standards that do not necessarily correspond to the views of the general populace. 2. Restricted Generalizability: Including dental students as participants may limit the broader applicability of the study’s findings, as their aesthetic attractiveness related to labiomental angles might not represent those of the general demographic.

Torul and Omezli20 evaluated the influence of education on facial profile esthetic perception and reported that dental students’ ratings differed significantly from those of laypersons, indicating that esthetic perception is influenced by education. Dourado et al.18 reported that laypeople assigned significantly higher facial pleasantness scores than orthodontists on both VAS and Likert scales. Based on these findings, we infer that the preference ratings of our students may fall between those of dental professionals and the general public. Although dental students may be stricter in their evaluations compared to the general public,12 it is important to note that individuals seeking orthodontic treatment, plastic surgery, or orthognathic surgery often adhere to higher aesthetic standards. As such, the aesthetic standards of dental students fall between those of the general public and professional practitioners, making their evaluations a valuable reference for clinical applications.

In the present study, the perspectives of dental students offer a significant comparative benchmark for aesthetic evaluations among diverse participant groups and contribute to the comprehension of patient orthodontic treatment needs and expectations. However, this study has several limitations: (1) the sample size was relatively small; (2) the three-point Likert scale may have been too restrictive to capture nuanced responses; and (3) the exclusion of lay participants may have affected how the results were interpreted.

In conclusion, regarding the labiomental angle, the most preferred angle for the Taiwanese male subject was 132°, while for the Caucasian male subject, it was 122°. In contrast, the least preferred angle for both the Taiwanese and Caucasian male subjects was 152°. A significant difference was observed in Taiwanese participants’ evaluations of labiomental angles between the Taiwanese and Caucasian male subjects. Before and after participating in the orthodontics lecture, dental students showed heightened sensitivity to the labiomental angle of the Taiwanese male subject, resulting in a greater polarization of attractiveness - stronger approval of favored angles and increased disapproval of less favored angles. This finding highlights the influence of ethnicity on the perception of facial features.
