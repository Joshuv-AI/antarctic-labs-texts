Improvement in health-related quality of life after complex biventricular repair
Authors: Kathryn O. Stack, Kimberlee Gauvreau, Sarah Campos, Michael S. Bamgbose, Elizabeth D. Blume, Sitaram M. Emani, Ryan L. Kobayashi, Rebecca S. Beroukhim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveComplex biventricular (BiV) and one-and-a-half ventricle (1.5V) repairs are alternatives to single ventricle palliation. We describe 1 domain of the health-related quality of life of patients undergoing BiV and 1.5V repairs.MethodsThis single-center study included children (≤ 18 years) who underwent BiV or 1.5V repairs between October 1, 2022, and July 7, 2024. Parents completed Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory Infant Scales or Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory 3.0 Cardiac Modules; physical function and physical symptom scores for patients younger than 2 years and heart problems and treatment scores for patients 2 years and older were evaluated.ResultsSeventy-three patients, 36 younger than 2 years and 37 ages 2 years and older, were included. Forty-one patients (56%) underwent BiV surgery, and 32 (44%) underwent 1.5V surgery. Preoperatively, patients younger than 2 years had a mean physical symptom score of 76 ± 14 and a mean physical function score of 70 ± 20; patients ages 2 years and older had a mean heart problems and treatment score of 64 ± 20. Thirty-six patients, 10 younger than 2 years and 26 ages 2 years and older, had paired pre- and postoperative health-related quality of life data. Following repair, physical symptom scores improved among infants ages 0 to 12 months (mean, 67 vs 79; P = .033), and heart problems and treatment scores improved (ages 2 to 4 years, 69 vs 84; P = .002; ages 5 to 7 years, 57 vs 86; P = .044). For patients ages 2 years and older, 5 symptoms drove score improvement: exertional dyspnea, chest pain, tachycardia, cyanosis, and fatigue.ConclusionsSix months after surgery, parents reported improvement in children's health-related quality of life following complex BiV or 1.5V repairs. Further work is needed to better understand long-term outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Improvement in health-related quality of life among patients ages 2 years and older after complex BiV or 1.5V repair.

Central Message

Parents report improvement in children's health-related quality of life (physical symptom and heart problems and treatment scores) after complex biventricular or one-and-a-half ventricular repairs.

Perspective

Complex biventricular and one-and-a-half ventricular repairs are alternatives to single ventricle palliation that aim to mitigate the sequelae of Fontan physiology. Mortality and clinical end points alone are insufficient to define patient outcomes. Health-related quality of life is a crucial outcome, and contextualizing patients' scores may contribute to patient selection and long-term management.

Complex biventricular (BiV) and one-and-a-half ventricle (1.5V) repairs are alternatives to single-ventricle palliation that aim to mitigate the long-term sequelae of Fontan physiology. By completely or partially eliminating passive pulmonary blood flow, creating a pulsatile path for hepatic blood flow to the lungs, and improving ventricular volume loading conditions, complex BiV and 1.5V repairs aim to augment cardiac output, improve cyanosis, and attenuate the repercussions of chronic venous congestion observed in patients with Fontan circulations.1 Because complex BiV and 1.5V repairs are relatively new surgical strategies, long-term outcomes remain unknown. Favorable short-term mortality and clinical outcomes, such as lower risk of liver dysfunction, have been reported.1, 2, 3, 4

However, mortality and clinical end points alone are insufficient to define outcomes, and clinical measures may fail to capture patients’ symptoms and functional limitations.5,6 With the shift toward a more holistic definition of health,7 health-related quality of life (HRQOL) measurements have become increasingly important outcomes.8, 9, 10, 11 For patients with congenital heart disease, a negative association between disease complexity and HRQOL has been described.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 Patients with single-ventricle physiology report not only the lowest HRQOL scores12,14,15 but also declining quality of life with age.18

As part of the evaluation of complex BiV and 1.5V repairs, it is incumbent to assess these surgical strategies' influence on patients’ quality of life. This study focuses on 1 HRQOL domain, physical functioning or symptomatology. The purpose of this study was 2-fold: to describe the physical functioning of patients undergoing complex BiV and 1.5V repairs and to evaluate the influence of these repairs on short-term physical functioning scores.

Methods

This single-center retrospective cohort study was an ancillary project to the Biventricular Parent-Reported Outcomes Initiative (BiV-PRO), a quality improvement initiative that collects longitudinal health information about patients who underwent complex BiV repair at Boston Children's Hospital (BCH). BCH's institutional review board approved this study and granted a consent waiver (IRB-P00049238) for secondary research on November 11, 2024. BiV-PRO enrollment occurs at time of surgery: BiV staging, 1.5V repair, BiV repair, or reoperation after BiV repair. Parents complete the outcomes survey at enrollment and every 6 months thereafter.

HRQOL Data

As part of BiV-PRO, parents complete either Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory Infant Scales (InfantQL) or Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory 3.0 Cardiac Modules (Cardiac PedsQL) based on their child's age at the time of survey administration. InfantQL is a validated, age-specific (1-12 or 13-24 months), parent-reported HRQOL survey that assesses 5 domains: physical functioning, physical symptoms, emotional functioning, social functioning, and cognitive functioning.19 Cardiac PedsQL is a validated modular instrument, specific to children with heart disease, that evaluates 5 domains: symptoms, perceived physical appearance, treatment anxiety, cognitive problems, and communication.20 Cardiac PedsQL is age-specific, with cohorts ages 2 to 4 years, 5 to 7 years, 8 to 12 years, and 13 to 18 years.20 The surveys are patient-report and/or parent-proxy-report.20 Because most patients undergo complex BiV repair before age 5 years, BiV-PRO administered only parent-proxy-report surveys.

For both InfantQL and Cardiac PedsQL, scores are generated for each domain. Questions are answered using a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never a problem to 4 = almost always a problem).19,20 Responses are reversed-scored and linearly transformed to a 0 to 100 scale, with higher scores indicating better HRQOL.19,20 In this study, we report InfantQL physical function and physical symptom scores for patients younger than age 2 years and Cardiac PedsQL heart problems and treatment scores for patients ages 2 years and older.

Patient Selection

All patients who underwent surgery through the BCH Complex Biventricular Repair Program were eligible for enrollment in BiV-PRO. The population of this ancillary study included only those who underwent 1.5V or BiV repair at enrollment and completed at least the baseline, preoperative BiV-PRO survey. Patients were excluded if their parents were non-English speaking. BiV-PRO surveys completed between October 1, 2022, and July 29, 2024, were analyzed.

Data Collection

Patients' demographic, clinical, imaging, surgical, and procedural information was collected from BCH's prospectively maintained Complex Biventricular Repair Program surgical database and review of patients' electronic medical records. HRQOL data were collected from completed BiV-PRO surveys.

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized for the entire cohort and stratified by InfantQL versus Cardiac PedsQL respondents. Categorical variables were summarized using counts and percentages; continuous variables were summarized using medians and ranges. HRQOL scores—physical function score, physical symptom score, and heart problems and treatment score—are displayed as mean ± SD. For patients who repeated the same survey (InfantQL vs Cardiac PedsQL within the same age cohort) preoperatively and 6 months postoperatively, changes between pre- and postoperative HRQOL scores were evaluated using paired t test. Analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 (StataCorp LLC).

Results

Between September 2022 and May 2024, 105 patients enrolled in BiV-PRO and completed at least the baseline, preoperative survey. Thirty-two patients were excluded because they underwent BiV recruitment surgery (n = 25) or reoperation after BiV repair (n = 7). The final study cohort included 73 patients who underwent either 1.5V or BiV repair as their index operation at enrollment.

BiV Surgical Repair Characteristics

The median age at repair was 2.1 years, and the median weight was 10.8 kg. Thirty-two patients (44%) underwent 1.5V repair, and 41 patients (56%) underwent BiV repair (Table 1). Patients spent a median of 6 days in the cardiac intensive care unit postoperatively, and the median length of hospital stay was 11 days.

Postoperative HRQOL Measurements

Of 73 patients included in this study, 36 patients (n = 10 younger than age 2 years; n = 26 ages 2 years and older) had comparable pre- and 6-month postoperative HRQOL data; that is, their parents repeated the same survey (InfantQL vs Cardiac PedsQL within the same age cohort) at baseline and 6 months postoperatively (Table 3). Thirty-seven patients lacked comparable pre- and postoperative results: 11 patients aged into a different survey cohort, 6 patients underwent their index operation within the final 6 months of the study period, and 20 patients’ families were lost to follow-up. Among patients with comparable data, increases in HRQOL scores were seen in several groups: InfantQL physical symptoms scores increased for infants younger than 12 months; and Cardiac PedsQL heart problems and treatment scores increased among children ages 2 to 4 years and 5 to 7 years (Table 3). InfantQL physical symptom scores for patients ages 13 to 24 months and InfantQL physical function scores for patients ages 0 to 12 and 13 to 24 months also increased but did not reach statistical significance (Table 3). Discussion

Assessing patients’ HRQOL following complex 1.5V or BiV repair provides meaningful insight into patient outcomes and may contribute to patient selection and long-term management. To our knowledge, this study is the first to report on physical functioning HRQOL in patients undergoing complex BiV repair and among the first to report changes in HRQOL scores after congenital heart disease surgery. Following complex BiV repair, there were significant improvements in InfantQL physical function scores among infants younger than 12 months and Cardiac PedsQL heart problems and treatment scores for all analyzed age cohorts, with additional trends toward improvement in InfantQL physical function scores among infants ages 13 to 24 months and InfantQL physical symptom scores among all infants. Evaluating the components of these scores, parents reported significant improvement in 5 symptoms: exertional dyspnea, chest pain, tachycardia, cyanosis, and fatigue. Mean postoperative HRQOL scores remained stable throughout the follow-up period.

Concordant with prior work investigating HRQOL scores in children with complex congenital heart disease,12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 parent-reported preoperative physical function, physical symptom, and heart problems and treatment scores were low. Factors hypothesized to contribute to patients' scores include disease severity, residual lesions, and morbidity associated with increased number of procedures, surgeries, and days in the hospital.15 Our cohort's mean preoperative Cardiac PedsQL heart problems and treatment scores of 64 ± 20 fell within the range (42.7-85.7) reported for patients with severe congenital heart disease.12,13,17,21 Considering most patients in our cohort initially were palliated down the single-ventricle pathway, the severe category is a generally comparable cohort.

Following complex BiV or 1.5V surgery, there was a significant increase in parent-reported Cardiac PedsQL heart problems and treatment scores among all analyzable age groups. Additionally, the mean postoperative heart problems and treatment scores exceeded much of the previously reported norms for patients with the most severe congenital heart disease, except for the 8- to 12-year-old cohort, which included only 1 patient.12,13,17,21 In the context of the limitations of our retrospective study, these observed improvements support the hypothesis motivating the complex BiV repair strategy—restoring a more physiologic circulation improves patients' physical functioning. One-and-a-half ventricular and BiV repairs establish parallel pulmonary and systemic circulations thereby increasing many patients' baseline oxygen saturations and augmenting their circulations' ability to increase pulmonary blood flow with activity. The symptoms for which parents reported the most improvement; that is, those driving the increase in heart problems and treatment scores, were exertional dyspnea, chest pain, tachycardia, cyanosis, and fatigue. These are symptoms elicited with challenges requiring increases in cardiac output. Thus, improving physiology with 1.5V and BiV repairs may enhance patients’ ability to augment cardiac output and oxygen delivery, thereby improving subjective physical functioning.

There was a similar trend toward improvement in InfantQL scores following 1.5V or BiV repair, although only the physical function score for infants younger than 12 months reached statistical significance. The lack of statistical significance may be secondary to the small size of the infant cohort. Alternatively, it may reflect differences in survey methodology. The Cardiac PedsQL is specific to patients with heart disease and assesses symptoms, such as cyanosis and tachycardia, not captured in the generic InfantQL. Components of the heart problems and treatment score in which parents reported improvement (eg, cyanosis, exertional dyspnea, and fatigue) directly assess the physiologic changes associated with complex BiV repairs. The specificity of the Cardiac PedsQL may better capture subtle improvements in patients' functioning. Finally, it is possible that the degree of change in HRQOL scores is an age-related phenomenon. Infants are inherently less active than toddlers and children. The physical limitations of single-ventricle physiology secondary to chronic cyanosis or passive pulmonary blood flow, for example, become most pronounced with activity. Parents of toddlers and children may appreciate a greater improvement in their child's physical functioning after 1.5V or BiV repair compared with parents of infants.

The improvement in reported HRQOL scores following complex 1.5V or BiV repairs demonstrated short-term durability. The mean, cross-sectional, postoperative HRQOL scores remained relatively stable over the 2- year study period. Tracking the long-term stability of HRQOL scores is critical in assessing the durability of these repairs. Some studies assessing HRQOL in adolescents and young adults with Fontan circulations raise concern that HRQOL declines with age.18 For example, Uzark and colleagues18 reported a predicted average 0.76-point decline in Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory 4.0 Generic Core Scales physical function score per year among adolescents younger than 19 years; the predicted decline in physical functioning was steeper for young adults ages 19 years and older at 2 points per year. It will be decades before equivalent long-term HRQOL data are available for complex BiV repair populations. However, it is critical to incorporate such data into the evaluation of this surgical approach.

Study Limitations

This study faces several limitations. First, as a retrospective, single institution study, it faces limitations inherent to retrospective reviews. Second, the study's relatively small sample size may limit the ability to detect changes in HRQOL scores. Third, the lack of a universal, age-indiscriminate HRQOL instrument limits comparability across age groups and over time. Fourth, although the Cardiac PedsQL validation studies demonstrated strong correlations between patient-reported and parent-proxy-reported scores,17 the use of parent-proxy-reports in BiV-PRO risks introducing score bias—possible confounding from surgical strategy validation or children's developmental progress.22 Fifth, this study lacks a control group, and there are no published normative Cardiac PedsQL scores—either for healthy children or patients with single-ventricle physiology—against which to compare this cohort's postoperative improvement. Sixth, including only patients with English-speaking parents limits the study's generalizability. Finally, it is impossible to determine the effect of survey nonresponders.

Conclusions

Parents report an improvement in children's physical functioning and symptom HRQOL following complex 1.5V or BiV repair. Future work should investigate longer-term outcomes and compare HRQOL scores of patients who underwent complex BiV repair to those with competing surgical strategies (eg, Fontan and heart transplant).
