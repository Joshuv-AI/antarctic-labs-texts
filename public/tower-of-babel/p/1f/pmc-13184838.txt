Iron Deficiency Screening and Treatment in Hospitalized HFrEF Patients

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIron deficiency affects up to half of patients with heart failure and is associated with worse symptoms, quality of life, and clinical outcomes, even in the absence of anemia. Contemporary guidelines recommend routine screening and intravenous (IV) iron therapy for eligible patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), yet real-world adoption remains limited.Project RationaleGiven persistently low screening and treatment rates, we sought to improve inpatient iron deficiency screening and IV iron use among patients hospitalized with acute HFrEF at a community teaching hospital.Project SummaryWe implemented a multifaceted quality-improvement intervention incorporating an IV iron order set, targeted provider education, and unit-based visual reminders. Compared with a 6-month preintervention period, iron deficiency screening increased from 28.7% to 40.6%, and overall IV iron use more than doubled from 8.5% to 17.5%.Take-Home MessagesSystem-based interventions can meaningfully improve the delivery of guideline-directed care in hospitalized heart failure patients. Continued efforts are needed to address remaining gaps and ensure sustained adoption of these guidelines.Key words: acute heart failure, iron metabolism disorders, systolic heart failure

Iron deficiency is found in up to 59% of patients with heart failure (HF) and is associated with higher mortality rates among this population, even in patients who are not anemic.1,2 It is also associated with worse symptoms and quality of life, as well as decreased exercise capacity.3

Clinical trials have shown variable results on outcomes of treating iron deficiency in HF patients. However, a recent large meta-analysis of 6 randomized trials (FAIR-HF, CONFIRM-HF, AFFIRM-AHF, IRONMAN, HEART-FID, and FAIR-HF2), encompassing more than 7,000 patients, suggested that intravenous iron use was associated with lower rates of recurrent HF hospitalizations and cardiovascular mortality, with the greatest treatment effects observed during the first 12 months after therapy.4

Current guidelines from the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) endorse intravenous iron supplementation for patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) who have iron deficiency, irrespective of the presence of anemia, to improve functional capacity and quality of life. This intervention carries a Class 2a recommendation in the AHA/ACC guidelines, whereas the ESC guidelines assign a Class 1 recommendation.5 The definition of iron deficiency that is commonly used in this population is serum ferritin <100 or ferritin 100 to 299 with a transferrin saturation (Tsat) of <20%.5

Project Rationale

Despite the growing evidence regarding the use of intravenous iron in HF patients with iron deficiency, important gaps remain regarding its real-world use. Some registries demonstrated <25% screening rates for iron deficiency among HF patients.6 Accordingly, we designed and implemented a quality improvement (QI) project aimed to increase iron deficiency screening and intravenous iron use in hospitalized patients with acute HF at our institution.

Project Description

This QI initiative was held at a large community teaching hospital. The project was reviewed and approved by the hospital's Institutional Review Board. We implemented a multifaceted QI intervention to increase iron deficiency screening and intravenous iron use in patients hospitalized with acute HFrEF. This initiative serves as an important addition to the comprehensive protocols for guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) at our institution, including a dedicated HF unit, an HF admission order set with evidence-based GDMT and diuretic dosing recommendations, standardized documentation to track GDMT optimization, nursing checklists for discharge planning, and palliative care involvement when appropriate.

The patient population included all adult patients with a primary diagnosis of acute HFrEF and its variants (eg, acute systolic heart failure, acute on chronic systolic heart failure, acute-on-chronic HFrEF), with the exclusion of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. We applied filters to identify patients who received inpatient iron panel testing and those who received intravenous iron therapy. The intravenous iron formulations available at our institution were ferric gluconate and iron sucrose.

The preintervention period spanned 6 months from January 1, 2025, through June 30, 2025. The intervention was implemented on July 1, 2025, with postintervention data over 6 months from July 1, 2025, through December 31, 2025.

Project Deliverables

The intervention consisted of 3 components: 1) an intravenous iron order set incorporated into the HF admission order set with a prompt to order ferritin level and iron panel on admission; 2) educational posters placed at every workstation in the telemetry, HF, and cardiac critical care units; and 3) educational presentations during resident lectures and email communications to clinical staff including cardiologists, hospitalists, residents, physician assistants, and nurse practitioners. We used SlicerDicer (Epic Electronic Health Record system), a self-serve data exploration tool, to track baseline and postintervention outcomes.

Project Outcome, Impact, and Future Directions/Next Steps

During the preintervention period, there were 635 total HF hospitalizations. Iron deficiency screening was performed in 182 patients (28.7%). Among patients screened with iron panels, 52 received intravenous iron therapy (28.6% of those screened). Only 2 patients who did not undergo screening received intravenous iron (0.4% of unscreened patients). Overall, 54 of 635 patients (8.5%) received intravenous iron therapy during the preintervention period.

After implementation of the QI intervention (postintervention), there were 593 total HF hospitalizations. Iron panels were checked in 241 patients (40.6%), representing an absolute increase of 11.9 percentage points compared with the preintervention period. Among patients screened with iron panels, 97 received intravenous iron therapy (40.2% of those screened), an absolute increase of 11.6 percentage points compared with the preintervention screening. Seven patients who did not undergo screening received intravenous iron (2.0% of unscreened patients).

Overall, 104 of 593 patients (17.5%) received intravenous iron therapy during the postintervention period, representing an absolute increase of 9.0 percentage points and a 106% relative increase in overall intravenous iron use compared with the preintervention period (Table 1).

Discussion

The pathophysiology of iron deficiency in HF remains unclear. Functional iron deficiency anemia, characterized by adequate iron stores but limited availability given elevated serum hepcidin levels such as chronic inflammatory conditions, is hypothesized. Absolute iron deficiency anemia, on the other hand, arises from factors such as insufficient dietary intake, gastrointestinal bleeding associated with antiplatelet medication use, and impaired iron absorption due to proton pump inhibitor use.7 Nevertheless, evidence has strongly supported treating iron deficiency in patients admitted with HF exacerbation.

Our intervention used ferric gluconate and iron sucrose formulations, which are available at our institution. This differs from ferric carboxymaltose and derisomaltose, which were used in the major randomized controlled trials.4 However, it was demonstrated that different intravenous iron formulations produced similar clinical benefits among HF patients. The dosages recommended in our intervention included ferric gluconate 125 mg daily or iron sucrose 200 mg daily for up to 5 days. While ferric carboxymaltose and derisomaltose allow for significantly higher single-dose administration (up to 1,000 mg), the fundamental benefit of intravenous iron appears consistent across different formulations. Nevertheless, ferric carboxymaltose was the most frequently studied and utilized formulation.4,8

The observed improvements in our study in screening and treatment rates are likely to translate into improved patient outcomes given the growing evidence of the benefit of appropriate intravenous iron repletion. The AFFIRM-AHF trial particularly showed that intravenous iron initiated during or shortly after hospitalization for acute HF reduced total HF hospitalizations by 26% over 52 weeks. Moreover, in the most recent meta-analysis on this topic, Anker et al4 in 2025 demonstrated a 28% reduction in the composite of recurrent HF admissions and cardiovascular mortality at 12 months. Additionally, the decrease in total hospitalizations from the first half to the second half of the year in our sample is consistent with well-established seasonal variation in HF admissions, which peak during winter months and reach their lowest point during summer months.9,10

Despite these improvements, substantial gaps in care remain. Nearly 60% of our patient sample still did not receive eligible screening. Iron deficiency screening and treatment have not yet been incorporated at an institutional level, and implementation remains subject to individual provider preference and clinical judgment. This variability may be further influenced by the current Class 2a recommendation status in the AHA/ACC guidelines, which may lead some providers to view iron deficiency management as discretionary rather than mandatory in the acute setting. The temporal spacing of our educational interventions over the 6-month period may have also resulted in waning awareness over time. Barriers to screening may have included the presence of recent iron panels before admission and competing clinical priorities during acute hospitalization. Additional barriers to intravenous iron administration may have included contraindications such as sepsis or documented hypersensitivity reactions, as well as delays or failures in reviewing iron panel results. However, it is important to note that our figures only represented inpatient intravenous iron administration and did not include postdischarge treatment, which likely makes the treatment rates higher than reported.

Room for improvement remains, with future interventions focusing on consolidating the iron panel as a default component of HF admission order sets, implementing automated best-practice alerts in the electronic health record, and expanding inpatient educational efforts to more frequent reinforcement such as monthly emails or recurrent lectures. It would also be beneficial to incorporate iron deficiency assessment and treatment recommendations into discharge planning to ensure continuity of care in the outpatient setting, as well as to analyze outpatient data. Future QI efforts should also analyze service-specific variation (eg, resident vs hospitalist teams) to identify targeted opportunities for education.

Conclusions

The successful implementation of this evidence-based intervention at a community hospital demonstrates that guideline-directed care can be effectively delivered across diverse practice settings, not just academic medical centers. Our intervention increased iron deficiency screening and intravenous iron use in hospitalized HFrEF patients, but gaps remain. Continued QI efforts, including standardized order sets, alerts, more frequent educational reinforcement, and integration into discharge planning, are needed to sustain improvement.
