Successful Preservation of the Penis After en Bloc Resection of Locally Recurrent Rectal Cancer Involving the Left Crus of the Corpus Cavernosum and Corpus Spongiosum
Authors: Tomonori Kanagawa, Masaya Ito, Misato Takao, Masahiro Toide, Shuichiro Kobayashi, Daisuke Nakano, Kazushige Kawai, Fumitaka Koga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionWe report a case of locally recurrent rectal cancer involving the penile base successfully treated with penis‐preserving salvage surgery.Case PresentationA 63‐year‐old man underwent abdominoperineal resection for locally advanced rectal cancer, followed by three metastasectomies for inguinal lymph node recurrences and a pulmonary metastasis. Thirty‐four months later, intrapelvic recurrences involving the seminal vesicle, prostate, left crus of corpus cavernosum penis (CCP), and corpus spongiosum (CS) developed. Introduction

Locally advanced rectal cancer rarely recurs or metastasizes to the penile structures [1, 2]. Although total penectomy may be considered for such recurrence, it compromises the patient's body image and quality of life [3]. We present a case of locally recurrent rectal cancer involving the prostate and the penile base, successfully treated with penis‐preserving salvage surgery.

Case Report

A 63‐year‐old man underwent a laparoscopic abdominoperineal resection with lymph node dissection for locally advanced rectal cancer, which was pathologically adenocarcinoma pT3N1b with negative margins. He underwent three metastasectomies for inguinal lymph node recurrences and a pulmonary metastasis within 28 months. Two intrapelvic recurrences developed 34 months later: a 2‐cm mass involving the prostate gland, the left crus of corpus cavernosum penis (CCP), and the corpus spongiosum (CS) and a 3‐cm mass involving the left seminal vesicle (Figure 1A,B). He declined the proposal of cystoprostatectomy with total penectomy and urinary diversion. Alternatively, he received chemotherapy with six cycles of capecitabine, oxaliplatin, and bevacizumab, resulting in a partial remission for one year (Figure 1C,D). He could not continue the chemotherapy due to adverse events and accepted salvage surgery with urinary diversion but declined total penectomy. Following chemoradiotherapy (CRT, 50 Gy in 25 fractions) to the pelvic floor with capecitabine, salvage surgery was scheduled at one‐month intervals. Based on preoperative imaging findings, we planned cystoprostatectomy with en bloc partial resection of the left crus of CCP and proximal CS to preserve the penis.

Positron emission tomography of the local recurrences before (A and B) and after chemotherapy (C and D). Two local recurrences were observed; one involved the prostate, left corpus cavernosum penis and corpus spongiosum (A) while the other involved the left seminal vesicle (B). A partial and complete remission was achieved for the respective lesion by systemic chemotherapy (C and D).

First, open cystoprostatectomy was done. Bilateral internal iliac arteries (IIA) but not the accessory pudendal artery (APA) were identified. Meticulous care was taken not to injure the main trunk of the right IIA branching off the IPA distally, which supplied the blood flow to the right CCP (Figure 2A). As the IPA arises as the terminal branch of IIA and passes through the inferior piriformis foramen, traumatic manipulations on the posterior pelvic floor were avoided along the anticipated anatomical course of the right IPA. The perineal resection margins comprised the medial border of the right crus of CCP, the perineal bulbar urethra, and the left CCP at the level of the caudal border of the pubic bone (Figure 3). A longitudinal incision was made to isolate and transect the perineal bulbar urethra. After identifying the bilateral crus of CCP, the medial border of the right crus of CCP was carefully dissected to expose the right surface of the proximal CS. Excessive coagulation of the penile base on the right side was avoided to preserve the right IPA. The left CCP was divided at the level of the caudal border of the pubic bone. The dorsal vein complex and soft tissues covering the left CCP and the proximal CS were dissected from the pubic bone and resected. The left crus of CCP was separated from its attachment to the sciatic bone (Figure 3). Following en bloc resection of the perineal muscular structures, the surgical specimen was extracted. Finally, ileal conduit was made. The total operative time was 466 min with an estimated blood loss of 2640 mL, requiring a blood transfusion of 1120 mL.

Right internal pudendal artery (IPA) visualized on preoperative dynamic contrast‐enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (A) and postoperative enhanced computed tomography (B). The bilateral corpus cavernosum penis was enhanced with a contrast medium postoperatively (B).

Schema of the transperineal surgery. (1) Dividing the corpus spongiosum and exposing the bilateral crus of corpus cavernosum penis; (2) Dissecting the medial side of the right crus of the corpus cavernosum penis; and (3) Dividing the left corpus cavernosum penis and separating its crus from the sciatic bone.

Histologically, viable tumor cells extensively infiltrated the prostate and CS but not the left crus of CCP. Surgical margins were negative. Neither microsatellite instability nor mutation in KRAS, NRAS, or BRAF was detected.

Postoperative, contrast‐enhanced computed tomography confirmed preservation of the penile and IPA blood flow (Figure 2B). Nine months after the salvage surgery, the patient developed pulmonary metastases, which progressed despite three lines of chemotherapy. Although he deceased from the disease in postoperative month 23 (80 months after the primary surgery), intrapelvic recurrence had not been observed.

Discussion

In the present case, the penis was successfully preserved after surgical extirpation of locally recurrent rectal cancer involving the unilateral crus of CCP and the proximal CS. Preservation of the contralateral IPA blood supply to the CCP was the key to success. Although the penile blood flow is mainly supplied by branches of the IPA, anatomical variants are common [4, 5, 6]. Doupy et al. proposed three types of penile arterial blood supply: Type I (the IPA only), II (both the IPA and APA), and III (the APA only) [4]. According to a meta‐analysis of 563 pooled subjects, the incidence of Type I/II/III was 61.9%/32.8%/5.4% [6], indicating that the arterial blood supply rarely derives exclusively from the APA. The APA most commonly originated in the obturator artery, followed by the inferior vesical artery but rarely the external iliac artery or its branches [6].

The IPA terminates in four branches supplying blood to the CCP: the bulbourethral artery, urethral artery (UA), cavernous artery (CA), and dorsal artery of the penis [7]. These arteries form an extensive arterial network between the CCP and CS via anastomoses between the CA and the spongious arterial network [8] and anastomoses between the CA and UA [9]. Figure 4 shows the presumed postoperative arterial network of the penis in the present case, where branches of the right IPA other than the CA might have been severed intraoperatively. Successful preservation of the penile blood flow in the present case underscores the fact that the unilateral IPA can supply blood to the distal CS and the contralateral CCP via extensive arterial networks even if the contralateral crus of CCP and the proximal CS are resected.

In most reported cases of rectal cancer involving the penis, penile lesions manifested as one of multiple metastases and surgical resection was not considered [1, 10]. We could not find reports on penis‐preserving curative surgery for locally recurrent rectal cancer involving the penile base in the literature.

In the present case, preoperative CRT was administered to improve local tumor control and eventually to achieve curative resection. According to the Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum guidelines and retrospective studies, CRT can be considered in selected cases where tumor shrinkage is expected to facilitate margin‐negative resection [11, 12, 13, 14]. A retrospective study of 565 patients with locally recurrent rectal cancer showed a higher curative resection rate for preoperative CRT (43%) than no CRT (26%) [15]. Preoperative CRT did not complicate the surgical procedure at least in the present case.

Conclusion

The present case demonstrated successful preservation of the penis after en bloc curative resection of locally recurrent rectal cancer involving the left crus of CCP and CS. A unilateral IPA can supply blood to the contralateral CCP and the CS via extensive arterial networks between CCP and CS.
