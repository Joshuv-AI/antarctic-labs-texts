Rapid germ line testing with hair root bulb-derived DNA for hereditary predisposition and inherited marrow failure states
Authors: Emma C St Martin, Abhishek A Mangaonkar, Ann Moyer, Juliana Perez Botero, Alejandro Ferrer, Clifford Csizmar, Ross Rowsey, Benjamin Kipp, Dong Chen, Talha Badar, Jenna Fernandez, Yael Kusne, Christy Finke, Jodi Rindflesch, Terra Lasho, Sean Harrington, Mrinal M Patnaik
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

Approximately 5% to 10% of patients with myeloid neoplasms (MN) have underlying germ line predisposition states (GPS) or inherited bone marrow failure syndromes (IBMFS).1,2 Individuals with GPS/IBMFS should be identified before the development of MN so that they receive appropriate hematologic monitoring and cancer screening. For example, patients with Fanconi anemia require periodic blood counts and bone marrow (BM) biopsies in addition to comprehensive skin examinations, magnetic resonance imaging of the breast, and endoscopies.3 In cases of malignant transformation, timely diagnosis of underlying GPS/IBMFS has critical implications in management, including selection of anticancer therapies, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) donors, and conditioning regimens.

Current specimens for hematologic germ line testing include peripheral blood (PB), saliva or buccal swabs, and skin biopsies. However, these approaches have limitations. Given the overlap between GPS and somatic driver mutations in MN (eg, GATA2, RUNX1, etc), PB samples should not be used for germ line confirmation; however, clinicians sometimes make a germ line estimation based on variant allele fractions (VAF) in the heterozygous range (35%-60%). VAF estimates can be confounded by somatic copy number changes, especially copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity and structural variants, resulting in erroneous classification of somatic events as germ line.4 Saliva or buccal swabs can be used as alternatives, but DNA found in saliva is mostly white blood cell-derived,5 and buccal samples can also be contaminated with blood cells, particularly in patients with MN who may have thrombocytopenia, oral sores, and malignant cell infiltration into the oral mucosa.6 Furthermore, somatic alterations in the blood, including clonal hematopoiesis,7,8 acquired aberrant clonal expansions,9 and circulating tumor fractions,10,11 have all been detected in saliva samples, raising concern about the routine use of saliva/buccal swabs for GPS testing. Skin biopsy with fibroblast culture-derived DNA remains the gold standard, but the process is expensive, takes ∼4 weeks to complete, and can fail in some patients with telomere biology disorders.12 In addition, skin biopsy is an invasive procedure with rare bleeding and infectious risks, particularly in patients with MN with cytopenias.13 Given these drawbacks and the need to promptly screen donors for allogeneic HSCT and adjust therapy in certain GPS/IBMFS, we developed a rapid hematologic germ line testing algorithm using hair root bulb (HRB)–derived DNA.

This study was conducted after institutional review board approval through the Mayo Clinic GPS/IBMFS Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. To perform next-generation sequencing (NGS)- and Sanger sequencing–based HRB testing, we collected 20 to 30 hair follicles per patient using sterile tweezers and retaining the HRB (Figure 1B). DNA was extracted from the HRB using a modified version of the QIAGEN DNA Purification from Dried Blood Spots protocol14 (Appendix 1). DNA quality was assessed by Nanodrop and Qubit (select cases). Testing indications included either validation of known or suspected individual germ line variants (eg, RUNX1, DDX41, ETV6) or gene panel testing (Appendix 2; NGS and whole-exome sequencing [WES] assays). Approaches to detect germ line copy number alterations (CNA) are detailed in Figure 1C. Pathogenic variants were validated by repeat testing in a College of American Pathologists/Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments-certified laboratory before medical decision-making.

Of 93 patients, 49 males (53%) underwent HRB-derived germ line DNA testing at a median age of 63 years (range, 19-88 years). Indications for germ line testing included the presence of a suspected variant on somatic (PB/BM) testing with a VAF between 35% and 60% (n = 66, 71%), patient age <50 years (n = 26, 28%), antecedent thrombocytopenia (n = 15, 16%), syndromic features (n = 4, 4%), patient history of multiple malignancies (n = 4, 4%), and known or suggestive family history of GPS (n = 15, 16%; Appendix 3). There were no complications associated with the HRB collection procedure. The time to report variants after receipt of HRB-derived DNA was <14 days. We extracted between 500 ng and 3 μg of genomic DNA per patient. DNA quality as assessed by Nanodrop revealed a median nucleic acid concentration of 26.28 ng/μL, with a median A260/A280 ratio of 1.87 (range, 0.701-2.133) and median A260/A230 ratio of 1.23 (range ˗8.193 to 9.223). DNA quantification was performed in 7 patients via Qubit with a median DNA concentration of 21.5 ng/μL (range, 2.62-40.2). Although DNA quality was sufficient for NGS-based approaches for identifying single-nucleotide variants, insertion-deletions, and CNA, the quality needs optimization for chromosomal microarray assessments. Of note, 3 patients not included in this analysis had attempted HRB-derived germ line DNA testing for variants in CEBPA (c.247C>T; p.Gln83∗), CHEK2 (c.470T>C; p.I157T), and TERT (c.219+3G>A; p.?), with sufficient DNA quantity extracted as per Nanodrop (median nucleic acid concentration 7.34 ng/μL, median A260/A280 ratio 1.705, and median A260/A230 ratio 0.851), but the extracted DNA failed amplification for Sanger sequencing, for unclear reasons.

Of the 93 patients evaluated, 67 (72%) had ≥1 germ line variant identified, with 4 having ≥2 germ line variants for a total of 72 variants. Pathogenic and likely pathogenic germ line variants as classified by American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics criteria15 were identified in the following genes: DDX41 (n = 11, 15.3%), GATA2 (n = 2, 2.8%), TP53 (n = 2, 2.8%), and 1 each (1.4%) in ANKRD26, ATM, CDKN2A, CEBPA, CHEK2, CSF3R, CXCR4, MPO, POT1, RPL5, RUNX1, and TERT (Figure 1A). Additionally, 19 (26.4%) germ line variants of uncertain significance were identified in DDX41, although 18 of these were deemed clinically relevant based on supporting literature.16 Germ line variants of uncertain significance were also identified in ANKRD26 (n = 3, 4.2%), RUNX1 (n = 3, 4.2%), JAK3 (n = 2, 2.8%), SAMD9/SAMD9L (n = 2, 2.8%), TERT (n = 2, 2.8%), TP53 (n = 2, 2.8%), and 1 each (1.4%) in ATM, BCL2, BCOR, BPGM, DNMT3A, ETV6, GATA1, GATA2, MPL, PTPN11, SH2B3, and STAT5A.

Twelve patients had concurrent germ line testing by an alternative method: 8 skin fibroblast cultures, 3 buccal swabs, and 1 saliva sample. In those patients with >1 testing method, results were concordant in all patients but 1, in which a TP53 variant was identified in PB and HRB-derived DNA but not in skin fibroblast DNA (technical failure of the assay vs germ line mosaicism). Eight patients had WES conducted on HRB-derived DNA and also had somatic variants detected via NGS testing on PB/BM (Table 1). In the germ line WES, no somatic variants reached the predefined calling criteria based on allele fraction and quality metrics, validating the fact that HRB-derived DNA has minimal contamination from hematopoietic cells. We conducted a manual review of the binary alignment map files for the 22 somatic variants listed in Table 1. The median depth of coverage of these sites was 154× (range, 84×-260×). Two of the 22 variants (ASXL1 p.G646Wfs∗12 and TET2 p.L1065∗), both from the same patient, were found in the raw data, but each was only present in 1 read (<1%) and would not meet the variant-calling threshold for this testing method. The total cost for HRB-based testing from collection through preparation for sequencing was $77.12 per sample compared with an estimated $2000 for skin biopsy and fibroblast culture (including the estimated cost of a dermatology clinic visit and procedure).

After identification of a germ line variant on HRB testing, 30 patients (44.7%) had a change in management. Eight patients (27%) with DDX41-GPS were observed rather than treated due to known indolent course of DDX41-mutant MN, 6 (20%) were referred for early HSCT (CEBPA, DDX41, GATA1, GATA2, RPL5, and TP53), 6 (20%) were placed on an enhanced cancer surveillance (1 ANKRD26, 3 TERT, and 2 TP53), 4 patients (13%) undergoing allogeneic HSCT had related donors removed from consideration as they were germ line carriers for the same variants (2 DDX41, 1 ANKRD26, and 1 SAMD9), and 2 patients (7%) with neutropenia had granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor therapy discontinued due to theoretical concerns for clonal stimulation (1 ATM and 1 DDX41).

In summary, we validated a rapid and simple HRB-derived DNA-based germ line assessment for patients with MN that offers significant advantages compared with current standard of care germ line testing methods, which are subject to confounding due to contamination by neoplastic cells and carry procedural risks. With the results of HRB testing, we were able to demonstrate a clinically meaningful impact on the management of 44% of patients with identified germ line variants. We are currently optimizing DNA quantity and quality so that germ line CNA can be better detected using chromosomal microarray and for whole-genome sequencing. Our experience supports the application of this testing method broadly to offer timely diagnosis and support critical management decisions in patients with MN in the context of GPS/IBMFS.
