Background

Breast cancer is the malignancy with the highest incidence and mortality rate among women globally [1]. In China, according to the 2022 National Cancer Center report, breast cancer ranks first in incidence and fourth in mortality among female malignant tumors, posing a serious threat to women’s health in China [2]. Radiation therapy, as one of the crucial treatment modalities for breast cancer, plays a vital role, which has been confirmed by large-scale clinical studies. In 2005, a major meta-analysis by the Early Breast Cancer Trialists’ Collaborative Group (EBCTCG) [3] (involving 42,000 breast cancer patients) provided the first evidence that radiation therapy following breast-conserving surgery or radiation therapy to the chest wall ± regional nodes following mastectomy not only reduced local recurrence but also translated into a survival benefit. This large-scale clinical study was the first to affirm the positive impact of radiation therapy on survival in breast cancer. Subsequently, the EBCTCG updated the long-term follow-up results for patients receiving radiation therapy after breast-conserving surgery in 2011 [4] and after modified radical mastectomy in 2014 [5] These updates demonstrated that postoperative radiation therapy reduced the risk of local recurrence and improved survival in patients with early-stage breast cancer. However, the EBCTCG group also acknowledged that the degree of benefit derived from radiation therapy varied significantly among breast cancer patients with different recurrence risks.

Early-stage breast cancer has now entered a phase of chronic disease management, with over 90% of cases being curable. However, the long-term toxicities resulting from breast cancer treatment, including poor recovery of upper limb function and persistent lymphedema, as well as non-breast cancer causes of death such as cardiovascular diseases, have become increasingly prominent. These issues significantly impact the quality of life for long-term survivors of early-stage breast cancer. Therefore, in the era of precision radiation therapy, a key research focus is how to maximize the net benefit of radiation therapy for early-stage breast cancer patients. This involves adopting a patient-centered approach, utilizing individualized recurrence risk assessment to tailor “escalation” or “de-escalation” radiation therapy strategies. Current research on precision radiation therapy for early-stage pN0 breast cancer post-surgery primarily focuses on “de-escalation” therapy for early-stage, low-risk pN0 disease, specifically radiation therapy omission. The CALGB 9343 trial was the first prospective phase III randomized controlled study exploring radiation therapy omission after surgery in low-risk pN0 breast cancer. Its 10-year follow-up results showed that for early-stage low-risk pN0 patients (defined as age ≥ 70 years, pathological stage I [tumor ≤ 2 cm], estrogen receptor [ER] positive, negative margins), the 10-year local recurrence rates were 2% vs. 9% (P < 0.05) for those receiving postoperative radiation therapy versus those without radiation therapy. However, there was no statistically significant difference in distant metastasis rates or overall survival [6]. In 2023, updated 10-year follow-up data from the Scottish Cancer Trials Breast Group’s (SCTBG) PRIME II trial showed that the 10-year overall survival rates were nearly identical between the radiation therapy omission group and the postoperative radiation therapy group (80.8% vs. 80.7%) among early-stage low-risk breast cancer patients receiving postoperative endocrine therapy (defined as age ≥ 65 years, maximum tumor diameter ≤ 3 cm, hormone receptor-positive, axillary node-negative, negative margins [≥ 1 mm]) [7]. Based on the results of these two large phase III clinical trials, both the US National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines and the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) Breast Cancer Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines now recommend considering de-escalation therapy (radiation therapy omission) for pN0 breast cancer patients meeting these low-risk criteria, provided patients are fully informed about recurrence and survival risks.

However, for “escalation” therapy in early-stage high-risk pN0 breast cancer patients, high-level evidence-based medical evidence is still lacking. Two large phase III randomized controlled trials, EORTC 22,922/10,925 [8] and MA20 [9] included a proportion of node-negative (pN0) patients defined as “high-risk”. In EORTC 22,922/10,925, high-risk pN0 was defined as tumors located in the medial or central quadrant, while in MA.20, it was defined as tumor size > 5 cm or tumor ≥ 2 cm with at least one adverse prognostic factor: histological grade III, hormone receptor negativity, or extensive lympho-vascular invasion. Although the definitions of high-risk pN0 differed between these studies, subgroup analyses from both trials suggested that high-risk pN0 patients receiving escalated radiation therapy (chest wall/whole breast irradiation plus comprehensive regional nodal irradiation [including internal mammary nodes]) compared to those receiving whole breast/chest wall irradiation alone, experienced an approximately 11% improvement in 10-year disease-free survival (DFS), a difference approaching statistical significance. Based on these subgroup analysis results, both the CSCO and NCCN guidelines recommend considering escalated radiation therapy (chest wall/whole breast combined with RNI) for postoperative high-risk pN0 breast cancer patients. However, the recommended evidence level is only 2B (based on single cohort studies or low-quality RCTs). In our previous study, we established a risk stratification system (tumor size, tumor location, lympho-vascular invasion (LVI) status and Ki-67 index) for identifying clinically high risk pN0 BC patients, who might benefit from RNI therapy [10]. Based on this clinical model, we perform this randomized controlled trial to clearly determine the survival benefit of RNI among clinically high-risk pN0 breast cancer patients.

Materials and methods

Hypothesis

Objectives

The primary endpoint of this prospective, open-label randomized controlled trial aimed to investigate whether the combination of RNI with chest wall/whole breast irradiation could significantly improve disease-free survival among high risk pN0 BC patients in comparison to chest wall/whole breast irradiation alone.

Methods

Trial design

This was a prospective, open-labelled, randomized controlled trial. We recruited histo-pathologically confirmed pN0 breast cancer patients after surgery and agreed to receive postoperative radiation therapy from Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiaotong university school of medicine. Eligible patients will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either RNI or non-RNI groups. for non-RNI group, irradiation is delivered to ipsilateral chest wall or whole breast alone. And for RNI group, irradiation is delivered to ipsilateral chest wall or whole breast with regional lymphatic regions (supra/infraclavicular nodes and/or IMN). The trial flow chart was shown in Fig. 1.

Study design

Randomization

Participants with informed consent would be checked for eligibility before randomization. Eligible patients would be randomized into the RNI group and non-RNI group in a ratio of 1:1. Randomization was conducted with computer-generated random number by using SPSS software version 21.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA), stratified by type of primary surgery (BCS or mastectomy).

Primary endpoint

The primary endpoint was disease-free survival, which was defined as the time from surgery to the time of a first recurrence in the ipsilateral breast or in nodal or distant sites, a contralateral breast cancer, or death from breast cancer, which was in consistent with definition of DFS in MA-20 trial [11].

Quality assurance

To ensure consistent interpretation of Ki-67 and lymphovascular invasion (LVI) status, all pathological specimens from enrolled patients undergo central review by dedicated breast pathologists prior to randomization. Ki-67 scoring follows the recommendations of the International Ki-67 in Breast Cancer Working Group [13], with a cutoff of ≥ 14% selected based on the St. Gallen consensus criteria for luminal B subtype classification and validated in our previously published risk model. LVI assessment is based on standardized hematoxylin and eosin staining, with equivocal cases resolved by immunohistochemistry. This central review process minimizes inter-observer variability and ensures that all patients meet the predefined high-risk criteria prior to trial entry.

Comprehensive radiation therapy quality assurance (QA) is also foreseen to ensure consistent and high-quality treatment delivery across all participants. The QA program includes the following components: (1) Pre-treatment contouring and plan review: For each participating radiation oncologist, the first 10 enrolled patients will undergo centralized review of target volume delineation and treatment plans to ensure compliance with the RUIJIN contouring guidelines and protocol-specified dose constraints. (2) Periodic dosimetric audits: Institutional treatment plans will be randomly sampled for independent dosimetric verification, including evaluation of target volume coverage and organs-at-risk doses. (3) Real-time treatment verification: Daily cone-beam CT or orthogonal kilovoltage imaging will be performed to verify setup accuracy prior to each fraction, with corrective action taken for deviations exceeding 5 mm. (4) Prospective credentialing: All participating radiation oncologists and institutions must complete a credentialing process, including contouring of a benchmark case and submission of a test plan, before enrolling patients. These measures are designed to ensure that all patients receive protocol-compliant radiation therapy, minimizing inter-institutional variability and maximizing the reliability of trial outcomes.

Treatment

General consideration

Radiation therapy (RT) should commence within 12 weeks following definitive surgery or within 8 weeks after the final dose of planned postoperative chemotherapy. The RNI target volumes encompassed the supraclavicular, infraclavicular, and internal mammary nodal (IMN) regions, with axillary inclusion if clinically indicated. Planned postoperative endocrine therapy and anti-HER2 therapy should be continued during RT.

RNI group

All patients with high-risk pN0 breast cancer following breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy will receive radiation therapy to the whole breast/chest wall plus regional nodal irradiation (RNI). Consistent with NCCN guidelines, when regional nodal irradiation is indicated, it is recommended to encompass all surgically untreated axillary levels—including levels I, II, III, and IV/supraclavicular—in addition to the internal mammary nodes (IMNs). However, we acknowledge that the optimal extent of regional nodal irradiation in pN0 breast cancer remains an area of ongoing debate and clinical uncertainty, as high-level evidence specific to this population is still lacking. Therefore, while comprehensive coverage of all undissected nodal stations is recommended as the preferred approach, the final extent of regional nodal coverage is left to the treating physician’s discretion, taking into account individual patient anatomy, the extent of axillary surgery (sentinel lymph node biopsy alone vs. axillary lymph node dissection), and the patient’s specific risk profile based on our validated high-risk criteria (≥ 2 factors: tumor size > 2 cm, medial/central location, Ki-67 > 14%, LVI+).

This discretionary approach reflects real-world clinical practice, where decisions regarding comprehensive nodal irradiation are tailored to each patient, and acknowledges the clinical equipoise that justifies our trial. For left-sided patients requiring internal mammary node irradiation, deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH) is strongly recommended, and strict cardiac dose constraints (mean heart dose < 4 Gy, V25 < 5%) are mandated to ensure safety.

Non-RNI group

All patients with high-risk pN0 breast cancer following breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy will receive radiation therapy to the whole breast/chest wall alone.

Radiation therapy

Localization, simulation, and immobilization

Radiation will be delivered with 6-MV photons, 5 days per week. The patient is positioned supine with both upper limbs elevated and abducted, immobilized using a breast board. A non-contrast CT scan is performed with the scanning range extending from the cranial base superiorly to 4 cm below the inframammary fold inferiorly, using a slice thickness of 3–5 mm.

Radiation therapy regimen

Both hypo-fractionated regimen (40 Gy/15 fractions, 2.67 Gy per fraction) and conventional regimen (50 Gy/25 fractions, 2 Gy per fraction) were permitted. For patient treated with breast-conserving surgery, sequential tumor bed boost irradiation might be delivered at the treating physician’s discretion, based on individual risk factors such as close margins, high grade, or young age. Radiation therapy interruption

In patients who experienced grade 4 hematological or grade 3 or higher nonhematological toxicity, radiation therapy will be delayed until the toxicity recover to grade ≤ 2. At most, a 2-week delay will be permitted. Patients with an interruption for more than 2 weeks would be excluded from the trial.

Sample size calculation

This Phase III randomized controlled clinical trial compared an experimental group (receiving whole breast/chest wall irradiation plus regional nodal irradiation, RNI) against a control group (omitting RNI). The primary endpoint was the 5-year disease-free survival (DFS) rate. Based on retrospective data from our institution, the 5-year DFS for postoperative clinically high-risk pN0 breast cancer patients receiving whole breast irradiation alone was 86% [10]. We hypothesized that adding RNI would improve the 5-year DFS rate in this population from 86% to 91%. With a two-sided α = 0.05 and 80% power, and accounting for a 10% dropout rate, the required sample size was calculated using PASS 11.0 software. The trial would enroll 1,355 patients (678 in the RNI group, 677 in the control group) under 1:1 randomization. The study timeline comprised 3 years enrollment period and 5 years follow-up period. And the total study duration were 8 years.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses would follow the intent-to-treat (ITT) principle, including all enrolled patients irrespective of eligibility deviations. Categorical variables would be compared using χ² or Fisher’s exact tests and reported as counts/percentages. Normally distributed continuous variables would be analyzed with t-tests and summarized as mean ± SD or median (IQR). The primary endpoint (5-year DFS) would be compared between RNI and non-RNI groups. Survival outcomes (RFS/OS) would be estimated with 95% CIs, visualized by Kaplan-Meier curves, and compared via log-rank tests. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Discussion

Guideline recommendations regarding regional nodal irradiation (RNI) for high-risk pN0 breast cancer lack unanimous consensus among breast radiation oncology specialists, and the debate over whether these patients require RNI remains unresolved. This uncertainty stems primarily from two factors. First, treatment toxicity remains a significant concern. Regional nodal irradiation, particularly including the internal mammary chain, inevitably increases cardiac radiation exposure. Radiation-induced heart disease is dose-dependent and irreversible, with each 1 Gy increase in mean heart dose associated with a 7.4% increase in major coronary events—a risk that persists for at least 20 years [16]. Furthermore, data from our center and previous studies had confirmed that regional nodal irradiation significantly increases the risk of upper limb lymphedema and functional impairment [11]. Second, there is ongoing controversy regarding the definition of high-risk pN0 patients, with considerable variability in criteria across different studies. To our knowledge, this study is the first prospective randomized controlled trial specifically targeting early-stage high-risk pN0 breast cancer patients. While the clinical benefit of RNI for pN1/2 disease has been established by multiple phase III trials [8, 9, 17, 18] and EBCTCG meta-analyses, high-level evidence supporting RNI for high-risk pN0 patients remains unavailable. This trial will provide rigorous clinical evidence to guide precision radiation therapy decision-making in this population.

Prior to initiating this randomized controlled trial, we conducted a retrospective analysis of 622 early-stage pN0 breast cancer patients who underwent breast-conserving surgery followed by whole-breast radiation therapy at Ruijin Hospital (2009–2016). Using Cox regression modeling, we screened 11 potential prognostic factors and identified four independent predictors of disease-free survival (DFS): tumor size, tumor location, lymphovascular invasion (LVI) status, and Ki-67 index [10]. As the number of adverse prognostic factors increased, DFS progressively declined. Patients with 0–1 risk factors demonstrated significantly superior outcomes compared to those with ≥ 2 factors, with the lowest DFS observed in patients harboring all four factors. Based on these findings, we established a clinicopathological risk model defining clinically low-risk pN0 (0–1 risk factor) and clinically high-risk pN0 (≥ 2 risk factors). The 5-year DFS was 97% in the low-risk group versus 86% in the high-risk group (p < 0.0001). External validation using the SEER database (N = 24,029 radiation-treated pN0 patients) confirmed the model’s prognostic value: 7-year overall survival was 90% for 0 risk factors, 88% for 1 risk factor, and 82% for ≥ 2 risk factors (p < 0.0001). This validated clinical prediction model provides a critical foundation for the current study.

The indication for chest wall irradiation (CWI) after mastectomy in early-stage breast cancer patients requires careful consideration. In our trial, CWI is indicated exclusively for patients meeting our validated definition of “clinically high-risk” pN0 breast cancer (≥ 2 risk factors: tumor size > 2 cm, central/medial location, Ki-67 > 14%, LVI+) [10]. While we acknowledge that T4 tumors would automatically qualify for CWI, our inclusion criteria deliberately focus on patients with T1-3N0 disease who harbor sufficient risk to potentially benefit from comprehensive locoregional irradiation. This rationale is strongly supported by the 15-year results of the EORTC 22,922/10,925 trial, which included a substantial proportion of patients treated with mastectomy (23.9%) and a large cohort of node-negative patients (44.4%) [8]. Subgroup analysis by nodal status demonstrated that the benefit of regional nodal irradiation was consistent across all nodal subgroups, with no evidence of heterogeneity (heterogeneity p = 0.86). The hazard ratio for overall survival with regional irradiation in pN0 patients was 0.88 (95% CI 0.72–1.08)—more favorable than that observed in pN1 patients (HR 0.94). These findings indicate that node-negative patients with high-risk features derive at least as much benefit from comprehensive regional nodal irradiation as node-positive patients. Notably, this benefit was achieved despite the inclusion of mastectomy patients and the use of primarily 2D and early 3D radiation techniques. In our trial, the use of modern intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) with deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH) for left-sided patients and stricter cardiac dose constraints is expected to further improve the therapeutic ratio. Thus, the EORTC 22,922 trial provides direct evidence supporting the premise that clinically high-risk pN0 patients—particularly those with medial/central tumor location—can achieve meaningful improvements in oncologic outcomes from escalated radiation therapy, including chest wall irradiation when indicated.

The role of internal mammary node (IMN) irradiation in node-positive or high-risk breast cancer remains debated, with two key controversies: cardiac safety in left-sided patients, and the necessity of routine IMNI in all right-sided patients despite minimal cardiac dose increase. The DBCG-IMN2 study, a large prospective cohort study, reported a significant overall survival benefit with IMNI in node-positive patients treated with modern systemic therapy and 3D-conformal radiation therapy (3D-CRT) (15-year OS: 65.0% vs. 60.8%; HR 0.85, p = 0.0016), with consistent benefit across all subgroups and low cardiac mortality (0.2% in right-sided patients receiving IMNI vs. 0.7% in left-sided patients without IMNI) [19]. However, this was not a randomized trial, and the use of 3D-CRT in free-breathing may not reflect contemporary techniques with stricter dose constraints. Moreover, whether all right-sided patients require IMNI remains unresolved; population-level benefit does not guarantee individual benefit, and potential toxicities including lung injury and second malignancies must be considered [20, 21].

Our own prospective randomized trial demonstrated the cardiac safety of left-sided IMNI with modern IMRT and dose constraints (MHD ≤ 6 Gy, V30 ≤ 20%, V10 ≤ 50%), showing 0% clinical cardiac events at 1 year [22]. In the current protocol, we apply even stricter constraints (MHD < 4 Gy, V25 < 5%) and mandate IMRT with DIBH for left-sided patients. IMNI is delivered only to clinically high-risk pN0 patients defined by our validated risk model [10], ensuring selective application to those most likely to benefit—a principle that applies equally to right-sided patients, where the absence of cardiac constraint does not justify routine IMNI. We believe that routine omission of IMNI in left-sided patients is not justified, given the consistent survival benefit observed in landmark trials, the consensus reflected in international guidelines, and the demonstrated cardiac safety with modern techniques. Similarly, for right-sided patients, IMNI should be guided by individualized risk assessment rather than routine application. In exceptional cases where dose constraints cannot be met, omission may be considered after multidisciplinary discussion.

The current trial has several potential limitations. First of all, the definition of high risk is based on risk clinicopathological factors, but lack of genomic recurrence score, which would provide additional information for early-stage BC patients. Secondly, this is a single institute, open-label, randomized controlled trial, which needed to be externally validated by other institutions treated with the same radiation therapy regimen.

In conclusion, this research project aims to utilize our validated risk model for individualized stratification of recurrence risk in postoperative early-stage pN0 breast cancer patients, thereby precisely guiding decisions regarding regional nodal irradiation. For clinically high-risk pN0 patients, the study will assess whether adding RNI can further reduce locoregional recurrence and improve survival outcomes. In the era of precision and individualized radiation oncology, this prospective randomized controlled trial will provide an optimized postoperative radiation therapy strategy for patients with early-stage high-risk pN0 breast cancer and generate high-level evidence-based recommendations for this population.
