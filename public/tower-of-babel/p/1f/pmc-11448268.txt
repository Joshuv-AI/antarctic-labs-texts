Mitochondrial dysfunction gene expression, DNA methylation, and inflammatory cytokines interaction activate Alzheimer’s disease: a multi-omics Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Xiao-Xue Zhang, Meng Wei, He-Ran Wang, Ya-Zhuo Hu, Hong-Mei Sun, Jian-Jun Jia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMitochondrial dysfunction (MD) is increasingly recognized as a key pathophysiological contributor in Alzheimer disease (AD). As differential MD genes expression may serve as either a causative factor or a consequence in AD, and expression of these genes could be influenced by epigenetic modifications or interact with inflammatory cytokines, hence, the precise role of MD in AD remains uncertain.MethodsMeta-analysis of brain transcriptome datasets was conducted to pinpoint differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with MD in AD. We utilized three-step SMR to analyze the AD genome-wide association study summaries with expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) and DNA methylation QTLs from the blood and brain tissues, respectively. Through SMR and colocalization analysis, we further explored the interactions between brain eQTLs and inflammatory cytokines.ResultsFive datasets were meta-analyzed to prioritize 825 DEGs in AD from 1339 MD-related genes. Among these, seven genes from blood samples such as NDUFS8 and SPG7 and thirty-two genes from brain tissue including CLU and MAPT were identified as candidate AD-causal MD genes and regulated by methylation level. Furthermore, we revealed 13 MD gene expression-inflammatory pathway pairs involving LDLR, ACE and PTPMT1 along with interleukin-17C, interleukin-18 and hepatocyte growth factor.ConclusionsThis study highlighted that the AD-causal MD genes could be regulated by epigenetic changes and interact with inflammatory cytokines, providing evidence for AD prevention and intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-024-05680-z.Keywords: Alzheimer disease, Mitochondrial dysfunction, Inflammation, Integrative omics, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), as the main cause of dementia, is an irreversible neurodegenerative disease characterized by the progressive memory loss, cognitive impairment and the subsequent affected daily life activities [1]. The underlying disease pathogenesis may be attributed to the complicated interactions between genetic variations and environmental factors [2]. Exploring the complexity behind those interactions may shed light on AD pathogenesis and provide possible clues for disease therapeutic targets.

The crucial role of mitochondria in regulating stress responses and maintaining homeostasis is particularly important for preserving the health of high-energy-demanding neurons [3]. Mitochondria dysfunction (MD) contributed to bioenergetics defects, disruption of calcium homeostasis, increased oxidative stress, defective axonal transport and neuroinflammation, which is one of the hallmarks of aging and related to AD [4–7]. Notably, high levels of mitochondrial DNA copy number assessed from brain tissue were found to be linked to a lower risk of AD neuropathological burden [8]. Multiple mitochondrial-related genes may result in the intricate multifactorial pathophysiology in AD. For instance, OPA1 could regulate mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, mitochondrial membrane potential and neuronal apoptosis in AD [9]. Tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated protein 1, predominantly localized in the mitochondria, also plays a pivotal role in the redox homeostasis and the preservation of ATP levels [10]. Actually, the mitochondrial-related gene set consists of 37 bioenergetic genes encoded by the mitochondrion itself and over 1000 additional nuclear genes [11]. It is noteworthy that genetic predisposition in these nuclear genes may potentially contribute to MD [11]. However, few studies have explored whether MD induced by the nuclear genes has a causative role in activating AD or merely imposes brain tissue damage.

Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have been conducted to explore genomic loci including AD-related mitochondrion genes [12]. However, the leading associated variants may not have causal relationships with AD due to the complex linkage disequilibrium structure within genomes [13, 14]. Significantly, the summary data-based mendelian randomization (SMR) approach, which combines AD GWAS data with expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) or DNA methylation QTL (mQTL), has been established to prioritize causal variants [15, 16]. Through latent heritable confounder-mendelian randomization, a study pointed out elevated levels of mitochondrial DNA copy number from blood were causally linked to decreased AD risk [17]. Previous AD studies mainly focused on the function of mitochondria DNA itself, while the cellular bioenergy levels were regulated by the mitochondrial gene set with the nuclear genes [18]. Thus, it is necessary to investigate the potential causal role of MD nuclear genes in AD by integrating AD GWAS data with these eQTLs or mQTLs. It must be mentioned that MD may exacerbate the inflammatory responses in several ways, meanwhile, inflammation, as a main pathogenic pathway in AD, could also cause the impairment of mitochondria [19, 20]. Nevertheless, the interactions of inflammation with the causal MD nuclear genes in AD are not well elucidated currently.

The study employed a multi-omics-based approach to uncover potential causal effects of MD nuclear genes and the associated epigenetic regulation in AD. Meta-analysis of brain transcriptomes was conducted to identify differential MD nuclear gene expressions in AD. We utilized the three-step SMR method to integrate the extensive AD GWAS summary with eQTLs and mQTLs data in blood and brain tissues, respectively. In addition, we integrated the brain eQTLs and inflammation cytokines by colocalization analysis to reveal the underlying interactions between mitochondria and inflammation in AD.

Methods

The flowchart of this study was illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the study. First, MD-related genes were extracted from the GeneCards database with the relevance score ≥ 7. Second, five brain transcriptome datasets were acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus database and underwent meta-analysis to prioritize differential expressed AD-related MD genes, subsequently cell type-specific expression analysis was conducted. Third, the potential MD genes and their regulatory elements related to the AD risk were identified using the three-step SMR, which involved integrating GWAS summaries and cis-eQTLs/cis-mQTLs data from the blood and brain tissues, respectively (PSMR_multi < 0.05 and PHEIDI > 0.05). The sensitivity analysis was performed after the primary SMR to evaluate heterogeneity (Cochran Q P > 0.05). Finally, the potential interactions between brain MD genes and inflammatory cytokines were explored through SMR, sensitivity and colocalization analysis (PSMR_multi < 0.05 and PHEIDI > 0.05, Cochran Q P > 0.05, colocalization PPH4 > 0.5)

Meta-analysis of differentially expressed MD genes

We used the keyword “mitochondrial dysfunction” to incorporate the 1339 genes with the relevance score ≥ 7 from the GeneCards database. Via the Gene Expression Omnibus database, we acquired five brain tissues transcriptome datasets (microarray) concerning 401 patients with AD and 388 healthy controls (HCs). The microarray data was log-transformed in R to achieve normalization. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed with a linear regression model adjusting for age and sex in the five datasets, respectively. Then, the above results were further integrated by the fixed-effects meta-analysis using the R package metafor. The Q test in the R package metafor was performed concurrently to assess heterogeneity.

Three-step SMR analysis and colocalization analysis

Data resources

GWAS summary statistics for AD were obtained from Finngen GWAS, yielding a sample size of 9,301 patients with AD and 367,976 HCs. Blood eQTL summary data were acquired from eQTLGen among 31,684 individuals [21]. Blood mQTL summary statistics were found from the meta-analysis involving 1,980 individuals [14]. Brain eQTL summary data was composed of RNA-seq data of 2,865 brain cortex samples [22]. Brain mQTL summary data (n = 1,160) were obtained from the meta-analysis conducted by Qi et al. [23]. The selection criteria for cis-eQTL and cis-mQTL involved single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) located within a 1 Mb distance from each gene. In addition, the acquisition of 91 inflammatory cytokines data was from a genome-wide meta-analysis involving 14,824 participants [24].

Statistical analysis

The analysis aimed to determine the AD-causal MD genes separately in blood and brain tissues by the SMR multi-tool and evaluate linkage disequilibrium with the 1000 Genomes Project. SNPs were instruments in the whole three-step SMR analysis. First, gene expressions were exposure and AD was the outcome. Second, DNA methylations (DNAms) were exposure and AD was the outcome. Third, only significant signals in the first two steps were included, then, DNAms were exposure and gene expressions were the outcome. The final results in three steps were needed to meet the following conditions: (1) PSMR_multi < 0.01 in the blood or PSMR_multi < 0.05 in the brain tissues; (2) PHEIDI > 0.05 (no heterogeneity in the dependent instrument (HEIDI) test); (3) P < 1 × 10−5 (significant genome-wide in eQTLs and mQTLs). The sensitivity analysis was carried out following the primary SMR to assess the heterogeneity. The Cochran Q statistic was utilized in the inverse variance weighting and MR-Egger methods (Cochran Q P > 0.05 indicating no heterogeneity).

Colocalization is a method to estimate the possibility of a shared causal variant for two traits (posterior probability hypotheses (PPH)4 > 0.5), which was chosen to estimate the potential interactions between brain MD gene expression and inflammatory cytokines in this study using the R package coloc. The coloc package was employed to test five hypotheses. The posterior probability was used to utilized to advocate for all the hypotheses, labeled as PPH1–PPH4: PPH0, no association with either trait; PPH1, association with the MD gene expression but not inflammatory cytokines; PPH2, association with inflammatory cytokines but not the MD gene expression; PPH3, association with inflammatory cytokines and the MD gene expression, with distinct causal variants; and PPH4, association with inflammatory cytokines and the MD gene expression, with a shared causal variant [25]. In our study, the threshold value of PPH4 > 0.5 presented moderate or strong colocalization [26]. The colocalization analysis was restricted to SNPs located within a 1 Mb region from the start and end of the target genes.

Enrichment analysis

The cell type-specific enrichment analysis was conducted to investigate whether brain DEGs were specific to any cell type with database (https://bioinfo.uth.edu/CSEADB/). The DNAms enrichment analysis was evaluated with eFORGE (http://eforge.cs.ucl.ac.uk/) and the annotation involving the regions of sites was assessed at http://grch37.ensembl.org/.

Results

Meta-analysis of differentially expressed MD genes

Five gene expression datasets were incorporated to compare MD gene expression in the brain tissues of patients with AD (401) and HCs (388) via the meta-analysis (Table S1). A total of 1339 MD-related genes, each with a relevance score ≥ 7, were obtained from GeneCards (Table S2). Afterward, 825 MD genes exhibited differential expression between AD and HCs (Fig. 2, Table S3). Moreover, through cell type-specific enrichment analysis, the DEGs were found to be significantly enriched in excitatory neurons (P < 0.01) among the 27 general cell classifications in the nervous system (Figure S1, Table S4), indicating an important regulatory role of these neurons in mitochondrial function.

Significant differentially expressed genes were indicated by red dots

Incorporating AD GWAS data and eQTL/mQTL of MD genes for analysis

We utilized the three-step SMR method to find the significant AD-causal MD genes and the associated epigenetic regulation. In blood samples, 14 MD casual genes were found through the analysis of eQTL data and AD GWAS summary statistics (PSMR_multi < 0.01 and PHEIDI > 0.05) (Table S5). In the meantime, the integration of the AD GWAS data and mQTL data contributed to 140 DNAm probes (PSMR_multi < 0.01 and PHEIDI >0.05) (Table S6). Subsequently, we identified 27 DNAm probes potentially regulating 7 neighboring genes such as NDUFS8 and SPG7 by analyzing the AD-causal eQTL and mQTL data (PSMR_multi < 0.01 and PHEIDI >0.05) (Table S7, Figure S2). Additionally, these CpG sites exhibited significant enrichment in the primary T cells (P = 4.26 × 10−5) and primary natural killer cells from blood (P = 1.41 × 10−2) (Table S8).

Genetic influences on gene expression may differ from blood and brain tissues, hence, the three-step SMR analysis were also conducted in the brain tissues [27]. A total of 68 MD genes were identified by analyzing eQTL data with AD GWAS data (PSMR_multi < 0.05 and PHEIDI > 0.05) (Table S9). Meanwhile, integrating AD GWAS data with mQTL data yielded 525 DNAm probes (PSMR_multi < 0.05 and PHEIDI > 0.05) (Table S10). Afterwards, with the combination of putative AD-causal eQTL and mQTL data for analysis, 122 DNAm probes were observed to influence 32 neighboring genes including CLU and MAPT (PSMR_multi < 0.05 and PHEIDI > 0.05) (Table S11, Figure S3). In addition, the results of significantly enriched CpG sites were showed in the Table S12.

Methylated regulation on gene expression influencing potential ADcausal genes

In blood samples, our study demonstrated that the SNP signals linked with NDUFS8 were significant across the AD GWAS, eQTL and mQTL studies. The DNAm probe cg1613285 exhibited a negative effect on NDUFS8 expression (betaSMR = − 0.10) and a positive effect on AD onset (betaSMR = 0.10), whereas the NDUFS8 expression was negatively correlated with the disease (betaSMR = − 0.05) (Fig. 3A, Figure S4) (Table S5–S7). Overall, our findings proposed a potential mechanism in which reduced DNAm levels resulted in an upregulation of NDUFS8 expression, then decreasing the risk of developing AD. Our findings also indicated a causal negative association between SPG7 expression and the DNAm probe cg16428612 (betaSMR = − 0.64). Elevated SPG7 gene expression (betaSMR = 0.10) and decreased methylation level (betaSMR = − 0.10) were observed to potentially raise the risk of AD (Fig. 3B, Figure S5) (Table S5–S7). Together, it was postulated that genetic variants may upregulate SPG7 expression by modulating the methylation level, resulting in a detrimental effect on AD.

In brain tissues, the DNAm probe cg21608792 was discovered to be situated in the promoter region, 105 kbp upstream of CLU. This DNAm site was negatively associated with CLU expression (betaSMR = − 0.02) and positively with AD onset (betaSMR = 0.11), while the CLU expression exhibited a negative association with the disease (betaSMR = − 0.56) (Fig. 4A, Figure S6) (Tables S9–11). Collectively, our results offered a potential explanation wherein lower DNAm level could enhance the CLU expression and subsequently decrease the AD risk. We also found that lower DNAm level (cg05721485) was causally related to higher MAPT expression (betaSMR = − 0.17). Elevated MAPT expression (betaSMR = 0.20) and decreased methylation levels (betaSMR = − 0.06) likely played causative roles in AD onset (Fig. 4B, Figure S7) (Tables S9–11). Thus, it was proposed that the genetic variants downregulated MAPT expression by influencing the DNAm status, showing a protective effect on AD.

Potential brain AD-causal MD genes interacting with inflammatory cytokines

To further test the hypothesis that genetic determinants of brain MD gene expression were shared with inflammatory cytokines, we combined 91 inflammatory cytokines (Table S13) with AD-causal brain eQTLs by colocalization analysis, subsequently identified 13 gene expression–inflammation pathway pairs (PPH4 > 0.5) (Table S14).

In brain tissues, we pinpointed LDLR as a MD causal gene in AD linked with the inflammatory cytokines. This study showed that LDLR expression was negatively related to the risk of developing AD (betaSMR = − 0.12) (Fig. 5A). Its expression shared genetic effects with interleukin-17 C (IL-17 C) (PPH4 = 0.57) (Fig. 5A1) and STAM-binding protein (STAMBP) (PPH4 = 0.54) (Fig. 5A2). Another candidate causal gene is ACE, reduced ACE expression likely exerted a causal role in AD (betaSMR = − 0.10) (Fig. 5B). We observed a co-location of genetic factors influencing ACE expression and interleukin-18 (IL-18) (PPH4 = 0.75) (Fig. 5B1). Additionally, this study showed a harmful effect of PTPMT1 expression on AD (betaSMR = 0.14) (Fig. 5C). The inflammatory factors including hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) (PPH4 = 0.60) (Fig. 5C1), tumor necrosis factor ligand superfamily member 14 (TNFSF14) (PPH4 = 0.63) (Fig. 5C2) and oncostatin-M (OSM) (PPH4 = 0.83) (Fig. 5C3) demonstrated overlapping genetic influences with PTPMT1 expression, respectively.

By colocalization analysis, we also found three inflammatory factors such as C-X-C motif chemokine 5 (PPH4 = 0.73) (Figure S8a), fibroblast growth factor 23 (PPH4 = 0.60) (Figure S8b) and matrix metalloproteinase-1 (PPH4 = 0.87) (Figure S8c) may share genetic variants with DTYMK expression, thus partially explaining the harmful effect of DTYMK expression on AD (betaSMR = 0.04) (Figure S8). Similarly, SNPs regulating RNASEH2C expression might also affect the inflammatory cytokines such as C-C motif chemokine 23 (PPH4 = 0.99) (Figure S9a), C-X-C motif chemokine 9 (PPH4 = 0.86) (Figure S9b), and leukemia inhibitory factor receptor (PPH4 = 0.72) (Figure S9c). Additionally, SLC25A39 expression shared the genetic variant rs2011895 with STAMBP (PPH4 = 0.58) (Figure S10).

Discussion

In this study, we detected MD-related DEGs out of potential genes in AD using the meta-analysis of brain transcriptome data. As far as we know, our study revealed the potential AD-causal MD genes and the associated epigenetic changes from blood and brain tissues by integrating AD GWAS with the eQTLs and mQTLs for analysis. Furthermore, interactions between brain genes expression and inflammatory cytokines were also explored through colocalization analysis. Our study intended to discover the causative role of MD genes and clarify the interactions with inflammation in AD.

Elevated blood mitochondrial DNA copy number was linked to improved present and future cognitive function according to population studies [28]. Similarly, high levels of mitochondrial DNA copy number assessed from brain tissue were found to be linked to a lower risk of AD neuropathological burden [8]. Previous mendelian randomization analyses indicated the causal effect of low mitochondrial DNA copy number on AD risk [17]. However, few studies explored the causal relationships between MD-related nuclear genes and AD risk. Studying gene expression patterns unique to specific tissues has been demonstrated to reveal diverse biological explanations, hence, we explored the putative causal relationships in the blood and brain tissues. This study proposed potential AD-causal MD genes such as NDUFS8 and SPG7 in the blood, CLU and MAPT in brain tissues.

In this study, the genetic mutations of NDUFS8 and CLU were associated with reduced AD risk. Our research validated that higher transcript level of NDUFS8 or CLU could causally reduce the risk of AD. NDUFS8 is an essential component of complex I in mitochondria, and CLU is localized to the mitochondrial matrix [29, 30]. Cell and animal studies proposed that with knockdown of NDUFS8, mitochondrial impairment manifested as reduced ATP production and increased oxidative stress [29]. And another study reported that decreased ATP levels would lead to mitochondrial diseases due to the knockdown of ND23 (the homologue of NDUFS8) [31]. Besides, CLU increased mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I activity and mitigated mitochondrial excessive fission [32, 33]. On the contrary, in our study, the genetic variants of SPG7 and MAPT were linked to increased AD risk. Similarly, previous studies proposed that the SPG7 mutations was also linked to cognitive dysfunction [34]. The knockdown of SPG7 resulted in augmented mitochondrial bioenergetics including ATP and oxygen consumption rate [35]. SPG7, as an essential component for the mitochondrial permeability transition pore complex, was required for the opening of this complex, leading to MD and cell death [35]. Besides, the role of tau in neurodegeneration was confirmed by the mutations of the MAPT gene [36]. Hyperphosphorylated tau has been demonstrated to impair mitochondrial function by inhibiting complex I activity and disrupting mitochondrial dynamics [37]. The mutations of the above genes influenced the mitochondria, further, mitochondrial defects were the hallmark of AD. Therefore, further research is required to clarify the underlying mechanisms regarding the impacts of these genes on AD risk.

Epigenetics may be essential to uncover the underlying mechanisms involving MD and AD. Variants linked to AD in GWAS were mainly found in non-coding regions, believed to affect disease progression by regulating the function of epigenetic elements [38]. Our study explored the associated epigenetic changes involved the above AD-causal MD genes from blood and brain tissues. We offered a potential mechanism in which lower DNAm level could enhance the NDUFS8 or CLU expression, then decreasing the risk of developing AD. Notably, epigenetic control of the CLU was partially attributed to the promoter region rich in methylation sites [39]. Indeed, in our study the DNAm site (cg21608792) was found to be located in the promoter region, which may downregulate CLU expression. In contrast, it was proposed that the genetic variants downregulated SPG7 or MAPT expression by increasing the methylation level, showing the protective effects on AD. Similarly, a previous study proposed that high methylation level was associated with the lower SPG7 expression [40]. Importantly, various DNAm sites may exert different impacts on MD genes expression, more studies are needed to explore the multiple epigenetic co-regulatory patterns deeply. Understanding the epigenetic regulation of mitochondrial-related nuclear genes is essential to comprehend the disorder of mitochondria, which may provide theoretical basis for deeply exploring the relationship between MD and AD.

We further conducted colocalization analysis to consider the interactions between inflammation and MD gene expression in brain tissue. In our study, genetic up-regulation of LDLR expression was linked to decreased AD risk. Notably, cognitive impairments in LDLR knockout mice were linked to oxidative stress, MD, and inflammation [41]. Importantly, we further revealed the colocalization of genetic mechanisms governing LDLR expression and inflammation. For example, the shared genetic variant rs7251216 may impact the LDLR expression and STAMBP together, the LDLR expression and IL-17 C levels may also be influenced by the common genetic variant rs113604534. In detail, IL-17 C could be induced by the proinflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β [42]. However, as a negative regulator of inflammasome, STAMBP has the potential to restrict overactive inflammasome activity, thereby mitigating harmful interleukin-1β signaling [43]. The precarious balance of inflammatory factors with LDLR expression may confer different influences on AD risk.

Our study also determined the colocalization of genetic regulations controlling IL-18 and ACE expression. In this study, high ACE expression decreased the risk of developing AD. IL-18, as a potent pro-inflammatory cytokine, may interact with the gene ACE, imparting multifaceted effects on the pathophysiology of AD. The revelations of common genetic factors influencing PTPMT1 expression and inflammatory cytokines were also demonstrated. Different inflammatory cytokines may exert different effects on AD, HGF has been shown to enhance the survival and maturation of neuron, and the neuroprotective effects of OSM have been reported in the previous studies, while TNFSF14 negatively regulated the neurite growth [44–46]. High PTPMT1 expression was found to increase the AD risk. Collectively, the genetic variants concurrently influenced inflammatory cytokines and PTPMT1 expression. The above colocalization analysis emphasized the importance of exploring the relationships between mitochondrial function, inflammation and the AD risk. Inflammation and MD are pathological hallmarks of AD, and these two hallmarks had complex interactions. Specifically, MD plays a role in triggering inflammation, while inflammatory factors can, in turn, affect and regulate mitochondrial function [47]. Together, it is necessary to clarify shared genetic variants involving MD and inflammatory factors, which may provide new insights into exploring the molecular mechanism in AD.

This is a comprehensive multi-omics study to explore the possible casually MD genes associated with AD risk and corresponding methylation modification via three-step SMR analysis. Importantly, the analysis was conducted in different tissues including blood and brain tissue, providing more clues for understanding the roles of MD genes in AD. Notably, we integrated the transcriptomic evidence with inflammatory factors via colocalization analysis to find the potential common genetic regulations. As for the limitations, first, the AD GWAS summary data in the FinnGen were restricted to European descent, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings to other populations; second, we conducted the analysis only using the cis-eQTL and cis-mQTL, despite trans-regulatory regions may also affect the regulatory networks widely; third, given that the MD genes expression can be influenced by various factors, incorporating additional proteins and metabolites data could potentially uncover new insights and enhance the understanding of the possible causal mechanisms in AD.

Conclusion

This study enhanced our understanding of the candidate causal MD genes in AD and the putative epigenetic modification on gene expression utilizing the multi-omics MR methods. We further demonstrated the possible interactions between MD genes expression and inflammatory factors via colocalization analysis. These findings pinpointed the role of MD genes in AD and provided new sight into the molecular mechanisms involving MD and inflammation in AD.
