Effects of elevated CO 2 and temperature on cocklebur ( X anthium strumarium L.) and its herbicide response
Authors: Islam Emrah Suer, Nihat Tursun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClimate change, characterized by rising atmospheric CO2 and temperature fluctuations, significantly alters crop‐weed interactions and weed management efficacy. This study evaluated the interactive effects of elevated CO2 (400, 600, 800, and 1000 ppm) and temperature regimes (26/16°C and 29/19°C) on the biomass accumulation of Xanthium strumarium and the efficacy of two herbicides with distinct modes of action: trifloxysulfuron‐sodium (systemic, ALS inhibitor) and fluometuron (soil‐active, PSII inhibitor).RESULTSElevated CO2 concentration up to 1000 ppm at the optimal temperature (26/16°C) significantly stimulated the vegetative growth and dry biomass of X. strumarium. Crucially, dose–response analyses revealed that this climate‐driven accelerated growth did not induce herbicide resistance; rather, it increased the weed's susceptibility to the systemic herbicide. Elevated CO2 facilitated the translocation of trifloxysulfuron‐sodium due to increased plant metabolism, thereby significantly lowering ED50 values. In contrast, the efficacy of the soil‐applied fluometuron remained highly stable and unaffected by variations in either CO2 or temperature.CONCLUSIONThe interaction between climate change variables and chemical weed control is fundamentally driven by the herbicide's mode of action. While elevated CO2 acts as a fertilizer for X. strumarium, the resulting rapid growth paradoxically increases the efficacy of systemic herbicides by accelerating the movement of active ingredients. Meanwhile, soil‐active herbicides like fluometuron offer a climate‐resilient weed management strategy. Future studies should incorporate molecular approaches to fully elucidate these mode‐of‐action‐specific physiological responses under changing climatic conditions. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Global climate change, driven by greenhouse gas emissions, significantly impacts both agricultural and natural ecosystems by compromising local biodiversity, agricultural productivity, and global food security. 1 Among the primary climate variables, increasing CO2 concentration is identified as one of the critical components related to climate change. 2 Atmospheric CO2 concentrations have risen substantially from pre‐industrial levels of approximately 280 ppm to over 410 ppm in recent years due to anthropogenic activities. 3 According to recent estimates, the CO2 concentration is projected to reach between 550 and 1000 ppm by the end of the 21st century, while the global air temperature is expected to exhibit average fluctuations between 1 and 3.7 °C. 4 These fundamental shifts in global climate can directly alter crop growth and productivity as well as crop‐weed‐pest interactions. 5 High CO2 concentrations and the associated increase in temperature can impose negative effects on the agricultural ecosystem by inducing more extreme weather conditions, including more frequent droughts, floods, and frost events. 6

Climate change has the potential to present serious challenges in the management of agricultural pests and weeds. This issue is particularly pertinent given that weeds generally possess higher genetic diversity and physiological flexibility than crops, enabling rapid adaptation to changing conditions and accelerated development. 7 , 8 Consequently, weeds are considered a significant factor contributing to yield losses in agricultural areas. It has been asserted that climate change will lead to increased proliferation, spread, and development of numerous weed species and enhance their invasiveness. 9 , 10 , 11 A notable example of this potential spread is the invasive weed species, cocklebur, which has spread extensively across many regions worldwide, resulting in substantial economic losses in both agricultural and non‐agricultural areas. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15

Cocklebur (Xanthium strumarium L.) is an annual, broad‐leaved, seed‐propagating weed of the Asteraceae family. 16 It causes significant yield losses worldwide, especially in row‐cropped crops such as soybean, cotton, corn, and peanut. 15 , 17 In Türkiye (Turkey), cocklebur is a resilient plant capable of growing in almost all regions and under all climatic and soil conditions. It has been reported that the yield losses due to cocklebur in Türkiye are approximately 27% in corn and 37% in cotton. 18 , 19 It is important to note that Xanthium strumarium is largely autogamous and self‐pollinating, which generally results in a fair degree of genetic uniformity among populations, even when distributed across diverse regions. 20 Despite this, Cocklebur, a C3 weed species, can increase its invasive potential through rapid distribution and spread due to its high reproductive capacity, long‐term seed viability, and adaptation to diverse climatic conditions. It is stated that C3 plants will photosynthesize more efficiently than C4 plants with increasing CO2 as a result of climate change, accompanied by a greater increase in biomass and yield. 21

It is reported that cocklebur is the most abundant and widespread weed species in cotton production areas in Turkiye. 22 Post‐emergence trifloxysulfuron‐sodium and early post‐emergence fluometuron herbicides are commonly used for the control of this species. However, due to extreme climate change, carbon dioxide and temperature have been observed to directly influence plant biochemistry and morphology. This alteration affects the adhesion and distribution of the herbicides on the plant, reducing herbicide performance. 23 It has also been reported that the herbicides applied under elevated CO2 conditions against some weeds, such as C3 plants, have low efficacy at label doses. 24 , 25 It is therefore critical to determine cocklebur's response to herbicides applied under elevated CO2 and temperature conditions to optimize weed control strategies. 26 , 27

Although previous studies have documented the individual effects of elevated CO2 or temperature on weed physiology, limited information is available regarding the combined impact of these climate variables on the efficacy of trifloxysulfuron‐sodium and fluometuron against Xanthium strumarium. Specifically, how the interaction between high CO2 and elevated temperature alters the susceptibility of different cocklebur populations to these herbicides remains a significant knowledge gap that requires investigation.

It is expected that the invasive potential of cocklebur will increase with rising CO2 and temperatures due to future climate change, making its control more difficult. This study had two objectives: 1) to evaluate the impact of elevated CO2 and temperature on the morphological and biological traits of Xanthium strumarium populations collected from different ecological regions of Türkiye, and 2) to determine the efficacy of trifloxysulfuron‐sodium and fluometuron herbicides against this weed species under combined high temperature and CO2 conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Site and plant material

The study was conducted in 2020 and 2021 in the carbon dioxide greenhouse at the Faculty of Agriculture, Turgut Ozal University, Malatya, Türkiye (38.27° N, 38.21° E). In the study, cocklebur seeds were collected from agricultural land (orchards, vegetables, and field crops) in 18 provinces representing seven regions of Türkiye (Table 1 and Fig. 1). The collected seeds were stored at +4°C.

Experimental conditions

The experiments were conducted within a fully automated greenhouse comprising four distinct compartments. Each compartment has an area of 25 m2, and the rooms' interiors are equipped with automated humidity, ventilation, and heating systems. The photoperiod was maintained at a 14‐h daytime and 10‐h nighttime cycle. The light intensity inside the compartments ranged from 3000 to 5000 lx (approximately 55–90 μmol m−2 s−1 of photosynthetically active radiation, PAR). This relatively moderate light intensity, characteristic of this specific greenhouse facility, accounts for the modest total dry biomass (g plant−1) accumulation observed over the 2‐month growth period.

The experiments were conducted using a completely randomized design (CRD) with four replications (pots) per treatment and a factorial arrangement of two temperature regimes (day/night 26/16 °C and 29/19°C) and four carbon dioxide concentrations (400 ± 50, 600 ± 50, 800 ± 50, and 1000 ± 50 ppm). Target CO2 levels were maintained continuously (24 h a day) by a computerized greenhouse automation system that injected pure CO2 from pressurized cylinders into the respective rooms. Since the ambient CO2 concentration outside the greenhouse ranged from 370 to 430 ppm, 400 ppm was set as the control concentration. In the study, 26°C and 400 ppm were evaluated as control treatments (IPCC, 2023). The plant growth and herbicide efficacy studies comprised eight treatments, namely: (1) 26/16°C (day/night temperature); 400 ppm (CO2 concentration) [control], (2) 26/16°C; 600 ppm CO2, (3) 26/16 °C; 800 ppm CO2, (4) 26/16°C; 1000 ppm CO2, (5) 29/19°C; 400 ppm (control), (6) 29/19°C; 600 ppm CO2, (7) 29/19°C; 800 ppm CO2, (8) 29/19°C; 1000 ppm CO2.

Experiment 1: Growth response of 18 cocklebur populations to elevated CO2 and temperature

In the study, cocklebur seeds, obtained from 18 provinces, were cultivated in 19 × 17 cm pots (three seeds per pot) in a peat‐perlite (2:1) mixture under greenhouse conditions as described in Section 2.2 experimental conditions. Two months after germination, the plants were harvested, and the following parameters were recorded: plant height (cm), fresh weight (g), and dry weight (g). Plant height was measured with a meter from the soil level to the top of the plant. Fresh weight was determined by cutting the plants at the soil level and weighing them on a precision scale. Subsequently, the plants were transferred to paper bags and dried in an oven at 72°C for 48 h, after which their dry weight (g) was determined.

Experiment 2: response of a cocklebur population to different herbicide doses under elevated CO 2 and temperature

Seeds of the Xanthium strumarium (Diy21 population) were planted in pots under the greenhouse conditions described previously. When the plants reached the 4–6 true leaf stage, herbicide treatments were applied. The herbicides were applied using a laboratory spraying cabin equipped with a flat‐fan nozzle (11002) at a pressure of 304 kPa (3 atm), calibrated to deliver a spray volume of 200 L ha−1. To determine the dose–response relationship, seven doses were applied for each herbicide: 0 (untreated control), 0.25, 0.50, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0, and 8.0 times the recommended field rate (Table 2). Doses significantly higher than the recommended rate (4x and 8x) were included to ensure complete plant mortality (estimating the upper limit of the sigmoidal curve) and to evaluate potential tolerance shifts under elevated CO2 and temperature conditions. At 28 days after treatment (DAT), the plants were harvested, dried in an oven at 72 °C for 48 h, and their dry weights were recorded.

Data analysis

Both Experiment 1 and Experiment 2 were established with four replications per treatment and repeated in time (two independent experimental runs) to validate the results. Preliminary analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate the consistency of the data across the two independent experimental runs. In this mixed‐model analysis, the experimental ‘run’ was designated as a random factor, while the treatments (temperature, CO2 concentration, and herbicide dose) were treated as fixed factors. Because the run‐by‐treatment interactions were not statistically significant (P > 0.05), indicating consistent treatment responses across both temporal repetitions, the data from the two independent runs were pooled for the final statistical analyses reported below.

Analysis of experiment 1: plant growth parameters

The data obtained (plant height, fresh weight, and dry weight) were analyzed according to a completely randomized design (CRD). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), chosen for its robust handling of General Linear Models (GLM). Prior to analysis, the assumptions of ANOVA were verified; normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene's test. Data were subjected to ANOVA, and means were separated using Duncan's Multiple Range Test at a significance level of P ≤ 0.05. Additionally, simple linear regression analysis was performed to quantify the relationship between CO2 concentrations (independent variable) and plant growth parameters (dependent variables). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed that temperature, CO2 concentration, and population origin all exerted highly significant main effects (P < 0.001) on plant height, fresh weight, and dry weight (Table 3). Crucially, the two‐way interaction between Temperature × CO2 was significant across all growth parameters. This indicates that the growth‐promoting potential of elevated CO2 is not absolute but is strictly regulated by the thermal regime.

Population responses

Geographical origin played a decisive role in growth performance (Table 4). Among the 18 populations tested, Kır39 (Kırşehir) proved the tallest, while Ant6 (Antalya) accumulated the highest fresh biomass. In terms of dry matter production, the Diy21 (Diyarbakır) population was the top performer. Conversely, the Erz25 (Erzurum) population consistently exhibited the lowest values for both fresh and dry weights, likely reflecting an adaptation to its shorter local growing season. Although the interaction between CO2 and Population was significant for plant height and fresh weight (P ≤ 0.05), the general trend remained consistent: biomass increased with rising CO2. Notably, the Diy21, Ant6, and Ada1 populations consistently produced the highest dry weights under elevated CO2 conditions, indicating they are highly responsive to CO2 fertilization.

Effect of herbicides on cocklebur control

Dose–response assays focused exclusively on the Diy21 population due to its high phenotypic uniformity and responsiveness. Data were analyzed using raw dry weight (g plant−1) to ensure biological precision.

Trifloxysulfuron‐sodium

The efficacy of trifloxysulfuron‐sodium was strongly modulated by atmospheric conditions. As visualized in Fig. 3, the herbicide became significantly more potent as CO2 levels increased. At 26°C, the effective dose required to reduce biomass by 50% (ED50) dropped precipitously from 1.90 g ha−1 at ambient CO2 (400 ppm) to just 0.40 g ha−1 at 1000 ppm (P < 0.05). This suggests a synergistic interaction where elevated CO2 sensitizes the plant to the herbicide. A similar trend was observed at 29°C, although the reduction in ED50 was less pronounced than at the optimal temperature (Table 5).

Fluometuron

In sharp contrast, fluometuron displayed remarkable stability against environmental fluctuations (Fig. 3). The dose–response curves for this herbicide overlapped significantly across treatments. There were no statistically significant differences in ED50 values, which ranged narrowly between 0.30 and 0.50 L ha−1 regardless of temperature or CO2 concentration (P > 0.05) (Table 5). This finding indicates that fluometuron's herbicidal activity is largely independent of the climate variables tested.

No significant differences in the efficacy of the fluometuron at 26 and 29°C were found. Similarly, no significant change was observed between the CO2 ratios at these temperatures (Table 5). Unlike the systemic herbicide, fluometuron's efficacy remained highly stable across all climate variables, demonstrating its climate‐resilient nature.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that elevated CO2 acts as a potent fertilizer for Xanthium strumarium, significantly enhancing biomass accumulation, but this advantage is strictly limited by thermal thresholds, with heat stress effectively suppressing these gains. Furthermore, herbicide efficacy varied by mode of action: the systemic herbicide trifloxysulfuron‐sodium exhibited greater efficacy under elevated CO2, whereas the soil‐active fluometuron remained highly stable across all environmental conditions. A critical aspect of this research, distinguishing it from many previous studies, is the rigorous statistical methodology employed. Rather than relying on relative percentage data, which can mask the true biological magnitude of responses, we utilized raw biomass values (g plant−1). Furthermore, the dose–response relationships were analyzed using the three‐parameter log‐logistic model (LL.3) with the lower limit constrained to zero. This approach, strongly advocated by some researchers, 28 , 30 prevents biologically impossible negative biomass predictions and ensures that the estimated ED50 values reflect a robust physiological reality. By adopting this framework, we aim to provide a more accurate prediction of weed‐crop‐herbicide interactions under future climate scenarios.

Impact of climate change factors on plant growth and population dynamics

The results of this study corroborate that Xanthium strumarium, as a C3 weed species, exhibits a robust positive growth response to atmospheric CO2 enrichment. In our experiments, elevated CO2 (1000 ppm) significantly stimulated plant height, fresh weight, and dry weight compared to ambient levels. This finding aligns with the fundamental principles of plant physiology: elevated CO2 suppresses the oxygenase activity of RuBisCO, thereby mitigating photorespiration and enhancing net carbon assimilation in C3 plants. 31 , 32 Similar increases in the biomass of Convolvulus arvensis L. have recently been reported under high CO2, 33 which reinforces the consensus that C3 weeds will likely become more competitive in carbon‐enriched environments.

Crucially, however, our interaction analysis (Fig. 2) reveals that this ‘CO2 fertilization effect’ is not limitless; it is strictly modulated by the thermal environment. The interaction between temperature and CO2 was highly significant (P < 0.001). While biomass accumulation was maximized at the optimal temperature of 26°C combined with 1000 ppm CO2, the slope of the growth response flattened significantly when the temperature was raised to 29°C. From a physiological standpoint, this suggests that 29°C represents a supra‐optimal thermal threshold for this species when coupled with high CO2.

Some researchers suggest that net growth is determined by the delicate balance between photosynthesis and respiration. 34 , 35 While high CO2 induces stomatal closure—improving water use efficiency—simultaneous heat stress accelerates the rate of dark respiration and maintenance respiration. In our study, it appears that the metabolic cost of maintaining cellular integrity and repairing heat‐induced damage at 29°C offset the additional carbon gain provided by the 1000 ppm CO2. This contradicts the broad generalization by some researchers that the combination of high CO2 and temperature almost universally accelerates weed growth. Our data indicates that even a moderate warming of 3°C above the optimum can act as a ‘physiological brake,’ preventing Xanthium strumarium from fully exploiting the CO2‐enriched atmosphere. This nuance is crucial for modeling future weed pressure; it implies that in regions where climate change involves both factors, weed biomass may not increase linearly. 36

Beyond physiological responses, this study highlighted significant intraspecific variation among populations collected from different altitudes. Low‐altitude populations (e.g., Diy21, Ant6, Şan63) consistently exhibited higher biomass accumulation and greater responsiveness to CO2 compared to high‐altitude populations (e.g., Erz25, Kay38). This variation can be interpreted through the lens of evolutionary trade‐offs. As noted by some researchers, plants originating from lower altitudes often adapt to longer growing seasons and higher nutrient availability by investing resources into rapid vegetative growth (a competitive strategy). In contrast, populations from high altitudes or harsh climates may be genetically programmed for slower, more conservative growth to ensure survival against abiotic stresses (a stress‐tolerant strategy). 37

The fact that the Diy21 population showed the highest phenotypic plasticity and responsiveness to CO2 suggests that certain genotypes within the Xanthium strumarium species complex are pre‐adapted to thrive under future climate conditions. Similarly, some studies have emphasized that seed origin plays a key role in morphological development. 33 , 38 This implies that as climate change progresses, we may observe a shift in weed population genetics, where highly plastic, fast‐growing genotypes like Diy21 become dominant in agricultural landscapes, potentially displacing less responsive populations. However, given that Xanthium strumarium exhibits relatively little overall genetic variation due to its largely autogamous nature, 20 the observed phenotypic differences among populations at various altitudes may be driven by localized epigenetic adaptations or subtle genetic shifts. Therefore, future studies should incorporate detailed molecular characterizations to better understand the population genetic structure and fully elucidate the basis of these differential responses.

Herbicide efficacy and mode of action mechanisms

Perhaps the most scientifically challenging finding of this research is the differential response of herbicide efficacy based on the mode of action. A prevailing hypothesis in weed science is the ‘dilution effect,’ which posits that the increased biomass produced under high CO2 ‘dilutes’ the herbicide concentration within plant tissues, thereby reducing its lethality. 23 While this phenomenon has been widely reported for contact herbicides and glyphosate, 39 our results for trifloxysulfuron‐sodium challenge this paradigm.

Trifloxysulfuron‐sodium, a systemic acetolactate synthase (ALS) inhibitor, became significantly more effective (ED50 values decreased) at 1000 ppm CO2, particularly at 26°C. We propose a mechanism based on ‘source‐to‐sink’ translocation dynamics to explain this unexpected result. Systemic herbicides rely on the plant's phloem transport system to move from the leaves (source) to the active growing points or meristems (sink). Under elevated CO2, the photosynthetic rate and sugar production in the leaves are significantly boosted. This creates a high‐pressure gradient in the phloem, accelerating the transport of photosynthates throughout the plant.

Since trifloxysulfuron moves concurrently with these photosynthates, the stimulated metabolism at 1000 ppm CO2 likely facilitated the rapid and efficient translocation of the herbicide to the apical meristems. Essentially, the physiological ‘pump’ driving the herbicide was working faster. Consequently, a lethal dose reached the target site more quickly, overcoming the plant's detoxification mechanisms before the biomass dilution effect could take hold. This mechanism aligns with findings from some studies, which observed that controlling certain weeds with systemic herbicides can be maintained or even improved under high CO2 conditions when the plant is metabolically active. This finding is crucial because it suggests that for systemic herbicides, the ‘dilution effect’ is not a universal rule; rather, efficacy depends on whether the rate of translocation outpaces the rate of biomass accumulation. 40

In sharp contrast, the efficacy of fluometuron remained remarkably stable across all temperature and CO2 regimes, with no significant statistical differences in ED50 values. Fluometuron is a photosystem II (PSII) inhibitor that is primarily applied to the soil and taken up by roots, moving via the xylem (transpiration stream). Its efficacy is less dependent on the complex carbohydrate translocation pathways that phloem‐mobile herbicides require.

Although high CO2 is known to induce partial stomatal closure which theoretically reduces transpiration and xylem transport our results suggest that this reduction was not sufficient to hinder the lethal uptake of fluometuron. The stability of fluometuron may also be attributed to its residual activity in the soil, which provides a continuous supply of the active ingredient, buffering against physiological fluctuations in the plant. This stability indicates that soil‐active herbicides are a ‘climate‐resilient’ management tool. As suggested by a study 41 relying on pre‐emergence or soil‐active chemistry might offer farmers a more consistent level of control compared to foliar‐applied herbicides, whose performance is highly sensitive to the instantaneous physiological state (e.g., leaf thickness, cuticle composition, metabolic rate) of the weed under changing environmental conditions. While relying on soil‐active herbicides offers a viable agronomic strategy for climate resilience, it is imperative to add a cautionary note about their environmental footprint. Increased use of these chemicals warrants careful management due to their potential for prolonged soil persistence, leaching into groundwater, and detrimental impacts on non‐target soil microbiomes and aquatic ecosystems.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provides empirical evidence that the future invasive potential of Xanthium strumarium will be driven by the complex interplay between atmospheric CO2 and temperature; While elevated CO2 acts as a potent fertilizer, enhancing biomass accumulation, this advantage is strictly limited by thermal thresholds, as heat stress effectively suppresses these gains. Consequently, X. strumarium is poised to become a more aggressive competitor, particularly in low‐altitude regions, yet our findings challenge the prevailing pessimistic view of chemical control by revealing a distinct divergence in herbicide efficacy by mode of action. Specifically, the systemic herbicide trifloxysulfuron‐sodium exhibited amplified efficacy under elevated CO2, likely due to accelerated phloem translocation, whereas the soil‐active fluometuron demonstrated remarkable stability regardless of environmental fluctuations.

These results imply that climate change need not result in a loss of control; instead, future management strategies should become ‘climate‐smart’ by leveraging the increased susceptibility of weeds to systemic herbicides during active growth periods while relying on soil‐applied chemistries as a resilient foundation under heat‐stressed conditions. It should also be noted that the relatively low light levels (55–90 μmol m−2 s−1 PAR) in this greenhouse study may have limited overall biomass accumulation and influenced the magnitude of the observed CO2 fertilization effect. Therefore, while our findings provide critical insights into mode‐of‐action‐specific responses, they require field validation under natural light regimes and realistic climate scenarios before definitive management recommendations can be made.
