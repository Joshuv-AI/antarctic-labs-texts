Enhancing explainability in epidemiological predictions using fuzzy logic integrated with machine and deep learning algorithms
Authors: Ubaida Fatima, Rabia Khushal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpidemiological data is often analyzed without fully accounting for the uncertainties that are key to understanding the nuances of the dataset. While traditional approaches like the SIR mathematical model provide valuable insights, our study aims to offer a different perspective by employing fuzzy logic. This methodology assigns weightage to selected factors, creating a framework that not only captures uncertainties but also improves data handling and interpretation. By reducing the number of features, our approach makes datasets more manageable while preserving their essential information. We applied this method to two epidemiological datasets, the H1N1 and seasonal vaccine dataset and the COVID-19 dataset. Initially, classical machine learning algorithms SVM, ensemble algorithm XGBoost, and deep learning algorithm ANN, were used to analyze the data. Subsequently, the features were fuzzified, resulting in the development of fuzzy machine learning and fuzzy deep learning algorithms. These algorithms consistently delivered reliable results within acceptable ranges. To further validate their effectiveness, we tested the proposed algorithms on datasets from other domains, including a diabetes healthcare dataset and a student performance dataset. The results demonstrated enhanced insights, optimized outcomes, and improved data management. Our study highlights how fuzzy logic can provide better insights and address uncertainties, offering a novel approach to studying epidemiological data.Keywords: Epidemiology dataset, Vaccine, COVID-19, Machine learning, Deep learning, Fuzzy logicSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Data integration, Data mining, Data processing

Introduction

Epidemiology

Epidemiology can be discussed as the fundamental science of public health that focuses on the individuals who share one or more than one observable characteristics for instance social group, income level, type of worker, or where they live, from which the data is extracted, analyzed and interpreted. The epidemiological data support objectives such as the prevention of disease, disability, and death and the promotion of well-being1. Epidemics can arise from infectious or chronic diseases. The infectious disease epidemic is the rapid spread of pathogens such as viruses, bacteria, fungi, or parasites. The pandemic is endemic on a colossal scale that can spread across regions, countries, and continents. The pandemic eventually became endemic which can be stated as the behavior of the infection is predictable however it remains present in the environment1,2. Some endemic diseases’ names have been visualized in Fig. 1.

Visualization of commonly found endemic diseases.

When infectious diseases are contagious, they are called communicable diseases otherwise noncommunicable diseases. Infectious diseases continue to threaten health today but prevention and control efforts for instance chlorine in water, penicillin, vaccines, and quarantine have declined infectious disease to be the primary reason for death1.

Influenza is an infectious communicable disease caused by influenza virus3,4. The seasonal influenza epidemic is the cause of millions of respiratory diseases and causes the death of over half a million individuals annually5,6. It swiftly evolves and changes over time, showcasing its complexity and need for targeted intervention to address public health concerns3. H1N1 flu also called swine flu is a type of influenza A virus. The World Health Organization (WHO) in 2009 declared the swine flu as a pandemic that was over in 2010. It approximately caused 284,400 deaths worldwide. However, people still can get H1N1 and spread it. The pandemic’s H1N1 flu virus has now turned into one of the strains that causes seasonal flu. It can cause illness, hospitalization, and death, especially for people who are at high risk7. Vaccination has lowered morbidity and mortality from infectious diseases. It promotes the immune defenses from infections8,9. Influenza vaccination is important for preparedness, prevention, and control of influenza10.

The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, has had a massive impact on global health, economies, and social systems since it first emerged in late 2019. The symptoms of COVID-19 can range from mild, like fever and cough, to severe respiratory problems11. High-risk individuals particularly the elderly, have been hit hardest, with higher mortality rates, which has highlighted the need for targeted health interventions. Countries worldwide responded with different strategies, from strict lockdowns to more relaxed measures, depending on their public health approaches12. One of the most crucial steps in controlling the virus has been the rapid development and distribution of vaccines11. This situation is reminiscent of the earlier SARS outbreak, which was caused by a similar coronavirus and first appeared in Southern China in 2002. That outbreak resulted in 8,096 cases and 774 deaths across 26 countries, but it was brought under control by 2003 due to quarantine and isolation efforts. Unlike SARS, COVID-19 spread much more widely and persistently, highlighting the need for stronger global responses, greater international collaboration, and quicker vaccine development13.

SIR model: a fundamental framework in epidemiology

The Susceptible-Infectious-Recovered (SIR) model is one of the most widely recognized compartmental models used to study the spread of infectious diseases. It divides the population into three distinct groups:

Susceptible (S): Individuals at risk of contracting the infection.

Susceptible (S): Individuals at risk of contracting the infection.

Infectious (I): Those who have the disease and can transmit it.

Infectious (I): Those who have the disease and can transmit it.

Recovered (R): Individuals who have recovered and gained immunity.

Recovered (R): Individuals who have recovered and gained immunity.

This model is particularly suited for infectious diseases that confer immunity post-infection. While the SIR model lacks a closed-form analytical solution for the temporal dynamics of its populations, it does provide explicit analytical solutions for key metrics such as the peak infectious population and the ultimate sizes of the susceptible and recovered groups at endemic equilibrium. These features make it a valuable tool for quickly estimating outbreak dimensions14.

Fuzzy machine learning and deep learning for epidemiology data

Machine learning algorithms are a subset of artificial intelligence that enables computers to learn from data, identify patterns, and make predictions or decisions15,16. Deep learning is a subset of machine learning that uses multilayered neural networks called deep neural networks to simulate the human brain’s complex decision-making power17. Fuzzy logic is a mathematical framework designed to handle uncertainty and imprecision in decision-making processes. It distinguishes itself from classical binary logic by allowing for degrees of truth18. Fuzzy logic has been integrated with machine learning and also with deep learning in several studies for disease prediction19,20. The integration has enhanced diagnostic accuracy, and treatment efficiency and facilitates early diagnosis in disease predictions19. This integration has laid the foundation of using methods of methods which are fuzzy machine learning and fuzzy deep learning for epidemiological data.

Recent studies have further emphasized the importance of this integration. For instance, in the study21, fuzzy logic was used to transform crisp biomedical data into fuzzy values, allowing for better handling of uncertainty in hormonal diagnosis. This approach demonstrated how assigning membership functions to features enhances model interpretability and leads to improved prediction accuracy. Applying similar fuzzy logic concepts to epidemiological datasets strengthens the model’s ability to deal with real-world uncertainty and complexity, which is crucial for public health decision-making.

The growing complexity and uncertainty in epidemiological data demand analytical models that go beyond traditional binary decision-making. Conventional machine and deep learning models, while powerful, often lack transparency and struggle with handling ambiguous, overlapping, or incomplete information common in healthcare datasets. Fuzzy logic offers a mathematical framework to manage such uncertainty, making it a valuable tool for enhancing interpretability and flexibility in predictive models used for disease surveillance, diagnosis, and risk assessment.

Integrating fuzzy logic with machine learning enables multi-valued classification, allowing models to capture degrees of disease presence or risk rather than simple yes/no outputs. This is particularly crucial in epidemiology, where symptoms and disease progressions often exist on a spectrum. By utilizing fuzzy logic, models can consider a broader set of input features and provide nuanced predictions, ultimately supporting more informed decision-making and early intervention strategies in public health.

Research gap

In several studies, the probability of getting the H1N1 vaccine and seasonal vaccine has been studied and superficial insights have been provided. However, no one analyzed the input feature uncertainties and risk of individual factors by giving weightage to the factors. In this study, the fuzzification of features from the epidemiological dataset has been executed. Doing so helps in analyzing the strong variable connection with other variables leading to better data handling and making better decisions to get vaccinated to combat future pandemics, especially for high-risk and very high-risk individuals. We extended this approach to the COVID-19 dataset, applying the same methodology to further validate its effectiveness and versatility.

Literature review

Research work related to SIR in epidemiology is, in22 development of an accurate prediction model has been studied, for dengue cases in Malaysia, where no vaccines or specific treatments are available. Three different forecasting techniques (Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (SARIMA), Dynamic Harmonic Regression (DHR), and Neural Network Autoregressive Model (NNAR)) were applied to six years of weekly dengue case data from Malaysia. The DHR has shown the most accurate results. Similarly. In this study23 extension of the deterministic SIR model has been performed. The model allows infected individuals to recover thus preserving a functional relationship between susceptible and removed populations. They explores three behavioral modes which are based on initial epidemic curve values. Hence provides new insights into epidemic curve properties. This study24 enhances the SIR mathematical model by differentiating the infected class and incorporating parameters to assess vaccine effectiveness on disease spread. In this study25 A susceptible-infected-recovered (SIR) model has been introduced for influenza that utilizes Caputo fractional derivatives. The model distinguishes between susceptible individuals and those who are infected, taking into account their vaccination status and treatment regimens. The research includes an analysis of the model’s stability and underscores the crucial roles that vaccination and treatment play in managing the spread of influenza.

Work related to machine learning in epidemiology is, in26 machine learning models such as support vector classifier (SVC), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and others have been used to predict vector-borne diseases based on tabular epidemiological data. A dataset comprising 65 features and 1262 records was employed for the training of machine learning models. Likewise, in27 machine learning techniques integrated with the SEIR (Susceptible-Exposed-Infectious-Recovered) mathematical model to analyze epidemiological data. The results achieved show a notable accuracy of 94% using the MAPE evaluation technique. Hence concluding that the developed model can successfully identify disease spread patterns. Similarly, in28 ourWorldIndia website has been extracted. A dedicated machine learning model was proposed to forecast the future using a real-life dataset of COVID-19. The study concluded that the discussed approach can be applied to the dataset of any country in the world to predict the number of daily cases infected by the coronavirus. In this study29 machine learning models have been used to determine whether an individual has Covid-19 based on their symptoms and also forecasts the potential severity of their condition. Hence aiding in decision making whether patients need to be admitted to the intensive care unit or require a ventilator.

Work related to fuzzy logic in epidemiology is, in30 fuzzy SEIR model has been introduced for a better understanding of amoebiasis, a disease that is caused by the protozoan Entamoeba histolytica. The fuzzy SEIR model incorporates uncertainties in paramount parameters such as the disease transmission rate, disease-induced death rate, and recovery rate. Likewise, the aforementioned methodology has been used to understand the dynamics of malaria disease in31. In32 Data on COVID-19 has been collected and analyzed using different statistical tests. Moreover, fuzzy logic has also been used to model the chances of COVID-19 based on the results of the statistical tests.

Research related to the hybridization of fuzzy logic and machine learning is, that this fusion of techniques has been applied in different studies, for instance, to find out the uncertainty of polycystic ovary syndrome21, for the prediction of a healthy lifestyle33, for prediction of diabetes risk using modifiable factors34, for prediction of osteoporosis risk35 and for the prediction of heart disease risk36. Relevant work related to biological datasets is in37 a fuzzy framework with Bio-Link Strength is introduced to quantify protein interaction strength and effectively identify crucial proteins in PPI networks that outperform traditional measures. Likewise, in38 a novel centrality metric, Global Clustering Coefficient-Dependent Degree Centrality, has been developed to analyze profitable products in large networks such as biological datasets of yeast protein–protein interaction networks. Similarly, researchers in39 worked on diabetes or healthcare datasets through supervised machine learning.

One study40 in the domain of explainable AI in healthcare investigated the ethical and practical importance of transparency in AI-based clinical decision systems. The authors adopted a multidisciplinary framework, analyzing explainability from technological, legal, and clinical viewpoints. Their evaluation used conceptual analysis alongside the “Principles of Biomedical Ethics” framework, emphasizing that omitting explainability may hinder the safe and ethical integration of AI in medical practice. Another study41 highlights the challenge of cryptocurrency price prediction due to high market volatility influenced by multiple factors, which often leads to investment uncertainty. To address this, machine learning algorithms including LSTM, SVM, RF, and a hybrid LSTM-RF model were applied, with the hybrid model showing superior predictive accuracy. The study also integrates Explainable AI (XAI) techniques to generate human-centric visualizations, aiding users and investors in understanding model outputs and making informed decisions.

Studies in32–36, particularly the study in21, demonstrate the effective integration of fuzzy logic with machine learning to enhance disease prediction using real-life medical datasets. These works emphasize how fuzzy logic enables models to go beyond binary classification by incorporating multi-valued logic—such as three-valued or four-valued systems—which better captures the uncertainty and complexity inherent in medical diagnoses. By combining multiple features simultaneously, the adaptive fuzzy machine learning logic model provides a more nuanced diagnostic output, such as identifying not only the presence or absence of a syndrome but also indicating the likelihood of its occurrence. This transformation from crisp binary classes to multi-class fuzzy categories enables a broader and more realistic spectrum of diagnosis, supporting improved decision-making in clinical settings.

Fuzzy machine learning and fuzzy deep learning on considered dataset

For a better understanding of input features and consideration of their uncertainties, the dataset has been fuzzified as done in different studies21,33–36. In this research work, one strong input feature has been compared with the remaining input features. By fuzzification, we are assigning membership degrees to the output of strong variables with others and increasing the weightage of other variables. A fuzzy membership function quantifies the membership degree (degree of belongingness) of a particular variable to a fuzzy set, thus representing the uncertainties in values61. By doing this the feature’s impact can be analyzed from different perspectives, the dataset can be handled appropriately since several features would be reduced, and uncertainties of features can also be considered. Previous studies have primarily focused on estimating the likelihood of receiving the H1N1 and seasonal vaccines, often yielding only surface-level insights. These approaches have largely overlooked the uncertainty inherent in individual input features and the varying impact of risk-related variables. To address this gap, our study introduces a fuzzy transformation of features within epidemiological datasets, assigning weighted degrees (membership values) of influence to each factor. This fuzzification process enhances the interpretability of variable relationships, supports more informed decision-making, and allows for improved identification of individuals at high or very high risk. To assess the robustness and adaptability of this methodology, we further applied it to COVID-19 data, demonstrating its broader applicability across different epidemiological contexts.

Overall vision

The results of this study demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed fuzzy logic integration in machine learning and deep learning algorithms. The analysis of two primary datasets, H1N1 and Seasonal Vaccine, and COVID-19 showed that the fuzzy-enhanced models outperformed traditional machine learning techniques like SVM, XGBoost, and ANN in terms of accuracy, precision, F1 score, and computational time. Additionally, validation with two other datasets, Diabetes Prediction and Student Performance Prediction, further highlighted the versatility of the proposed approach. The fuzzy logic methodology contributed to better data handling and more insightful decision-making, particularly in terms of managing uncertainties and identifying key variable relationships, leading to improved predictive performance across all domains tested.

Also, The study in70 demonstrates how high-dimensional medical data can be effectively transformed into a lower-dimensional space using fuzzy logic conceptualizations, enhancing both interpretability and computational efficiency. The Fuzzy Quantified Machine Learning (FQML) framework allows the inclusion of all significant features, leading to improved outcome prediction. Additionally, the introduction of novel algorithms—Fuzzy Quantified Support Vector Machine (FQSVM) and Fuzzy Quantified K-Nearest Neighbors (FQKNN)—provides efficient and accurate prediction of chronic diseases. This approach highlights the value of integrating fuzzy logic with machine learning models for handling complex and uncertain healthcare data.

Limitations

i.The proposed fuzzy logic-based framework requires data scientists to possess strong domain knowledge in epidemiology for effective data transformation and accurate interpretation. Without this expertise, there is a risk of misrepresenting key factors during the fuzzification process, potentially impacting the quality and reliability of the analysis.

Conclusion

Usually, in different studies, superficial insights into epidemiological datasets have been provided. The uncertainties have not been considered which are essential for gaining better insights of the dataset. Moreover, usually SIR mathematical model has been considered to study the epidemiological data. Thus, to study the data from a different perspective, that is providing weightage to the randomly chosen factors using the fuzzy logic methodology has been executed. Doing so aids in analyzing the uncertainties of the variables, provides better insights, and also aids in data handling. Since the number of features has been reduced data can be handled more appropriately. Two epidemiological datasets have been considered which are H1N1 and seasonal vaccine dataset and COVID-19 dataset. The first typical machine learning algorithm is SVM, the ensemble machine learning algorithm is the Xgboost and the deep learning algorithm has been applied to the normal dataset afterward the considered features of the dataset have been fuzzified by providing the weightage to the factors. Hence fuzzy machine learning and fuzzy deep learning algorithms have been produced which provide results that are found to be in an acceptable range. The validation of the proposed algorithms has also been executed using the healthcare dataset of diabetes and the education dataset of student performance which provide optimized results. Thus, leads to the verdict that the proposed algorithm can be applied to any dataset of domain to gain deeper insights, better data handling, and for consideration of uncertainties. The future would be to integrate the proposed algorithm with the SIR mathematical modeling.

In conclusion, the proposed fuzzy machine learning logic and fuzzy deep learning logic concepts that transform the data and consider multiple features, effectively address the uncertainties often overlooked in traditional epidemiological studies. Unlike conventional approaches, which generally provide superficial insights, the proposed methods introduce a mechanism for assigning weightage to factors, allowing for a deeper analysis of the variables’ uncertainties. By reducing the number of features and enhancing data handling, these approaches offer improved insights and decision-making capabilities. The methods were applied to two major epidemiological datasets—H1N1 and seasonal vaccine data, and the COVID-19 dataset—using SVM, XGBoost, and deep learning algorithms, with results showing that the fuzzified models produce outcomes within an acceptable range. These contributions advance the field by offering more reliable and interpretable models for epidemiological data analysis.
