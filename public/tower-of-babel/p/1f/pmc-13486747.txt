Hierarchical nano-orderings enable extraordinary cryogenic strength-ductility synergy in a medium-entropy alloy
Authors: Dongdong Zhang, Hengchao Shi, Jinyu Zhang, Jianjun Bian, Yating Ran, Qi Liu, Xuanlai Chen, Junhua Luan, Zengbao Jiao, Tao Yang, Jun Sun, Zibin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCryogenic alloys simultaneously achieving ultrahigh yield strength (YS ≥ 2.0 GPa) alongside substantial uniform elongation (UE) are critically needed, yet fundamentally constrained by the strength-ductility trade-off. Guided by Eshelby’s inclusion theory, we reconcile this challenge by designing a novel hierarchical nano-ordering (HNO) architecture within an ultrafine-grained NiCoCr-based multi-principal element alloy, fabricated via combining additive-manufacturing and tailored post-processing. The HNOs—featuring a controlled size distribution and interfacial properties—comprise dispersed incoherent σ-dispersoids (~70 nm), a high density of bimodal coherent L12 precipitates (~5 and 26 nm) and widespread, highly-distorted local-chemical-orderings (~0.7 nm). This configuration introduces a stepwise increase in elastic strain energy, which in turn triggers the sequential activation of potent dislocation sources. The resulting mobile dislocations engage in extensive and varied interactions with the nano-orderings during deformation, thereby activating cooperative strain-hardening mechanisms. These nano-architectures thus function simultaneously as dislocation generators, strain-hardening enablers, and strengthening agents. Consequently, this HNO-mediated self-hardening and self-ductilizing mechanisms yield an exceptional cryogenic (77 K) property set: YS of ~1.96 GPa, ultimate tensile strength of ~2.35 GPa, and UE of ~22%, surpassing both the cast counterpart and all previously reported advanced alloys. This strategy establishes a transformative paradigm for designing ultrastrong-yet-ductile materials through integrated manufacturing approaches.Subject terms: Metals and alloys, Mechanical properties

Introduction

The rapid advancement of modern industry and technology—particularly in fields such as liquefied gas storage, aerospace engineering, and fusion reactor systems—has generated a critical demand for ultrastrong-yet-ductile metallic materials for cryogenic applications1–6. However, simultaneously achieving a 2-GPa yield strength (YS) with sufficient uniform elongation (UE) at 77 K remains a significant challenge. This is due to the pronounced deterioration of the strength-ductility trade-off in ultrahigh-strength alloys at ultralow temperatures, often causing a severe compromise in ductility7,8. A notable example is the widely recognized, cold-worked MP35N alloy, which achieves a YS of 1.8–2.2 GPa at 77 K but is typically limited to a UE of less than 5%9,10.

Recently emerging multi-principal elemental high/medium-entropy alloys (H/MEAs) appear to be a potential candidate to mitigate this dilemma in reaching ultrahigh YS and large UE simultaneously7,11–14. However, this material system has largely failed to fulfill its initial promise. While numerous face-centered cubic (FCC)-structured HEAs have been developed, exhibiting high ductility especially at low temperatures, their YS typically remains insufficient, rarely exceeding 1.5 GPa1,5,15–17. To elevate alloy strength to ultrahigh levels (≥ 2.0 GPa), various strengthening mechanisms—including solid solution, grain refinement, nanoprecipitation (NP), dislocation strengthening, and their combinations—have been explored. Nevertheless, these approaches often result in inadequate strain-hardening capacity, characterized by low strain-hardening rates (SHRs), rapid saturation, abrupt SHR declines, Lüders banding or serrated plastic flow, causing inferior UE18–20. A representative case is the ultrafine-grained (UFG) NiCoV MEA, which achieves 2.1 GPa YS at 77 K but exhibits unstable Lüders strain preceding its stress-strain curve18.

Recent advances demonstrate that the strength-ductility trade-off can be mitigated through innovative strengthening mechanisms that simultaneously enhance strain-hardening capacity by promoting dislocation multiplication, accumulation, and entanglement via microstructural heterogeneities5,18,21,22. This suggests that an effective strategy should integrate mechanisms that not only strengthen the alloy but also sustain adequate strain hardening through diverse dislocation interactions, which are crucial for preventing strain localization in materials. Consequently, the key challenge for 2 GPa-grade alloys lies in achieving this synergistic integration through meticulous microstructural design.

In this work, we address this challenge through a novel microstructural design strategy that introduces hierarchical nano-orderings (HNOs) with precisely controlled variations in size, crystal structure, interfacial characteristics, and density within a UFG NiCoCr-AlTa MEA (see Supplementary Note 1 for details). Our approach builds upon the theoretical framework of classical Eshelby’s inclusion theory23,24, which demonstrates that the elastic strain energy (W) of the surrounding matrix fundamentally depends on both the size (r) of hard inclusions/orderings and their lattice misfit (δ). Crucially, larger ordering size and greater misfit strains generate higher distortion energy: W ∝ r3δ2 (discussed later), thereby lowering the dislocation nucleation barrier and facilitating dislocation generation during deformation. The implementation of this design principle involves two synergistic mechanisms that clearly separate the functional roles of HNOs during plastic deformation. First, a carefully engineered stepwise increase in strain energy, achieved through a broad size distribution of nano-orderings and tailored interfacial properties, facilitates the sequential activation of potent dislocation sources. Second, the resulting mobile dislocations engage in extensive and diverse interactions with HNOs, activating cooperative strain-hardening pathways that withstand high flow stresses. Importantly, these HNOs simultaneously function as effective strengthening components for high YS while maintaining excellent strain-hardening capacity. However, the conventional preparation process of alloys is quite challenging in achieving the abovementioned structural hierarchy, and thus it is urgent to adopt innovative processes and strategies for microstructure precise control.

Herein, the HNOs nanostructure was elaborately manipulated through an innovative approach via hybrid-additive manufacturing (AM) processing that exploits the extreme non-equilibrium kinetics afforded by AM, combined with carefully controlled post-treatment in a (NiCoCr)92Al6Ta2 (at.%) MEA (see Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Figs. 1–12 for details) and denoted as HNO MEA hereafter. This distinctive nanostructural arrangement within UFGs (~0.76 μm in grain diameter) comprises: (i) dispersed incoherent σ-nanoparticles (INPs, ~70 nm), (ii) a high density of bimodal coherent L12 nanoprecipitates (CNPs, ~5 and ~26 nm), and (iii) widespread highly-distorted local-chemical-orderings (LCOs, ~0.7 nm). In addition to their synergistic strengthening, the HNOs facilitate the activation of efficient sequential dislocation sources and cooperative strain-hardening mechanisms. Consequently, our HNOs MEA exhibits an exceptional combination of mechanical properties, including an ultrahigh YS of ~1.96 GPa, an ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of ~2.35 GPa, and a large UE of ~22% at 77 K. This remarkable strength-ductility synergy at 77 K surpasses that of all previously reported high-performance alloys.

Furthermore, the hybrid-AM manufacturing strategy we proposed not only fully leverages the inimitable advantages of AM in creating unique precursors but also subsequently applies established post-processing techniques to overcome the inherent defects of printed materials (such as porosity). It transforms these precursors into stable, superior overall performance—a feat difficult to achieve with any single process alone. Furthermore, this hybrid-AM manufacturing strategy demonstrated the multifaceted utilization of AM’s strengths, which expands its application scope and enriches manufacturing potential, representing significant promise of this strategy in practical applications. This work also provides an innovative inspiration and solution for manufacturing next-generation high-performance alloys or components for extreme environments, one that integrates microstructural design freedom, scalable production, and practical manufacturability.

Results and discussions

Unique hierarchical nano-ordering structures

Figure 1 systematically illustrates the designed HNO-structures in this alloy. Electron-backscatter-diffraction (EBSD, Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 13) analysis confirms the presence of equiaxed UFGs with an average grain size (dg) of 0.76 ± 0.23 μm (considering annealing twin boundaries). Figure 1b–t further elucidate the atomic-to-nanoscale structural hierarchy and interfacial characteristics. As shown in the high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image (Fig. 1b) and SEM image (Supplementary Fig. 14a), profuse nanodispersoids in bright contrast are uniformly dispersed along GBs and within grain interiors. Through combined analysis of atomic-resolution structural image and fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns (Fig. 1c, d), these precipitates were identified as the σ-phase (Fig. 1e). Figure 1f reveals an incoherent σ/matrix interface. Additionally, STEM energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping reveals substantial Ta enrichment within these INPs (Fig. 1g). The σ-INPs have a mean diameter of 70 ± 25.4 nm and a number density of 6.3 × 1019 m−3 (Fig. 1s, Supplementary Fig. 15). While achieving a uniform nanoscale dispersion is often a challenge for INP synthesis, it was successfully realized in this work. Furthermore, during annealing, a robust Zener drag effect exerted by σ-particles on GB migration effectively promotes grain refinement, resulting in a UFG structure.

HAADF-STEM image further reveals a high-density of bimodal L12 CNPs embedded within the matrix (Fig. 1h). Quantitative analysis reveals mean diameters of 25.6 ± 6.5 nm (for coarse-L12) and 5.3 ± 0.81 nm (for fine-L12), with corresponding densities of 2.2 × 1022 m−3 and 3.9 × 1024 m−3, respectively (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 15). Atomic-scale TEM imaging and FFT analysis (Fig. 1i, j) confirm a fully coherent L12/matrix interface, with the absence of misfit dislocations. Atom-probe-tomography (APT) characterization shows a bimodal distribution of L12 nanoprecipitates, as visualized using Ni/Al/Ta iso-concentration surface (Fig. 1k, Supplementary Fig. 16a). The architecture of bimodal-L12 results from the AM-enabled heterogeneous distribution of Ta—a potent L12 stabilizer, creating pronounced spatial variations in the thermodynamic propensity for L12 formation (Supplementary Note 1). The proxigram across the interface reveals elemental partitioning between the L12 and matrix, with Ni, Al, and Ta preferentially segregating to the L12, while the matrix is enriched in other elements (Fig. 1l).

Atomic-scale HAADF-STEM imaging (Fig. 1m) reveals the presence of sub-nanoscale clusters within the matrix, distinguished by Z-contrast variations. These clusters were identified as Ta-rich, as confirmed by the brightest atomic columns in the enlarged HAADF image (Fig. 1n) and their corresponding 3D intensity profiles (Fig. 1o). The FFT pattern (Fig. 1p) displays additional diffuse diffraction spots located midway between the primary reflections, indicative of localized structural modulations. Through inverse FFT (IFFT) analysis of these extra reflections, the atomic clusters were identified as short-range LCOs (Fig. 1q). These LCOs exhibit a mean size of ~0.66 nm and an ultrahigh density of 2.6 × 1026 m−3 (Fig. 1s, Supplementary Fig. 15). Obviously, the presence of LCOs accompanied by chemical heterogeneities at the sub‑nanometer scale is extensive in our alloy. The primary thermodynamic driving force for the LCO nucleation is the varying chemical affinities, quantified by mixing enthalpies, between different elemental pairs. In the present alloy, for instance, the Ni-Ta (−29 kJ mol−1) pairs exhibit large negative mixing enthalpies25. On one hand, this drives the formation of nanoscale L12 intermetallics via ordered arrangement within the lattice during heat treatment. On the other hand, those remnant atoms embedded within the matrix continue to drag them together disorderedly due to their relatively high chemical affinities, leading to the formation of LCOs. Geometric-phase-analysis (GPA) mapping (Fig. 1r) further unveils significant strain fields induced by the Ta-enriched LCOs, distinguishing them from conventional LCOs observed in systems such as NiCoV and NiCoCr MEAs26,27, where atomic clusters composed of atoms with similar sizes produce negligible lattice distortion. Figure 1s summarizes the hierarchical nanostructural architecture, where these ordered features—spanning three orders of magnitude in size (atomic to nanoscale) and six orders in density—are uniformly distributed within the UFG matrix, as schematically represented in Fig. 1t. This novel nanostructure was achieved via AM-enabled Ta-mediated INPs and chemical heterogeneity, which is unattainable in the cast counterpart (Supplementary Note 1) and significantly different from those reported multi-scale structures in terms of approaches and characteristics28–30.

Exceptional mechanical properties at 77 K

Figure 2 presents the exceptional mechanical properties of the HNOs-structured MEA (AM-P sample) at 77 K. To elucidate the critical role of HNOs in determining tensile properties, a comparative test was performed on a cast-P counterpart (representing a sample that the cast alloy underwent the same post-treatment as the AM-P sample) containing micro-scale Laves phase and nanoscale L12.

The YS and UTS of the HNOs alloy were measured to be 1963 ± 42 MPa and 2354 ± 46 MPa, respectively, accompanied by a high UE (corresponding to the engineering strain at peak stress) of 22.4 ± 1.1% at 77 K. The high YS arises from the thermal component of lattice friction stress (605 MPa) and the athermal parts, including precipitates hardening (699 MPa)—coherent-L12 (571 MPa) and incoherent-σ (128 MPa)—and GB hardening (529 MPa), as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 17. Furthermore, fractographic analysis reveals fine and densely distributed dimples (Supplementary Fig. 19), indicative of a ductile fracture mode in this MEA. In contrast, the cast-P sample exhibits significantly inferior properties, with YS, UTS, and UE values of only 1541 ±; 28MPa, 1652 ± 32MPa, and 14%, respectively. These values represent a 27% reduction in YS, a 42% reduction in UTS, and a 57% reduction in UE compared to the HNO-structured AM-P sample. Cross-sectional analysis of the fractured cast-P sample revealed that micro-scale Laves phase acts as primary crack initiation sites due to stress concentration (Supplementary Fig. 12d), thereby severely degrading ductility—even at substantially lower stress levels than those observed in the AM-P sample. Additionally, the formation of Laves consumes significant amounts of Ta, an element critical for L12 stabilization, thereby weakening precipitate strengthening.

As mentioned earlier, a sustained strain-hardening is the key to attaining high ductility22,31,32. Strain-hardening analysis further provides critical insights into the unique deformation behavior of the HNOs alloy (Fig. 2b, c). The SHR as a function of true strain εt reveals that this HNOs alloy exhibits exceptionally steady and sustained strain-hardening capacity at 77 K, significantly surpassing that of other tested samples. Notably, this material demonstrates a high initial SHR of up to 5.0 GPa and maintains a consistently elevated level (> 2.8 GPa) across a wide strain range, enabling it to withstand ultrahigh flow stresses. This impressively stable deformation behavior is highly advantageous compared to most reported 2.0 GPa-class alloys, which often exhibit undesirable deformation characteristics such as unstable Lüders strains, serrated plastic flow, plunges in the SHR curve, and even negative SHR18–20,33. In addition, achieving substantial SHR in alloys with ultrahigh YS is typically challenging; however, this HNOs alloy distinguishes itself by reconciling them—evidenced by a substantial strength differential (UTS - YS = 390 MPa) alongside high YS (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 4).

Furthermore, this HNOs-MEA exhibits exceptional cryogenic strength-ductility synergy, in terms of ultrahigh YS versus strength-ductility product (UTS × UE, ~52 GPa%) and UE, as demonstrated in Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 20 and Supplementary Table 4. This extraordinary property distinguishes the alloy from all reported high-performance structural materials, even surpassing recently developed best-performing HEAs3,5,15–17 and thus representing the most desirable strength-ductility combination of our alloy among all reported alloys tested at 77 K. Additionally, the fracture toughness KIc of our HNOs alloy was measured as 78.3 ± 3.1 MPam1/2 at 77 K (Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Fig. 21a). Considering the ultrahigh YS of this alloy, achieving such a toughness level is exceptionally challenging and is seldom reported in other alloy systems. A three-dimensional Ashby plot (Supplementary Fig. 21b and Supplementary Table 5) further substantiates the superior cryogenic combination of strength, ductility, and toughness in this MEA at 77 K. This unprecedented performance, ensuring substantial engineering reliability margins, positions our HNOs MEA as a promising candidate for next-generation cryogenic applications.

Microstructural origins of sustained strain-hardening and high uniform ductility

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the high UE of this HNOs MEA at 77 K, detailed TEM analysis of the deformed microstructures and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted. TEM observations indicated that the dislocation planar-slip dominates plasticity at low engineering strain ε (3%, Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 22), while the deformation mode transformed to the stacking-faults (SFs) dominance when the sample was stretched to medium (9%, Fig. 3c and d) and large (22%, Fig. 3e and f) strains (Supplementary Note 4). Furthermore, observations revealed that progressively denser SFs with ongoing strain (exhibiting SF spacings ranging from hundreds to several nanometers) lead to the formation of dense nanoscale SF-grids with Lomer-Cottrell (L-C) lock configuration, which dynamically refines the microstructures (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Note 4). These SF-structures not only hinder dislocation motion but also efficiently store dislocations, thereby enhancing strain hardening19,32,34. Furthermore, the formation of sessile dislocations through the intersection of non-parallel SFs and elastic repulsion between parallel SF-bundles also effectively contributes to strain hardening34,35.

The above insightful analysis envisages a size- and misfit-dependent dislocation-source mechanism, wherein the additional strain energy in the lattice could effectively reduce the energy barrier for dislocation nucleation37. In other words, coarser ordered domains preferentially facilitate dislocation nucleation at low strains, while finer ordered populations become activated at higher strains, as observed in our alloy. Consequently, these HNOs enable sequential and cooperative dislocation multiplication, with different orderings serving as sustainable dislocation sources under straining. This mechanism enables diverse subsequent interactions among different nano-orderings, thereby further enhancing strain hardening (discussed later). By contrast, systems containing uniformly sized inclusions with similar interfacial features would exhibit rapid exhaustion of dislocation sources at a relatively narrow stress/strain regime, causing limited deformability. The widespread distribution of HNOs-enabled dislocation sources generates dislocations/SFs at numerous sites, which not only spread out the plastic strain throughout the deformed volume but also increase the chances for dislocation entanglements and reactions, producing sessile junctions that dynamically harden alloys.

Bimodal shearable L12-precipitates: Fig. 5d–f further reveals extensive shearing interactions between dislocations/SFs with L12 precipitates. Atomic-scale TEM imaging proves that precipitate shearing induces complex planar defects within the ordered L12-particles, including superlattice intrinsic SFs (SISFs, Fig. 5f) and antiphase boundaries (APBs) (see Supplementary Fig. 26e). These residual defects serve as substantially more potent barriers to subsequent dislocation motion, compared to the ordinary defects. As strain precedes and an increasing fraction of precipitates undergo shearing, the progressive accumulation of these residual defects significantly produces a pronounced and sustained increase in flow stress with straining, enhancing strain-hardening. Furthermore, shearing of L12 was activated across multiple slip systems, leading to the formation of L-C lock within the ordered L12 (Fig. 5e). This architecture is less commonly observed and differs from the typical L-C lock within a disordered FCC matrix. They feature a stronger lock than ordinary ones and thus are more effective for multiplying and storing dislocations. Considering the dual role of L-C locks and interfaces as both dislocation sources and barriers19,32,44, the defective precipitates facilitate a distinctive self-hardening and self-ductilizing mechanism that sustains strain hardening.

The strain hardening mechanism operates through the following interrelated effects. First, the planar defect energy (γp) of the precipitates is substantially elevated by Ta additions46, enabling improved shearing resistance during straining. Second, the multiscale L12 facilitates their sequential deformation via shearing over an extended stress/strain range during deformation. Distorted-LCOs: To investigate the influences of LCOs on dislocation motion and strain hardening response, we performed MD simulations on a model NiCoCr alloy containing LCOs (Fig. 5g), using pure Cu (FCC, low SFE) as a reference (Fig. 5h). In NiCoCr, the dislocation line adopts a jagged, wavy configuration during glide, resembling experimental observations in our alloy (Fig. 5i). Specifically, dislocation motion proceeds via intermittent, nanoscale forward slips of local segments. The nanoscale swept areas associated with individual detrapping events occur sequentially along the dislocation line (highlighted in blue in Fig. 5g). This nanosegment-wise propagation contrasts sharply with the uniform, smooth glide of a long, straight dislocation line in conventional FCC Cu.

The above results unequivocally demonstrate the pronounced effect of LCOs on dislocation dynamics. The underlying mechanism originates from the substantial nanoscale structural and chemical heterogeneities introduced by LCOs within the concentrated solid solution. First, atomic misfit volumes generate heterogeneous elastic strain fields and spatially fluctuating Peierls friction stresses, even in equiatomic NiCoCr-based systems composed of constituents with similar atomic sizes47–49. Dislocations are thus attracted to energetically favorable fluctuations and repelled by unfavorable ones, creating energy barriers that must be overcome by local bowing (Fig. 5i). Second, LCOs cause spatially variable SFE, leading to uneven partial dislocation separation even along a single line21, as also observed in our alloy (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 22). Collectively, a gliding dislocation navigates through a “choppy sea” of LCO-mediated highly undulated energy landscape. Thus, each nanosegment encounters spatially varying energy barriers when transitioning between favorable and unfavorable lattice positions, resulting in dislocation proceeding via nanosegment trapping-detrapping alternately during interactions49. To minimize the total potential energy, the LCOs-driven unusual dislocation dynamics cause intermittent and sluggish motion by adopting a wavy, jagged line configuration, as schematically depicted in Fig. 5j. This mechanism is fundamentally different from conventional dilute solutions, where all segments experience equivalent local environments with negligible barriers between them.

Critically, such intermittent, jerky, and nanosegment-wise motion mechanisms significantly increase the likelihood of dislocation entanglement and interlocking, thereby promoting dislocation accumulation and storage, which in turn enhances the strain hardenability of the material21. Furthermore, previous studies have established that the efficacy of this mechanism scales with the degree of LCOs49,50. In the present alloy, the highly distorted LCO domains, further reinforced by the incorporation of large-sized Ta-atoms, which also reduce SFE in the FCC system51,52, amplify the fluctuations of lattice strain fields, Peierls stress, and SFE to a more pronounced extent relative to the LCO configuration in the model NiCoCr. Consequently, these mechanisms operate more efficiently in the current alloy, contributing to its superior strain hardening.

In this work, our designed HNO architectures exhibit three synergistic functions: serving as dislocation generators, strengthening agents, and strain-hardening enablers. This tri-functional mechanism endows the material with unique self-hardening and self-ductilizing capabilities. The cooperative interplay of multiple mechanisms activated by HNOs—including diverse dislocation generation pathways and enhanced strain hardening—effectively stabilizes plastic deformation under ultrahigh flow stresses. This contributes to high ductility while maintaining the ultrahigh YS enabled by potent strengthening. Collectively, these distinctive mechanisms work in concert to achieve a record-high strength-ductility synergy in our HNOs alloy at 77 K, successfully overcoming the inherent strain-hardening limitations typical of UFG alloys. Beyond its immediate findings, this work provides a foundation for innovative microstructure design strategies and establishes a viable framework for maximizing mechanical performance. By integrating novel manufacturing approaches with conventional processing techniques, we demonstrate that material properties can be elevated beyond the limits imposed by traditional manufacturing methods. Furthermore, this work expands the application scope of the AM technique for microstructural design and enriches its significance in materials science.

Methods

Materials preparation

A bulk (NiCoCr)92Al6Ta2 MEA precursor was synthesized from high-purity raw metals (purity ≥ 99.9%) via vacuum levitation melting to produce ingots, which were subsequently atomized into pre-alloyed powders with particle sizes ranging from 15 to 53 μm by the electrode induction melting gas atomization method. The bulk samples were then fabricated via laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) using a commercial DiMetal-100H system with 316 stainless steel serving as the substrate. The optimized processing parameters were set as follows: laser power p = 400 w, scanning speed v = 1100 mm/s, hatch spacing h = 70 μm, and layer thickness t = 30 μm. A standardized x/y alternating scanning strategy with a 67° interlayer rotation was employed during printing. The entire building process was conducted in a protective argon atmosphere with the oxygen concentration rigorously maintained below 1000 ppm. The casting ingot of this alloy (about 200 g) for comparison was prepared by the arc-melting technique using pure metals with a purity greater than 99.9 % under a highly pure argon atmosphere. Subsequently, both the additively manufactured precursor and the cast ingot were subjected to a series of post-treatments. First, the materials were cold-rolled into sheets with a total thickness reduction of approximately 80%. The cold-rolled plates were then subjected to high-temperature annealing at 950 °C for 5 min. Afterward, the recrystallized specimens were further annealed at 650 °C for 2 h. The details of post-treatment and the corresponding microstructural evolutions of samples were presented in Supplementary Note 1.

Microstructural characterization

EBSD experiments were carried out in a scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS Gemini 30) equipped with the Oxford Instruments-Aztec Crystal system. EBSD scans were operated at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV with a step size of 0.1-0.2 µm, and the hit rate for all unformed samples was at least 95%. EBSD and SEM specimens were first mechanically ground and polished to a mirror surface. Then, the polished samples were electrolytically etched in a solution of 5 vol% HNO3 + 95 vol% C2H5OH under 20 V at room temperature for ~30 s. The detailed microstructures in the multiscale before and after deformation were characterized by TEM (FEI Talos F200X and Spectra 300). For the TEM examination, bright-field, dark-field, selected-area electron diffraction, and high-resolution TEM techniques were employed on these thin foils. Atomic-scale TEM images and the (inverse) Fourier transformation were analyzed using the Digital Micrograph software. TEM discs for the deformed microstructure observation were taken from the uniform deformation area in tensile samples. All the TEM foils were initially mechanically ground to a thickness of 30 μm and subsequently subjected to ion beam milling using a Gatan precision ion polishing system (PIPS II). The ion milling process employed sequential beam voltages of 5 kV, 3 kV, and 2 kV, with corresponding milling angles of ±7°, ±5°, and ±3° for the rough milling, fine milling, and final cleaning, respectively. The features of nano-orderings were determined by the widely used image analysis methodology strictly according to the standard ASTM E1245. The statistical average ordering diameter (d) was determined based on several HAADF-STEM images, by employing ImageJ software53. The number density (Nv) was calculated according to Nv = NA/d, where the precipitate areal density NA was calculated by counting the number of particles per unit area from several given TEM images54. Then, the precipitate (L12 and σ) volume fraction was estimated by f = Nv(4πr3/3), where r = d/2 is the radius of precipitates54. Chemical composition was analyzed using a local electrode APT (CAMECA LEAP 5000 XR). Needle-shaped samples were annularly milled in a focused-ion-beam/SEM system (FEI Scios). The 3D-APT experiment was conducted in voltage mode at 70 K, at a pulse fraction of 20%, a pulse repetition rate of 200 kHz, and an evaporation detection rate of 0.2% atom per pulse. Corresponding data were analyzed using AP Suite 6.1 workstations for the 3D reconstructions.

Mechanical property tests

The flat dog-bone-shaped samples with a gauge size of 8 × 2.0 × 1.5 mm3 for the tensile tests were truncated using electrical discharge machining from sheets along the rolling direction. Uniaxial tension tests at both 298 and 77 K were performed on a Zwicks mechanical machine under a strain rate of 10-3 s-1. Before testing, the specimen surfaces were polished using 2000-grit SiC paper to eliminate scratches. The samples were always immersed in liquid nitrogen during cryogenic tension, and all tensile tests were repeated at least 3 times to ensure reproducibility.

Atomistic simulations

In the current work, classical MD and hybrid MD/Monte Carlo (MD/MC) simulations were employed. All simulations were performed using the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) package55. Atomic interactions among different species were described using the embedded atom method (EAM)56, with potential parameter files adopted from previous studies49,57. All MD processes were conducted within the framework of isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensembles, with system temperature adjusted by a Nose-Hover thermostat58,59.

First, to investigate dislocation nucleation behavior at the incoherent σ/matrix interface, a cubic atomic model with dimensions of ~20 × 20 × 20 nm3 was created. The σ-particle was approximated by an incoherent precipitate within the FCC NiCoCr matrix. After equilibration, a uniaxial tensile strain was applied at 77 K. Second, to investigate the effects of LCOs on dislocation motion, we performed simulations on a model NiCoCr alloy containing LCOs, with pure Cu as a reference. Especially, the initial atomic sample of NiCoCr was constructed as a random solid-solution cuboid with dimensions of ~40 × 80 × 10 nm3. The chemical potential differences between elements and the variance constraint parameter were adopted from the previously calibrated atomic systems49. The hybrid MD/MC simulations within a variance-constrained semi-grand-canonical ensemble (VC-SGC) were carried out at an annealing temperature of 1000 K, and subsequently, the equilibrated system was quenched to 0 K by conjugate-gradient energy minimization to obtain the relaxed LCO configuration49. Then, an extended edge dislocation was introduced on the [111] slip plane. Following the procedure adopted in the previous study49, the sample was dynamically equilibrated at 300 K for 100 ps. After equilibration, the system was cooled down to 77 K, and shear strain was further initiated. To track dislocation characteristics in the above simulations, local atomic structures were identified using the common neighbor analysis (CNA) algorithm60. The geometrical profiles of dislocation lines were further constructed using an algorithm for extracting dislocations from atomistic data61.
