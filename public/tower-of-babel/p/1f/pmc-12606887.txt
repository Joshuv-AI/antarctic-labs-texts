Association of substance use and vaginal infection with UTI in adolescent pregnant patients: a retrospective study
Authors: Orly Grobeisen-Duque, Carlos Daniel Mora-Vargas, Oscar Villavicencio-Carrisoza, Janet Flores-Villanueva, Maria Guadalupe Martinez-Salazar, Hector Flores-Herrera, Moises Leon-Juarez, Veronica Zaga-Clavellina, Jorge Francisco Cerna-Cortes, Sandra Rivera-Gutierrez, Alejandro Rosas-Balan, Ma. ABSTRACT
BackgroundUrinary tract infections in pregnant women, pose a global health concern, especially in adolescents due to their unique physiological changes. In Mexico, 18.5% of births occur in adolescent mothers, making it crucial to develop effective protocols for prevention, diagnosis, and treatment. Vaginal infections involving Gardnerella vaginalis and Candida albicans, create a pro-inflammatory environment conductive to the growth of Escherichia coli within the uroepithelium. Additionally, psychoactive substance use compromises the immune system, leading to infections acquisition.MethodsOne hundred forty-four adolescent patients were enrolled between 8 and 20 weeks of gestation at the Instituto Nacional de Perinatología in Mexico City. Urine cultures were performed to detect UTI caused by E. coli. Phylogroup classification, clinical evaluation, and statistical analysis were conducted to assess risk factors and maternal/neonatal complications.ResultsStatistical correlation was found between UTI and infections due to G. vaginalis andC. albicans. Additionally, psychoactive substance use incremented risk of maternal UTI.ConclusionThis study highlights the strong association between some vaginal infection and UTI caused by E. coli. Furthermore, psychoactive substance use promotes the acquisition of infections. These findings emphasize the need for comprehensive healthcare for adolescent pregnant patients.

Background

Urinary tract infections (UTI) are one of the most prevalent transmissible diseases in women worldwide. The most common clinical features are dysuria, pollakiuria, tenesmus, abdominal and pelvic pain, though many patients may be asymptomatic [1]. Women have a higher risk of developing these infections due to their anatomy and physiology. Hormonal changes during the different reproductive stages impact urinary pH, making it more alkaline, creating a rich environment for bacterial growth [2]. Among the anatomical changes that can be seen, we may find ureteral dilation due to higher levels of progesterone and relaxin, pelvic compression that may lead to developing hydronephrosis, urinary stasis, renoureteral reflux, and biochemical changes in the urine. The reported perinatal complications that can be seen in pregnant women with UTI are preterm labor, intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight, and neonatal sepsis, among others [2].

Vaginal infections can cause genitourinary inflammation and dysbiosis, which generate uroepithelial changes, increasing the risk of Escherichia coli colonization, the most common bacterial agent that causes UTI [3]. The most common bacterial agent causing vaginal infection is Gardnerella vaginalis, which causes a white-gray vaginal discharge. The most common risk factors for this infection are unprotected sexual contact and douching, which may produce vaginal dysbiosis due to microbiota sweep [4, 5]. One of the most common non-bacterial agents that may cause vaginal infections is Candida albicans [6]. This fungus is associated with poor vaginal hygiene and vaginal dysbiosis. Evidence has shown association of vaginal infections with an increased risk of UTI due to an increase in urinary pH levels and vaginal dysbiosis [7].

In addition, there is an association between substance abuse, such as alcohol, marijuana, and cocaine, among others, and the development of infections [8]. In recent years, research has been conducted to examine the effect of psychoactive substance use with the immune system, and its impact on the prevalence of local and systemic infections [9–11]. Marijuana is considered an immunotoxic substance, creating a deficiency in the cellular and humoral immune responses. Human and animal model studies have also shown an association between marijuana use and increased urinary retention, increasing the risk of developing UTI [10]. Furthermore, the use of cocaine is associated with immunosuppression targeting T cells, B cells and Natural Killers, creating a deficiency in immune response [11]. Moreover, its consumption is associated with renal ischemic events due to vasospasm, endothelial renal lesions, increased platelets aggregation, and thromboxane synthesis, thereby creating a tissue prone to infection [12]. Similarly, alcohol abuse, cocaine, methamphetamines, heroin, and morphine generate a dysbiotic state, decreasing the diversity of Lactobacillus spp., which is necessary for a healthy microbiome to defend against infectious pathogens [13].

One of the populations at higher risk of developing UTI, vaginal infections and psychoactive substance abuse are adolescents [14–16]. In Mexico, adolescents aged 15 to 19 account for 7.3% of all urinary tract infection (UTI) cases in women and 15.9% of adolescents at middle and high school have used psychoactive drugs at least once, with marijuana being the most commonly consumed substance [17, 18]). In Mexico, 18.5% of births are from adolescent mothers, creating a maternal and child health problem that few entities address [19]. There are many risk factors that impact the immune system, such as nutritional deficiencies, diabetes, hormonal changes, stress, age, among others, thus allowing the development of urinary tract infections. Therefore, in this research, we aim to analyze the association that exists between the presentation of vaginal infection, especially Candida albicans and Gardnerella vaginalis, and psychoactive substance abuse as a risk factor for UTI in adolescent pregnant patients that were attended at the “Instituto Nacional de Perinatología” in Mexico City, as well as its correlation with perinatal complications. Understanding the link between vaginal infections, psychoactive substance use, and urinary tract infections in pregnant adolescents may help reduce maternal and neonatal complications. Being a middle- income country with high incidence of psychoactive substance use and infection prevalence, especially among younger and immunologically vulnerable population, we hypothesize that E. coli urinary tract infection will be highly associated with vaginal infections and psychoactive substance use in adolescent pregnant patients.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of Instituto Nacional de Perinatología Isidro Espinosa de los Reyes (protocol code 2017-3−131 approved on February 7th, 2018). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved in the study. Written informed consent has been obtained from the patient(s) to publish this paper.

This is a retrospective, observational and descriptive study of adolescent pregnant women who attended the INPer, Mexico, during their first or early second pregnancy trimester (8–20 gestation weeks), between January and December 2018. The sampling method was non-probabilistic and based on convenience, using a one-year period as the sampling frame. Accordingly, all patients who met the inclusion criteria during the defined timeframe were included in the study. Inclusion criteria: adolescents without any infection at the time of recruitment, without antibiotic therapy for 3 weeks, aged 12 to 17 years old, and those who gave their consent to participate (with the parents or legal guardian acceptance). Adolescents included were followed up during their pregnancy until delivery, and their neonates were followed up until hospital discharge.

UTI identification and groups definition

Pregnant adolescent patients donated a urine sample during each visit to the hospital (one urine sample per month), and a urine culture was performed to search for UTI caused by E. coli. Bacterial identification and microbial susceptibility testing were performed by means of the Vitek 2, BioMerieux® (BioMerieux Inc, Durham, NC).

Patients were divided into two different groups: The control group (without UTI at any point during pregnancy), and the UTI group (patients with at least one episode of UTI caused by E. coli; since this species is the most common in our population, we chose it to avoid the potentially confusing effects caused by different organisms.).

Clinical data search and statistical analysis

We searched for risk factors described for UTI acquisition and collected data from maternal and neonatal outcomes. All the clinical data of mothers and neonates were obtained from electronic clinical files. All patient data were anonymized before analysis was performed. E. coli phylogroup was included in the UTI group database.

Risk factors associated with UTI and neonatal outcomes were determined using statistical chi-square tests and odds ratio (OR) or Relative Risk (RR) by 2 × 2 table analysis. The maternal variables analyzed included pre-gestational BMI, Z-score calculation [20, 21], age, gestational weight gain, and infections such as HIV, syphilis, cervicovaginitis, Ureaplasma and Mycoplasma vaginitis, candidiasis, bacterial vaginosis, and group B Streptococcus. We calculated GWG based on the guidelines of the Institute of Medicine and the recommendations of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (Table 1) [22]. Other maternal factors were psychoactive substance use (alcohol, cannabis, cocaine, inhalants), chorioamnionitis, preeclampsia, and sepsis. Neonatal variables included birth weight (categorized according to the World Health Organization ([23, 24]. as low weight < 2,499 g, adequate 2,500-3,999 g, macrosomic >4,000 g), and gestational age (late preterm 32–36.9.9 weeks of gestation, term 37–41.9.9 weeks of gestation, posterm >42 [24], sepsis, enterocolitis, meningitis, respiratory distress syndrome, tachypnea, premature rupture of membranes, and intrauterine growth restriction. Neonatal sex was only used for descriptive purposes. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 (SPSS Inc., IL, Chicago, USA). We obtained percentages and measures of central tendency, frequency calculation using One Way ANOVA for parametric variables; we also conducted com-parisons using the chi-square test, with a significance level of P < 0.05. We assessed risk using odds ratio (OR) analysis based on 2 × 2 contingency tables, along with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI). For data sets with fewer than five observations, unilateral Fisher’s exact tests were used. A one-sided analysis was chosen because we aimed to assess the effect of the variable specifically within the IVU group, focusing on significance in a single direction. Graphs were generated utilizing the ForestPloter 1.1.1 package on the R platform.

Uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC) phylogroup classification

E. coli genomic DNA was isolated using Quick-gDNA™ MiniPrep (Zymo Research, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and isolates were classified into four phylogenetic groups using 100 ng of DNA in the multiplex-PCR described by Clermont et al. [25]. Amplified products were analyzed in 3% agarose gels and visualized in the EpiChemi II Darkroom transilluminator (UVP, USA).

Results

During the study period, 144 pregnant adolescents fulfilled the inclusion criteria: 59 suffered from UTI at any time throughout the pregnancy and 85 corresponded to healthy controls. There was no statistical difference in terms of pre-gestational BMI (21.75 ± 3.19 vs. 22.51 ± 3.69, healthy and UTI patients respectively; p = 0.192) and maternal age (15.61 ± 0.92 vs. 15.41 ± 1.03, p = 0.216) indicating that both groups were homogeneous. The characteristics of the patients and the association with UTI are shown in Fig. 1. We did not find an association with weight gain during pregnancy and co-infections such as HIV, syphilis, mycoplasma vaginitis, GBS, and ureaplasma vaginitis. However, we observed a slight increase in patients with excessive weight gain in our pregnant adolescents who contracted UTI compared to the healthy control group. This finding should be deeply evaluated with a bigger population.

There was a statistically significant association between vaginal candidiasis with Candida albicans (p < 0.001), bacterial vaginosis with Gardnerella vaginalis (p = 0.010), and the abuse of psychoactive substances (p = 0.032) with the presence of UTI in our study population. It is important to note that both infections (candidiasis and bacterial vaginosis) increased the risk of suffering UTI by 2.27 and 1.79 times, respectively (based on RR estimation). Additionally, the abuse of psychoactive substances was observed more frequently in patients with UTI, increasing the risk by 2 times (Fig. 1).

We also analyzed maternal outcomes including chorioamnionitis, preeclampsia, and maternal sepsis, and their association with UTI during pregnancy. There was no association between the presence of UTI and the identification of chorioamnionitis, preeclampsia, or sepsis (Fig. 1). We’ve noted a potential correlation between UTI and maternal sepsis, syphilis, and HIV infection. However, it’s important to mention that none of these correlations reached statistical significance, likely due to the absence of cases within one of the adolescents’ groups.

Among the neonatal characteristics (Fig. 2), gestational age at delivery, birth weight, and sex were similar in both groups. We also explored the association between maternal UTI and neonatal outcomes. Maternal UTI did not increase the risk of preterm delivery, Low Birth Weight (LBW), neonatal sepsis, enterocolitis, meningitis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, tachypnea, premature rupture of membranes, or intrauterine growth restriction in our studied population. Discussion

The adolescent pregnant population is a low-studied group with high vulnerability. They are considered a high-risk group due to the prevalence of morbidities and mortality rates among them. Owing to their transitional and in-development endocrine, reproductive and immune systems, they face an elevated risk of acquiring perinatal infections and subsequent complications.

There are several risk factors for women of reproductive age to contract a urinary tract infection due to Escherichia coli. In the present study, we found a strong association between vaginal infection and UTI in pregnant adolescent patients, especially due to Candida albicans and Gardnerella vaginalis. A study conducted in Nepal regarding the association between vaginal infections in nonpregnant women with UTI with E. coli, showed a significant association with C. albicans [26]. The pathophysiology of this association is not fully understood; nevertheless, a study conducted in mice showed that the infection with E. coli also enhances the virulence of C. albicans by increasing the biofilm formation through upregulation of the ASL-3 gene, responsible for biofilm formation and the HWP-1 gene promoting initial uroepithelial attachment [27]. These studies show a possible vicious circle, with C. albicans acting as a risk factor for UTI caused by E. coli, and this bacterium promoting the persistence of C. albicans vaginitis. Regarding the relation with G. vaginalis, a study conducted in non-pregnant women showed an association between bacterial vaginosis and UTI by E. coli due to an increase in pH and a decrease in the density of Lactobacillus spp in the urogenital tract [26]. Another study by Das et al. demonstrated a significant relationship between the co-infection and the lack of menstrual hygiene practices and water access in women of reproductive age (from adolescent to pre-menopausal period) [28] (Fig. 3). To have a better comprehension of the interaction of these two bacteria, Gilbert et al. performed a clinical trial using an animal model with mice, showing that G. vaginalis induces uroepithelial damage, specifically causing exfoliation and apoptosis of urinary epithelium. G. vaginalis elicits an E. coli reservoir in the bladder epithelium, allowing for UTI recurrence (RUTI). This association is not only related to RUTI but also to increased severity, enabling E. coli proliferation to the kidney, leading to pyelonephritis, urosepsis, and death of the infected mice [29]. O´Brien et al. also demonstrated an association between RUTI and the pre-existence of G. vaginalis due to exfoliative and apoptotic processes in the uroepithelium. Further research using human models is needed to determine the pathophysiologic processes underlying this association [30].

Regarding the association of UTI with the E.coli phylogroups, we found a higher incidence of isolated E. coli in phylogroup A and D. Various studies have shown that phylogroups B2 and D are more strongly associated with extraintestinal E. coli involved in the development of UTI [31, 32]. This difference may be due to the variations in the pregnant population, including factors such as age, hormonal changes, and physiological changes during this period. During the analysis of phylogroups presence in the E. coli samples from the pregnant adolescent patient with UTI and coinfection with vaginosis due to C. albicans, we identified that 23.1% had a phylogroup D and 11.5% had the phylogroup B2, both of which are associated with the development of UTI and RUTI. This finding supports the hypothesis that C. albicans and E. coli create a vicious circle that allows their mutual proliferation. Furthermore, we found the presence of 26.7% of E. coli with phylogroup D and coinfection with G. vaginalis but 0% with phylogroup B2. This reinforces and confirms that the presence of RUTI is highly associated with this coinfection, enabling bacterial growth and a uroepithelial reservoir of E. coli. Further research with a larger population is needed to corroborate these results in pregnant adolescent patients.

Additionally, psychoactive substance use has been linked to many infections due to their impact on the immune system in non-pregnant women. In our study, we found a strong correlation between psychoactive substance use and the development of UTI by E. coli. To date, no published research has established a clear link or explanation for this connection. Nevertheless, various studies have shown the implications of using these substances in the immune system. Some of these known effects are mentioned in Table 3. Plein et al. observed a decrease in cellular and humoral response to infection, resulting in a deficit in the immunological response against bacteria and viruses [9]. Although many recent studies have focused on the anti-inflammatory effects of cannabis on the immune system for the treatment of different diseases, Maggirwar et al. showed that chronic cannabis use impairs cell-mediated and humoral immunity [10]. In another study, Marasco et al. observed that cocaine addiction is associated with immune suppression by decreasing the production of T cells, B cells, and Natural Killers and their response [11] (Fig. 3). Therefore, further research is necessary to fully comprehend their implication for the maternal- fetal health. All of the aforementioned studies do not specifically focus on the adolescent pregnant population or pregnant population. Focusing on the urological complication of psychoactive substance use, Skeldon et al. conducted a study on bladder tissue and showed that the use of cannabis decreases bladder contractility, leading to urinary retention. Furthermore, the use of cocaine increases resistance to blood flow in the right renal artery, causing renal vasospasm, renal endothelial damage, and increased platelet aggregation and thromboxane production [12].

Additionally, we considered an important aspect noted in previous research: the association between psychoactive substance abuse and increased sexual risk behaviors during adolescence. For example, in a study by Scivoletto et al. on Brazilian adolescents, they concluded that sexual risk behaviors were more likely when alcohol and marijuana were used concurrently [34]. In our study, we analyzed the number of sexual partners among patients who reported substance abuse, as this was the only risk behavior recorded as part of clinical care. Our analysis found no significant association between the number of sexual partners in the psychoactive substance abuse group and an increased risk of developing UTIs. This suggests that, within our population, the sexual behavior (specifically the number of sexual partners) associated with substance abuse does not contribute to an elevated UTI risk.

It is crucial to address the fact that all the above-mentioned risk factors for UTI carry the potential to develop perinatal complications. The presence of vaginosis due to G. vaginalis and C. albicans is associated with low birth weight, preterm birth, premature rupture of membranes, chorioamnionitis, intraamniotic infection, spontaneous abortion, neonatal sepsis, admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), and birth asphyxia [5, 35–40].

Furthermore, the use of psychoactive substances increases the risk of presenting structural abnormalities with dysmorphic features, intrauterine growth restriction, gastroschisis, congenital heart disease, spina bifida, neurodevelopmental abnormalities, low birth weight, neonatal irritability and seizures, microcephaly, admission to NICU, preterm birth, sudden infant death syndrome, preeclampsia, stillbirth, and further dependence on the elected substance [41–43]. All of this added to the vulnerability for complications due to UTI infection such as preterm birth, premature rupture of membranes, intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight, stillbirth, intraamniotic inflammation, and preeclampsia creates a hazardous environment if not prevented [2, 44, 45].

In our population, we didn’t find any maternal or neonatal complication associated with the presence of UTI, vaginal infection with G. vaginalis or C. albicans, or psychoactive substance use with statistically significant results (p < 0.05). At the “Instituto Nacional de Perinatología” we have a clinic that specializes in adolescent care due to their high-risk profile during pregnancy. In the healthcare protocols, these patients receive a comprehensive assessment from maternal-fetal medicine, nutrition, psychology, infectology, hematology, and cardiology, among others. This creates a highly regulated environment for prompt diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of infectious diseases, safeguarding the binomial from perinatal complications. Due to the lack of research in this specific population, there is no information regarding the relationship between age, adolescent hormonal and physiological changes, and healthcare protocols with the presence of perinatal complications. Further research is needed to address this correlation and provide the best healthcare to these patients.

In recent years, the relationship between the presence of malnourishment and perinatal infections has been explored [46, 47]. In our research, we did not find a correlation between the pre-gestational BMI and weight gain as a risk factor for infection. However, the observation of a moderate weight gain increment in pregnant adolescent patients with UTI highlights a crucial risk factor that needs to be further investigated. To date, no study is conclusive on the relationship between pre-gestational weight and weight gain during pregnancy, neither for adults nor for adolescents. A key strength lies in our meticulous tracking of each enrolled patient through delivery, exclusively selecting healthy adolescents without urinary tract infections (UTIs) during their initial or early second pregnancy trimester. We monitored UTI development, analyzing samples at every follow-up appointment and thoroughly examining complete clinical records to ensure a homogeneous study population. However, a limitation persists in the relatively small sample size. Despite this, we have identified significant risk factors for UTI development, underscoring the importance of expanding the population size for a more comprehensive investigation into the outcomes of UTIs in adolescent pregnancy.

Conclusions

Our study highlights the strong association between vaginal infection, particularly with C. albicans and G. vaginalis, and urinary tract infections caused by E. coli in adolescent pregnant patients. Additionally, we demonstrated a significant correlation between psychoactive substance use and the contraction of UTI by E. coli. Understanding this correlation is key to promoting better control and prevention of UTIs, especially in pregnant adolescent patients. By doing so, we can mitigate complications associated with UTIs in the binomial.

As specific suggestions to ensure a better approach and treatment for this population include routine screening for asymptomatic bacteriuria and vaginal infections during every trimester, personalized management based on E. coli phylogroup in recurrent or severe infections, multidisciplinary care involving gynecology, infectious disease specialist and mental health progression for targeted health education on substance- related risks and ensuring access to appropriate antimicrobial therapy, improving adherence through social support and establishing epidemiological surveillance system. Further research, particularly in human models and specifically in pregnant women, is needed to enrich our knowledge on this topic and facilitate an integrative approach in women’s healthcare.

Limitations

One limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size, which may affect the generalizability of our findings to the broader population of pregnant adolescents. Additionally, certain potential risk factors—such as anemia and vitamin D deficiency were not analyzed, though these are known to influence immune function and could play a role in UTI susceptibility. The limited number of cases, especially within specific subgroups, reduced the statistical power for some analyses and restricted our ability to assess additional variables like condom use and a comprehensive view of sexual behaviors. Addressing these limitations in future studies, with larger sample sizes and detailed data on nutritional status, smoking, and substance use, would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the risk factors associated with UTIs in this population.
