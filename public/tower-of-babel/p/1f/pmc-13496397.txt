Regulating human reproductive and developmental biotechnologies: a UK reference model
Authors: Emma Cave
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRapid scientific change is exposing the limits of the UK’s regulation of human reproductive and developmental biology. Embryo-like structures, in vitro gametogenesis, organoids and heritable genome editing advance understanding of human development and physiology and support new applications in assisted reproduction and clinical care, yet the current regulatory framework is ill-equipped to govern them coherently. A case is made for coordinated, technology-neutral regulatory change. The article proposes the principles of a model UK Human Reproductive and Developmental Biotechnology Act and regulator, the Human Reproductive and Developmental Biotechnology Authority—or HRBA, pronounced ‘harbor’—to provide a permissions route across research and translation. The model Act is a modular, risk-tiered scheme with consistent standards and tailored red lines. It is designed to accommodate boundary cases, to integrate and build upon the existing remit of the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority, and coordinate with other established regulators to retain institutional expertise and avoid unnecessary duplication. The proposal is ambitious and would help give effect to UK Government commitments to safely advance the life sciences in this area. But even if piecemeal amendments are ultimately favored, it clarifies the underlying choices and trade-offs and provides a benchmark against which changes can be tested.

I. INTRODUCTION

Advances in how human cells and related structures can be derived, cultured and modified are expanding what can be studied in the laboratory and, in some instances, what might be translated into clinical practice.1 The rapid scientific change is exposing global limitations of the regulation2 of human reproductive and developmental biology.3,4 The UK fertility regulator, the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority (HFEA), has called for reforms to modernize the law in relation to embryo-related research and treatment.5 Meanwhile the Nuffield Council on Bioethics (NCoB)—an independent think tank and the closest the UK has to a national bioethics committee,6 and the Regulatory Horizon’s Council (RHC)— an independent expert committee set up to guide the Government on regulatory reforms needed to support technological innovation,7 have emphasized the need for regulation to advance ethical research on some of the parallel biotechnologies.

Politically, we should be cautious about supposing that today’s emerging biotechnologies are categorically different from earlier innovations and therefore deserve a more radical regulatory solution. That said, there are features of these new biotechnologies that are different in character and form, suggesting that the status quo is poorly equipped to harness their potential and respond appropriately to their inherent risks. The Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1990 (the 1990 Act) responded to a profound shift that enabled key aspects of human reproduction to occur outside the body. Since that time, as I discuss below, biotechnological advances have largely been accommodated through targeted amendment within the current law. By contrast, some stem cell-based technologies more directly strain the legal structure, destabilizing definitional boundaries, producing hybrid applications cutting across existing regulatory domains and with potential for diffuse translational pathways that can require the input of multiple regulators and are sometimes not captured at all. Overall, the UK system is currently workable, but it leaves gaps and friction at transition points, creates uneven oversight that depends on provenance or setting and can make it difficult to identify a single point of responsibility for boundary cases and converging technologies. Moreover, those difficulties are accentuated as the scientific applications advance and coalesce.

There is wide acceptance that regulatory change is needed, and the more relevant questions concern the legislative route by which it should be achieved and the applications that should be included. Focusing on the UK, I will suggest answers to both questions, proposing a reference model for reform.

II. MECHANISMS OF REFORM

Regarding the first of those questions, an important objective is for governance to balance legal certainty with sufficient flexibility to adapt as the science develops.8 The UK Government has several options from which to choose. Reactive approaches may serve as temporary stopgaps. One example is what Roger Brownsword calls ‘re-connecting’ in which judges apply existing regulatory schemes to new technological contexts.9 Brownsword identifies risks of substantive misfit and heightened vulnerability to legal challenge, as statutory language and legislative purpose are stretched to cover developments they were not designed to address. When necessary, Parliament can move swiftly to close loopholes, as it did in the Human Reproductive Cloning Act 2001 which made reproductive cloning a criminal offense, but there is wide agreement that anticipatory governance is preferable to reactive regulation because rules made only after controversy or harm can be disproportionate and undermine public trust.10

Three anticipatory regulatory mechanisms dominate the landscape. Initially, advances are often best dealt with through soft law, usually taking the form of voluntary oversight and researcher commitment to principles while the science is developing and regulators gather the necessary expertise and legal authority.11 For example, when developments in embryology led to law reform proposals from the Warnock Committee in 1984,12 a Voluntary Licensing Authority (later called the Interim Human Fertilisation Licensing Authority) initially issued guidance ahead of a White Paper in 198713 and subsequently the 1990 Act. A current example is the international guidance from the International Society for Stem Cell Research (ISSCR), which sets out best practices for the stem cell research field14 and with which research-intensive countries are taking steps to align their governance frameworks.15 Soft law is initially optimal for reasons of flexibility but rarely provides a suitable long-term basis for enabling biotechnologies while maintaining public trust, especially as here, where it would lead to inconsistent regulatory treatment of entities that raise some common ethical and societal concerns.16 Also, while soft law can deal with certain gaps in the law, it cannot address areas where current legislation prevents progress, even where that is the result of an accident of framing at a time when a technology or application was not even envisaged.

A second mechanism is targeted amendment of the 1990 Act, as occurred in 2008.17 For example, amendments could usefully impose red lines on novel stem cell-based research applications and facilitate their oversight,18 and might in future introduce new assisted reproductive technologies (ARTs) for clinical use, if they are shown to be safe and viable. To achieve the latter, section 3ZA, which sets out the types of gametes and embryos that are ‘permitted’ to be placed in a woman under section 3(2), might be extended to include certain novel forms of embryo and gametes created through stem cell-based applications. New powers to make Regulations (secondary legislation) could facilitate future commencement, as was used to introduce mitochondrial donation in the UK.19 These reforms would maintain continuity and strong parliamentary control, but piecemeal reform proceeding one technology at a time risks slow, costly and uneven application.20 Additionally, the HFEA focuses on embryo and gamete research and on fertility treatment but has limited oversight of translational activities, which makes it difficult to produce the evidence of safety and efficacy needed to permit new ARTs. Another amendment could address this, such as an exception to section 3(2) to allow non-permitted embryos and gametes to be transferred to a woman in a new quasi trial process, but this could generate legitimacy and accountability concerns, or technology specific powers which would need to be revisited when new technologies mature.

While not discounting the potential relevance of the first two mechanisms, my focus here is a third, more ambitious proposal to future proof regulation in this area: comprehensive statutory reform that provides for an expanded and adaptive research scope and incorporates a translational mechanism from research to clinical application. I set out the principles and key design features of a model statute and regulatory body. The Human Reproductive and Developmental Biotechnology Act (the model Act) would create a specialist regulator, the Human Reproductive and Developmental Biotechnology Authority (HRBA, pronounced ‘harbor’). For ease of exposition, and to avoid repeated conditionals, I describe the model Act in the present tense. The model Act repeals and replaces the 1990 Act and amends the Human Tissue Act 2004, aligning functions through a harmonized tiered oversight system. Significant parts of the 1990 Act are re-enacted, particularly in relation to the licensing of clinics, and in practice, the HFEA transitions into the HRBA with an expanded remit. The intention is not to reopen the proposal to merge the HFEA and Human Tissue Authority (HTA), which regulates the removal, storage and use of human tissue. This proposal was rejected in 2007 on the grounds of reputational damage and increased costs and bureaucracy.21 Instead the model Act aligns areas in which the regulatory division of applications involving human tissue has become increasingly difficult to justify. The model Act avoids the over-concentration of power sometimes associated with super regulators, causing loss of specialist expertise and transition failure. HRBA’s function is not to absorb all oversight but to coordinate regulatory oversight via a single regulatory door and through aligned research-application schemes, while filling gaps where meaningful regulation is absent and the matter cannot coherently be appended to the remit of an existing regulator.

The model Act is organized around a series of modules contained in Schedules (essentially appendices providing detail on the main clauses) (Fig. 1). Module A regulates gametes (eggs and sperm), embryos and embryo-related biotechnologies that sit close to reproduction in both research and clinical settings. These technologies can raise contested questions about moral status and developmental potential,22 implicating the biological and regulatory transitions that bear directly on early human development. Module A encompasses the HFEA’s focus on treatments, such as in vitro fertilization (IVF), in vitro maturation, intrauterine insemination,23 pre-implantation genetic testing (PGT) and mitochondrial donation,24 and embryos created and cultured for research in the laboratory, currently constrained by the statutory 14-day or primitive streak limit.25 It also makes provision for new research applications including stem cell-based models of gametes and embryos, some of which may have future clinical and possibly reproductive use. This is the most urgent area for reform, but the model Act will also make provision for additional modules and specifically for a parallel Module B which will govern advanced organoids and stem cell-based systems. Some organoids involve novel interfaces with computers or robotic bodies, animal research and welfare or consciousness-related issues raising moral issues.26 The NCoB has argued that regulation of neural organoids may be needed as the science advances.27 In time, there is potential for clinical uses such as personalized medicine, targeted drug screening and regenerative medicine, and it is the combination of moral issues, translational potential and overlap with module A that elevates the need for enhanced regulatory coordination and coherent oversight.

There are limitations to this project. Experience from the period between the Warnock report and the 1990 Act shows that the hardest questions often arise in implementation.28 Nevertheless, I focus on principal changes rather than the details of the scheme and suggest it would be imprudent to do otherwise ahead of public debate and further scientific development. The timing of different aspects of the proposed model will also depend on cost, scientific promise and the pace of development and I will set out how that might work in practice. My focus on key changes also neglects what I propose would stay the same, resulting in more detail on research than treatment. Additionally, my focus is on cells and structures of human origin and for reasons of space, I largely put aside important matters relating to interspecies models and applications.

III. THE POLITICS OF COMPREHENSIVE RESTRUCTURING

The next section frames the problem, and the subsequent sections describe the proposed model and its intended application, but first it is important to acknowledge that comprehensive restructuring is politically difficult. Not only are there considerable pressures on parliamentary time for health reform but this area invokes diversity of opinion and ethical controversy. Indeed, for a considerable time, the Warnock proposals were, as the late Margaret Brazier candidly observed, ‘a hot potato [the Government] would rather not pick up’.29

One reason the UK Government might consider the benefits of such reform today is its ambition to harness life sciences for health and economic benefit.30 International alignment is also a goal,31 and that commitment could temper some of the boundary changes suggested below, but it might equally present an opportunity for the UK to shape emerging international approaches, as it has done in the past. Intuitively, comprehensive reform seems to sit uneasily with the Government’s commitment to deregulation,32 but by consolidating and rationalizing the existing framework, it could increase coherence, proportionality and flexibility across the regulatory landscape in ways that targeted amendment cannot.33 I propose measures to contain transition costs and preserve regulatory expertise by building on the HFEA’s existing remit and coordinating with other regulators, while showing throughout that these proposals are consistent with recognized modern regulatory principles.

Acknowledging that the political choice between reform options is finely drawn, even if my preferred option of comprehensive reform is not immediately pursued, and soft law governance is relied upon in the short term, and targeted piecemeal reform in the medium term, a consolidating statute along the lines of the model Act could conceivably become a longer-term objective. And even if not, the proposals I set out are offered as a benchmark to clarify the direction of travel by showing how an overarching framework might accommodate emerging biotechnologies if or as they develop and regulation becomes expedient.

IV. THE CASE FOR REFORM

The statute currently governing much of the proposed Module A is the 1990 Act (as amended), which established the HFEA as the specialist regulator.34 The 1990 Act responded to breakthroughs in reproductive technology that enabled embryos to be created and cultured outside the body. As Brazier recognized, ‘[t]he trouble with the embryo is that … we cannot resolve [its] disputed nature’.35 Cognizant of this fact, the Warnock Report recommended a compromise grounded in social consensus that was simultaneously permissive and tightly regulated,36 an approach subsequently adopted by the 1990 Act. Fertility treatment was confined to licensed clinics, and embryo research required an HFEA license, granted only where ‘necessary or desirable’ for specified purposes37 and subject to a strict prohibition on developing embryos beyond 14 days.38

Today, the HFEA licenses and inspects fertility treatment and embryo research undertaken in licensed centers, regulates the creation, storage and use of embryos and gametes in those settings, and issues a Code of Practice that provides operational guidance.39 The 1990 Act was amended in 2008,40 removing barriers for female same-sex couples, expanding legal parenthood and enabling secondary legislation permitting mitochondrial donation to avoid serious inherited disorders. Further adjustments have been made mainly through secondary legislation, including to facilitate controlled data access for research and to revise statutory storage periods for gametes and embryos,41 and through interpretation in the courts, as when the House of Lords concluded that embryos created by cell nuclear replacement were embryos for the purposes of the 1990 Act.42

The 1990 Act prohibits embryo testing except for one or more of the purposes expressly permitted in paragraph 1ZA(1) of Schedule 2 of the Act. PGT is sanctioned only where there is a ‘particular risk’ that a child would be born with a ‘serious illness, disability or other condition’.43 As testing becomes more sophisticated, calls to expand the law to allow embryo selection using polygenic scoring (PGT-P) are currently countered by insufficient evidence of accuracy or utility.44 A distinct mechanism applies to mitochondrial donation. This is a form of assisted reproduction that takes mitochondria from a donor to avoid transmission of serious mitochondrial disease. Because the intervention results in heritable changes to mitochondrial DNA, it is subject to enhanced scrutiny and is authorized by the HFEA on a case-by-case basis in clinics assessed as suitable, where there is a ‘particular risk that the eggs or embryos of the woman seeking treatment may have mitochondrial abnormalities’ and a ‘significant risk’ that the future child will develop serious mitochondrial disease.45 The UK was the first jurisdiction to legislate specifically to make mitochondrial donation lawful, and the regulatory framework is deliberately narrow in light of the technique’s novelty. However, by specifying only two permitted mitochondrial donation techniques (maternal spindle transfer and pronuclear transfer), the Regulations also preclude other viable methods, even if they prove safer or more effective.46 Moreover, it is already used elsewhere and could feasibly be used in the UK in future, as an infertility treatment.47

The 1990 Act primarily addresses embryos formed via the fusion of gametes in the process of fertilization. Consequently, human embryo-like structures exist in a ‘legal grey area’.48 These structures, sometimes called stem cell-based embryo models,49 are three-dimensional self-organizing structures of varying form and complexity, derived from human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) or induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs), that model aspects of early development. In the research context, they are already enhancing knowledge and understanding of human development and could potentially reduce reliance on embryo and animal research. Potential clinical uses include (inter alia) toxicity testing of drugs used in pregnancy and improvements in IVF, but they are not intended for reproductive use.50 Currently they lack the capacity to form a viable fetus51 and most are intrinsically constrained by the absence of components essential to further development.52 However, in future, those structures that model embryogenesis more comprehensively might conceivably develop fetal features,53 which can raise ethical concerns, including those relating to sentience or other morally salient characteristics, as I discuss below.

The HFEA considers that embryo-like structures are not embryos in current UK law and cannot be used for reproductive purposes.54 Concurring, the NCoB has recommended filling a gap in the legal framework governing research on embryo-like structures by modest reform of the 1990 Act55 and, separately, a UK non-statutory Code of Practice has set out research oversight expectations as a first stage of governance.56 Currently, a threat of default regulation persists.57 If politicians, regulators or a court consider that an embryo-like structure achieves a level of sophistication that passes a hypothetical ‘Turing test’ of equivalence to a natural embryo,58 it risks being subsumed under the rigid HFEA licensing regime and the 14-day limit for embryo culture, which the NCoB report considered to be difficult to time and potentially disproportionate.59 The 14-day limit is poorly suited for models that do not develop in a linear fashion and lack a clear day zero, and there is growing national and international agreement that embryo-like structures and embryos should be regulated as distinct legal entities.60

IVG aims to derive gametes from stem cell lines or somatic cell nuclear transfer61 and is relevant both as a research tool and potentially as a future clinical pathway for individuals who cannot produce eggs or sperm, or who require PGT for a serious monogenic disorder (PGT-M), in which case laboratory-fertilized IVG could lead to a better selection of embryos free of the condition for IVF purposes.62 Current UK law prohibits clinical use of IVG for reproductive purposes63 but the HFEA has recommended that some IVG applications should in time be subject to statutory regulation,64 while the NCoB has emphasized the need for extensive safety evidence and public engagement ahead of any clinical translation,65 and the RHC has used IVG as a case study to demonstrate the need for technology-neutral reform.66

Genome editing in human gametes or embryos is licensable for research under HFEA oversight,67 but transfer for treatment is currently prohibited.68 There is a potential route to legalization in the UK based on Rosamund Scott’s argument that tightly restricted human heritable genome editing to correct a serious genetic condition in a future child could be framed as ethically permissible and compliant with international Conventions, in circumstances where prospective parents who lack reasonable alternatives seek to have an unaffected and genetically related child.69 A 2020 international report identified potential initial applications of heritable human genome editing aimed at preventing transmission of serious inherited disease that could be ethically defensible if safety were demonstrated.70

IVG can generate gametes for creating embryos and raises an unresolved question as to whether deliberately non-viable entities would be classified as embryos or embryo-like structures. Conversely, embryo-like structures could, in principle, provide developmental cues to support IVG derivation in future. Embryo-like structures and extended culture can target later developmental stages, and genome editing may be applied across embryos, gametes and embryo-like structures. This creates a connected set of governance questions rather than discrete, technology-specific issues such that regulating them piecemeal risks both temporal and substantive incoherence.

Module B is more nascent from a position of regulatory need. It concerns a spectrum of stem cell-based models including three-dimensional organoids,71 human-animal chimeric models72 and transplantation-related research. It includes studies in which human organoids are introduced into animal hosts to drive maturation and encompasses complex organoids and assembloids,73 where the ethical focus may evolve as systems mature or integrate. It also includes multi-organ microphysiological systems,74 where engineered tissues or organoids are linked within organ-on-chip devices to model interactions. Organoids are physiologically relevant models of human development and disease which support drug discovery and therapy evaluation in ways that can reduce reliance on animal models.75 They can be generated at scale and could be commercialized, raising questions about the scope and durability of donor consent, including for profit-making.76 They also raise ethical and governance issues arising from their complexity, their integration with other tissues and model systems and, in some cases, their intended clinical translation. Neural organoids (stem cell-based three-dimensional models of brain tissue) raise more obvious ethical issues.77 In 2022, a paper reported that human and mouse neurons learned to play an embedded rudimentary computer game, indicating the potential for sentience at some future point of organoid modeling.78 There is not a dedicated statutory pathway equivalent to embryo research licensing, and Emily Jackson has warned: ‘we should not wait until a brain organoid exhibits signs of sentience before we start to consider whether the law that applies to stored blood samples is fit for purpose’.79

Of these models, neural organoids constitute the more pressing case for regulation. The 2026 NCoB report has aptly observed that ‘use of neural organoids and similar models are not subject to legislative control, and are not squarely within the remit of any existing regulatory authority’.80 While the necessity for Module B is some way off, there are still good reasons to incorporate it at the design stage. One is practical and facilitative: it would resolve current issues around distributed and convoluted regulation, reducing the regulatory burden on researchers. The HTA, created by the Human Tissue Act 2004,81 regulates the removal, storage and use of human ‘relevant material’ for defined purposes in licensed settings. It issues a series of statutory Codes of Practice,82 thereby governing many forms of tissue-based research and biobanking, including the collection of somatic tissues that may later be reprogrammed into iPSC lines. Safety and quality of tissues and cells intended for human application are governed by the Human Tissue (Quality and Safety for Human Application) Regulations 2007,83 and research ethics review is coordinated through the Health Research Authority (HRA) and the Research Ethics Service,84 which provides ethical opinions for studies involving participants, their data and, where applicable, their tissue. Where animals are involved, the principal statutory control is the Home Office licensing regime under the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986, administered through the Animals in Science Regulation Unit.85 Where organoid-derived products or other stem cell applications move toward clinical translation, the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) becomes central through medicines and advanced therapy regulation and clinical trial authorization, and it is also relevant where technologies are regulated as medical devices or in vitro diagnostics, including software-based tools. Other regulators might also be involved. For example, the UK Space Agency approved UK research in an organ-on-chips project as part of the Microgravity Science Programme,86 where the environment on the International Space Station facilitates simulation of disease processes on accelerated timescales compared with earth-based conditions. The net effect is a patchwork in which governance can change as a project moves from tissue procurement to in vitro research, to animal studies and then to clinical development, with no single capability-focused framework spanning the life cycle of the work. Stem cell governance illustrates how oversight currently varies by provenance. hESC derivation depends on embryo use under the HFEA licensing environment, and derived hESC lines are subject to governance through the UK Stem Cell Bank and its Steering Committee, which oversees deposit and access arrangements.87 By contrast, hiPSC lines typically begin with HTA- and HRA-governed tissue collection and ethics review, but once established as cell lines and distributed for research applications, oversight is less consistently anchored to a dedicated regulatory mechanism, and tends to rely more heavily on institutional governance and consent documentation, bolstered by funder requirements and general research standards.

The dangers of regulatory incoherence and inconsistency across reproduction-adjacent and organoid research should not be over-stated. To begin with, they are mitigated by the foresight and adaptive practice of the regulators. The very creation of regulators has built-in flexibility that has enabled the legislative schemes to remain relevant for so long.88 The HFEA, for example, actively scans the horizon, adapts its advice, Code of Practice and processes, collaborates with other regulators and advises Government where practical and legislative changes are needed.89 The latter function is strengthened by independent sources of analysis that inform and scrutinize regulatory development. As mentioned, the NCoB has run and is running interconnected reviews in this area. Additionally, the Regulatory Horizons Council,90 which sits within the Government’s Regulatory Innovation Unit, identifies the implications of technological advances and provides expert advice to the Government on regulatory reform to facilitate its safe introduction.

Nonetheless, a compelling advantage of Module B’s inclusion is to aid coherent regulation of applications sitting at the interface between the proposed Modules A and B, which are also among the most scientifically and translationally promising. For example, they may integrate embryo or embryo-like structure research with advanced tissue systems that model the maternal environment, enabling the study of the embryonic implantation and embryo-endometrium interface.91 Or they might combine IVG with gonadal organoids or assembloids to support maturation.92 A single regulatory door approach for researchers would facilitate collaboration and coherence between regulators, streamline oversight mechanisms and facilitate future integration of these biotechnologies. Clearly, this must be achieved proportionately, and I will set out mechanisms to achieve that. For now, suffice to say that commencement of Module B could be introduced through a staged transition, with an initial focus on learning and memoranda of understanding between the HRBA and other regulators. The flexibility of the tiered risk-based scheme I describe below would allow for oversight to increase over time if there is evidence of heightened risk.

V. THE MODEL ACT

Having argued that the current regulatory scheme is flawed and unprepared, and that a more comprehensive and aligned regulatory scheme is the optimal landing point, I now turn to the key features of the model Act.

The 2024 NCoB report analyzed governance goals for emerging biotechnologies, and I build on this foundation rather than repeating it in detail here.93 The features of the model Act seek to align with well-established accounts of effective regulation, including the National Audit Office’s principles of clarity of purpose, transparency, accountability, coordination across the regulatory landscape and the capacity to evaluate impact and improve over time,94 and the World Health Organization values of inclusiveness, caution, fairness, social justice, non-discrimination, equal moral worth, respect for persons, solidarity and global health justice.95 In the model Act, that translates to oversight that escalates with risk, rules that can be applied consistently across modules, decisions that are open to scrutiny and requirements that can be monitored and enforced through record-keeping, inspection, incident reporting and graduated sanctions. A register is central to transparency, horizon scanning and research. Registration requirements, which are not set out here in detail, would need to follow both purpose and intended beneficiaries. Hence, the UK Code of Practice on SCBEMs proposes a register of applications and outcomes,96 whereas the 1990 Act contains far more extensive registration requirements covering information, licenses, serious adverse events and adverse reactions and further empowers the HFEA to keep a voluntary contact register.97 The model Act therefore requires separate registers for treatment and research purposes.

Nor do I deal with issues of consent in any detail,98 as this is a matter that merits an article of its own. Mechanisms developed for human tissue and embryo research require adaptation to reflect stem cell research applications, particularly uncertainties around donor identity and future uses. The model Act would implement the HFEA’s recommendations for embryo banking, enabling allocation of stored embryos to approved projects within the scope of the donor’s broad consent.99 Further debate is needed on how far analogous arrangements should extend to stem cell research. A donor of tissue may not foresee the creation of an iPSC line subsequently used to develop embryo-like structures, IVG fertilization, neural organoids, genome editing, implantation-interface research or for commercial collaborations, which may require more specific consent in at least some of the research tiers I set out below. The model Act will address this through licensing conditions and statutory Codes of Practice, identifying sensitive uses requiring explicit consent, setting workable points for withdrawal from research and requiring traceability to confirm ethical sourcing within the agreed scope.

V.A. Regulation Is Proportionate to Risk

Proportionality is key because the scheme must protect boundaries and maintain public trust without imposing unnecessary burden on low-risk research or routine clinical practice. Risk in this domain is not only biological but also ethical and social, and capability thresholds can be difficult to specify and audit as science evolves, creating pressures toward over-classification or inconsistent decisions. In relation to treatment, inspection and compliance monitoring will, as currently, be calibrated to the nature of the service and the history of compliance. For research projects, oversight is, as I describe below, linked to activity and capability. In accordance with risk-based regulation,100 it escalates across defined tiers, while remaining sufficiently adaptable to accommodate scientific development. This means that registration and reporting can be sufficient for research that does not trigger defined capability triggers, while licensing and, where necessary, enhanced licensing conditions are reserved for activities that approach reproductive transition points, ethically significant developmental features or credible translational pathways. I set out how this could be operationalized in a tiered system in the next section.

The ISSCR has set out a list of red lines for any development that ‘lacks compelling scientific rationale or is ethically concerning’, and these are incorporated in the model Act which means they could only be changed through primary legislation.101 They include transfer of an embryo-like structure to a living host or artificial in vitro system to viability, human reproductive cloning, breeding human-animal chimeras where there may be human germ cells, transferring human-animal chimeric embryos to a human or ape reproductive tract and transferring any human embryo to an animal’s reproductive tract.

V.B. Focus on Activity and Capability

To future-proof the regulation in a technology-neutral manner, the model Act focuses on the effects and functions of the biotechnology rather than purely on its classificatory boundaries.102 The danger of setting out detailed definitions of biotechnologies that are still emerging is illustrated by the ISSCR’s shift away from a binary distinction between ‘integrated’ and ‘non-integrated’ embryo-like structures in August 2025 when that distinction became unstable.103 The ISSCR replaced it with the inclusive term ‘SCBEMs’ and introduced associated shifts in regulatory emphasis to base oversight on research activity and risk. Even where regulatory categories appear settled, overly specific definitions may become fragile as technological developments produce novel entities at the margins. In Australia, definitional specificity that enhanced clarity as to the meaning of ‘embryo’ also led to the unanticipated inclusion of certain embryo-like structures, which are now regulated as human embryos for the purpose of licensing and the 14-day limit on research culture.104

Accordingly, there is broad support for regulation that focuses less on definitions and more on activity and capability. The ISSCR sets out review categories based on the nature of the system and the oversight intensity required rather than formal definitions.105 The World Health Organization framework for governance of human genome editing focuses on the type of intervention and intended use rather than considering genome editing a single object category.106 Nationally, the NCoB in its report on embryo-like structures focused its governance recommendations on the features, uses and ethical salience of research rather than on single legal classifications,107 and in the related context of engineering biology, the Regulatory Horizons Council recently recommended to the UK Government that the products are governed based on their properties not the ‘platform technology from which they originate’.108

The model Act takes a similar approach. The capabilities approach accommodates both the increasing convergence among biological systems and their integration with other technologies. Consider, for example, in silico computational models.109 IVG may be combined with computer modeling to optimize differentiation pathways or predict gamete quality, while in silico integration with embryo-like structures may assist in predicting trajectories or even assessing developmental potential. ‘Digital twins’ are representatives of physical systems designed to simulate and forecast the behavior of their real-world counterparts.110 In research settings, they enable investigation where practical limitations or ethical constraints limit experimentation on biological systems. In clinical contexts, they offer the prospect of personalized medicine by allowing potential interventions to be modeled and evaluated in silico before implementation. Although the incorporation of computational components into biological research does not inherently generate regulatory concerns, certain applications may give rise to issues of data governance, data security and privacy that are not entailed by biological models alone. Under the model Act, HRBA will assess the functional capabilities of the composite systems, rather than relying on rigid category boundaries that may fail to capture such hybrid forms of research.

All that said, the focus on capabilities does not deny that definitions are also needed. The more open-textured the definitions, the greater the potential for uncertainty, which in turn could lead to ethical breaches. My proposed compromise is to set out high level statutory definitions to give researchers and HRBA clarity as to which modules a project falls into, while the regulatory tiers focus on capability and activity, the details of which will be set out in secondary legislation and Codes of Practice which can be more swiftly updated as dictated by scientific advances.111 Accordingly, the model Act intends to minimize the recurring misfit between fixed statutory categories and rapidly evolving science by anchoring legal obligations to function, capability and risk while retaining a clear definitional perimeter and issuing adaptable operational guidance.

V.C. Managing Interface Between Categories and Modules

The capabilities approach, while appealing in principle, presents difficulties when applied in context. Viewed from a functional or platform-based definition, modules A and B are closely connected by their model type—both include self-organizing three-dimensional tissues (such as embryo-like structures in Module A and organoids in Module B) mimicking an aspect of human development. Even when distinguished according to capability, as I have suggested, the two are often combined scientifically. Some projects begin at the interface by design, for example, an implantation study that combines embryos or embryo-like structures with endometrial assembloids, or an IVG project that is coupled to gonadal organoids to support maturation. These projects would be assigned to the Module most pertinent to their combined aim. Other projects may develop unpredictably as methods evolve or results prompt new questions, for example a neural organoid program might focus initially on in vitro disease modeling but later propose transplantation into an animal host to drive maturation. A workable system must therefore be able to identify when different module rules are engaged. Moreover, there needs to be close correlation between the modules so that restrictions apply logically and predictably. Operationally, this requires formal classification and reclassification based on triggers, a streamlined license-variation process so that projects can be brought under revised conditions without creating incentives for strategic under-description and recognizing the scope for genuine uncertainty, and default to HRBA review where reasonable uncertainty arises. The aim is that oversight follows ethically and translationally relevant features as they emerge, even if they cannot reasonably be predicted.

V.D. A Single-Door Approach

A related commitment in a landscape where biotechnologies converge and are increasingly combined within single projects is to facilitate movement between categories and modules. In practice, this will also involve integrated responses from regulators including the HTA, MHRA and HRA to ensure the application of appropriate expertise and institutional learning. However, a project that combines different technologies should not require researchers to navigate multiple uncoordinated pathways and then reconcile separate conditions. The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development Regulatory Policy Committee, which seeks to build international consensus on matters of regulatory policy recommends ‘one-stop shops’ as a mechanism to reduce bureaucracy and enhance efficiency.112 This has been effectively utilized in many areas in the UK, including the once fragmented review and permissions for clinical research projects.113 There have, historically, been calls for a single submission point for researchers seeking approvals and licenses from the HFEA,114 and more recent claims of unwarranted duplication.115

The model Act therefore aims to move toward a single point of entry for permissions, license conditions, monitoring and accountability, with interfaces between categories within modules, between modules and with other regulators. I do not deny that, while conceptually attractive, this will be challenging to implement and require significant political investment.116 From HRBA’s point of view, the single portal requires the development of experience within and across the modules and across research and clinical applications,117 and effective collaboration with other regulators.118 From a researcher perspective, clarity is needed as to the precise route to be taken, conditions that apply and pause or stop points, to limit the regulatory burden. This necessitates a high degree of predictability, which might be achieved through decision matrixes in the statutory Codes of Practice and clear processes to govern borderline and hybrid cases.119

A challenging aspect of the single-door approach is that it should address both research and clinical application, while preserving a clear legal separation between them. Research authorization should be limited to defined purposes and endpoints and subject to strict non-transfer requirements. Clinical use should require separate authorization, grounded in evidence of safety and benefit, appropriate quality and governance standards and monitoring and follow-up, and following public deliberation on ethical and social implications. The horizon scanning functions of the HFEA would be retained in HRBA to enhance regulatory readiness for new applications and developments. In addition, I propose three alternative and integrated mechanisms to manage translation from basic research to applied research or to clinic. First, Parliament might (depending on the evidence available at the relevant point) specify the transition pathway from research to clinic in the model Act itself. Additionally, the model Act will, as discussed next, establish a Clinical Gateway, supported by secondary regulation-making powers, to permit time-limited and closely supervised clinical introduction under defined conditions. Finally, some potential routes to clinical use should be expressly barred unless or until further primary legislation permits them. On this approach, some tightly defined uses of IVG and heritable (germline) genome editing might, in time, be capable of authorization through the first or second route, whereas reproductive use of embryo-like structures and reproductive cloning, for example, would remain confined to the third.

V.E. Flexible Regulation

The HFEA has called for legislative change to give it greater discretion to support innovation.120 The Authority seeks powers to approve treatments and research in licensed centers where there are no safety concerns through a sandbox: essentially a testbed where a regulator provides targeted oversight of new approaches. Meanwhile, the NCoB has proposed a related sandbox scheme to help establish suitable levels of regulation for embryo-like structures.121 The UK is one of the foremost countries to adopt sandboxes,122 and the model Act will lever this experience to enhance flexibility in two regards.

First, within clearly defined limits, HRBA will be granted the flexibility to define and refine the tier allocation of relevant activities, including new technological developments and combinations insofar as they are caught by the model Act. Second, a Clinical Gateway will facilitate transition from research to treatment, functioning to enable staged clinical introduction without treating legally permitted early use as routine care.123 Some applications under the Clinical Gateway, such as certain IVF treatment add-ons, will be relatively uncontroversial and can be assessed and sanctioned by HRBA on the terms of the model Act. Other more controversial first-in-human uses, such as heritable genome editing or reproductive use of IVG will, as I discuss further below, require legislation. Where it applies, the Clinical Gateway will allow HRBA to grant conditional permissions supported by evidence, caps on patient numbers, predefined endpoints and pause or stop rules, mandatory adverse-event reporting and transparent registration of both negative and positive outcomes. Where no other regulator has a clear remit, HRBA can then impose additional controls, such as validation standards or consent requirements, or restrictions on promotion and advertising, while coordinating with other regulators when their statutory responsibilities are engaged.

V.F. Route to Introducing New Modules

The model Act contains mechanisms for adaption as technologies develop and new ones emerge. This is increasingly recognized as an essential feature of biotechnological regulation. The European Union’s recent Regulation on Substances of Human Origin,124 for example, adopts a broad framework capable of accommodating newly developed substances, while the proposed ‘European Biotech Act’ would introduce regulatory sandboxes to enable innovative applications to be assessed within controlled and adaptable regulatory conditions.125 It is also an approach that has found favor with the UK Government in (for example) its position on the regulation of artificial intelligence (AI), which is principles- and regulator-led.126 Despite the support for this approach, flexibility must be managed carefully because it can risk offending the rule of law and the very legitimacy of the statute. Mechanisms for future adaption should therefore be framed to preserve legal certainty and democratic legitimacy by confining delegated powers within clear statutory limits and subjecting material changes to enhanced scrutiny. They should be developed through public deliberation to secure an acceptable compromise between adaptability and accountability.

The model Act provides for Modules A and B in Schedules (Fig. 1). Provision will be made for variable commencement dates between and within the Schedules, depending on the state of technical capability and evidence of safety and public debate. To future proof the model Act, additional dormant Schedules could be added and debated, anticipating relevant biotechnologies on the horizon, implementable through secondary legislation and satisfaction of conditions set out in the primary legislation. A tentative suggestion is for a dormant Module C to address reproductive decision technologies and embryo selection, including expanded PGT, embryo whole-genome sequencing and AI-assisted embryo assessment. This currently falls within Module A but may in time deserve a separate module as it increasingly intersects with medical device and in vitro diagnostic regulation,127 including software and AI as medical devices.128 A dormant Module D might focus on clinical approaches to artificial gestation and ex utero development, including the interface between extended ex vivo culture, artificial womb technology and associated interventions. This module could encompass both research and clinical uses. For example, it could also encompass the development of embryo-like structures for research,129 and potentially in future, the extended gestation of embryos and fetuses.130

In time, further modules could be added through primary legislation, which, while traditionally slow and cumbersome, is better suited to change in the model modular system.

VI. MODULE A: EMBRYO-RELATED TREATMENT AND RESEARCH PROJECTS

Module A is currently the most urgent focus for reform because scientific capability is advancing most rapidly in an area bounded by the most settled legal and ethical limits, placing the greatest strain on the current framework. Whereas my analysis to this point has been primarily conceptual, this more tentative section translates those principles into an illustrative account of how the scheme might operate in practice, including some additional red lines and core licensing conditions (Fig. 2).

Within the model Act, a distinction is drawn between the regulation of treatment and research.131 Dealing briefly with the treatment component, the model Act will retain the HFEA adoption of a premises-based licensing system and confinement of certain activities to licensed clinics. The HFEA has made reform proposals that could be incorporated into the model Act, including (inter alia) a risk-based inspection program enabling light-touch oversight for consistently high-performing clinics and intensive oversight where there is evidence of poor compliance, a wider array of proportionate enforcement tools and extended remit to consider the ways in which treatments and unproven add-on treatments are researched, promoted and advertised.132 As outlined above, licensing requirements will be applied to specified activities. The relevant approved activities can be expected to expand as some techniques translate into clinical use, and the risk-graded mechanisms utilized to approve PGT-M by condition and mitochondrial donation case-by-case could in future incorporate new ‘advanced reproductive clinical interventions’ potentially including IVG and heritable genome editing if they are proved sufficiently safe and effective, and PGT-P if accuracy and utility are established or regulation is considered necessary because patients are unsafely accessing treatment abroad. The model Act would set out conditions by which secondary legislation could make this feasible.

Research oversight sits alongside, and sometimes within clinics but it is organized differently. It is governed through project-based permissions that escalate in intensity according to defined tiers, as activities move closer to ethically relevant transition points and as the capabilities pursued become more developmentally integrated or closer to reproduction. Codes of Practice will guide tier allocation which HRBA will confirm, then assign applicable module conditions and specify escalation triggers for later review if the project changes course. In both treatment and research, the organizing principle is that oversight should track what is being done and what the system may become capable of, rather than turning solely on the provenance of the starting material or the name of the technique.

Functions of HRBA—Module A.

VI.A. The Research Tiers

The tiered model through which Module A operates seeks to match regulatory intensity with the activities undertaken and the capabilities pursued. The 2024 NCoB report identified options for research projects that could be tested in a sandbox to govern embryo-like structures in an incremental governance approach building on existing law, from certification, to registration to licensing.133 Building on these oversight options, the model Act would set out four research tiers and the Schedules would then set out high-level module- and technology-specific criteria, leaving the detail to be developed and adapted by HRBA in the Codes of Practice:

Research project tiers—Module A134

Tier 1: Baseline governance

Tier 1 covers routine human cellular and gamete research conducted at the level of individual cell types or simple cell systems that do not model coordinated embryonic development, such as research on gametes, non-fertilized IVG, iPSCs and most two-dimensional embryo-like structures that do not exhibit organized multicellular patterning. This work studies cellular function, differentiation or genetic modification and is subject to baseline institutional governance rather than HRBA registration or licensing.

Research remains subject to baseline statutory duties relating to consent, provenance, traceability and record-keeping, together with any applicable institutional ethics or governance requirements, with guidance set out in a HRBA Code of Practice. Compliance is secured primarily through institutional governance and audit, with serious or repeated breaches capable of regulatory referral where they suggest non-compliance with underlying statutory duties or misclassification of work.

Tier 2: Mandatory registration and ethical review

Tier 2 is triggered where the research models aspects of coordinated human developmental processes rather than examining isolated cellular components. Typical work in this tier includes multicellular developmental models, partial three-dimensional embryo-like structures, organized stem cell aggregates or later-stage IVG approaching the generation of functional gametes.

Following approval through a relevant ethics route, such as an NHS Research Ethics Committee or the UK Stem Cell Bank Steering Committee, the project must be registered with HRBA before it begins. Registration is a light-touch, procedural requirement rather than a permission-based process. It is justified where work remains outside licensing thresholds but is sufficiently novel, uncertain or embryo-relevant to require regulatory visibility and the ability to identify misclassification or movement into Tier 3. End-of-project reporting is mandatory, and breaches, failures to register or material departures from registered terms are recorded on a public register and taken into consideration in future applications.135

Tier 3: Licensing

Tier 3 applies where activities or capabilities engage recognized reproductive transition points or involve entities that approach embryo-like organization, thereby presenting elevated ethical and safety risks. It encompasses the creation and culture of embryos for research up to 14 days or the appearance of the primitive streak, the creation and culture of 3D embryo-like structures up to specified features, embryo creation using IVG-derived gametes and the genetic alteration of embryos or 3D embryo-like structures (see discussion below).

The model Act sets out licensing conditions tailored either to particular biotechnologies or to relevant modular regulatory triggers, supplemented in Codes of Practice, and including requirements for periodic review and project reporting. Applications are made via a single portal to HRBA and a specialized stem cell research ethics committee within the NHS HRA Research Ethics Service,136 with ethical review and regulatory licensing operating as distinct but coordinated approvals.

Tier 4: Enhanced licensing conditions for ethically sensitive and translational proposals

Tier 4 is reserved for exceptional proposals that approach the outer boundary of what is ethically permissible in research but are permitted under the model Act, including requests to move beyond Tier 3 default developmental limits. It also covers Clinical Gateway projects seeking a new clinical pathway where no other regulatory route exists. This route provides bespoke oversight for translational activities that fall outside existing clinical regulatory pathways, while the clinical route is explored, ahead of it being paused, stopped or standardized within the HRBA treatment licensing scheme. Some applications might be permitted under the model Act itself, whereas others would require commencement, secondary legislation or primary legislation before they could proceed. The Clinical Gateway is not a free-standing power to permit first-in-human use, but a procedural mechanism capable of being triggered only under the powers and conditions specified in the Act.

This tier imposes a high justificatory threshold, enhanced monitoring and reporting duties, and tightly specified endpoints, with permissions time-limited and project-specific, and subject to periodic review or modification in light of emerging evidence.

My proposed tiered model is broadly aligned with the ISSCR Guidelines137 in that it distinguishes routine activity from research requiring increased oversight, reserves specialist oversight for higher-risk interventions and treats movement toward reproductive use as the point of greatest regulatory sensitivity. In functional terms, Tier 1 corresponds to ISSCR Category 1A, Tier 3 to Category 2 and Tier 4 to an enhanced form of Category 2 for exceptionally sensitive activity. The model Act creates a distinct intermediate layer (Tier 2) requiring registration for activities that ISSCR treats as ‘reportable’ (Category 1B), and it embeds an exceptional Tier 4, providing a case-by-case route for certain ISSCR Category 3A activities, and including a Clinical Gateway within Tier 4 for clinical translation.

VI.B. Embryo Research

Within this structure, creation of a hESC line falls within Tier 2, whereas creation of a hiPSC line will ordinarily fall within Tier 1. Research utilizing embryos, gametes and stem cell lines falls within Tiers 1 to 4 depending on levels of complexity, integration, features and triggers, as I now describe.

There are wide international variations and clinical, ethical and legal disagreement about how the embryo should be defined.138 The model Act retains the current open, functional legal definition139 and Codes of Practice will provide practical classification guidance on application in marginal cases. The model Act retains the current developmental boundary, expressed as 14 days from fertilization or the appearance of the primitive streak if earlier. Sarah Franklin and Emily Jackson have convincingly shown that the 14-day limit was justified from sociological, ethical and scientific positions, and that this has helped to underpin the social compromise through which embryo research has been permitted.140 Scientific advances that enable extended culture have renewed debate about extending embryo research beyond 14 days, and meanwhile, there is evidence of shifting views among the public on what may be acceptable in research and clinical care.141 In 2021, the ISSCR removed embryo research beyond 14 days from its list of prohibited activities and called for international and national conversations which, if they culminate in jurisdiction-specific public support, could justify research beyond 14 days.142 The UK Government has stated that it has no plans to extend the limit,143 but the HFEA has expressed openness to law reform to permit an extension beyond 14 days,144 and an (ongoing at the time of writing) NCoB project has conducted a public dialogue and working group deliberation to better understand public views and values.145 The model Act presents an opportunity to provide a structured and transparent level of oversight, enhancing conditions for a legitimate reassessment of the permissible-impermissible line without compromising public trust. Accordingly, I propose that under the model Act, embryo research up to 14 days or the appearance of the primitive streak requires a Tier 3 license. Extension beyond the default 14-day limit up to a 28-day limit is available only through Tier 4 restricted licensing on a case-by-case basis, with a requirement to demonstrate that the scientific aim cannot reasonably be met by alternative means.146 Decisions will be time-limited, project-specific and subject to enhanced monitoring. A summary of all Tier 4 research will be made publicly available.

VI.C. IVG

An explicit clinical boundary applies to IVG research under the model Act, which distinguishes between research activities directed to derivation, characterization and testing of IVG-derived gametes and any clinical use of IVG-derived gametes to initiate a pregnancy. Research to derive, characterize and test IVG-derived gametes requires only baseline governance, while research that uses such gametes to create embryos is permissible only under a Tier 3 license, at which point the embryo is subject to the conditions applied to all research embryos, including the developmental limit and prohibitions on transfer. Additional IVG-specific license conditions reflect provenance, which requires adaptations to donor consent, scale (because IVG embryos could potentially be created in large numbers requiring quantitative limits and necessity justifications) and transparency (so that IVG-derived embryos are clearly labeled as such in the register and research publications).

The model Act also creates a structured pathway for translation by making provision for the clinical use of IVG-derived gametes to initiate pregnancy147 through the Tier 4 Clinical Gateway, but treats that step as prohibited by default unless and until the permitted uses are changed through secondary legislation. This mechanism is intended to ensure that any move beyond research occurs only after further parliamentary scrutiny and an evidence base demonstrating safety, efficacy and societal acceptability. At that point, it remains confined to a designated research project under Tier 4, approved only on a case-by-case basis, with a presumption against authorization absent a compelling justification and a robust risk management plan, and with built-in stop conditions, requirements for long-term follow up and transparent (and privacy-respecting) reporting. In effect, the Act permits the scientific work needed to evaluate IVG while holding clinical deployment behind a legislative gate, and then, if opened, treating first-in-human use as clinical research rather than routine treatment. Beyond that, standard use in fertility treatment would require additional legislative change, which the model Act could provide for by way of secondary legislation subject to conditions.

VI.D. Heritable Genome Editing

Genome editing follows the same separation between research permission and clinical authorization. Genome editing in human gametes, embryos or embryo-like structures for research purposes is permitted only within Tier 3 licensed research projects, under strict endpoints and non-transfer conditions. Reproductive (heritable) use remains prohibited by default. The model Act includes a delegated power allowing secondary legislation to activate a Tier 4 clinical authorization pathway, subject to public deliberation, high evidential thresholds and enhanced monitoring and reporting requirements.

VI.E. Embryo-Like Structures

The ISSCR guidance recommends that all three-dimensional embryo-like structures are subject to specialist oversight review,148 which translates to Tiers 2–4 in the model Act. As we have seen, embryo-like structures is an umbrella term that encompasses a range of model-types. The nomenclature follows the argument I have set out elsewhere that some entities currently described as models may, in future, replicate embryos more closely and therefore raise different classification and governance questions.149 Even then, there are strong reasons to regulate embryo-like structures separately, including their diversity, their non-linear and heterogeneous development and the need for proportionate oversight.150 Accordingly, the model Act recognizes three-dimensional embryo-like structures as a distinct legal category to embryos.

To enhance parity across modules based on capability and activity, partial models representing specific embryonic tissues or systems should be aligned with organoid model regulation in Module B and regulated under Tier 2, with defined pause points, justified culture limits and triggers for escalation to a higher tier. They should only be developed ‘for the minimum time necessary to achieve the scientific objective’.151 Embryo-like structures that exhibit most or all features of an embryo (comprehensive structures), on the other hand, should be subject to a development-based limit comparable to the 14-day limit that applies in embryo research. A different form of ceiling is justified for these structures: a morphology- and capacities-based backstop that is better suited to entities without a day zero.152 Since the structures may be designed to lack particular features, the trigger is the appearance of any morphological feature that would ordinarily be present in an embryo beyond 14 days. As with embryos, development beyond this stage can be authorized under Tier 4, subject to additional monitoring and oversight requirements. Consistent with the NCoB report, no research project should proceed beyond the stage at which the embryo becomes a fetus at approximately 56–60 days.153 In addition, for projects authorized beyond the equivalent 14-day stage, a stop point is specified at the outset and justified by reference to the research objective.154

This dual developmental limit for embryo-like structures that closely resemble the natural embryo is supported in a recent commentary by Alejandro De Los Angeles et al., who propose a limit of 28-day equivalence as a standard pathway and 56-day equivalence for exceptional cases.155 My proposed 14-day limit under Tier 3 is not intended to reject the putative scientific value of extending the culture period for embryo-like structures. Rather, parity with embryo research is sought at the lower boundary because the 14-day limit functions as a widely understood and legally entrenched trigger for heightened scrutiny, thereby supporting regulatory clarity and public confidence. However, parity at this point does not entail identical treatment thereafter. For embryos, any extension beyond 14 days is appropriately framed as incremental (such as to 28 days, as I have suggested) and subject to case-by-case licensing and a ‘shortest necessary’ principle, reflecting the embryo’s special status and the fragility of the public settlement. For embryo-like structures, by contrast, even those closely resembling the natural embryo do not constitute embryos at a molecular level. Moreover, they are not created by fertilization, and the model Act prohibits them from being designed or authorized for reproductive purposes. An upper threshold of 56-day equivalence is defensible as a precautionary limit aligned with the embryo-to-fetus transition and as a guard against the development of ethically concerning capacities, while remaining a ceiling rather than a target and operating only under Tier 4 authorization with pre-specified stop points and enhanced monitoring. This complies with the NCoB report’s view that embryos and embryo-like structures should be regulated separately and the recommendation for the UK to impose an upper limit of 56-day (8 weeks) equivalence for more comprehensive structures.156 This upper limit predates the possibility of sentience or pain. In 2010, the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (RCOG) considered that conscious pain perception is unlikely to occur in a human fetus before 24–28 weeks gestation.157 This remains the case, but prior to 24 weeks, the fetus may experience hormonal, physiological or neural responses to noxious stimuli, termed ‘nociceptive activity’.158 In 2022, the RCOG noted that evidence of nociceptive onset is lacking, but 56 days predates by a considerable margin evidence of spontaneous movements from 24 weeks, or grimace reflexes from 23 weeks.159

Like IVG, special embryo-like-structure-specific license conditions would be required. Emily Jackson has cogently argued that the statutory purposes that currently apply to embryo research160 would require adaptation to work for embryo-like structures.161 The latter can usefully be created at scale and utilized to test the toxicity of drugs used in pregnancy, but this would not currently be a permitted use for embryo research. Nor, she argues, would it be sensible to apply to embryo-like structures the necessity principle162 that currently applies to embryo research. This provides that the research can only be conducted on embryos if it could not be carried out in some other way, such as using animal models.163 Instead the focus in ISSCR guidance (and in the proposed model Act), is on ensuring the evaluation of

the scientific rationale for the use of the particular model, the reasons why a less complex model is not appropriate, and a proposed time limit for the culture, related to the developmental milestones under study.164

These features highlight the importance of maintaining a regulatory distinction between embryos and embryo-like structures so that license conditions are coherent across comparable entities and aligned with embryo regulation where appropriate, while also addressing the features that make embryo-like structures distinct. The model Act will set out red lines, including a prohibition on transfer for fertility treatment. The 1990 Act currently prohibits the transfer into a woman’s body ‘an embryo other than a permitted embryo’,165 which would not include embryo-like structures under the model Act because it is defined there as a separate entity. As such this wording would be repealed and new provisions made to ensure that neither human research embryos, nor human embryo-like structures can be transferred into a person, animal or gestated artificially to viability.

VII. MODULE B—INTEGRATED MODELS OF HUMAN ORIGIN

For Module B, regulation uses the same tiered structure, but escalation through the tiers is triggered by different capabilities. In Module A, tier shifts are driven by reproductive proximity, developmental-stage boundaries and the emergence of embryo-associated developmental features. They are driven in Module B by integration and maturation, the creation of new biological interfaces, translational potential and chimera-related trajectories.

Most organoid work is currently low-risk and will fall within Tier 1, with baseline requirements on consent, provenance and traceability. The model Act will anticipate a time when tier escalation is needed where organoids are developed or maintained in ways that materially increase complexity or translational relevance—for example, linking different neural organoids into an integrated assembloid and keeping it in culture for extended periods, or adding vascular or immune components to support more sustained maturation. In chimera and transplantation pathways, escalation turns on the method and timing of human cell introduction, the likelihood of contribution to brain function or the germline, the degree of integration sought and any animal welfare concerns.

The module is designed to work with, rather than replace, existing regulators while providing a coherent permissions pathway for escalation. Tissue procurement and storage will continue to engage the HTA requirements where applicable, and research ethics review will remain central for participant and donor protections. Where animals are involved, Home Office licensing under the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act continues to apply, and Module B’s added structured assessment would seek to align with the animal research framework. Where work moves toward human clinical use, the scheme requires early engagement with MHRA expectations for quality, manufacturing and safety and treats clinical deployment as a distinct authorization step. Across these examples, the model Act will make amendments to existing legislation to enhance alignment between the regulatory schemes.

Module B indicative activities

Tier 1: in vitro organoid research with limited integration and short to moderate culture durations.

Tier 2: advanced neural organoids with increased integration, organoids with translational relevance (eg organ-on-chip systems), organoids developed on a clinical pathway (eg advanced therapy candidates).

Tier 3: integrated systems with plausible clinical deployability, transplantation into animals and high-risk chimera trajectories.

Tier 4: neural integration, first-in-human deployment, integrated systems at boundary of therapy and research.

Difficult classification questions will arise. For example, an embryo-like structure used to model cardiac tissue could plausibly fall into Module A or B. In the model Act, the first question is not the categorization of the technology, but what activities are being undertaken and what capabilities are being pursued. If the construct is organized to mimic the whole or partial embryo in three-dimensional form, it will fall within Module A, including developmental-feature endpoints and the non-transfer prohibition. If it is a tissue-directed organogenesis model without plausible whole embryo mimicry, the issues align with Module B. Where features point in both directions, HRBA assigns a lead module and applies integrated conditions, maintaining tier equivalence as far as possible by ensuring that the same level of oversight intensity attaches to comparable profiles. In cases of uncertainty, the project is treated as within the higher-relevance tier pending HRBA review. In practice, then, the researcher will select the appropriate module based on classification, but HRBA will have discretion to reassign module or tier criteria. The disruptive potential will be mitigated because comparable capabilities attract comparable regulatory intensity.

VIII. CONCLUSION

I have described a UK reference model for regulating human reproductive and developmental biotechnologies at a moment when scientific capability is outpacing regulation. I propose a new statute and specialist regulator, building on and extending the existing HFEA remit, to provide a single, coherent permissions route across research and translation, while preserving clear boundaries that sustain public trust, and integration with other regulators to preserve specialist expertise.

The model’s novelty lies in its modular structure and tiered oversight. It sets out common baseline standards on consent, provenance, traceability and transparency. Within Module A, Tier 1 is reserved for routine work operating at the level of isolated cells, gametes or simple cell cultures manageable through institutional governance; Tier 2 for research that constructs multicellular developmental models or reproductive cell systems approximating embryonic processes, and which requires registration and ethical review; Tier 3 for activities engaging specified developmental or reproductive thresholds requiring licensing; and Tier 4 for sensitive or translational frontier work subject to enhanced licensing conditions. On this model, regulatory intensity increases not simply because a technology bears a particular label, but because the activity in question models, enables or crosses developmentally or reproductively significant thresholds. Tailored red lines for embryo-related work and for advanced organoid and integrated systems enhance public trust and researcher confidence, while a distinct registration tier for embryo-relevant work below licensing thresholds proportionately improves regulatory visibility without imposing full ex ante control. Explicit provision for escalation between tiers, for boundary cases, and for a Clinical Gateway route for translation from research to clinic helps to clarify when work remains within ordinary research governance, when it requires registration or licensing, and when further legislative or regulatory action is needed before clinical use can proceed. It is a system designed to support legitimate innovation while keeping the most sensitive steps, such as embryo creation, developmental extension and any move toward reproduction under the highest level of scrutiny to prevent ethical breaches and maintain public trust. It combines continuity with the existing HFEA approach and greater coherence while allowing oversight to be calibrated proportionately with what the research does and what regulatory concerns it raises.

The article sets out relevant regulatory principles, and more tentatively assigns emerging biotechnologies to tiers based on their current trajectories. The latter part is not a complete model and ought not to be one until public and stakeholder deliberation have further defined the boundaries of what is ethically and socially acceptable and scientifically optimal. I hope the value is illustrative and practical, clarifying the design options and trade-offs and providing a benchmark against which revisions could be tested as science matures. The principles for reform, meanwhile, set out the kind of comprehensive overhaul I believe will ultimately be required. If the UK Government seeks to harness emerging biotechnologies to advance science, improve health and strengthen the economy, regulation must be coherent and consistent across biotechnologies to support both their development and their amalgamations. At present, the framework is shaped on outdated sector boundaries (for example tissue regulation versus assisted reproduction) and by technology-specific silos, with incremental rules increasingly ill-suited to the pace and direction of scientific change. The UK therefore faces a strategic choice: to update the system in a coordinated way and set a leading, internationally credible standard, or to respond piecemeal and belatedly, ceding leadership and accepting avoidable friction for research and translation.
