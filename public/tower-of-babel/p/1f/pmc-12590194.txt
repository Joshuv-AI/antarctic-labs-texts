Experiences of mothers in the context of a staffing intervention in select newborn units in Kenyan public hospitals
Authors: Dorothy Oluoch, Nancy Odinga, Caroline Waithira, Gloria Ngaiza, Justinah Maluni, Edna Mutua, Michuki Maina, Kenneth Karumba, Sassy Molyneux, Sebastian Fuller, Fred Were, Mike English, Caroline Jones, HIGH Q Hospital Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal involvement, effective communication and adequate support are widely recognised as promoting respectful care and better maternal experience of small and sick neonate care. Nurses play a critical role in neonatal care, but staffing shortages and workplace stress significantly impact their ability to provide quality care. Few studies have examined the implications of staffing interventions for maternal care experiences in newborn units.MethodsIn this ethnographic study, we explored mothers' experiences following a staffing intervention (addition of 3 nurses and 3 ward assistants) across four newborn units in Kenyan County referral hospitals. Data were collected through nonparticipant observations and interviews with mothers. Across the four hospitals, we interviewed 46 mothers and conducted over 1000 observation hours over three phases of data collection. We examined mothers' experiences of care and any changes with the staffing intervention in relation to communication, involvement in care, nurse-mother relationships and support.FindingsMothers in newborn units face intense emotional, physical and practical challenges with limited guidance and support to cope with the demands of neonatal caregiving. We did not observe significant changes in communication, nurse-mother relationships, or the support offered to mothers due to the additional nurses. However, the addition of ward assistants was valued by mothers, who appreciated their role in orientation, maintaining ward cleanliness, and cleaning feeding cups. Following the staffing intervention, mothers continued to report challenges with adequacy of information, disrespectful communication, and limited support with tasks. Peer-to-peer support between mothers was evident across all sites and study phases. Contextual factors and norms of care greatly influenced nursing practices.ConclusionsEnhancing mothers' experiences in neonatal units will require integrated approaches that acknowledge their critical role in newborn care. Mothers need structured psychosocial and practical support, strengthened communication with healthcare providers, and broader structural interventions that significantly increase staff numbers and shift care norms.Keywords: Mothers, Newborn care, Involvement in care, Support, Respectful communication, Experiences of care

Background

It is widely recognised that nurses play a critical role in caring for small and sick newborns in neonatal units (Gathara et al., 2020). They provide direct nursing care to vulnerable babies and have educational and supportive roles for mothers who are often distressed after giving birth and unprepared for the subsequent admission of their baby into the neonatal unit (Aagaard et al., 2015; Adu-Bonsaffoh et al., 2022; Apedani et al., 2021). The WHO and professional bodies such as the American Academy of Paediatrics (AAP) and the UK department of health recommend a nurse-to-baby ratio of 1:1 in Neonatal Intensive Care Units (NICUs),1:2 for High dependency unit and 1:4 for neonatal units (ACOG Committee on Obstetric Practice, 2017), but Kenya, like many Sub-Saharan African countries, continues to grapple with health workforce shortages and struggle to meet these recommendations (Gathara et al., 2020; Wakaba et al., 2014). These shortages has been linked to gaps in communication and education of mothers in neonatal care settings (Gathara et al., 2020), which in turn contribute to heightened anxiety and fears among mothers (Lorié et al., 2021).

Life in the newborn unit for mothers is usually marked with anxiety and fear, with constant worry about their baby's outcome, the technological aspects of support and care (such as Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) and phototherapy), and their ability to safely handle their babies (Apedani et al., 2021; Oluoch et al., 2023; Russell et al., 2014). The importance of involving caregivers in caring for their hospitalised small and sick newborns is widely recognised and recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) (World Health, 2020). Family Centred Care (FCC) (Irlam and Bruce, 2002), a care approach recommended in neonatal units promotes collaboration among healthcare providers, patients and their families. Besides communication, the other core principles of FCC include, dignity and respect, information sharing, participation and collaboration. As highlighted in literature, FCC is associated with benefits that include reduced health utilisation, lower parental stress and greater caregiver confidence in managing child's illness (Aljawad et al., 2025). As highlighted in Maleki et al.'s (2022) systematic review, mothers gain both emotional and practical support when nurses refer to and encourage FCC model, encourage skin-to-skin care, provide parent emotional support and education programmes about daily care (Maleki et al., 2022),

Parental participation in care is associated with better clinical outcomes, including better weight gain, early discharge, improved bonding with the baby, and less anxiety among caregivers (De Bernardo et al., 2017; O'Brien et al., 2018; Wanduru et al., 2023). Their involvement is especially valuable through low-cost interventions such as promoting interaction and dynamic communication with their baby. Bonding and early parent-infant interactions play an important role in the development of communication skills and should be encouraged and integrated in neonatal care practices (Harding et al., 2019). A mother's capability to provide bedside care for their fragile and small newborns is influenced by various factors, such as previous experience caring for an infant, the level of support received in the neonatal unit, and their mental and physical well-being (Black et al., 2009). Although family-centred care is encouraged in newborn units and recommended by the WHO (Alshahrani et al., 2018; De Bernardo et al., 2017; World Health, 2020), the challenging environments of the neonatal unit can hinder staff from offering sufficient support to mothers, including the timely and appropriate provision of information, which undermines effective mother-provider relationships (Russell et al., 2014).

In many LMICs, ineffective mother-provider relationships are exacerbated by health worker shortages, high workloads and workplace stress, factors that often lead to nurse burnout and both physical and emotional exhaustion (Afulani et al., 2021; Laschinger et al., 2011; Shah et al., 2021). Indeed, evidence suggests that nurse burnout results in depersonalisation and feelings of helplessness, affecting interpersonal skills, job satisfaction and performance (Coulter, 2013). In turn, this affects care quality, patient safety, staff attitudes to patients, and providers' experiences of care (27–29), highlighting the inter-relationship between staff and patient experiences (Dawson, 2018). Further effects of nursing workforce deficits include task prioritisation, with prominence given to biomedical rather than caring tasks, and much direct bedside care off-loaded to caregivers (McKnight et al., 2020; Nzinga et al., 2019; Oluoch et al., 2023).

Despite the recognition of nurses' critical role in delivering effective neonatal care and shaping the experiences of mothers and families, very few studies examine nurse staffing interventions. Thus, to address this gap, we conducted the HIGH Q (Harnessing Innovation in Global Health for Quality Care) programme in four level 5 public hospitals in Kenya. In Kenya, essential neonatal care is provided across level 2–6 hospitals. Special and advanced care is typically offered in levels 5 and 6 respectively (see supplementary material 1).

A central element of the HIGH Q study was an intervention first to add nurses and, subsequently, non-professional staff (ward assistants) to newborn units. All four HIGH-Q study hospitals were part of the NEST 360 (Newborn Essential Solutions and Technologies) programme. The NEST 360 programme focuses on sustaining innovation, developing an education ecosystem, and implementing evidence-based care in neonatal units in partnership with the government and 13 public hospitals in Kenya (NEST360, n.d.). The NEST 360 project has integrated a bundle of cost-effective technologies across the neonatal units of these public hospitals (NEST360, n.d.).

We conducted a coordinated set of quantitative and qualitative studies to measure and document the processes and outcomes of the HIGH Q staffing intervention. These studies are described in detail elsewhere (Imam et al., 2022) and span intervention effects on: i) nursing tasks completed; ii) use of recently supplied supportive care technologies; iii) nurses; and iv) mothers experiences. In this paper, we focus on mothers' experiences of neonatal care and explore the extent to which the staffing interventions influenced their participation and experiences of providing care for their babies in the neonatal units.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection methods

This study explored mothers' participation and their care experiences in the neonatal units before, during and after implementing the HIGH-Q staffing intervention. Data were collected through non-participant observations and interviews with mothers across the four purposively selected County hospitals. The research team comprised female social scientists (NO, CW, EM, DO, SM and CJ), with many years of experience in hospital and community-based ethnographic work in Kenya. We held weekly reflexive sessions to ensure rigour. We undertook three rounds of data collection: at baseline (January–April 2022; phase 1), after the introduction of the three additional nurses between (August–January 2023; phase 2), and after the addition of three ward assistants (May–July 2023; phase 3). Data was collected through non-participant observations across the four hospitals. We also interviewed 46 purposively selected mothers. We interviewed mothers whose babies had been admitted to the newborn unit, were aged above 18 years, and were willing to be followed up and interviewed at home within two weeks post-discharge. Mothers were recruited in the hospital upon discharge and follow up visits were made to their homes to conduct the interviews. Interviews lasting between 60 and 90 min were conducted in the national language- Kiswahili and translated and transcribed in English. Data were collected by NO, CW, JM, Kenyan female social scientists with experience in qualitative data collection in health research. GN, a Tanzanian female medical doctor with training in qualitative data collection methods, also contributed to data collection.

Transcripts and observation notes were uploaded to NVIVO 12 software for management and analysis. Data were analysed using an inductive thematic approach, following Braun and Clarke's (2006) six phase process: familiarisation with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes and producing the final report (Braun and Clarke, 2006). NO, CW and DO developed the initial codebook after reviewing a few transcripts. New codes were added iteratively as additional transcripts and observation notes were analysed. All additions to the codebook were jointly discussed by the team throughout the coding process. Our analysis was informed by the HIGH-Q theory of change (ToC), one aspect of which focused on the anticipated effects of the intervention on the roles and experiences of the mothers in the neonatal unit (Maina et al., 2025). The expectation was that the additional nurses would lead to a better staff-to-patient ratio and improve nurses' experiences, allowing them more time and capacity to communicate with the mothers. Adding ward assistants to carry out non-technical tasks was also expected to free up nurses' time, help orient mothers, and provide support in their roles. Thus, the intervention as a whole was expected to improve the experience of mothers and result in better family-centred care. Importantly, a key assumption was that real increases in nursing and non-professional staff time would be achieved.

Drawing on the TOC and following a thematic inductive coding process (Braun and Clarke, 2006), we charted the baseline data by hospital to identify key similarities and differences in the roles and experiences of mothers across the four hospitals and identified shared experiences across hospitals. We then examined the data for changes in these roles and experiences with the addition of new nurses and ward assistants with a focus on expected changes in roles and communications, recognising that in each phase, the mothers were different, making it impossible to look for changes in the experiences of an individual mother over time. We focussed on observations of any changes in practices within each neonatal unit and the mothers' reports in the wards at these three time points.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Kenya Medical Research Institute (KEMRI) Scientific and Ethics Review Committee (SERU) KEMRI/SERU/CGMR-C/229/4203. Additional approvals were sought from the National Commission for Science, Technology, and Innovation (NACOSTI) (NACOSTI/P/22/21337) and the Oxford Tropical Research Ethics Committee (Reference-26-21). Furthermore, the implementing Counties and the respective hospitals granted formal approvals to execute the HIGH-Q project (Karumba et al., 2024). We obtained written informed consent from all the mothers interviewed, and through ongoing engagements, we received permission from the respective neonatal unit staff and mothers to conduct the non-participant observations (Karumba et al., 2024). Ethical issues encountered during the research were regularly discussed by the team in structured debrief meetings. In this embedded health systems research, we developed a framework for handling practical and ethical dilemmas in the project. Notable challenges included the emotional distress experienced by the researchers witnessing deaths among extremely preterm and critically ill newborns, as well as the need to manage expectations from both mothers and healthcare providers. Details of the developed protocols, issues captured in the debrief meetings, and our responses which included feeding back to the hospitals are covered extensively as a separate paper currently under review (Mutua et al., 2025). Safety concerns requiring urgent attention were addressed through immediate action, which involved alerting staff to ensure a timely response. These processes are detailed in a separate paper currently under review (Mutua et al., 2025).

Study setting

We conducted the study across four County public hospitals in Kenya. The selected hospitals were a mix of urban (2 hospitals) and semi-urban (2 hospitals). These hospitals provide intermediate care to small and sick newborns. As part of the NEST 360 programme, these hospitals benefited from the trainings and support with the NEST bundle of technologies ((NEST360°)). All the study hospitals had a low staffing level per shift (nurse and non-professional staff) compared to the admissions, resulting in low nurse-baby ratios. Data from other work packages of this project showed variations in staffing levels throughout the project (Imam et al., 2025; Odinga et al., 2025).

Results

We collected data covering over 140 shifts in the four hospitals (day and night shifts), covering the three study phases (baseline, after introduction of new nurses and after introduction of ward assistants) and 46 interviews with mothers.

Mothers' experiences at baseline

Involvement in care and support they receive

At baseline, the mothers' roles in the neonatal unit were similar across the four study hospitals (Table 2). We observed that mothers were centrally involved in caring for their babies, albeit with minimal support or information on how to perform the required tasks. These caring tasks included activities always performed by mothers: feeding (expressing, nasogastric tube (NG) tube feeding, cup feeding, and breastfeeding), cleaning their baby (known as top tailing), administering supplements, monitoring/observing the baby, cord care, diaper change, adjusting oxygen masks and eye covers for babies on phototherapy and rinsing of cups. In the absence of patient attendants/casuals, they cleaned feeding cups.

A range of contextual issues, such as staffing and availability of resources, influenced mothers' abilities to perform these tasks effectively. For example, the cleaning of cups was done either by nurses or by casuals or patient attendants were present at baseline. When nurses were overstretched, or the casuals were absent, delays in feeding occasionally occurred, and mothers had to wait for the feeding cups. These delays were frustrating for mothers. Besides cleaning the cups, hot water for top tailing also required input from the casuals or patient attendants. These delays affected the ability of mothers to undertake their required tasks.

“We used to wake up at six and wash them, at twelve o'clock, and at other times, there is no water because someone who warms water is not within, so we will not wash them. Yes, they are all dirty if you see if there is no one to wash the cup, also there is no one to wash those clothes”

(H2 R1 M04).

There were additional tasks specific to some hospitals. The mothers in HI were also expected to rinse the bowls used in cleaning their babies and in H4 they filled in the comprehensive monitoring charts after each feed. These mothers captured data on feeding, the amount of milk the baby was fed, whether the baby had an IV line, vomited, or passed urine and stool.

Although limited, mothers across all hospitals reported receiving some support with their many tasks. Nurses, for instance, prepared formula milk and occasionally offered guidance on tasks like cleaning the baby and feeding. However, this support was inconsistent—often dependent on the nurse's availability, workload, and interpersonal dynamics.

The first time yes [I received help with washing my baby] but the other times I do it myself. I was scared to wash especially that one, I used to see as if I was going to press him. That's when another nurse came and helped me with this one while showing. She told me that you just do it gently not that you scrub him hard, just gently

(H2 R1 M03).

We also observed support from student nurses, non-professional staff (casuals and patient attendants), and peer-to-peer support among the mothers (Table 2). The support staff and patient attendants cleaned the cots and incubators in H1, H2 and H3, student nurses and other mothers supported by showing mothers struggling with feeding by explaining how to express, how to measure milk. The demands of providing neonatal care in these environments was observed and described in the informal conversations as being physically burdening for the mothers, especially those who had delivered via caesarean section.

Mothers experienced significant emotional burden. Daily life in all study hospitals was marked by fear and anxiety, especially among those whose babies' required technologies like CPAP or phototherapy. Mothers worried about what such interventions signified for their baby's health and future. A major source of distress was the fear of losing their baby. In such cases, psychosocial support and grief counselling were essential but, as noted by a mother and observed by researchers, not always available.

Okay, let's say your child has passed away, there are no people to talk to…when a child passes away, they just leave you there crying and screaming until you scare other mothers.

(H3 R1 M5).

Another cause of concern for the mothers was their baby's weight. All hospitals had a specified discharge weight for preterm babies. A baby not gaining weight, a drop in their weight, or instructions against feeding the baby due to their condition contributed to distress.

“You see that was a drop in baby's weight, since for two weeks the baby did not feed and had been only on IV fluids and drugs, so the baby became weak. It was not making me happy and yet the others would be fed milk after the mother expresses it, while you are just expressing and pouring the milk over and over”.

(H1 R1 M03).

Across all hospitals, mothers whose babies were being cup-fed worried about the baby not consuming enough milk, and there were inadequacies in the guidance provided, as depicted in this observation:

She (a cup-feeding mum) is however concerned that when she feeds the baby with a cup, the baby pours most of the milk. I ask her to raise her concerns with the nurse. She goes to student nurses to request them to fix feeding tubes for her baby. The response she gets is that feeding tubes are not fixed on any baby and that there is a reason why not all babies have feeding tubes [she was not given the reason]. She is asked to continue feeding the baby with a cup and position it well so the baby doesn't pour milk.

(R1 Observations Hos2)

Mothers whose babies were on technology expressed particular challenges with feeding and top tailing these babies. Mothers across the hospitals received support from nurses, students, ward assistants, casual staff, specialists, and other mothers. We observed that mothers who had been in the ward longer across the four facilities had developed more trusting relationships with nurses and were likely to be called upon to assist with the orientation of mothers.

Communication and relationships

One of the key points of communication between nurses and mothers was when the babies were first admitted to the ward and their mothers arrived, often separately, trying to locate their babies and understand what they should do. The orientation of mothers to the spaces and routines of the neonatal unit were handled differently across the four hospitals, but in all cases, the communication involved only brief instructions on where to get feeding cups, wearing of gowns while in the neonatal unit, as well as other expected hygiene practices. In H3, the daily group health talk was facilitated by a nurse at the beginning of every morning shift, which disadvantaged new mothers who arrived in the ward after the group morning talk. In H4, the nursing station was right by the entrance to the neonatal unit and so nurses could identify new mothers arriving in their home clothes and give them gowns and orientation on arrival. We observed that orientation seemed more detailed overall in H3 and included more information on the importance of breastfeeding and why it was important to maintain hygiene. Furthermore, depending on the nurse delivering the orientation, the mothers were sometimes encouraged to ask questions. The morning health talk in this hospital started with fellowshipping and prayers.

Communication and relationships also varied across and within the hospitals. There was one-to-one communication in all hospitals. However, the details provided and the depth of communication were often limited, as illustrated by the quote below.

“Now, when I got them in there [neonatal unit], I wondered why they had put those things in them [feeding tubes]. Do you know that I was not aware of that, and then in that place, it seems that every person is minding their own business. No one talks to you and tells you, ‘It is like this and that’, no. You just have to go and take care of your children. When I got them with those tubes, I did not enquire from anyone, but then when I looked around, I realised that there were others in those tubes, and they were being given milk. Even when it came to feeding them [twins] on milk, no one showed me how to feed them using the tubes”

(H1_R1_M6).

We observed and noted from the interviews that all the mothers in the neonatal unit had concerns about receiving inadequate or no information, for example, where a mother was not informed that her baby had been moved to a different cot or incubator or about having been started on a new technology. Some mothers complained of lack of or inadequate information concerning the treatment the baby was receiving, leading to feelings of powerlessness, especially as they could not easily question the nurses who were taking care of their babies.

“I just felt I was a little tense, I said that I won't keep on asking, let me just accept that he has been removed from the machine and all will be well.... I didn't even find someone to explain to me”

(H3 R1 M4).

The nature of the communication and interaction depended on the specific nurse's characteristics. Across the hospitals, there were reports of good nurses as well as nurses who were considered rude and thus avoided by mothers. Disrespectful communication was reported across all hospitals at baseline:

“I was scared of asking the nurses because they would…there is a time I asked something and I was asked, “How long have you been here?” And I told them one week. One week and you are not aware of things? “So you see this will make you afraid of approaching them. Maybe they will answer you in a bad way or they ignore you.”

(H1 R1 M05)

To avoid being scolded or shouted at, mothers sought out approachable “good” nurses or those they perceived as friendly, reaching out to and relying on them as valued sources of information and help.

“When you had a question there is that person, there is that nurse that you are comfortable with and you will go and ask them, why is the child like this?”

(H1 R1 M 04).

“The good ones were talking to me nicely. Even when they were putting the feeding tube, they were putting it while talking to the baby in a good way not like the one who was scolding him”

(H2 R1 M 03)

Mothers were aware that their emotional state when receiving information interfered with their understanding, as explained by this mother,

“I could not ask them at that point because I was emotional and I was also crying. So, I did not ask them at that time. Afterwards I asked them and they told me that the child had respiratory distress which meant that breathing was impossible unless being assisted by a machine. I did not understand at that time but later on I think after one or two days, I followed one doctor and this one properly explained to me. I was satisfied but I was afraid, but I was satisfied because they were helping the child”.

(H1 R1 M5).

Intervention effect on caregiver involvement in providing care and support received

During the second and third rounds of observations, after the introduction of new nurses and ward assistants, there appeared to be no difference in the role the mothers undertook in the neonatal unit. The challenges and frustrations expressed by the mothers in the neonatal unit at baseline persisted across all the study sites and phases. In large part, many of the reported and observed difficulties emanated from ongoing gaps in the support provided to the mothers. There were challenges with milk expression and feeding – both breastfeeding and cup feeding, especially for first-time mothers and mothers with twins or triplets.

At least they should support such mothers [mothers of twins, triplets], because at the same time you should express the milk and do all that stuff on your own and you end up feeling tired

(H4 R2 M 02).

This lack of critical and clear information on feeding contributed to mothers' stress, and mothers were likely to be blamed or scolded when errors occurred. Mothers in H4 also discussed their concerns regarding delegating tasks to students. A few mothers also had reservations about students performing specific procedures, such as fixing NG tubes. They were concerned about the delays, repercussions, and potential harm to the baby.

I was not happy about the people who were drawing blood from babies during their shifts. You would find that someone would come, and they didn't know how to cannulate the baby so they kept pricking the children and the child was crying and they couldn't find that line. And then to make it worse one would come and then find that line so easily within a minute. You couldn't say anything because your child's life was in their hands.

(H4 R2 M 02)

Some of the mothers also observed that nurses were overstretched and noted that this limited their ability to care for all the babies, especially when they were alone or when few nurses were on shift.

It's just being alone and then at times they get tired and sleepy. It affects them because while she is alone at night and falls sleepy, then you find that there are many children crying. And even taking care of the children, there are those who belch and it comes out through the nose and he is alone and has to do all those rounds and maybe he should be there with his students. And so you find yours has belched and then it comes out through the nose and he has not had time to check on him because he has to attend to many children. And so because she has not had not time to get to your child so the child is left unattended because he has to do all those rounds (H4 R2 M1).

Nurses provided varying levels of support to the mothers. No significant shifts in practice were observed across the study phases. Where it was provided, the mothers appreciated receiving support from healthcare workers as illustrated by this mother,

“The best thing someone has ever done for me is to help me with feeding the children. That really made me happy and then when you called them they would help you fix that tube so that really made me happy and then helping me prepare that Nan that made me happy”

(H3_R2_M2).

Our observation made at baseline regarding the cruciality of nurses' interpersonal qualities to the support offered to mothers continued to apply post-intervention. During their stay in the neonatal unit, mothers observed the interpersonal differences among the nurses. Across the study hospitals, they reported receiving support and help with tasks when they had an issue from the “good nurses”.

Our observations throughout all phases indicated that mothers continued to seek support from student nurses and ward assistants. In one facility, we observed that mothers would request the ward assistant to approach the nurses on their behalf. Notably, the same areas identified as challenges—such as feeding—were also points where support was received. Mothers reported help from nurses, students, and ward assistants. Nurses often delegated to students when busy. More attention was given to mothers of babies flagged for poor weight gain.

He taught me how to give my baby milk through a tube and also put it back in case it comes out. How to clean the baby and put back the machine

(H4 R2 M 01).

Mothers also appreciated when staff encouraged them, especially when their baby was not doing well.

“I would say that the nurse I'm telling you about calmed me down because when you find out that your child is surrounded by other babies in the unit, you get worried. When she told me was, ‘Don't worry, your baby will be okay.’ I felt like someone cared about me. You know how you can feel like you're alone in your own world? But then I saw that there was someone else who was mindful about me. She left what they were being taught and came to me to explain how things were set up”

(H1 R2 M 04).

Other sources of support: peer-to-peer

Peer-to-peer support continued post-intervention in all study hospitals. At baseline, the existing non-professional staff (casuals and patient attendants) seemed to largely perform hygiene and sanitation roles (cleaning of floors, walls and surfaces). Following their introduction, ward assistants (WAs) took on more supportive roles in neonatal units. With orientation (Table 3), they helped mothers during feeds by demonstrating baby positioning, preparing formula for those with lactation challenges and guiding new mothers in locating their babies. They also provided clean gowns, directed handwashing, reminded mothers of feeding times, and occasionally changed diapers, fed abandoned babies or those whose mothers were recovering or facing difficulties.

Our findings from phase 3 also noted a general increase in ward cleanliness and infection prevention and control measures due to the ward assistants' roles across all the study hospitals. The mothers appreciated ward cleanliness and obtaining clean feeding cups without delays.

Intervention effect on communication and relationships between nurses and caregivers

Our data did not suggest notable changes in communication between nurses and mothers in response to the intervention. Mothers continued obtaining information from nursing students or existing non-professional staff rather than qualified nurses. Issues covered and sources of information remained similar across sites. Areas or topics covered included feeding, visiting hours, cleaning the baby, infection prevention control measures, machines, and baby health and treatment (Table 4).

However, the ward assistant's intervention improved the general hygiene of the ward environment and the support they provided to mothers, which was not pre-specified.

Discussion

In this study, we have examined and identified key issues for mothers in their neonatal unit experience. These include, concerns surrounding tasks expected of them such as feeding, delays in care, gaps in orientation, inadequate support, and disrespectful communication. We also explored the intervention effect, and our results show that the intervention used had little or no effect on the intended intervention's desired changes for improved support, communication and better nurse-mother relationships.

Our study shows that mothers in the study hospitals actively took on bedside care, performing tasks such as feeding and bathing the baby, observations aligning with our previous findings (Oluoch et al., 2023). The nurses provided some support, though mothers reported challenges, especially of delayed responses or minimal support, particularly when staff were busy or when there were fewer staff on duty. Mothers also recognised the interpersonal differences where some nurses were considered unfriendly, thereby avoided. As a coping strategy, we noted reliance on peer-to-peer support, and in round three, ward assistants became another crucial alternate source of support for the mothers, aligning with findings from previous studies (Al Maghaireh et al., 2016; Fernández-Medina et al., 2021; Maluni et al., 2025; Oluoch et al., 2023).

Factors hindering effective communication are an area that has been widely explored (Kwame and Petrucka, 2020). These include work environment characterised by high workloads, lack of time, lack of support for staff, staff conflict, lack of privacy or lack of referral pathways, fear and anxiety of making the patient more distressed, lack of skills or strategies to cope with difficult reactions or emotions-grief counselling remains a challenge (Gheshlagh et al., 2024; Kwame and Petrucka, 2020). The above difficulties leading to gaps in communication could also potentially contribute to the exclusion of mothers during intrusive procedures. Our findings highlighted the few instances where some participants reported being excluded from certain procedures, such as blood transfusion and medication.

The quantitative arm of this study (Imam et al., 2025) and our earlier qualitative work (Oluoch et al., 2023) also noted minimal nurses' engagement in bedside tasks such as cleaning, cord care, top-tailing, and NGT or cup feeding. The majority of the bedside care tasks are shifted to unsupervised students and mothers, often with minimal supervision or support, contributing to maternal stress as our findings highlight. Our findings also highlighted mothers' concerns about undertaking these tasks without support, as well as concerns about being scolded for not doing what was expected of them. As shown by our findings, mothers are also concerned about delegation of tasks to unsupervised students, questioning their competency levels.

Nurse burnout and stress attributed to staff shortages and stressful working environments are widely documented in the literature, including qualitative studies conducted in Kenya on nurses' experiences of newborn care (Nyikuri et al., 2019; Nzinga et al., 2019). A qualitative study by McKnight and colleagues reported on the coping strategies devised by nurses who offer newborn care in Kenya in challenging resource-constrained environments (McKnight et al., 2020). This and our previous work in Kenyan newborn units have shown that high workloads result in task prioritisation, with care for critical babies taking precedence (Nzinga et al., 2019; Oluoch et al., 2023). The downside to this approach to care is that little attention is paid to software elements of care, such as communication and psychosocial support, which can be detrimental to the mother's experience and well-being. Communication between nurses and mothers is critical to neonatal care. Communication entails verbal and nonverbal communication, incorporating gestures and body language (Abramson and Mancini, 2020). Communication is one of the critical tenets outlined in Family Centred Care (FCC) (Enke et al., 2017), which advocates for timely and effective communication and information provision to parents in neonatal units. Effective communication includes active listening, respecting beliefs and culture, and providing appropriate discussion time (Bramhall, 2014). In this study, we observed, and mothers also reported about good supportive nurses who, despite the challenging work environments, continued to offer supportive and respectful care,

In this study, we found that nurses communicated rapidly to mothers during orientation and in the ward, focusing on instructions on what was expected of them, the do's and don'ts and their expected roles while in the neonatal unit. However, these approaches made mothers concerned about communication gaps, leading to a lack of clarity among the mothers, especially regarding what and how to do things and the reasons behind what was expected of them. Our findings align with conclusions reported in other studies, which noted that these communication gaps contribute to mother's frustrations, stress, and dissatisfaction and threaten high-quality care (Enke et al., 2017; Guttmann et al., 2024; Labrie et al., 2021).

In addition to three extra nurses, the HIGH-Q intervention added three ward assistants with the intention that this would allow nurses to spend their time on technical tasks and offload the more non-technical tasks. The aim was to give nurses more opportunities for effective communication with mothers. However, the patterning and normalised routines of work, which are very ingrained, limited the gains in achieving the intended outcomes of improved communication with mothers and support to mothers. The observed engrained type of communication in the study hospitals is task-oriented (Kwame and Petrucka, 2021), which is described in the literature as being centrally biomedically-focused, nurses pay more attention to the clinical tasks, paperwork and treatment, paying less attention to patient's needs. Kwame and Petrucka propose the adoption of two other types of communication: i) Process-centred communication where “care providers make an effort to know patients and their caregivers as they perform care routines allowing patients to ask questions”, and ii) Person-centred communication “where the patients and caregivers are treated as unique persons with specific care needs and are seen as collaborators in the care process” (Kwame and Petrucka, 2021). However, in understaffed wards with deeply engrained norms, achievement or expected gains of these two communication models may be problematic.

While not unique to our study, we also reported on disrespectful communication, as evidenced by reports of rudeness and mothers being scolded. Our findings show that these responses led to some mothers refraining from asking questions, which could be detrimental to their well-being and the baby's outcomes. These findings are not unique, as disrespect has been reported elsewhere (Abuya et al., 2015; Horwood et al., 2019; Lavender et al., 2021). One of the factors that has been attributed to poor nurse-mother communication and poor relationships is burnout, which is linked to busy healthcare environments such as neonatal units, where staff work under stressful conditions and pressures (Afulani et al., 2021; Musitia et al., 2022; Rochefort and Clarke, 2010; Shin et al., 2018). As Dall'Ora et al. noted in their literature review that examined the relationships between burnout and outcome variables, burnout contributes to job dissatisfaction, emotional exhaustion, and depersonalization (Dall'Ora et al., 2020).

These observations align with the findings from the quantitative arm of the broader HIGH Q study, whose findings noted minimal intervention effects. The domains tracked by the qualitative study are highly dependent on staffing levels. The quantitative data showed a slight increase in nurse time per baby, with the total time remaining very low, even after the intervention: An increase in the median nursing hours per patient per 12-hour shift across all four intervention neonatal units from 0.56 h (33.6 min) before the intervention to 0.72 h (43.2 min) after the intervention (Imam et al., 2025). Staff movement and nurses taking their overdue leave days across the hospitals also impacted the available nursing numbers across the study hospitals. However, the minimal additional time on a typical shift that resulted was likely insufficient to be felt by nurses in such conditions. As such, the intervention did not result in a substantive change in working conditions for nurses.

Drawing on these results, we argue that while sufficient skilled staffing is important, other factors come into play that could affect the attainment of optimal outcomes of interventions aimed at strengthening the support nurses offer mothers in neonatal units in complex health settings such as Kenya. Existing data highlights other factors that came into play, such as frequent staff movements and contextual factors and relationships among staff that impacted the intervention fidelity (Odinga et al., 2025). The quantitative data from this work also points to the importance of intervention fidelity to the success of intended intervention outcomes. The nursing time did not substantially increase throughout the study, suggesting that a higher intensity of staffing numbers would have achieved better-intended outcomes (Imam et al., 2025). All these confounding factors could explain the minimal changes observed in the mothers' experiences data. In addition to increasing staffing numbers, staff experiences and motivation through targeted interventions such as communication and emotional well-being have been shown to lead to individual-level attitude change (Musitia et al., 2022), which could ultimately positively impact patients.

Practice implications

Enhancing the experiences of mothers in newborn units is critical for the recovery and well-being of both mother and neonate. Positive maternal experiences also promote the uptake of recommended interventions and support continuity of care. Our findings underscore how chronic understaffing in newborn units constrains nurses' capacity to provide effective, respectful, and detailed communication. High workloads compel nurses to prioritise clinical tasks, often at the expense of meaningful engagement with mothers. As a result, communication during orientation and routine interactions tends to be rapid and directive, focusing primarily on instructions, rules, and expected maternal roles within the unit.

This task-oriented approach contributes to significant communication gaps. Mothers frequently reported feeling uncertain about procedures, expectations, and the rationale behind care practices. Such ambiguity can undermine maternal confidence and hinder active participation in care. To improve the quality of care in newborn units, it is essential to address staffing shortages and implement strategies that support nurses in delivering comprehensive, empathetic care. Strengthening nurse–mother interactions should be prioritised as a core component of neonatal care.

Conclusion

While a staffing intervention significantly enhances nurses' experiences (Odinga et al., 2025), which can eventually positively impact mothers' experiences, there are broader factors such as interpersonal characteristics, norms of care, and gaps in nurses' skills, especially in communication, as well as lack of support structures, limits their ability to communicate better and offer more supportive and respectful care. Further research should explore specific strategies that promote staff well-being and capacities, ensuring the sustainability of intervention benefits in the long term.

Study strengths and limitations

The ethnographic approach enabled rich contextual data collection through non-participant observations, informal conversations, and in-depth interviews, allowing for triangulation of findings. This study, conducted across diverse hospitals, offers a nuanced understanding of how ward contexts shape mothers' care experiences. While observations are inherently subjective, weekly debrief sessions were held to enhance rigour. Different mothers were interviewed and observed across the study phases. Although findings are not generalisable due to the limited number of hospitals, key insights may be transferable to similar settings.
