People attitudes toward shrine-based faith healing in Sindh, Pakistan
Authors: Javed Memon, Ameer Ali Abro, Sajid Soofi, Imtiaz Hussain, Feroz Sadruddin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFaith healing at Sufi shrines holds enduring cultural and spiritual significance in Pakistan. Despite advancements in biomedical healthcare, many individuals, particularly in rural and underserved areas, continue to seek healing through shrine-based practices. This persistence reflects a complex integration of spirituality with health-seeking behavior, influenced by cultural beliefs, socioeconomic constraints, and limited access to formal healthcare services. This study investigates the socio-demographic and economic factors influencing visitation to the Makhdoom Sakhi Syedi Mossani shrine, explores the motivations behind faith healing, and examines the ceremonial and symbolic practices employed in the healing process.MethodsWe used an exploratory qualitative design based on grounded theory principles. We conducted in-depth interviews (IDIs), focus group discussions (FGDs), and participant observation with purposively selected shrine visitors. The FGDs and IDIs were conducted in the local language, Urdu, using a semi-structured interview guide and were audio-recorded with the participants' consent. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved and was subsequently analyzed using NVivo software version 14. Data analysis was conducted using a consensual approach to identify themes and sub-themes.ResultsTen IDIs and Four FGDs with a diverse group of participants, including native residents, visitors, devotees, caretakers, religious leaders, and key informants. This diversity allowed us to capture a well-rounded understanding of the phenomenon under investigation.Faith healing practices were found to be shaped by an interplay of factors, including low educational attainment, economic hardship, cultural belief systems, and distrust or inaccessibility of formal medical care. Participants frequently employed mixed treatment approaches, combining biomedical and spiritual practices.ConclusionShrine-based faith healing in rural Pakistan reflects a culturally embedded, pragmatic response to economic hardship, healthcare inaccessibility, and spiritual belief systems. Rather than opposing biomedicine, such practices represent adaptive strategies where spiritual and medical approaches coexist. These findings highlight the need for culturally responsive healthcare interventions that integrate spiritual worldviews with improved access to formal services. The study underscores the need for culturally responsive health interventions that respect traditional belief systems while promoting accessible and effective medical care, particularly for marginalized populations.Keywords: Faith Healing, Shrine, Sindh, Ritual Practices

Background

Faith healing through the visitation of shrines (mazars and ziarats) holds profound cultural and spiritual resonance in Pakistan [1]. These shrines, often dedicated to revered Sufi saints, serve as spaces of solace and spiritual refuge, where individuals seek divine intervention for a range of health, psychological, and social challenges [2]. This phenomenon is particularly prevalent in rural and economically disadvantaged regions, where access to formal healthcare services remains limited [3]. In such contexts, many individuals believe in the healing power of saints'blessings which are thought to alleviate illness, offer spiritual relief, and resolve life difficulties [2].

A report from the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2013 showed that about 80% of the ailing people in developing countries, including Pakistan, use traditional methods for seeking primary healthcare (PHC) needs [4]. Despite notable advancements in biomedical healthcare, a substantial segment of the Pakistani population continues to engage with shrine-based healing,either as a complementary measure or, in some cases, as a substitute for conventional medical treatment. This tendency is particularly pronounced when modern healthcare is perceived as unaffordable, inaccessible or ineffective [3, 4].

Faith healing encompasses spiritual practices such as prayer, shrine visitation, and reliance on divine intervention rather than conventional medicine [5, 6]. Special significance is attributed to'Mazars'and'Ziarats'connected to holy saints. Visitors seek solutions for a variety of personal and medical issues, including infertility, illness, emotional well-being and economic hardships [7, 8].

Sufi shrines function not only as spiritual centers but also as sites of communal care, offering meals, healing rituals, and moral support [9]. However, most studies to date focus on urban or semi-urban shrines, leaving rural shrine settings underexplored. There is limited documentation on the therapeutic rationales behind specific rituals or on how patients navigate between spiritual and biomedical care modalities [10, 11].

To address these gaps, this study examines faith healing practices at the rural shrine of Makhdoom Sakhi Syedi Mossani in northern Sindh. By using grounded theory and Kleinman’s explanatory model [12, 13], the research explores how illness narratives and treatment logics are constructed and negotiated. The structural-functionalist perspective also informs the analysis, highlighting how shrines serve as informal healthcare alternatives within broader systems of social inequality [14].

Research objectives

The objectives of this study are: (1) to examine the socio-demographic and economic characteristics of shrine visitors and how these relate to healing behaviors; (2) to explore the underlying motivations for visiting the shrine, including psychological, spiritual, and social needs; and (3) to document the specific healing rituals practiced and their perceived efficacy.

Methods

Study design

This was a qualitative exploratory research design to explore attitudes towards faith healing among visitors at Makhdoom Sakhi Syedi Mossani’s shrine located in rural of Sindh province. This study utilized grounded theory as its primary methodology allowing themes to naturally emerge from the data. It employed a hybrid framework that integrates both inductive and deductive elements to analyze and interpret the collected data, focusing on in-depth exploration and understanding of the phenomena.

Data collection

This study used in-depth interviews (IDIs), focus group discussions (FGDs), and participant observation to look at attitudes toward faith healing at Makhdoom Sakhi Syedi Mossani’s shrine. Each method was selected for its special role in answering the research questions. IDIs allowed the gathering of detailed, personal stories about what drives participants and their experiences, which is a key advantage of qualitative research [15].

FGDs were very instrumental in aiding to have interactive discussions and gain an understanding of group dynamics and shared beliefs, which helps in the interpretation of cultural practices ([16]. Participant observation would contextualize rituals and behaviors at the shrine, which reveals things not otherwise uncovered in interviews [17].

Study population

The target population for this study includes participants of diverse ages and backgrounds, encompassing native people, visitors, devotees, caretakers, religious leaders, and key informants. This varied group was chosen to capture a comprehensive view of the phenomenon under investigation. Different participants were selected for IDIs and FGDs to ensure a broader range of individual and group perspectives.

Study period and locale

Data collection for this study conducted from March 2018 to April 2018 at the shrine of Makdoom Sakhi Syedi Mossani. The shrine is situated in ‘Nao Goth’ (New village) Taluka Mehar, District Dadu in the province of Sindh, Pakistan.

Sample size and sampling technique

In qualitative research, the sample size is determined by reaching saturation, which occurs when no new information emerges from the data. For this study, data collection continued until saturation was achieved. This point was reached during the 8th in-depth interview and the 3rd focus group discussion. However, to ensure thoroughness, a total of four FGDs and ten IDIs were ultimately conducted [18–20].

A purposive sampling approach was used to ensure participants of different socio-demographic backgrounds who regularly visited the shrine for healing purposes were considered in the study [19].

Data collection tool

Data was collected using semi-structured interview guides adopted from pervious studies done related to research objectives [1, 10]. These tools were also pilot tested to evaluate the effectiveness of interview guides for In-Depth Interviews (IDIs) and Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) in gathering data from various participants. The interview guides featured a set of open-ended questions, supplemented by probes, with major themes including attitudes towards faith healing, purposes of visits, belief system, and performance of rituals.

Interview guide was developed in English to ensure the data quality and then translated into Sindhi (the local language of the study area) by professionals to enhance comprehension among the study population. Subsequently, the materials were retranslated back to English language to evaluate its consistency.

Data collection process

Observation at the shrine indicated a balanced presence of both male and female visitors. Consequently, an effort was made to maintain gender balance in the sample selection. While In-Depth Interviews (IDIs) were successfully conducted, but Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) with female participants could not be held due to the unavailability of a female moderator and the lack of consent from the female participants. The interview locations were mutually agreed upon by participants and researchers to ensure the interview process did not disrupt other visitors and to provide a comfortable environment for participants to express their views. Additionally, care was taken to choose interview spots where outside interference or interruption was minimized.

Before each interview began, participants were informed about the study’s objectives and reassured that they could withdraw at any time if they felt uncomfortable or annoyed.

The interviews were conducted in the native language to accurately capture participant’s expressions and statements. Focus group discussions (FGDs) lasted between 60 to 80 minutes, and in-depth interviews (IDIs) lasted between 30 to 50 minutes. Both were facilitated by a moderator and a note-taker who documented detailed notes and observations. With participant’s consent, all sessions were voice recorded. These recordings were transcribed within 7–10 days. Note-taking and transcriptions were reviewed and cross-verified against the original audio recordings by the researcher for accuracy. Subsequently, the documents were translated into English.

Validity and reliability

To improve the study's reliability and validity, several approaches were utilized to ensure accuracy and credibility. Triangulation was employed through the utilization of diverse data sources and participant groups, whereas member checking permitted participants to check the findings for accuracy. Moreover, peer review ensured an external validation of methodology and findings in favor of objectivity and credibility. Supplementing these, the MPhil student used a quality assurance (QA) checklist to systematically screen transcripts of in-depth interviews (IDIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs), field notes, study design, and contextual information. This was done using the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ), a 32-item checklist that encourages transparency, methodological rigor, and thorough reporting in qualitative research. By incorporating COREQ guidelines, the researcher ensured proper compliance with best practices and improved the overall credibility of the research [21].

Data analysis

The qualitative data analysis was guided by the grounded theory principles, using both inductive and deductive methods to capture the richness and depth of participants'experiences. The interviews and focus group discussions were translated, transcribed, and then carefully read and re-read to allow for the development of initial codes. Transcripts were coded in NVivo (v14) with a two-stage process: initial directed content analysis (DCA) based on a pre-defined coding framework [22], and then inductive open coding to identify emerging themes. The coding framework, based on interview domains and initial data familiarization, was progressively refined during the process. For reliability, two researchers coded a subset of transcripts independently and resolved any discrepancies by consensus. Remaining transcripts were also coded by a primary coder and secondary coder. Analytic memos and summary notes were used to record important insights. Final themes were synthesized through NVivo queries and MS Excel matrices, findings being organized according to research questions and supplemented by illustrative quotes Constant comparison was kept between themes and the study's research aims. Additionally, an MPhil researcher drew up summaries and interpretations to inform synthesis and facilitate data reduction, improving the rigor, transparency, and credibility of the analysis [23]. Making sure findings remained data-driven while informing a cohesive understanding of attitudes and practices surrounding faith healing at the shrine.

Ethical approval

This survey was conducted as part of a student thesis. Ethical approval and clearance for the research proposal and taking verbal consent from participants were obtained from the University of Sindh’s Ethical Review Board prior to commencing data collection, ensuring the well-being of participants and the integrity of the data. All physical copies of interviews and audio recordings were securely stored under lock. Respondents were thoroughly explained about the study’s objectives of the study. Participant identities were protected, and data was anonymized to maintain confidentiality. Participants were also informed about the study's purpose and procedures and provided their voluntarily informed verbal consent. Acknowledging cultural sensitivities and the potential discomfort associated with formal documentation due to low literacy levels among some participants, verbal consent was obtained instead of written consent.

Results

The study revealed varied age ranges among participants with the average age being approximately 35 years for in-depth interviews (IDIs) and 37 years for focus group discussions (FGDs). IDIs participants ranged from 19 to 50 years, showcasing a broad age diversity, while FGDs participants were aged between 25 to 55 years. Such diversity is crucial for understanding the range of experiences and attitudes towards faith healing.

Educational background

A significant proportion of participants in both FGDs (43%) and IDIs (40%) had no formal education, highlighting a trend towards lower education levels within the study group. Only 20% of IDI participants had attained higher education levels, which was less compared to those in FGDs.

Employment status and household dynamics

Employment status varied, with 21% of FGD participants being jobless and 50% identified as housewives. The IDIs showed similar trends, with 20% jobless and 50% as housewives. This significant representation of homemakers suggests that domestic responsibilities might influence healthcare choices.

Rural and urban distribution

Both sets of data exhibited a strong rural presence, with 64% of FGD participants and 70% of IDI participants coming from rural areas. The FGDs included only male participants, while IDIs maintained a balanced gender representation of 50% male and 50% female.

Sindhi ethnicity was predominant in both groups, accounting for 50% of participants. Other ethnicities such as Mohajir, Siraiki, Punjabi, and Balochi were present in varying proportions. Economic conditions revealed that 39% of FGD participants and 60% of IDI participants had monthly incomes below 10,000 PKR, indicating significant economic constraints among the study population (Table 1).

Socioeconomic determinants of faith healing

Participants frequently cited financial hardship and inaccessible healthcare services as primary motivators for seeking healing at the shrine. Low-income individuals, particularly those from rural areas, emphasized their inability to afford diagnostic tests, medications, or transportation to hospitals.

“We come from a poor family… I cannot bear huge expenses of doctor fees and medicines… for us, bowing at this shrine and praying here is our only source of healing” (FGD No. 4)

The preference for shrine-based healing was not due to rejection of modern medicine but rather a pragmatic necessity. Health-seeking behavior was shaped more by structural barriers than ideological opposition to biomedical care.

Skepticism toward conventional medicine

Even when participants had some access to biomedical care, many expressed skepticisms about medical professionals, citing misdiagnoses, high costs, and overcrowded public facilities.

“One doctor said I had kidney failure; another said it was a liver issue… this showed me doctors don’t know. So, I came here,” (IDI No. 2)

This mistrust led participants to reframe illness in spiritual terms, shifting agency to the saint and bypassing perceived medical failures. The shrine thus served not only as a healing site but also as a refuge from institutional inadequacies.

Mixed treatment approaches: pluralism in practice

Participants commonly employed mixed healing strategies, combining shrine rituals with biomedical consultations, herbal remedies, and self-medication. Faith healing was often the starting point or fallback option, particularly when formal care was ineffective or inaccessible.

“When I’m not feeling well, I try everything… medicine, home remedies… sometimes the shrine. I don’t often go to doctors because it’s expensive and far” (FGD No. 2)

Rather than reflecting confusion, this pluralistic health behavior demonstrated an adaptive pragmatism, where shrine-based healing filled therapeutic gaps left by formal health systems. Even among more literate or urban participants, faith healing remained part of an integrated care pathway.

Diverse ailments and spiritual framing

The shrine attracted individuals suffering from a wide spectrum of conditions – ranging from non-communicable diseases (e.g., diabetes, cardiac issues) to psychosomatic symptoms and culturally bound syndromes like possession and black magic.

“I have hepatitis C... My cousin advised me to come here and pray to the Sufi for recovery” (FGD No.4)

“I came here with my father to offer prayers for my diabetes” (FGD No. 1)

Participants viewed the shrine as a universal therapeutic space, capable of treating physical, emotional, and spiritual ailments. This non-compartmentalized view of illness reinforces the perception that the saint’s healing powers transcend the limitations of biomedical categorization.

Belief in miraculous healing and divine agency

A deep spiritual reverence for the saint underscored the healing process. Participants saw the saint as a divine intermediary whose blessings could cure even untreatable diseases.

“’Sain’ is a doctor from Allah… the shrine is like a hospital where he does operations in dreams” (FGD No. 2)

“Sain Makhdoom Sakhi Syedi Mossani is alive… he fulfills all our needs” (IDI No. 8)

Such beliefs were not merely symbolic, they influenced treatment timelines, health behaviors, and perceived recovery outcomes. The perceived immediacy of the saint’s intercession made shrine visits an emotionally and spiritually fulfilling experience, often absent in conventional clinical encounters.

Supernatural etiologies of illness

Several participants attributed illness to supernatural causes, including the evil eye, black magic, and ‘jinn’ possession. These beliefs framed biomedical care as ineffective against spiritual afflictions, necessitating divine intervention.

“My child cried all night for days… doctors couldn’t help… this may be due to an evil eye.” (IDI No. 10)

“Someone has done black magic on my daughter. She lies in bed all day” (FGD No. 2)

Faith healing was thus seen as the only viable pathway when medical approaches failed to align with local understandings of illness causation. Rituals like dream-based healing, ‘Dhamaal’, and symbolic offerings provided culturally resonant therapeutic responses to these perceived threats.

Institutional gaps in healthcare delivery

Participants described the lack of healthcare infrastructure in their communities as a major driver of reliance on shrine-based healing. Public hospitals were described as under-resourced, overcrowded, and unreliable.

“Our village has no health facility at all… this shrine is our preference.” (IDI No. 9)

“The shrine is like our local clinic, and it is open 24/7.” (FGD No. 1)

Shrines thus filled the void left by the state, functioning as informal yet dependable healthcare institutions. Their availability, affordability, and familiarity rendered them more trustworthy than formal services, particularly in underserved areas.

Participant narratives and adaptive pragmatism

Individual stories reflected trial-and-error journeys through various health systems before arriving at the shrine. These narratives emphasized the emotional, financial, and spiritual costs of chronic illness in marginalized contexts.

“I tried doctors, healers, home remedies – nothing worked. A friend advised me to come to this shrine, so I came.” (FGD No. 1)

“My heart issues didn’t improve even with multiple treatments. I now rely on the saint.” (IDI No. 7)

Such experiences reveal that faith healing is not a passive default but a deliberate, emotionally informed choice, grounded in both cultural belief and practical necessity.

This study highlights that faith healing at shrines like Makhdoom Sakhi Syedi Mossani is not merely a cultural tradition but a comprehensive health strategy shaped by economic deprivation, health system failure, and culturally embedded understandings of illness and recovery. The shrine operates as a spiritual clinic, offering accessible care where formal systems fall short. Recognizing these patterns is essential for developing culturally responsive and integrated health interventions that respect spiritual agency while improving biomedical service delivery.

The healing process within the Makhdoom Sakhi Syedi Mossani shrine is expressive of a comprehensive web of sacred, symbolic, and ritual practices, deeply infused in the life of the group. The patients described a multitude of ways where healing is solicited – indeed, not merely physical intervention but also involvement of spiritual, emotional, as well as group dimensions. Dream-based healing

Dream healing was among the themes that stood out most. It was common that several participants cited experiences of dreaming that they either received healing guides or went through spiritual interventions where they believed, directly from the saint, gave them instructions about healing. They made them have faith and beliefs in divine intercession. An example is described by one participant as follows:

“Sometimes, I dream that the saint visits me and performs a healing. These dreams make me feel like he’s helping me get better.” (IDI No. 5)

“Chowk Ratiyon” Tradition (Four-Night Stay)

The tradition of “Chowk Ratiyon”, where devotees stay at the shrine for four consecutive nights, is central to the healing experience. During this time, it is believed the saint appears in dreams to offer relief from illness or life problems. The practice represents both a spiritual test of devotion and an act of surrender to divine care.

Lamp and salt healing

A more physical form of healing involves the application of lamp oil to painful body parts and the ingestion of salt from the shrine. These materials are believed to carry the shrine’s spiritual energy and thus have curative powers:

“I rub the lamp oil on the places that hurt and drink a bit of salt. I believe this will cure my condition” (IDI No. 3)

Healing power of water

Water sourced from the shrine’s hand pump is imbued with sacred significance. Participants used the water for drinking, bathing, and even laundering, believing in its all-encompassing healing properties:

“I believe the water from the shrine can heal me. I drink it, bathe in it, and even wash my clothes with it” (IDI No. 7)

Despite the absence of biomedical validation, such practices persist, demonstrating a strong dependence on spiritual healing even in the face of scientific skepticism:

“Even if science says otherwise, I depend on the shrine’s spiritual help to get better.” (IDI No. 2)

“Dhamaal” for spiritual possession

“Dhamaal”, a rhythmic devotional dance, was noted as a communal ritual used for exorcising spirits or Djinns. It is seen as a spiritual remedy for non-physical ailments, reinforcing the shrine’s role in mental and spiritual health care.

“Langar's” curative significance

“Langar” (blessed food) is widely considered to carry therapeutic benefits. Participants linked the act of consuming or distributing food at the shrine with the reception of divine blessings and physical healing, emphasizing a social-spiritual dimension of health.

Additional healing rituals

Besides the central rituals, a variety of auxiliary practices were reported, including prayers, vows, gift offerings, and thread-tying. These practices indicate the pluralistic and layered nature of faith healing, reinforcing the notion that multiple rituals may be necessary or used in combination to achieve desired healing outcomes:

“At the shrine, we do more than just dream-healing. We pray, make vows, offer gifts and tie threads. We believe these rituals can heal us in many ways at the shrine of Makhdoom Sakhi.” (FGD No. 1)

Together, these themes illustrate the intricate interweaving of faith, tradition, and healing at the shrine. Healing is not limited to the physical body but is perceived as encompassing spiritual, emotional, and communal well-being. The shrine serves as a holistic healing space for thousands of visitors, particularly those from marginalized or underserved communities who seek care beyond the scope of formal medicine.

Discussion

This study offers novel insights into the motivations and contextual factors driving individuals to seek faith healing at the Makhdoom Sakhi Syedi Mossani shrine in rural Sindh, Pakistan. While previous research has explored shrine-based healing practices, this study uniquely focuses on a rural setting, highlighting how economic constraints, limited healthcare access, spiritual beliefs, and sociocultural norms intersect to influence health-seeking behaviors. Notably, it reveals that faith healing is not perceived as an alternative to biomedical care but rather as a complementary approach within a pluralistic treatment strategy. The documentation of specific rituals, such as Chowk Ratiyon (four-night stays), dream-based interventions, and the use of shrine elements like oil, salt, and water, provides a detailed ethnographic account that enriches the existing literature. Furthermore, the study underscores the role of failed or inconclusive biomedical diagnoses in reinforcing spiritual interpretations of illness, emphasizing the need for culturally competent healthcare approaches.​

Economic insecurity and access to healthcare

A dominant theme was the influence of financial hardship and inadequate local health infrastructure in shaping reliance on faith healing. Many participants were unable to afford hospital consultations or medications, and public healthcare facilities were often described as overcrowded and unreliable. These findings aligns with study conducted by Abbas et; al. (2020), who noted that economic vulnerability strongly correlates with faith-based treatment-seeking, particularly when medical costs are prohibitive [10]. The context of this study, a remote rural shrine, amplifies these findings by illustrating how geographic and infrastructural barriers limit access to biomedical care. This contrasts with studies in urban centers like Islamabad and Lahore, where proximity to healthcare does not always translate to trust or affordability [24] (Chaudhry, 2013).​

Educational background and treatment pluralism

Most participants had limited formal education, which influenced their understanding of illness and treatment. This demographic pattern aligns with research from rural Punjab [11] (Khan & Sajid, 2011), where low education levels were similarly associated with spiritual interpretations of health. Participants frequently engaged in pluralistic treatment strategies, combining home remedies, biomedical care, and shrine rituals such as salt consumption, oil rubbing, and thread knotting. This aligns with findings by Mohyuddin and Ambreen (2014) in Baluchistan, who observed similar integrative practices as a pragmatic response to fragmented health services [7]. These adaptive behaviors reflect a non-exclusive worldview, where spiritual and medical paradigms coexist rather than compete.​

Cultural explanations and supernatural etiologies

The explanation of illness as having a supernatural cause – e.g., black magic, possession by Djinn, or the evil eye – was common. Such beliefs exist not only at the study location but also in Muslim-majority cultures around the world, including Saudi Arabia [25, 26] (Alosaimi et al., 2014; Saeed, Gater, Hussain, & Mubbashar, 2000). Participants’ accounts demonstrate how culturally rooted meanings of disease and misfortune authorize spiritual recourse, especially when biomedical testing is ambiguous or contradictory. Qualitative findings from this research emphasize that supernatural accounts tend to appear following unsuccessful biomedical experience, hence being explanatory and also therapeutic frameworks. This calls for culturally attuned care that honors these worldviews while providing suitable health education.

Saint veneration and miraculous healing

A striking finding was the perception of the saint as a living spiritual healer. Participants believed the saint could conduct dream surgeries, intercede with God, and respond to personal supplications. These beliefs reflect long-standing Sufi traditions of saint mediation and barakah ([9]Charan, Wang, & Yao, 2018). The saint’s role was not limited to symbolic reassurance; he was described as an active healer capable of tangible outcomes. This illustrates the enduring relevance of spiritual charisma in South Asian health cultures, where healing is not merely physical but also emotional and social.

Conclusion

This study offers an in-depth exploration of faith healing practices at the Makhdoom Sakhi Syedi Mossani shrine in rural Pakistan, revealing how spiritual beliefs, structural healthcare access barriers, and socioeconomic constraints shape community health-seeking behaviors. The findings suggest that shrine-based healing is not a replacement for biomedical care, but rather a culturally relevant and adaptive response to systemic gaps in healthcare provision. While the study highlights the adaptive strategies of marginalized communities, it does not assess or endorse the medical efficacy of faith healing. The findings underscore the importance of developing inclusive, trust-based health systems that acknowledge and respectfully engage with local belief systems while improving healthcare access. The study was conducted with cultural sensitivity, aiming to understand and represent participants’ perspectives rather than critique their beliefs.

Recommendations

While based on a particular case scenario, the research findings of this study are potentially transferable and may apply to rural, faith-based communities. In terms of healthcare practice, training practitioners in cultural competence is critical in building trust and enhancing treatment compliance. Furthermore, the use of mobile health clinics around shrines during high-demand seasons can help fill gaps in access and provide primary healthcare services where needed most. At the community health programming level, joint health education campaigns should be planned with the participation of shrine leaders and respected community members to guarantee cultural compatibility and efficacy. Training community health workers from within shrine-attending populations can also act as a potential link between spiritual and medical care systems. From a policy perspective, public health programs could consider recognizing pluralistic health practices by testing faith-based outreach designs in pilot studies, particularly in areas of need. In addition, involving the Auqaf Department in surveillance of healing stories and the strengthening of shrine facilities, including sanitation and clean water supply, can help create safer and more supportive health settings. For research purposes in the future, longitudinal studies could evaluate perceived and real health impacts of shrine-based healing. Cross-study comparisons within shrines and across different regions would enhance comprehension of variations in practices, whereas investigation into the role of faith healing in dealing with mental health issues could influence integrated care strategies.

Limitations

This study is context-specific, focused exclusively on the Makhdoom Sakhi Syedi Mossani shrine, and therefore does not seek to generalize findings to all shrine-based healing practices. Its strength lies in providing deep, localized insights into a culturally embedded phenomenon rather than broad generalizations. While the use of triangulation and member checking enhanced the credibility of the data, participant narratives may still be influenced by social desirability bias or selective recall, which are limitations inherent in self-reported qualitative data. Additionally, the study does not evaluate the clinical efficacy of shrine-based healing but rather explores its sociocultural and structural underpinnings. Future research could incorporate mixed-methods approaches, including biomedical assessments, to further examine health outcomes.
