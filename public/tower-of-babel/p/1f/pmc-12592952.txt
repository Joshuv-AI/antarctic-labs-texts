Author Response: A Neurological Outcome Score for Ventilated PICU Patients Should Only Include Neurological, Objective Outcome Parameters
Authors: Prateek K Panda, Indar K Sharawat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHow to cite this article: Panda PK, Sharawat IK. Author Response: A Neurological Outcome Score for Ventilated PICU Patients Should Only Include Neurological, Objective Outcome Parameters. Indian J Crit Care Med 2025;29(10):878–879.Keywords: Neurocritical care, Neurodisability, Neurological outcomes, PCPC score

Dear Editor,

We thank the authors for their interest in our work and for the opportunity to clarify. In our prospective cohort, NOPS-VC showed excellent discrimination for poor outcome (PCPC ≥ 4 or death) at admission and at 24 hours, with good calibration and an optimal cut-off of 18 points.1

The authors have suggested that two of the eight parameters—cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and oxygenation—are not related to neurological outcome and therefore should not be included in the score. We respectfully disagree. A substantial proportion of children who undergo CPR in the PICU develop poor neurological outcomes, and it is well established that even in-hospital cardiac arrest survivors frequently suffer neurological sequelae.2 Likewise, hypoxic brain injury remains one of the leading causes of long-term neurological dysfunction. Importantly, not only severe global hypoxia but also chronic or intermittent hypoxemia—as encountered in children with cyanotic congenital heart disease or following cardiac surgery—can result in significant brain injury.3 Furthermore, poor neurological outcomes often occur in mechanically ventilated patients without any primary neurological disorder, underscoring the systemic impact of hypoxia and resuscitation events on the developing brain. Reflecting this understanding, the most recent Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) guidelines explicitly include secondary brain dysfunction as a major diagnostic category. For these reasons, we maintain that CPR and oxygenation are indeed critical predictors of neurological outcome and their inclusion in NOPS-VC is both evidence-based and clinically relevant.

Secondly, NOPS-VC is a prediction tool; therefore, it uses predictors measured early (risk factors), not downstream diagnostic tests. Both CPR and oxygenation indices are strongly linked to brain injury and subsequent neurological outcome in critically ill children. Pediatric resuscitation studies consistently report neurologic outcome by PCPC after CPR, with hypoxic-ischemic injury being the major determinant.3 Similarly, hypoxemia and lung–brain interactions (ARDS/PARDS) are mechanistically and clinically associated with delirium, cognitive impairment, and poor neurodevelopmental outcomes; validated oxygenation indices (OI/OSI, SpO2) are standard severity markers and correlate with neurological sequelae.4 Our a priori item selection explicitly drew on this evidence to maximize timely, bedside feasibility within 6 hours of ventilation, which is why resource-intensive tests (EEG/CSF/MRI) were not mandated for score computation.

As outlined in our study methodology, the NOPS-VC was deliberately designed to focus on parameters that can be measured immediately within the first 24 hours, even in resource-constrained settings. Procedures such as CSF examination are not feasible in many cases of acutely elevated intracranial pressure, particularly in the presence of brain lesions such as abscesses. Likewise, MRI is often impractical in critically ill children on multiple vasoactive agents, since shifting such unstable patients carries significant risk. In addition, continuous EEG monitoring is not universally available, especially in many PICUs outside high-resource centers, and critical illness neuropathy generally develops later in the PICU course rather than within the first hours of mechanical ventilation.5

While we acknowledge that EEG, MRI, CSF findings, and critical illness neuropathy are indeed important predictors of neurological outcome, including them in our score would have resulted in substantial missing data, made the score more resource-intensive, and reduced its generalizability. Our objective was to establish a pragmatic tool that could be applied globally, including in low- and middle-income countries, without requiring sophisticated investigations. We encourage future researchers to adapt, refine, and validate the NOPS-VC in diverse geographical and resource settings.

It is also important to emphasize that our score was developed specifically for mechanically ventilated children, many of whom are admitted primarily for gastrointestinal, cardiac, or respiratory conditions, but who nonetheless may develop poor neurological outcomes after prolonged PICU stay. In such children, routine performance of EEG, MRI, or CSF studies within the first 24–48 hours of ventilation is neither feasible nor practical.

Routine lumbar puncture (LP) in all ventilated PICU patients is neither feasible nor universally safe; guidelines advise deferring LP when there are contraindications (raised ICP, shock, coagulopathy, unstable airway) and emphasize that treatment should not be delayed awaiting LP or imaging.6 In our study, CNS infection during PICU stay was defined using clinical features supported by imaging and laboratory data when available; LP was performed when clinically indicated and safe, consistent with best practice. As a prediction score, NOPS-VC does not require etiologic confirmation in every case to function accurately. In the Indian context, the standard definition of acute encephalitic syndrome (AES), as adopted by the National Vector Borne Disease Control Programme, does not mandate CSF findings for case identification. Consistent with this widely accepted approach, our study employed standard clinical definitions to determine the presence of neuroinfection.

The authors noted that clinical assessment of raised ICP can be misleading in sedated, ventilated patients. We agree that isolated signs (e.g., Cushing's triad) can be late or blunted by sedation. That is why our definition of raised ICP used a composite of bedside signs (Cushing response, pupil asymmetry/dilation, abnormal posturing) with imaging corroboration when feasible, and inter-rater agreement was assessed. Elevated ICP remains a robust predictor of poor outcome across pediatric neurocritical conditions.6 Importantly, the aim was early risk prediction in diverse PICU settings, including those without invasive ICP monitoring. Universal invasive ICP monitoring using intraparenchymal or ventricular catheters is neither practically feasible nor clinically indicated in all mechanically ventilated children. The risks of infection, hemorrhage, and technical complications make routine use unjustifiable, particularly in patients without primary neurological pathology. Consequently, most intensivists and pediatric neurologists rely on established clinical and radiological signs—such as Cushing's triad, pupillary asymmetry, abnormal posturing, or neuroimaging findings—to assess the presence of raised ICP in ventilated patients.

The authors have opined that every patient should undergo CNS imaging (preferably MRI). Universal neuroimaging of all ventilated PICU patients is not logistically feasible: MRI requires transport and sedation with non-trivial risk in unstable children. Evidence shows risks during intrahospital transport of critically ill patients and the limited benefit of routine scans in low-yield scenarios.

The authors also suggested that it is unjustified to assign a poor neurological outcome (PCPC > 3) to patients who died. We acknowledge that a proportion of deaths in our cohort were attributable to refractory septic shock, severe ARDS, or other systemic causes rather than a primary neurological disorder. However, it is important to recognize that survivors of such prolonged critical illness frequently sustain secondary brain dysfunction due to hypoxia, systemic inflammation, and CPR, leading to poor long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes. Excluding these patients from the analysis would have introduced selection bias and reduced the generalizability of the score. Conversely, classifying them as having “good neurological outcome” merely because the immediate cause of death was non-neurological would have been misleading, as secondary neurological injury is a well-documented sequela of prolonged multi-organ failure and cardiorespiratory compromise. For these reasons, we followed the widely adopted convention of considering death as part of the poor neurological outcome category within the PCPC framework.7

By definition, the PCPC score includes death/brain death as category 6. Numerous pediatric critical care and resuscitation studies classify death within the “poor neurological outcome” composite, while “favorable outcome” is typically PCPC 1–2. Our analysis followed this convention (death or PCPC 4–5 = poor outcome).

We fully agree that external, multicenter validation by independent centers represents the gold standard for any prognostic tool. In this initial prospective study, we pre-specified blinded outcome assessments, performed inter-rater agreement checks to reduce bias, and undertook internal validation at our center prior to pursuing external validation.
