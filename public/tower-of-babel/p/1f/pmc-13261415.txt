Metal–Carbon Synergy in C60@Ru Nanocomposite for High-Performance Hydrazine Fuel
Authors: Xiaogang Mu, Junda Wang, Shenghui Wang, Yue Zhang, Jingjing Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exploring metal–carbon synergies in propellants, we developed Ru-embedded fullerene (C60@Ru) additives for hydrazine fuel (HF) enhancement. Hydrothermal synthesis yielded well-defined C60@Ru nanoparticles, which were extensively characterized using electron microscopy for morphology, XRD for crystal structure, and spectroscopic methods for chemical analysis. Performance evaluations demonstrated a density increase of 0.0377 to 0.0419 g/cm3 and specific combustion energy improvement of 14.2% to 20.1% for the C60@Ru/HF composite, with completely preserved low-temperature rheological properties. DFT computations further elucidated the Ru-mediated charge transfer mechanisms driving combustion enhancement. These findings demonstrate the potential of C60@Ru as an efficient performance modifier for HF, offering new insights into C60-based energetic materials.

Introduction

Liquid propellants serve as the foundation of spacecraft propulsion systems due to their high specific impulse, adjustable thrust, restart capability, and pulsed operation. They represent the most extensively utilized propellants worldwide, dominating both primary space transportation systems and auxiliary propulsion applications. − Hydrazine-based fuels, as the predominant choice for global spacecraft and satellites, demonstrate superior energy density and specific impulse compared to alternative propellants. − With ongoing advancements in spacecraft technology demanding greater speed, extended range, and heavier payload capacity, the need for higher-performance liquid propellants has intensified accordingly. −

Current efforts to develop high-energy-density fuels follow two main approaches: chemical synthesis of new fuel types versus incorporation of high-energy-density additives into existing liquid propellants. The first approach presents significant challenges in rapid fuel development and practical implementation. Consequently, researchers predominantly focus on the second method. Since the late 20th century, institutions such as NASA research centers have investigated metal particle additives for enhancing fuel energy. − For instance, aluminum powder has been mixed with RP-1 fuel in various mass fractions, but its coarse particle size limited the improvements in combustion efficiency and energy enhancement. −

The advancement of nanotechnology in the 21st century has substantially increased the relative surface area of metal particles, thereby improving oxygen contact and reaction efficiency. − Studies by Mueller et al. demonstrated that nanosized metal particles can enhance energy release during combustion. Concurrently, the increasing demand for functional materials has spurred the development of cluster “super atoms”, representing a novel direction in materials science. − These clusters, characterized by their minute size and extensive surface area, have been widely applied in energy storage and catalysis. − Fullerene clusters, for instance, feature a unique cage-like atomic arrangement that imparts high strain and binding energies. During combustion, the breakdown of this structure converts these energies into kinetic energy. − Furthermore, hybrid structures combining fullerene clusters with zero-dimensional nanomaterials display outstanding combustion and catalytic properties, contributing to both high calorific value and catalytic effects. These characteristics make them particularly valuable for energetic materials. Nevertheless, the application of metal-loaded fullerene clusters as novel functional additives in liquid propellants has rarely been documented.

This study employs hydrothermal synthesis to successfully prepare C60-supported Ru nanocomposite (C60@Ru) by loading metallic ruthenium (Ru) onto fullerene clusters. The obtained C60@Ru was then physically blended with hydrazine fuel (HF) to formulate a new cluster liquid propellant. Systematic investigations were conducted to evaluate the influence of C60@Ru on key fuel properties including density (ρ), freezing point (FP), viscosity (μ), and combustion performance (Q) of HF. The findings provide valuable theoretical insights and technical support for developing advanced high-performance liquid cluster propellants.

Experimental Section

Materials

Unsymmetrical dimethylhydrazine (UDMH): Purity ≥98% (Liming Chemical Research Institute); Hydyne: Prepared in laboratory by mixing 40 wt % diethylenetriamine and 60 wt % UDMH, followed by magnetic stirring at 300 rpm for 30 min under ice bath condition (0 ± 2 °C). Both diethylenetriamine and UDMH are high-purity reagents, which excludes the effect of raw material purity on the final fuel performance; C60: Purity ≥99% (Henan Fullerene Nano New Materials Technology Co., Ltd.); Anhydrous ethanol: AR grade (Tianjin Fuyu Fine Chemical Co., Ltd.); Ruthenium trichloride (RuCl3): AR grade (Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technologies Co., Ltd.); Lecithin: Purity ≥98% (Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.).

Synthesis and Characterization

Preparation of C60@Ru

Figure depicts the structural schematic of the as-prepared sample. Initially, 10 mg of C60 was ultrasonically dispersed in 15 mL of ethanol at 300 W power, 50% amplitude, and 25 ± 2 °C to prepare a uniform suspension, which achieves the optimal dispersion of C60 without structural damage. Subsequently, 10 mg/mL of RuCl3 aqueous solution was added to the C60 suspension and thoroughly mixed. The hydrothermal parameters including temperature (160/180/200 °C), reaction time (4/6/8 h), and precursor concentrations (C60:0.67 mg/mL, RuCl3: 10 mg/mL) were systematically optimized via preliminary experiments. 180 °C and 6 h were determined as the optimal conditions, because low temperature or short duration led to incomplete reduction of Ru3+, while excessively high temperature or long duration caused serious agglomeration of Ru nanoparticles and structural collapse of C60. The resulting mixture was transferred to a 25 mL reactor and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C for 6 h in a forced-draft drying oven. After cooling, the solution was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 30 min. Centrifugation was adopted instead of filtration because the as-prepared C60@Ru nanoparticles are 2–5 nm, which can easily pass through filter paper (pore size ≈ 220 nm) and result in low product recovery. In contrast, centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 30 min provides high separation efficiency, enables effective isolation of nanoscale products, and avoids agglomeration during the collection process. The obtained product was washed with deionized water and ethanol, followed by drying at 60 °C for 12 h to yield the final C60@Ru clusters.

Preparation of Ru nanoparticle-decorated C60 hybrid catalyst.

Preparation of Propellant Mixtures

Initially, an appropriate amount of C60@Ru was weighed in a fume hood. Subsequently, 20 mL aliquots of hydrazine fuels (UDMH and Hydyne) were separately measured into transparent beakers, followed by addition of lecithin as surfactant. Centrifugation was performed using a TG16-WS unit (Hunan Xiangyi) at 12,000 rpm for nanoparticle separation. Finally, the mixtures were ultrasonically treated at 250 W power, 40% amplitude, and 25 °C until complete dispersion, yielding homogeneous UDMH/C60@Ru and Hydyne/C60@Ru solutions and avoiding local overheating. The mass fraction of C60@Ru additive was controlled at 0.3 wt %, and the mass fraction of lecithin surfactant was fixed at 0.2 wt % for all fuel formulations.

Multiscale Characterization of Fullerene-Enhanced Hydrazine Fuel Composites

Microstructural Analysis

Morphological and microstructural characterization was performed using a Zeiss SIGMA field emission scanning electron microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) operating at 10 kV accelerating voltage for macroscopic feature analysis, with samples prepared through ultrasonic dispersion in anhydrous ethanol using a Shenzhen JieMeng Technology cleaner followed by deposition onto carbon-coated copper grids, while nanoscale structural details were resolved using a Talos F200X high-resolution transmission electron microscope (FEI Company, USA) operating at 200 kV acceleration voltage.

Crystallographic Characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed for phase identification using a D/MAX-3C diffractometer (Rigaku Corporation, Japan) operating with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.1541 nm) at 40 kV accelerating voltage and 40 mA tube current, with continuous scanning over the 2θ range of 5–50° at a rate of 5°/min.

Surface Chemistry Analysis

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out using an AXIS SUPRA spectrometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) equipped with an Al Kα X-ray source operated at 12 kV accelerating voltage and 6 mA emission current, with all binding energies referenced to the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV for charge correction.

Physicochemical Property Measurements

The physicochemical properties were systematically characterized: density was determined via a WMD-430 densitometer (Shanghai INESA, China), calibrated with deionized water (ρ = 0.9982 g/cm3) at 20 °C, with triplicate measurements of 5 mL samples at 20 °C; freezing point was evaluated in parallel using KD-F8076 (Changsha Kaidun) and ST-1568A (Beijing Xu Xin) cryoscopic systems, calibrated with n-octane reference material (FP = −56.8 °C), tested at −80 °C; rheological properties including kinematic viscosity were measured at 20 °C using an NXS-11A rotational rheometer at a shear rate of 100 s–1, calibrated with silicone oil reference material (2.0 × 10–2 Pa·s) for both base and modified fuels; while combustion characteristics were examined using a Parr 6200 calorimeter (Parr Instrument Company, USA), calibrated with benzoic acid (calorific value 26454 J/g), with 0.3 g samples under 3 MPa oxygen pressure to determine the heat of combustion.

Crystalline and Molecular Structure of C60-Supported Ru Nanocomposite

The structural verification of C60 and C60@Ru was conducted through XRD and Raman spectroscopy analyses (Figures and ). The XRD pattern of C60@Ru demonstrates the high phase purity of the synthesized material. A characteristic (002) diffraction peak at 10.81° confirms the interlayer spacing of C60. Additional distinct peaks are observed at 17.7° (220), 20.79° (311), 21.7° (222), and 30.9° (422), further corroborating the crystalline structure of the composite.

The XRD pattern of C60@Ru maintains all characteristic peaks of pristine C60 while showing no detectable signals from metallic Ru or its oxides. The absence of Ru diffraction peaks is a typical size effect of nanomaterials: the ultrasmall Ru nanoparticles (Figure d) are far below the critical size for sharp XRD diffraction, leading to severe peak broadening and complete disappearance. The absence of Ru diffraction peaks in the XRD pattern is attributed to the ultrasmall size and high dispersion of Ru nanoparticles on the C60 surface, which leads to severe peak broadening and signal weakening below the detection limit of XRD. This phenomenon is a typical size effect of nanomaterials. The diffraction peak of C60 at approximately 35° corresponds to its (511) crystal plane, and the disappearance of this peak in C60@Ru is caused by the slight lattice distortion of C60 due to the strong chemical interaction between Ru and C60. The two distinct diffraction peaks at around 42.5° observed in both samples are assigned to the (600) crystal plane of C60, and their unchanged presence verifies that the main crystalline structure of C60 is well preserved after Ru incorporation. This observation confirms the successful deposition of Ru nanoparticles with ultralow loading amounts and extremely small particle sizes exclusively in metallic form on the C60 surface. Corresponding Raman analysis (Figure ) reveals preserved fundamental spectral features of C60 after Ru modification, demonstrating structural integrity maintenance. Notably, the C60@Ru spectrum exhibits marginally broadened peaks with ∼1 cm–1 redshift, which directly reflects the lattice distortion and electronic interaction between C60 and Ru, proving the strong metal–carbon coupling. These minimal structural modifications likely contribute to improved catalytic activity while maintaining the overall C60 scaffold stability.

XPS peak-fitting results show that metallic Ru0 accounts for ∼89% of total Ru species, while Ru4+ (RuO2) accounts for ∼11%, which is a common surface oxidation phenomenon of nano-Ru exposed to air. Ru0 acts as the main catalytic active site for N–H bond cleavage in hydrazine fuel. Quantitative analysis of the peak areas reveals that metallic Ru0 accounts for ∼89% of the total Ru species, while RuO2 contributes only ∼11%, indicating that the majority of Ru in the C60@Ru composite remains in the metallic state, with only a thin surface oxide layer formed during sample handling. Regarding the catalytic role of different Ru species: metallic Ru0 is the primary active site for N–H bond cleavage in hydrazine fuels, as it provides abundant d-band electrons to facilitate interfacial charge transfer and weaken N–H bonds; the minor surface RuO2 species act as a cocatalyst, which can further promote the adsorption and activation of fuel molecules through oxygen-containing sites, while the C60 carrier stabilizes the Ru nanoparticles and prevents excessive oxidation. Thermophysical Property Assessment of Catalyst-Doped Fuels

The performance of propellant fuels is fundamentally governed by their physicochemical properties, with density, freezing point, viscosity, and calorific value being the most critical parameters.

Density determines both fuel storage capacity and energy densityhigher values enable more compact energy storage. The freezing point establishes operational temperature limits, where lower values ensure reliable low-temperature performance. Viscosity governs fuel flow characteristics, directly impacting engine lubrication and distribution efficiency. Calorific value quantifies the energy release potential during combustion, with higher values indicating greater energy output per unit mass.

To systematically evaluate these performance metrics, comparative analyses were performed on both base fuels and their C60@Ru-modified counterparts. The complete experimental results are summarized in Figure , demonstrating the measurable effects of C60@Ru incorporation on each key performance parameter. It reveals that incorporating the C60@Ru composite catalyst into both UDMH and Hydyne fuels enhances their density and heat of combustion, with more pronounced improvements in the latter parameter.

The baseline densities of 0.796 g/cm3 (UDMH) and 0.861 g/cm3 (Hydyne) showed minimal increases to 0.797 and 0.865 g/cm3 respectively after C60@Ru addition, indicating limited solubility of the catalyst in these fuels. To address this solubility limitation, lecithin was introduced as a surface-active agent, resulting in significantly enhanced densities of 0.8379 g/cm3 for C60@Ru/UDMH (5.26% increase) and 0.8987 g/cm3 for C60@Ru/Hydyne (4.38% increase) compared to their respective base fuels.

The addition of C60@Ru composite catalyst to hydrazine fuels resulted in significant improvements in multiple performance parameters while maintaining acceptable operational characteristics. The most notable enhancement occurred in combustion performance, where the heat of combustion was normalized to the mass of hydrazine fuel (UDMH, Hydyne) and excluded the mass contributions of C60@Ru additive and lecithin surfactant; the heat of combustion increased from 19.84 to 23.82 MJ/kg (20.07% gain) for UDMH and from 24.27 to 27.71 MJ/kg (14.19% gain) for Hydyne following surfactant-assisted formulation. The optimal mass fraction of C60@Ru in hydrazine fuels was determined as 0.3 wt % via optimization experiments at 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 wt % loadings, and the mass fraction of lecithin as surfactant was fixed at 0.2 wt % to stabilize the dispersion of C60@Ru nanoparticles. The thermal behavior showed differential freezing point adjustments, with the UDMH formulation displaying a modest 0.5 °C depression from −57.2 to −57.7 °C, while the Hydyne formulation exhibited an 8.9 °C elevation from −65.5 to −56.6 °C, keeping both well within practical operating ranges. Rheological measurements revealed controlled viscosity increases of 1.2% (2.47 to 2.50 × 10–2 Pa·s) for UDMH-based fuel and 1.7% (2.89 to 2.94 × 10–2 Pa·s) for Hydyne-based fuel, consistent with the expected effects of additive incorporation. These comprehensive results demonstrate that C60@Ru addition provides meaningful combustion performance improvements while maintaining the fundamental operational characteristics of hydrazine propellants, with only marginal changes in other critical parameters.

These comprehensive results demonstrate that C60@Ru addition provides meaningful combustion performance improvements while maintaining the fundamental operational characteristics of hydrazine propellants, with only marginal changes in other critical parameters. Notably, the slight increase in fuel density is not the dominant factor leading to the significantly improved combustion performance in this work. As a noncombustible metal, Ru does not contribute to energy release by itself, and the enhanced calorific value is not a measurement artifact. The prominent enhancement of combustion properties is mainly attributed to the metal–carbon synergy and catalytic effect of the C60@Ru nanocomposite: the C60 cage acts as an electron carrier and structural scaffold to suppress the agglomeration of Ru nanoparticles, maintaining the high dispersion and stability of the composite; metallic Ru serves as the core active site to catalyze the cleavage of N–H bonds in hydrazine fuels and promote complete fuel oxidation. These structural, catalytic and dispersion effects of the C60@Ru nanocomposite play a far more decisive role in elevating combustion efficiency and heat release than density alone. The mild increase in fuel viscosity after incorporating C60@Ru originates from the weak nanoparticle network formed by uniformly dispersed C60@Ru nanocomposites in the fuel matrix. This network not only induces the negligible viscosity elevation but also ensures sufficient contact between C60@Ru active sites and fuel molecules, which further facilitates the combustion reaction and performance improvement. Meanwhile, the extremely small viscosity increment imposes no negative influence on the practical flow and atomization behaviors of the hydrazine fuels.

Control experiments show that adding C60 or Ru nanoparticles alone improves the heat of combustion by less than 2%, while the C60@Ru composite increases it by 14.19%–20.07%. The physical mixture of C60 and Ru also shows a much weaker effect than C60@Ru. These results prove that the metal–carbon synergy of C60@Ru is the core of catalytic enhancement, rather than the simple superposition of single components.

Lecithin (0.2 wt %) acts as a surfactant, which maintains good dispersion without sedimentation/agglomeration within 15 days.

DFT Insights into the Catalytic Mechanism of C60@Ru Nanocomposites

DFT Method Establishment

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) , with the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method. , The exchange-correlation potential was treated within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional. A plane-wave cutoff energy of 450 eV was employed for the basis set. The C60 cluster was placed in a cubic vacuum box (20 × 20 × 20 Å3) to eliminate interactions between periodic images. For modeling the C60@Ru system, a Ru atom was positioned above the hexatomic ring of C60. Structural relaxations were carried out until the forces on all atoms converged below 0.01 eV/Å, using a Γ-point (1 × 1 × 1) k-mesh for Brillouin zone integration. The van der Waals interactions were accounted for by the DFT-D3 method.

The adsorption energy (ΔE ads) of UDMH on the C60@Ru cluster was calculated using the following equation ΔEads=Emolecule+surface−(Esurface−Emolecule) where E surface is the energy of the C60@Ru cluster, E molecule represents the energy of the isolated UDMH molecule, and E molecule+surface denotes the total energy of the adsorption system.

Figure displays the three-dimensional structures of C60, UDMH, and C60@Ru. UDMH consists of two functional groups: a nonpolar CH3 group and a polar NH2 group. In this work, UDMH was initially positioned near C60@Ru, followed by geometric optimization. The stable adsorption configuration with no imaginary frequencies was selected for further analysis.

Chemisorption Behaviors of Ru on C60

The adsorption energy of Ru on C60 was calculated to be −3.562 eV, where the negative value confirms the exothermic nature and stability of the adsorption process. The large magnitude of this adsorption of energy suggests a strong chemical interaction between Ru and C60. To further verify this chemisorption behavior, we analyzed the density of states (DOS) of pristine C60, Ru, and C60@Ru systems. The local density of states (LDOS) presented in Figure provides additional evidence supporting the chemical adsorption mechanism between C60 and Ru.

Local density of states (LDOS) distributions: (a) C60, (b) C60 and Ru, (c) C60@Ru.

A comparison between the density of states (DOS) of C60 and C60@Ru clearly shows significant changes near the Fermi level following Ru adsorption, confirming a strong electronic interaction characteristic of chemical adsorption rather than weak van der Waals forces. This robust bonding ensures that Ru remains firmly anchored to the C60 surface without detachment or agglomeration. As a result, the C60@Ru clusters maintain excellent dispersion stability in UDMH. The homogeneous distribution effectively prevents density variations that could arise from particle aggregation or sedimentation, thereby enabling stable enhancement of UDMH’s bulk density.

Chemisorption Mechanism of UDMH on C60@Ru

Due to the inherent symmetry of the C60@Ru molecular structure, this study focuses on two distinct adsorption configurations, as illustrated in the accompanying figure. In configuration a, the CH3 group of the UDMH molecule is positioned above the five-membered ring of C60@Ru, while in configuration b, the CH3 group aligns above the six-membered ring. This comparative analysis allows for a systematic investigation of the adsorption behavior between these two characteristic orientations.

Two types of adsorption configurations for UDMH are depicted in Figure . The adsorption energies of UDMH in both configurations were calculated as −0.227 and −0.225 eV, respectively. These relatively modest values suggest that UDMH molecules adsorb onto C60@Ru primarily through physical adsorption. The strong chemisorption between Ru and C60 only anchors Ru nanoparticles firmly on the C60 carrier to prevent agglomeration and ensure structural stability and dispersion of the catalyst, rather than directly activating fuel molecules. The weak physisorption of UDMH is favorable for the dynamic adsorption-activation-desorption process in catalytic combustion: it enables effective activation of UDMH molecules and rapid desorption from the catalyst surface for combustion, avoiding catalyst poisoning caused by strong adsorption. Further confirmation comes from density of states (DOS) analysis of UDMH, C60@Ru, and the two adsorption configurations, with the corresponding local density of states (LDOS) presented in the accompanying figures.

DFT-optimized adsorption configurations of UDMH on C60@Ru catalyst: (a) Five-membered ring adsorption; (b) Six-membered ring adsorption.

The minimal changes observed in the DOS near the Fermi level of C60 after UDMH adsorption provide additional evidence for physical adsorption. This moderate interaction strength offers optimal characteristics: while strong enough to maintain stable proximity of UDMH molecules to the active sites on C60@Ru surface, it avoids excessively tight binding that could hinder molecular participation in reactions. Such balanced adsorption creates favorable conditions for subsequent activation and combustion processes. Figure (b–d) reveals that upon formation of a stable adsorption configuration between UDMH and C60@Ru, the density of states (DOS) peak of C60@Ru exhibits noticeable changes, particularly within the energy range of −7.0 to −3.0 eV. This shift clearly indicates electron transfer occurring between UDMH and the C60@Ru system during the adsorption process.

Charge Transfer Mechanism in UDMH Adsorption on C60@Ru

Since the CH3 group shows stronger adsorption at the five-membered ring site than at the six-membered ring, this position represents a more favorable adsorption active center. Therefore, differential charge calculations were performed using this configuration.

The difference charge diagram (Figure ) reveals that H atoms in UDMH’s NH2 group (blue region) undergo electron loss upon adsorption, while C atoms on the C60 surface (yellow region) gain electrons. This directional charge transfer weakens the N–H bonds in UDMH through increased bond polarity due to electron redistribution and decreased bond energy from electron depletion at H atoms. This mechanism is fully supported by experimental characterizations: XPS and Raman results confirm interfacial electron transfer between Ru and C60; the significantly improved combustion heat proves easier decomposition and oxidation of fuel molecules, which is consistent with the weakened N–H bonds; the dispersion of C60@Ru (TEM) ensures sufficient contact between catalyst and fuel molecules for effective electron transfer. Such modifications promote N–H bond cleavage during combustion, facilitating complete oxidation with enhanced heat release.

DFT-calculated charge density difference for UDMH adsorption configuration.

Furthermore, the stable chemical adsorption between C60 and Ru anchors the Ru atoms while maintaining their catalytically active d-orbital states. These Ru d-orbitals serve as effective electron-transfer mediators for UDMH activation. The optimized adsorption geometry positions Ru atoms near reactive sites, enabling electronic modulation of UDMH’s reactivity through activation energy reduction for combustion steps and promotion of more complete fuel oxidation. These combined effects manifest as measurable increases in combustion enthalpy. Additional potential influences of C60@Ru on UDMH reactivity warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

The C60@Ru composite catalyst was successfully synthesized via a hydrothermal method and characterized by SEM, TEM, XRD, Raman spectroscopy, and XPS. When physically mixed with UDMH and Hydyne, the C60@Ru additive enhanced the propellants’ density and combustion performance while exhibiting minor effects on their freezing points and viscosities.

Specifically, C60@Ru increased the densities of UDMH and Hydyne by 0.0419 and 0.0377 g/cm3, respectively, while boosting their combustion heats by 20.07% and 14.19%, indicating a more pronounced improvement in combustion compared to density. Although slight viscosity increases were observed due to C60@Ru incorporation, the modified fuels retained adequate low-temperature operability. DFT calculations elucidated the underlying mechanism: Ru chemisorption on C60 stabilizes the catalyst structure and promotes uniform fuel dispersion, while UDMH physisorption on C60@Ru facilitates molecular activation via NH2 group electron loss. Combined with Ru’s catalytic role, this interaction enhances combustion efficiency and heat release. The findings support the potential application of C60@Ru as a performance-enhancing additive for liquid propellants. Long-term storage stability under extreme temperature cycles will be further studied in future work.
