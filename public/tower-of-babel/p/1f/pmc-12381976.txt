Comparison of sequential cryoprobe and biopsy forceps in endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration for mediastinal and hilar lesions: a prospective observational study
Authors: Ching-Kai Lin, Sheng-Yuan Ruan, Hung-Jen Fan, Hao-Chun Chang, Yen-Ting Lin, Chao-Chi Ho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) is a safe and widely used technique for diagnosing mediastinal/hilar lesions. Recent studies showed that combining EBUS-TBNA with cryobiopsy (EBUS-transbronchial mediastinal cryobiopsy, EBUS-TMC) or forceps biopsy (EBUS-intranodal forceps biopsy, EBUS-IFB) enhances diagnostic accuracy by obtaining larger tissue samples. However, limited data is comparing the efficacy of EBUS-TMC and EBUS-IFB. This study aims to assess the effectiveness of these biopsy techniques in diagnosing mediastinal and hilar lesions.MethodsPatients with mediastinal/hilar lesions who underwent EBUS-TBNA and mediastinal biopsy from November 2022 to March 2024 were enrolled. Following needle aspiration, cryobiopsy and forceps biopsies were performed. The primary endpoint was diagnostic accuracy.ResultsA total of 219 mediastinal/hilar lesions were evaluated in 183 patients. EBUS-TMC demonstrated higher diagnostic accuracy than EBUS-IFB (95.4% vs. 84.9%, p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis also showed superior yield rates for EBUS-TMC (96.1% vs. 89.6%, p = 0.027 in all malignancies; 96.1% vs. 86.8%, p = 0.042 in malignancies for re-biopsy; 95.2% vs. 76.2%, p = 0.040 in non-lung cancer malignancies; 93.8% vs. 73.8%, p = 0.002 in benign processes). The tissue specimens obtained via EBUS-TMC were significantly larger than those via EBUS-IFB (16.3 mm2 vs. 3.1 mm2, p < 0.001). Adding EBUS-IFB did not improve accuracy when EBUS-TMC was already used with EBUS-TBNA. No severe complications leading to death or disability were observed.ConclusionsEBUS-TMC is an effective diagnostic and auxiliary method for mediastinal/hilar lesions, enhancing yield rates and tissue acquisition.Keywords: Diagnostic accuracy, endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial intranodal forceps biopsy, endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration, endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial mediastinal cryobiopsy, mediastinal/hilar lesions, sample size

Introduction

Endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) is a minimally invasive tool for diagnosing mediastinal and hilar lesions. It has been widely used due to its long history of safety and high diagnostic accuracy [1–3]. Previous studies have also reported that EBUS-TBNA is superior to mediastinoscopy for accessing lymph nodes in the subcarinal and hilar regions [4,5]. However, the limited tissue material obtained through needle aspiration may reduce accuracy in certain uncommon diseases, such as lymphoma and sarcoidosis [6–8]. A substantial tissue sample is required for advanced molecular and histological subtyping to guide cancer management [9–11]. These limitations have prompted investigators to develop alternative modalities.

Forceps biopsy has been used to assess the mediastinal lesion under EBUS guidance for specimen collection, a technique known as EBUS-guided intranodal forceps biopsy (EBUS-IFB) [12,13]. Agrawal et al. confirmed that EBUS-IFB can improve the diagnosis of uncommon diseases, such as sarcoidosis and lymphoma [14]. Recently, a new method, EBUS-guided transbronchial mediastinal cryobiopsy (EBUS-TMC), which uses a cryoprobe to obtain tissue specimens from mediastinal lesions, has been developed [15,16]. Zhang et al. also demonstrated that EBUS-TMC provides higher diagnostic accuracy and yields larger specimen collection compared to EBUS-TBNA [17]. However, few studies have compared these devices for mediastinal biopsy with EBUS guidance. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effectiveness and safety of EBUS-TMC and EBUS-IFB for diagnosing mediastinal/hilar lesions.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This was a prospective, single-center observational study. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB #202109077RINC) of the National Taiwan University Cancer Center. Informed consent was obtained from all included patients prior to their participation in the study. All aspects of the study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

We prospectively recruited adult patients referred for EBUS-TBNA at the National Taiwan University Cancer Center between November 2022 and March 2024. The inclusion criteria were: (1) aged ≥18 years, (2) mediastinal or hilar lesions with a short-axis diameter of at least 5 mm, (3) no hemorrhagic diathesis, and (4) discontinuing anticoagulant and antiplatelet agents at least seven days before the procedure if the patient had been receiving them. The exclusion criteria were: (1) lesions identified as abscess or cyst, (2) presence of tracheostomy or endotracheal tube, (3) uncontrollable mental disorders or dementia, and (4) any other factor that precluded inclusion in the study as determined by the investigators.

The character of patients and bronchoscopy procedures were recorded prior or during the procedure. The recorded data included age, gender, prophylactic antibiotics used, the indication (initial diagnosis or re-biopsy), lesion size (short-axis measurement by EBUS), lesion location, procedure time, radiation dose, and major procedure-related adverse events.

Procedures

All procedures were performed by pulmonologists with over ten years of experience in EBUS-TBNA. These procedures were conducted in a hybrid bronchoscopy room equipped with a C-arm cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) angiography system (Artis Zee Ceiling; Siemens Healthcare GmbH, Forchheim, Germany). A CBCT image was done before the procedures to confirm the absence of pneumothorax (PTX) or pneumomediastinum.

All patients underwent conscious sedation with intravenous midazolam, propofol, and fentanyl. A convex probe EBUS (BF-UC290F; Olympus; Tokyo, Japan) was inserted through a supraglottic airway (i-gel®; Intersurgical Ltd, Berkshire, UK). Scanning was performed using a 7.5 MHz frequency ultrasound, and images were generated with a dedicated ultrasound processor (EU-ME2 PREMIER PLUS, Olympus). After identifying the target mediastinal or hilar lesions, needle aspiration was performed using a dedicated 21-gauge needle (NA-U401SX-4021; Olympus; Tokyo, Japan) for three passes. To enlarge the puncture hole and establish a tract to the target lesion, repeated needle punctures in different directions were made at the original puncture site. A flexible 1.1-mm cryoprobe (Erbe Elektromedizin GmbH, Tübingen, Germany) was then advanced through the tract created by the TBNA needle under real-time ultrasound guidance via convex probe EBUS. A freeze activation time of 3–5 s was used for TMC to obtain 3–4 histological samples. Subsequently, IFB was performed using 1.5-mm forceps (FB-233D; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at the same tract to retrieve 3–4 tissue specimens. No adjunctive tools, such as an electrocautery knife or needle knife, were used to facilitate tract creation in our study population. The severity of bleeding observed during the procedure was classified as mild (spontaneous stop oozing within 1 min), moderate (spontaneous stop oozing more than 1 min or need balloon wedge tamponade), severe (selective intubation needed for <20 min), and life-threatening (prolonged intubation, transfer to the intensive care unit, or resuscitation).

Rapid onsite cytologic evaluation (ROSE) with a rapid staining method (Hemacolor; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was consistently available during EBUS-TBNA, TMC, and IFB. All histological specimens obtained from the procedures were individually placed in 10% formalin for histological evaluation. The area of the histological specimen via TMC and IFB was measured using a digital camera attached to a microscope. Rinse fluid from the aspiration needle was collected for microbiological analysis. Post-procedural CBCT was conducted to detect potential PTX and pneumomediastinum.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was diagnostic accuracy. Diagnostic criteria for target lesions were established based on pathological evidence, microbiology, or clinical follow-up. Histological diagnoses deemed inconclusive, such as nonspecific fibrosis, chronic inflammation, necrosis, atypic cells, or ‘suspicious’ findings were classified as non-diagnostic. Benign inflammations, which could not be determined through pathological or microbiological means, were confirmed through radiological and clinical follow-up (stable or reduced lesion size on CT imaging) at least one year after our procedure. Malignancy was still considered in cases where lesions showed enlargement on follow-up imaging after cancer treatment, provided that infection or inflammation was excluded, even in the absence of histological confirmation.

The secondary outcomes included a comparison of sample size between TMC and IFB, as well as procedure-related adverse events, such as fever, bleeding, PTX, and pneumomediastinum.

Statistical analysis

Diagnostic accuracy was defined as the ratio of correctly biopsy-proven classes to the total testing classes. Comparisons were made using Student’s t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables, and the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. A significance level of 0.05 was used to assess statistical significance. We utilized SPSS version 22.0 (IBM, SPSS, Chicago, IL) for statistical analysis.

Results

Patients

A total of 188 consecutive patients with 228 mediastinal/hilar lesions underwent EBUS-TMC and EBUS-IFB following EBUS-TBNA. In all cases, both the cryoprobe and biopsy forceps were successfully inserted into the target lesions. After excluding 9 lesions (TMC failure to obtain tissue in 2 lesions, IFB failure in 5 lesions, both TMC and IFB failure in 1 lesion, and loss follow-up in 1 lesion), 183 patients with 219 mediastinal/hilar lesions who successfully received EBUS-TMC and EBUS-IFB were included in the analysis. In the subgroup analysis comparing the two biopsy methods, TMC consistently demonstrated superior diagnostic performance to IFB. Specifically, TMC showed higher accuracy for malignant target lesions (96.1% vs. 89.6%, p = 0.027) and for malignant lesions undergoing re-biopsy (96.1% vs. 86.8%, p = 0.042). It also provided better yield in non-lung cancer malignancies (95.2% vs. 76.2%, p = 0.040) and exhibited superior diagnostic accuracy in benign processes compared to IFB (93.8% vs. 73.8%, p = 0.002). Furthermore, TMC outperformed EBUS-TBNA in diagnosing benign lesions (93.8% vs. 72.3%, p = 0.001).

Sample size

The mean sample area of specimens obtained via TMC was significantly larger than that of IFB (16.3 mm2 vs. 3.1 mm2, p < 0.001) (Table 2). Each biopsy specimen was considered sufficient.

Adverse events

Moderate oozing was observed in 71 patients (38.8%), with no severe or life-threatening bleeding was reported in the study. Asymptomatic pneumomediastinum was detected in 11 patients (6.0%) on post-procedural CBCT imaging, which did not require further intervention. Fever requiring additional antibiotic treatment developed in six patients (3.3%) during the 4-week follow-up period; notably, no such events were observed among the 57 patients who received prophylactic antibiotics prior to the procedure. No other severe procedure-related adverse events, such as mediastinitis, hypoxia, or death were noted in this study (Table 3).

Discussion

This prospective observational study demonstrated that EBUS-TBNA, EBUS-TMC and EBUS-IFB are all safe and effective for diagnosing mediastinal/hilar lesions. Among the biopsy methods, EBUS-TMC appears to be superior, offering higher diagnostic accuracy and yielding larger histological specimens compared to EBUS-IFB. The advantages of EBUS-TMC are particularly evident in cases involving malignant lesions, malignant lesions undergoing re-biopsy, non-lung cancer malignancies, and benign processes. Furthermore, the effectiveness of EBUS-IFB may be diminished when EBUS-TMC is performed concurrently with EBUS-TBNA.

In this era of gene-guided therapy, there is an increasing demand for higher-quality histological specimens for advanced molecular analysis [9–11]. New biopsy devices and methods have been developed for the EBUS-guided mediastinal approach. Larger gauge needles are now being used for EBUS-TBNA. Although histological specimens obtained with larger needles are bigger and more cellular, most studies have shown no significant improvement in diagnostic accuracy for malignancy, and there is increased blood contamination compared to standard-sized TBNA needles [18–21]. EBUS-IFB is also a relatively new technique for mediastinal biopsy. Previous studies have demonstrated that the diagnostic yield of EBUS-IFB, when combined with EBUS-TBNA, is significantly higher than EBUS-TBNA alone, especially in cases of lymphoma and sarcoidosis [14,22]. However, most of these studies used mini-forceps (1.1–1.5 mm), which obtained only small tissue specimens. This technique appears to be insufficient.

Cryobiopsy freezes the surrounding tissue, allowing for the extraction of a larger specimen while preserving its internal structure. This method has been employed for diagnosing endobronchial tumors, interstitial pulmonary diseases, and peripheral pulmonary lesions [23–27]. Previous publications have shown that cryobiopsy provides more sufficient tissue for gene analysis and histological subtyping than forceps biopsy, aiding physicians in determining further cancer treatment [10,28]. This benefit also applies to EBUS-TMC. In the present study, EBUS-TMC demonstrated higher accuracy than EBUS-IFB across our entire study population (95.4% vs. 84.9%, p < 0.001), and also showed a higher overall diagnostic yield compared to EBUS-TBNA (95.4% vs. 89.5%, p = 0.019). The histological specimens obtained via TMC were significantly larger than those obtained via IFB (16.3 mm2 vs. 3.1 mm2, p < 0.001). These results closely align with a previous study by Cheng et al. which reported a superior diagnostic yield for cryobiopsy compared to forceps biopsy (85.8% vs. 70.8%, p = 0.001) [29]. Furthermore, in this study, IFB did not improve the diagnostic yield when TMC was already performed alongside EBUS-TBNA. A similar trend was observed in Madan’s study, where EBUS-TBNA combined with TMC demonstrated a higher diagnostic yield compared to EBUS-TBNA combined with IFB [30]. We believe that EBUS-TMC serves as a better diagnostic and auxiliary technique for diagnosing mediastinal/hilar lesions.

In our subgroup analysis, EBUS-TMC demonstrated higher diagnostic accuracy for lesions with a final diagnosis of non-lung cancer malignancies and benign conditions, compared to IFB. It also showed significantly superior accuracy for benign lesions compared to EBUS-TBNA. These findings are consistent with Zhang’s report, which indicated that EBUS-TMC is superior to TBNA for diagnosing uncommon tumors and nonmalignant diseases, such as sarcoidosis [17]. Additionally, tumor fibrosis and necrosis that occur after cancer treatment may reduce the diagnostic accuracy of re-biopsy [31,32]. Although the re-biopsy population has been rarely discussed in previous studies, our findings demonstrated that EBUS-TMC provided a higher diagnostic yield for malignant cases requiring re-biopsy compared to EBUS-IFB. The larger and more cellular specimens obtained via cryobiopsy may facilitate a more accurate diagnosis in these conditions.

The accuracy remained high for lesions smaller than 10 mm in the short axis, with rates of 95.7% for EBUS-TMC, 87.0% for EBUS-TBNA and 82.6% for EBUS-IFB in the present study (Table 2). To our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze sub-centimeter lesions in EBUS-guided mediastinal biopsy. Although the difference among the three methods was not significant, a trend suggesting a higher yield rate for EBUS-TMC was still observed. This may be due to the small sample size, as our study included only 23 sub-centimeter lesions. We believe that with a larger sample size, the difference could achieve statistical significance.

In the present study, no significant difference was observed in the diagnostic accuracy for primary lung cancer among EBUS-TBNA, EBUS-TMC and EBUS-IFB (Table 2). This finding contrasts with the results reported by Cheng et al. [29]. To our knowledge, preservation of overall tissue architecture is not always essential for the diagnosis of primary lung cancer, which may limit the added value of cryobiopsy in this context. Furthermore, previous studies have shown no significant improvement in the diagnostic accuracy of primary lung cancer when using TMC compared to EBUS-TBNA [16,17]. Similarly, the diagnostic accuracy of IFB for primary lung cancer has been reported to be comparable to that of EBUS-TBNA [33]. We therefore believe that, in this patient population, the choice of diagnostic tool may not be the primary determinant of diagnostic success.

Serious adverse events associated with EBUS-TBNA, EBUS-TMC and EBUS-IFB, such as severe to life-threatening bleeding, mediastinitis, hypoxia, or death did not occur in the present study. Only controllable fever, mild to moderate wound oozing, and minimal pneumomediastinum without clinical symptoms were observed after the procedures. The use of prophylactic antibiotics prior to the procedure may have contributed to the prevention of infection-related complications, as no patients in this study required subsequent antibiotic modification. Similarly, very few adverse events have been reported in other studies of EBUS-TMC and EBUS-IFB [14–16,29]. This may be due to the use of real-time EBUS imaging for guidance, which helps operators avoid major injuries. We believe that EBUS-TBNA, EBUS-TMC and EBUS-IFB are all safe procedures.

Based on the above results, performing EBUS-TMC is also safe for sub-centimeter lesions. The tip of a 1.1-mm cryoprobe is only 3 mm long, so the effect of cryobiopsy may not extend beyond lesions larger than 5 mm in the short axis. Additionally, we used a TBNA needle for tract creation, which caused less damage to bronchial mucosa compared to an electrocautery knife. Repeat punctures may be necessary to create a new tract, as these small lesions can easily shift position. However, we cannot guarantee that the surrounding normal structure will remain unharmed if the lesion is smaller than 5 mm. Therefore, systemic mediastinal staging is still not suitable for EBUS-TMC.

In the present study, both the cryoprobe and biopsy forceps were successfully inserted into all target lesions, resulting in a 100% technical success rate for tract creation. However, difficulty in advancing the biopsy tools through certain lesions was still encountered, particularly in small benign lymph nodes or malignant lesions requiring re-biopsy after cancer treatment. In such cases, repeated punctures were necessary to enlarge the puncture hole or tract to complete the procedure successfully. This may lead to prolonged procedure time, which is a potential drawback. Although the use of an electrocautery knife or needle knife may facilitate tract creation and improve penetration, we believe these tools may carry a higher risk of over-penetration through the lesion and potentially injuring the normal tissue beneath the target, particularly in sub-centimeter lesions. Their use is also associated with additional medical costs. Furthermore, performing EBUS-TBNA with ROSE prior to TMC and IFB can assist in confirming the presence and location of malignant cells or identifying specific cytologic patterns, such as granulomas. Using the TBNA needle for initial tract creation may offer additional advantages for both TMC and IFB.

In the present study, EBUS-TMC was performed followed by EBUS-IFB within a single procedure. According to Kim’s report, the formation of organized blood clots or edematous changes at the biopsy site of peripheral lung nodules after cryobiopsy may affect the accuracy of subsequent forceps biopsy [34]. However, other randomized controlled trials on TMC have reported no significant difference in diagnostic yield related to the order of procedures [17,29]. Therefore, we believe our results remain clinically applicable.

Our study has several limitations. First, all patients underwent EBUS-TMC followed by EBUS-IFB in a single procedure, making it difficult to distinguish the complications associated with each method. Additionally, we were also unable to identify which biopsy tool ultimately provided the specimen used for molecular or next-generation sequencing analysis. As a result, it was not possible to determine which biopsy method is more suitable for molecular applications. Second, approximately 60% of the study population had primary lung cancer, while cases of less common malignancies, such as lymphoma and benign processes were relatively rare. This may limit the generalizability of our findings to patients at other institutions. Therefore, a prospective randomized study with a more diverse patient population is warranted.

Third, the biopsy sites for both the cryoprobe and forceps were confined to the created tract. Lesions with abundant blood supply were also avoided for sampling [35], further narrowing the range of biopsy sites. Finally, we used mini-forceps (1.5 mm) instead of standard forceps (1.8 mm) due to the difficulty in passing the latter through the bronchoscopy’s working channel. The difference in sample volume could also have impacted diagnostic accuracy [36,37].

Conclusions

EBUS-TBNA, EBUS-TMC and EBUS-IFB are all safe and effective methods for diagnosing mediastinal/hilar lesions. EBUS-TMC serves as both a diagnostic and auxiliary technique, offering a higher accuracy rate and larger tissue specimens. This technique can also be applied to sub-centimeter lesions at least 5 mm in size and to lesions requiring re-biopsy. We believe that EBUS-TMC will prove to be valuable in the era of gene-guided therapy.
