3D bioprinting of flap-tissue with heterogenous cells and flexible perfusable vessels
Authors: Shan Mou, Yingqian Zhou, Litao Suo, Yaqi Guo, Xinfang Xie, Di Sun, Jinfei Hou, Zhipeng Li, Yifan Zhang, Jiaming Sun, Yong He, Zhenxing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite advancements in bio-manufacturing, the fabrication of large-scale vascularized tissue with heterogeneous cells remains a daunting challenge. To address this challenge, the reverse engineered structured tissue (REST) three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting method was proposed. This strategy enables the seeding and subsequent assembly of multiple tissue-specific constituent cells onto a robust, flexible, 3D biomimetic vascular scaffold with long-term perfusion capability. The material choices for the vessel network and seeding cells could be decoupled, and separate co-culture of multiple seeding cells in a customized bioreactor could be realized. The cellular layers could be reassembled into an engineered tissue by manually folding the robust vessels. Centimeter-scale vascularized skin-flap-like tissues that comprise epidermal, dermal, and adipose layers were engineered with an in vivo-like communicating vascular network using this strategy. The engineered tissue exhibits three-layered cellular heterogeneity (containing HaCaT cells, fibroblasts, and adipose-derived stem cells) and perfusable tubes (containing HUVECs and VSMCs). The complex tissue could be remolded through in vitro perfusion of vessel network and specific culture/differentiation medium supplied to tissue-specific cellular layers. After in vivo transplantation for three months, the tissue construct formed a viable complex tissue with a rich network of blood vessels. Our results demonstrate that “REST” bioprinting technology can be used to fabricate multicellular tissues, ranging from millimeter to centimeter scales with perfusable vessels, opening new avenues for functional artificial organ bioprinting.

Introduction

The field of biofabrication has progressed significantly. Bionic tissues, including the cornea, nerves, and cartilage, can be constructed effectively; however, the fabrication of large-scale vascularized tissue with heterogeneous cells remains a daunting challenge [[1], [2], [3]]. The construction of vascular networks within large-scale tissues (≥1 cm) and mimicking multicellular complexity play important roles in manufacturing artificial tissue replacements [4,5]. Coaxial three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting is an efficient method for constructing perfusable vessels in 3D scaffolds [6,7]. Ge demonstrated that biomimetic small vessels containing endothelial and smooth muscle cells could be generated using coaxial printing [8]. Additionally, the coaxial extrusion system was improved to create circumferentially multilayered tubular tissues with digitally tunable layers in a single step [9]. Our previous study demonstrated that large-scale tissues with built-in microchannels could be constructed by depositing coaxial printed tubes [10,11]. However, current bioprinting technologies have limitations in terms of constructing native tissue-like structures. The core challenges are as follows: (1) The bio-fabricated blood vessels are difficult to support long-term perfusion; (2) different cells require different culture media and environments, making it difficult to culture the multicellular tissues successfully. Although Kacey et al. described the creation and application of a tissue-chip platform that interconnected mature human heart, liver, bone, and skin tissue compartments through a recirculating vascular system while also providing specific culture/differentiation environments to different cell components in vitro, this multi-organ tissue chip was designed for drug screening and further research is required to investigate its potential for constructing multicellular tissues [12].

To resolve these issues, we propose a novel 3D bioprinting strategy called “reverse engineered structured tissue (REST)” to fabricate large-scale (>1 cm) vascularized multi cellular flap tissue for the first time. This method enables disassembling complex tissues into coaxial printed vessel-connected functional cellular layers that can be separately co-cultured in a customized bioreactor with specific culture/differentiation medium. Finally, multicellular tissues were fabricated through reassembling of monocellular layers.

The skin flap is a paradigmatic illustration of a complex tissue structure with a multilayered cellular architecture that includes the epidermis, dermis, hypodermal adipose tissue, and an intricate network of vasculature. Skin flap transplantation is a surgical method that is widely used in chronic wound repair, organ reconstruction (such as the nose and breast), and so on [13,14]. Axial flaps with known large vessels can be anastomosed with blood vessels in the recipient area to obtain an abundant blood supply, which helps grafts survive. However, skin flap transplantation often faces problems, including donor site injury and donor shortage. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate the biofabrication of the bionic axial flap.

In this study, we attempted to construct large-scale skin-flap-like tissues using the “REST” strategy (Scheme 1). 3D skin flap was disassembled into a two-dimensional (2D) planar arrangement of the epidermis, dermis, and subcutaneous fat (interconnected by a vascular network). The 2D tissues were constructed using coaxial printing and cell loading, while the 3D skin-flap-like tissue was fabricated through folding and manual assembling of the 2D tissues. Specifically, a composite hydrogel containing alginate, gelatin methacrylate (GelMA), and polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) was used to fabricate the vascular network. A perfusable large-scale scaffold with three parallel compartments was constructed by depositing bionic vessels. Moreover, HaCaT, fibroblast, and adipose-derived stem cells (ADSC) were deposited into three compartments and cultured in a custom-made bioreactor (containing three culture cells) to create a relatively independent culture environment for different cell types. Simultaneously, nutrients were infused into the scaffolds through bionic vessels. Finally, a skin-flap-like tissue was fabricated by “Z”-like tri-folding of the 2D tissue. Construction of GAP inks with high mechanical performance for 3D printing

GAP ink was composed of GelMA, alginate, and PEGDA (Fig. 1A) and could be crosslinked using Ca2+ and ultraviolet (UV) light (Fig. 1B). The mechanical properties of different hydrogel composite formulations were measured using compression tests. The pre-hydrogel was poured into cylindrical molds and allowed to solidify. After crosslinking, alginate and composite hydrogels containing alginate exhibited semitransparent appearances, whereas the others were transparent (Fig. S1A, Supporting Information). The weight change ratio of GAP hydrogel before and after swelling was lower compared with that of G (Fig. S1B, Supporting Information). The stress-strain curves revealed that GAP possessed the highest Young's modulus among all the groups (Fig. 1C–D). The improved mechanical properties of the hydrogels imply that the printed tubes or tubular scaffolds based on hydrogels might better withstand exposure to inside/outside stresses. The viscosities of inks with different formulations were determined using rheological measurements. The results revealed that the viscosities of the inks increased with the addition of alginate, while the increase in PEGDA content exhibited no significant effect on the viscosity. Moreover, all inks exhibited shear-thinning behavior, in which the viscosity gradually decreased with increasing shear rate (Fig. 1E–F). The printability of GelMA is affected by temperature [15]. To better understand the temperature sensitivity of the GAP composites, their viscosities were evaluated at varying temperatures (15–50 °C). The viscosity increased significantly as the temperature decreased from approximately 25 °C. The GAP hydrogel exhibited very low viscosity when the temperature exceeded 30 °C. Moreover, higher concentrations of GelMA increased the viscosity at temperatures below 25 °C (Fig. 1G). Additionally, higher concentrations of PEGDA increased the compressive Young's modulus of the hydrogel (Fig. 1H–J). To evaluate the printability of the inks, different formulations of inks were used to print tubular scaffolds with coaxial nozzles at 25 °C. Alginate (2%, 3%, and 4% w/v) combined with GelMA (3%, 5%, and 7% w/v) and PEGDA (5%, 10%, and 15% w/v) were printed to form scaffolds (Fig. 1K). The tubular scaffolds could be printed continuously when the flow rate of the outer bioink phase was twice that of the inner CaCl2 solution. Platelet adhesion to hydrogels was evaluated by exposing GA, PA, GP, and GAP to human whole blood under static conditions, and the number of adherent CD41-positive cells was counted. The results revealed that the degree of platelet adhesion supported by PA and GAP was lower than that of GA and GP, while there were no significant differences between PA and GAP groups (Fig. 1L–M). It has been previously reported that the addition of PEGDA to collagen can enhance the thromboresistance of hydrogels [16]. This could be attributed to the passivating nature of PEGDA. We hypothesized that the relatively small number of adherent platelets could be associated with the aforementioned reason. Biofabrication of tissue-engineered vessel

For the biofabrication of the tissue-engineered vessel (TEV), GAP-based bioink containing VSMCs was coaxially printed, and HUVECs were seeded (Fig. 2A). Hollow tubes with varying diameters were created by printing GAP inks using a dual coaxial printhead with a series of gauges (10G/19G, 14G/22G, and 17G/22G). The outer diameter of the printed tubes ranged from 761.7 to 1208 μm, and the inner diameter ranged from 306.2 to 710.9 μm, which could mimic the mesoscale blood vessel size [17] (Fig. 2B–C). Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that thicker walls could hinder the permeability of oxygen and nutrients, whereas thinner walls might lead to insufficient mechanical strength [18,19]. Live/dead staining was performed to assess the viability of VSMCs and HUVECs. The viability of VSMCs decreased progressively with increasing culture time, and there was no significant difference among the A, GA, and GAP groups (Fig. 2D). Several reasons might account for this phenomenon. Increasing the polymer concentration may result in dense hydrogel networks with smaller pores, which could negatively affect cell viability. However, the viability of HUVECs increased in the GA and GAP groups (Fig. 2E). The addition of RGD-containing GelMA may improve the bioactivity of the hydrogel. Nesrine et al. demonstrated that even a small amount of RGD (0.1%) was sufficient to support HUVEC adhesion to the hydrogel surface [20]. Additionally, phalloidin staining revealed that HUVECs on GA and GAP tubes exhibited a comparatively larger spreading area than those on A tubes, further confirming the superior biocompatibility of GA and GAP (Fig. 2F). Engineered vessels were prepared using Cherry-VSMCs and GFP-HUVECs, and the results revealed that the vessels were compartmentalized into vascular constructs containing endothelial and muscular layers (Fig. 2G). Moreover, the CCK-8 assay results showed no significant difference in VSMC viability among the A, GA, and GAP groups (Fig. 2H). For HUVECs, viability was higher in the GAP group than in the A group, though the GA group appeared to have the highest viability; additionally, cell spreading area was quantified by phalloidin staining, and the results showed that HUVEC spreading area was larger in the GAP group than in the A group (Fig. 2I–J).

Characterization of TEV

The tubes were sandwiched between two glass slides, and weights (10, 20, 50, and 100 g) were used to apply pressure to the top glass slide (Fig. 3A). When 20 g of weight was applied, fluid retention was observed in the GA group, leading to the rupture of the tubes. When 50 g of weight was applied, the liquid could only pass through the GAP tubes (Fig. 3B–C). As previously stated, the mechanical strength of GAP was significantly higher than that of the other groups, indicating that GAP tubes might better withstand external stress. Moreover, weights (10, 20, 50, and 100 g) were lifted using A, GA, and GAP tubes, respectively. The results revealed that only GAP tubes could withstand a 50 g weight (Fig. 3D–E). GAP tubes embedded with green fluorescent beads could be manually shaped into bow-ties, pentagrams, Chinese knots, and other shapes due to their good mechanical performance (Fig. 3F). The tubes were embedded in 5% (w/v) GelMA, and rhodamine-conjugated dextran (40 kDa) was perfused into the TEVs to assess its permeability (tubes composed of only GAP hydrogel [GAP tubes] served as negative controls). Fluorescent images taken at the same sites at 0 and 30 min after perfusion are depicted in Fig. 3G. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference in diffusional permeability between GAP and TEV groups (6.35 ± 0.55 versus 7.78 ± 2.87 × 10−5 mm/s) (Fig. 3H). Biofabrication of perfusable tubular scaffolds

For tubular scaffold printing, CaCl2 and bioinks were extruded through the inner and outer channels using syringe pumps (Fig. 4A). The printing path is depicted in Fig. 4B. The scaffold was about 20 mm long and 20 mm wide, exhibiting a three-layer tubular structure. The printing process is depicted in Fig. 4C and Video S1 (Supporting Information). To investigate the shape fidelity of different inks, the area of the open windows of the tubular scaffolds was measured to determine the ink spreading (SP) [21]. For an ideal printing process, the printed tubular scaffolds should be consistent with the 3D model. In practice, there is a relative difference between the actual area (Aa) and theoretical area (At) of the window, which is referred to as SP (Fig. 4D). The inks A, GA, and GAP were printed at approximately 25 °C. The open windows could not be maintained by 2% (w/v) alginate alone, while windows could be formed in the GA and GAP groups (Fig. 4E). Besides, the SP of A (almost 100%) was significantly greater than that of GA (37.29 ± 2.19%) and GAP (33.5 ± 4.16%) (Fig. 4F). The addition of 5% (w/v) GelMA was believed to increase the viscosity of the inks and improve printing fidelity. Moreover, the optimal temperature for printing GAP bioinks was determined similarly because GAP bioinks are temperature-sensitive. The open window area increased as the temperature decreased from 29 to 25 °C. At 29 °C, it was difficult for GAP bioinks to maintainthe printed shape (Fig. 4G), while further cooling (for instance, to 23 °C) resulted in failure to form stable adhesion between hollow tubes (data not included). To better understand the 3D structure of the tubular scaffolds, micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) was performed. The tubular scaffolds could be perfused with a contrast agent without leakage (Fig. 4I). Representative CT images of the cross-sectional views of the scaffolds demonstrated that the scaffolds had three layers, and the tubes were squeezed at the intersections but remained patent (Fig. 4J). The fluorescent and white light images of the tubular scaffolds (cross-section) are depicted in Fig. 4K.

To evaluate the cell viability of the vascularized scaffolds, live/dead staining was performed at 3 and 7 days after HUVECs seeding. Images of different sampled locations are depicted in Fig. 4L–M. The death of VSMCs and the area covered by HUVECs increased progressively (Fig. 4M–N). Immunofluorescent staining of CD31 and alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) demonstrated that the vascularized scaffolds contained compartmentalized vascular constructs (endothelial and muscular layers).

Subcutaneous transplantation of the tubular scaffold for cellular infiltration assessment

The fabricated vascularized (with cells, VS group) and tubular scaffolds (without cells, blank group) were subcutaneously implanted in nude mice (Fig. 5A). The samples before and after implantation are depicted in Fig. 5B. One month post-implantation, blood vessels were uniformly distributed throughout the entire scaffold in the vascularized scaffold (VS) group, whereas they were sparsely distributed in the blank group (Fig. 5B). Representative hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunofluorescence staining images of the two groups at low and high magnifications are presented in Fig. 5C. The architecture of the scaffolds was maintained, and tube collapse was barely observed in both groups after one month of subcutaneous transplantation. Additionally, compared with the blank group, H&E staining images of the VS group demonstrated obvious disintegration of the scaffold wall, which was closely associated with extensive infiltration of host tissue into the VS scaffold; in contrast, minimal cell and blood vessel infiltration was observed in the blank group. Quantitative analysis revealed that the density of CD31−and α-SMA-positive cells was higher in the xenografts of the VS group (Fig. 5D–E). These data demonstrated that the HUVEC and VSMC layers could promote graft vascularization. As previously reported, recellularized scaffolds containing epithelial cells, muscle cells, or adipose tissue-derived stem cells exhibited enhanced host cell invasion and increased ingrowth of epithelial and smooth muscle cells when transplanted into nude mice [22]. We hypothesized that vascularized scaffolds containing VSMCs and HUVECs might produce similar effects. Biofabrication of skin flap-like composite tissue

Skin flap-like composite tissues composed of the epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis were fabricated using the following method (Fig. 6A). A complex tubular scaffold (containing HUVECs and VSMCs) with three compartments was printed (the printing process and perfusion are illustrated in Fig. S3 and Video S2, Supporting Information) and seeded with HaCaT cells, fibroblasts, and ADSCs. A custom bioreactor was designed to accommodate the three components (epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis) of the scaffolds in separate chambers. Moreover, the tubular scaffolds were connected to a peristaltic pump and perfused with VSMC culture medium. The matured tissues were manually assembled to form a skin flap-like composite tissue. The engineered composite tissues were co-cultured individually. The tissue was perfused, and there was no evidence of medium leakage (from the medium chamber) to the left and right chambers (Fig. 6B and Video S3, Supporting Information). Live/dead staining of cross-sections revealed more viable cells in the perfused group after 7 days of in vitro culture (Fig. 6C–D). Rhodamine -dextran was allowed to diffuse through the tubes (Fig. 6E and Video S4, Supporting Information). Gray value mapping along lines ab and a'b’ in the two groups confirmed greater dextran diffusion in the perfused group at 1, 30, and 60 min (Fig. 6F–G). Moreover, the gray value changes between the two groups confirmed improved dextran diffusion in the perfused group at 60 min (Fig. 6H). The composite tissue could be assembled by “Z”-folding the layers (Fig. 6I and Video S5, Supporting Information). Furthermore, the tissue remained patent after folding, with no blue liquid leakage detected (Fig. 6I d and Video S5 Supporting Information). Additionally, different cell types were assembled using this folding process (Fig. 6J–K). P63-positive, α-SMA-positive, and perilipin-positive cells were detected in the composite tissue after 14 days of in vitro culture (Fig. 6L). Moreover, different types of microspheres were assembled using this method; microspheres were loaded into different windows of the scaffold to form E-shaped patterns (Figures S4A–C, Supporting Information) or into the three units of the complex scaffold (Figure S4D, Supporting Information) to form a structure with three layers of heterogeneous components (Figure S4E, Supporting Information). The microsphere-loaded scaffold was also perfused with rhodamine-dextran (Figures S4F-G, Supporting Information). Subcutaneous transplantation of skin flap-like tissue

Skin flap-like composite tissue (Transplant group) and cell-free scaffolds (without HUVEC, VSMC, HaCaT, fibroblast and ADSC; Cell-free Transplant group) were subcutaneously transplanted into nude mice for three months (Fig. 7A). Gross photographs showed obvious vascular invasion in the Transplant group, whereas minimal invasions were observed in the Cell-free Transplant group (Fig. 7B). In constrast, H&E staining revealed evident cell infiltration in both groups (Fig. 7B). 3D images of the vascular network demonstrated substantial vascular ingrowth in both groups after 3 months (Fig. 7C and Videos S6-7, Supporting Information). The Transplant group had a significantly larger average vascular volume than the Cell-free Transplant group (Fig. 7D). Discussions

Herein, we proposed a strategy for biofabricating vascularized skin flap-like tissue with heterogeneous cells using the “REST” approach. The construct contained perfusable vascular structures (seeded with HUVECs and VSMCs) and a three-layered heterogeneous cell composition (HaCaT cells, fibroblasts, and ADSCs). The biofabricated tissue construct could be perfused in vitro, and three types of cell-specific culture media could be supplied via a customized bioreactor to promote functional maturation of different cell populations. The GAP bioink exhibited appropriate mechanical properties, enabling manual folding of the construct to form heterogeneous cellular layers. Following three months of subcutaneous transplantation, the construct achieved favorable vascularization in vivo. Moreover, the skin flap-like tissue could accelerate full-thickness skin wound healing in vivo. However, achieving successful anastomosis between the construct and host vessels requires further investigation. Collectively, we developed a novel biofabrication strategy for generating vascularized tissues with heterogeneous functional cells. This strategy is compatible with various cell types and holds significant potential for creating personalized organ-specific tissues with embedded vascular channels.

In solid, functional tissues, a robust vascular system is indispensable for delivering nutrients and oxygen to cellular components and removing metabolic waste. When tissue dimensions exceed a threshold of approximately 200 μm, the distance surpasses the limit of oxygen diffusion, making de novo blood vessel formation necessary [23]. This angiogenic process is not only critical for maintaining natural tissue homeostasis but also essential for ensuring the viability and functionality of tissue-engineered scaffolds [24,25].

Numerous strategies have been explored to incorporate angiogenic cues, either via growth factors or cellular therapies, to enhance vascularization within tissue-engineered constructs [26,27]. However, constructing a stable, perfusable vascular network using these methods remains challenging. Sacrificial templating and organ-on-a-chip stacking approaches can fabricate vascular networks with complex spatial architectures; nonetheless, mimicking the intricate cellular composition of vascular walls remains difficult [28,29]. Additionally, studies have shown that encapsulating vascular endothelial cells and supporting cells (e.g., embryonic fibroblasts) in hydrogels enables endothelial cells to form vascular structures and sprout in natural hydrogels, such as collagen and fibrin. However, these matrix materials have limited mechanical properties, restricting their practical applications [30,31]. Coaxial bioprinting technology offers advantages in simulating the diverse cellular components of vascular walls [8]. Concurrently, the mechanical properties of biomimetic blood vessels can be modulated by adjusting the hydrogel composition, providing a versatile strategy for constructing vascularized tissues.

Furthermore, fabricating vascular structures that can anastomose with the host vasculature post-implantation would be ideal for tissue engineering [32]. Numerous scholars have conducted related investigations, yielding encouraging results. Boyang Zhang constructed a vascular chip using a layer-by-layer assembly and crosslinking method [29]. This chip could be anastomosed with the hindlimb vessels of rats to establish immediate blood perfusion. The results demonstrated that the anastomosed group exhibited more blood vessels than the non-anastomosed group. Ariel and Xin Liu, respectively, used 3D printing and molding methods to construct anastomosis-capable tissues [4,33]. However, the embedded cells in their constructs were relatively homogeneous. In this study, the hydrogel scaffold was anastomosed with blood vessels, allowing immediate blood perfusion into the scaffold; however, long-term perfusion was difficult to maintain, which we will further explore. Nonetheless, the complex construct achieved favorable vascularization in vivo (Fig. 7), indicating that this approach could provide a potential method for constructing vascularized tissues without anastomosis.

In addition to constructing vascular networks, mimicking multicellular complexity plays a vital role in developing artificial tissue replacements. Advancements in biofabrication technology have enabled the construction of biomimetic tissues with compositional heterogeneity [34]. Multimaterial bioprinting is an emerging technique derived from traditional 3D printing technology that can fabricate heterogeneous multicellular structures [35]. A common printing mode involves extruding different types of bioinks through a single printhead. This method typically requires multiple ink storage devices or ink mixers. The former involves connecting the reservoirs containing different bioinks to the print nozzle, and the ink flow rate and switching can be controlled using electromagnetic valve technology to print heterogeneous tissues [36]. The latter uses an ink mixer for printing: this technique involves mixing different bioinks via the device to prepare multilayer fibers with internal microstructures, which are then printed [37]. However, this single-nozzle printing technique poses a risk of ink cross-contamination. This problem can be solved using a multi-nozzle bioprinter. Complex heterogeneous tissues can be constructed by connecting different inks to different print nozzles and using software to control the orderly printing of these nozzles. Currently, skin analogs with layered structures have been constructed through 3D printing; however, these tissues contain minimal vascular channels capable of anastomosis [[38], [39], [40]].

Additionally, Zhiguang Qiao's team first constructed three-layered scaffolds using electrospinning. They infused photosensitive ink into the mesh of the first scaffold and crosslinked it using UV irradiation. The second scaffold was stacked on top of the first, photosensitive ink was infused into the mesh of the upper layer scaffold, and the structure was crosslinked again. The third layer was constructed similarly. A scaffold with a three-layer heterogeneous structure was constructed using this layer-by-layer stacking and crosslinking method [41]. This scaffold assembly method is instructive; however, the different components were not cultured separately. This study aimed to construct a flap-like composite tissue containing the epidermis, dermis, subcutaneous tissue, and nutrient channels, and to achieve separate co-culture and maturation of different cell components in vitro.

In the field of biofabrication, the differentiation of cells into undesirable phenotypes currently restricts the full realization of the potential benefits of various bioprinting technologies [42]. Although orthogonal induction can direct differentiation of induced pluripotent stem cells into different cell types, further optimization of stem cell differentiation protocols and the synergistic integration of this technology with biofabrication techniques are required to advance the biomanufacturing of complex tissues [5]. Tissue-chip systems can be used to optimize the culture or differentiation of different cellular components; however, further research is needed to explore their applications in large-scale complex tissue construction. The provision of an optimal microenvironment for different cell types during the construction of multicellular tissues has rarely been reported in the literature. Our study provides an advanced co-culture/differentiation strategy that enables the delivery of unique culture media or growth factors into the bioreactor environment. Furthermore, this approach facilitates the detachment of vascular scaffolds from the surrounding matrix hydrogels, allowing the loading of compartments with different hydrogels according to the specific requirements of the corresponding cells.

A limitation of the present study is the use of immunodeficient nude mice as the implantation model. This animal model was selected primarily to verify the core biological performance of the engineered three-layer skin construct, including tissue survival, vascular infiltration and structural integrity, by eliminating confounding effects of immune rejection on tissue integration and remodeling. However, nude mice lack a functional adaptive immune system, and this immunodeficiency precluded a comprehensive evaluation of the construct's immunogenicity and potential fibrotic encapsulation responses induced by host immune reactions—key biological properties that are critical to assess for subsequent clinical translation. Immunocompetent animal models (e.g., SD rats and C57BL/6 mice) will be employed to comprehensively evaluate the immunogenicity and immunological compatibility of the construct in future studies. Another limitation of this study is that the construct cannot be perfused in vivo for long time. The mechanical strength of the printed vessels is not yet sufficient, making suturing these hydrogels tubes not easy. Beside, endothelial cells failed to achieve full coverage of the inner vascular wall, and the angled folds of the scaffold structure may potentially induce blood flow turbulence and elevate the risk of thrombus formation during in vivo perfusion. In the future, the biomaterial formulation will be modified to improve the mechanical strength of the printed vessels and the design of the vascular network will be optimized to reduce flow resistance. These strategies are expected to alleviate thrombus risk and improve the hemocompatibility of the scaffold for in vivo applications.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel and versatile strategy termed “REST” was introduced for the biofabrication of vascularized tissue with heterogeneous cells. The construct was subjected to vascular perfusion, and a cell-specific microenvironment was provided for different cell populations. The engineered skin flap-like tissues achieved favorable vascularization in vivo. Collectively, the “REST” fabrication strategy is an adaptable technique that represents a fundamental step toward the development of various artificial functional tissues or organs.
