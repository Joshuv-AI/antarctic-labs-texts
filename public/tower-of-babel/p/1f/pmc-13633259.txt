Merlin deficiency supports an immunosuppressive milieu in breast cancer
Authors: Mohamed H Elbahoty, Brandon J Metge, Amr R Elhamamsy, Ian Miranda, Melanie Aldridge, Bohan Ning, Hua Guo, Dongquan Chen, Lalita A Shevde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite substantial advances in early detection and therapeutic strategies, breast cancer remains a major cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide. Changes in cytoskeletal proteins that typically regulate cellular architecture underpin tumor progression. Merlin, encoded by the NF2 gene and a member of the ezrin–radixin–moesin protein family, functions as a critical cytoskeletal linker protein and a tumor suppressor that governs multiple oncogenic pathways and restricts epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity. While Merlin deficiency alters cellular architecture, the precise impact of a Merlin-deficient breast tumor on remodeling its immune microenvironment remains largely undefined. In this study, we demonstrate that in breast cancer, Merlin deficiency displays attributes consistent with enhanced epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity and enrichment of Hedgehog signaling. In vivo, Merlin-deficient tumors displayed accelerated growth and an immunosuppressive milieu characterized by increased abundance of regulatory T cells and M2-like macrophages. Treatment with Vismodegib, a pharmacological Hedgehog pathway inhibitor, reduced tumor growth of Merlin-deficient basal-like tumors, accompanied by a decrease in tumor-infiltrating regulatory T cells and immunosuppressive M2-like macrophages, and increased recruitment of pro-inflammatory M1-like macrophages. The data present Hedgehog inhibition as a targetable vulnerability of Merlin-deficient basal-like, triple-negative breast cancer.Subject terms: Cancer, Breast cancer

Introduction

Despite recent advances in early detection and treatment approaches, including immunotherapy [1], breast cancer remains a major concern due to its heterogeneity and the development of drug resistance. Understanding the molecular underpinnings that drive breast cancer initiation, progression, and metastasis is crucial for the development of more effective and targeted therapies [2].

Tumor growth and metastasis warrant dynamic cytoskeletal changes that enable cancer cells to alter morphology, detach from the primary tumor, invade tissues, and infiltrate distant organs. These changes underpin essential activities such as cell motility, invasion, intravasation, and extravasation [3]. ERM (Ezrin, Radixin, and Moesin) proteins are cytoskeletal linker proteins that connect the plasma membrane to the actin cytoskeleton, regulating cell shape, adhesion, and motility. ERM proteins have numerous crucial functions in tumor development and metastasis, including promoting cytoskeletal remodeling, altering cell polarity, facilitating migratory and invasive behaviors, and activating oncogenic signaling pathways [4]. Merlin, also known as neurofibromin 2 (NF2), is a critical member of the ERM protein family that functions as an important tumor suppressor. First discovered for its involvement in neurofibromatosis type II [5], Merlin modulates a variety of cellular processes, including proliferation, adhesion, and migration, by modifying critical signaling, such as Hippo, PI3K/AKT, receptor tyrosine kinase, and Hedgehog signaling pathways [6, 7].

Merlin interacts with the actin cytoskeleton and membrane-associated proteins to control cellular architecture and polarity; its loss disrupts these structural programs, thus licensing stemness, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), invasion, and dissemination [8, 9]. In breast cancer, Merlin deficiency is associated with enhanced tumor growth, increased metastatic capacity, and treatment resistance [10], and Merlin loss impairs Hippo pathway control, thereby enhancing oncogenic gene expression [7]. Loss of Merlin has been linked to metastasis, with reduced Merlin expression observed in metastatic breast lesions compared to primary tumors [11]. Recent research suggests that Merlin loss may also influence the tumor microenvironment (TME) [12–14] and increase breast cancer heterogeneity [15]. As such, understanding how Merlin deficiency promotes tumor progression may reveal actionable vulnerabilities for precision therapy. However, no targeted treatments currently exist for malignancies driven by Merlin loss, particularly in breast cancer [16, 17]. In this study, we demonstrate that Merlin loss exacerbates breast cancer aggressiveness by licensing EMT-driven progression, enabling immune evasion, and shaping an immune-suppressive microenvironment, while revealing inhibition of Hedgehog signaling as a promising therapeutic strategy for Merlin-deficient breast cancer.

Results

NF2 loss in breast cancer is clinically frequent and predicts poor prognosis

We first explored the landscape and prognostic relevance of NF2 alterations across multiple cancer types using pan-cancer datasets. Across TCGA, NF2 is altered at variable but generally low frequencies, with the highest rates observed in mesothelioma (MESO), uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS), and glioblastoma (GBM). These alterations are dominated by mutations and deep deletions, whereas amplifications are uncommon, consistent with Merlin’s role as a tumor suppressor. In breast cancer and several other malignancies, genomic alterations of NF2 are relatively rare. Instead, NF2 dysregulation appears to arise predominantly from reduced expression rather than coding mutations or structural variants (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Pan-cancer RPPA (Reverse Phase Protein Array) analyses mirrored these findings, revealing widespread NF2 downregulation across epithelial malignancies, including breast, prostate, and uterine cancers (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Pan-cancer survival analyses further demonstrated that NF2-deficient patients experienced significantly worse progression-free survival (HR = 0.804; p < 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. 1C) and overall survival (HR = 0.768; p < 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. 1D). Consistent with this, pan-cancer analysis of whole genomes (PCAWG) also revealed poorer overall survival in NF2-deficient patients (HR = 0.804; p < 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. 1E), reinforcing Merlin’s tumor-suppressive function.

Next, we assessed the prognostic impact of NF2 in The Cancer Genome Atlas Breast Invasive Carcinoma (TCGA-BRCA) dataset. Low NF2 protein expression defined a poor-prognosis subset with significantly worse overall survival (OS; HR = 0.527; p = 0.0243; Fig. 1A). We then interrogated large-scale clinical datasets to characterize NF2 expression patterns in breast cancer. Analysis of CPTAC (Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium) data revealed that NF2 protein levels were significantly lower in primary breast tumors (n = 125) compared with normal breast tissue (n = 18; p = 3.65 × 10⁻¹²; Fig. 1B). Stage-specific analysis revealed progressive downregulation of NF2 from stage I through stage III, with the most pronounced loss observed in stages II and III (p = 2.46 × 10⁻¹³ and p = 2.62 × 10⁻⁹, respectively, Fig. 1C). Across all molecular subtypes, NF2 expression was significantly reduced (Fig. 1D) relative to normal breast.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis of the TCGA-BRCA RPPA (Reverse Phase Protein Array) dataset further demonstrated that patients with low NF2 protein expression had significantly reduced overall survival compared to those with high NF2 expression (HR = 0.6098; log-rank p = 0.0138; Fig. 1E). Consistent with these observations, low NF2 expression also predicted poorer outcomes across multiple clinical endpoints in breast cancer cohorts: shorter relapse-free survival (HR = 0.7336; p < 0.0001; Fig. 1F), overall survival (HR = 0.809; p = 0.0352; Fig. 1G), distant metastasis-free survival (HR = 0.799; p = 0.0058; Fig. 1H), regional recurrence-free survival (HR = 0.696; p = 0.0023; Supplementary Fig. 2A), and inferior post-progression survival (HR = 0.355; p = 0.0396; Supplementary Fig. 2B).

To investigate the biological consequences of Merlin loss, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using transcriptomic data from breast tumors in the context of NF2 expression. Hallmark pathway analysis identified upregulation of EMT alongside key oncogenic programs, including MYC targets, mitotic spindle, E2F targets, glycolysis, and TNFα signaling via NF-κB [18, 19] (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Reactome GSEA demonstrated enrichment of pathways associated with MET-driven cell motility (Supplementary Fig. 2D), whereas KEGG GSEA revealed dysregulation of adherens junction organization and DNA homologous recombination (Supplementary Fig. 2E). Consistent with upregulated EMT signature in Hallmark, GSEA of the TCGA-BRCA dataset revealed significant enrichment of EMT-associated genes in low NF2 tumors (NES = 1.424, p = 0.0122) (Supplementary Fig. 2F). Collectively, these results suggest that loss of NF2 promotes transcriptional reprogramming that enhances invasive potential and oncogenic signaling in breast cancer.

Merlin silencing induces transcriptomic reprogramming of oncogenic and immune-modulatory pathways

Given Merlin’s established roles in cytoskeletal regulation and tumor progression, we first assessed Merlin across a series of breast tumor cells spanning metastatic potential: non-metastatic (67NR), weakly metastatic (4T07), moderately metastatic (66cl.4), aggressive triple-negative (4T1), and their potently metastatic derivative (4T1.2) [20]. A stepwise decrement in Merlin expression was observed in association with increasing metastatic behavior: 66cl.4, 4T1, and 4T1.2, whereas Merlin protein levels in 67NR and 4T07 cells were comparable (Fig. 2A). Guided by these data, we proceeded with 4T07 and 67NR cells for targeted knockdown of Merlin. To engineer Merlin-deficient derivatives, we screened six pGIPZ mouse Nf2 shRNAs spanning the ORF/3′UTR and identified clone V3LMM_487447 as the most potent and reproducible, achieving ~60–65% protein reduction versus non-transfected controls (Fig. 2B). This construct was advanced for stable line generation. We established matched Nf2 knockdown and empty vector controls in 4T07 and 67NR cells (Fig. 2C). These pairs formed the foundation for all downstream functional, transcriptomic, and in vivo studies.

Building on pan-cancer and breast clinical analyses (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2; Fig. 1), which linked low NF2 to progressive disease, poor survival, and enrichment of oncogenic and stemness programs, we next functionally characterized the impact of Merlin loss in our cell models. Bulk RNA-seq was performed in 4T07 and 67NR cells engineered to stably knockdown Nf2 (KD) or empty vector (EV) (Fig. 2C). The knockdown was confirmed by qRT-PCR and immunoblotting (Fig. 2C).

Guided by the EMT signatures observed in our TCGA-BRCA analyses (Supplementary Fig. 2C, F), we interrogated our bulk RNA-seq datasets from 4T07 and 67NR Nf2 KD cells for EMT-specific transcriptional changes. Targeted EMT GSEA demonstrated that Merlin loss in both 4T07 and 67NR cells led to significant enrichment of EMT signatures together with multiple metastasis-associated gene programs (Fig. 2D, E). Collectively, these results show that NF2 loss drives EMT-dominant transcriptional reprogramming, reinforcing Merlin’s essential role in restraining invasive progression.

Unsupervised clustering robustly separated KD from EV in both cell lines, underscoring broad transcriptomic reprogramming (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). Canonical EMT markers were dysregulated: both 4T07 and 67NR KD cells presented with increased Vim (Vimentin) accompanied by changes in Cdh1 (E-cadherin) and Cdh2 (N-cadherin) (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Volcano plots (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B) followed by Hallmark GSEA revealed enrichment of MYC and E2F targets, cholesterol homeostasis, oxidative phosphorylation, KRAS signaling, and negative enrichment of pro-inflammatory networks (Supplementary Fig. 4C, D) [21–35]. These findings were reinforced with Reactome and KEGG analyses, revealing enrichment for cilium assembly, cholesterol biosynthesis, immune-modulatory pathways, and ECM organization/remodeling (Supplementary Fig. 5A–D).

Merlin deficiency elicits an EMT program in breast cancer cells

The transcriptional enrichment of EMT and ECM remodeling pathways observed in NF2-deficient human breast tumors (Supplementary Fig. 2) and our bulk RNA seq analysis (Supplementary Figs. 3–5) provided a compelling rationale to investigate EMT in our model systems. Merlin deficiency resulted in reduced E-cadherin and increased vimentin (VIM) protein expression in both model systems (Fig. 3A). To complement transcriptomic and immunoblotting analyses, we quantified the steady-state levels of EMT-associated transcripts. Both KD models registered decreased E-cadherin and upregulation of N-cadherin, ZEB2, and SNAI2. 67NR KD cells exhibited an increase in VIM (Fig. 3B), supporting a model-specific EMT response. An independent NF2-targeting shRNA validated similar impacts on EMT (Supplementary Fig. 6), cumulatively, informing that Merlin loss elicits an EMT program in breast cancer cells.

To characterize EMT-associated cellular behavior, we functionally queried the 4T07 and 67NR KD cells for migration and invasion. Merlin depletion accelerated wound closure in scratch assays, with KD cells out-migrating EV controls at 48 h in both models (Fig. 4A, B). In transwell assays, Nf2 loss increased the invasive capacity of tumor cells (Fig. 4C, D), and in 3D growth conditions, the KD spheroids exhibited reduced circularity and prominent radial projections (Fig. 4E, F). In agreement with these attributes, supportive of enhanced oncogenic potential, KD cells formed significantly more colonies than EV (Fig. 4G, H). These phenotypic and functional changes were recapitulated with an independent shRNA targeting Nf2 (Supplementary Fig. 7). The EV and Nf2-KD cells proliferated at comparable rates (data not shown), indicating that enhanced migration and invasion are reflective of an acquired program, independent of cell growth. Collectively, these assays demonstrate that Merlin loss elicited alterations functionally consistent with transcriptomic rewiring (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4) and EMT induction (Fig. 3).

Merlin deficiency enhances in vivo tumor growth and remodeling

Building upon the evidence thus far and to model clinical manifestation in immunocompetent models, we established orthotopic mammary tumors using luciferase-labeled 4T07 or 67NR cells stably silenced for Merlin (KD) or transduced as empty vector controls (EV). Merlin depletion significantly accelerated tumor growth in both model systems, with KD tumors displaying greater mean tumor volumes (MTV) than EV controls (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. 8A).

Tumor tissue analysis presented that Merlin loss (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Fig. 8B) was concordant with increased CD31⁺ microvessel density (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Fig. 8C) and higher PCNA positivity (Fig. 5D, Supplementary Fig. 8D), indicating enhanced angiogenesis and proliferation, respectively. In contrast, Annexin V staining was consistently reduced in KD tumors, supporting decreased apoptosis (Fig. 5E, Supplementary Fig. 8E). In order to pathologically ascribe such remarkable changes to enhanced aggressiveness and malignant potential, we assessed the tumor by Argyrophilic Nucleolar Organizer Region (AgNOR) staining. This stain identifies nucleoli that form around ribosomal DNA that is undergoing rapid transcription and is a pathological indicator of increased proliferation, malignancy, and metabolic activity. AgNOR counts were elevated in 4T07 KD tumors (Fig. 5F), indicative of higher tumor grade and proliferative activity [36–38]. 67NR KD tumors trended towards increased AgNOR staining, albeit not significant (Supplementary Fig. 8F); however, the nucleoli were highly irregular, indicative of heightened metabolic activity. These findings underscore a potential role for NF2/Merlin loss in permitting vascularization and malignant potential in vivo.

Merlin deficiency perturbs Hedgehog signaling and imparts selective therapeutic vulnerability to Hedgehog pathway inhibition

Bulk RNA-seq analysis revealed that Merlin loss was associated with significant upregulation of cilium assembly programs and cholesterol biosynthesis (Reactome, Supplementary Fig. 5A). Given that the primary cilium serves as the central hub for Hedgehog (Hh) signaling, providing the platform for PTCH, SMO, and GLI regulation, enhanced ciliogenesis suggested the potential of activated Hh signaling. Prior work from our group demonstrated that Merlin loss-associated oxidative stress spurred Hh activation [7]. We performed GSEA of bulk RNA-seq data from 4T07 and 67NR models using multiple Hh-related gene sets curated in MSigDB. Both cell lines demonstrated enrichment of Hh signaling following Nf2 knockdown, indicating pathway activation (Supplementary Fig. 9A, B). Aligned with these findings, 4T07 KD cells expressed increased GLI1 protein and transcript relative to EV controls (Supplementary Fig. 9C). Surprisingly, 67NR KD cells showed no significant change in GLI1 protein or transcript levels compared with EV controls (Supplementary Figs. 9D, 10). The divergent behavior of 4T07 and 67NR cells can be attributed, at least in part, to their intrinsic molecular phenotypes. While 4T07 cells exhibit a basal-like/triple-negative phenotype, characterized by higher proliferative, invasive, and mesenchymal features [39], 67NR cells display a luminal B/ER⁺ profile, associated with epithelial differentiation and limited metastatic potential [40, 41].

Next, we undertook further analysis to query if Hh activation presents a dependency of Merlin-deficient tumors. 4T07 (EV and KD) cells stably expressing luciferase were implanted orthotopically in the inguinal mammary fat pad (Fig. 6A). Merlin-deficient tumors exhibited a consistent trend of accelerated growth by both caliper measurement and bioluminescence imaging (BLI). Following Vismodegib initiation, normalized MTV (relative to day 1 of Vismodegib treatment) rose steadily in KD tumors and diverged from EV controls between days 6 and 13. Vismodegib had little effect on EV tumors but significantly curtailed KD growth (Fig. 6B; Supplementary Fig. 11A). Waterfall plots depicting percent change in MTV and MTD showed consistent increase in tumor growth of KD DMSO versus EV DMSO, with a broad leftward shift (growth restraint) in KD Vismodegib-treated tumors, consistent with vulnerability to Hh inhibition upon Merlin loss (Fig. 6C; Supplementary Fig. 11B, C). Endpoint BLI mirrored these findings: KD tumors exhibited intense signals that were reduced by Vismodegib, whereas EV signals remained low and unaltered (Fig. 6D).

We conducted similar analysis with EV or Nf2 KD 67NR cells (Supplementary Fig. 12A). KD tumors were consistently larger than EV counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 12B). Longitudinal measurements indicated that KD tumors expanded more rapidly than EV controls, with clear divergence in MTV from day 18 onward (Supplementary Fig. 12C). In contrast to the robust drug sensitivity observed in 4T07 tumors, Vismodegib produced a modest, non-significant growth delay in Nf2-deficient 67NR tumors while remarkably restraining EV tumor outgrowth, suggestive of reduced reliance on canonical Hh despite Merlin loss. In summary, while Merlin loss accelerated tumor growth in both models, the basal-like 4T07 tumors displayed vulnerability to Hh inhibition, suggestive of a subtype-specific therapeutic dependency.

The immunosuppressive tumor milieu of Merlin-deficient tumors is remodeled by Hedgehog pathway inhibition

Hh inhibition reshapes the TIME of breast cancer [42, 43]. Building on the sensitivity of 4T07 KD tumors to Hh inhibition (Fig. 6, Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12), we first assessed the cytokine profile of 4T07 EV and KD tumors treated with vehicle control (DMSO) or Vismodegib. In the vehicle-control groups, relative to EV tumors, the KD tumors showed significant upregulation of factors linked to myeloid recruitment and matrix remodeling (RBP4, Lipocalin-2, Endostatin, MPO, VCAM-1, MMP-3, CD14 and Angiopoietin-1) and suppression of cytokines which are associated with immune cell trafficking and stromal inflammatory signaling (CCL22, LIX, Pentraxin 2, FLT3 Ligand, G-CSF and CXCL family members) (Fig. 7A–C). The differential enrichment of cytokines impinged upon TNF, MAPK, and PI3K-AKT signaling, IL-1 and IL-2 family signaling, and HIF-1 signaling (Supplementary Fig. 13A).

Vismodegib treatment elicited changes in the cytokine profile of KD tumors marked by an increase in inflammatory and matrix remodeling mediators (CXCL1, LIX, CCL11, ECGF, MMP-3, CXCL2, G-CSF, and MMP-9) and decreased inflammatory and immunoregulation-associated factors (Resistin, Myeloperoxidase, CD40, and CXCL13) (Fig. 7D–F), with corresponding enrichment of chemokine signaling, IL-17, and IL-1 pathways, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Supplementary Fig. 13B).

We next assessed the tumors for the immunosuppressive cells, namely, regulatory T cells (Tregs) and anti-inflammatory M2-like macrophages. In 4T07 tumors, Nf2 knockdown did not perturb the relative abundance of CD4⁺ Foxp3⁺ T cells (Tregs) (Fig. 8A), CD206⁺ M2-like macrophages, or inflammatory CD80⁺ M1-like macrophages (Fig. 8B). Vismodegib elicited a remarkable reduction in the abundance of Tregs and M2-like macrophages in the KD tumors (Fig. 8A, B) simultaneous with a significant increase in tumor-infiltrating M1-like macrophages in KD tumors, with little effect in EV tumors (Fig. 8B). Proportionately examined, Vismodegib-treated KD tumors exhibited a significant increase in inflammatory macrophages (Fig. 8C).

Substantiating these assessments, we also evaluated the tumor tissues by IHC. We stained the tissue sections for Foxp3 to visualize Tregs. In alignment with flow cytometry-immunophenotyping, staining the tumor tissues for Foxp3 demonstrated higher abundance of Tregs in KD tumors and their depletion following Vismodegib therapy (Fig. 8D). Abundant CD206⁺ macrophages were observed in both groups with a treatment-dependent increase in CD80⁺ cells only in KD tumors (Fig. 8E, F). Taken together, these results demonstrate that Vismodegib reprograms the TIME of 4T07 Nf2-deficient tumors by decreasing Tregs and suppressive macrophages and boosting inflammatory macrophages. In the 67NR model, Merlin status did not impact Tregs or macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 14A, B). Vismodegib significantly reduced Tregs and M2-like macrophages in both EV and KD tumors and significantly increased CD80⁺ M1-like macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 14A, B). Hh inhibition markedly altered the influx of M1 macrophages to EV and KD tumors with a concomitant reduction in M2 macrophages. Vismodegib significantly increased the proportion of M1 macrophages relative to M2 macrophages only in the KD tumors (Supplementary Fig. 14C). We also assessed the impact of Vismodegib on tumor-infiltrating Th1 cells and cytotoxic T cells, both of which orchestrate anti-tumor responses. Interestingly, Vismodegib treatment elicited an increase in CD4+ Tbet+ Th1 cells in 4T07 KD and 67NR KD tumors (Supplementary Figs. 15A and 16A) without remarkable changes in CD8+ Granzyme B+ cytotoxic T cells (Supplementary Figs. 15B and 16B). Collectively, the data indicate that Merlin loss is associated with features of an immunosuppressive TIME potentially linked to Hh activity characterized by Treg expansion, an M2-like macrophage bias, and cytokine milieu that promotes myeloid recruitment and matrix remodeling.

Discussion

Merlin, the protein product of NF2, is recognized as a pivotal tumor suppressor in multiple tissue contexts [44]. Large-scale pan-cancer analyses demonstrate frequent Merlin loss across epithelial malignancies, including breast carcinoma, malignant mesothelioma, papillary renal cell carcinoma, and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [45]. Here, we integrated clinical-omics data with functional and experimental studies in two immunocompetent breast cancer models, triple-negative 4T07 and luminal B/ER⁺ 67NR cells, and showed that Merlin deficiency is associated with oncogenic signaling activation, reshaping the TIME, and subtype-specific therapeutic vulnerability.

Merlin loss consistently promoted aggressive tumor behaviors, as reflected by increased migration, invasion, 3D spheroid expansion, and colony formation. Analogous increases in proliferative and migratory capacity have also been documented when Merlin is lost in hepatocellular carcinoma [46], glioblastoma [47], and meningioma [48].

A consistent pattern emerging from the bioinformatic interrogation of patient cohorts and our in vitro and in vivo experiments is the linkage between Merlin loss and an EMT program. Within TCGA and CPTAC datasets, tumors harboring low Merlin expression displayed significant enrichment of Hallmark EMT, MYC, and E2F gene sets. Merlin deficiency led to downregulation of epithelial determinants (e.g., CDH1) and upregulation of mesenchymal markers (VIM, CDH2) together with EMT-driving transcription factors (SNAI2, ZEB2) [49]. These alterations were functionally reflected as accelerated wound closure and increased invasion, hallmarks of a migratory invasive state consistent with EMT [50].

In vivo, in murine models of breast cancer, Merlin deficiency accelerated tumor growth, characterized by increased angiogenesis and resistance to apoptosis. This behavior mirrors patterns reported in other malignancies, including mesothelioma, glioblastoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and non-small cell lung cancer, where Merlin deficiency similarly fuels unchecked growth, vascular remodeling, and survival advantages [44, 51]. As such, given the aggressive biology associated with Merlin loss and the scarcity of effective targeted options, especially in breast cancer, there is a pressing need to explore new therapeutic strategies.

The present study demonstrates that the EMT-associated transcriptional and functional changes driven by Merlin loss provide a biological framework for understanding its impact on tumor progression in vivo. Merlin-deficient tumors harbored increased immunosuppressive tumor-infiltrating Foxp3⁺ Tregs and CD206⁺ M2-like macrophages, a pattern reminiscent of other Merlin-deficient malignancies [52]. Complementing the immune cell shifts that we observed, Merlin KD upregulated myeloid-recruiting, inflammatory, and matrix remodeling mediators (e.g., RBP4, Lipocalin-2, Endostatin, MPO, VCAM-1, MMP-3, CD14, and Angiopoietin-1) while decreasing mediators for inflammatory T cell trafficking and stromal inflammatory signaling (e.g., CCL22, LIX, Pentraxin 2, FLt3 Ligand, G-CSF, and CXCL family members), consequently generating an immunosuppressive milieu with a myeloid skewed state. Functionally, this signature is associated with enhanced myeloid recruitment and M2 polarization (via factors such as M-CSF and IL-10) while ANGPT1/VCAM-1 supports endothelial activation and myeloid tethering. Similar cytokine landscapes have been reported in pleural mesothelioma [53] and vestibular schwannoma [52], where Merlin deficiency is usually associated with macrophage-rich, lymphocyte-poor, and dysfunctional T-cell microenvironments [52]. Aligning with our previous study [7], bulk RNA-seq and GSEA uncovered enrichment of Hh-related gene sets in Merlin-deficient triple-negative 4T07 tumors, which associated with sensitivity to Vismodegib, supporting the potential therapeutic relevance of this clinically approved SMO inhibitor for Merlin-deficient triple-negative breast cancer. Supporting this possibility, Vismodegib alleviated the immunosuppressive tumor milieu by depleting Tregs and M2-like (pro-tumor) macrophages while promoting M1-like (anti-tumor) macrophages, thereby fostering a more inflammatory and immune-stimulating microenvironment. These current findings extend prior foundational work by demonstrating that pharmacologic Hh blockade induces immune reprogramming across mammary tumors representing basal-like and luminal B/ERα⁺ subtypes [43, 54].

In both models, Hh inhibition led to reduction of tumor-infiltrating Treg cells and M2-like macrophages, expansion of M1-like macrophages, and a cytokine profile aligned with an inflammatory tumor immune environment, demonstrating that Hh pathway blockade can alleviate the immunosuppression induced by Merlin loss.
