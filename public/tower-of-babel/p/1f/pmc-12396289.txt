Facing the challenge maxillofacial penetrating teta injury: A case report
Authors: Shuvro Saha, Kanij Fatema Ishrat Zahan, Kazi Irfan Subhan, Gazi M. Habibullah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
When a deadly non-missile object like a teta gets impacted in the maxillofacial region, the management becomes challenging. This region has a complex anatomical structure with high vascularity and contains vital structures like the orbit and airway. To avoid ophthalmic or neurological abnormalities in addition to the risk of infection and bleeding, accurate clinical and radiological diagnosis and optimal surgical techniques with postoperative management are crucial. This paper emphasizes the significance of careful preoperative planning and a concerted effort of the multidisciplinary team in managing penetrating maxillofacial injuries.A 26-year-old man was admitted under the Department of Neurosurgery at Dhaka Medical College Hospital (DMCH) with a teta impacted in his face just below the right lower eyelid, which travelled through the area of the right maxillary sinus, passing the internal carotid artery (ICA) and rested in the middle part of clivus just before basilar artery. We successfully removed the teta without any vital structure injury or visual problem.Penetrating injury in the maxillofacial region with teta due to interpersonal violence is uncommon because of the presence of protecting reflexes, and there is a dearth of documentation in the literature. The presentation, diagnosis, risk evaluation, surgical management, and outcome made this case unique.

Introduction

A penetrating facial injury occurs in either a missile or non-missile pattern. Non-missile penetrating injuries are an essential subset of penetrating facial injuries where a low-velocity sharp object penetrates the hard and soft tissues and remains confined within the maxillofacial skeleton, causing localized damage along the tract of the offending object that is not associated with the thermal and kinetic components found in high velocity penetrating injuries [1]. The culprit object is considered a foreign body and can cause harm to the vasculature and the tissues within the orbit [2]. The object may be wood, knife, nail, metal, or teta. Teta is a long metallic spear-like traditional instrument with a long wooden or bamboo-made handle typically fitted with sharp points, barbs, or toggling mechanisms to catch fish in shallow water [3]. This non-missile type traditional instrument from northeastern Bangladesh is often misused in interpersonal or intergroup violence [3]. It is very uncommon for patients with the impacted weapon or foreign body to present for surgical intervention. However, impacted object causes difficulties in the investigation and standard operating procedures [4]. Around the world, accidental injury is the most common cause of non-missile penetrating injury [5]. But in Bangladesh, interpersonal or intergroup violence is the most common cause where males are more affected [4]. Due to protective reflexes, which can help divert from the attack, the incidence of maxillofacial injury is less common [6]. Risk is enormous due to the complex anatomical foundation at the maxillofacial region. Primary injury can cause neurological deficit, visual loss and even death, whereas secondary injury may cause vascular lesions and infections [7]. A multidisciplinary approach is needed for proper management [6]. In Dhaka Medical College Hospital (DMCH), Dhaka, Bangladesh under the department of Neurosurgery, we successfully removed the teta without any neurological deficits or visual impairment. This case was written in the line with the Surgical Case Report Guidelines (SCARE Guideline) 2023 [8].

Case presentation

A 26-year-old man came to the emergency department with an impacted teta on his face just below the right eye (Fig. 1). He was referred from a secondary care hospital to Dhaka Medical College Hospital (DMCH), Dhaka, Bangladesh for multidisciplinary management. The patient was shot with a teta during a fight with other individuals. The patient initially appeared to be panicked and anxious-looking. He was well-oriented and stable on neurological examination, with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of E4V5M6. There were no motor deficits. Ipsilateral hemifacial numbness was present. All the cranial nerves were intact except the sensory division of trigeminal nerve. There was unrestricted eye movement, with the pupils reacting to light. However, the patient could not walk independently due to the weight of the teta and the obstruction of his right sided vision by the impacted teta. General, systemic and ophthalmologic examination revealed no abnormalities. On local examination, a penetrating wound just below the right eyelid measuring approximately 1 ∗ 1 cm or resembling Zone 2 based on the Turbin classification [9,10]. Initial X-ray skull showed impacted teta through the face (Fig. 2). Computed tomography (CT) scan of the brain with bone window view and 3-D reconstruction views revealed that the teta penetrated the right orbital floor, transversed the maxillary sinus medially and finally impacted in the middle (sphenoid) part of the clivus just before basilar artery (Fig. 3). The middle part of the clivus is laterally bounded by the paraclival internal carotid arteries (ICAs), the foramen lacerum and petroclival fissure. At the level of the middle clivus the ICA enters the cavernous sinus. Management

After taking informed consent, the patient was brought to the emergency operation theatre from admission room to remove the teta. The patient was positioned supine under general anaesthesia (GA). Adequate draping and padding after an antiseptic wash were initiated. A 1 cm transverse incision was made with a no. 15 BP blade at the entry wound (Fig. 5). The impacted teta was removed using the knockout technique [5], where a vice grip was attached perpendicular to the retained portion of the teta and then gently tapped with a hammer with stabilization of the head to remove the teta (Fig. 6). As the impacted teta was hooked, the exit trajectory was same as the entry trajectory for harmless removal in knockout technique. Before that, the teta was cut short with a metal cutting saw for a better working experience. Very minimal bleeding was noticed, and no adjacent structure was damaged. Copious regular saline irrigation was given after the removal of the teta. Adequate hemostasis was ensured and closed by layers. The patient got empirical, broad-spectrum, antibiotic, and tetanus prophylaxis. The postoperative period was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on the fifth postoperative day in good clinical condition with no signs of infection, no new neurological deficit or neuro-ophthalmic problems (Fig. 7). During discharge, the patient was advised to complete the tetanus vaccination for prevention of tetanus, oral antibiotic and one month later follow-up. On his regular follow-up at outpatient department (OPD) to us after one month he was doing very well (Fig. 8) but the hemifacial numbness persisted. Discussion

The penetrating injuries of the skull and brain represent approximately 0.4 % of all head injuries, a rarity in Western countries [4]. The management of the penetrating facial injury depends on several factors, such as aetiology, the nature of the retained foreign object, the injury's location, and the damage to the hard and soft tissues [2]. Immediate clinical assessment, diagnostic imaging, adjuvant anti-microbial therapy, foreign object removal, early wound exploration, wound debridement, and hard and soft tissue repair are indicated for penetrating facial injury patients [11]. Immediate life-threatening complications like airway obstruction, breathing problems, haemorrhage, and shock should be accessed and promptly managed under the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) protocol. The extent of the injury is assumed when the patient is stabilized. After the radiological investigation is completed, proper visualization clears. Plain radiographs are taken in two different planes to identify foreign bodies. A plain X-ray is essential in visualizing either a hooked or not metallic tip pattern. In the case of the knockout-removal technique, this information is very crucial. The panoramic view of the scenario is established after a CT scan of the brain with a bone window view and a 3-D reconstruction view. All information is gathered from CT scan images, impacted foreign body, extension of the lesion, localization of the foreign body and bone fragments, hematomas, penetration pathway, other relevant lesions and surgical planning. Angiography may be an important diagnostic tool in penetrating maxillofacial injuries to rule out vascular injury, when clinically indicated [12]. Removing any annoying foreign bodies, in addition to any devitalized tissue, as quickly and safely as possible is the fast goal of resuscitation. Standard practices include adequate hemostasis, minimizing brain injury, and removing metal items under direct visualization. Chrcanovic BR et al. suggested managing this case in the operating theatre and warned against attempting it at the bed site [13]. Anatomically complex structured and highly vascular maxillofacial region has a pneumatized cavity, and resistant support is suited to dilute shocks. This advantageous anatomical construction results in less injury to the adjacent vital structures. Moreover, the presence of protective reflexes diverts the attack in this region. The surgical plan should involve the most straightforward route for removing the foreign body, usually through the entrance wound and the tract created by the object. Sometimes, the entry wound may be extended to facilitate adequate exploration of the tract and uneventful removal of the object. BM Harrington et al. suggested a knockout technique for the removal of retained foreign bodies in case of penetrating head injuries [5]. They used the knockout technique under local anaesthesia for the patient with GCS 15 and the knockout technique under general anaesthesia for the patient with a GCS score <15. Primary treatment should be prompt, ideally within 48 h of injury. Delayed surgery increases the risk of infection, the entrance tract becomes obliterated, and the object gets surrounded by thick fibrous tissue, making it difficult to remove the object [13]. Non-missile low-velocity penetrating facial injury is favourable for early treatment. However, delayed treatment may be applied in case of contaminated, infected lacerations, complex soft tissue injuries, and oedema [13]. Assessment, along with prevention of infection, is very crucial for this highly vascular region injury. Preoperative and postoperative antibiotic coverage with tetanus injections must be considered [15]. Chowdhury FH and Hague MR reported 17 cases of non-missile penetrating injuries, of which only four were due to teta injury [4]. Several factors have been identified for the outcomes of non-missile penetrating injury, such as GCS score, weapon in situ or not, vascular injury, eloquent brain involvement, and time interval of intervention after occurrence [5]. The pattern, position and trajectory of the tip of a teta is very important to know for safe removal as it may be hooked. For uneventful removal, the entry and exit trajectory should be same to reduce the chance of injury to the adjacent structures. GCS score on admission 15 with an in situ teta, no vascular and eloquent brain injury, 12 h intervals of intervention after occurrence, applying knock out technique made successful management of this infrequent maxillofacial penetrating injury by teta.

Conclusion

Like the complex anatomical structure of the maxillofacial region, management is also a complex task. Hooked foreign bodies like teta make this procedure more challenging. It is essential to assess the extent of penetration, identify the adjacent structures, and remove the impacted teta. No secondary injury during removal should be the prime goal of surgery.
