Colouring dysbiosis: FetB-dependent Mn-PPIX produced by Porphyromonas gingivalis shapes the oral microbiota
Authors: Yupaporn Phonok, Alysha Pyne, Siyuan Liu, Sandro F Ataide, Haijing Gu, Xiaoyan Zhou, Jinlong Gao, Ann H Kwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolymicrobial diseases, such as periodontitis, are shaped by microbial interactions and nutrient‑responsive metabolic adaptations. Here, we identify novel porphyrin pigments produced by Porphyromonas gingivalis in response to haemoglobin concentrations resembling those found in the gingival sulcus and periodontal pocket. Under these conditions, P. gingivalis develops a distinctive pink, highly fluorescent phenotype. Spectroscopic and mass‑spectrometric analyses revealed the accumulation of protoporphyrin IX (PPIX), manganese‑substituted PPIX (Mn‑PPIX), and haem. Deletion of the haemin‑binding protein gene fetB greatly reduced Mn‑PPIX production and fluorescence, whereas complementation restored both. Structural and biochemical characterisation demonstrated that FetB shares homology with bacterial metal chelatases and catalyses the insertion of metal ions into tetrapyrrole rings. Mn‑PPIX exhibited selective antimicrobial activity against several oral bacteria, including Streptococcus mitis, Streptococcus salivarius, Enterococcus faecalis, and oral Lactobacillus species, while other streptococci were resistant. These findings indicate that P. gingivalis modulates porphyrin metabolism in response to host‑derived haemoglobin, producing extracellular pigments with selective antimicrobial properties that may shape the oral community and promote dysbiosis. Targeting Mn‑PPIX production or limiting haemoglobin availability may offer new strategies to restore microbial balance and mitigate disease progression.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Microbiology

Introduction

Polymicrobial diseases such as periodontitis involve diverse communities of microorganisms that act collectively to build pathogenic consortia. Ecological interactions in the polymicrobial consortium occur when organisms affect each other’s survival and proliferation. Such interactions could manifest as the production and release of extracellular metabolites that specifically impact other community members. Extracellular pigments, a class of extracellular metabolites absorbing light and presenting with various shades of colours, exhibit a range of biological functions in shaping the polymicrobial community. For example, the membrane-bound yellow pigment staphyloxanthin produced by Staphylococcus aureus provides cross-species protection against neutrophil attack during co-infections in vivo1. The blue-green pigment pyocyanin released by Pseudomonas aeruginosa contributes to inhibiting the growth of competing microorganisms by generating reactive oxygen species2. This dynamic interaction is intensified in a nutrient-responsive environment when bacteria infect the host3. The production and accumulation of bacterial extracellular metabolites, including pigments, are generally affected by nutrient cues4. In the case of pyocyanin, P. aeruginosa enhances its production in response to the presence of aromatic amino acids in the cystic fibrosis sputum5.

Periodontitis is one of the most common chronic inflammatory diseases caused by a dysbiotic oral bacterial consortium affecting the supporting structures of the teeth. The Gram-negative anaerobe Porphyromonas gingivalis is a “keystone” pathobiont of periodontitis, which can orchestrate the host's innate immune system and contribute to the dysbiotic shift of the oral microbiota associated with disease pathogenesis6. Meanwhile, the ecological shift resulting from local inflammation is also pivotal for the development of dysbiosis, leading to the progression from reversible inflammation and tissue alterations associated with gingivitis to irreversible tissue damage seen in periodontitis7. In addition, accumulating evidence suggests that the established infection with P. gingivalis in the oral cavity is associated with chronic systemic diseases with significant health burden, including Alzheimer’s disease, rheumatoid arthritis, atherosclerosis, and even cancers8–11.

As a porphyrin auxotroph, the survival and growth of P. gingivalis are dependent on exogenous haem (iron protoporphyrin IX), predominantly from host haemoglobin. When growing on conventional complex media supplemented with abundant blood and haemin, P. gingivalis biofilms accumulate haem on the cell surface and are characterised by black pigmentation. In contrast to such ex vivo growth conditions in the laboratory, the landscape of nutrient availability varies significantly in vivo. In the healthy periodontal state, free haemoglobin is scarce as the host sequesters haemoglobin released from erythrocytes to restrict haem availability and limit the growth of invading pathogens12. During periodontitis, despite severe inflammation and extensive tissue damage, causing bleeding, which helps to enrich the local environment with haemoglobin, its concentration remains significantly lower than that typically used in standard laboratory culture conditions13. In response to nutrient cues such as haem availability, P. gingivalis exhibited multiple adaptive responses, including morphological changes, alterations in gene and protein expression, and modulation of virulence14,15.

Here, we discovered P. gingivalis produces novel porphyrin pigments in response to varying haemoglobin availability. We further explored the structure and function of outer membrane haemin-binding protein FetB as the potential chelatase in the production of these novel pigments. We also examined the potential of P. gingivalis in manipulating the composition of the polymicrobial community during disease onset and progression by deploying extracellular pigment with selective antimicrobial properties.

Results

Isolation and characterisation of novel pigments produced by Porphyromonas gingivalis under varying haemoglobin concentrations

To mimic the nutrient cues available in periodontium under healthy and disease states, P. gingivalis was cultured on agar under different haemoglobin concentrations. As shown in Fig. 1A, various pigmentation phenotypes were observed. Black pigmentation developed under high haemoglobin concentrations (>20 μM), in agreement with the phenotype observed under conventional biofilm growth conditions with haem-rich medium. Beige cell pellets were observed for P. gingivalis growing under low haemoglobin concentrations (<1.25 μM). Interestingly, a novel pink pigmentation was observed when P. gingivalis was cultured under intermediate levels (1.25 μM and 2.5 μM), similar to the levels of haemoglobin available in the gingival sulcus and periodontal pocket13. The pink P. gingivalis cell pellet harvested from the agar plate emitted strong fluorescence under Wood’s light (Fig. 1A). To further investigate the fluorescence phenotype, the number of P. gingivalis cells harvested from different haemoglobin concentrations was normalised, and the fluorescence intensities were recorded (excitation = 365 nm, emission = 638 nm). As shown in Fig. 1B, P. gingivalis cells from the 2.5 μM haemoglobin group showed the highest fluorescence intensity among all groups, while bacterial cells grown with the lowest and the highest haemoglobin supplementation (<~0.625 μM and >~10 μM, respectively) fluoresced significantly less. The reduced fluorescence at the highest haemoglobin concentrations likely reflects quenching of the fluorescence due to increasing accumulated haem on the P. gingivalis cell surface. An addition of 100 μM dipyridyl to limit iron availability resulted in an increase of fluorescence intensity in all haemoglobin groups, but the pattern remained similar (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

To identify the compounds responsible for the novel pink pigmentation, the pigment was extracted and subject to analytical characterisation. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2, crude pigment extract exhibited properties commonly found in porphyrin-based compounds, including high hydrophobicity, distinctive UV-Vis absorption and fluorescence spectral profiles. Upon further separation with reverse phase high-performance liquid chromatography (rp-HPLC), four major peaks were isolated from the pigment extract (Fig. 1C). Identification of these compounds using UV-Vis spectrum, MALDI-TOF MS, and ESI MS revealed three compounds with a retention time of 41.2, 42, and 48.4 min to be manganese protoporphyrin IX (m/z = 615.16), haem (m/z = 616.18), and protoporphyrin IX (m/z = 563.24), respectively (Fig. 1D). The peak eluted at 51.2 min (Compound D) appeared to be a protoporphyrin IX-based compound, but we could not unambiguously identify what it was.

Identification and structural characterisation of the FetB chelatase involved in novel pigment formation

The novel chromatic and fluorescent phenotype of P. gingivalis resulting from the accumulation of PPIX and Mn-PPIX on the bacterial surface suggested that P. gingivalis can modify the tetrapyrrole rings in response to iron and haem availability. Chelatase, an enzyme that catalyses the insertion of metal ions into a tetrapyrrole ring, would likely be required by P. gingivalis to achieve the synthesis of Mn-PPIX. We conducted a bioinformatic search for potential chelatase homologues in the genome of the P. gingivalis wild-type strain W83 using known chelatases. The match with the highest score was found to be sirohydrochlorin cobaltochelatase/haem-binding protein FetB (WP_005873542.1) of P. gingivalis with 37% sequence identity to CbiK from S. enterica serovar Typhimurium (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). FetB was previously characterised as an outer membrane iron transport protein that possesses potential ferrochelatase activities16.

To further characterise the protein, recombinant FetB with an N-terminal His-tag was overexpressed in Escherichia coli. FetB eluted as a single monodisperse peak on size-exclusion chromatography (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, the expressed FetB consists of a mixture of apo- and holo- forms, the latter of which was associated with porphyrin-based compounds according to the UV-Vis spectral pattern (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). We optimised the expression conditions to obtain predominantly holo- and apo-FetB preparations (SI Appendix, Table S3) and subjected both to crystallisation attempts. Only apo-FetB produced diffracting crystals. Desulfovibrio vulgaris periplasmic CbiK (PDB ID: 2XVX) was used to solve the structure of FetB by molecular replacement. P. gingivalis FetB structure was solved to a resolution of 2.14 Å (PDB ID 9B86). The summary of structure statistics is shown in SI Appendix, Table S4. Apart from 13 residues at the N-terminus and 3 residues at the C-terminus, the entire 269-residue protein can be observed in the electron-density map. The structure contains two domains with a similar core structure present in α/β topology connected by a loop resulting in a cleft between the two domains (Fig. 2B). No ligand was observed except for magnesium and glycerol from the crystallisation condition. The N-terminal domain (residues 1‒138) consists of a four-stranded parallel β sheet arranged as β2-β1-β3-β4 surrounded by four α helices, while the C-terminal domain (residues 146‒269) shares a similar topology except for an extra C-terminal helix near the α1 helix at the N-terminal domain. The structure of FetB is highly similar to the periplasmic Cbik from D. vulgaris, with a backbone average root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 1.291 Å between the two structures (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Therefore, FetB might also function as a chelatase.

Next, the chelatase activities of apo-FetB with a range of metals were explored using deuteroporphyrin IX 2,4-disulfonic acid dimethyl ester (DPIX-DME), a PPIX analogue (SI Appendix, Fig. S6) with improved aqueous solubility and stability. Upon addition of Co(II) and Mn(II), the UV-Vis spectra showed significant changes with characteristics of metal-conjugated porphyrins becoming more prominent (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). FetB possesses cobalt and manganese chelatase activity, and the rate of chelatase activity accelerated with the increasing apo-FetB concentration (Fig. 2D). However, the chelatase activity was not observed for Fe(II) and Ni(II) under the experimental condition tested (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C, D). The reverse chelatase activity of apo-FetB was also not observed with haemin (SI Appendix, Fig. S8).

Deletion of the fetB gene diminished the production of Mn-PPIX pigment by Porphyromonas gingivalis

Recognising that the production of Mn-PPIX was responsive to the haemoglobin cues, fetB gene expression was assessed under varying haemoglobin concentrations using qPCR. As shown in Fig. 3A, the expression level of fetB was also responsive to haemoglobin levels, with the highest expression observed with 3.3−8.3 µM haemoglobin supplemented in the medium, corresponding to the novel pigment phenotype.

To evaluate the impact of the fetB gene on Mn-PPIX production, the fetB deletion mutant (ΔfetB) and complementary mutant (ΔfetB + ) were constructed (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). The formation and composition of extracellular pigments were investigated and compared between the wild type, ΔfetB mutant, and the ΔfetB+ mutant. In comparison with the wild-type strain, the fluorescent intensity (excitation 365 nm, emission 638 nm) of the ΔfetB mutant with normalised bacterial cell number was significantly reduced but not diminished completely (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A, B), suggesting the fetB gene is involved in the pigment production but not the sole gene responsible for all pigment production. There was no difference in chromatic pigmentation phenotype between the wildtype and the mutants, where black pigmentation was observed with 80 µM haemoglobin and pink pigmentation with 2 µM haemoglobin (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C, D). Notably, two peaks in rp-HPLC corresponding to manganese protoporphyrin IX and haem were absent in the pigment extracts from ΔfetB mutant (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A). However, protoporphyrin IX and protoporphyrin IX-based compounds found on W83 are still present in ΔfetB (SI Appendix, Fig. S12B, C). Restoration of the fetB gene resulted in regaining the ability of P. gingivalis to produce Mn-PPIX as seen in the ΔfetB + (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S11B).

The selective antibacterial activity of Mn-PPIX contributes to the keystone property of Porphyromonas gingivalis in orchestrating the polymicrobial community

To evaluate the potential antimicrobial activity of Mn-PPIX, inhibition assays were conducted using a panel of bacterial species commonly found in the oral cavity (Fig. 4A). Mn-PPIX, at concentrations ranging from 10 mg/mL to 0.01 mg/mL, along with 20% chlorhexidine digluconate (positive control) and DMSO (negative control), was spotted onto bacterial lawns. Mn-PPIX produced distinct inhibition zones against Streptococcus mitis, Streptococcus salivarius, Enterococcus faecalis, and oral Lactobacillus species (Fig. 4B, SI Appendix, Fig. S13, Table S5). For these species, as expected, the inhibition zone size decreased progressively with lower Mn-PPIX concentrations. Notably, even at 0.01 mg/mL, Mn-PPIX retained measurable inhibitory activity. In contrast, no inhibition zones were observed for Streptococcus oralis, Streptococcus gordonii, Streptococcus sanguinis, Streptococcus mutans, and Serratia marcescens, even at the highest concentration tested (10 mg/mL) (Fig. 4C, SI Appendix, Fig. S13, Table S5). These findings suggest that Mn-PPIX selectively targets specific bacterial species, and this selectivity may underlie the keystone role of P. gingivalis in modulating the composition and dynamics of the oral polymicrobial community in response to the environmental nutrient cues.

Discussion

Bacteria have evolved diverse strategies to adapt, survive, and proliferate at the host-microbe interface, where the availability of essential nutrients fluctuates between health and disease. In this study, we demonstrate that the periodontal pathobiont P. gingivalis exhibits distinct pigmentation phenotypes in response to varying haemoglobin concentrations during biofilm growth. Notably, a novel pink pigmentation emerged under intermediate haemoglobin levels, resembling those observed in vivo across healthy and disease states. The pink pigmentation was found to result from the formation and accumulation of non-haem porphyrins, including PPIX and Mn-PPIX, expanding on previous findings that linked black pigmentation to the accumulation of monomeric and µ-oxo dimeric haem on the outer membrane surface17.

The ability of P. gingivalis to manipulate and synthesise porphyrins may enhance its virulence and pathogenicity, by enabling the substitution of iron-containing porphyrins with manganese analogues in key enzymes18. Iron is the most common metal utilised by bacteria in biomolecular complexes and biochemical pathways, including in iron-sulfur clusters essential to cellular respiration. However, its susceptibility to oxidation in oxygen-rich environments can exacerbate oxidative stress. Manganese, by contrast, is more resistant to oxidation and can functionally replace iron in certain enzymes under oxidative conditions12,19. For example, in E. coli during hydrogen peroxide stress, HemF, a manganese-containing isozyme of the iron-containing HemN in the haem biosynthesis pathway, is induced. Consistent with our finding that P. gingivalis accumulates Mn-PPIX on its surface, manganese has been shown to protect P. gingivalis against oxidative stress and is incorporated in enzymes that play critical roles in virulence and survival in highly oxidative environments20.

Accumulation of porphyrins has also been reported in other bacterial species such as Corynebacterium diphtheriae21, Cutibacterium species22 and E. coli23. The production of porphyrin pigments has been suggested to be associated with nutrient cues and related to virulence21. Cutibacterium acnes enhances porphyrin production in the presence of Vitamin B12 in the medium22. The accumulated PPIX contributes to the virulence of C. acnes in the development of skin infections by activating the NRLP3 inflammasome assembly through inducing K+ leakage of the keratinocytes24. Similarly, clinical isolates of C. diphtheriae produce high levels of extracellular PPIX, which may modulate biofilm formation and host immune responses, contributing to invasive disease21. Given the known proinflammatory properties of PPIX, it is plausible that P. gingivalis may exploit this metabolite to modulate host inflammation. P. gingivalis has been shown to influence NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL-1β release in periodontal disease, with lipopolysaccharide proposed as a potential trigger25,26. Our findings suggest that PPIX may act in concert with other virulence factors to sustain chronic inflammation and promote tissue destruction, thereby sustaining nutrient availability, acquisition, and persistence within the host. Beyond host modulation, extracellular porphyrins may also influence the structure and dynamics of polymicrobial communities. In the gut, porphyrins produced by E. coli support the growth of Bacteroides spp. through metabolite cross-feeding. In the oral cavity, accumulated PPIX and Mn-PPIX may serve as nutrient reservoirs for porphyrin-auxotrophic pathobionts such as Tannerella forsythia and Prevotella intermedia, potentially explaining their frequent co-occurrence with P. gingivalis in disease-associated biofilms7.

On the other hand, the released extracellular metabolites could also be deployed as arsenals to influence and shape the composition of the polymicrobial community. The pyocyanin produced by P. aeruginosa demonstrated antimicrobial effects against Streptococcus pneumoniae, which also shares the local niche of polymicrobial airway biofilms. Similarly, non-iron metal protoporphyrins have been reported to possess antimicrobial properties27. The antimicrobial property of Mn-PPIX was also previously reported by Stojiljkovic et al. where the Mn-PPIX inhibits the growth of several bacterial species, including S. aureus, Y. enterocolitica, M. smegmatis27. In this study, we found that several oral bacteria, including commensals, such as S. mitis, S. salivarius are sensitive to the Mn-PPIX. Our findings are also aligned with previous observations where P. gingivalis was reported to induce S. mitis cell death by an unknown mechanism28. Streptococci are considered the predominant group of early colonisers in the oral biofilm, with S. mitis and S. salivarius associated with dental plaque in healthy oral status. This finding further demonstrates the “keystone” properties of P. gingivalis in a polymicrobial community where the selective antimicrobial property of Mn-PPIX produced by this bacterium perturbs the diversity and composition of polymicrobial consortia, contributing to the transition from a symbiotic biofilm to dysbiosis.

In this study, we demonstrated that Mn‑PPIX exhibits selective antimicrobial activity against several oral commensals. Although the highest concentrations used in our in vitro agar diffusion assays exceed expected free pigment levels in vivo, we observed detectable inhibition for some species at concentrations as low as 0.1 mg/mL, indicating that relatively low amounts of Mn‑PPIX are sufficient to influence bacterial growth. Importantly, Mn‑PPIX has limited aqueous solubility and, in P. gingivalis, accumulates predominantly as a surface‑bound pigment rather than as a freely diffusible metabolite. This mode of association suggests that the local concentration of Mn‑PPIX in the immediate microenvironment of the bacterial surface, particularly in densely packed biofilms, may be substantially higher than what can be modelled in diffusion‑based assays. Thus, while direct comparison between bulk in vitro concentrations and physiological conditions is not possible, the observed activity at low concentrations, combined with surface accumulation, supports the potential for localised, contact‑proximal antimicrobial effects in vivo.

We did not test antimicrobial activity using crude pigment extracts as they contain a complex mixture of porphyrins, and our objective was to attribute activity-specific compound(s). Nevertheless, evaluating the combined effects of native pigment mixtures in future work may further clarify the collective contribution of P. gingivalis porphyrins to community modulation within subgingival biofilms.

Collectively, our findings suggest that P. gingivalis manipulates haem reservoir and porphyrin metabolism to adapt to nutrient cues, modulate host responses, and influence microbial community structure. Understanding the role of extracellular porphyrin metabolites is critical for deciphering the complex interactions and ecological dynamics within subgingival biofilms. Building on this understanding, modulating Mn-PPIX and PPIX availability in the polymicrobial community offers an alternative avenue to restore microbial symbiosis and manipulate biofilm-associated diseases. Clinically, managing the initial local inflammatory responses to limit the nutrient cues, such as haemoglobin for P. gingivalis, to reduce the synthesis of virulent porphyrin species might represent a meaningful preventive strategy. Alternatively, targeting Mn-PPIX biosynthesis or scavenging extracellular porphyrins may offer novel strategies to disrupt the pathogenic cycle and restore microbial homoeostasis. Preventive approaches aimed at limiting early inflammatory responses and nutrient leakage, such as haemoglobin, may mitigate disease progression.

Methods

Bacterial strains and cultures

The Bacterial strains used in all experiments, unless otherwise defined, are Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) strain W83.

P. gingivalis were grown in enriched Tryptic Soy Broth (eTSB (Oxoid), 30 g/L Tryptone (Oxoid) soya broth, 0.5 g/L of L-cysteine (Sigma-Aldrich), 2 mg/L of menadione (Sigma-Aldrich), and 5 g/L haemin (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C in an anaerobic chamber (Don Whitley Scientific Limited) with an atmosphere of 80% N2, 10% CO2 and 10% H2. For cells grown on solid media, the media were supplemented with 1.5% (w/v) bacteriological agar (Oxoid) and 40 mL/L sheep blood (Amyl Media Ltd., Australia).

For pigmentation screening and pigment production that required haem depletion, P. gingivalis was inoculated into eTSB media lacking haemin (Sigma-Aldrich). The culture was then passaged daily into haem-free media at 1:10 to deplete haem stores, as previously described29. When the biomass of the subsequent passage could no longer reach an OD600 of 0.5 after 24 h, sufficient haem depletion was considered achieved, and the penultimate culture was used for the following experiments.

Bioinformatic analyses

The Basic Local Alignment Search Tool for protein Sequence (BLASTp) programme30 was used to search for the putative P. gingivalis protein with similarity to known chelatases at the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). The chelatases used were CbiK from Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (NP_460970.1), SirB (NP_389445.1) and PpfC (NP_388894.1) from Bacillus subtilis. The results were aligned, and a guide tree was created using the default pairwise method on Clustal Omega31.

Recombinant FetB construction, synthesis and cloning

The cloning of fetB gene in E. coli DH5α was performed as detailed in Gao et al. (2010)32. Briefly, the fetB gene was amplified by PCR from P. gingivalis W83 genomic DNA using primers rFetBNcoIF and rFetBTEVXhoIR (SI Appendix, Table S6). PCR was performed using AccuPrime Pfx DNA polymerase (Invitrogen). The amplicon and pET-24d(+) vector (Novagen) were digested with NcoI and XhoI, purified, and ligated to generate plasmid pET-TEV-rFetB. The ligation mixture was transformed into E. coli DH5α, and transformants were selected on kanamycin-containing agar plates. Following plasmid amplification and extraction, the construct was confirmed by DNA sequencing. The primers used in the cloning and sequencing are listed in SI Appendix, Table S6, and the sequence map of vector pET-24d (+) used in this study is shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S14.

Recombinant FetB overexpression, purification and characterisation

Recombinant FetB was expressed as a His-tag protein in Rosetta™(DE3) competent cells. The preculture of the expression host was used to inoculate 2 L of Luria Broth (LB), and cells were grown until an OD600 of 0.7. Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM isopropylthio-β-galactoside (IPTG, Sigma-Aldrich). The medium was supplemented with 10 mg/mL of δ-Aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) in the case of recombinant holo FetB expression. Cells were grown using expression conditions as shown in SI Appendix, Table S3. Cells were then harvested by centrifugation at 6000 × g for 15 min and stored in lysis buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM Imidazole, pH 8).

The host cells were sonicated, and the lysate was cleared by ultracentrifugation at 150,000 × g for 45min, then loaded onto a pre-equilibrated column containing Ni-NTA resin (Qiagen). The column was washed with ten times column volumes of wash buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 20 mM Imidazole, pH 8) and the protein was eluted with elution buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 200 mM Imidazole, pH 8) and desalted using PD-10 desalting columns (Cytiva). Protein was incubated with TEV protease (10,000 U per 25 mg of FetB protein) overnight at 4 °C in a storage buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8). The cleaved protein was separated from TEV by Ni-NTA affinity purification. Protein was concentrated with an Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter (Merck Millipore Ltd) to 10 mg/mL and stored at −80 °C.

Protein concentration was measured on a Nanodrop® ND-1000 UV-Vis spectrophotometer at 280 nm. The extinction coefficient specific for FetB (ε of 32890 M−1 cm−1) was used as calculated at 280 nm by the ExPASy ProtParam programme based on protein sequence33.

Reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (rp-HPLC)

Method for pigment analysis using rp-HPLC was modified from Fyrestam et al.34. A reverse-phase HPLC (rp-HPLC) system with a Photodiode-Array Detection (PDA) unit (Agilent Technologies, Inc.) and a NUCLEODUR C18 analytical column (Macherey-Nagel) was used to separate the crude pigment extract according to the parameters listed in Table S7. All absorbance spectra were recorded at room temperature. Fractions with absorbance patterns resembling porphyrin-based compounds were collected and lyophilised for further analysis.

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS)

Elemental analysis using ICP-MS was applied to identify the metal contents of P. gingivalis pigment. The pigment sample was prepared by digesting the lyophilised powder in 100 µL of concentrated aqueous nitric acid and incubating overnight. The samples were then diluted to a 100-fold dilution in 1% nitric acid to determine the elements. Analyses were performed on the Nexion 350X Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometer (PerkinElmer). A general scan covering Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, K, Ca, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, As, Se, Sr, Ag, Cd, Ba and Pb was carried out.

Electrospray ionisation mass spectrometry (ESI-MS)

Pigments lyophilised fractions from rp-HPLC were dissolved in TA30 buffer (30% acetonitrile (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.1% Trifluoroacetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich)). The samples were then sonicated for 5 min in an ultrasonic water bath and spun down at maximum speed to separate insoluble components.

Mass spectra were acquired using amaZon SL mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics). Samples were injected through the syringe pump with instrument parameters as outlined in SI Appendix, Table S8. The spectra were collected in positive ion mode.

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS)

Sample preparations were conducted according to the protocol provided by Bruker Daltonics. Samples were dissolved in TA30 buffer as described in the previous section. The dissolved samples were mixed with saturated α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (HCCA) in TA30 buffer at a 1:1 ratio. The sample-matrix mixtures were immediately deposited onto a polished stainless steel surface plate and allowed to air-dry.

Spectra were obtained using Solarix 2xR 7 T FTICR (Bruker) and Autoflex Speed LRF (Bruker) with FlexControl 3.4 software (Bruker) in positive reflectron ion mode. Laser intensity and a number of shots were adjusted to achieve signal intensities of 106-108 AU and detected in the m/z range of 500–1000 Da. The instrument was pre-calibrated using peptide Calibration Standard II (Bruker). The spectra were analysed on FlexAnalysis software (Bruker).

The column was washed with ten times column volumes of wash buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 20 mM Imidazole, pH 8) and the protein was eluted with elution buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 200 mM Imidazole, pH 8) and desalted using PD-10 desalting columns (Cytiva). Protein was incubated with TEV protease (10,000 U per 25 mg of FetB protein) overnight at 4 °C in a storage buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8). The cleaved protein was separated from TEV by Ni-NTA affinity purification. Protein was concentrated with an Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter (Merck Millipore Ltd) to 10 mg/mL and stored at −80 °C. Protein samples were taken throughout the purification process for Sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Buffers used for protein expression and purification can be found in SI Appendix, Table S9.

Protein concentration was measured on a Nanodrop® ND-1000 UV-Vis spectrophotometer at 280 nm. The extinction coefficient specific for FetB (ε of 32890 M−1 cm−1) was used as calculated at 280 nm by the ExPASy ProtParam programme based on protein sequence33.

Size exclusion chromatography

For size exclusion chromatography, recombinant FetB was concentrated using a 3-kDa cut-off centricon filter to a concentration of 10 mg/mL. The recombinant proteins were then applied to a Superdex 75 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare Biosciences) equilibrated with a storage buffer connected to an ÄKTA pure system (Cytiva). Proteins were eluted with the same buffer with a flow of 0.5 mL/min, and fractions of 0.5 mL were collected and monitored at 280 nm.

Sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis

Sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) was used to resolve protein samples according to their molecular weight. Buffers used in SDS-PAGE are listed in SI Appendix, Table S10. Discontinuous pH acrylamide gels were prepared using Mini-Protean II Electrophoretic Cell (Bio-Rad) comprising a 4.5% (w/v) stacking gel, pH 6.8 and 14% (w/v) resolving gel, pH 8.8. Protein samples were resuspended in 1× SDS-PAGE Sample buffer and heated for 10 min at 95 °C to denature before loading. Molecular weight marker Novex™ Sharp (Invitrogen) pre-stained protein standard (Invitrogen) was loaded onto the gel. Samples were run on a 14% polyacrylamide gel at 90 V for the stacking gel and then adjusted to 110 V through the resolving gel for 40 min. The proteins were visualised in Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 (Sigma-Aldrich) with agitation for 30 min before destaining in water.

Porphyromonas gingivalis pigmentation screen and pigment production

Gradient haemoglobin agar plates were prepared by casting eTSB agar without haemin and sheep blood (Amyl Media Ltd., Australia) into sterile 96-well plates, then adding haemoglobin (Sigma-Aldrich) in concentrations ranging from 0 µM to 80 µM. The plates were supplemented with or without 100 µM dipyridyl (Sigma-Aldrich) and equilibrated in an anaerobic chamber for at least 18 h29.

Haem-depleted P. gingivalis strains W83 cultures (100 µL) with the biomass of OD600 of 0.5 were pipetted into each well. The plates were sealed to prevent dehydration and light exposure, then incubated in anaerobic conditions for 5 days. The cells were pulled and visualised under white light and UV Transillumination MultiDoc-It (UVP) at 365 nm. For fluorescence measurement, P. gingivalis cells were collected from each well and suspended in phosphate buffer saline (PBS). The cells were then normalised using OD600, and their Fluorescence intensities were measured. Fluorescence emission spectra were recorded using SpectraMax® i3 (Molecular Devices) at room temperature, set to a photomultiplier effect (PME) of 850. The excitation and emission bandwidths were 9 nm and 15 nm, respectively. The fluorescence emission was read in black 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One) to prevent cross-well interference. An excitation wavelength of 365 nm was used, and fluorescence emissions were measured at 638 nm.

For pigment production of the wild type W83, Haem-depleted P. gingivalis W83 was inoculated at a volume of 100 µL onto 2 µM Haemoglobin agar plates. After 5 days, the cells were collected by scraping the haemoglobin plates and resuspended in 20 mL pre-equilibrated extraction buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8). The suspension was gently pipetted then centrifuged at 25000 x g for 30 min using an ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter) to separate the pigment from the cell pellet. The pigment layer was collected and lyophilised using a freeze dryer (Ilshin) overnight. For pigment extraction, the lyophilised pigment powder was reconstituted in 100% acetonitrile and sonicated in an ultrasonic water bath with ice water for 10 min whilst minimising light exposure. The solution was then adjusted by adding water to 50% acetonitrile and centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The top layer was collected and mixed with mobile phase buffer A (SI Appendix, Table S7) to bring acetonitrile concentration to 15% for rp-HPLC analysis.

Analysis of Porphyromonas gingivalis pigment

For pigment production, haem-depleted P. gingivalis was inoculated onto haemoglobin-containing plates. After 5 days, cells were collected, and the pigment was extracted and lyophilised. Lyophilised pigment was reconstituted in acetonitrile and centrifuged. The top layer was collected and adjusted to a final acetonitrile (Sigma-Aldrich) concentration of 15%. Rp-HPLC was used to separate and analyse the composition of the crude pigment extract. All fractions containing prominent absorption were collected and lyophilised for further analysis by ICP-MS, ESI-MS, and MALDI-TOF MS.

Protein crystallisation

Commercial kits, JBScreen PACT + + , JCSG + + (Jena Bioscience), SaltRx HT, and Index HT (Hampton Research) were used for crystal screening. Crystals of purified recombinant apo-FetB were grown by vapour diffusion using the sitting drop method at 20 °C by mixing 300 nL of reservoir solution with 300 nL protein solution (10 mg/mL in storage buffer). Crystal condition optimisation was performed in 100 mM Bis-Tris pH 6.4 and 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES, Sigma-Aldrich) pH7 in MgCl2 salt conditions (150 mM to 275 mM) and in the presence of precipitant PEG4000 (20–30% w/v) Crystals were harvested after 2 weeks and were cryoprotected by adding well solutions containing 25% (v/v) glycerol (Sigma-Aldrich) prior to harvested and flash-freezing.

Data collection and refinement

Diffraction data were collected on the MX1 and MX2 beamlines of the Australian Synchrotron (Melbourne, Victoria). Data reduction and scaling were processed with the XDS package35. The structures were solved by molecular replacement using the D. vulgaris periplasmic CbiK structure (PDB ID: 2XVY) with PHASER36 from the CCP4 programme suite37. Desulfovibrio vulgaris periplasmic CbiK was used because of its similarities to FetB, as both belong to the same family of CbiK and can bind to metalated porphyrins38.

Structure refinement was performed with iterative rounds of model building with COOT39 and model refinement with PHENIX40. The crystal belonged to the space group P 21 21 21, and the unit-cell parameters were a = 48.63, b = 49.82, 98.91, α = 90°, β = 90°, and γ = 90°. The structure was solved at a resolution of 2.14 Å. All protein structure figures were generated using PyMOL41. The alpha helices and beta sheets are numbered consecutively according to their order from the N-terminal.

Recombinant FetB chelatase assay

Deuteroporphyrin IX 2,4-disulfonic acid dimethyl ester (DPIX-DME) (Frontier Specialty Chemicals) stock solutions were prepared freshly before use to 10 mM by dissolving porphyrin powder in 0.1 M NaOH, pH 12, gentle pipetting and inversion for 5 min. Samples were incubated for 10 min at ambient conditions before use.

Chelatase assays were performed using the spectrophotometric method. Assay mixtures were prepared by incremental addition of recombinant apo-FetB between 0.4–8 µM into DPIX-DME at a final concentration of 20 µM in an assay buffer (1.5% Tween 20 (Astral Scientific), 20 mM Tris (Sigma-Aldrich), pH 7.4). Immediately prior to initiation of the programme, 100 µM of the tested metal solution (CoCl2, MnCl2, FeSO4, NiCl₂, CuSO4, and ZnSO4) was added to the assay mixture. Absorbance intensities were measured at 40 s intervals. All absorbance spectra were recorded at room temperature using Greiner UV-Star® 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One) on SpectraMax® i3 (Molecular Devices) or Infinite M1000 PRO (Tecan).

Antibacterial activity assay of Mn-PPIX

Nine species of bacteria (Streptococcus mitis, Streptococcus salivarius, Enterococcus faecalis, oral Lactobacillus species, Streptococcus oralis, Streptococcus gordonii, Streptococcus sanguinis, Streptococcus mutans, and Serratia marcescens) were streaked onto eTSB agar plates without haem or whole blood from glycerol stocks stored at −80 °C. The inoculated plates were incubated for 16 h at 37 °C.

A single colony from each subcultured plate was then inoculated in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth (Oxoid) and incubated for 16 h at 37 °C. The liquid cultures were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1 and incubated at 37 °C at 200 rpm until an OD600 of 1.0 was reached. The liquid cultures were inoculated onto the plates at a volume of 100 µL. Six circles of diameter 5 mm from sterilised filter paper were placed equidistantly around each plate. To each filter paper, 10 µL of 100% DMSO, 20% chlorhexidine digluconate (CHX) or one of four 1:10 dilutions of Mn-PPIX, ranging from 10 mg/mL to 0.01 mg/mL, was applied. The plates were incubated in aerobic conditions at 37 °C for 16 h. All experiments were conducted with two technical replicates and two to four biological replicates. The zone of inhibition around each filter paper was observed in four directions and averaged.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction

To collect their RNA, P. gingivalis cells grown on an eTSB plate supplemented with 0.2 µM to 65 µM haemoglobin (Sigma-Aldrich) were collected in PBS. The cells were resuspended and normalised using OD600. RNA Protect was added to cell suspensions, incubated for 5 min, centrifuged at 5000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was removed. Total RNA was extracted using RNAqueous Kit (Invitrogen) and performed according to a protocol described in Gao (2010)32. Lysis solution (200 μL) was added to the cell pellet, vortexed and then spun down at top speed. Following this, 64% ethanol (Univar) (200 μL) was added, and the mixture was filtered. It was then washed with Solutions 1, 2 and 3, eluted with pre-heated elution, and immediately centrifuged at 16,060 × g for 30 s at room temperature. Samples were supplemented with 5 μL of 10× DNAse I (Ambion) buffer, mixed, and 1 μL DNAse I was added. The samples were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, then 1/10 volume of Inactivation Reagent was added and left at room temperature for 2 min. Following this, the samples were centrifuged at maximum speed for 90 s and transferred to RNase-free tubes. The RNA samples were stored at −20 °C.

Synthesis of cDNA was performed using Invitrogen SuperScript® First-Strand Synthesis System according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer. For a 10 μL reaction, the mixture contained 1.5 μL RNase-free H2O, 5 μL first-strand master mix, 1 μL Random hexamers, 0.5 μL Affinity Block enzyme mixture, and 2 μL Isolated RNA sample. The PCR reaction was performed using a cycle of 25 °C for 5 min, 42 °C for 15 min, and 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were diluted to a 200-fold dilution and stored at −20 °C until qPCR analysis was performed.

Real-Time PCR reactions were performed using the TaqMan probe and primers present in SI Appendix, Table S6 as previously described. The 20 μL reaction mixture contained 3.5 μL of DNase-free H2O, 10 μL of 2× SuperMix-UDG, 0.8 μL of primers each, 0.4 μL of Probe, 0.3 μL of ROX Reference dye, and 5 μL of cDNA template. The reaction was carried out at 95 °C for 10 min for 1 cycle, then repeated 45 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s (denaturation) and 60 °C for 1 min (annealing/extension). Each PCR reaction was performed in triplicate.

Construction of fetB deletion and complementation mutants

The construction of ΔfetB and ΔfetB+ mutants was carried out using an established method29. The constructs were confirmed using DNA sequencing. The primers used for the construction and sequencing of mutants are listed in SI Appendix, Table S7.

Pigmentation analysis of fetB deletion mutants

For pigment analysis, P. gingivalis was grown as described above. The fetB deletion and fetB complementation mutants were grown with the supplement of 5 µg/mL erythromycin. Pigmentation of the wild-type, ΔfetB and ΔfetB+ mutants was extracted and compared using rp-HPLC as described above.
