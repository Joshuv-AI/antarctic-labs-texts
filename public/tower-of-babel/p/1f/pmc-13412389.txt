Lessons learned from Micra leadless pacemaker retrieval: a case series
Authors: Qi Guo, HanHua Gao, ZhiHua Deng, ShangHai Li, ShuXian Zhou, JingFeng Wang, YangXin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRetrieving an implanted leadless pacemaker is a challenging component of its management. However, there are no dedicated tools specifically designed for retrieving the Micra leadless pacemaker. Given the lack of commercial retrieval systems, there is an urgent need to document and validate alternative interventional strategies to manage complications associated with device malfunction or migration.Case summaryIn this series, we present cases involving increased capture threshold, device embolization into the right pulmonary artery, and broken tines following Micra implantation. We successfully completed four retrieval procedures using a coordinated approach with existing devices, including the Micra transcatheter pacing system, coronary angiography catheter, and endovascular snare system.DiscussionNotably, these cases exhibited different characteristics during the retrieval of the Micra leadless pacemaker, which may assist colleagues in dealing with such scenarios. Our experience demonstrates that a strategy combining the Micra delivery catheter with an endovascular snare system, without the need for a steerable sheath, was sufficient and effective for successful Micra retrieval.

Introduction

Leadless cardiac pacemakers have emerged as a safe and effective alternative to conventional transvenous pacemakers for patients indicated for single-chamber ventricular pacing.1 Compared with the transvenous pacing system, the use of Micra leadless pacemakers (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) has been demonstrated to result in high-implant success rates and significantly lower complications.2,3 Accordingly, it is especially suitable for application in many hospitals with different levels. However, in certain circumstances, retrieval of the Micra is deemed necessary.4 Although several cases have been reported, there are no dedicated tools or detailed operational protocols for the retrieval of the Micra leadless pacemaker.5,6 Herein, we report four successful retrieval cases to highlight the coordinated use of different devices, including the Micra transcatheter pacing system, a coronary angiography catheter, and an endovascular capture system (e.g. a snare). Notably, these cases exhibited different characteristics during the retrieval of the Micra leadless pacemaker, which may assist colleagues in dealing with such scenarios and avoiding similar problems.

Patient 1

An 80-year-old female patient complaining of recurrent dizziness and syncope was diagnosed with third-degree atrioventricular (AV) block combined with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Given the patient's slender physique and fragile skin, which predispose her to a higher risk of pocket infection, we recommended the implantation of a leadless pacemaker. She underwent successful Medtronic Micra AV implantation with confirmed stable device parameters (R-wave amplitude, 6.4 mV; capture threshold, 0.63 V at 0.24 ms; impedance, 470 Ω) prior to tether removal. An abnormal device parameter with a capture threshold of 4.0 V at 0.4 ms was detected 24 h post-operatively, followed by failure to capture the device several hours later. Fluoroscopy revealed that the device had fallen into the right pulmonary artery. The combined use of a guide wire and a Judkins Right Four catheter provided access to the remote right pulmonary artery, which helped secure the Micra and avoid complete vascular occlusions during the operation. We used a single-loop snare to capture the Micra leadless pacemaker by holding the tines and slowly pulling the device into the right atrium. Multiple attempts failed to position the device in the sheath. We then attempted to use triple-loop and single-loop endovascular snare systems to capture the Micra’s tines and proximal retrieval feature, respectively, and adjust the Micra to be coaxial with the sheath successfully. The Micra was safely withdrawn while being held by the two snares (Figure 1). After the retrieval, a new Micra was successfully implanted, and the device parameters were satisfactory to date.

Patient 2

A 64-year-old male patient was diagnosed with intermittent high-grade AV block. The patient elected to undergo leadless pacemaker implantation to better accommodate his high-demand physical activities. He received a Micra AV leadless pacemaker implantation to the medial septum with confirmed optimal parameters (R-wave amplitude, 8.0 mV; capture threshold, 0.5 V at 0.24 ms; and impedance, 650 Ω). However, the capture threshold (4.6 V at 0.4 ms) was significantly elevated 6 months after implantation. X-ray video revealed that two of the four tines of Micra were fractured, and the decision was made to retrieve the Micra and re-implant a new one. Prior to the retrieval, a guide wire was preset in the sheath for new Micra implantation. The pulmonary artery was protected by placing a single-loop snare through a guide wire and an angiography catheter. The Micra tail was successfully ensnared by the triple-loop snare, and the device was gently pulled back to the top of the sheath. Another single-loop snare was then used to capture the Micra head so that the orientation of the Micra could be precisely modulated and easily pulled into the sheath. The entire operation was completed within 20 min. The used Micra was found to have only two tines, with the other two broken tines remaining in the right ventricular septum. Because the Micra could not be pulled out through the haemostatic valve of the sheath, extraction of both the sheath and the Micra was necessary. A new catheter was inserted via the preset wire, and the Super Stiff Guidewire was exchanged. A new Micra was successfully implanted (Figure 2). At the 1-year follow-up, echocardiography revealed generally normal cardiac function and structure; chest radiography confirmed that the two tines remained in their original positions; and device programming demonstrated normal parameters for the leadless pacemakers.

Patient 3

A 57-year-old male patient was diagnosed with intermittent high-grade AV block as well as atrial fibrillation. Previous mechanical mitral valve replacement was performed 20 years ago. The patient opted for a leadless pacemaker to minimize occupational interference and facilitate his ongoing work requirements. A Micra leadless pacemaker was implanted in the superior septum with acceptable parameters (sensed R-waves, 20.0 mV; capture threshold, 0.5 V at 0.24 ms; and impedance, 1050 Ω). However, on the third post-operative day, the capture threshold increased to 3.5 V at 0.24 ms. Fluoroscopy revealed that the Micra tended to dislodge from its original position with obvious shaking. After pre-setting a guide wire in the sheath, we attempted to capture the Micra using one triple-loop snare but failed. Afterwards, a triple-loop snare was placed into the pulmonary artery near the outflow tract of the right ventricle through a guide wire in conjunction with an angiography catheter. Because of the large-scale motion of Micra’s tail, we used the preset triple-loop snare to limit the motion of Micra and successfully hold the retrieval feature. The Micra was successfully pulled back into the delivery system, completing the retrieval within 20 min. Finally, a new Micra was successfully implanted in the inferior ventricular septum (Figure 3; Supplementary material).

Patient 4

A 67-year-old male patient was diagnosed with third-degree AV block. He underwent implantation of a Micra leadless pacemaker according to his preference. Although the Micra was implanted in the medial septum, the intraoperative programming parameters were acceptable (sensed R-waves, 10.0 mV; capture threshold, 0.63 V at 0.24 ms; and impedance, 800 Ω). After tether removal, fluoroscopy revealed that the Micra had migrated into the inferior vena cava. An immediate decision was made to capture the Micra. Two single-loop snares were placed in the right atrium. After holding the head and tail of the Micra, it was successfully retrieved within 15 min. After retrieval, a new Micra was implanted in the medial septum, and the tether was carefully removed under fluoroscopy.

Discussion

Retrieving Micra leadless pacemaker is a very challenging but desirable technique for percutaneous interventional cardiologists. There is a clinical demand for simplified techniques for the retrieval of leadless pacemakers.7 Four cases of successful retrieval of the Micra leadless pacemaker were reported here, and each case had distinct characteristics. Our work demonstrated that the coordinated use of different devices (Figure 4), including the Micra transcatheter pacing system, coronary angiography catheter, and endovascular snare system, is feasible for the retrieval of the Micra leadless pacemaker, regardless of whether it has dislodged.

Among the four cases, the duration from implantation to retrieval varied from a few minutes to 6 months (Figure 5). There have also been reports of successful extraction of Micra leadless pacemakers that had been implanted for over 5 years.8 Indications for device retrieval included increased capture threshold, dislodgement, and migration.9 Case 1 highlights the importance of the gooseneck bend during the release process. When the gooseneck bend was not sufficiently performed, it could result in inadequate capture of myocardial tissue. If the amount of myocardial tissue was too low to provide sufficient holding force for the Micra or was even broken down, the possibility of dislodgement or migration would greatly increase. In Cases 2 and 3, significant shaking resulting from rapid blood flow could occur if the Micra implant site was too close to the right ventricular outflow tract. The repetitive mechanical motion might lead to tine fractures10 and further dislodgement.11 In Case 4, excessive tension without better hand sensation and a lack of fluoroscopy observation during tether removal were postulated as possible contributing factors to this immediate migration.

Unlike the Nanostim or Aveir leadless pacemakers, which have standard retrieval catheter systems, there are no dedicated retrieval tools for the Micra leadless pacemaker.12 Two strategies have been recommended for Mirca retrieval: Using a delivery catheter or a steerable sheath. Our experience revealed that the use of a delivery catheter with an endovascular snare system, but not a steerable sheath, was sufficient for Micra retrieval. A guide wire could help place the snare in the target position, such as the pulmonary artery or right ventricular outflow tract. Both the sheath and the Micra should be extracted because the sheath has the haemostatic valve, and the Micra is difficult to pull out from the haemostatic valve by the snare. After the extraction, the preset wire could facilitate the reinsertion of the sheath, avoiding secondary punctures and related complications. In certain simple cases, the use of a single-loop snare has been sufficient to capture and retrieve the leadless pacemaker.13 Compared with one snare, we preferred the combined use of two snares, which has the advantages of easier coaxial orientation,14 limited shaking amplitude, and reduced risk of second migration or pulmonary artery embolism. A similar double-snare technique has also been reported for the retrieval of the Aveir leadless pacemaker.15 In addition, if the patients need temporary pacing protection, we can also implant a new Micra as the pacing system and then retrieve the old Micra; we also have experience with several such successful cases. Certainly, this method may increase the difficulty of capturing the old Micra by the snare because of avoiding influencing the new implanted Micra.

Several key points should be noted to avoid unnecessary retrieval. First, careful attention should be paid to the programming parameters during and after implantation. Second, the implantation site should not be too close to the outflow tract to prevent the additional force of blood flow on the device. If the implantation site is too high or if the tail of the pacemaker shows excessive range of motion, it is preferable to adjust the implantation site immediately. Third, each step of the standard implantation procedure is important. Appropriate tension and essential fluoroscopy are crucial during tether removal, especially for unskilled operators.

In conclusion, we reported four successful Micra leadless pacemaker retrievals. Coordination of different devices, including the Micra transcatheter pacing system, coronary angiography catheter, and endovascular snare system, for Micra leadless pacemaker retrieval was feasible.
