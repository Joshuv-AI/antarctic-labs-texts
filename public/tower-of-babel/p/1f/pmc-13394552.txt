Hypericin Suppresses Liver Cancer Through Autophagic Degradation of AKT and Eliciting Antitumor Immune Response
Authors: Sien Ma, Qianqian Xu, Mengjuan Zhu, Xianming Ge, Jian Xu, Yan Pan, Mengli Wang, Yuting Zhu, Aoxuan Xu, Hanmeng Liu, GuangHai Xiang, Weipeng Gong, Bao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most prevalent primary liver malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide, with high mortality and limited clinical treatment options. In this study, we identified hypericin as a potent inducer of HCC cell death by screening traditional Chinese Medicine Monomer Library screening. Hypericin, a natural photosensitizer derived from Hypericum perforatum , significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration, and colony formation of HCC cell lines, and induced apoptosis in vitro. In vivo, hypericin effectively suppressed tumor growth in Hepa1‐6 subcutaneous tumor and AKT/c‐Myc‐driven orthotopic mouse HCC models. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that hypericin treatment increased the CD8+ T cell proportion and activity within the tumor microenvironment. Combination therapy with hypericin and anti‐PD‐L1 antibody resulted in significant tumor growth inhibition and prolonged survival. Mechanistically, hypericin promoted the interaction between AKT and HSPA5, leading to autophagic degradation of AKT. This process inhibited the AKT/mTOR/S6 signaling pathway, downregulated the expression of downstream anti‐apoptotic proteins MCL‐1 and XIAP, and triggered mitochondria‐mediated apoptosis in HCC cells.Keywords: AKT, antitumor immunity, autophagy, HCC, HSPA5, Hypericin

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for approximately 90% of primary liver cancers and predominantly arises from chronic liver disease and cirrhosis. HCC is the sixth most common cancer globally and the third leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide [1, 2]. Treatment options for early‐stage HCC include surgical resection (with a cure rate of approximately 20%), liver transplantation, and local ablative therapies (including radiofrequency ablation), which can achieve 5‐year survival rates of 60%–70% [3]. However, 70%–80% of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, rendering them ineligible for curative interventions. For intermediate‐stage HCC, trans‐arterial chemoembolization remains the standard of care, although clinical responses vary. In advanced HCC, treatment strategies mainly involve multikinase inhibitors including sorafenib, lenvatinib, and cabozantinib [4], as well as immune checkpoint inhibitors [5] and combination therapies. Nevertheless, only a small proportion of patients with advanced HCC achieve durable clinical responses, highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches [6].

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), a medical system with thousands of years of history in China, has revealed promise in inhibiting tumor growth, overcoming chemoresistance, and alleviating the side effects of antitumor therapies [7]. Growing studies have demonstrated that bioactive compounds derived from TCM formulations exert unique antitumor effects [8, 9]. Through screening a TCM monomer library, we identified hypericin as a promising candidate for HCC therapy. Hypericin (C30H16O8) is a phenanthroperylenequinone derivative purified from Hypericum perforatum (St. John's Wort) and is known for its potent photosensitizing properties [10]. Structurally, it features a conjugated polycyclic aromatic system with multiple hydroxyl groups, which enables strong light absorption and redox activity [11]. Previous studies have revealed diverse mechanisms by which hypericin exerts antitumor activity in various cancers, including breast [12, 13], gastric [14], and colon cancers [15] as well as cholangiocarcinoma [16], melanoma [17] and T‐cell lymphoma [18]. Regarding HCC, some studies suggest that hypericin inhibits proliferation and induces apoptosis in HCC cell lines [19, 20]. Conversely, other research has reported minimal effects on cell viability or apoptosis [21, 22]. Therefore, the precise role and mechanism of hypericin in HCC remain unclear and warrant further studies.

The phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K)/AKT signaling pathway is vital for the pathogenesis of various cancers, including HCC [23, 24]. AKT, a key oncogenic protein, regulates cell survival, proliferation, metastasis, and metabolism. Aberrant AKT activation can occur via multiple mechanisms, including mutations in PIK3CA, loss of PTEN, and activating mutations in AKT [25]. AKT is activated by phosphorylation at Thr308 by PDK1 and at Ser473 by mTORC2. Once activated, AKT phosphorylates numerous downstream substrates, including mTOR, GSK3β, FOXOs, MDM2, and proapoptotic proteins, including Bad and procaspase‐9 [26, 27, 28, 29]. AKT signaling is activated in approximately 50% of HCC cases, making it a compelling therapeutic target.

Several AKT inhibitors have been developed for clinical use. For instance, capivasertib, an ATP‐competitive pan‐AKT inhibitor, has been approved for advanced breast cancer [30, 31], whereas ipatasertib is under clinical evaluation for metastatic castration‐resistant prostate cancer (IPATential150) and locally advanced or metastatic triple‐negative breast cancer [32, 33]. Additionally, allosteric pan‐AKT inhibitors (targeting the PH domain), including MK‐2206 and miransertib (MK‐7075), exhibit lower toxicity and are being investigated for treating Proteus syndrome. A new generation of covalent allosteric AKT inhibitors (CAAIs), including borussertib, has demonstrated enhanced efficacy; however, their use is limited by poor pharmacokinetic profiles [23, 34]. Therefore, developing novel AKT inhibitors with improved safety and bioavailability is an urgent priority.

In this study, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of hypericin against HCC in vitro and in vivo. We identified a novel mechanism by which hypericin treatment promotes the interaction between AKT and HSPA5, leading to AKT degradation via the autophagy‐lysosomal pathway. Furthermore, hypericin enhances the function of CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment. Combination therapy with hypericin and PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade significantly suppressed tumor growth and prolonged survival in syngeneic Hepa1‐6 HCC mouse models. These findings highlight hypericin as a promising candidate for HCC therapy.

Materials and Methods

Cell Lines, Plasmids and Drugs

The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line Huh7, HepG2, mouse Hepa1‐6 (Hycyte Bioscience Co. Ltd.) and HepG2.2.15 Cells (EK‐Bioscience) were cultured in DMEM (KGM12800‐500, KeyGEN Bio TECH) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (A5256701, Gibco) in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. were maintained in DMEM (KGM12800‐500, KeyGEN Bio TECH) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (A5256701, Gibco) at 37°C under 5% CO2.

pLV3‐CMV‐Myr‐Flag‐AKT1‐mCherry‐Puro, pLV3‐CMV‐Myr‐Flag‐AKTS473D‐mCherry‐Puro, and pLV3‐CMV‐Myr‐Flag‐AKTS473A‐mCherry‐Puro plasmids were constructed in our lab by inserting Myr‐Flag‐AKT1, AKTS473D, and AKTS473A gene fragments into EcoRI/BamHI digested plasmid. HA‐AKTS473D plasmid was kindly provided by Prof. Jianping Guo from the Institute of Precision Medicine, the First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat‐sen University. pLV3‐CMV‐HSPA5 (human)‐3 × Myc‐CopGFP‐Puro (P57337) was obtained from MiaoLingBio, China.

Hypericin (T6S0923, TargetMol) and inhibitors (including the apoptosis inhibitor Z‐VAD‐FMK (T7020, TargetMol), autophagy inhibitor 3‐methyladenine (3‐MA, T1879, TargetMol), ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin‐1 (Fer‐1, T6500, TargetMol), necroptosis inhibitor necrostatin‐1 (Nec‐1, T1847, TargetMol), lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine (CQ, C843545, Macklin), and proteasome inhibitor MG‐132 (T2154, TargetMol)) were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). All experiments involving hypericin were performed under strictly controlled low‐light conditions to prevent unintended photochemical activation. Cells were handled under a dim indirect light source (≤ 10 lx, no direct illumination) and maintained in a light‐shielded incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 for the duration of treatment. No external light sources, including UV lamps or bright fluorescent lights, were used near the cell culture area during the experiments.

CCK‐8 Assay

HYP's inhibitory effect on HepG2, Huh7, Hepa1‐6, and HepG2.2.15 cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8, 40203ES80, Yeasen Biotechnology). Cells (10,000 per well) were plated in 96‐well plates with 10% FBS‐supplemented DMEM and exposed to graded Hypericin concentrations in triplicate. After treatment, 10 μL CCK‐8 reagent diluted in 90 μL DMEM was added per well, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured following a 2–4 h incubation at 37°C. Readings were taken at 24, 48, and 72 h, with all experiments repeated in triplicate.

Colony Formation Assay

Cells (1000 per well) were plated in 6‐well plates and treated overnight with 0, 1, or 2.5 μM Hypericin before a 14‐day incubation. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Yeasen Biotechnology, 40402ES50) for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Yeasen Biotechnology, 60505ES25) for 30 min. Colonies exceeding 50 cells were counted to determine survival fractions, with triplicate independent replicates performed for each condition.

Migration Assay

HepG2 and Huh7 cell migration was evaluated using 24‐well Transwell chambers (6.5 mm diameter, 8‐μm pores; Corning Costar). Cells (5 × 105/mL) in serum‐free medium were seeded in the upper chamber (100 μL), while the lower chamber contained 600 μL of complete medium with 10% FBS. Following a 4 h preincubation, the upper chamber medium was replaced with Hypericin (0, 1, or 2.5 μM). After 48 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and nonmigrated cells were mechanically removed. Migrated cells were quantified in three random fields (40× magnification; Olympus) per replicate.

Apoptosis Assay

Cells cultured in 12‐well plates were treated with Hypericin (2 or 4 μM) for 48 h. Apoptotic cells were detected using an AnnexinV‐APC kit (40304ES50, Yeasen Biotechnology). Collected cells were washed with PBS, stained with Annexin V and SYTOX Green in binding buffer (30 min, dark), and analyzed by flow cytometry (NovoExpress, Agilent) with FlowJo (v10).

CRISPR‐Cas9‐Mediated Gene Knockout

HSPA5/Hspa5 knockout in Huh7, HepG2, and Hepa1‐6 cells was achieved using pLenti‐CRISPR‐V2. Lentivirus expressing HSPA5 gRNA (CCAATCAGACGTTCCCCTTC) or Hspa5 gRNA (CCACATACGACGGCGTGATG) was packaged in 293 T cells with psPAX2/pMD2G. Filtered supernatants (0.2 μm) were used for dual infections, followed by puromycin selection (2 μg/mL, 3 days). Knockout efficiency was confirmed by Western blot.

Lentiviral Transduction and Stable Cell Line Establishment

For stable knockdown of ATG5, short hairpin RNA targeting human ATG5 (shATG5) and nontargeting control shRNA (pLVX‐shRNA‐EF1a‐ZsGreen‐puro‐NC) were cloned into the corresponding lentiviral vector. Lentiviral particles were packaged in HEK293T cells by cotransfecting the shRNA constructs with psPAX2 and pMD2.G using PEI 40000. After 48 h, the viral supernatant was collected, filtered, and used to infect Huh7 and HepG2 cells with 8 μg/mL polybrene. Infected cells were selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin for 2 days to establish stable cell pools. Knockdown efficiency was verified by Western blot analysis.

Transmission Electronic Microscopy (TEM)

Huh7 cells were seeded in 6‐well plates at 5 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight at 37°C in 5% CO2. After treatment with 2 μM Hypericin or vehicle for 48 h, cells were collected, fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, and processed into ultrathin sections (50–60 nm) for observation under a TEM (JEM‐1400, JEOL).

RNA Sequencing

HepG2 cells were seeded in 100 mm cell culture dishes at a density of 3 × 106 cells per well and incubated overnight at 37°C under 5% CO2. After treatment with 5 μM Hypericin or vehicle for 48 h, total RNA was extracted and assessed for integrity by agarose gel electrophoresis, with concentrations quantified using NanoDrop. mRNA was isolated from total RNA using oligo‐dT magnetic beads and fragmented. Reverse transcription generated cDNA, which underwent end repair and 3′ adenylation before ligation to sequencing adapters. Adapter‐ligated products were purified to remove incomplete fragments and self‐ligated adapters, followed by PCR amplification with adapter‐specific primers. The final library was purified using magnetic beads, quantified with Qubit, and fragment size distribution analyzed using an Agilent fragment analyzer. Qualified libraries underwent paired‐end 150 bp sequencing (PE150) on an NGS platform. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with iDEP2.0, defining significant genes as those with a fold change > 1.5 and p < 0.05. KEGG pathway analysis was conducted using the same platform.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and Proteomics of Mass Spectrometry (MS)

Huh7 cells expressing Flag‐AKT were treated with vehicle or 2 μM Hypericin plus 300 μM 3‐MA for 48 h, then lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with Anti‐DYKDDDDK Affinity Resin (GenScript, L00907) under gentle agitation, washed five times with cold RIPA buffer, and submitted to Bioprofile (Shanghai, China) for LC/MS to identify AKT‐interacting proteins (Table S1). For validation, Flag‐AKT‐expressing Huh7 cells treated with 2 μM Hypericin or left untreated were lysed, immunoprecipitated as above, and eluted with SDS sample buffer at 95°C for 10 min before western blotting.

To confirm the AKT‐HSPA5 interaction, Myc‐HSPA5‐expressing Huh7 cells were treated with vehicle or 2 μM Hypericin plus 300 μM 3‐MA for 48 h. Coimmunoprecipitation was performed using a commercial kit (P2193M Beyotime), with cell lysates incubated overnight at 4°C with protein G agarose and primary antibody or control IgG. Beads were washed three times with lysis buffer and boiled at 95°C for 5 min prior to analysis.

In Vivo Antitumor Study

All animal care and experiments were performed in the specific‐pathogen free facility and conducted in accordance with procedures approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Institute of Health and Medicine, Hefei Comprehensive National Science Center (approved No. IHM‐AP‐2024‐053). For evaluating antitumor activity without affection of T cells, Hepa1‐6, Hepa1‐6‐sgNTC or Hepa1‐6‐sgHspa5 (2 × 106/mouse) cells were injected subcutaneously into 6–8‐week‐old female Rag1 −/− B6 mice to establish xenografts. When tumors reached ~100 mm3, mice were randomized into vehicle or Hypericin group. Hypericin treated mice were intraperitoneally injected with Hypericin (5 mg/kg) every 2 days, for 6 times. To assess whether hypericin‐induced cell death activates T cell‐mediated immune response, wild‐type C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously inoculated with Hepa1‐6 cells (2 × 106/mouse), When tumors reached ~100 mm3, mice were randomized into four group. Tumor‐bearing mice received intraperitoneal Hypericin (5 mg/kg) every 2 days for three times or vehicle, combined with intraperitoneal injection of anti‐PD‐L1 antibody (100 μg/mouse) or isotype IgG antibody every 3 days for three doses. Tumor dimensions and body weight were measured every other day, with volume calculated as (Length × Width2)/2.

For the liver orthotopic HCC model, 6‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mice were hydrodynamically injected with pT3‐EF1a‐c‐Myc (13 μg/mouse), pT3‐EF1a‐myr‐Akt (7 μg/mouse), and pCMV/SB10 (2 μg/mouse) (Plasmids were kindly provided by Prof. Ningning Liu from Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Science) in sterile saline solution (10% body weight) via tail vein over 5–7 s. All animal procedures were performed under standard housing conditions without hypericin photoactivation, and no intentional light irradiation was applied during animal treatment to avoid photodynamic effects.

Immunohistochemistry

Liver tissues from control and experimental mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (#P0099; Beyotime) and paraffin‐embedded. Sections (3–4 μm thick) underwent deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through an ethanol gradient (100%, 95%, and 70%). Antigen retrieval employed citrate buffer, followed by endogenous peroxidase blockade with 3% H2O2 for 10 min at room temperature. After blocking nonspecific sites with 5% BSA for 30 min, sections were incubated with AKT (1:100), Ki‐67 (1:100), and Cleaved Caspase3 (1:100) primary antibodies for 60 min at room temperature (or overnight at 4°C for increased sensitivity). HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 30–60 min before immunoreactivity visualization using DAB. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated through an alcohol gradient, and mounted with permanent medium.

Results

Hypericin Inhibited HCC Cell Proliferation and Induced Mitochondria‐Mediated Apoptosis

To identify potent inhibitors of HCC treatment, a TCM monomer library was screened using the cell counting kit‐8 assay. The results identified hypericin, which effectively reduced the viability of the HCC cell line HepG2.2.15 (Figure 1A,B). Furthermore, validated these findings using two additional HCC cell lines. The results revealed that hypericin significantly inhibited HCC cell proliferation in a dose‐ and time‐dependent manner (Figure 1C,D). The half‐maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of hypericin at 48 h were 1.83 μM for HepG2.2.15, 0.83 μM for HepG2, and 1.13 μM for Huh7. Transwell migration assays demonstrated that hypericin significantly impaired HCC cell migration (Figure 1E). Additionally, colony formation assays revealed that hypericin treatment significantly suppressed the clonogenic ability of Huh7 and HepG2 cells (Figure 1F), further confirming its potent antiproliferative effect.

To further elucidate the mechanism underlying hypericin‐induced cell death, HCC cells were treated with hypericin in the presence of various cell death inhibitors for 48 h. Figure 1G,H and Figure S1 depict that Z‐VAD and 3‐MA significantly rescued hypericin‐induced cell death, indicating the involvement of apoptotic and autophagic pathways in hypericin‐mediated cytotoxicity. Furthermore, Annexin V‐APC/SYTOX staining followed by flow cytometry confirmed that hypericin induced apoptosis in HepG2 and Huh7 cells dose‐dependently (Figure 1I,J). Western blotting revealed increased cleaved caspase‐3 expression and reduced levels of the anti‐apoptotic proteins XIAP, Bcl‐XL, and MCL‐1 (Figure 1K,L). These findings suggest that hypericin primarily inhibits HCC cell proliferation by inducing mitochondria‐mediated apoptosis.

Hypericin Activated Autophagy and Suppressed the PI3K/AKT Signaling Pathway in HCC Cells

To elucidate the mechanism underlying hypericin‐induced apoptosis in HCC cells, we treated HepG2 cells with hypericin for 48 h and performed RNA‐seq analysis. The analysis revealed 996 upregulated and 1296 downregulated genes (fold change > 1.5; adjusted p value [p adj] < 0.05) in hypericin‐treated cells than in untreated controls (Figure 2A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis demonstrated the significant upregulation of autophagy‐related pathways and downregulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway in hypericin‐treated HepG2 cells (Figure 2B).

We further investigated the effects of hypericin on autophagy in Huh7 cells. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis confirmed a substantial increase in autophagosome formation following hypericin treatment (Figure 2C). Using mCherry‐EGFP‐LC3 to monitor autophagic flux, we observed an increase in mCherry+GFP− LC3 puncta (red), indicating enhanced autolysosome formation in hypericin‐treated HCC cells (Figure 2D). To obtain direct visual evidence of hypericin‐induced autophagy‐dependent AKT degradation, we performed AKT‐LC3B colocalization assays in vehicle‐ or hypericin‐treated Huh7 and HepG2 cells. Figure 2F (Huh7) and G (HepG2) indicate that hypericin significantly increased the colocalization of AKT (iFluor‐647, gray‐white) and mCherry‐LC3B+ autophagic structures (red) compared to the vehicle, verifying AKT recruitment to autophagic structures during hypericin treatment. Western blotting supported these findings, exhibiting increased LC3B‐II conversion and Beclin‐1 levels alongside reduced p62/SQSTM1, ULK1, and p‐ULK1‐Ser757 expression (Figure 2E). The increase in red fluorescence in immunofluorescence assays and the dose‐dependent reduction in p62 protein levels upon hypericin treatment indicate that hypericin induces autophagy in HCC cells rather than causing late‐stage autophagic blockade.

Hypericin also modulated the PI3K/AKT pathway in HCC cell lines, reducing the phosphorylation of PI3K, AKT, mTOR, and S6 while lowering the total protein levels of key pathway components (Figure 2H). Hypericin had minimal effect on AKT mRNA levels, ruling out transcriptional regulation as the primary mechanism (Figure S2).

Consistent with prior reports demonstrating hypericin‐PDT's effects on NF‐κB and p38 MAPK pathways in other cell types, we confirmed NF‐κB inhibition and p38 MAPK activation in hypericin‐treated HepG2 and Huh7 cells (Figure 2I,J). These results indicate that hypericin promotes autophagy in HCC cells, likely by suppressing the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway.

Hypericin Strongly Inhibited HCC Tumor Growth In Vivo

We evaluated the antitumor effects of hypericin using a mouse Hepa1‐6 HCC model. Initial in vitro experiments demonstrated that hypericin reduced Hepa1‐6 cell proliferation dose‐ and time‐dependently, with an IC50 of 9.54 μM at 48 h (Figure 3A,B). To determine whether hypericin induces autophagic apoptosis, we cotreated the cells with Z‐VAD, 3‐MA, Fer‐1, and Nec‐1 for 48 h. Z‐VAD and 3‐MA significantly attenuated hypericin‐induced cell death (Figure 3C,D and Figure S3). Flow cytometry confirmed a dose‐dependent increase in Annexin‐V‐APC+SYTOX+ apoptotic cells (Figure 3E). Western blotting analysis revealed that hypericin downregulated XIAP, Bcl‐XL, and MCL‐1 while elevating cleaved Caspase‐3. Additionally, it suppressed AKT/mTOR signaling and enhanced autophagic activity (Figures 3G and S4).

For in vivo validation, we subcutaneously implanted Hepa1‐6 cells into Rag1‐deficient mice. When tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, hypericin or vehicle was administered via intraperitoneal injection. Hypericin markedly inhibited tumor growth without affecting body weight, indicating good tolerability (Figure 3H–L). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining revealed reduced Ki‐67 and AKT expression in treated tumors (Figure 3M), while Western blotting confirmed AKT/mTOR pathway suppression (Figure 3N). Hypericin effectively suppresses Hepa1‐6 HCC proliferation in vitro and in vivo by modulating the AKT/mTOR pathway.

Hypericin Triggered HCC Cell Death via the Autophagy‐Lysosome Pathway‐Mediated AKT Degradation

Given the significant AKT downregulation in hypericin‐treated HCC cells, we tested whether AKT overexpression could counteract this effect. Lentiviral transduction introduced constitutively active myr‐AKT into HepG2 and Huh7 cells; however, AKT overexpression failed to prevent hypericin‐induced cell death (Figure S5A). Western blotting confirmed that hypericin degraded overexpressed AKT (Figure S5B). To determine whether proteasomal or lysosomal pathways mediated this degradation, we treated HCC cells with hypericin in the presence of proteasome or lysosome inhibitors. Flow cytometry revealed that lysosomal inhibitors (3‐MA or chloroquine (CQ)) substantially mitigated hypericin‐induced cell death (Figure 4A and Figure S1B), whereas the proteasome inhibitor MG132 had no effect (Figure S5C). Western blotting analysis revealed that 3‐MA and CQ restored AKT levels and downstream signaling (Figure 4B,C), whereas MG132 did not (Figure S5D). These results indicate that hypericin predominantly induces HCC cell death via autophagy‐lysosome‐dependent AKT degradation rather than proteasomal pathways.

To further confirm that Hypericin‐induced AKT degradation is mediated through the autophagy–lysosome pathway, we generated stable ATG5‐knockdown HCC cell lines using lentiviral shRNA transduction. As shown in Figure S6A,B, Hypericin treatment significantly reduced cell viability in shNC‐transduced Huh7 and HepG2 cells, while this effect was largely rescued in shATG5 cells. Consistent with the cell viability data, immunoblot analysis revealed that Hypericin treatment led to a marked reduction in AKT, p‐AKT, and downstream effector proteins including XIAP, MCL‐1, and p‐S6 in shNC cells (Figure S6C,D). In contrast, these proteins were significantly restored in shATG5 cells following Hypericin exposure. These results indicate that inhibition of autophagy via ATG5 depletion blocks Hypericin‐induced AKT degradation and partially reverses the cytotoxic effects of Hypericin, providing direct evidence that AKT is degraded through the autophagy pathway in response to Hypericin treatment.

To assess whether blocking autolysosomal AKT degradation reverses the antitumor effects of hypericin in vivo, we used hydrodynamic tail vein injection to establish myr‐AKT/c‐Myc‐driven HCC tumors in mice. One week after oncogene delivery, hypericin was intraperitoneally administered with or without CQ. Hypericin‐treated mice exhibited significantly reduced liver tumor burden, lower liver weight, and lower liver‐to‐body weight ratios compared to vehicle controls, without affecting overall body weight, suggesting its minimal toxicity. IHC analysis revealed diminished Ki‐67 and AKT and elevated cleaved caspase‐3 levels in hypericin‐treated tumors. As anticipated, CQ cotreatment attenuated these effects, restoring tumor burden, Ki‐67, and AKT levels while suppressing cleaved caspase‐3 (Figure 4D–J). These findings establish the autophagy‐lysosome pathway as central to hypericin‐induced AKT degradation and HCC cell death in vitro and in vivo.

Hypericin Promoted AKT Autophagic Degradation via HSPA5

To elucidate how hypericin triggers AKT degradation through autophagy, we treated Flag‐AKT‐overexpressing Huh7 cells with hypericin in the presence or absence of 3‐MA for 24 h. Immunoprecipitation coupled with LC–MS/MS (IP‐MS) was performed to identify AKT‐binding proteins in hypericin‐and vehicle‐treated Flag‐AKT‐reconstituted Huh7 cells, with the top 15 proteins with the highest scores from the screening results presented in Figure S7 and Table S1. Quantitative analysis using intensity‐based absolute quantification (iBAQ) values revealed that HSPA5 enrichment was significantly higher in hypericin‐treated cells than in vehicle‐treated controls (Figure 5A). Coimmunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assays confirmed that hypericin strengthened the interaction between AKT and HSPA5 in Huh7 cells transfected with either Flag‐AKT or Myc‐HSPA5 (Figure 5B,C). To verify the specificity of the hypericin‐triggered AKT‐HSPA5 interaction and rule out ER stress‐related artifacts, we performed a series of controlled experiments. Hypericin treatment did not increase the levels of core ER stress markers (CHOP, PERK, p‐eIF2α) in Huh7 and HepG2 cells (Figure S8A,B), indicating that the canonical ER stress pathway was not activated under our experimental conditions. To further substantiate this finding, we utilized SubA, a potent ER stress inducer, as a noncytotoxic comparator to rule out ER stress‐mediated artifacts. In Flag‐AKT–overexpressing Huh7 cells, SubA strongly induced ER stress but failed to promote AKT‐HSPA5 association, whereas hypericin clearly enhanced this interaction (Figure S8C). These results confirm that hypericin‐induced AKT‐HSPA5 binding is independent of ER stress.

CRISPR‐Cas9‐mediated HSPA5 knockout attenuated hypericin‐induced proliferation inhibition (Figure 5D,E), while flow cytometry demonstrated that HSPA5 downregulation significantly reduced hypericin‐triggered cell death in HepG2 and Huh7 cells (Figure 5F,G). Western blotting indicated that HSPA5 depletion restored PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activity and reversed the hypericin‐mediated suppression of pS6 and MCL‐1 (Figure 5H). HSPA5 knockout also blocked hypericin‐induced autophagy (Figure 5I,J), demonstrating that HSPA5 ablation disrupts hypericin‐dependent AKT degradation and HCC cell death.

To assess the role of HSPA5 in hypericin‐mediated tumor suppression in vivo, we generated Hspa5‐deficient Hepa1‐6 cells. Consistent with the in vitro findings, Hspa5 knockdown diminished hypericin‐induced cell death and inhibited the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway (Figure 5K–N). Subcutaneous implantation of Hspa5‐knockout or control Hepa1‐6 cells into Rag1 −/− mice, followed by hypericin treatment, revealed that hypericin significantly suppressed tumor growth in controls but not in Hspa5‐deficient tumors (Figure 5O–Q). Immunoblotting confirmed that hypericin failed to inhibit PI3K/AKT signaling or downregulate MCL‐1 and XIAP in Hspa5‐deficient tumors (Figure 5R). These findings establish that hypericin induces HCC cell death by facilitating HSPA5‐dependent autophagic degradation of AKT.

Hypericin‐Induced Autophagic Degradation of AKT Depended on S473 Phosphorylation Status

Previous studies have reported that AKT phosphorylation at serine 473 is vital for activating downstream signaling pathways and promoting tumor cell survival [36]. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that overexpression of wild‐type (WT) AKT failed to rescue cells from hypericin‐induced death. Similarly, the kinase‐dead mutant AKTS473A also could not restore cell survival; in contrast, cells expressing the constitutively active phosphor‐mimetic mutant AKTS473D showed significantly improved survival, indicating that only functionally active AKT could exert a protective effect (Figure 6A,B). However, Western blot analysis showed that following hypericin treatment, WT AKT was significantly degraded, while AKTS473D mutant and AKTS473A mutant were not. Overexpression of WT AKT and AKTS473A mutant could not reverse hypericin reduced phosphorylation of downstream effectors (mTOR, S6) and decreased expression of anti‐apoptotic proteins (XIAP, MCL‐1); whereas these inhibitory effects were significantly attenuated in cells expressing the S473D mutant (Figure 6C–E).

To explore the underlying mechanism, we examined the interaction between AKT and HSPA5, a key mediator of chaperone‐mediated autophagy. Coimmunoprecipitation assays showed that the interaction between AKT and HSPA5 was dependent on the phosphorylation status at Ser473, where WT AKT bound strongly to HSPA5, both the phosphor‐mimetic (S473D) and kinase‐dead (S473A) mutants failed to interact with HSPA5 (Figure 6F). Notably, the AKTS473D‐mediated rescue occurs independently of HSPA5 binding. Mutation of AKT Ser473 disrupts the interaction with HSPA5, suggesting that the structural integrity of this region, not only the phosphorylation status, may be critical for binding. Furthermore, the decrease in p‐AKT (Ser473) appears greater than that of total AKT, which should be investigated in the future to assess the possibility that hypericin may also affect Ser473 phosphorylation independently of protein degradation.

Collectively, these results indicate that the phosphorylation status of AKT at Ser473 regulates its interaction with HSPA5 and its subsequent degradation by autophagy.

Hypericin Promoted Antitumor Immunity and Synergized With Anti‐PD‐L1 Antibody Therapy

T cells, particularly CD8+ T cells, are vital for inhibiting tumor growth [37]. To determine whether hypericin‐induced cell death activates T cell‐mediated immunity, we established Hepa1‐6 tumors in C57BL/6 mice. When tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice received hypericin or vehicle via intraperitoneal injection every other day for three treatments. Hypericin treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth without affecting body weight (Figure 7A,B). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated increased CD8+ T cell infiltration in hypericin‐treated tumors, while CD4+ T cell proportions remained unchanged, indicating selective enhancement of cytotoxic T cell responses (Figure 7C). Tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ and CD4+ T cells from hypericin‐treated mice produced higher levels of interferon (IFN)‐γ and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α compared to controls (Figure 7D,E). Hypericin treatment also differentially modulated T cell exhaustion markers: it reduced Tim3+, PD‐1+, and double‐positive CD8+ T cell populations, while decreasing Tim3+ expression on CD4+ T cells (Figure 7F–I).

Given the clinical relevance of PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade in HCC [38, 39], we studied whether hypericin could enhance anti‐PD‐L1 therapy. Mice bearing Hepa1‐6 tumors were administered vehicle, hypericin, anti‐PD‐L1 antibody, or combination treatments. Although both monotherapies reduced tumor growth and improved survival, combination therapy exhibited superior efficacy without affecting body weight (Figure 7J–L). These findings suggest that hypericin remodels the tumor microenvironment and sensitizes HCC to anti‐PD‐L1 therapy.

Hypericin Exhibited no Adverse Effects in Mice

To assess the safety of hypericin, we administered eight intraperitoneal injections to wild‐type C57BL/6 mice at 48 h intervals. The treatment caused no significant body weight loss or gross pathological changes in the spleen, kidney, or liver (Figure S9A–D). Plasma ALT and AST levels in hypericin‐treated mice were comparable to those in the placebo controls (Figure S9E,F). Further assessment of additional plasma biochemical indicators associated with hepatic and renal function—including TBIL, DBIL, ALB, ALP, UREA, CREA and UA—also revealed no significant differences between the hypericin and placebo groups (Figure S9G–M). Histological analysis via hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed no morphological differences in these organs between the treatment groups (Figure S9N). These findings demonstrate the in vivo safety of hypericin in this preclinical model.

Discussion

Over the past two decades, despite significant advances in surgical techniques and drug development, HCC treatment has reached a bottleneck worldwide. The 5‐year survival rate remains < 20% in patients with advanced, recurrent, or metastatic HCC [40]. Consequently, effective adjuvant therapies are urgently needed to improve patient survival. In this study, we identified hypericin as a compound that effectively inhibits HCC cell proliferation, migration, and colony formation, while inducing mitochondria‐mediated apoptosis in vitro. Furthermore, we demonstrated that hypericin suppresses tumor progression in a subcutaneous HCC Hepa1‐6 mouse model in both Rag1 −/− and wild‐type C57BL/6 mice. Mechanistically, RNA‐seq analysis revealed that hypericin may induce HCC cell death by modulating the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway and autophagy.

Although this study demonstrated a significant anti‐HCC effect of hypericin without photodynamic therapy (PDT) activation, this finding may appear inconsistent with previous reports focused on hypericin‐based PDT, in which hypericin alone exhibited no obvious inhibitory effect on HCC growth. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by fundamental differences in the experimental designs of the two study types. Most previous PDT‐focused studies relied on short‐term hypericin incubation followed by brief light irradiation to trigger anti‐HCC activity, which is highly dependent on reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts induced by light activation. Therefore, hypericin alone without light exerts no significant anti‐HCC effect under such short‐term incubation conditions. Conversely, all experiments in this study were conducted without light irradiation, and we adopted a long‐term hypericin treatment duration of 24–48 h in vitro, combined with sustained intermittent administration in vivo. This experimental design allowed hypericin to exert anti‐HCC effects through PDT‐independent mechanisms identified in our study, including the regulation of autophagy/AKT signaling pathways in HCC cells and remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment.

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is activated in approximately 50% of patients with HCC and plays a critical role in hepatocarcinogenesis and disease progression [6]. Recent studies have also indicated that AKT is involved in metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis‐driven HCC [27, 41]. Several selective AKT inhibitors, including Ipatasertib and Capivasertib, have been approved or are in phase III clinical trials for treating metastatic castration‐resistant prostate cancer or hormone receptor–positive advanced breast cancer [30, 31, 33, 42]. These drugs are selective ATP‐competitive small‐molecule inhibitors that target all three AKT isoforms. We identified that hypericin significantly inhibited mTOR and S6 phosphorylation by promoting AKT protein degradation. Moreover, we demonstrated that autophagy or lysosome inhibitors (3‐MA and CQ), but not the proteasome inhibitor MG‐132, effectively rescued AKT protein levels and downstream signaling suppressed by hypericin.

A previous study reported that proteasome inhibitors increase arginyltransferase 1‐mediated R‐HSPA5 levels in an ROS‐dependent manner, and that R‐HSPA5 interacts with fully ubiquitinated AKT to promote its degradation via the autophagy‐lysosome pathway [43]. In this study, we used multiple biochemical approaches, including immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry, to demonstrate that hypericin induces the interaction between AKT and HSPA5. To further assess this mechanism, we knocked out HSPA5 in human Huh7 and HepG2 cells and Hspa5 in murine Hepa1‐6 cells using the CRISPR‐Cas9 system. HSPA5/Hspa5 depletion significantly rescued AKT from autophagic degradation, restored downstream pathway activation, reduced cell death in vitro, and attenuated tumor growth in vivo. Under hypericin treatment, overexpressed WT AKT was significantly degraded, whereas the AKTS473D and AKTS473A mutants remained stable. Overexpression of AKTS473D mutant reverse hypericin reduced phosphorylation of downstream effectors and expression of anti‐apoptotic proteins, whereas cells transfected with WT AKT or the AKTS473A mutant did not show this effect. Coimmunoprecipitation assays further revealed that WT AKT bound strongly to HSPA5, both the phosphor‐mimetic (S473D) and kinase‐dead (S473A) mutants failed to interact with HSPA5, suggesting that the AKTS473D‐mediated rescue occurs independently of HSPA5 binding. In Figure 6, the decrease in p‐AKT (Ser473) appears more pronounced than that of total AKT, a discrepancy that warrants further investigation to assess whether it may also affect Ser473 phosphorylation independently of protein degradation.

Autophagy is a conserved and dynamic catabolic process essential for cellular homeostasis. It involves the sequestration of cytoplasmic components (including damaged organelles, protein aggregates, and pathogens) into double‐membrane vesicles called autophagosomes, which subsequently fuse with lysosomes for degradation [44]. The role of autophagy in HCC is context‐dependent and can be either tumor‐suppressive or tumor‐promoting, depending on the upstream signals and treatment conditions [45, 46, 47, 48, 49]. These data indicate that hypericin‐induced autophagy is essential for mediating cell death and promoting AKT degradation. Using an AKT/c‐Myc‐driven hydrodynamic transfection‐based orthotopic HCC mouse model, we observed that hypericin treatment significantly suppressed tumor progression. Moreover, the autophagy‐lysosome inhibitor CQ effectively reversed hypericin‐mediated inhibition of orthotopic HCC growth.

To date, the immunomodulatory effects of hypericin have not been reported and warrant further investigation. In this study, we treated wild‐type C57BL/6 mice bearing subcutaneous Hepa1‐6 tumors with hypericin and observed a significant increase in CD8+ T cell infiltration into the tumor microenvironment, enhanced secretion of effector cytokines (IFN‐γ and TNF‐α) by CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, and reduced exhaustion of CD8+ T cells. However, the precise mechanisms underlying these effects remain to be elucidated in future studies. Furthermore, in vivo experiments demonstrated that the combination of hypericin with an anti‐PD‐L1 antibody significantly suppressed tumor progression and prolonged the survival of tumor‐bearing mice compared to either treatment alone, without causing notable side effects. These findings suggest that hypericin may serve as a tumor microenvironment modulating agent with the potential to enhance the efficacy of anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 immune checkpoint blockade therapy in HCC.

In summary, this study reveals that hypericin induces HCC cell death by autophagic degradation of AKT, thereby activating CD8+ T cell‐mediated antitumor immunity, and synergistically enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of anti‐PD‐L1 immunotherapy (Figure 8). These results suggest that hypericin is a promising candidate for HCC treatment and warrants further evaluation in clinical trials.
