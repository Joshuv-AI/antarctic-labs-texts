Human iPSC‐derived GABAergic interneuron transplantation restores circuit balance and cognitive function in an Alzheimer's disease model
Authors: Xinzhe Zhang, Gaojie Wang, Haodong Chen, Ye Sun, Ziteng Wangliu, Lijing Wang, Miao Sun, Qing Liu, Xue Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAlzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by disrupted excitatory–inhibitory (E:I) balance and impaired synaptic function, yet current treatments fail to repair these fundamental circuit impairments.METHODSHuman induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived post‐mitotic medial ganglionic eminence‐originated inhibitory neurons (MGE‐pINs) were bilaterally transplanted into the hippocampus of 10‐month‐old 5xFAD mice. Cell transplantation effects were assessed by behavioral analysis, electrophysiology, immunofluorescence staining, immunoblotting, and RNA sequencing analysis.RESULTSMGE‐pIN integration restored local inhibition, correcting E:I imbalance and suppressing electroencephalogram (EEG)‐detected epileptiform discharges. This network recovery, underpinned by normalized receptor subunit levels and restored synaptic plasticity – as evidenced by long‐term potentiation recordings, morphological analysis, and transcriptomic profiling – led to the rescue of cognitive deficits. Importantly, these functional benefits occurred independently of amyloid beta levels.DISCUSSIONThe study's findings suggest that targeted interneuron replacement can reverse network dysregulation and cognitive decline in AD, underscoring the potential of cell‐based modulation as a route to restore brain function in neurodegenerative disorders.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, cell therapy, cognitive function, excitatory–inhibitory balance, medial ganglionic eminence

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common form of dementia, characterized by the accumulation of extracellular amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and intraneuronal aggregates of hyperphosphorylated tau (p‐tau) known as neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 AD patients exhibit a progressive decline in cognitive abilities, impacting their quality of life. 5 , 6 Various animal models of AD have been established to study the mechanisms of the disease and explore potential therapeutic approaches, significantly advancing our understanding of the basic biology of AD. 7 , 8 Despite the existence of three US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved medications that demonstrate some capacity to slow the progression of AD, clinical treatment remains significantly challenged by the lack of effective therapies that can enhance overall disease outcomes. 9 , 10 Consequently, it is essential to develop innovative therapeutic strategies aimed at effectively improving the condition.

AD is associated with inhibitory interneuron dysfunction or loss. 11 , 12 Pallial GABAergic neurons originate from the subpallial region of the medial ganglionic eminence (MGE), where they mature and acquire distinct neurochemical and physiological characteristics before tangentially migrating to the cortical and hippocampal regions, ultimately achieving functional integration. 13 , 14 These GABAergic neurons play a crucial role in various neural activities, influencing cell proliferation and differentiation and maintaining the excitatory–inhibitory (E:I) balance within neural circuits, which is essential for cognitive functions through the modulation of GABAergic receptors and inhibitory synapses. 15 , 16 Given their critical functions, damage to GABAergic neurons has been implicated in a range of neurological disorders. 11 , 12 , 17 Research has demonstrated that human MGE‐derived GABAergic interneurons can mitigate seizure activity and abnormal behaviors in epileptic mouse models. 18 , 19 Furthermore, studies have shown that the transplantation of MGE‐derived GABAergic interneurons can enhance cognitive functions and comorbid seizure in AD animal models. 20 Collectively, these findings highlight the significant therapeutic potential of human MGE‐derived GABAergic interneurons in the treatment of neurological diseases.

Recent advances highlight that GABAergic interneuron loss exacerbates network hyperexcitability and accelerates tau pathology in AD. 10 While neural stem cell transplantation shows transient benefits, its inability to restore precise E:I balance and risks of tumorigenesis limit clinical translation. 19 , 21 Human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)‐derived MGE progenitors without proliferative ability offer a unique solution capable of targeted migration, subtype‐specific integration, and stable GABAergic function without proliferation risks. 20 , 21 Here, we asked whether these properties could be harnessed to simultaneously correct circuit dysfunction and modify AD pathogenesis. The generation of MGE‐derived pallial GABAergic neurons originating from hiPSCs has been extensively documented. 22 , 23

Safe and integrative MGE‐derived pallial inhibitory neurons (MGE‐pINs) represent key elements for effective cell therapy. In our study, LHX6 highly expressed MGE‐pINs that exhibit migratory capabilities and exit the cell cycle 22 , 23 are transplanted into the hippocampus of a 10‐month‐old 5xFAD mouse model, a widely utilized model of AD. 24 These MGE‐pINs differentiate into specific GABAergic subtypes, achieve chemical and electrophysiological maturation, and functionally integrate into the host neural circuitry. Here, we demonstrate that the transplantation rescues behavioral deficits by re‐establishing the E:I balance and restoring synaptic plasticity across molecular, structural, and functional levels, a therapeutic efficacy independent of amyloid load. Our findings identify interneuron‐mediated restoration of E:I balance as a critical circuit‐specific strategy to reverse functional disconnection in AD, providing a promising cellular approach for recovering network integrity and cognitive resilience.

The MGE‐derived pallial GABAergic inhibitory interneurons described in this study were derived from iPSCs generated by reprogramming peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from a 39‐year‐old healthy female with APOE ε3/ε3 genotype and no known pathogenic variants in genes associated with neurological disorders. PBMC extraction was conducted in the Clinical Biobank (ISO 20387) of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH). PBMCs were transduced with Sendai virus (A16517, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to introduce reprogramming factors, including octamer‐binding transcription factor 4 (OCT4), SOX2, cMYC, and KLF4. The iPSCs were cultured on plates pre‐coated with Matrigel (Corning, 354277) using a complete mTeSR1 medium (Stemcell, No. 85850). G‐band analysis confirmed normal chromosomal integrity (46, XX). The cell line was derived, expanded, and stored under current Good Manufacturing Practice conditions using qualified and certified raw materials. The pluripotency of the iPSC cells was confirmed by the expression of pluripotency markers such as TRA‐1‐60, OCT4, and NANOG; the ability to differentiate into endoderm, ectoderm, and mesoderm lineages in vitro; and the formation of teratomas upon transplantation in vivo. Additionally, the pluripotent stem cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination, with no contamination detected.

For passaging the iPSCs, we employed ReLeSR (Stemcell, No. 100‐0483) for colony passaging. During downstream differentiation experiments, Accutase (Stemcell, No. 07920) was used to digest the iPSCs to achieve single‐cell dissociation, facilitating subsequent cell counting.

During the differentiation of MGE organoids, we seeded iPSCs at a density of 60,000 cells/mL into low‐attachment 96‐well V‐bottom plates (MS‐9096VZ, Molecular Devices), resulting in approximately 9000 cells per well to induce the formation of a subpallium embryoid. At this stage, the ROCK inhibitor (Y27632 10 µM; Selleck, S6390) was added to enhance the survival of iPSCs. Two days later, we replaced the culture medium with knockout serum replacement (KSR) basal medium, which consists of DMEM/F12 (85%; Life Technologies, No. 21331) and KSR (15%; Gibco, No. 10828010), supplemented with 1X GlutaMAX (Life Technologies, No. 35050‐061) and 1X Penicillin‐Streptomycin (Life Technologies, No. 15140‐122). Concurrently, we introduced dual SMAD inhibitors, including the TGFβ inhibitor (SB431542 10 µM; Selleck, S1067) and the BMP4 inhibitor (dorsomorphin 5 µM; Selleck, S7306) to promote neuroectoderm differentiation. Additionally, the Wnt pathway inhibitor (XAV939 2 µM; Selleck, S1180) was added to induce forebrain fate commitment, along with the ventralizing factor (purmorphamine 1 µM; Selleck, S3042) to facilitate subpallium patterning.

On day 14 of in vitro differentiation, we replaced the culture medium with N2B27 basal medium, which consisted of Neurobasal‐A (50%; Life Technologies, No. 10888) and DMEM/F12 (50%; Life Technologies, No. 21331), supplemented with 1X GlutaMAX (Life Technologies, No. 35050‐061), B‐27 without 1X Vitamin A (Life Technologies, No. 12587‐010), N2 supplement‐B (1X; Stem Cell Technologies, No. 07156), 1X Penicillin‐Streptomycin (Life Technologies, No. 15140122). Concurrently, purmorphamine was added as a ventralizing factor, along with the neurotrophic factors (FGFb 10 ng/mL; Novoprotein, C046) and EGF (10 ng/mL; MCE, HY‐P7109) to promote more robust ventral forebrain neuroepithelial development. After 21 days of in vitro differentiation, corresponding to 3 weeks, the MGE‐like progenitors embryoid were dissociated into single cells using 0.5X TrypLE (Thermo Fisher Scientific, No. 12604013). The cells were then plated in a monolayer format in 10‐cm2 culture dishes for continued culture.

Monolayer cells differentiated in vitro from week 3 to week 5 were continuously cultured in N2B27 basal medium. During this phase, a mitogen‐activated protein kinase kinase (MEK) inhibitor (PD0325901 2 µM; MCE, HY‐10254) was added to induce the sustained proliferation of MGE progenitor cells and the preliminary differentiation of GABAergic precursor cells. From week 5 to week 7, in addition to the continued treatment with the MEK inhibitor, NOTCH inhibitors (DAPT 10 µM; HY‐13027, MCE) and cyclin‐dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitors (PD0332991 2 µM; MCE, HY‐50767) were introduced to induce cell cycle exit in the cells during this stage. Ultimately, cells were collected for subsequent localization injections into the hippocampus of mice.

Animals

All efficacy studies were conducted as non‐glucagon‐like peptide research in accordance with Neurona Therapeutics IACUC protocol for Alzheimer's disease research, following ARRIVE guidelines. Animal experiments complied with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by Peking Union Medical College (IACUC No. ACUC‐A01‐2021‐050). 5xFAD transgenic mice (Stock No. 008730; RRID:MMRRC_034848‐JAX) were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). A maximum of 48 female C57BL/6 mice, aged approximately 10 to 18 months, were used in each study. The experiments were divided into control, cell transplantation, and non‐transplantation groups. Female mice were selected due to their more consistent cognitive behavioral phenotypes in this model. Upon arrival, each mouse was assigned a unique identification number and marked with ear tags. All mice were housed in the animal facility of Neurona Therapeutics, under standard temperature and humidity conditions, and maintained on a 12‐h light/dark cycle. Mice had free access to bottled water and standard chow and were housed in disposable cages with irradiated Alpha‐Dri bedding. Following cell transplantation, all mice received cyclosporine A (10 mg/kg, subcutaneously) as an immunosuppressant, administered daily.

Bilateral hippocampus transplantation of hiPSC‐derived MGE‐pIN

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane mixed with 2.0 to 2.5 L/min oxygen via calibrated vaporizer continuous isoflurane (1%) mixed with oxygen (2.0 to 2.5 L/min) and then maintained with 0 to 2.5 L/min oxygen using a snout mask. MGE‐pIN cells in microclusters containing an estimated density of 200,000 cells per side were injected into both sides of the hippocampus. The final cell density for injection was 1,000,000 cells/mL, with 2 µL of the cell suspension in PBS injected unilaterally into the hippocampus (2 µL per hemisphere). The injection coordinates were anteroposterior −3.25 mm, L ± 3.0 mm, V −2.9 mm with a Hamilton syringe mounted on a Kopf stereotaxic instrument (Kopf, Tujunga, CA, USA) with a mouse adapter (Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL, USA). To serve as sham controls, WT and 5xFAD mice were injected with an equal volume of PBS vehicle bilaterally into the same stereotaxic coordinates.

Immunocytochemistry staining for cells and brain slices

For tissue immunostaining, anesthetized mice were perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were post‐fixed and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose before Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) embedding and frozen sectioning. After antigen retrieval in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0), sections were permeabilized and blocked with 1X PBS containing 0.3% Triton X‐100 (0694, Amresco) and 5% blocking serum (goat serum SL038, Solarbio; or donkey serum YZ‐017‐000‐121, Solarbio). Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (for antibody list see Table S1). Subsequently, sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (10 µg/mL; ID2250, Solarbio) for 5 min and mounted with anti‐fade mounting medium (ab104135, Abcam). Images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 STED 3X confocal microscope.

For cell immunofluorescence (IF), cells cultured on 12‐mm2 coverslips were fixed with 4% PFA (G1101, Servicebio) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After fixation, cells were permeabilized and blocked with 1X PBS containing 0.3% Triton X‐100 and 5% blocking serum. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. Subsequently, cells were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (10 µg/mL; ID2250, Solarbio) for 5 min. Images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 STED 3X confocal microscope. The list of antibodies used in this study is detailed in Table S1.

Golgi staining

Mice were deeply anesthetized, and brains were rapidly removed from the skull. After a brief rinse with distilled water to remove surface blood, the brains were immediately immersed in Golgi fixative (G1069, Servicebio). Tissue blocks were then incubated in Golgi staining solution (G1069‐1, Servicebio) at room temperature for 14 days, with the solution replaced every 2 days. Following staining, tissues were transferred to a processing solution (G1069‐3, Servicebio) and maintained at 4°C in the dark for 3 days; the processing solution (G1069‐2, Servicebio) was replaced once during this period. The tissues were sectioned into 60‐µm slices using a vibratome (VT1000S, Leica). Sections were developed in Golgi developing solution for 30 min, rinsed with water, and mounted. Images were acquired using a digital slide scanner (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH) and analyzed using CaseViewer 2.4 software.

Behavioral analysis

Open field test

The open field test was used to assess the locomotor activity, exploratory behavior, and anxiety‐like emotions of animals. In the experiment, animals underwent two independent tests at 2 and 3 months post‐transplantation (MPT). An open field area (60 × 60 cm for each) was used to analyze multiple animals simultaneously. At the beginning of the experiment, animals were placed at the center of the open field and allowed to explore freely for 10 min before being returned to their cages. The TopScan behavior analysis system (CleverSys) was used to analyze the locomotor behavior of mice, including total time spent in the center of the maze, movement speed, and distance traveled. The open field equipment was cleaned with 70% ethanol between each animal to minimize odor cues. Statistical analysis was performed using one‐way ANOVA.

Novel Object Recognition (NOR) test

The NOR test was used to assess the short‐term learning and memory capabilities of animals. In the experiment, an open testing box (60 × 60 cm) was used, containing two different objects: one novel object (TN) and one familiar object (TF). The experimental procedure consisted of three phases: adaptation phase, familiarization phase, and testing phase. During the adaptation phase, mice were placed in the testing box and allowed to explore freely for 10 min without any objects, with the aim of acclimating them to the environment and reducing anxiety associated with the new setting. After the adaptation phase, the TF was placed in the testing box, allowing the mice to interact with it for 10 min while recording the exploration time spent with the familiar object. At the end of the familiarization phase, the TF was removed, and the TN was introduced. The mice were then placed back into the testing box and allowed to freely explore between the TN and TF for another 10 min. During this phase, the exploration time spent with both the TN and the TF was recorded. A camera positioned above the box was used to track the time spent exploring the TN and the TF. The discrimination index (DI) was calculated by comparing the difference in exploration time between the TN and the TF to the sum of the exploration times for both objects. A higher DI indicates better recognition memory.

Elevated plus maze test

The elevated plus maze (EPM) test is based on the natural tendency of mice to exhibit anxiety in open spaces while feeling safe in enclosed spaces, and it is used to measure anxiety‐like behaviors in animals. Each mouse was placed in the central area of a maze shaped like a cross, consisting of two open arms and two enclosed arms (35 cm long, 5 cm wide, and 50 cm high; RWD, Shenzhen, China), facing one of the open arms. The TrackingMaster video imaging system was used to measure the time spent by the mice in the open arms and enclosed arms, as well as the number of entries into the open arms. Longer durations spent in the open arms and more frequent entries into the open arms typically indicate lower levels of anxiety.

Y‐maze test

The Y‐maze alternation test is used to assess exploratory behavior and spatial memory in mice. The Y‐maze consists of three arms (A, B, C), each providing a distinct visual cue, and the TrackingMaster video imaging system was used to measure the mice's behavioral activity over a 10‐min period. In the novel arm exploration experiment, during the familiarization phase, one arm of the Y‐maze (designated as the novel arm, e.g., Arm C) was blocked, allowing the mice to freely explore the remaining two arms (the familiar arms, Arms A and B) for 10 min. After this phase, the blockade was removed, and the mice were reintroduced to the Y‐maze, allowing them to freely explore between the novel arm (Arm C) and the familiar arms (Arms A and B) for another 10 min. The number of entries and time spent in the novel arm (Arm C) versus the familiar arms (Arms A and B) were analyzed. Increased exploration of the novel arm (higher entries and longer duration) indicates enhanced spatial recognition memory.

In the spontaneous alternation experiment, the number of times the mice switched from one arm to another during exploration was recorded (defined as the number of alternations). A higher spontaneous alternation rate (spontaneous alternations [%] = number of alternations [n]/[N−2] × 100%) indicates better working memory abilities in the mice (N was defined as total arm entries).

Morris water maze

The Morris water maze test was conducted to assess spatial learning and memory abilities in mice. A circular pool with a diameter of 120 cm was used, with water temperature maintained at 22 ± 1°C. Distinct spatial visual cues were placed around the pool walls. A hidden platform (10 cm in diameter) was positioned in the target quadrant, submerged 1 cm below the water surface. The experiment consisted of a 5‐day acquisition phase with four trials per day. During each trial, mice were released from different starting points, and the escape latency to find the platform was recorded (maximum 60 s). Mice were allowed to remain on the platform for 15 s after successful location. A probe trial was conducted 24 h after the final acquisition session, during which the platform was removed and mice were allowed to swim freely for 60 s. Parameters including time spent in the target quadrant, number of platform crossings, and escape latency were analyzed. All experiments were performed at consistent times of day. Behavioral data were automatically recorded and analyzed using a video tracking system (EthoVision XT).

ELISA analysis

Brain tissues were weighed and homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (P0013C, Beyotime; 10 µL/mg) supplemented with phosphatase (4906845001, Roche) and protease inhibitors (11836153001, Roche). Lysates were incubated on ice for 40 min and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected as the RIPA‐soluble fraction. The remaining pellets were re‐suspended in 6 M guanidine hydrochloride (SRE0066, Sigma; 10 µL/mg initial weight) containing the same inhibitors. Samples were vortexed for 15 s every 10 min for 1 h on ice, followed by centrifugation to obtain the guanidine‐soluble fraction. Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels were quantified using specific ELISA kits (KMB3481 and KMB3441, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm (Epoch, BioTek).

Western blot (WB) analysis

Brain tissues were weighed and homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (P0013C, Beyotime; 10 µL/mg) supplemented with phosphatase (4906845001, Roche) and protease inhibitors (11836153001, Roche) using magnetic beads. Homogenates were incubated on ice for 40 min and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to collect the supernatant as whole‐cell lysate. Protein loading buffer (9173, TAKARA) was added, and samples were boiled at 99°C for 5 min. A prestained protein marker (M221, Genstar) was used for size reference. Antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Electroencephalogram (EEG) recording

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and secured in a prone position. Subdermal needle electrodes were inserted into the temporal region, with a ground electrode placed on the tail. EEG signals (0.5 to 64 Hz) were amplified and acquired using a PowerLab system and LabChart 8 software. Each mouse underwent 30 min of continuous recording. Epileptiform events were quantified using the following criteria: duration > 5 s, frequency > 0.3 Hz, and amplitude > 2‐fold of the individual baseline.

Electrophysiological recordings

Brain slice preparation

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with ice‐cold dissection buffer to ensure tissue viability. The brain was quickly removed, and coronal slices (300 µm thickness) containing the hippocampus were prepared using a vibratome (VT1200S, Leica, Germany). The cutting was performed in ice‐cold dissection buffer containing (in mM): 210 sucrose, 25 NaHCO3, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 0.5 CaCl2, 7 MgSO4, 10 d‐glucose, 1.3 L‐ascorbic acid, and 3 Na‐pyruvate. Slices were recovered at 32°C for 30 min and then maintained at room temperature for at least 30 min in standard artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) containing (in mM) 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, 12.5 d‐glucose, 2 CaCl2, and 1.3 MgCl2. All solutions were continuously bubbled with 95% O2/5% CO2 (pH 7.3 to 7.4).

Whole‐cell recording setup

Individual slices were transferred to a submerged recording chamber and continuously perfused with oxygenated ACSF (2 to 6 mL/min) at 32.5°C. Neurons were visualized using infrared differential interference contrast (IR‐DIC) microscopy. Patch pipettes were pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries (Harvard Apparatus) to a resistance of 3 to 5 MΩ. Recordings were performed using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices), digitized with a Digidata 1440A, and acquired using pCLAMP 10 software. Data were filtered at 2 kHz and sampled at 10 kHz. Series resistance (Rs) was monitored throughout the experiment; cells with Rs > 25 MΩ or showing a > 20% change were excluded.

AP recording

A K‐Gluconate‐based internal solution was used, containing (in mM) 137 K‐Gluconate, 5 KCl, 10 HEPES, 0.2 EGTA, 10 Na2‐phosphocreatin, 4 Mg‐ATP, and 0.5 Na‐GTP (pH 7.3, 285 mOsm). Resting membrane potential (RMP) was recorded immediately after membrane rupture in current‐clamp mode. To evaluate ionic currents, the amplifier was temporarily switched to voltage‐clamp mode, and transient inward Na+ currents and sustained outward K+ currents were elicited by depolarizing voltage steps. Subsequently, AP threshold and amplitude were determined using depolarizing current steps in current‐clamp mode.

Evoked excitatory postsynaptic current (eEPSC) and miniature excitatory postsynaptic current (mEPSC)/miniature inhibitory postsynaptic current (mIPSC) recording

Synaptic currents were recorded in voltage‐clamp mode at a holding potential of −70 mV. For evoked EPSCs (eEPSCs), a Cs‐based internal solution (117 CsMeSO4, 10 EGTA, 8 NaCl, 10 TEA‐Cl, 10 HEPES, 4 Mg‐ATP, 0.3 Na‐GTP, 5 QX‐314‐Cl) was used. AMPA receptor‐mediated currents were isolated by adding 10 µM Bicuculline and 20 µM D‐AP5 to the bath. A concentric bipolar stimulating electrode was positioned to stimulate afferent fibers (intensity ∼1.5× threshold). Stable responses were recorded for 10 min at 0.033 Hz, and maximum amplitudes were analyzed. For miniature postsynaptic currents, 1 µM tetrodotoxin was added to block APs. For mEPSCs/mIPSCs, a high‐chloride Cs‐based solution (115 CsCl, 5 EGTA, 5 NaCl, 10 HEPES, 4 Mg‐ATP, 0.3 Na‐GTP, 5 QX‐314‐Cl) was used. In 5xFAD and pIN‐transplanted 5xFAD mice, mIPSCs and mEPSCs were recorded in the presence or absence of gabazine. Slices were perfused with gabazine (20 µM) or vehicle for 30 min prior to recording. Data were analyzed using Mini Analysis Program 6.0.3 (Synaptosoft).

Long‐term potentiation (LTP)

Excitatory postsynaptic responses were recorded from the molecular layer of the dentate gyrus (DG) by electrical stimulation of Schaffer collateral fibers using a bipolar stimulating electrode placed in stratum radiatum. An input–output (I/O) curve was first generated, and the baseline stimulation intensity was adjusted to yield a field excitatory postsynaptic potential (fEPSP) slope of approximately 40% to 60% of the maximum response. The initial slope of the fEPSP (measured at 10% to 90% of the rising phase) was quantified to avoid contamination by population spikes. After a 20‐min stable baseline, LTP was induced using high‐frequency stimulation (HFS; four trains of 200‐Hz stimuli, 15‐s interval). fEPSP slopes were normalized to the average baseline value (first 20 min) and plotted as a percentage of baseline. These values were then averaged across cells to generate the final summary graphs. LTP magnitude was quantified as the mean normalized EPSC slope measured over a 1‐min window centered at 2 min and 60 min after HFS.

RNA sequencing

During sample collection and preparation, RNA was extracted and assessed for integrity and quantity using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. For library construction, polyA‐tailed mRNA was enriched using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads, followed by cDNA synthesis and purification. After end repair, A‐tailing, and adapter ligation, cDNA fragments of 370 to 420 bp were selected using AMPure XP beads, followed by PCR amplification and additional purification to obtain the final library. The completed library was quantified using the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer to ensure an effective concentration above 2 nM. Qualified libraries were pooled and subjected to sequencing using the sequencing by synthesis method. Data analysis included quality control of sequencing reads, filtering of low‐quality reads, and alignment of sequences using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified with featureCounts, and differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2. Gene Ontology (GO) analyses were conducted with clusterProfiler, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to assess the enrichment of gene sets.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). To compare mean differences between two groups with normal distributions, an unpaired t‐test was used with a significance level set at 0.05; for those with non‐normal distributions, the Mann‐Whitney test was used with a significance level of 0.05. To compare multiple groups with normal distributions, one‐way ANOVA was used with a significance level of 0.05; for those with non‐normal distributions, a non‐parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was used with a significance level of 0.05. If significant differences were observed from one‐way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test, post hoc analysis was conducted using Dunnett's multiple‐comparisons test with a significance level of 0.05. No statistical method was used to pre‐determine sample sizes. However, the sample sizes used in this study were similar to those in previous publications.

For synapse analysis, images were captured using a Leica TCS SP8 STED confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and processed using ImageJ (Bethesda, MD, USA) software, which allows for objective counting of synaptic puncta based on absolute fluorescent intensity. In the DG, synaptic puncta were counted when the sizes of the inhibitory presynaptic marker VGAT, the inhibitory postsynaptic marker gephyrin, the excitatory presynaptic marker synaptophysin, and the excitatory postsynaptic marker PSD95 were greater than 0.5 µm. Co‐localization of VGAT+ puncta and gephyrin+ puncta was identified as inhibitory synapses, while co‐localization of synaptophysin+ puncta and PSD95+ puncta was identified as excitatory synapses. The results were expressed as the number of synapses per area in the DG (100 µm2).

For in vivo quantification, 12 equally spaced 10‐µm‐thick sections (separated by 40 µm) from one series were immunostained to identify the co‐localization of HNA+/STEM121+ cells with SRY‐box transcription factor 6 (SOX6), gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA), somatostatin (SST), neuropeptide Y (NPY), parvalbumin (PV), and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA).

RESULT

Generation of human iPSC (hiPSC)‐derived MGE‐pINs

hiPSCs were differentiated into MGE‐pINs in vitro over a period of 7 weeks (the culture protocol is shown in Figure 1A) and the hiPSCs were efficiently differentiated into MGE‐like progenitors through a staged protocol combining dual SMAD inhibition, Wnt pathway suppression, and ventralizing factors. Further, hiPSCs were generated into subpallium organoids and dissociated on day 21 (3 weeks) to obtain monolayer MGE progenitor cells. To characterize the cell types of the derived MGE progenitor cells, we performed in vitro immunocytochemical analysis. The results showed that over 98% of the cells expressed the MGE progenitor marker NKX2‐1, the neural progenitor marker SOX2, and the forebrain fate commitment marker FOXG1. In addition, the MGE lineage differentiation marker SOX6 was fully expressed. The expression of OCT4 was negligible, indicating the absence of residual iPSCs in the MGE progenitors differentiated in vitro for 21 days (Figure 1B, E).

LHX6 and LHX8 were both induced in NKX2‐1‐positive cells, with LHX6 being persistently expressed in GABAergic neurons (including somatostatin and neuropeptide Y subtypes), while LHX8 was predominantly expressed in cholinergic neurons. 25 The expression of ERBB4 was essential for the tangential migration of MGE‐derived GABAergic cells to the cortex. 26 Furthermore, migratory MGE‐GABAergic cells co‐expressed MAF and MAFB, accompanied by the downregulation of LHX8. To further differentiate MGE progenitors into GABAergic interneuron precursor cells, we treated the MGE progenitors with MEK inhibition for up to 5 weeks. IF analysis at this stage revealed that over 80% of the cells co‐expressed ERBB4, MAF, and MAFB, which are required for intermediate neuronal migration. 27 , 28 In addition, 53% of the cells expressed LHX6, and 47% of the cells were positive for LHX8 (Figure 1C,F).

Prolonged treatment with MEK, NOTCH, and CDK inhibitors (week 7) enriched LHX6+ pallial GABAergic neurons (95%) and reduced the proportion of proliferative cells. At this stage, LHX8‐positive cells decreased to ∼20%. Additionally, 80% of the cells still expressed ERBB4, MAF, and MAFB, and the proportion of proliferative cells (as indicated by KI67) was reduced to less than 1% (Figure 1D,G). In summary, these results demonstrate that iPSCs can be efficiently induced to differentiate into MGE progenitors and further differentiated into migratory GABAergic precursors with controlled differentiation potential.

Survival and functional integration of grafted hiPSC‐derived MGE‐pINs in 5xFAD mice

We transplanted MGE‐pINs into the hippocampus of 10‐month‐old 5xFAD mice and subsequently analyzed them, as summarized in Figure 2A. Transplanted cells exhibited robust survival and migration across the hippocampus and cortex at 4 MPT, confirmed by human nuclear antigen (HNA) staining (Figure S1A). Furthermore, quantitative analysis showed that 81.6% of the cells expressed SOX6, confirming the maintenance of MGE lineage identity. Moreover, GABA staining (71.2%) and the subtype markers SST (9.8%) and NPY (7.8%) verified their differentiation into functional inhibitory neurons, whereas PV expression was not detected (Figure 2B).

To address the potential tumorigenic risk associated with the proliferative activity of transplanted cells, we assessed the expression of the proliferation marker PCNA in HNA‐positive human cells at 4 MPT. No PCNA‐positive human cells were detected, indicating the safety of the transplanted cells (Figure 2B). Furthermore, to evaluate whether MGE‐pIN transplantation induced neuronal apoptosis in the host brain, we examined the expression of caspase‐3 in the cortex and hippocampus. Neither cleaved caspase‐3 nor changes in the levels of full‐length caspase‐3 were detected, suggesting that the transplantation of human‐derived cells did not trigger neuronal apoptosis in the host tissue (Figure S1B). These results demonstrate that the transplanted MGE‐pINs integrate into the host brain in a safe and well‐regulated manner, primarily differentiating into GABAergic neural cells without inducing tumorigenesis or neuronal apoptosis.

Critically, grafted cells showed functional maturation. To investigate the electrophysiological properties of transplanted cells in the mouse brain, we labeled human‐derived cells with a lentivirus expressing enhanced green fluorescent protein under the control of the human GAD67 promoter prior to transplantation. At 4 MPT, whole‐cell patch‐clamp recordings of the transplanted cells in the mouse brain demonstrated a resting membrane potential of −57.29 mV (Figure 2C). In voltage‐clamp mode, the transplanted cells exhibited a transient inward current followed by a sustained outward current, indicating the presence of functional voltage‐gated Na+ and K+ channels (Figure 2D). In current‐clamp mode, injection of depolarizing current induced AP firing, with a threshold of −39.04 mV and an amplitude of 50.21 mV (Figure 2C, E). Furthermore, eEPSCs were recorded in transplanted inhibitory neurons following host excitatory neuron stimulation (Figure 2F). These findings demonstrate the electrophysiological maturation of MGE‐pINs and confirm their ability to receive synaptic inputs from the host.

Rescue of cognitive and behavioral deficits in 5xFAD mice upon MGE‐pIN transplantation

A hallmark of AD patients is cognitive decline. 29 Previous studies showed that 5xFAD mice, similar to AD patients, displayed cognitive deficits. 7 Furthermore, it has been reported that neural stem cell transplantation in mice can ameliorate these cognitive impairments. 21 We observed that 5xFAD mice exhibited impaired short‐term working memory in the Y‐maze test. In the novel arm exploration task, these mice showed a reduced number of entries into the novel arm compared with wild‐type (WT) mice (p = 0.0024), and the spontaneous alternation rate (p < 0.0001), defined as the frequency of selecting different arms, was significantly lower compared with WT mice (Figure 3A). And in the NOR test, 5xFAD mice showed a significantly lower discrimination index compared with WT (p = 0.0142) (Figure 3B). At 3 MPT, these deficits were significantly attenuated in 5xFAD mice, with entries into the novel arm increased by 24.4% compared with control 5xFAD mice (p = 0.0198) (Figure 3A), spontaneous alternation rates increased from 51.59% (untreated) to 65.52% (transplanted), matching WT levels (65.64%) (p = 0.0095) (Figure 3A), despite the fact that no significant improvement in NOR was observed at 2 MPT (Figure S2A). At 3 MPT, the NOR discrimination indices improved from 0.36 (untreated) to 0.62 (transplanted) (p = 0.0017), comparable to WT (0.55) (p = 0.0142) (Figure 3B), although there was no significant improvement observed at 2 MPT (Figure S2B). In the Morris water maze test, statistical analysis of escape latency during the acquisition phase revealed that, compared to WT mice, AD mice exhibited significantly prolonged escape latency to locate the hidden platform (Figure 3C,D). Following pIN transplantation, the escape latency of AD mice was substantially shortened (Figure 3C,D), indicating that pIN treatment ameliorated the impaired spatial learning ability and short‐term spatial memory in AD mice. During the probe trial, although no significant differences in swimming speed were observed among the experimental groups, AD mice demonstrated significantly longer escape latency compared to WT mice (Figure 3E). This deficit was effectively attenuated by pIN transplantation (0.56‐fold decrease, p = 0.0378) (Figure 3E). While pIN treatment did not fully reverse the reduced number of platform crossings in AD mice compared to WT controls (Figure 3E), likely reflecting the severe nature of spatial memory deficits in this late‐stage (13‐month) AD mouse model, its therapeutic effect on escape latency nevertheless demonstrates efficacy in improving impaired long‐term spatial memory.

Interestingly, we observed that 5xFAD mice entered the center zone of the open field more frequently than WT mice (p = 0.0214), suggesting a lower level of anxiety‐like behavior (Figure 3F). The number of entries into the center zone did not change after MGE‐pIN transplantation (p = 0.0998) (Figure 3F). To further characterize the effects of MGE‐pIN transplantation on anxiety‐like behavior in 5xFAD mice, we conducted the EPM test. Consistent with previous reports indicating reduced anxiety levels or disinhibition in 5xFAD mice, 29 , 30 we found that 5xFAD mice exhibited a significantly increased number of entries into (p < 0.0001) and spent more time in (p = 0.0155) the open arms compared to WT mice (Figure 3G). However, MGE‐pIN transplantation did not induce statistically significant changes in these parameters (entries: p = 0.1012; duration: p = 0.0713; Figure 3G), indicating that the transplantation did not significantly alter the anxiety phenotype in 5xFAD mice.

Previous studies indicated that excessive activation of GABA receptors may lead to side effects such as sedation, drowsiness, and motor weakness due to overinhibition. 31 Therefore, we conducted an open field test to assess locomotor activity at 3 MPT of MGE‐pINs. The results indicated that there were no significant differences in locomotion speed (p = 0.5463) and total distance traveled (p = 0.5159) between 5xFAD mice and WT mice (Figure S2C). Additionally, there were no significant differences in the locomotion speed (p = 0.2351) and total distance (p = 0.2169) traveled by 5xFAD mice before and after MGE‐pIN transplantation (Figure S2C). This indicates that the transplantation of MGE‐pINs did not cause excessive inhibition in 5xFAD mice, demonstrating the safety of the transplantation procedure.

Overall, these results indicate that the transplantation of MGE‐pINs can improve cognitive behavioral deficits in 5xFAD mice without affecting general motor abilities, indicating no excess inhibition.

Transcriptome analysis reveals neural circuit regeneration in MGE‐pINs transplanted 5xFAD mice without glial activation

RNA sequencing analysis was performed on the cortex of MGE‐pIN‐transplanted 5xFAD mice and their age‐matched littermates at 2 MPT to identify the initial molecular responses (Figure 4A–C). The volcano plot (Figure 4A, left panel) revealed significant gene expression changes in the transplanted mice, including the upregulation of ion transport proteins Slc8a1 and Kcnh5, neurotransmitter receptors Gabra5, Gabrb3, and Chrm2, and neuron‐related markers Sema5a (DG marker) and Nrip3 (CA1 marker), 32 suggesting enhanced neuronal ion homeostasis, balanced E: I dynamics, and heightened neural plasticity. Additionally, the elevated expression of Shisa6 indicated its potential role in modulating synaptic plasticity through AMPA receptor regulation. Conversely, the downregulation of Cd4, Apoe, and major histocompatibility complex II‐type microglial markers (H2‐Ab1, H2‐Aa, H2‐Eb1, and Cd74) reflected reduced microglial activation and a diminished neuroinflammatory state. GO term analysis (Figure 4A, right panel) further supported these findings, with upregulated pathways associated with calcium ion homeostasis, synaptic transmission, and neuronal signaling, and downregulated pathways related to inflammatory responses and apoptotic processes.

To further characterize the cellular changes induced by MGE‐pIN transplantation, we used cell‐type‐specific gene signatures from published single‐cell RNA sequencing murine atlases 33 , 34 , 35 as input for GSEA to characterize cell identity programs enriched or depleted in the MGE‐pIN‐transplanted cortex. Compared to untreated 5xFAD mice, MGE‐pIN‐transplanted mice showed upregulation of neuronal genes (Figure 4B). GSEA analysis, utilizing an adult neuronal cell type atlas that included both cortical inhibitory and excitatory neurons, 35 revealed significant enrichment of glutamatergic and GABAergic neuronal signatures (Figure 4B). In addition, GSEA analysis revealed that MGE‐pIN‐transplanted mice exhibited upregulation of radial glial cell (RGC) genes (Figure S3A). However, gene expression analysis using a neurodegenerative disease‐associated microglial signature 36 revealed that no significant changes were observed in canonical microglial marker genes (Aif1, Cd68, or Trem2), with astrocytic marker Gfap expression also remaining unchanged (Figure S3B). IF staining also showed no significant changes in IBA1‐positive microglia or GFAP‐positive reactive astrocytes at post MGE‐pIN mice (Figure S3C,D), despite the notably elevated numbers of IBA1‐positive microglia and GFAP‐positive reactive astrocytes in 5xFAD mice compared to WT mice in the cortex and hippocampus (Figure S3C,D).

To evaluate long‐term effects, we determined whether these neural plasticity changes were maintained following MGE‐pIN integration. Significant alterations were revealed in synaptic plasticity markers in 5xFAD compared to WT mice. Notably, given the pivotal role of c‐Fos in initiating synaptic plasticity, 37 , 38 , 39 the 5xFAD mice demonstrated substantially reduced c‐Fos levels in both cortical and hippocampal regions (Figure 4C,D; Figure S4A,C). Upon MGE‐pIN transplantation, a remarkable enhancement in c‐Fos expression was observed across these neural regions, as confirmed by both WB and IF staining (83.33% increase in cortex by WB, p < 0.0001; 3.64‐fold increase in cortex by IF, p = 0.0046; 2.66‐fold increase in hippocampus by IF, p = 0.0236) (Figure 4C,D, Figure S4A, C). Arc can participate in the process of synaptic remodeling by mediating the internalization of AMPA receptors. 39 , 40 WB analysis of Arc molecules revealed that cortical Arc expression showed comparable levels between 5xFAD and WT mice, with MGE‐pIN transplantation significantly elevating Arc levels in the cortical region (32% increase, p = 0.0493) (Figure 4C,E). IF staining further revealed no significant difference in cortical Arc expression between 5xFAD and WT mice (p = 0.3134 in cortex; p = 0.2978 in hippocampus), while indicating a pronounced increase in hippocampal Arc expression in transplanted mice (1.94‐fold increase; p = 0.0006) without changes in the cortex (p = 0.7238) (Figure S4A,D). The neurotrophin brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is released in an activity‐dependent manner in the hippocampus and functions as a paracrine factor to exert neurotrophic effects, thereby regulating synaptic plasticity. 41 No significant differences in cortical BDNF levels were observed between 5xFAD and WT mice (detected by WB and IF); however, MGE‐pIN transplantation significantly elevated hippocampal BDNF in AD mice (WB: p = 0.0010; IF: p = 0.0003) (Figure 4C,F; Figure S4B,E). These findings collectively suggest that MGE‐pIN transplantation may selectively enhance specific synaptic plasticity markers, potentially offering a targeted therapeutic approach for neural plasticity modulation in 5xFAD mice.

Previous studies have demonstrated a pathological reduction in hippocampal neurogenesis in AD patients, 42 , 43 , 44 which exacerbates AD pathology. Similarly, mouse models of AD show impaired hippocampal neurogenesis, resembling the deficits observed in AD patients. 45 We conducted IF staining to assess the presence of Doublecortin (DCX)‐positive newborn neurons at 6.5 MPT, examining normal neurogenesis (indicated by DCX‐positive newborn neurons in the granule layer) and abnormal neurogenesis (indicated by DCX‐positive newborn neurons in the dentate hilus [DH]). Our analysis revealed that 5xFAD mice exhibited a significant reduction in normal neurogenesis and an increase in aberrant neurogenesis compared to WT mice. MGE‐pIN transplantation resulted in a 1.1‐fold increase in neurogenesis within the granule layer (p = 0.039) and a 0.31‐fold decrease in the DH (p = 0.0384) (Figure 4G,H). Given that intermediate neurons typically guide newborn neuron migration to the granule layer, 46 we observed a 0.56‐fold reduction in Reelin‐positive intermediate neurons in the DH of 5xFAD mice compared to WT mice (p = 0.0005) (Figure 4G,I). Notably, despite the persistent deficit in host Reelin‐positive intermediate neurons following MGE‐pIN transplantation (p = 0.81) (Figure 4G,I), the observed neurogenic recovery suggests that transplanted MGE‐pINs may functionally compensate for this loss to facilitate neurogenesis, although the precise interplay with the host microenvironment remains to be elucidated.

As prior research showed a strong correlation between neurodegenerative diseases and abnormal mossy fiber sprouting, 44 we assessed ZnT3‐labeled mossy fiber sprouting in 5xFAD mice, and these mice exhibited significantly increased abnormal mossy fiber sprouting in DH compared to WT mice (Figure 4G,J). MGE‐pIN transplantation notably reduced the abnormal sprouting by 47.6% (p = 0.0021) (Figure 4G,J).

These findings suggest that MGE‐pIN transplantation promotes neural circuit regeneration in 5xFAD mice independent of glial activation.

Restoration of E:I balance in 5xFAD mice through MGE‐pIN transplantation

Aberrant neural network activity, including seizures and epileptiform discharges, has been documented in patients with AD 47 , 48 and mouse models, 49 which may further accelerate the process of cognitive decline. 50 , 51 , 52 In this study, we used needle electrodes to perform scalp EEG recordings to monitor seizure‐like events (SLEs) in mice. The EEG recording results revealed that the epileptiform discharges were significantly more frequent in 5xFAD mice compared to WT mice (Figure 5A,B). Notably, after 6.5 months of MGE‐pIN transplantation, EEG revealed a 72.4% reduction in these discharges in transplanted AD mice (p = 0.0005) (Figure 5A‐B).

Earlier studies suggested an imbalance between excitation and inhibition in AD mice models that is associated with cognitive deficits and seizure activity in these mice. 49 , 53 Based on the improvement of epileptiform activity in 5xFAD mice due to MGE‐pIN transplantation, we aimed to investigate whether MGE‐pIN transplantation could ameliorate the E:I imbalance in 5xFAD mice. The hippocampus is a pivotal region for memory formation, where the DG receives cortical inputs from the entorhinal cortex (EC). To investigate synaptic transmission within this critical circuit, we conducted electrophysiological recordings on DG granule cells. Compared to AD controls, AD mice transplanted with MGE‐pINs displayed a marked elevation in mIPSC frequency (30.1% increase, p < 0.05) and a concomitant reduction in mEPSC frequency (35.9% decrease, p < 0.05), with the amplitudes of both synaptic events unchanged (Figure 5C–F). These electrophysiological findings indicate that MGE‐pIN transplantation restores the disrupted E:I balance in the hippocampal network by simultaneously enhancing inhibitory tone and suppressing aberrant excitatory drive.

To determine whether these electrophysiological alterations were driven by the integration of transplanted GABAergic neurons, we pre‐incubated slices with the GABAA receptor antagonist gabazine. Gabazine treatment resulted in the near‐complete silencing of mIPSC events in both groups, rendering the frequency barely detectable and erasing any transplantation‐induced differences. Crucially, gabazine treatment also abolished any significant differences in mEPSC metrics between the groups (Figure 5C–F). These results indicate that the transplanted MGE‐pINs functionally integrated into the host DG circuitry and enhanced inhibitory synaptic transmission via GABAA receptor‐mediated signaling. Furthermore, the restoration of mEPSC frequency to AD levels upon gabazine blockade indicates that transplantation did not diminish the underlying excitatory tone. Rather, the transplanted neurons provided a functional inhibitory gating that actively suppressed the aberrant excitation.

Subsequently, WB analysis was conducted on cortical and hippocampal tissues from WT and 5xFAD mice before and after MGE‐pIN transplantation. In cortical tissue, while inhibitory γ‐aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptor subunit levels (GABAAα1 and GABAAα3) remained unchanged in 5xFAD mice compared to WT mice, we observed a trend of decline in the GABAAα1/GABAAα3 ratio (Figure 6A,C). Following MGE‐pIN transplantation, this ratio increased, primarily due to elevated GABAAα1 levels (Figure 6A,C). In hippocampal tissue, GABAAα1 levels were lower in 5xFAD mice, resulting in a reduced GABAAα1/GABAAα3 ratio (Figure 6B,C). It appears that MGE‐pIN transplantation restored both GABAAα1 levels and the ratio (Figure 6B,C).

Chloride ion co‐transporters critically regulate GABAergic neuron inhibitory function, particularly during neuronal depolarization. 54 , 55 In the cortex, NKCC1 and KCC2 expression levels remained unchanged in 5xFAD mice, but the NKCC1/KCC2 ratio was elevated (Figure 6A,D). MGE‐pIN transplantation significantly reversed this ratio (Figure 6A,D). Conversely, hippocampal NKCC1 and KCC2 levels and their ratio showed no significant differences between 5xFAD and WT mice (Figure 6B,D), suggesting potential compensatory mechanisms. These data imply that MGE‐pIN transplantation can relieve the GABAergic dysfunction in 5xFAD mice caused by GABA receptor‐mediated tonic inhibition and chloride co‐transporter‐mediated depolarization of GABAergic neurons.

We also examined glutamate receptor subunit expression, focusing on GluA1/GluA2 and GluN2B/GluN2A ratios. Consistent findings across cortex and hippocampus revealed a reduced GluA1/GluA2 ratio in 5xFAD mice, primarily due to decreased GluA1 levels (Figure 6A,B,E). MGE‐pIN transplantation improved this deficit by recovering GluA1 levels (Figure 6A,B,E), potentially addressing synaptic dysfunction associated with AMPA receptor signaling. 50 While hippocampal GluN2A and GluN2B levels remained unaltered following MGE‐pIN grafting (Figure 6B,F), cortical tissue exhibited distinct changes. In 5xFAD mice, reduced GluN2A expression resulted in an elevated GluN2B/GluN2A ratio (Figure 6B,F), which was subsequently normalized by MGE‐pIN transplantation through restoration of GluN2A levels and ratio (Figure 6B,F). These findings indicate that transplanted MGE‐pINs can modulate the dysfunction of glutamatergic neurons mediated by AMPA and NMDA receptor subunits present in 5xFAD mice, suggesting a complex and nuanced role in addressing cognitive deficits and abnormal neuronal excitation.

In summary, corresponding to reduced epileptiform discharges, normalized protein expression demonstrates that MGE‐pIN transplantation effectively restored E:I imbalance in 5xFAD mice.

MGE‐pIN transplantation restores synaptic dysfunction in 5xFAD mice

In agreement with the well‐established synaptic loss characteristic of AD pathology, 56 , 57 the IF analysis revealed a significant decrease in the synaptic density, both excitatory and inhibitory, in 5xFAD mice (Figure 7A–C). MGE‐pIN transplantation resulted in a substantial increase in inhibitory densities marked by VGAT and Gerpyrin, and in excitatory densities marked by PSD95 and Synaptophysin, with inhibitory synapse density increasing by 113.1% (p < 0.0001) and excitatory synapse density increasing by 68.9% (p = 0.019) (Figure 7A–C). These may explain the increased GluA1 AMPA receptor subunit and GluN2A NMDA receptor subunit expression, which should be associated with synaptic maturation and, further, cognitive function. 57 , 58

Furthermore, we found that the density of dendritic spines confirmed by Golgi staining in 5xFAD mice was significantly reduced compared to WT mice (p < 0.0001). At 6.5 MPT, AD + pIN mice showed a 38.3% recovery of dendritic spine density compared to untreated AD controls (5.752 vs 4.159 spines/10 µm; p = 0.002), reaching 70.5% of WT levels (8.158 spines/10 µm) (Figure 7D,E).

Given the well‐established impairments in LTP in the 5xFAD model, 59 we investigated whether MGE‐pIN transplantation could restore synaptic plasticity in the perforant path‐dentate gyrus (PP‐DG) pathway. First, to exclude the possibility that changes in plasticity were driven by altered baseline excitability, we examined basal synaptic transmission. I/O curves generated by stimulating the PP‐DG pathway revealed no significant differences in fEPSP slopes between 5xFAD mice and those transplanted with MGE‐pINs (Figure 7F), indicating that transplantation did not alter basal synaptic strength. We next induced LTP using a HFS protocol designed to elicit robust, long‐lasting synaptic potentiation and observed slices from 5xFAD mice exhibited severe deficits in LTP induction and maintenance, characterized by a reduced fEPSP slope relative to baseline (Figure 7G,H). This reduction revealed a severe vulnerability of the synaptic machinery. It appears that the aged pathological environment renders synapses unable to sustain high‐frequency activity, leading to a paradoxical suppression of transmission instead of potentiation. Strikingly, MGE‐pIN transplantation effectively rescued these deficits (Figure 7G,H). In the early phase of LTP (measured 2 min after HFS), 5xFAD mice with MGE‐pIN grafts displayed significantly enhanced potentiation compared to untreated 5xFAD (128.9% vs 93.37%; p < 0.05) (Figure 7G–I). This enhancement was sustained throughout the late phase of LTP; the mean fEPSP slope averaged over the last 60‐min recording was significantly higher in transplanted mice compared to 5xFAD controls (137.1% vs 77.84%; p < 0.05) (Figure 7G,H, J).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that transplanted MGE‐pINs integrate into the host circuitry, thereby functionally restoring hippocampal synaptic plasticity in 5xFAD mice.

Aβ pathology is not altered upon MGE‐pIN transplantation

The production and processing of Aβ plaques are characteristic features of AD pathology. 2 , 4 We quantified soluble and insoluble levels of Aβ40 and Aβ42, two amyloidogenic products of APP, using ELISA and simultaneously assessed Aβ plaque deposition using IF staining with the 6E10 antibody. At 4 MPT, while a modest decline in soluble Aβ40 was observed (decreased by 19%, p = 0.0071), comprehensive analyses ultimately revealed that the transplantation failed to mitigate Aβ pathology in 5xFAD mice. These measurements included insoluble Aβ40 (p = 0.3281), soluble and insoluble Aβ42 (soluble Aβ42, p = 0.2164; insoluble Aβ42, p = 0.2039), and their respective ratios (soluble Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios, p = 0.5461; insoluble Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios, p = 0.4185) (Figure S5A–C), and IF assessment of hippocampal 6E10+ Aβ plaques (Aβ plaque number, p = 0.5702; Aβ plaque area, p = 0.6894) (Figure S5G,H) in AD versus AD + pIN mice. Consistently, at 6.5 MPT, we found no significant changes in soluble and insoluble Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels or their ratios in the cortex of 5xFAD mice (soluble Aβ40, p = 0.3649; insoluble Aβ40, p = 0.1969; soluble Aβ42, p = 0.4469; insoluble Aβ42, p = 0.9543; soluble Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios, p = 0.9500; insoluble Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios, p = 0.1140) (Figure S5D–F). Moreover, 6E10+ Aβ deposition in the cortical and hippocampal regions remains unchanged (Aβ plaque number, p = 0.9937 in the cortex; Aβ plaque area in the cortex, p = 0.9991; Aβ plaque number, p = 0.6192 in the hippocampus; Aβ plaque area in the hippocampus, p = 0.9945) (Figure 8A,B). These results indicate that MGE‐pIN transplantation did not provide long‐term improvement in Aβ deposition in 5xFAD mice.

As phosphorylated APP (p‐APP) may enhance β‐secretase activity, leading to increased Aβ production, 4 , 60 and the phosphorylated state may contribute to AD pathological progression, we investigated the effect of MGE‐pIN transplantation on APP levels in 5xFAD mice. The expression levels of full‐length APP were elevated in the hippocampal and cortical tissues of 5xFAD mice (Figures 8C,D and S5I). Notably, the engraftment of MGE‐pINs did not alter the expression levels of full‐length APP protein in 5xFAD mice (Figures 8C,D and S5I). We subsequently analyzed the expression levels of p‐APP (Thr668). Similarly, increased levels of p‐APP (Thr668) were detected in the cortex and hippocampus of control 5xFAD mice, with no changes observed following MGE‐pIN engraftment (p = 0.3661 in the cortex; p = 0.9368 in the hippocampus) (Figure 8C‐E). We also employed IF staining to assess the impact of MGE‐pIN engraftment on APP and p‐APP (Thr668) levels in 5xFAD mice. Consistent with the WB results, elevated levels of APP and p‐APP were observed in the cortical and hippocampal regions of control 5xFAD mice, which was not improved after MGE‐pIN transplantation (APP in cortex: p = 0.9971; p‐APP in cortex: p = 0.1387; APP in hippocampus: p = 0.8446; p‐APP in hippocampus: p = 0.1629) (Figure 8F,G).

Furthermore, WB analysis targeting key APP processing enzymes associated with amyloidogenic processing revealed that beta‐site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1). expression levels were increased in control 5xFAD mice, while MGE‐pINs did not affect BACE1 levels in the cortical and hippocampal tissues of 5xFAD mice (BACE1 in cortex: p = 0.2965; BACE1 in hippocampus: p = 0.8139) (Figure S5J,K). These data indicate that MGE‐pIN transplantation may have transient effects on Aβ pathology but cannot achieve a prolonged alleviation.

Overall, MGE‐pIN transplantation improves cognitive function in 5xFAD mice through a mechanism that is independent of amyloid production and deposition.

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrates that transplantation of hiPSC‐derived MGE‐pINs into the 5xFAD hippocampus restores cognitive function by rebalancing E:I circuitry, attenuating neurogenesis, and enhancing structural plasticity 11 , 61 , 62 ; yet this work extends prior work by establishing a direct link between interneuron grafting and multifaceted recovery.

Restoring E:I balance as a therapeutic strategy for structural plasticity in 5xFAD mice

AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder driven by Aβ protein deposition and hyperphosphorylated tau accumulation. 2 , 3 , 4 This pathology triggers widespread synaptic dysfunction, severely impairing both GABAergic and glutamatergic signaling. 11 , 57 , 63 The consequent disruption of E:I balance in cortical circuits directly compromises learning and memory capabilities. 58 , 62 At the network level, this imbalance precipitates aberrant neural activity, particularly epileptiform discharges. These discharges contribute to cognitive decline even in the absence of overt motor seizures. 50 , 51 , 52 Notably, in our study, the electrophysiological signatures of the observed SLEs – characterized by intermittent spikes and sharp waves – closely resemble this epileptiform activity described in human AD patients 47 , 48 and other AD mouse models. 49

Concurrent with this functional imbalance, AD brains exhibit a hostile microenvironment characterized by reduced dendritic complexity, spine loss, and impaired regeneration. 64 These structural deficits accelerate neurodegeneration. Importantly, our findings demonstrate that targeting E:I rebalancing effectively reverses this hostility. By suppressing aberrant activity, the transplantation creates a permissive environment for structural plasticity, evidenced in our study by enhanced neurogenesis, improved mossy fiber organization, increased dendritic spine density, and robust LTP enhancement.

MGE‐pIN transplantation restores circuit balance and cognitive function in 5xFAD mice

The therapeutic efficacy of MGE‐pIN transplantation critically depends on two key aspects: successful differentiation into specific inhibitory neurons and their functional integration into host circuits. In our study, transplanted MGE‐pINs in 5xFAD hippocampus predominantly differentiated into SST‐ and NPY‐expressing interneurons. These specific interneuron compositions proved advantageous for E:I rebalancing, as SST/NPY‐expressing interneurons effectively modulate network excitability. 65 , 66 Evoked EPSCs confirmed that grafted cells received synaptic inputs from the host, and the correction of host mIPSC/mEPSC ratios demonstrated functional modulation. Together with the recovery of synaptic density and LTP, these findings strongly indicate the successful functional integration of MGE‐pINs. In addition to GABAergic modulation, MGE‐pINs may contribute to optimizing the local microenvironment through their intrinsic neural stem cell properties. These transplanted cells can secrete neurotrophic factors, promote neurogenesis, and enhance structural plasticity in the host tissue, supported by enhanced expression of c‐Fos, Arc, and BDNF in our MGE‐pIN‐transplanted mice. The derivation of MGE‐pINs to a post‐mitotic state with minimum Ki‐67 expression may simultaneously prevent the tumorigenic properties of these cells. This dual mechanism – combining circuit‐level E:I rebalancing with neurotrophic support – may explain the sustained therapeutic effects observed without sedation or motor deficits, representing a significant advancement for translational applications in AD treatment.

Mechanistically, our results indicate that transplanted MGE‐pINs functionally integrated into the host circuitry, significantly enhancing inhibitory synaptic transmission via GABAA receptor‐mediated signaling. Crucially, the observation that mEPSC frequency reverted to baseline levels upon gabazine blockade confirmed that transplantation did not simply diminish the underlying excitatory drive. Instead, the grafted neurons provided a “functional inhibitory gate” that actively suppressed aberrant hyperexcitability. This circuit‐level modulation is substantiated by molecular evidence: The restoration of GABAAα1 levels and the GABAAα1/α3 ratio, alongside normalized chloride transporter ratios, suggests a systematic re‐equilibration of inhibitory tone. Furthermore, MGE‐pIN transplantation reversed the deficits in GluA1 and GluN2A levels in the 5xFAD model, highlighting the therapy's profound potential to rescue the molecular machinery essential for synaptic plasticity. Notably, MGE‐pIN transplantation did not affect Aβ pathology. This lack of effect on Aβ is consistent with previous studies of inhibitory neuron transplants, which also showed no impact of Aβ burden on cognitive performance. 67 Critically, the comprehensive improvements across transcriptomic, histological, and electrophysiological domains – encompassing the restoration of key neuroplasticity regulatory molecular expression, enhanced neurogenesis with marked reduction of aberrant mossy fiber sprouting, and particularly the significant augmentation of dendritic spine densities alongside the robust recovery of LTP – collectively substantiate how excitatory/inhibitory rebalancing generates a permissive microenvironment for structural plasticity, a mechanism that remarkably persists even in the advanced stages of AD.

Our evaluation of MGE transplantation leveraged both cortical and hippocampal tissues to capture disease modifications. Cortical samples were used for bulk RNA sequencing to provide reliable omics data and reflect macroscopic molecular shifts. Conversely, targeted functional assessments, including electrophysiology and synaptic tracing, were restricted to the hippocampus to verify target‐site repair. Because HNA tracing confirmed that transplanted MGE cells migrated into the cortex, this multiregional effect was expected. Ultimately, this extensive cellular integration allowed the grafts to stabilize the wider hippocampal‐cortical network, driving the overall improvements in E:I balance and behavioral outcomes.

LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSIONS

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, we observed a notable absence of PV+ interneurons in the transplanted grafts, indicating potential subtype‐specific constraints that necessitate refinement of our differentiation protocols. Second, our investigation of Aβ dynamics was limited to 6.5 MPT, leaving longer‐term effects unexplored. Third, although the recovery of mossy fibers and dendritic spines suggests circuit restoration, establishing direct causal relationships between these structural changes and behavioral improvements requires further validation through optogenetic or chemogenetic approaches. Looking ahead, advancing this treatment toward clinical application will require focused efforts on enhancing MGE‐pIN generation efficiency and precision, alongside rigorous evaluation of safety parameters, scalability potential, and sustained therapeutic efficacy in AD patients.

Despite these limitations, the restoration of synaptic and network activity through E:I balance correction and the regeneration of disrupted neural circuits collectively highlight iPSC‐derived MGE‐pINs as a promising therapeutic strategy for AD.
