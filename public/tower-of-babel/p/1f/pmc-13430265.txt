BK channel deficiency promotes lipolysis via an AKT-independent activation of the cAMP/PKA/HSL pathway
Authors: Jin Zhang, Xin Shi, Mengxuan Zhang, Yuying Han, Guang Yang, Ruiyang Li, Feng Zhao, Ping Liu, Xinxin Wen, Wenxuan Wei, Bojing Zuo, Biao Zheng, Hongshan Liu, Wenjing Lu, Meili Wang, Zhifei Zhang, Haixia Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdipocyte lipolysis mobilizes stored triglycerides during energy deprivation, a process tightly regulated to maintain metabolic homeostasis. The large conductance calcium-activated potassium channel (BK) is present in adipocytes and is involved in intracellular lipid metabolism. This study aims to investigate the role of BK in regulating adipocyte lipolysis. Using BK knockout (BK-KO) rats, adipocyte-specific BK knockout (BKadipo−/−) mice, and in vitro differentiated adipocytes, we demonstrate that BK channel deficiency promotes lipolysis. We found that deletion of BK channels attenuated body weight gain accompanied by upregulation of the lipolysis pathway. BKadipo−/− mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD) showed attenuated weight gain, reduced adipocyte hypertrophy, and improved insulin sensitivity. Meanwhile, BK deletion enhanced lipolysis of mature adipocytes stimulated by CL-316243 compared with control cells. Mechanistically, BK deletion enhanced hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) phosphorylation at Ser660 via an AKT-independent activation of the cAMP/PKA pathway while bypassing the inhibitory phosphorylation at Ser565. BK depletion facilitates lipolysis and attenuates diet-induced obesity via activating the cAMP/PKA/HSL pathway. These findings identify BK channels as critical negative regulators of adipocyte lipolysis, suggesting that targeting BK channels may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for obesity and related metabolic disorders.Supplementary key words: adipocytes, BK channels, cAMP/PKA/HSL pathway, lipolysis, obesity

The balance of lipid metabolism plays a pivotal role in energy regulation (1), cellular function (2), hormone synthesis (3, 4, 5), thermoregulation (6, 7), immune responses (8), and overall metabolic well-being. Dysregulation of lipid metabolism is intricately linked to conditions like type 2 diabetes (9, 10), cardiovascular disease (11), fatty liver (12), and even cancer (13, 14). Sustaining a healthy lipid metabolism necessitates a delicate equilibrium between lipid synthesis and lipolysis. Lipolysis is an important part of the fat metabolism process and involves the participation of a variety of enzymes, and the main lipolytic enzymes include adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL), and monoglyceride lipase (MGL) (15). Transsynaptic viral tract tracers have unveiled central sympathetic and sensory circuits within white adipose tissue, encompassing feedback loops that potentially regulate lipolysis (16). Cold stimulation or hunger conditions can activate the sympathetic nervous system, activate the classical lipolysis pathway through the β3-adrenergic receptor (β3-AR), promote adipocyte lipolysis, and provide energy to the body. Although researchers have outlined the intracellular signaling pathways triggered by sympathetic activation (17), the orchestration of lipolysis, as a fundamental process in fat metabolism, involves a complex network of enzymes and regulatory molecules in adipocytes. Further research is imperative to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms governing lipolysis.

BK channels, characterized as large conductance calcium activated potassium channels, are distributed in neurons (18), muscles (19), chromaffin cells (20), inner hair cells of the cochlea (21), and adipocytes (22). BK channels are activated by membrane depolarization and intracellular calcium elevation (23), playing pivotal roles in diverse physiological processes. Genome-wide analyses have associated the KCNMA1 gene, responsible for encoding BK channel α-subunits, with susceptibility to obesity, and heightened BK channel expression has been observed in the adipose tissue of obese individuals (22). BK channel knockout mice exhibited reduced weight gain when fed with a high-fat diet (24). These findings collectively underscore the intimate relationship between BK channels and the regulation of lipid balance.

Recent research from our lab highlights the involvement of BK channels in insulin-induced lipid deposition (25) and the regulation of adipocyte differentiation (26). To delineate the physiological role of BK channels in lipolysis, global BK knockout rats (BK-KO) and adipose-tissue-specific BK knockout mice (BKadipo−/−) were engineered, together with utilizing the BK inhibitor Paxilline (PAX) and the BK agonist NS1619. Our investigations have unveiled that BK channel deficiency accelerates lipolysis via the cAMP-dependent protein kinase A (PKA) pathway, effectively curbing lipid accumulation. This underscores the potential efficacy of BK channel deficiency or inhibition in reducing body weight and enhancing systemic insulin sensitivity. A comprehensive comprehension of the intricate interplay between BK channels and lipolysis in adipocytes lays a robust foundation for the development and implementation of therapeutic interventions targeting obesity and associated metabolic conditions.

Material and methods

Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Capital Medical University. Animals were housed in cages under controlled environmental conditions, with temperatures maintained between 22°C and 24°C, humidity at 40%–70%, and 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. BK channel knockout (BK-KO) rats were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 approaches as previously described (25). All rats used in this study were male and were sacrificed after fasting or feeding for 24 h, unless stated otherwise. All experiments were performed in 12- to 16-week-old rats.

C57BL/6J mice

All mice used in all experiments were on a C57BL/6J background and were male. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Capital Medical University. All mice used in this study were bred and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions (12 h/12 h light and dark cycle, 22°C ± 2°C). BKflox/flox mice (Cyagen Biosciences) and Adipoq-Cre mice (Cyagen Biosciences, China) were generated through CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing. BK adipocyte-specific knockout (BKadipo−/−) mice were generated by mating the floxed-BK mouse strain with adipoq-Cre transgenic mice, to obtain adipoq-Cre+/−BKflox/flox mice. Adipoq-Cre−/−BKflox/flox mice (BKadipo+/+) littermates were used as controls. Age- and sex-matched mice were co-housed and used for all the experiments in this study. Genotyping was carried out using tail tips that were incubated with 200 μl 50 M NaoH buffer for 30 min at 100°C. And we add 16 μl 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) to the buffer. Genotyping was performed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using Cre-, LoxP1-, and LoxP2-specific primers. BKadipo−/− mice displayed a 200 bp band for Cre, a 239 bp band for LoxP1, and a 279 bp band for LoxP2. BKadipo+/+ mice showed no band for Cre, but identical band sizes for LoxP1 and LoxP2. The primers for mice genotyping are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Food and water intake measurements

Rats (or mice) were individually housed in metabolic cages (maintained at 22 ± 2°C, 40%–70% humidity, with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle) for a 24-h acclimation period with ad libitum access to food and water to minimize stress-induced disturbances in feeding and drinking behaviors. After acclimation, the metabolic cages were cleaned, and the animals were returned to the cages. A fixed amount of feed (50 g per cage) and autoclaved distilled water (100 g per cage) were provided, followed by continuous monitoring for 5 days. Daily food intake was calculated as "initial feed weight minus remaining feed weight," and daily water intake was calculated as "initial water weight minus remaining water weight." The average daily food intake and water intake per animal (rat or mouse) was determined by averaging the measurements over the 5-day period.

Fasting experiment in SD rats

12-week-old male SD rats were randomly assigned to either the ad libitum feeding group or the 24 h fasting group on a per-cage basis. For the fasting model, all cage bedding was replaced with fresh bedding prior to the start of the experiment. Rats in the fasting group were subjected to complete food deprivation for 24 h but were provided with ad libitum access to water throughout the fasting period. Control rats in the ad libitum group were given free access to fresh food and water under the same housing conditions.

Fasting-refeeding experiment in SD rats

12-week-old BK KO and WT rats were fasted for 24 h or fasted for 24 h and then re-fed ad libitum for 6 h.

Diets in mice

6-week-old male BKadipo−/− and BKadipo+/+ mice were randomly assigned to two groups and fed ad libitum for 16 weeks: one group received a chow diet (CD) (LabDiet 5001, USA), a nutritionally complete maintenance diet with 10% of calories from fat; the other group was given a high-fat diet (HFD) containing 60% of calories from fat (Research Diets). Weight gain in the mice was assessed biweekly over the course of the study.

Glucose tolerance test in mice

For glucose tolerance tests (GTT), mice were overnight fasted for 14 h with free access to water. Following the fasting period, baseline blood glucose levels were measured from tail vein blood samples using a handheld glucometer. Mice were then administered an intraperitoneal injection of glucose (1 g/kg body weight). Blood glucose concentrations were subsequently measured at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min post-injection. Glucose levels were recorded for subsequent analysis.

Insulin tolerance test in mice

For insulin tolerance tests (ITT), mice were fasted for 4 h with ad libitum access to water. Following the fasting period, baseline blood glucose levels were measured from tail vein blood samples using a handheld glucometer. Mice were then administered an intraperitoneal injection of insulin (0.75 U/kg body weight). Blood glucose concentrations were subsequently measured at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min post-injection. Glucose levels were recorded for subsequent analysis.

Body composition testing

Body composition was assessed in 12-week-old rats using the EchoMRI-900™ small animal in vivo body composition analyzer (Echo Medical Systems). Total fat mass and lean mass were quantified by Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), and the ratios of fat mass and lean mass to total body weight were calculated.

For precise quantification of total fat mass and lean mass in mice, animals were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane in O2 and positioned within a small animal body composition analysis and imaging system (small animal PET/CT/SPECT three-in-one system) for detailed analysis.

In this study, lean body mass was defined as body components excluding fat and free water, mainly comprising bone, skeletal muscle, and visceral tissues. Fat mass was defined as the total fat tissue. It should be noted that lean body mass is not equal to total body mass minus fat mass.

Indirect calorimetry

For metabolic cage analysis, mice were housed in the Pan Lab/Harvard Apparatus metabolic cage system at week 16 of HFD or CD feeding, with one mouse per chamber. The mice were allowed to adapt to the metabolic cages for 12 h, and then the oxygen consumption rate (VO2), carbon dioxide production rate (VCO2), and energy expenditure (EE) were measured continuously for 48 h. Metabolic cages were maintained at 22 ± 2°C with 40%–70% humidity and a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle.

Adipose tissue collection

Following body composition assessment, mice were euthanized, and various adipose tissue depots were carefully dissected. The iWAT, eWAT, and BAT were excised, weighed to determine individual fat pad mass, and processed for subsequent analyses. Tissue samples were either fixed in 10% formalin solution for histological examination or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for further investigations.

Histological analysis

Upon dissection, adipose tissue samples were immediately fixed in 10% formalin solution in 0.1 M PBS overnight, then embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections were cut at a thickness of 5 μm and mounted onto glass slides. For morphological analysis, sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and stained with H&E according to established protocols. Stained sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with coverslips. Digital images were acquired utilizing a Zeiss light microscope. To evaluate adipocyte area within the specified adipose tissue sections, ImageJ software was employed. For each sample, approximately 20–50 adipocytes from at least three randomly selected fields were analyzed to ensure representative sampling.

Biochemical analysis

Upon completion of the dietary intervention, blood samples were collected from mice after a 12 h overnight fast. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, and aliquots were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Serum concentrations of various metabolic parameters were determined using specific kits as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Free fatty acid (FFA) levels were measured using NEFA-HR reagents (Wako Chemicals). Glycerol concentrations were measured using free glycerol reagents (Cat# F6428, Sigma-Aldrich). The levels of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were measured using kits from Beijing Sino Bio-Tech Research Institute (Beijing, China).

Isolation, culture, and differentiation of rat adipocytes

Adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) were extracted from the epididymal fat pads of 3-month-old male SD rats using our laboratory's established method (26). The fat pads were minced and digested in 5 ml of Krebs-Ringer solution (Cat# K4002, Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 0.8 mg/ml type I collagenase (Cat# LS004196, Worthington), 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), and 1% free fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (Cat# V900933, Sigma-Aldrich). Following a 50-min digestion at 37°C in an air bath with agitation at 100 cycles/minute, the cells were sieved through 80# and then 400# steel mesh before being centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min. ADSCs were cultured in a 6-well plate using DMEM/F12 (1:1 vol/vol) medium (Cat# 10-092-CV, Corning) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Cat# 10100-147, Gibco), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin (Cat# 15140122, Gibco). The cells were maintained in a humidified cell incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C.

Upon reaching 85%–95% confluence, the cells were exposed to differentiation medium I, comprising 5 μg/ml insulin (Cat# P3376-400IU, Beyotime), 0.5 mM 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (Cat# I5879, Sigma-Aldrich), 1 μM dexamethasone (Cat# D4902, Sigma-Aldrich), 5 μM troglitazone (Cat# HY-50935, MCE), and 10% FBS in DMEM/F12 (1:1 vol/vol). After 48 h, differentiation medium I was replaced with differentiation medium II, containing 5 μg/ml insulin, 5 μM troglitazone, and 10% FBS in DMEM/F12 (1:1 vol/vol), with cells incubated for an additional 48 h. Subsequently, cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 (1:1 vol/vol) supplemented with 10% FBS for 4 days, with the medium refreshed every alternate day. Following the induction of differentiation, the necessary experiments were carried out.

Glycerol assay

Glycerol content released in culture medium of adipocytes served as an index of lipolysis. Differentiated mature adipocytes were washed twice with PBS to remove residual medium, then incubated in phenol red-free, serum-free DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 1% fatty acid-free BSA. Cells were stimulated with either 1 μM CL-316243 (Cat# C5976, Sigma-Aldrich), 10 μM Forskolin (Cat# HY-15371, MCE), or 1 mM 8-Bromo-cAMP (Cat# HY-12306A, MCE) for 1 h at 37°C in an incubator with 5% CO2. Control cells were incubated under identical conditions without stimulation. Following incubation, conditioned medium was then collected and determined by absorbance at 490 nm, by use of a glycerol assay kit from Applygen (China). The lipolysis data were normalized to each milligram of protein by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) quantification.

Calcium imaging

ADSCs were seeded in glass-bottom culture dishes and differentiated into mature adipocytes following the aforementioned protocol. On day 8, the adipocytes were washed three times with Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) and subsequently loaded with 5 μM Fluo-4 AM by incubation at 37°C for 30 min. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a TCS SP8 STED confocal microscope (Leica) with an excitation wavelength set at 488 nm. The acquired fluorescence signals were quantified for intensity using ImageJ software, and further image analysis was conducted according to previously established methods (25).

Western blotting

Adipose tissues underwent homogenization, and adipose cells were lysed using a standard RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Cat# 11206893001, Roche) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Cat# P5726, Sigma-Aldrich) on ice for 40 min. The lysates were subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C to remove insoluble debris. Total protein concentration was quantified using the BCA method. Following this, equal amounts of protein (20 μg per group) were separated via 4%–20% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) at room temperature for 1 h, then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. After washing three times with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using a standard electrochemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Cat# KGP1123, KeyGEN BioTECH) and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Band densities were quantified using ImageJ software. The primary antibodies utilized included p-HSL (Ser660) (Cat #45804, Cell Signaling), p-HSL (Ser565) (Cat #4137, Cell Signaling), HSL (Cat #4107, Cell Signaling), ATGL (Cat #2138, Cell Signaling), Phospho-PKA substrate (RRXS∗/T∗) (Cat #9624, Cell Signaling), p-AKT (Ser473) (Cat #9018, Cell Signaling), and AKT (Cat #2920, Cell Signaling). HSP90 (Cat #A5027, ABclonal) and β-Actin (Cat #66009-1-Ig, Proteintech) densities were employed as internal controls for total protein levels.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from tissues and cells utilizing the RNeasy kit (Cat# RC112-01, Vazyme) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Subsequently, the total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA employing the FastKing RT Kit (Cat# KR116, Tiangen) following the manufacturer's protocol. For the quantification of mRNA expression, qRT-PCR was conducted using the 2X PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Cat# A25742, Applied Biosystems). The expression levels of individual genes were normalized to the expression of 36B4 or β-Actin using the ΔΔCt method. All reactions were performed in triplicate. The primer sequences utilized are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

RNA-seq analysis of primary cultured adipocytes

RNA-seq assay was conducted on primary cultured adipocytes isolated from the epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) of BK KO and WT rats, following the methods previously described (26). In simple terms, total RNA was extracted using an RNAprep Pure Tissue Kit (Cat# DP431, Tiangen) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. The construction of the transcriptome library, sequencing, and data analysis were carried out by OE Biotech Co, Ltd (Shanghai, China) and Novogene Co, Ltd (Beijing, China). Firstly, the research data set from the laboratory was normalized and standardized. The R package "limma" (version 3.56.2) was utilized to analyze the data from five WT samples and five KO samples, with the conditions for differential gene selection set at P value < 0.05 and |Log2FC| > 1. The differential genes were visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps created with the R packages "ggplot2" (version 3.5.1) and "heatmap" (version 1.0.12). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted on the selected differentially expressed genes, employing the species annotation "org.Rn.e.g.db", while Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis utilized "Rno" as the species annotation. A P value of < 0.05, sorted in ascending order, was used as the selection criterion to identify the top 10 KEGG pathways as well as the GO biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions. The results of the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were then visualized.

RNA-seq analysis of eWAT

RNA extracted from eWAT of BK KO rats (n = 4) and their corresponding littermates (n = 4) was utilized for analysis. Total RNA isolation was carried out using the RNeasy kit (Cat# RC112-01, Vazyme) following the recommended protocols. Post-extraction, RNA quality was assessed using the Agilent Technologies 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technology, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. Qualified RNA were quantified using the Qubit3.0 with the QubitTM RNA Broad Range Assay kit (Cat# Q10210, ThermoFisher). For RNA-seq investigations, stranded RNA sequencing libraries were generated with the KC-Digital™ Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit utilizing 2 μg of total RNA. Library fragments spanning 200–500 bp were enriched, and sequencing was conducted on the Novaseq 6000 sequencer (Illumina) using the PE150 model. Initial raw data underwent filtration with Trimmomatic (version 0.36) to remove low-quality reads and adaptor sequence contaminants. Clean reads were further processed with custom scripts to address duplication bias introduced during library preparation and sequencing.

Differential gene expression analysis between groups was performed using the edgeR package (version 3.12.1), excluding genes with zero read counts. Statistical significance in gene expression alterations was determined by a P value of < 0.05 and fold-change values exceeding 2. Subsequent gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted for all genes identified using GSEA software. Gene sets were considered to be significantly enriched with a P value of < 0.05 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25.

Statistical analysis

All experiments necessitating statistical analysis were conducted a minimum of three times, yielding consistent outcomes. All data were verified for normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test before statistical analysis, and P > 0.05 was considered indicative of data conforming to a normal distribution. For normally distributed data, unpaired two-tailed Student's t test was employed for comparisons between two groups. Comparisons involving multiple groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post-hoc test or two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc analysis for continuous variables. For non-normally distributed data, Mann–Whitney U test (two-group comparisons) and Kruskal-Wallis H test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test (multiple-group comparisons) were employed. Data were shown as mean ± SEM. A significance level of P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant for all tests.

Results

Systemic knockout of BK channels significantly decreases white adipose tissue (WAT) mass

To demonstrate the role of BK channels in adipose tissue, we constructed homozygous BK knockout (BK-KO) rats (BK−/−) (25). Weekly monitoring of rat body weights, ranging from 4 to 12 weeks, revealed that BK-KO rats exhibited significantly lower weight gain compared to WT rats (Fig. 1A, B). Metabolic cage assays evaluating 24-h food and water intake in 12-week-old WT and BK-KO rats demonstrated no notable differences (Fig. 1C, D), indicating that the difference in body weight was not related to food intake. Subsequent EchoMRI scans and adipose tissue analyses confirmed a marked reduction in fat mass, which contributed to the diminished body weight observed in BK-KO rats (Fig. 1E, F). Notably, BK-KO rats exhibited a higher proportion of lean mass to body weight, a significantly lower ratio of fat mass to body weight, and a reduced fat-to-lean mass ratio in comparison to WT rats (Fig. 1G-I). Furthermore, the adipose tissues in different parts of the body showed a significant reduction in inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT) and visceral epididymal adipose tissue (eWAT) in BK-KO rats, and the ratio of WAT mass to body weight was significantly reduced, with eWAT exhibiting the most significant reduction in ratio. Conversely, there were no substantial changes observed in the scapular region brown adipose tissue (BAT) (Fig. 1J-L). Together, these results underscore that systemic BK channel knockout leads to a substantial decrease in WAT mass, accounting for the body weight loss. The mechanisms underlying the pronounced reduction in eWAT and associated factors will be further investigated.

BK channel deficiency enriches pathways related to lipolysis

To investigate the reasons and related mechanisms for the significant reduction of eWAT mass when BK channels were knockout, transcriptomics based on an RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) assay was used to investigate the differences in gene expression between primary adipocytes derived from the eWAT of BK-KO and WT rats. We conducted a reanalysis of the previous RNA-seq results obtained by our research group (26). We conducted standardization of the dataset to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 2A). According to the defined criteria, a total of 357 DEGs were screened out in the BK-KO group in comparison with the WT group, of which 198 genes were upregulated and 159 genes were downregulated (Fig. 2B). There were significant differences in the overall gene composition through cluster analysis (Fig. 2C). To identify the biological functions and signaling pathways related to lipid metabolism, DEGs were used for gene ontology biological process (GO-BP) enrichment and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the enrichment of DEGs was associated with lipolysis-related pathways, namely PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, cAMP signaling pathway and AMPK signaling pathway in adipocytes (Fig. 2D). Subsequently, the DEGs enrichment in the GO biological terms performing functions in biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF), we found that BK-KO is closely related to glycerolipid metabolic process in BP enrichment analysis (Fig. 2E). RNA-seq results indicate that deletion of the BK channels might promote the lipolysis process through the cAMP, PI3K-AKT and/or AMPK signaling pathway.

Subsequently, we employed transcriptomics based on RNA-seq assays to investigate the differences in gene expression between the eWAT of BK-KO and WT rats. As illustrated in the volcanic plot (Supplemental Fig. S1A), a total of 342 DEGs—comprising 174 upregulated genes and 168 downregulated genes—were identified between the eWAT samples isolated from the BK-KO and WT rats. This identification was based on the criteria of adjusted P value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1. Furthermore, DEGs were presented in a heatmap (Supplemental Fig. S1B), clearly indicating significant differences in gene expression profiles between the two groups. To gain further biological insights regarding DEGs, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed. The results indicated that two pathways closely related to lipid metabolism were significantly upregulated, namely, the metabolic pathway and cAMP signaling pathway (Supplemental Fig. S1C). The RNA-seq results are validated by the primary adipocyte RNA-seq results, indicating that the deficiency of BK channels could regulate the lipolysis process.

BK channel deficiency increases fasting-induced lipolysis in vivo

To clarify whether BK channels in adipocytes affect lipolysis, we fasted WT and BK-KO rats for 24 h. When subjected to 24 h of fasting, BK-KO rats showed a greater decrease in the ratio of the body weight change to total body weight after fasting than WT rats (Fig. 3A, B). The findings indicated that the magnitude of weight reduction observed in BK-KO rats is substantially higher compared to that in WT rats. We also measured the weights of different adipose tissues in the fed state and after 24 h of fasting. The results showed that under fasting conditions, the adipose tissue weight of BK-KO rats was significantly reduced, and the ratio of adipose tissue weight to body weight was also significantly decreased, with eWAT being particularly affected (Fig. 3C–H), which may be an important reason for the observed body weight loss. Within the same genotype, compared to the fed state, after 24 h of fasting, there was no statistically significant reduction in the weight of iWAT and eWAT adipose stores or the percentage of adipose store weight relative to body weight in WT and BK-KO rats; only a downward trend was observed (Fig. 3C–H). This finding may be related to the short duration of fasting, as 24 h of fasting has a limited effect on mobilizing fat reserves, not reaching a level detectable by gross weight measurement. Hemoglobin and eosin (H&E) staining and adipocyte size analysis of eWAT revealed smaller adipocytes in BK-KO rats than in WT rats, under both fed and fasting conditions (Fig. 3I–K). Direct comparison of adipocyte size within the same genotype between fed and fasting states showed that after 24 h of fasting, the adipocyte area of both WT and BK-KO rats was significantly reduced, and the cell diameter was correspondingly shortened (Fig. 3J, K), indicating that 24 h of fasting is sufficient to induce significant changes in adipocyte morphology, demonstrating that lipolytic mobilization has been initiated.

It is widely recognized that the functions of ATGL and HSL, pivotal lipases for fat mobilization, hold significant importance in adipose tissue (15). The RNA-Seq analysis suggested that BK channels affect the lipolysis process through the cAMP pathway. Consequently, we used western blotting to determine the effects of the BK channel deficiency on the expression levels of various proteins associated with lipolysis in vivo. Our results demonstrated that consistent with a reduction in eWAT mass (Fig. 3D, G), the BK channel deficiency significantly increased the phosphorylation level of HSL on Ser660, which indicates the activation of HSL (Fig. 3L, M). Meanwhile, ATGL protein expression levels were significantly elevated after the BK channel deficiency. Notably, however, the phosphorylation level of HSL on Ser565 was not altered (Fig. 3L, M), which is recognized as a negative regulatory element (27, 28, 29). The abovementioned data tentatively suggest that BK channel deficiency accelerates lipolysis of eWAT after 24 h of fasting.

BK channel deficiency resists high-fat diet-induced weight gain

To prevent the impact of BK channel deficiency in non-adipose tissues on adipose tissue lipolysis, we subsequently constructed adipocyte-specific BK knockout mice (BKadipo−/−) using Crisp/Cas9 technology (Supplemental Fig. S2A). Adipoq-Cre−/−BKflox/flox littermate mice (BKadipo+/+) were used as wild-type controls. Genotyping was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis, and qRT-PCR confirmed the effective and specific knockout of BK channels in adipose tissue (Supplemental Fig. S2B, C).

After feeding 6-week-old BKadipo−/− mice and their BKadipo+/+ controls with a 60% high-fat diet (HFD) for 16 weeks, significant observations emerged. BKadipo−/− mice displayed significantly lower weight gain compared to HFD-fed BKadipo+/+ mice (Fig. 4A–D). Subsequent EchoMRI scans and adipose tissue analysis affirmed that the reduced body weight in BKadipo−/− mice stemmed from decreased fat mass relative to BKadipo+/+ mice, with no notable changes in lean tissue weight (Supplemental Fig. S3A–E). Photographic comparison of different adipose tissues from BKadipo−/− mice and BKadipo+/+ mice demonstrated a notable reduction in WAT in BKadipo−/− mice (Supplemental Fig. S3F). Notably, the reduced weight gain in HFD-fed BKadipo−/− mice was not associated with variations in food and water intake compared to HFD-fed BKadipo+/+ mice (Supplemental Fig. S3G, H). We examined blood parameters associated with obesity, including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL). The results showed that these parameters were improved in BKadipo−/− mice (Supplemental Fig. S3I–L).

BKadipo−/− mice had greater glucose and insulin tolerances compared to BKadipo+/+ mice (Supplemental Fig. S3M–P). In conclusion, adipocyte-specific deletion of BK channels protected mice from HFD-induced obesity and insulin resistance. Meantime we fed 6-week-old BKadipo−/− mice and their BKadipo+/+ control mice a normal chow diet (CD) for 16 weeks. The majority of the phenotypes observed in BKadipo−/− mice on the HFD regimen diminished when comparing BKadipo−/− mice with BKadipo+/+ mice under the CD diet condition (Supplemental Fig. S4A–T).

In alignment with our fasting trials, histological analyses through H&E staining of eWAT revealed a reduction in the size of adipocytes in BKadipo−/− mice compared to BKadipo+/+ mice under HFD conditions (Fig. 4E–G). Consistent with our fasting experiment results, under HFD conditions, compared with BKadipo+/+ mice, BKadipo−/− mice exhibited significantly increased phosphorylation of HSL at Ser660 in eWAT, while phosphorylation of HSL at Ser565 remained unchanged. In contrast to the eWAT findings in BK-KO rats, the expression levels of ATGL remained unaltered in BKadipo−/− mice (Fig. 4H, I). After 16 weeks of HFD feeding, there was a noticeable trend towards increased release of glycerol and free fatty acid (FFA) into circulation in BKadipo−/− mice (Fig. 4J, K). This indicates that BKadipo−/− mice are resistant to HFD-induced weight gain, at least in part due to stimulated lipolysis. We further performed indirect calorimetry analysis and found that HFD-fed BKadipo−/− mice exhibited significantly elevated oxygen consumption rate (VO2), carbon dioxide production rate (VCO2), and energy expenditure (EE) compared to their BKadipo+/+ littermate controls. Following ANCOVA adjustment with lean mass as the covariate, statistically significant differences were observed for VO2 (P = 0.0291), VCO2 (P = 0.0454), and EE (P = 0.0319) (Supplemental Fig. S3Q–S). Similar experimental assessments were conducted on CD-fed BKadipo−/− mice and their BKadipo+/+ counterparts, revealing no discernible difference between the two groups (Supplemental Fig. S5A–J), indicating that the effect is specifically triggered by metabolic stress. Taken together, these results demonstrate that adipocyte-specific deletion of BK channels reduces fat mass through concurrent enhancement of lipolysis and energy expenditure.

BK channel deficiency promotes lipolysis by improving the phosphorylation of HSL at Ser660

To eliminate potential confounding effects from other cells in adipose tissue, epididymal adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) obtained from BK-KO and WT rats were induced into adipocytes and subsequently subjected to further investigations (Fig. 5A). The β3 receptor agonist CL-316243 was used to induce lipolysis in adipocytes. Results indicated a significant increase in glycerol levels in the culture medium of BK channel deficiency adipocytes 1 h after CL-316243 stimulation (Fig. 5B). For a brief (1-h) intervention on BK channel function in mature adipocytes in the presence of CL-316243, a BK channel antagonist, PAX (3 μM), and a BK channel agonist, NS1619 (20 μM), were utilized. It was observed that inhibition of BK channels significantly increased glycerol levels in the culture medium in the presence of CL-316243. Whereas BK channel activation reduced glycerol levels in the culture medium under the same conditions (Fig. 5C).

To gain insight into the related mechanisms through which BK channel deficiency accelerates lipolysis, we examined the levels and phosphorylation status of key proteins regulating lipolysis in adipocytes in vitro. Following CL-316243 stimulation for 1 h, the phosphorylation levels of HSL at Ser660 were significantly elevated in the context of BK channel deficiency (Fig. 5D, E). Moreover, the mRNA levels of HSL and ATGL were significantly elevated in BK channel-deficient adipocytes (Fig. 5F). Using the BK channel antagonist PAX (3 μM) and the BK channel agonist NS1619 (20 μM) for a short (1 h) intervention on the BK channel function in mature adipocytes in the presence of CL-316243, we found that inhibition of the BK channels significantly increased phosphorylation levels of HSL at Ser660 in the presence of CL-316243. This effect diminished upon BK channel activation (Fig. 5G, H). Interestingly, there were no significant changes in the mRNA expression levels of HSL and ATGL when BK channel function was either inhibited or activated (Fig. 5I).

Taken together, these results support that BK channels deficiency or inhibition enhances CL-316243-stimulated lipolysis in mature adipocytes in vitro. Meanwhile, activation of BK channels suppresses lipolytic response to CL-316243-stimulation.

BK channel deficiency promotes lipolysis by activating the cAMP/PKA/HSL signaling pathway

It has been established that the phosphorylation level of HSL at the Ser660 site is regulated by PKA. Following a 24-h fasting period, we noted that the phosphorylation level of the PKA substrate in BK-KO eWAT was significantly higher in comparison to WT eWAT (Fig. 6A, B). This finding also corroborates the RNA-seq analysis, which indicates that the absence of BK channels promotes lipolysis through the cAMP/PKA/HSL signaling pathway. To further elucidate the role of BK channels in regulating lipolysis through cAMP/PKA/HSL pathway, we pretreated mature adipocytes derived from BK-KO and WT rats for 1 h using H89 (10 μM), an inhibitor of PKA, a key protein in lipolysis, while simultaneously administering CL-316243 to stimulate lipolysis. Our findings revealed that BK channel deficiency led to increased glycerol levels in the culture medium during CL-316243-stimulated lipolysis compared to the WT group. However, the effect of BK-KO on increased glycerol release disappeared after treatment with H89 (Fig. 6C). In addition, the BK-KO-induced upregulation of HSL-Ser660 phosphorylation was eliminated after inhibition of PKA by H89 (Fig. 6D, E). The absence of BK channels promotes the release of glycerol from the culture medium and enhances phosphorylation levels of HSL at Ser660 in the presence of CL-316243, which can be mimicked by the adenylate cyclase activator forskolin (10 μM) or 8-bromo-cAMP (1 mM), a soluble cAMP analog (Fig. 6F–H). These results collectively indicate that BK-KO promotes the lipolysis process by activating the cAMP/PKA/HSL pathway.

It is well-known that the activity of BK channels, as calcium (Ca2+)-activated potassium (K+) channels, is closely related to changes in intracellular Ca2+ concentration (30, 31). Previous studies have demonstrated that sustained elevation of intracellular calcium primarily promotes lipid synthesis, whereas the activation of lipolysis depends more critically on transient, localized Ca2+ spikes (32, 33). To further investigate the role of Ca2+ signaling in BK channel deficiency-induced lipolysis, we examined intracellular Ca2+ dynamics in mature adipocytes differentiated in vitro from BK-KO and WT rats following stimulation with CL-316243. Our results showed that, compared to WT adipocytes, both BK-KO adipocytes and WT adipocytes pre-incubated with PAX to inhibit BK channel function exhibited transient increases in both the average and Δfold change in fluorescence intensity of fluo-4 (Fig. 6I, J). These data indicate that under β3-adrenergic stimulation, genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of BK channels transiently elevates intracellular Ca2+ levels, thereby modulating the cAMP/PKA/HSL signaling axis to promote lipolysis.

BK channel deficiency facilitates AKT-independent activation of cAMP-PKA pathway to promote lipolysis

Our previous results showed that phosphorylation levels of AKT-Ser473 were elevated by insulin stimulation, and the inhibition of BK channels hindered this process (25). Meanwhile, it has been reported that elevated phosphorylation levels of AKT-Ser473 can inhibit cAMP levels and thus indirectly inhibit the lipolysis process (34). After 24 h of fasting, there was no significant difference in the phosphorylation levels of AKT-Ser473 between WT eWAT and BK-KO eWAT. Moreover, in both the WT and BK-KO groups, there was no statistically significant difference in the p-AKT (Ser473)/AKT ratio between the fed and fasted states (Fig. 7A, B). Meanwhile, inhibiting BK channel function in the presence of CL-316243 did not impact the phosphorylation levels of AKT-Ser473 (Fig. 7C, D), but it resulted in elevated phosphorylation of HSL-Ser660 and enhanced lipolysis (Fig. 5G, H). These combined results indicate that the inhibition of BK channel function involves a signaling pathway independent of the PI3K-AKT signaling axis, which can directly regulate the lipolysis process.

Discussion

The relationship between adipocyte lipolysis and energy homeostasis is crucial, as any dysregulation in adipocyte lipolytic mechanisms can disrupt homeostasis and contribute to metabolic disorders (35, 36, 37). Excessive lipolysis in WAT can lead to systemic insulin resistance and play a role in the development of diabetes (38). It is essential to delve deeper into the finely tuned regulatory mechanisms of lipolysis to identify new targets that can modulate the rate of lipolysis, offering resistance to obesity while preventing lipotoxicity-induced diabetes resulting from excessive lipolysis. Fasting is not only a means of dietary restriction, but also a strategy for metabolic regulation, making it a topic of great interest to those seeking a deeper understanding of the processes associated with lipolysis. During periods of energy deprivation, triacylglycerols stored in WAT are hydrolyzed by lipolytic enzymes, releasing fatty acids (FA) into the circulation for utilization by other organs as an energy source. This study has demonstrated that BK channels exert an inhibitory effect on PKA activation-induced lipolysis in adipocytes. It is known that BK expression in adipocytes is influenced by nutritional status, and increased BK channel expression is observed in the adipose tissue of obese individuals (22). The study findings indicate that BK channel deficiency or pharmacological inhibition accelerates lipolysis, whereas pharmacological activation inhibits this process. Specifically, enhanced activation of HSL was observed in BK-KO rats and BKadipo−/− mice. BKadipo−/− mice displayed resistance to HFD-induced weight gain and improved insulin sensitivity.

In our study, we initially observed decreased body weight and reduced WAT mass in BK-KO rats compared to WT controls, with visceral WAT exhibiting the most significant reduction (Fig. 1).Consistent with previous findings different fat depots showed different phenotypes responses to fasting, and visceral WAT was particularly sensitive to lipolytic stimulation (39, 40, 41). To further explore the underlying mechanisms for the significant reduction in eWAT weight following BK deletion, we performed RNA-Seq analysis. Enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed pathways associated with the cAMP and PI3K-AKT pathway linked to lipolytic metabolism (Fig. 2). As a critical intracellular second messenger, cAMP responds to various hormonal stimuli such as adrenaline and glucagon. These hormones act through G protein-coupled receptors on the cell surface, activating adenylate cyclase (AC) and subsequently increasing intracellular cAMP levels. Previous studies have shown that cAMP promotes lipolysis by activating its downstream-effector, PKA (15). Based on these observations, we propose that BK channel deficiency influences lipid metabolism via β3-adrenergic-mediated PKA activation, leading to enhanced expression and activation of key lipolytic enzymes. Under fasting conditions, our results showed that BK channel knockout led to increased protein expression of ATGL, increased phosphorylation of HSL at the Ser660 site (Fig. 3), and upregulated levels of PKA substrate phosphorylation (Fig. 6). Moreover, in vitro experiments revealed that stimulation with CL-316243, forskolin and the 8-bromo-cAMP significantly enhanced lipolysis in BK-deficient adipocytes (Figs. 5 and 6), whereas pharmacological inhibition of PKA with H89 completely abolished this effect (Fig. 6). Collectively, these findings indicate that BK- channel deficiency promotes lipolysis by activating the cAMP/PKA/HSL pathway.

Research by Illison et al. has indicated that BK channel deficiency protects mice from HFD-induced weight gain and fat accumulation, with adipocytes relying on UCP1 -mediated energy dissipation to reduce lipid storage (24). It has also been established that the induction of lipolysis is essential for UCP1-dependent energy dissipation in adipocytes (42, 43). To delve deeper into this phenomenon, we constructed BKadipo−/− mice and subjected them to via HFD-induced obesity. Consistent with the findings of Illison et al., we observed resistance to HFD-induced obesity in our BKadipo−/− mouse model. Importantly, our study extends these observations by providing the first evidence that BKadipo−/− mice display a significant increase in HSL phosphorylation at the Ser660 site, thereby promoting lipolysis (Fig. 4). In contrast, under a CD, BKadipo−/− mice did not exhibit elevated HSL phosphorylation at the Ser660 site (Supplemental Fig. S5), indicating that the effect is specifically triggered by metabolic stress. These results suggest that BK channel deletion heightens adipocyte sensitivity to β3-adrenergic-stimulated lipolysis, leading to a rapid reduction of visceral fat mass and resistance to HFD-induced obesity. Notably, our study reveals a previously unrecognized role for BK channels in regulating lipolytic processes through the modulation of HSL phosphorylation. Furthermore, growing evidence suggests that chronically elevated FFA levels can promote lipid accumulation and insulin resistance in non-adipose tissues (44, 45, 46). The markedly improved insulin sensitivity observed in BKadipo−/−mice raises the possibility that additional mechanisms may also contribute to this phenotype. Indeed, compared to BKadipo+/+ littermates, BKadipo−/− mice exhibited not only increased eWAT lipolysis but also elevated energy expenditure (Supplemental Fig. S3), which may further explain their leaner phenotype—thereby extending and refining the observations originally reported by Illisonet et al. In conclusion, our findings establish BK channels as negative regulators of β3-adrenergic receptor-stimulated lipolysis and highlight BK channel inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance lipolysis and combat obesity and insulin resistance.

The Ser565 site of HSL is recognized as a phosphorylation target for multiple kinases, with different kinases modulating the activity and lipolytic process of HSL through phosphorylation at this site (15, 47). Among these kinases, AMPK is notably highlighted as a key regulator of HSL phosphorylation at the Ser565 site. It has been suggested in previous studies that AMPK-mediated regulation of HSL phosphorylation at the Ser565 site serves as a negative regulatory mechanism (27, 28, 29). Our investigation revealed that the BK-KO did not change the phosphorylation level of the inhibitory Ser565 site of HSL (Figs. 3 and 4). Additionally, the phosphorylation level of AMPK was also unchanged by BK-KO (26). Importantly, our data corroborate these findings and further demonstrate that BK deficiency significantly enhances the phosphorylation of HSL at Ser660, while having no discernible impact on the phosphorylation of HSL at Ser565. Thus, BK deficiency-mediated acceleration of lipolysis is primarily attributable to activating phosphorylation of HSL at Ser660, distinctly independent of inhibitory phosphorylation of HSL at Ser565.

The molecular mechanisms by which BK channels regulate the cAMP/PKA/HSL signaling pathway in adipocytes remain incompletely understood. As calcium (Ca2+)-activated potassium (K+) channels, BK channel activity is closely coupled to dynamic changes in intracellular Ca2+ concentrations (30, 31). In the present study, we demonstrated that BK channel deficiency or pharmacological inhibition significantly enhanced β3-adrenergic receptor agonist CL-316243-induced lipolysis, accompanied by transient elevations in intracellular Ca2+ levels (Fig. 6). These findings suggest that BK channels may influence the activity of the cAMP/PKA/HSL signaling axis through modulating transient Ca2+ dynamics. According to relevant published literature, there is a close and complex crosstalk between intracellular Ca2+ and the cAMP/PKA signaling pathway. On the one hand, Ca2+ can directly modulate the activity of various adenylyl cyclase (AC) isoforms to promote or inhibit cAMP synthesis (48, 49). On the other hand, Ca2+ also influences the catalytic efficiency of phosphodiesterases (PDEs) to affect cAMP degradation (50). Through these two pathways, Ca2+ jointly alters intracellular cAMP levels, thereby regulating the activation of downstream PKA and the phosphorylation of its substrate HSL, and ultimately controlling the lipolytic process. Of note, the regulation of lipolysis by intracellular Ca2+ is complex. Previous studies have shown that sustained elevations of intracellular Ca2+ in adipocytes primarily promote lipid synthesis and suppress lipolysis (32, 51), whereas the activation of lipolysis is more critically dependent on transient, localized Ca2+ spikes (33). Consistent with these observations, our study revealed that BK channel deficiency did not induce sustained Ca2+ elevations but rather triggered transient Ca2+ increases upon β3-adrenergic stimulation. This spatiotemporally distinct Ca2+ signaling pattern may represent a key mechanism underlying the pro-lipolytic effect of BK channel deficiency.

It is well known that the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway is a major downstream pathway of the insulin receptor, and it is a classical signaling pathway that regulates lipid deposition in adipocytes. Our previous results showed that activation of BK channels induced lipid deposition in adipocytes through the insulin/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which regulates lipid synthesis (25). It has now been demonstrated that insulin inhibits lipolysis in an AKT-dependent manner (52, 53). Further investigation revealed that in the experimental system of CL-316243-induced lipolysis, either inhibition or activation of BK channels did not change the phosphorylation level of AKT at Ser473 (Fig. 7), whereas the phosphorylation level of HSL at Ser660 was correspondingly increased or decreased (Fig. 5). These data indicate that, while we cannot exclude the possibility that BK deficiency may affect AKT signaling in the presence of insulin, the BK channel can also directly regulate lipolysis independently of AKT signaling under insulin-free conditions in vitro. Together, these findings indicate that BK channels promote lipolysis process via a PI3K-AKT-independent mechanism, involving direct activation of the cAMP/PKA/HSL cascade.

In summary, the BK channel is capable of responding to changes in metabolic signals and regulating the lipolysis process, thereby affecting the metabolic homeostasis of the whole organism. In this study, we identified that BK channels regulate lipolysis through the cAMP/PKA/HSL pathway, which provides a new entry point to understand the intricate regulation of the lipolysis mechanism and lays the foundation for the subsequent elucidation of the mechanism by which BK-KO attenuates obesity and related metabolic diseases. The specific mechanisms of BK channels in the regulation of lipolysis and their potential roles in metabolic diseases need to be further explored, which will provide an important theoretical basis for the development of new treatment strategies for obesity-related metabolic diseases.

Limitations and future directions

This study still has certain limitations. We only confirmed the regulatory relationship among BK channels, Ca2+ dynamics and lipolytic signaling, but did not further verify the precise molecular interaction between Ca2+ and the cAMP/PKA axis. Future studies will focus on exploring the detailed mechanistic crosstalk of this signaling cascade to supplement and improve the regulatory network of BK channels in adipocyte lipid metabolism.
