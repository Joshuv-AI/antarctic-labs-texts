Navigation and intraoperative imaging in pelvic and acetabular trauma
Authors: Lynn Hutchings, Douglas Haase, Sven Meylaerts, Frank Ijpma, Ashraf El Naga, Kornelis J. Ponsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe introduction of 3D technology in pelvic and acetabular surgery has the potential to change preoperative and intraoperative management. This technology is being developed in a number of areas, including improved preoperative planning and the development of patient-specific implants, and intraoperative visualization and assistance in traditionally technically challenging procedures. This expanding field holds exciting avenues for future changes in practice but requires knowledge of its limitations for appropriate application.

1. Introduction

The inclusion of 3D technology has the potential to revolutionize the management of pelvic and acetabular fractures, by enhancing visualization, education, surgical planning, intraoperative fixation, and postoperative evaluation.1 These advancements provide value to surgeons and patients alike, by improving understanding, surgical accuracy, and eventually long-term prognosis.

The indications and uses of 3D technology continue to develop, with use in preoperative decision making, intraoperative analysis of percutaneous fixation or fracture reduction, and development of patient-specific implants. Use of this evolving technology involves suitable access to appropriate systems, and knowledge of its uses and limitations.

2. Intraoperative 3D Fluoroscopy

Operative treatment of pelvic and acetabular fractures is challenging due to the nature of the injuries, the intricate soft tissue anatomy, and the complex osteology. Although there are well-described techniques for percutaneous fixation using conventional 2D fluoroscopy, intraoperative assessment of fracture reduction is often limited.2–6 The complex three-dimensional anatomy can be challenging to clearly visualize with 2D fluoroscopy, particularly when evaluating the position of screws within osseous pathways, and reduction of the acetabular articular surface and sacroiliac joints. These limitations can be amplified in patients with large body habitus, prominent bowel gas, or poor bone quality.7

The consequences of inaccurate reductions and malpositioned implants are well-established. The quality of acetabular reduction is directly correlated with patient outcomes, including rate of conversion to total hip arthroplasty.8–10 Anatomical sacroiliac joint reduction and pelvic ring symmetry correlate with improved patient outcomes in pelvic ring injuries.11,12 As minimal invasive or percutaneous surgery is increasingly used, the ability to accurately place implants is paramount. Rates of implant malposition using conventional methods have been reported as high as 15%, with neurological injury occurring in 2% of patients.13–16

Multidimensional, or 3D, fluoroscopy refers to a mobile fluoroscopy unit that is capable of providing both standard 2D imaging, and intraoperative cross-sectional imaging using a motorized rotational movement combined with a portable computer workstation. This allows the treating surgeon to obtain intraoperative imaging of the surgical field in 2D and 3D with minimal workflow disruption. This method improves the management of pelvic and acetabular fractures in 3 main ways: guiding intraoperative decisions, assessing reductions, and ensuring safe placement of implants. 3D fluoroscopy has been shown to be effective in evaluating implant placement in pelvic ring injuries17–19 and maintaining both quality and safety in both pelvic and acetabular fractures.20

The sequence of reduction and fixation in pelvic and acetabular injuries varies based on multiple factors and is often a dynamic intraoperative process, altered by patient positioning, muscle relaxation, reduction maneuvers, and patient physiology. The use of 3D fluoroscopy can allow alteration in surgical tactics and can reduce the surgical insult to the patient. Accurate assessment of malreduction, particularly at the articular surface, is better defined on postoperative CT scans than on intraoperative 2D fluoroscopy.21 The ability to identify and correct malreductions before the conclusion of the procedure would improve the patient's treatment, making 3D fluoroscopy a valuable intraoperative imaging tool.

The early literature on 3D fluoroscopy predominantly has evaluated implant placement in the posterior aspect of the pelvis due to the risk of neurologic injury with malpositioning.4,12,16–18 Insertion of posterior screws can be challenging using 2D fluoroscopy, particularly when the pelvis is dysmorphic.22 Intraoperative 3D fluoroscopy is useful for screw placement, allowing assessment of the position of the guide wire within the osseous pathway before definitive implant placement.

3. 3D Navigation in Pelvic Surgery—A Single Unit Experience in The Netherlands

Our experience in 3D navigation during pelvic surgery started in 2011, with use predominantly during fixation of pelvic ring injuries to assist in the accuracy and safety of placement of percutaneous screws.

The method followed involves connecting a patient frame to the pelvis, usually at the anterior superior iliac spine, with the O-arm in position and connected to the StealthStation S8 navigation device (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN). A demonstration of the set-up is given in Figure 1. Once all connections are complete, a run is performed to acquire images. The drill guide, which also has a frame on it, is placed on the patient frame for calibration. This allow both the drill and the pelvis to be viewed on a screen in multiple projections.

We performed a retrospective consecutive cohort study on 90 patients with pelvic ring fractures who were treated using 3D navigated screw fixation using the O-arm and StealthStation devices under local ethical approval. This included injuries both from high energy trauma and fragility fractures, with specific attention paid to surgical and neurological complications. An outline of the results is given in Table 1.

One notable feature has been that the guide wires can bend within the bone due to changes in bone density. Because the wire cannot be seen on the screen, but the drill guide extrapolates the guidewire position, this can lead to mistakes in placement. However, analysis of 3D guidance for pelvic surgery in a single center does appear to suggest increased safety compared with conventional screw placement. In addition, we feel 3D guidance may be helpful for surgeons in lower volume centers to reduce the time for the learning curve for screw placement.

Our experience in 3D-guided percutaneous acetabular surgery is more limited, and to date comprises mainly of minimally displaced or periprosthetic fractures. Acetabular fractures with significant displacement require different clamping techniques to move percutaneous techniques further. We believe this to be the next step in acetabular surgery and an opportunity to significantly decrease surgical impact and risks.

4. 3D-Printed Implants in Acetabular Surgery

The role of 3D imaging can revolutionize acetabular surgery, allowing for preoperative assessment of fracture displacement, optimization of preoperative planning, and facilitation of operations with patient-specific implants.

4.1. Preoperative Visualization

For all surgeons, 3D imaging enhances understanding of complex fracture patterns. Traditional 2D modalities, such as X-ray (XR) and Computed Tomography (CT), offer limited perspectives and can lead to high intraobserver and interobserver variability in determining fracture displacement and treatment planning.23 3D visualization provides a more intuitive understanding of fracture morphology, improving decision making and communication between surgeons. In addition, 3D models serve as valuable educational tools to trainees and assist in patient counseling by offering a clear visual representation of the injury and planned surgical approach.

4.2. 3D Virtual Surgical Planning

Acetabular fractures have traditionally required meticulous preoperative planning to achieve anatomical reduction and reduce complications. Using 3D virtual modeling, surgeons can create a tailored plan to optimize fracture reduction, implant placement, and screw positioning. This approach minimizes surgical uncertainty, allowing surgeons to anticipate challenges and refine strategies before entering the operating room. Using a quantitative 3D-CT method, surgeons can quantify intra-articular displacement. Recently, we introduced the “3D gap area,” defined as the three-dimensional surface area between fracture fragments, including all gaps and step-offs and which represents the total fracture displacement24 (Fig. 2). Studies indicate that larger initial and residual 3D gaps result in higher risks of conversion to total hip arthroplasty.25,26

3D models demonstrating the “3D gap area” (in red) representing the 3D surface between all fracture fragments.

4.3. 3D-Assisted Surgery and Patient-specific Implants

Recently, we introduced a 3D-assisted technique incorporating patient-specific implants for acetabular fracture surgery.27,28 This innovative approach enables precise fracture reconstruction by tailoring implants for osteosynthesis to individual patient anatomy, starting with 3D virtual surgical planning based on CT imaging. The fracture fragments are virtually reduced, allowing patient-specific plates and drill guides to be developed to optimize fixation and screw placement (Fig. 3). These custom implants can be manufactured within days, allowing for rapid clinical application. Intraoperatively, the 3D guides assist in accurate positioning, minimizing errors, reducing time required for manual plate contouring, and ensuring alignment with the preoperative plan.6–8 Our studies indicate that this technique may improve the accuracy of acetabular reconstruction, reducing the risk of post-traumatic arthritis.27–29

5. Navigation in Spinopelvic Surgery

Historically, spinopelvic surgery has relied heavily on fluoroscopy, requiring multiple views to ensure accurate instrumentation. This creates a number of issues, including radiation exposure of the patient and surgical team, difficulties in maintaining precision in tight anatomical corridors, and challenges in cases with suboptimal fluoroscopic views30–33 (Fig. 4).

Poor pelvic corridor visualization on the (A) inlet view and (B) outlet view in the setting of osteopenic bone and overlying bowel gas. Clear demonstration of the trans-iliac-trans-sacral corridor using navigation in an osteopenic patient with metastatic disease (C).

In select cases, navigated spinopelvic instrumentation can offer several advantages. By providing enhanced intraoperative imaging and real-time guidance, navigation can reduce surgical team radiation exposure, allow surgeons to safely place hardware in cases with suboptimal fluoroscopic images (eg, osteopenia or overlying bowel gas), facilitate instrumentation of nontraditional anatomical corridors, and the opportunity to streamline cases with narrow bony corridors or where instrumentation is required around preexisting hardware.

However, navigation does have several limitations. When instrumenting with fluoroscopic guidance, the surgeon's attention is placed on the quality of the images and views obtained. With navigated techniques, this attention shifts to whether a navigated projection is accurate. The accuracy of the system can be compromised by unnoticed shifts of a fiduciary array (if used), and mobility between fracture fragments in the case of trauma. Further, a learning curve exists with the use of navigated technology.34,35 One current limitation as it applies to navigated fracture care is that if the instrumentation is being used to reduce an injury (such as with placement of an iliosacral lag screw), all subsequent navigated projection may no longer be reliable. However, with experience, careful intraoperative vigilance, and a thoughtful workflow, these challenges can be mitigated (Fig. 5). With the above limitations, the ideal application to leap into navigated spinopelvic surgery is for percutaneous spinopelvic instrumentation in a reduced pelvis.

Navigation, therefore, has value in spinopelvic surgery in overcoming some of the limitations of fluoroscopy and enhancing surgical precision. However, its implementation requires understanding of its limitations to maximize patient safety.

6. Conclusions

The integration of 3D technology into management of pelvic and acetabular surgery marks an ever-evolving paradigm shift in assessment, planning, and management. By using 3D techniques, surgeons can enhance preoperative preparation, improve intraoperative precision, and optimize postoperative outcomes. As 3D technology continues to evolve, we have an opportunity to define and develop our surgical practices to optimize the advantages to surgeons and patients alike.
