Custom, 3-Dimensional Patient-Specific Instrumentation in Anatomic Total Shoulder Arthroplasty: Part 1—Preoperative Assessment, Preoperative Planning, and Guide Design
Authors: Benjamin Kerzner, Zeeshan A Khan, Suhas P Dasari, Christopher M Brusalis, Johnathon R McCormick, Vitor La Banca, Mario Hevesi, Nikhil N Verma
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuccessful total shoulder arthroplasty relies on a multitude of factors specific to patients, implant selection, and surgical technique. Among technical factors, correct intraoperative placement of prosthetic components is paramount. Three-dimensional computed tomography has emerged as a vital tool, allowing surgeons to measure glenoid inclination, glenoid version, and humeral head subluxation more accurately and reproducibly. Several commercial preoperative planning software resources are available to determine the optimal size and positioning of prosthetic components yet do so at significant cost and with meaningful time constraints. At our institution, we have applied the principles of these technologies to develop a custom, 3-dimensionally printed glenoid guide for accurate placement of the central pin. In this technical note, we describe our evaluation of patients with glenohumeral arthritis, as well as our step-by-step procedure for designing and printing a custom patient-specific instrumentation glenoid guide for anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty.

The incidence of total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) in the United States has continued to rise dramatically.1, 2, 3 Commensurate with the increasing popularity of TSA, advancements in preoperative imaging modalities, surgical techniques, and implant design have revolutionized the care of patients with glenohumeral osteoarthritis (OA).4, 5, 6, 7

The success of shoulder arthroplasty is predicated on not only proper surgical indications but also correct intraoperative placement of prosthetic components within the glenohumeral joint. Although preoperative templating has been applied extensively in hip and knee arthroplasty,8, 9, 10, 11, 12 analogous tools in shoulder arthroplasty have only recently become widely available.7,13,14 Three-dimensional (3D) computed tomography (CT) enables surgeons to measure glenoid inclination, glenoid version, and humeral head subluxation more accurately and to determine the corrections needed prior to surgery in a reproducible fashion.15,16 This is of particular importance with respect to the glenoid component because glenoid loosening has been shown to be the leading cause of failure after anatomic TSA and is commonly a result of malpositioning, bone loss, and altered joint reactive forces.17, 18, 19 Several commercial preoperative planning software resources are available to determine the optimal size and positioning of prosthetic components yet do so at significant cost and with meaningful time constraints. At our institution, we have applied the principles of these technologies to develop a custom, 3D printed glenoid guide for accurate placement of the central pin.20

In part 1 of this 3-part technique series, we describe our preoperative workup for patients presenting with glenohumeral arthritis, as well as our technique for creating a custom patient-specific instrumentation (PSI) glenoid guide that is inexpensive, reproducible, and effective (Video 1).

Clinical Evaluation

Evaluation of patients with suspected glenohumeral OA begins with a comprehensive clinical history. A thorough collection of a patient’s medical comorbidities, current medications, metal and/or drug allergies, and history of adverse reactions to anesthesia provides invaluable data to guide perioperative care and determine candidacy for arthroplasty.21 Next, the patient’s complaint of shoulder pain must be investigated carefully to understand its chronicity, location, severity, and exacerbating and alleviating factors, as well as prior treatment. Typical characteristics associated with glenohumeral OA include advanced patient age, atraumatic onset, chronic shoulder pain associated with stiffness and decreased shoulder function, and night pain, as well as transient improvement after intra-articular corticosteroid administration. However, the clinical presentation of glenohumeral OA is varied, and the differential diagnosis includes adhesive capsulitis, rotator cuff pathology, biceps tendinitis, acromioclavicular joint arthritis, and multiple cervical spine conditions. Understanding a patient’s functional status and desired activity level impact surgical decision making and inform preoperative patient education.

Within the systematic physical examination performed on all patients who present with suspected shoulder pain, 3 components provide essential information for appropriate decision making in the surgical management of glenohumeral OA. First, the patient’s active and passive range of motion in forward flexion, external rotation with the arm at the side, and internal rotation behind the patient’s back are evaluated. Patients with glenohumeral OA often have limited active and passive range of motion. Second, the health and function of the patient’s rotator cuff are assessed with manual resistance strength testing. Third, a neurovascular examination is performed to ensure the absence of pre-existing neuropathy, particularly of the axillary nerve as it relates to deltoid function.

A standard series of 3 radiographs is obtained in all patients with suspected glenohumeral OA. The Grashey view is a variation of a traditional anteroposterior shoulder radiograph obtained in the plane of the glenohumeral joint. In this view, the x-ray beam is aimed approximately 30° laterally to account for the native anteversion of the scapula.22 The presence of an osteophyte at the inferior margin of the humeral head is a classic radiographic sign of OA. The axillary lateral view provides the most informative radiographic view to assess the severity of glenohumeral OA. Arthritic progression is classically associated with posterior humeral head subluxation relative to the glenoid and, subsequently, attritional wear of the posterior aspect of the glenoid surface. Finally, the scapular-Y view provides further information regarding humeral head subluxation, as well as acromial morphology.

Once the diagnosis of glenohumeral OA is confirmed based on clinical history, physical examination, and radiographic evaluation, advanced imaging is routinely obtained to guide surgical decision making and aid in preoperative planning. In our practice, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is obtained routinely to evaluate the integrity of the patient’s rotator cuff. Finally, a non-contrast CT scan with 3D reconstruction of the shoulder is performed routinely in patients indicated for shoulder arthroplasty. In subsequent sections, we discuss how data obtained from CT guide the development of PSI that improves the accuracy of intraoperative component positioning.

Planning and Production of PSI

The application of preoperative planning and in-house production of 3D printed, patient-specific glenoid drill guides were previously described by the senior author (N.N.V.).20 For accurate preoperative planning, we recommend a CT scan for all patients undergoing shoulder arthroplasty. The CT scan is performed with the patient in the supine position with the following parameters: 0.625-mm slice thickness, 120 kV, 260 milliampere-seconds, and image matrix size of 512 × 512. To generate a head-subtracted 3D model of the scapula, the resulting 2-dimensional (2D) Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) CT image files are exported into Mimics Medical segmentation software (version 24.0; Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). Manual segmentation is performed using the axial view of the CT scan, with careful attention directed toward approximating native glenoid anatomy and removing any humeral osteophytes (i.e., goat’s beard osteophytes) or glenoid osteophytes because they may impact the PSI guide fit.

The subsequent scapular models are used to characterize native glenoid morphology via the Walch classification, native version using the Friedman line, and native glenoid inclination. Scapular models are then exported in standard tessellation language (STL) file format and used as input data for a custom-written program in Visual C++ (Microsoft, Redmond, WA), which calculates the geometric coordinates for the following 3 anatomic landmarks of each patient’s scapula: (1) centroid of the glenoid face, (2) trigonum scapulae, and (3) tip of the inferior angle of the scapula. The scapular plane is defined by the most distal point of the inferior angle of the scapula, the glenoid fossa center, and the scapula trigonum. The 3D scapular model is then imported into Rhinoceros 3D (version 73D; Robert McNeel & Associates, Seattle, WA), in which the scapular plane and axial plane are established, and a pin trajectory passing through the exact center of the glenoid and achieving target version and inclination is determined (Fig 1). The PSI guide is constructed to perfectly reflect the individual osseous anatomy of each patient’s glenoid for an optimized onlay fit. The guide is etched with the patient’s unique medical record number to ensure the correct guide is used for the appropriate patient. The finalized 3D model with focus on pin trajectory and glenoid placement is confirmed by the senior surgeon and then 3D printed using Formlabs Dental SG Resin (Somerville, MA) (Fig 2).

Standard print time is approximately 4 hours. On completion of printing, the guide undergoes a 20-minute wash in resin-saturated isopropyl alcohol and is subsequently allowed to dry at room temperature for 60 minutes to avoid potential ignition of the residual alcohol during the curing process (Fig 3 A and B). To finalize and solidify the guide, a final curing process is completed at 60°C for a total of 30 minutes (Fig 3C). Sterilization is completed using the Sterrad process (Advanced Sterilization Products, Irvine, CA) at the surgery center the day prior to the surgical procedure, with storage in a sterile peel pack. On the day of surgery, on opening the peel pack, the patient’s medical record number etched within the guide is reviewed to ensure the proper guide is being used intraoperatively (Fig 4).

Discussion

In the surgical management of glenohumeral OA, the advent of 3D CT reconstruction has allowed for more advanced analysis of patients’ native glenoid version and inclination that, unlike intraoperative assessment, is not biased by patient positioning.23,24 Several commercial software programs exist for automatic segmentation of CT scans to identify the landmarks of the glenoid and humerus independently, in addition to allowing for virtual planning of implants and assessment of central peg perforation risk.25,26 To assess the accuracy of these products, Erickson et al.25 compared measurements of 81 preoperative CT scans performed by 5 surgeons versus 4 different commercially available software planning programs and found that the commercial software measurements tended to show more superior inclination (P = .03), more retroversion (P = .023), and more posterior subluxation (P < .001) than the surgeon measurements. Additionally, the surgeons showed high inter-reliability for version, inclination, and posterior subluxation. Hartzler et al.26 performed a retrospective review of 6,483 cases using a commercial 3D planning system for TSA. There was a high rate of matching for version (66%), inclination (72%), or both version and inclination (55%) with that of the preoperative plan, but as the degree of retroversion and the surgeon case volume increased, the rate of accepting the commercial software plan decreased significantly (P < .0001). These studies highlight the importance of surgeon judgment and clinical decision making when using preoperative imaging software platforms.

PSI offers the ability to more accurately determine glenoid placement and could potentially result in reduced rates of glenoid loosening and improved implant survivorship.15,16 Cabarcas et al.20 performed a cadaveric study comparing custom in-house printed PSI glenoid guides with standard TSA guides. They found that the PSI guides had a significantly lower deviation in inclination compared with the standard guides, as well as significantly less deviation of the glenoid entry site. Dallalana et al.27 assessed the accuracy of CT-based PSI for both anatomic and reverse TSA in 20 patients at their institution and found that a PSI guide allowed for the final component to closely replicate the preoperative template plan. Iannotti et al.13 randomly assigned 46 patients to PSI or standard glenoid instrumentation using 3D CT templating and compared them with a non-randomized group undergoing 2D imaging as a historical control. They found that using 3D imaging regardless of PSI status resulted in significant improvement in achieving the desired inclination and version compared with the 2D imaging group and standard instrumentation.13

In our experience, the utility of PSI in severe deformity cases (i.e., >15° of retroversion) is a beneficial tool intraoperatively and allows for an additional data point when deciding the appropriate placement of the glenoid component (Table 1). Furthermore, we have created a reproducible guide in-house that costs less than $30 to produce and requires less than 5 hours from manual segmentation to printing.20 These benefits are of great importance to the surgeon and patient and do not result in delayed time to surgery, as can occur with commercially available PSI products.28 Additionally, there is potential for the utility of PSI applied by less experienced and/or lower-volume shoulder arthroplasty surgeons for the correct orientation of the central pin. Whether more consistent and accurate glenoid placement seen with PSI confers improved long-term clinical outcomes and implant survivorship has yet to be determined and ultimately will require further investigation.3,24 As preoperative technology for anatomic and reverse shoulder arthroplasty continues to expand, the use of PSI and implant design must continue to be scrutinized and appropriately adopted by surgeons.
