Dietary capsaicin attenuates type 2 diabetes via gut microbiota and bile acid metabolic pathways
Authors: Jing Xia, Shuhan Chen, Ping Qian, Minghong Gan, Jiashuo Wu, Huiyang Wang, Fangqing Zhang, Yanfei Mo, Lili Yin, Li Gu, Dongfang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDiabetes is a serious global health concern. Notably, individuals who prefer spicy food exhibit a lower diabetes risk, making the exploration of capsaicin—the pungent compound in chili peppers—highly relevant. The gut microbiota and its metabolites are key targets in diabetes management. This study demonstrates that dietary capsaicin alleviates type 2 diabetes (T2D) in mice. It reduces intestinal levels of conjugated bile acids (e.g., TDCA, GCA, and TCA) and normalizes the abundance of bile acid-related bacteria, Parabacteroides and Lactobacillus. Capsaicin also lowers the compensatory increase of intestinal bile salt hydrolase and regulates the function of bile acid-stimulated receptors. Bioinformatics analysis of ileum gene profiles indicated that regulated genes are primarily linked to immunity. This was confirmed by measuring inflammatory cytokines in serum and pancreas and by assessing immune-related gene expression in the ileum. In conclusion, capsaicin can prevent and treat T2D by modulating gut microbiota, bile acid metabolism, and inflammatory responses.

Introduction

According to Konno,1 diabetes is a condition characterized by consistently elevated levels of blood glucose, which is a metabolic disorder. According to the IDF’s Diabetes Atlas, approximately 537 million adults were impacted by diabetes in 2021. Out of these cases, China accounted for the highest number with 141 million individuals diagnosed.2 Thus, diabetes is a globally prominent disease that is greatly affecting human health and the economy. Type 2 diabetes (T2D), accounting for over 90% of cases among the different forms of diabetes mellitus, is distinguished by the presence of insulin resistance and beta cell dysfunction.3 The typical symptoms of T2D are commonly known as “the three polys and one loss,” meaning that T2D patients have polydipsia, polyuria, increased food intake, and weight loss. Furthermore, diabetes can result in serious complications such as cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular accident, kidney failure, limb removal, and loss of vision.4

The pathogenesis of T2D is complicated and multifactorial. It is related to heredity, environment, insulin resistance, damage of islet β cells, and inflammatory responses. According to a recent investigation, the gastrointestinal microbiome plays a vital part in the pathophysiology of T2D.5 A number of studies have found that individuals with T2D have changes in their gut microbiota, including a reduction in Bifidobacterium, Faecalibacterium, and other microbial groups.6 Modifications in the gastrointestinal microbiome can alter an individual’s physical condition through the impact on the quantities of crucial metabolites.

The discovery revealed that bile acids (BAs) play a crucial role as a metabolite that impacts T2D. By regulating the secretion of insulin, glucagon-like peptide-1, fibroblast growth factor 15/19, and various other factors, they have an impact on the metabolism of glucose and lipids.7 Furthermore, a research study revealed that people diagnosed with T2D displayed increased concentrations of serum BAs, including GCDCA, GCA, GUDCA, GCDCAS, TCDCA, TCA, and TUDCA, in comparison to those without the ailment.8 The results of these studies suggest that regulating the gut microbiota and BA metabolism could be an effective strategy for preventing and treating T2D.

A Chinese study drew a map of China’s dietary preferences and found that types of diets were associated with the incidence of diabetes.9 According to Zhao et al.,9 the risk of diabetes was found to be higher with the regular consumption of fried, grilled, and sweet foods over a prolonged period. On the other hand, the intake of piquant cuisine exhibited a contrary correlation with the likelihood of developing diabetes. Several clinical trials have demonstrated that modifying eating patterns can impact the composition and quantity of gut microbiota in individuals with T2D.10 Chili, as a vegetable and spice, is a typical spicy food widely eaten by people all over the world. Chili, the fruit of Capsicum annuum L., has long been used in traditional Jamaican medicine to treat diabetes.11 Based on this evidence, we believe that capsaicin (CAP), the main active constituent of the spicy taste in chili peppers, might affect T2D. CAP exhibits diverse pharmacological properties, including pain relief, inhibition of bacterial growth, widening of blood vessels, reduction of lipid levels, and the ability to modify the composition of gut microbiota.6,12 In previous research, it was found that obese diabetic mice treated with CAP showed a notable rise in the ratio of Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes in their gut. Human BA metabolism and blood glucose regulation are regulated by these bacteria, as highlighted by Baars et al.13

Using our current knowledge as a basis, this research is an in-depth exploration into the impact of dietary CAP on the management of T2D. The aim of our research is to further elucidate the effects of CAP on the intestinal microbiota, BA metabolism, and signaling pathways. Combined with 16S rRNA gene, metagenomics sequencing, targeted BA metabolome, and transcriptome sequencing technology, we found the mechanism via which CAP regulates the gut microbiome, related enzymes, and metabolite chains to further affect biological pathways. We also explored the role of CAP in improving T2D using bioinformatics analysis. In general, this research emphasizes the significance of consuming CAP-rich foods to control the gut microbiome and BA metabolism, which can help prevent and treat T2D

Results

Dietary CAP alleviates T2D symptoms

This study examined the impact of dietary CAP on the gut microbiome of mice with T2D using a combination of a high-fat diet (HFD) and a streptozotocin (STZ)-induced mouse model of T2D.13 Four groups of mice were given different interventions and dietary treatments at weeks 1–4 and 5–9 (Figure 1A). Throughout the entire duration of the test, we observed that the body weight of mice in the T2D + standard diet (SD) and T2D + CAP diet (CD) groups increased by week 5 (∗∗p < 0.01) when monitoring their body weight every 2 weeks (Figure 1B), suggesting a correlation with the HFD. By week 9, mice from the T2D + SD group experienced weight loss, which aligns with real clinical scenarios. Additionally, the body weight of mice on the CAP diet decreased, possibly due to the lipid-lowering function of CAP.14 To evaluate the effects of CAP on glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in mice, an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT) were conducted on the mice in each group during the ninth week. The results of the OGTT consistently showed higher blood sugar levels in the T2D + SD group compared to the control (CON) + SD group throughout the entire test period. Furthermore, the area under the curve (AUC) for the T2D + SD group was notably higher than that of the CON + SD group (∗p < 0.05) (Figures 1I–1L), suggesting a decrease in glucose metabolism and the onset of glucose intolerance after the combination of HFD and STZ injection. Moreover, after the inclusion of CAP in the diet, the T2D + CD group consistently displayed lower blood glucose levels compared to T2D + SD mice throughout the entire duration. Furthermore, the AUC demonstrated a noteworthy reduction (∗p < 0.05), nearing the established threshold. The ITT results revealed that the T2D + SD group displayed increased levels of blood glucose in comparison to the CON + SD group at every time interval, accompanied by a significantly greater AUC than the CON + SD group (∗p < 0.05). In the model group, there was a decrease in insulin sensitivity among mice, but mice treated with dietary CAP showed improved insulin sensitivity and an increased AUC of ITT (∗p < 0.05). Three days before euthanasia, serum GLP-1, HbA1c, glucose, and insulin levels were measured in fasting mice, and homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated. It showed that CAP was associated with a decrease in all indices, except for insulin content, in type 2 diabetic mice (Figures 1C–1G). Dietary CAP improves histopathology in T2D mice

As showcased in Figure 1M, in the CON + SD group, the shape of hepatic lobules was regular, and the morphology of hepatocytes was normal. The liver cords were arranged neatly and distributed radially. In the T2D + SD group, the hepatic lobules were not obvious, and the arrangement of hepatocytes was scattered. The liver cords disappeared with the occurrence of intercellular spaces. The islets in CON + SD group were oval in shape, with neatly arranged cells and small pores, and the size of nuclei was almost identical. In the T2D + SD group, the islet morphology was damaged and deformed with vacuolization in the center, and the nuclei were different in size and free at the cell edge. In the CON + SD group, the structure of small intestinal mucosa was intact without ulceration, the villi were arranged closely and regularly, and the epithelial morphology was normal. In the T2D + SD group, the intestinal wall was obviously ulcerated, the villi were sparse and disordered, and there were different degrees of shedding, and the intestinal glands in the mucosal layer were lost. The structure of the tissues in each organ in the T2D + CD group resembled that of the CON + SD group, with minimal distinction between the CON + CD and CON + SD groups.

The presence of CAP in one’s diet enhances the composition and quantity of gut microbiota

By conducting a thorough analysis of fecal samples using 16S sequencing, we successfully assessed how the intestinal microbiota in mice suffering from T2D was influenced by the consumption of CAP diet. Operational taxonomic unit (OTU) refers to a basic taxon representing a species in phylogenetic or population genetics studies. The findings from our study indicated a rise in the OTU of the intestinal microbiota in mice subjected to HFD and STZ treatment (Figure S2). According to the Venn diagram in Figure 2A, the CON + SD, T2D + SD, T2D + CD, and CON + CD groups had 478, 535, 548, and 489 OTUs, respectively. There were 433 overlapping OTUs between the CON + SD and T2D + SD groups and 475 overlapping OTUs between the T2D + SD and T2D + CD groups. We also found that mice treated with HFD and STZ had higher OTUs than other mice. The rarefaction curve of the microbial community (Figure 2B) indicated that the number of OTU measured in samples at the same sequencing depth indirectly reflected the abundance of species.15 Additionally, the findings indicated that the CON + SD group had a reduced quantity of OTU compared to the other groups when considering the identical sequencing depth.Figure 2Improvement of gut microbiome structure in type 2 diabetic mice with dietary CAP(A) Venn diagram depicting the richness and overlap of OTU.(B) Rarefaction curve to assess sequencing depth.(C) Rank abundance curves.(D–G) α diversity (ACE index, Chao1 index, Shannon index, and Simpson index).(H) Principal-component analysis (PCA).(I and J) Unweighted UniFrac distance-based principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and (J) weighted PCoA.(K) Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA).

The rank abundance curve’s length and shape on the horizontal axis can indicate the richness and evenness of the microbial samples. Through the use of CAP, we were able to partially restore the richness and evenness of the gut microbiome in T2D mice (Figure 2C). Along with the ACE index, the Chao1 index, the Shanno index, and the Simpson index, the diversity index serves as a crucial measure of species richness and diversity in individual samples. Our observations revealed that CAP had the ability to enhance the number and evenness of gut microorganisms to some extent, as it restored all the indices (Figures 2D–2G).

The analysis of β diversity employs principal-component analysis (PCA), principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), and partial least squares-based discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) to evaluate the separation of sample projections from different groups. A shorter projection distance on the coordinate axis indicates a higher similarity in community structure within a specific dimension. Our results showed similar microbial community structures between the CON + SD and T2D + CD groups, while that of the T2D + SD group was different (Figures 2H–2J).

The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) method (Figures 3A and 3B) was used to determine the dominant microbial community and investigate whether CAP could enhance the intestinal microflora of type 2 diabetic mice. Each group’s microbiota phylum and genus were assessed for significant differences (LDA score > 3.0). The T2D + SD group had the highest number of distinct communities (up to 33). The most diverse communities included f_Prevotellaceae, p_Proteobacteria, g_Prevotellaceae_UCG_001, c_Deltaproteobacteria, f_Desulfovibrionales, O_Desulfovibrionales, and g_Alloprevotella. At the genus level, unexplored_microorganism_f_Muribaculaceae and Lactobacillus decreased, while prevotellaceae_UCG-001 and Alloprevotella increased in the T2D + SD group compared to the CON + SD and T2D + CD groups (Figure 3D). CAP in the diet regulates BA composition and amount in the digestive system, as well as the bacteria related to bile salt hydrolase

The impact of changes in the relative presence of Parabacteroides and Lactobacillus on intestinal BA metabolism in mice was investigated in this study using BA metabolomics. Only the intestinal BA content of mice from the CON + SD, T2D + SD, and T2D + CD groups was measured, as the gut microbiome of mice from the CON + CD group remained relatively unchanged compared to the CON + SD group. PCA revealed similarities in intestinal BA composition among different groups of mice. Figure 4A demonstrated that the BA composition of the CON + SD and T2D + CD groups was similar but different from that of the T2D + SD group. A heatmap was generated to display the BA content after removing BAs with a content of 0 (Figure 4B). The levels of ileum-conjugated BAs, including TCDCA, THDCA+TUDCA, TDCA, GCA, TCA, T-alpha-MCA, and T-beta-MCA, were significantly higher in T2D mice compared to regular mice (∗p < 0.05) (Figure S5). However, following CAP dietary intervention, a significant reduction in their content was observed (∗p < 0.05). Correlation analysis revealed that Blautia, Lactobacillus, and Parabacteroides were closely associated with conjugated BAs (∗p < 0.05). Since the abundance of Blautia in the mouse intestine did not significantly change, the focus shifted to the effects of Lactobacillus and Parabacteroides on BA metabolism. The heatmap results of the correlation analysis also show that Parabacteroides and Lactobacillus have the highest correlation with the changes in BAs (Figure 4C). Therefore, it is hypothesized that Parabacteroides and Lactobacillus could potentially influence the release of bile salt hydrolase (BSH), thereby regulating BA metabolism.15 Since 16S rRNA sequencing has been widely validated in the analysis of microbial community structure, the structural inference of metagenomics may be affected by genomic coverage and database biases (such as the detection of low-abundance species). Therefore, regarding the quantitative and structural changes of the microbial community, we present the results using 16S rRNA, while the metagenomic data are focused on functional annotation. Metagenomic sequencing of mouse feces was conducted to explore the interaction between the gut microbiome and BA metabolism. Hence, we hypothesized that CAP could impact intestinal gene expression through the regulation of BAs. To test this hypothesis, we conducted full-length transcriptome sequencing analysis on mouse intestinal tissues. Based on the ileal transcriptome results of the three mouse groups, there were differences in the expression of some genes between the T2D + CD group and the T2D + SD group (Figure S8). Dietary CAP attenuates systemic and local pancreatic islet inflammation

Based on the transcriptome findings, we assessed the inflammation in mice by examining the levels of serum inflammatory markers in the mice.6 The results indicated that CAP successfully reduced the abnormal increases of TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ in T2D mice (Figures 6H–6J). Additionally, qPCR analysis of pancreatic inflammatory genes demonstrated an increase in the relative expressions of Nfkb1, Ifngr1, and Stat1 in T2D mice, whereas CAP effectively decreased the expression of these pro-inflammatory genes (Figures 6K–6M). It was observed that T2D mice exhibited elevated levels of both systemic and pancreatic inflammation, while CAP dietary intervention alleviated the majority of these inflammations. Therefore, these findings imply that CAP could also ameliorate intestinal inflammation by regulating BA metabolism.

Prediction of dietary CAP in improving the intestinal pathway

To enhance understanding of the biological significance of different protein clusters in the gene expression profile of the intestines induced by CAP and the potential pathways they regulate, we performed molecular complex detection (MCODE) analysis to identify the central group of protein genes (Figure 7).17 The results revealed that the central genes in cluster 1 were associated with the cellular response to interferon-beta, regulation of B cell activation, response to endoplasmic reticulum stress, lipid production, type Ⅱ interferon signaling (IFNG), primary immunodeficiency, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, and fatty acid synthesis. Furthermore, the main gene set of cluster 2 was linked to lipid metabolism, aromatase activity, control of cellular death, fatty acid metabolism, biological oxidations, B cell activation, the PPAR signaling pathway, and cholesterol metabolism. Discussion

T2D is a metabolic disease that severely impacts human health. T2D prevalence is on the rise globally, and China has emerged as the nation with the highest patient count. T2D is frequently accompanied by a range of complications, including cardiovascular diseases, retinopathy, hypertension, cerebrovascular accidents, and nephropathy, all of which pose significant threats to human health. Notably, in certain regions of China where there is a higher consumption of chili peppers, the prevalence of diabetes appears to be comparatively lower (Figure S1).9 This phenomenon may be attributed to the hypoglycemic properties of CAP, a bioactive compound found in chili peppers.11 This study investigated how dietary CAP regulates physical and pathophysiological damage to the gut.18 Previous studies showed that dietary intervention might considerably impact the incidence of T2D.19 The study indicates that increased consumption of spicy foods may modulate the intestinal microbiota associated with BA metabolism, suggesting a potential therapeutic approach for the management of T2D. Our results showed that dietary CAP could alleviate T2D, as shown by the reduction in the AUC of OGTT and ITT, fasting glucose, serum HbA1c, and insulin resistance index in T2D mice. In addition, CAP improved pancreatic, liver, spleen, and cecum weight and organ index in T2D mice. After intervention with the CAP diet for 4 weeks, T2D mice had altered intestinal flora structures and an abundance of bacterial communities associated with BA metabolism. Moreover, the CAP diet further affected BSH secretion and regulated the type of BAs. Full-length transcriptome sequencing results and the inflammation condition of serum and pancreas showed that CAP could regulate systemic inflammation and pancreatic inflammation in mice and alleviate the associated insulin resistance. The use of bioinformatics tools predicted the possible intestinal pathways that BAs could control. The results indicated that consuming CAP in one’s diet could potentially improve, control, and possibly prevent T2D by influencing the gut microbiome and its BA metabolites.

Clinical research has demonstrated that imbalances in the gut microbiota are linked to T2D and other metabolic disorders.20 The composition of intestinal flora also plays a crucial role in the management and treatment of T2D.21 Insufficient microbial variety leads to pro-inflammatory characteristics that heighten the risk of insulin resistance.22 By utilizing complete 16S sequencing, the study investigated the intestinal microbiota in each group of mice and uncovered changes in the structure and abundance of intestinal flora in mice with T2D. In mice subjected to HFD and STZ injection, the number of OTUs in the gut microbiome increased, potentially due to an upsurge in harmful bacteria. The diversity indices (ACE, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson) indicated a decrease in the abundance and uniformity of gut microflora in mice with T2D, as well as a decline in overall diversity. Nevertheless, these impacts were partially counteracted through CAP intervention. PCA, PCoA, and PLS-DA outcomes indicated that the T2D + CD group exhibited overall resemblance to the CON + SD group, whereas the T2D + SD group diverged from the CON + SD group, demonstrating CAP’s significant capability in rectifying the composition of the intestinal microbiota. The prevailing populations in each group were identified through the implementation of LEfSe analysis. Among them, the T2D + SD group exhibited the highest number of distinct populations, with a total of 33 prevailing communities. This suggests that the combination of HFD and STZ treatment resulted in considerable alterations and harm to the gut microbiome. However, the T2D + CD group had fewer different communities, which was related to the fact that the abundance of most bacteria was restored to normal. Further, we observed that CAP could regulate most abnormal species at different levels of microorganisms (from phylum to genus). Parabacteroides and Lactobacillus are very important bacteria in the metabolic process of BAs. The genus Parabacteroides possesses a wide range of BA conversion capabilities. Its representative strains can hydrolyze conjugated BAs such as glycocholic acid and taurocholic acid through BSH and further convert them into secondary BAs, thereby regulating the host’s metabolism.23 According to Oplinter et al.,24 the production of succinate and secondary BAs by Parabacteroides has the potential to alleviate obesity and metabolic dysfunction. Lactobacillus is also an important bacterium in the BA metabolism process. It degrades BAs through BSH, reduces lipid absorption, and activates signaling pathways such as FXR and LXR through secondary BAs, thereby inhibiting lipid synthesis and promoting cholesterol transport.25 Lactobacillus is involved in secondary BA metabolism, and it was reported that increased BA excretion could lead to improved intestinal quantity in mice with elevated cholesterol.26 On the basis of the heatmap of species abundance at the top 80 genus level, we found that CAP regulated bacteria involved in BA metabolism effectively. Specifically, it could lower the relative abundance of Parabacteroides and enhance Lactobacillus to a normal level.

BAs, which are the end products of cholesterol breakdown, play a vital role in regulating cholesterol balance. Additionally, several studies have proposed that BAs can influence glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity, and energy metabolism.27 BAs have a dual function: they promote the absorption and transport of lipids in the intestines, while also acting as inflammatory factors and signaling molecules that activate cellular signaling pathways related to glucose, lipids, and energy metabolism.28 Consequently, BAs can enhance glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity, and energy metabolism by stimulating specific receptors like FXR and TGR5.29 In this investigation, targeted BA metabonomics was conducted on the intestinal contents of mice in the CON + SD, T2D + SD, and T2D + CD groups to further examine the potential impact of microbial changes on intestinal BAs. PCA analysis revealed that the structural composition of BAs in mice fed with CAP resembled that of normal mice. The heatmap demonstrated abnormally elevated levels of BAs that bind to the intestines, such as TCDCA, THDCA + TUDCA, TDCA, GCA, TCA, T-alpha-MCA, and T-beta-MCA, in T2D mice. However, these levels could be returned to normal through CAP intervention. BSH is a metabolite produced during the growth and reproduction of microorganisms. It can degrade conjugated bile salts and reduce the content of conjugated BAs. Additionally, it has a significant function in signaling pathways involving BA, where it regulates the absorption of lipids, the metabolism of glucose, and the maintenance of energy homeostasis.15 To assess the intestinal BSH activity, we conducted metagenome sequencing on mouse feces and observed a correlation between the elevated BSH content in the ileum of T2D mice and the necessity to efficiently break down a substantial buildup of conjugated BAs. However, the administration of a CAP diet appeared to restore the BSH levels toward normalcy. Under the stimulation of elevated concentrations of conjugated BAs in the ileum, the downstream FXR-FGF15 signaling pathway and genes (Fxr, Fgf15, Asbt, Ostα, and Ostβ) associated with transport functions exhibit a compensatory upregulation. CAP modulates the BSH-related microbiota, progressively restoring them to normal levels. Some studies in the early stage have explored the regulation of intestinal BAs by CAP.30,31 Researchers found that CAP increased TβMCA, thereby inhibiting the FXR signaling pathway. Nevertheless, the previous research did not adequately filter out bacteria impacting BSH from a functional standpoint, and there was a lack of comprehensive investigation into other BAs. This study addresses these gaps by providing an in-depth analysis and conducting extensive omics evaluations of BAs and the microbiota. Additionally, it investigates the alterations in gene clusters within the ileum and examines their potential mechanisms.

To assess the impact of extensively modified gut microbiota and metabolites, a comprehensive analysis of mouse ileal tissues was conducted. Our findings indicate that T2D mice have distinct gene expression profiles compared to normal mice, potentially due to microbial differences. The changes in gene expression observed in mice on the CAP diet were minimal and similar to those in normal mice. Enrichment analysis revealed numerous pathways associated with the immune system and inflammation, including the inflammatory response, chemokine and cytokine signaling pathways, neutrophil chemotaxis, type Ⅰ interferon signaling pathway, response to interferon-beta, innate immune response, and regulation of interferon-gamma production. These results suggest that the CAP diet may regulate intestinal inflammation by altering gut microbiota and degrading conjugated BAs, thereby improving T2D. Intestinal inflammation has been identified as a key characteristic of T2D pathogenesis.32 To further confirm the potential of dietary CAP to reduce the inflammatory response in mice, we measured the levels of several pro-inflammatory cytokines in the mice’s serum at the protein level. The findings demonstrated that T2D mice exhibited elevated levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ cytokines, which were subsequently significantly reduced upon implementation of the CAP diet. This suggests that incorporating CAP into the diet may effectively suppress the overall systemic inflammatory response. We also found that pancreatic inflammatory genes, Nfkb1, Stat1, and Ifngr1, were upregulated in T2D mice via qPCR analysis at the mRNA level, which might be related to pancreatic damage due to inflammation. CAP could effectively downregulate these genes. Inflammatory responses are an important factor associated with insulin resistance.33 CAP could alleviate insulin resistance by improving systemic and local inflammatory responses.

Next, we explored the core biological significance of different protein clusters through enrichment analysis to further predict the potential intestinal pathways regulated by BAs. In the core gene set of cluster 2, it was discovered that inflammation had a strong correlation with B cell activation, the PPAR signaling pathway, and cholesterol metabolism, as reported by Shang et al.34 and Del Cornò et al.35 In a report, B cells were found to release IL-6 that contributes to inflammation. Additionally, the activation of PPARβ/δ in adipocytes was observed to hinder the ERK1/2 phosphorylation pathway and prevent lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced NF-κB activation. Therefore, adipocytes produced fewer pro-inflammatory cytokines.36 These might be the underlying mechanisms by which CAP regulates inflammatory responses by altering BAs.

In summary, this study comprehensively demonstrates that dietary CAP effectively mitigates T2D by modulating gut microbiota composition and BA metabolism, which in turn reduces systemic inflammation and insulin resistance. The findings highlight CAP’s role in restoring microbial balance, enhancing BA signaling, and suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby offering a promising dietary strategy for T2D management and prevention. These insights underscore the potential of CAP diet in addressing metabolic diseases through gut-based mechanisms.

Limitations of the study

Although this research indicates that a CAP diet can effectively modulate BA levels and inflammatory levels in diabetic mice, there are several limitations. To begin with, this conclusion was drawn from experiments in mice and has not been supported in human patients, leaving a gap between this finding and its application in improving clinical conditions. Furthermore, this study made predictions regarding the potential inflammatory pathways through which CAP improves via BAs; however, it did not completely clarify the specific downstream signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms of this process. More in-depth research is necessary to gain a more comprehensive understanding of this procedure.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice, aged ten months, were acquired from Beijing Hua Fukang Bioscience Co., Ltd (located in Beijing, China). The mice were housed in a regulated setting where they experienced a 12-hour cycle of light and darkness. The temperature was consistently maintained between 20-22°C, and the humidity was kept at 45 ± 5%. The environment ensured the absence of specific pathogens. Each mouse was familiarized with the laboratory setting, being provided with unbounded availability of nourishment and hydration for at least one week. The investigation involving animals adhered to the animal experimentation regulations outlined by China Medical University, and the research protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of China Medical University (approval No. CMU2020381).

Method details

Preparation, purification, and identification of CAP

Target Molecule Corp (Targetmol, Boston, MA, USA) was the source of the CAP powder acquisition. Capsaicin, alternatively referred to as trans-8-methyl-N-vanillyl-6-nonenamide, is a colorless alkaloid with a crystalline structure and a high affinity for lipids. Its molecular formula is C18H27NO3. The preparation, purification and identification of CAP have been previously reported by Fan et al.43

Experimental design

Twelve mice were randomly divided into two groups: the control + standard diet (CON+SD, n=6) group and the control + capsaicin diet (CON+CD, n=6) group. From week 2 to week 7, the mice in the CON+SD and CON+CD groups were given a regular diet (SD), while the rest of the uncategorized mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD) obtained from Beijing Hua Fukang Bioscience Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). The composition of the HFD can be referred to in the Table S1. During the final four days of the fifth week, streptozotocin (STZ) (98%, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in a sodium citrate buffer with a pH range of 4.2-4.5. Mice that were given a high-fat diet (HFD) received an intraperitoneal injection of the STZ solution at a dose of 40 mg/kg body weight every other day. The CON+SD and CON+CD groups only received an injection of sodium citrate buffer. Before the first injection, all mice in each group fasted for 12 hours without consuming any water. Some mice died under normal conditions and were excluded. The remaining mice with fasting blood glucose levels ≥11.1mmol/L were considered T2D mice and were divided into two groups: T2D + standard diet (T2D+SD, n=6) and T2D + capsaicin diet (T2D+CD, n=6). From week 6 to 9, both T2D+CD and CON+CD groups were fed the Capsicin Diet (CD) containing 0.02% capsaicin (99.41%, Targetmol, Boston, MA, USA), while CON+SD and T2D+SD continued with the standard diet. Body weight was measured every two weeks.44 At the end of the ninth week, mice were given pentobarbital to induce anesthesia and then euthanized by cervical dislocation for blood sample, organ, and tissue collection. The organ indexes were calculated by the following formula: organ index (%) = organ weight/body weight × 100%.

Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)

The OGTT is a diagnostic procedure that assesses the amount of glucose in the body after oral ingestion. The OGTT was conducted during the 9th week. After fasting for 12 hours, the mice received an oral glucose solution (5 g/kg) via an oral gavage from Rhawn in Shanghai, China. Glucose levels in the bloodstream were assessed at 0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 minutes after glucose administration using an ACCU-CHEK Active glucose meter (Roche, Mannheim, Germany). The AUC was calculated by applying the trapezoidal rule to the measured glucose levels.

Insulin tolerance test (ITT)

Every single mouse underwent an ITT during the 9th week. To summarize, they had to abstain from eating for 12 hours and were subsequently administered insulin intraperitoneally at a dose of 0.5 IU/kg. Glucose levels were assessed at 0, 15, 30, 60, and 90 min using the ACCU-CHEK Active glucose meter (Roche, Mannheim, Germany). The trapezoidal rule was applied to calculate the AUC for glucose levels.

Histopathology

Samples of pancreatic, hepatic, and ileal tissues were excised and then preserved in a solution of 10% formaldehyde buffered with phosphate saline (pH 7.4). Following the dehydration process in consecutive ethanol and xylene solutions, the specimens were subsequently immersed in paraffin for embedding. Following the sectioning of the tissue, it underwent preparation for histopathological analysis utilizing Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) as well as Masson's Trichrome stains.

ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay)

The levels of Glucagon-Like Peptide-1(GLP-1), glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), insulin, and the cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ in the serum of mice were determined using the ELISA method. The ELISA method was performed following the instructions provided by the manufacturers (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. for HbA1c and TNF-α, ExCell Bio, China for insulin and IFN-γ, and CUSABIO for IL-6). In brief, serum enzyme molecules were combined with an antibody or antigen. Then, the enzyme-labeled antibody or antigen specifically bound to the known antigen or antibody adsorbed on the solid phase carrier. The antigen-antibody complex was separated from other substances using the solid phase carrier through multiple washings. The enzyme catalyzed the substrate, resulting in the formation of colored products, which were then measured for their optical density value. To convert cytokine concentrations, the optical density values were utilized with the assistance of the standard curve.

Insulin resistance index (homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance, HOMA-IR)

The HOMA-IR, also known as the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance, measures the index of insulin resistance. Based on previous study, the HOMA-IR calculation utilizes the equation HOMA-IR = [fasting insulin (mIU/L) × fasting blood glucose (mmol/L) divided by 22.5].45

Sequencing of the complete 16S ribosomal RNA gene

At first, the complete DNA from the mouse feces was acquired using the PowerSoil® DNA Extraction kit following the manufacturer's instructions. After extracting the sample, a primer was developed for amplifying the desired region (10 μL system, Solexa PCR) using the conservative region 27F-1492R as a basis for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the complete 16S sequence. The primers 27F-1492R, which were specifically synthesized, were designed in the following manner: The forward primer 27F has the sequence AGRGTTTGATYNTGGCTCAG, and the reverse primer 1492R has the sequence TASGGHTACCTTGTTASGACTT. Following that, the sequencing library went through a thorough examination of its quality, and the resulting high-grade circular consensus sequencing (CCS) was handled, encompassing the identification of barcodes. The optimization CCS that was produced was clustered with 97% similarity using USEARCH, version 10.0. Analyzing the arrangement of operational taxonomic unit (OTU) sequences determined the classification of the species. To analyze the species annotation, taxonomy, and diversity of gut microbiota, the Silva database and the RDP Classifier were utilized as platforms for 16S. In order to evaluate the range and assortment of species in a solitary sample, we performed an examination of α diversity and calculated the ACE, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices for each individual sample. The comparison of community composition and structure among different samples was conducted using beta diversity analysis. Ultimately, the groups were compared at the genus level through the utilization of Metastats analysis. Subsequently, biomarkers exhibiting significant statistical differences between the groups were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis, adhering to the biomarker screening criteria of an LDA score greater than 3.

Targeted bile acid metabolomics

The BA concentration of each sample was measured using UPLC-TQMS (Waters, Milford, MA), following our previously established procedure.46

Metagenome sequencing

Microorganisms’ genomic DNA samples were acquired through the extraction of complete DNA utilizing the DNeasyPowerSoil Kit (QIAGEN, Inc., Netherlands) following the manufacturer’s provided instructions. The quantity and quality of acquired DNAs were evaluated using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. The microbial DNA that was obtained underwent processing to create libraries for metagenome shotgun sequencing. The libraries had a size of 400 bp and were created with the Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Preparation Kit. Each library underwent sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq X-ten platform (Illumina, USA) using the PE150 method at Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Quality-filtered reads were obtained for further analysis after processing the raw sequencing reads. The tool Cutadapt (v1.2.1) was employed to remove sequencing adapters from the sequencing reads. The sliding-window algorithm was utilized to remove low-quality reads. To eliminate host contamination, BWA was utilized to align the sequencing reads with the host genome. After obtaining filtered reads of high quality, a metagenome was constructed for each sample by de novo assembly using IDBA-UD, which is an assembler for sequencing data with uneven depth based on the iterative De Bruijn graph method. All coding regions (CDS) of metagenomic scaffolds longer than 300 bp were predicted using MetaGeneMark. The CD-HIT tool was utilized to cluster the CDS sequences of all samples at a protein sequence similarity of 90% in order to acquire a non-repetitive gene catalog. Soap. coverage was used to estimate the gene abundance of each sample by considering the number of aligned reads. After performing a BLASTN analysis (with an e value < 0.001) to compare the non-duplicate genes with the NCBI-NT database, we acquired the classification of their shared ancestor. Similarly, the distinctive genes' functional traits were obtained by annotating them against the KEGG and EggNOG databases using the DIAMOND alignment algorithm at Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd (Beijing, China).

Full-length transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using the Trizol reagent, and a reverse transcript reaction was carried out using a reverse transcript enzyme (TOYOBO, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The real-time PCR reaction was performed using the ABI 7500 system, utilizing primer sequences obtained from the NIH qPrimerDepot. Transcriptome analysis was performed as previously described.47 To summarize, BioMarker Technologies, based in Beijing, China, handled the entire RNA used for Illumina Digital Gene Expression tag profiling. The library was built according to the instructions given by Illumina and subsequently sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencer situated in San Diego, California, United States. Filtered were the tags that were mapped to reference sequences from multiple genes. The remaining tags that were left were created to be flawless clean tags. The number of perfect clean tags for each gene was calculated and normalized in reads per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (RPKM) using a previously described method.48 In the end, only the genes associated with KEGG pathways and having RPKM values greater than 0 were selected and compared using log2 (RPKM).

Western Blot analysis

Western blot analysis was carried out as previously described. Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors.49 Lysates were centrifuged, and supernatants were collected for protein concentration determination via a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (typically 20-30 μg) were mixed with Laemmli sample buffer, denatured, and separated by SDS-PAGE on a 10-12% polyacrylamide gel. Proteins were subsequently transferred onto a PVDF membrane using a wet transfer system. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 hour at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the appropriate primary antibody diluted in blocking buffer. Following three washes with TBST, the membrane was incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. After extensive washing, protein bands were visualized directly using a fluorescence imaging system. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-FGF15 (ab229630, Abcam), antiFXR (ab28480, Abcam), and anti GAPDH(ab181602, Abcam).

Preparation of pancreatic islets and qPCR analysis

Total RNA of the pancreatic islets from mice was isolated using the TRIZOL reagent (Bioss, Beijing, China). The quantity and integrity of the RNA were assessed by measuring absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm. The RNA underwent reverse transcription using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) from Vazyme, a Nanjing-based company in China. The qRT-PCR was conducted utilizing the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Endogenous control was employed using beta-actin. The mean Ct values acquired from threefold examinations were standardized by the mean Ct values of Beta-actin. The primer sequences for this study were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and can be found in Table S2. The target genes were Fxr, Fgf15, Asbt, Ostα, Ostβ, Nfkb1, Stat1 and Ifngr1.

Bioinformatics analysis

A heatmap analysis was conducted to determine the prevalence of various proteins that were expressed differently in each group. Zhang et al. utilized the TCseq R software package to conduct protein cluster analysis, aiming to identify the biological processes and pathways linked to the expressed proteins in each group.50 The gene ontology analysis and pathways were conducted using WebGestalt (www.webgestalt.org) as described.51 To identify concentrated areas of protein-protein interaction (PPI), the researchers conducted Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) analysis.52 Images were visualized using the Rstudio and Cytoscape software.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The SPSS version 19.0 was used to conduct the statistical analysis. The information is presented as the mean value with the addition or subtraction of the standard deviation. Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed in R (Boston, MA, US) to assess potential relationships among the parameters. In the case of normally distributed data, the one-way ANOVA test was employed, with multiple comparisons adjusted using the Tukey’s method. In situations involving skewed distribution data, non-parametric testing, specifically the Kruskal-Wallis test, was used. The p-value was adjusted for multiple comparisons post the Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney test. Bar plots were used to represent the datas mean at the centre values, with error bars to indicate SEM.
