Widely targeted metabolomics reveals blue-green light-mediated regulation of bioactive compound accumulation in postharvest pepper fruits
Authors: Ruixing Zhang, Zijin Wei, Yulin Liu, Yu Shi, Yi Zhang, Abid Khan, Qianqian He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPepper (Capsicum annuum L.) fruits are rich in bioactive compounds essential for nutritional quality. This study evaluated the effects of combined blue-green light on postharvest metabolite accumulation, using dark treatment as a control. After 36 h exposure, widely targeted metabolomics identified 1270 metabolites, with flavonoids, phenolic acids, and lipids being the major classes. KEGG analysis showed significant enrichment in flavonoid biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and linoleic acid metabolism under combined light. Compared with the control, combined light significantly markedly increased carotenoids, total flavonoids, and total phenolics by 12.76%, 262.50%, and 99.19%, respectively, and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities by 24.61%, 31.67%, and 268.16%. Soluble sugar, protein, and vitamin C contents also increased by 68.48%, 31.48%, and 37.77%, respectively. Notably, combined light induced more upregulated differential metabolites than single blue or green light, indicating a synergistic effect. Overall, blue–green light enhances bioactive compound accumulation and antioxidant capacity via coordinated metabolic regulation in pepper fruits.Keywords: Pepper, Widely targeted metabolomics, Blue-green light, Flavonoids, Functional lipids, Antioxidant capacity

Introduction

Pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) is an economically important horticultural crop widely valued for its rich nutritional and functional properties. Pepper fruits are abundant in bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, carotenoids, and vitamins (Mendes & Gonçalves, 2020), which contribute to antioxidant capacity and provide significant health benefits (Imran et al., 2019; Shen et al., 2022). These compounds play essential roles in reducing oxidative stress and are closely associated with the nutritional quality and functional value of pepper fruits.

The accumulation of these bioactive compounds is closely linked to fruit pigmentation and developmental processes. Fruit color in pepper is primarily determined by the coordinated accumulation of chlorophylls, carotenoids, and flavonoids (Lightbourn et al., 2008), which not only define visual quality but also reflect underlying metabolic activities. Among these, flavonoids and phenolic compounds are key secondary metabolites involved in plant defense, stress response, and antioxidant activity, and their biosynthesis is tightly regulated by both developmental cues and environmental factors (Kivimäenpä et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2025).

Light quality is one of the most critical environmental factors influencing plant metabolism (Wu et al., 2025), particularly in postharvest conditions. Previous studies have shown that specific light wavelengths, especially blue light, can enhance the accumulation of phenolic compounds, carotenoids, and vitamin C, thereby improving antioxidant capacity in horticultural crops (Darko et al., 2022; Fayezizadeh et al., 2024; Taulavuori et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025). Moreover, combined light treatments have been reported to exert synergistic effects on secondary metabolism in some plant systems, suggesting that multiple photoreceptors and signaling pathways may be co-activated (Ahmadi et al., 2022; Bungala et al., 2024; Carranza-Ramírez et al., 2025; Palma et al., 2022; Ptak et al., 2024). However, most existing studies have focused on single light wavelengths, and the regulatory effects of combined blue–green light on metabolite accumulation remain insufficiently understood, particularly at the metabolome-wide level.

At the molecular level, light signals are perceived by photoreceptors such as cryptochromes and phototropins, which regulate downstream transcriptional networks (Jing & Lin, 2020). The transcription factor ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5) is a central regulator in light signaling and plays a key role in controlling phenylpropanoid (Liu et al., 2023) and flavonoid biosynthesis (Du et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2022). Although previous studies have suggested that HY5 is involved in light-mediated metabolic regulation, its role in postharvest pepper fruits under combined light conditions has not been systematically investigated. Therefore, a clear knowledge gap remains regarding how combined blue–green light regulates the accumulation of multiple classes of bioactive compounds in pepper fruits and whether such regulation involves coordinated metabolic and transcriptional responses. In particular, it is unclear whether combined light induces a synergistic effect on metabolic flux toward secondary metabolism and how this relates to light signaling pathways.

Based on these considerations, we hypothesize that combined blue–green light enhances the accumulation of bioactive compounds in pepper fruits by promoting coordinated metabolic reprogramming and activating light-responsive regulatory networks. To test this hypothesis, widely targeted metabolomics was employed to systematically characterize metabolic changes under different light treatments, combined with physiological and biochemical analyses to evaluate antioxidant capacity and nutritional quality. This study aims to elucidate the effects of combined blue–green light on the accumulation of bioactive compounds in pepper fruits, identify the key metabolic pathways involved, and further explore the potential regulatory mechanisms of light-induced metabolic changes.

Materials and methods

Preparation of plant materials and treatment of different light qualities

In this study, the high-generation inbred line ‘GS4’ of pepper was selected (Fig. S1). The pepper seeds of cultivar ‘GS4’ were provided by the Pepper Research Group of Northwest A&F University, Yangling, China. This variety has a light green color during the green ripening period, an orange-red color during the color change period, and a bright red color during the full ripening period. Seeds were soaked in a 55 °C water bath for 15 min and then sown in 72-cell plug trays. Seedlings were cultured in an artificial climate chamber under conditions of 20–25 °C, 60% relative humidity, and a 12 h photoperiod. At the eight-leaf stage, they were transplanted into 20 cm × 25 cm plots at the garden station of Shanxi Agricultural University (37.42° N, 112.55° E). The peppers should be picked 40 days after flowering during the color change period. For each treatment, 12 disease-free peppers with uniform growth were selected. First, perform a 2-h dark processing. Then it was transferred to a blue (460 nm), green (520 nm) (Zhang, He, et al., 2024), or combined blue-green LED light at a total intensity of 150 μmol m−2 s−1 for 36 h. For the combined treatment, the 1:1 ratio was achieved by providing equal photon flux density from blue (460 nm) and green (520 nm) LEDs, resulting in a total PPFD of 150 μmol m−2 s−1. The single blue and single green treatments were applied at the same total intensity (150 μmol m−2 s−1) and duration (36 h) to ensure comparability across treatments.

The 36-h exposure duration was selected based on preliminary experiments showing that physiological indicators and pigment accumulation reached a peak at 36 h without inducing visible dehydration or stress damage, whereas longer exposures (48–72 h) did not further enhance metabolite accumulation and occasionally resulted in slight moisture loss. Samples were taken from the upper surface directly exposed to the light source. This sampling strategy was adopted to minimize variability caused by potential light attenuation within the fruit curvature and to ensure consistent exposure conditions among replicates. Preliminary tests confirmed that the light intensity distribution on the exposed surface was uniform within ±5%. The seeds and placenta were removed, and the pulp was promptly snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C, which was used for the determination of physiological and biochemical parameters and the analysis of an extensive set of targeted metabolites. Each treatment consists of 3 biological replicates, and each metabolomic sample is composed of 12 fruit mix samples.

Determination of physiological indicators

All physiological and biochemical parameters were measured using three technical replicates for each sample. Results are expressed on a fresh weight (FW) basis unless otherwise stated. Total carotenoid content was determined according to Gao (2006) and expressed as mg g−1 FW.Total flavonoid content was measured using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method and expressed as mg rutin equivalents (RE) per g FW. Total phenolic content was determined using a modified Folin–Ciocalteu method and expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per g FW (Zhang, Cheng, et al., 2024). Antioxidant enzyme activities include superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) activity were assayed following established protocols with slight modifications. Enzyme extracts were prepared using chilled extraction buffer and centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used for analysis. (Yuan et al., 2010). SOD activity was determined based on its ability to inhibit the photochemical reduction of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT). One unit (U) of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to cause 50% inhibition of NBT reduction under assay conditions, and the activity was expressed as U g−1 FW (Beauchamp & Fridovich, 1971). POD activity was measured using the guaiacol oxidation method by monitoring the increase in absorbance at 470 nm due to the formation of tetraguaiacol. One unit (U) of POD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme causing an increase of 0.01 in absorbance per minute, and the activity was expressed as U g−1 FW. CAT activity was determined by measuring the decomposition rate of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) at 240 nm. One unit (U) of CAT activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to decompose 1 μmol of H₂O₂ per minute under assay conditions, and the activity was expressed as U g−1 FW (Sun et al., 2021). Soluble sugar content was determined using the anthrone‑sulfuric acidmethod and expressed as mg g−1 FW (Tang et al., 2021). Soluble protein content was measured using the Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 method and expressed as mg g−1 FW. Vitamin C content was determined by titration with a 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol solution and expressed as mg 100 g−1 FW. (Gao et al., 2018). All absorbance measurements were performed using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer, and calibration curves were established using appropriate standards.

Sample preparation and extraction

The pepper samples were subjected to vacuum freeze-drying in a freeze dryer (Scientz 100F), followed by grinding into powder using a grinder (MM 400, Retsch) at 30 Hz for a duration of 1.5 min. Pepper sample powder (50 mg) was precisely weighed with an electronic balance (MS105DM) and 1200 μL of 70% methanol aqueous internal standard extract that had been pre-cooled at −20 °C was added. The sample was vortexed for 30 s every 30 min, completing 6 cycles in total. Subsequent to centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was drawn, filtered via a 0.22 μm microporous membrane, and stored in a sample vial for UPLC-MS/MS detection. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by mixing equal volumes of all sample extracts and were injected at regular intervals (every 10 runs) during UPLC-MS/MS analysis to monitor instrument stability and reproducibility. Each sample was prepared with three technical replicates to ensure extraction reproducibility.

UPLC-MS/MS analysis and quality control (QC)

The sample extracts were analyzed using an UPLC system(ExionLC™ AD, SCIEX, USA) coupled with a QTRAP® 6500+ mass spectrometer (SCIEX, USA). Chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent SB-C18 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). The mobile phase consists of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in ultrapure water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The gradient elution progra was set as follows: 95% A and 5% B at 0 min; a linear gradient to 5% A and 95% B within 9 min; maintained at 5% A for 1 min; then returned to 95% Awithin 1.1 min and equilibrated for 2.9 min. The flow rate was 0.35 mL min−1,the column temperature was40°C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. For mass spectrometry, an electrospray ionization (ESI) source was operated in both positive and negative ion modes. The ion spray voltage (IS) was set at 5500 V (positive mode) and − 4500 V ((negative mode), respectively. The source temperature was 500 °C. The curtain gas (CUR), gas I (GSI), and gas II (GSII) and were set at 25 psi, 50 psi, and 60 psi, respectively. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode was used for quantitative analysis in triple quadrupole (QQQ) scanning. The collision gas (nitrogen) was set to medium, and declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) were optimized for each MRM transition. Metabolite-specific ion pairs were monitored according to their retention time windows to ensure accurate detection (Fig. S2). To ensure data quality and analytical reproducibility, quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots of all experimental samples. The QC samples were used to evaluate instrument stability and system repeatability throughout the analytical run. During UPLC–MS/MS analysis, QC samples were injected at regular intervals (every 10 injections) to monitor signal stability, retention time consistency, and peak intensity variation. The high overlap of total ion chromatograms (TIC) among QC samples confirmed the robustness and reliability of the analytical system (Fig. S2). All samples were analyzed in randomized order to minimize systematic analytical bias.

PCA analysis

Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using the prcomp statistical function in R software (www.r-project.org), with the data subjected to unit variance scaling prior to analysis. All samples included three biological replicates.

Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and pearson correlation coefficients (PCC)

The results of HCA for both samples and metabolites are displayed in the form of heatmaps accompanied by dendrograms, whereas PCC among samples is computed via the cor function in R and presented exclusively as heatmaps. Both HCA and PCC analyses were performed with the R package ComplexHeatmap. For HCA, the metabolite signal strengths subjected to unit variance scaling were presented in the heatmap. PCC analysis was also used to explore the correlation between physiological indicators and key metabolite contents, with significance set at P < 0.05.

Differential metabolites selected

Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were screened based on pairwise comparisons (B vs D, G vs D, BG vs D), with clear, unified statistical thresholds and supplementary 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of log₂ fold change (log₂FC) to assess the reliability of metabolite abundance variations.

Metabolites were identified as DAMs only when satisfying all three screening criteria simultaneously: (1) variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 extracted from the OPLS-DA model; (2) absolute log₂FC ≥ 1.0; (3) adjusted false discovery rate (FDR, q-value) < 0.05. Metabolites whose 95% CIs of log₂FC crossed zero were further excluded to eliminate unreliable differential signals.

Prior to OPLS-DA modeling, all metabolite signal intensities were log₂-transformed and mean-centered to remove dimensional disparities. A total of 200 permutation tests were performed to evaluate model overfitting. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied for FDR correction to control false positive rates, and q-values <0.05 were regarded as statistically significant. OPLS-DA modeling, permutation testing, and calculation of log₂FC 95% CIs were implemented using the R package MetaboAnalystR.

KEGG annotation and enrichment analysis

Identification of the metabolites of compounds using KEGG database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/) for comments, and then map the comments after the metabolites to KEGG pathways database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html). Enrichment significance was determined by P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.05, and the top enriched pathways were used to illustrate the metabolic regulation of combined blue-green light on pepper fruits.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc test (P < 0.05) to identify significant differences between treatments. For metabolomics data, FDR correction (Benjamini-Hochberg method) was applied with q < 0.05 as the significance threshold.

Result

Impacts of blue-green light combined treatment on pepper fruit phenotypes and physiological parameters

As shown in Fig. 1, with the increase of light exposure time, the color of GS4 pepper fruits gradually deepens. Moreover, with darkness as the contrast, under the combined blue and green light treatment, the color change of the fruits is the most obvious. At 36 h of light exposure time, the fruit color changes from orange-red to bright red.

By detecting the levels of carotenoids, total flavonoids and total phenolic contents at different time points under combined blue-green light irradiation, we found that their levels continuously increased from 0 to 36 h, remained stable from 48 to 72 h, and reached the peak at 36 h (Fig. 2A–C). Specifically, compared with the initial level at 0 h, carotenoid, total flavonoid, and total phenolic contents increased by approximately 12.76%, 262.50%, and 99.19% at 36 h, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2D-F, compared with darkness, combined blue-green light rapidly increased the activities of antioxidant enzymes including SOD, POD, and CAT. Compared with the dark control at the same time point, SOD, POD, and CAT activities in the combined light group were elevated by 24.61%, 31.67%, and 268.16% at 36 h, respectively.

Compared with the dark control, soluble sugar, soluble protein and vitamin C contents were significantly elevated in all light treatments at 36 h (P < 0.05). Specifically, these three nutritional indicators increased by 68.48%, 31.48% and 37.77% under combined blue-green light, by 54.08%, 11.52% and 24.77% under single blue light, and by 28.94%, 6.93% and 4.68% under single green light, respectively. Collectively, combined light irradiation exerted the strongest promoting effect, which revealed that combined light stimulation facilitates carbon assimilation and boosts the biosynthesis of nutritional compounds in pepper fruit.(Fig. 2G-I).

Quality control of metabolomic data

The total ion chromatography (TIC) curves of the samples have a relatively high overlap rate (Fig. S2A and B), indicating good stability and repeatability of the UPLC-MS/MS detection system. Multimodal detection images of metabolites obtained in the MRM mode (Fig. S2C and D). In these graphs, each mass spectrometry peak of a different color represents the metabolite detected in the sample. In each treatment group and QC sample, the correlation among the three biological replicates was very high (Fig. S3), confirming the accuracy and reliability of the data.

Metabolic analysis after combined blue-green light treatment

Extensive targeted metabolome analysis of GS4 pepper pulp after 36 h of combined blue-green light treatment was performed via UPLC-MS/MS technology. A sum of 1270 metabolic compounds were detected (Fig. 3), including flavonoids (323, 25.43%), phenolic acids (172, 13.54%), lipids (158, 12.44%), alkaloids (148, 11.65%), amino acids and derivatives (141, 11.1%), organic acids (60, 4.72%), nucleotides and their derivatives (57, 4.49%), lignans and lignans and coumarins (41, 3.23%), terpenoids (21, 1.65%), quinones (8, 0.63%), tannins (21, 0.47%), and others (135, 10.63%) (Fig. 3A).

Multivariate statistical analysis confirmed distinct metabolic differences between the four groups. As reflected in the principal component analysis (PCA) score plot, clear group separations were observed. Specifically, the first principal component (PC1, 27.11%) and the second principal component (PC2, 18.36%) jointly contributed to 45.47% of the total variance, indicating good discriminative power of the PCA model (Fig. 3B). The QC samples were grouped in the central area of the PCA graph, indicating that the credibility of this group of data is high. There were significant differences in the PC1 level among each light-treated groups, and the blue-green light irradiation was at a high level. HCA heatmap showed that combined blue-green light treatment samples were clearly clustered and separated from single light and dark treatment samples (Fig. 3C).

Analysis of differential metabolites after combined blue-green light treatment

A total of 39 common DAMs were identified in the three groups of B_vs_D, G_vs_D, and BG_vs_D (Fig. 4A). Among them, BG_vs_D had the most upregulated DAMs, 25 more than B_vs_D and 98 more than G_vs_D, which was remarkably higher than the other two groups. It is demonstrated that blue-green light has the best effect on promoting the color change of pepper and the accumulation of nutrients, followed by B_vs_D. indicating reduced color change and delayed ripening under green light (Fig. 4B).

(A) Venn diagrams of the overlapping and uniquely upregulated DAMs of B_vs_D, G_vs_D, and BG_vs_D; (B) The accumulation of differences in metabolites among the three groups; (C–E) OPLS-DA score plots of B_vs_D, G_vs_D and BG_vs_D.

To more effectively identify DAMs, we employed the OPLS-DA model for the visualization of metabolic data from the tested samples. Fig. 4C–D shows that the control groups yield high scores, indicating that, with the exception of the green-light treatment, the other two light treatments significantly altered color-related metabolism in the pepper fruits. To further evaluate whether the combined blue-green light treatment exerts synergistic effects beyond the additive effects of individual wavelengths, a two-way ANOVA was performed considering blue light, green light, and their interaction. The results showed that several physiological parameters exhibited significant interaction effects between blue and green light (P < 0.05), including total flavonoid content, CAT activity, soluble sugar content, and soluble protein content (Table S10), indicating a synergistic regulation under combined treatment. In contrast, other parameters such as carotenoid content, total phenolic content, SOD activity, POD activity, and vitamin C content showed no significant interaction effects, suggesting predominantly additive or independent responses. Similarly, for representative metabolites, multiple flavonoids, phenolic acids, and lipids displayed significant interaction effects (P < 0.05) (Table S11), including hispidulin, naringenin chalcone, pinobanksin, vitexin-7-O-glucoside, and several lipid-related metabolites. These results demonstrate that the combined blue-green light treatment exerts a metabolite-specific synergistic effect, particularly in the regulation of flavonoid and lipid biosynthesis. Further quantitative verification of differential metabolites confirmed a significant interactive effect of blue and green light (P < 0.05). Compared with the dark control, the combined blue-green light treatment yielded 143 upregulated differential metabolites, which was markedly higher than the number induced by single blue light (118) and single green light (45) (Fig. S4), further verifying the synergistic combination effect. Overall, these findings provide statistical evidence that the combined blue-green light treatment induces both synergistic and additive effects, depending on the specific metabolite or physiological trait.

Flavonoids

Fig. 6 shows the metabolic pathway diagram of flavonoid biosynthesis, along with the heat maps of each differential metabolite. Compared with the dark control treatment, a sum of 322 flavonoids were identified under the blue-green light combined treatment, among which 64 flavonoids were upregulated and 12 were downregulated (Table. S1). The mean fold change of the upregulated flavonoids was 5.96, ranging from 2.00 to 41.98, whereas the downregulated flavonoids showed a mean fold change of 0.33, ranging from 0.07 to 0.49. Although several individual flavonoids exhibited relatively lower fold change values, blue-green light treatment triggered widespread upregulation of flavonoid compounds. A total of 321 flavonoids were identified during blue light treatment, among which 50 flavonoids were upregulated and 4 were downregulated (Table. S2). The mean fold change of the upregulated flavonoids was 10.40 (range: 2.01–49.54), while that of the downregulated flavonoids was 0.35 (range: 0.09–0.49). Blue light induced higher mean fold change of upregulated flavonoids, but the number of upregulated metabolites was obviously less than that of combined light treatment. In green light treatment, 316 flavonoids were identified. Among the 9 flavonoid compounds such as naringenin chalcone, there was an expression upregulation observed, while 14 flavonoids were downregulated (Table. S3). The upregulated flavonoids exhibited a mean fold change of 6.07 (range: 2.41–10.61), whereas the downregulated flavonoids showed a mean fold change of 0.26 (range: 0.10–0.49). These results indicate that the combined blue-green light treatment significantly enhanced flavonoid accumulation in pepper fruits. Despite the slightly lower mean fold change of upregulated metabolites, combined light treatment activated more flavonoid members and exerted a broader regulatory effect. Overall, it possessed the largest number of upregulated flavonoids and showed superior comprehensive regulation efficiency compared with single light treatments (95% CI, P < 0.05).

Phenolic acids

Fig. 7 shows the biosynthetic pathway for phenolic acids together with heatmaps for the differential phenolic acids. Compared with the dark control treatment, a sum of 171 phenolic acid compounds were identified under the blue-green light combined treatment. Among them, 17 phenolic acid compounds such as 4-caffeoylshikimic acid*, 3-O-caffeoylshikimic acid* and sinapic acid were significantly upregulated. Only 4 phenolic acid compounds were significantly downregulated: 1-O-feruloylquinic acid, methyl cinnamate, 2-caffeoyl-6-(4-hydroxycinnamoyl) glucoside, and 6’-O-feruloyl-D-sucrose (Table. S4). The mean fold change of the upregulated phenolic acids was 6.69, ranging from 2.17 to 19.22, whereas the downregulated phenolic acids showed a mean fold change of 0.36, ranging from 0.12 to 0.49. During the blue light treatment, 171 phenolic acid compounds were also identified, with 25 showing significant upregulation and 7 exhibiting significant downregulation. (Table. S5). The mean fold change of the upregulated phenolic acids reached 7.15 (range: 2.02–20.59), while that of the downregulated phenolic acids was 0.29 (range: 0.02–0.49). Although single blue light induced a higher average upregulation amplitude for individual phenolic acids, it also caused more downregulated metabolites compared with the combined light treatment. Under the green light treatment, 170 kinds of phenolic acid compounds were identified, among which only 6 phenolic acids were significantly induced. Downregulated phenolic acids were about three times as many as upregulated ones, with no significant differences among the remaining compounds (Table. S6). The upregulated phenolic acids exhibited a mean fold change of 7.26 (range: 2.11–19.87), whereas the downregulated phenolic acids showed a mean fold change of 0.23 (range: 0.03–0.48). Collectively, these results indicate that the combined blue-green light treatment exerted the most favorable regulatory effect on phenolic acid accumulation in pepper fruits. It achieved a balanced regulation with the least number of downregulated metabolites, and showed superior comprehensive promotion effect on phenolic acid biosynthesis compared with single light treatments (95% CI, P < 0.05).

Correlation analysis

The correlation analysis (Fig. 9A–C) revealed distinct associations between differential metabolites and antioxidant-related indices. In Fig. 9A, naringenin chalcone exhibited strong positive correlations with carotenoid content, total phenolic content, and POD activity (r ≥ 0.95, P < 0.05), while moderate correlations were observed with SOD and CAT activities. Pinobanksin showed a similar trend, particularly displaying a significant positive association with vitamin C content and CAT activity, suggesting its potential contribution to the non-enzymatic antioxidant system. In Fig. 9B, 4-p-coumaroyl-rhamnosyl-(1 → 6)-d-glucose was strongly correlated with CAT activity and vitamin C content (r ≥ 0.95, P < 0.05), whereas phthalic acid exhibited weaker or non-significant relationships with most antioxidant parameters, indicating a limited role in antioxidant capacity. In Fig. 9C, lipid-derived metabolites such as 1-oleoyl-sn-glycerol and LysoPC 20:2 (2n isomer) were highly positively correlated with soluble sugar, soluble protein, and vitamin C contents (r ≥ 0.95, P < 0.05), but negatively or weakly associated with SOD and POD activities. Collectively, these results suggest that flavonoid-related compounds are primarily associated with enzymatic antioxidant activities, whereas lipid metabolites are more closely linked to soluble nutrient accumulation, highlighting their differential contributions to fruit quality and antioxidant properties.

Expression analysis of CaHY5 under different light treatments

The relative expression level of the light-responsive transcription factor CaHY5 was significantly influenced by different light treatments (Fig. S5). Compared with the dark control (D), neither single blue light (B) nor green light (G) significantly altered CaHY5 expression. In contrast, the combined blue-green light (BG) treatment markedly upregulated CaHY5 expression, showing the highest transcript level among all treatments, which was significantly higher than those in the D, B, and G groups (P < 0.05). Specifically, CaHY5 expression under BG treatment increased by approximately 25% compared with the dark control, indicating a strong synergistic effect of combined blue and green light on the activation of CaHY5. Neither monochromatic blue nor green light alone was sufficient to significantly induce CaHY5 expression. These results suggest that the combined light treatment more effectively triggers light signal transduction pathways associated with transcriptional regulation of CaHY5.

Discussion

Combined blue-green light treatment promotes antioxidant enzyme activities in pepper fruits

The combined blue-green light treatment significantly enhanced fruit coloration (Fig. 1) and promoted the activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD and CAT), thereby alleviating oxidative stress and delaying postharvest senescence in pepper fruits (Fig. 2D-F). These physiological responses are consistent with previous reports in horticultural crops where blue light enhances ROS-scavenging capacity and improves postharvest quality (Lu et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026; Zhang, He, et al., 2024).

At the mechanistic level, these responses are likely initiated through blue-light photoreceptors, primarily cryptochromes (CRY1/CRY2) and phototropins (PHOT1/PHOT2), which perceive specific wavelengths and trigger downstream signaling cascades (Paik & Huq, 2019). Upon activation, these photoreceptors suppress the activity of the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex COP1/SPA, thereby stabilizing the central transcription factor HY5 (Podolec & Ulm, 2018). HY5 is a key regulator of light-responsive genes involved in antioxidant metabolism (Zhu et al., 2025) and secondary metabolite biosynthesis (Jin et al., 2025). Consistently, our results showed that CaHY5 expression was significantly upregulated under combined blue–green light, but not under monochromatic light treatments (Fig. S5), suggesting a synergistic activation of light signaling pathways. Importantly, green light may further modulate blue-light signaling by balancing the abundance and activity of blue-light photoreceptors. For instance, green light downregulates the expression of CRY2/3 and PHOT2 to reshape blue light signal transduction (Zheng et al., 2019) and counteracts blue light-induced transcriptional suppression of the HvNCED gene (Hoang et al., 2014), thereby rewiring downstream secondary metabolic responses. Additionally, green light may function through complementary phytochrome-associated signaling pathways to synergistically or antagonistically regulate blue light signaling crosstalk, although the detailed upstream regulatory mechanisms require further validation. Therefore, the observed enhancement of antioxidant enzyme activity may be mediated through a photoreceptor–COP1/SPA–HY5 regulatory module, which coordinates oxidative stress responses. Overall, these results suggest that combined light enhances antioxidant capacity through coordinated signaling rather than single-wavelength dominance; however, the regulatory hierarchy remains partially inferred and requires further molecular validation.

The combined blue-green light promotes flavonoids and phenolic acids accumulation via putative metabolic flux reprogramming in pepper fruits

The color transition and quality improvement of pepper fruits are closely associated with the accumulation of flavonoids and phenolic acids. In this study, combined blue–green light significantly increased the abundance of multiple flavonoids (e.g., naringin, hispidulin, quercetin derivatives) and phenolic acids (e.g., ferulic acid, sinapic acid), suggesting a broad activation of phenylpropanoid metabolism (Fig. 6, Fig. 7). Beyond pathway enrichment, the sequential accumulation of upstream and downstream intermediates (e.g., naringenin chalcone, naringenin, glycosylated flavonoids) suggests a putative redistribution of metabolic flux toward flavonoid biosynthesis rather than mere accumulation of isolated metabolites. Similarly, coordinated increases in phenylpropanoid intermediates imply enhanced flux through phenolic acid branches. At the photoreceptor level, cryptochromes are the primary blue-light receptors regulating phenylpropanoid metabolism, while green light may modulate photomorphogenic signaling by altering excitation balance or interacting with additional light-sensing systems, thereby amplifying metabolic responses under combined irradiation.

Although direct metabolic flux measurements were not performed in this study, the coordinated upregulation of metabolites at consecutive nodes of the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway-naringenin chalcone, naringenin, and downstream glycosylated flavonoids-under blue-green light suggests an increased metabolic flux through this branch. This pattern is consistent with enhanced activity of key enzymes such as chalcone synthase (CHS) and chalcone isomerase (CHI), which are known to be light-regulated (Gangappa & Botto, 2016). Isotopic labeling experiments would be required to directly quantify metabolic flux changes, representing an important direction for future research (Koley et al., 2024).

Combined blue-green light treatment enhances lipid metabolism through coordinated signaling and stress modulation in pepper fruits

Combined blue–green light also significantly increased the accumulation of lipid-related metabolites, including sphingolipids and hydroperoxide fatty acid derivatives (Fig. 8), These metabolites are closely associated with membrane stability, signaling regulation, and stress adaptation (Huby et al., 2020). Photoreceptor-mediated signaling may also contribute to lipid metabolic regulation. Blue light perception via cryptochromes and phototropins can influence membrane-associated processes (Lin, 2000), while downstream HY5-dependent transcriptional regulation may indirectly modulate genes involved in lipid remodeling and ROS balance (Li et al., 2024). The observed increase in phytosphingosine suggests activation of lipid-mediated signaling pathways potentially linked to stress tolerance and membrane homeostasis. Moreover, the positive correlations observed between lipid metabolites, soluble nutrients, and antioxidant enzyme activities suggest that lipid metabolism is integrated into a broader stress-response network. This coordination may reflect a system-level metabolic reprogramming rather than isolated pathway activation. Similar to flavonoid metabolism, the observed lipid accumulation patterns suggest a possible shift in metabolic allocation toward membrane-associated lipid remodeling pathways, but without direct enzymatic or transcriptomic validation, this interpretation remains inferential.

Differential effects of green light and its potential regulatory mechanisms

Green light alone exhibited a relatively weak or even inhibitory effect on the accumulation of secondary metabolites in pepper fruits compared with blue or combined blue-green light treatments. To better interpret this phenomenon, several possible explanations are proposed. First, green light may activate distinct photoreceptors or signaling components, including cryptochromes under specific conditions or yet unidentified green-light-responsive receptors, thereby triggering signaling cascades different from those induced by blue light. Cryptochromes are well known blue/UV-A receptors, but accumulating evidence suggests that plant photoreceptors can exhibit broader spectral responsiveness under complex light environments (Ahmad & Cashmore, 1996; Chen et al., 2004). Second, although green light can penetrate deeper into plant tissues, its lower photon energy compared with blue light may result in weaker photomorphogenic responses, thereby limiting the activation of downstream metabolic pathways. It has been reported that blue light is more efficient in triggering photomorphogenesis and secondary metabolism than green light due to higher energy absorption and stronger activation of signaling cascades (Materová et al., 2017). Third, previous studies in photobiology have reported the so-called “green light effect” or “green light reversal phenomenon,” in which green light can antagonize or reverse blue-light-mediated physiological responses. This antagonistic interaction has been observed in stomatal regulation and phototropin-mediated signaling, suggesting that green light may act as a modulatory signal in light signaling networks (Frechilla et al., 2000; Wang & Folta, 2013). Finally, the weak effect of green light alone may also be attributed to insufficient activation of flavonoid biosynthetic genes, which are predominantly regulated by the CRY–HY5 signaling module under blue light or UV-A perception. HY5 is a central transcription factor linking light perception to phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis (Jakhar et al., 2026). Collectively, these findings suggest that green light may function more as a modulatory or fine-tuning signal rather than a primary driver of secondary metabolite biosynthesis in pepper fruits.

Limitations and future perspectives

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the absence of a white or broad-spectrum light control limits the ability to distinguish wavelength-specific effects from general light responses. Second, the relatively small sample size (12 fruits per treatment, three biological replicates) may reduced statistical power and dose not fully capture within-group variability. Third, the 36 h treatment was optimized under laboratory conditions, but its applicability to industrial postharvest systems with longer storage durations and variable environments remains uncertain. Fourth, metabolomic profiling provides only static snapshots and dose not refiect dynamic metabolic fluxes, and the proposed mechanism are not supported by transcriptomic, proteomic, or enzymatic validation. Finally,functional validation experiments, such as gene manipulation or enzyme activity assays, were not performed to confirm the regulatory pathways. Future studies integrating multi-omics approaches, isotopic flux analysis, and functional validation, together with broader light controls and larger experimental scales, are needed to further elucidate the mechanisms and improve the practical relevance of blue–green light regulation in postharvest pepper quality.

Conclusion

As an emerging non-invasive postharvest light treatment, combined blue–green light exposure effectively enhanced color development and improved the nutritional quality of pepper fruits. It increased key quality-related components, including soluble sugars, soluble proteins, and vitamin C, and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD and POD), indicating an improved antioxidant capacity. Metabolomic analysis revealed that combined light treatment was associated with increased accumulation of multiple bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, and selected lipid metabolites. These changes suggest that blue–green light may contribute to coordinated regulation of secondary metabolism and nutritional quality in postharvest pepper fruits. Overall, this study provides metabolomic and physiological evidence that combined blue–green light is associated with improved postharvest quality and altered bioactive compound profiles in pepper fruits. However, the underlying regulatory mechanisms require further validation through molecular and functional studies.
