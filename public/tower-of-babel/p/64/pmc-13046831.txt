A systematic review on mindfulness-based immersive interventions in depressive disorders
Authors: Peng Tan, Yuqi Wu, Xinrong Chen, Junfang Chen, Zhixing Guo, Xiaobing Lu, Jie Chen, Hongtao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDepressive disorders are characterized by persistent negative affect, rumination, and impaired emotion regulation, contributing substantially to global disease burden. As recurrent rumination and negative attentional biases are central cognitive mechanisms of depression, interventions targeting these processes have gained increasing attention. Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) effectively treat depressive disorders but face adherence challenges, which immersive technology may resolve through multi-sensory stimulation and real-time feedback. This systematic review assesses the therapeutic potential of mindfulness-based immersive interventions (MBIIs, which combine immersive technologies with MBIs) for depressive disorders. Following PRISMA guidelines, we identified 37 eligible studies from 670 screened records. Across the included studies, a proportion of studies (69%) reported greater improvements in depressive and anxiety-related symptoms in MBII conditions compared with conventional therapies, while 78% of the trials reported improvements in psychological processes. A subset of trials reported greater feasibility and acceptance for immersive implementations compared with non-immersive approaches (67%), and 89% reported improvements in physiological outcomes. However, significant gaps remain, including substantial variability in sessions and VR configurations, insufficient active controls, and limited long-term validation. Future research is recommended to prioritize mechanistic studies to clarify neurocognitive pathways, standardize sessions, and evaluate cost-effectiveness.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Depression continues to be a major public health issue worldwide, affecting individual well-being, economic productivity, and suicide prevention efforts1–4. Psychotherapy, especially cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), is effective for treating depression; however, substantial limitations remain with respect to long-term outcomes and treatment adherence5. Mindfulness is the awareness that arises from paying attention in a particular way to the unfolding of experience6. The definition of mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) is a structured psychological intervention method combined with mindfulness practice, aiming to promote mental health and emotional well-being by cultivating individuals’ non-judgmental awareness of the present moment7–9. In recent years, MBIs have emerged as a significant non-pharmacological intervention in the treatment of depression. For example, MBIs have been shown to reduce depressive symptoms10, improve emotion regulation11, and lower the risk of relapse12. A meta-analysis of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) reported robust effects for relapse prevention, whereas evidence for its efficacy in alleviating current depressive symptoms remains inconclusive, underscoring the need for further investigation of long-term outcomes and comparative effectiveness across therapeutic approaches13. In general, MBIs aim to cultivate mindfulness skills through structured sessions, focusing on promoting awareness, attention control, and emotional regulation12. The primary therapeutic targets of MBIs include reducing rumination, enhancing self-compassion, and increasing present-moment awareness, all of which are key to alleviating depressive symptoms11,14. However, there are challenges in the clinical application of traditional MBI approaches. For instance, the typical 8-week duration of standard MBI programs often leads to low treatment adherence, as patients may struggle to maintain consistent participation over such an extended schedule7,15. Furthermore, patients with depression frequently experience difficulties concentrating their attention on the task of MBIs14,16. Depressive symptoms, particularly rumination, can impair cognitive flexibility and make it challenging for patients to stay focused during mindfulness exercises17. Additionally, some studies have reported that patients may find MBI sessions monotonous, further reducing patients’ motivation and engagement in the therapeutic process18,19. These challenges highlight the need for innovation in MBIs to provide a more effective and engaging intervention program for depressive disorders.

Mindfulness-based immersive interventions (MBIIs)—integrating immersive technologies (core technological layer in the Fig. 1) with MBIs—represent a potential solution20. In this review, immersive technologies were defined as digital systems that provide users with perceptually enriched or spatially simulated environments and support embodied or multisensory interaction. MBIIs leverage multiple sensory modalities (visual, auditory, haptic) to amplify the effectiveness and personalization of MBI training21–25. By placing patients in digital environments that enhance attentional focus26, MBIIs counteract rumination27,28 through embodied presence—a state where multisensory engagement anchors awareness to the present29,30. For example, previous studies have suggested that VR-mediated environments can reduce anxiety and stress by facilitating experiential acceptance through biofeedback loops31,32 that reward nonjudgmental awareness33,34. Critically, MBIIs are increasingly combined with complementary evidence-based therapies (adjunctive therapeutic layer in the Fig. 1) to create multimodal synergies: Virtual environments incorporate exercise regimens promoting adjunctive symptom reduction35–37. Multisensory feedback systems (e.g., heart rate variability monitoring) provide objective biomarkers for real-time self-regulation, representing continuous and actionable feedback to enhance emotional and cognitive control38,39; CBT-based virtual scenarios seamlessly address maladaptive thoughts within mindfulness practices37,40. Hybrid approaches amplify physiotherapeutics (e.g., accelerated transcranial magnetic stimulation, accel-TMS) via synchronized neural modulation41 and strengthen attentional control through music-driven stimulation26. These integrations translate to measurable clinical outcomes (clinical outcomes layer in the Fig. 1), including evaluation on depressive/anxiety symptom alleviation42–44, change of psychological processes45,46, change in physiological signal (e.g., reduced HRV47,48), and clinical adaptation of MBIIs49,50. Collectively, MBIIs represent a paradigm-shifting approach that transcends traditional MBI limitations—harnessing immersive technology to deliver personalized, multi-mechanistic interventions with demonstrable efficacy.

Despite the promising holistic potential of MBIIs in depressive disorders, comparative evidence between MBIIs and classical therapies remains fragmented. For example, while MBIIs show promise (e.g., superior emotion regulation vs. audio-only meditation51), direct comparisons against structured psychotherapies52 or physiotherapeutics like accel-TMS41 remain scarce and methodologically inconsistent (e.g., mismatched controls53, underpowered trials54). This obscures whether MBIIs replace, augment, or synergize with first-line interventions. Moreover, heterogeneity in protocols (e.g., dosing, VR hardware55) and outcome measures56 precludes identification of why MBIIs succeed—limiting personalized application. Limited synthesis of subjective experiences, such as acceptability and presence of MBIIs57,58, that mediate adherence. Cost-prohibitive development55, absent session standardization59, and lack of long-term validation60 stall real-world adoption. These gaps fragment the evidence base and stall clinical translation—highlighting an urgent need for comprehensive synthesis to guide implementation.

To the best of our knowledge, this review represents a potential perspective on the design and effects of research methods in the field of MBIIs for depressive disorders, which has not been systematically discussed in existing literature. This paper addresses a critical evidence gap by investigating three primary goals: (1) to systematically synthesize and compare the therapeutic effects of MBIIs for depressive disorders across different control conditions and comparator interventions, including CBT, physiotherapeutic approaches, multisensory stimulation, and conventional MBIs (Section “Main results”); (2) to evaluate the methodological characteristics and experimental rigor of included studies, encompassing study design, intervention implementation and session structure, comparison methods, and outcome measurement strategies, in order to contextualize the robustness of reported findings (Sections “Selection and inclusion of studies”, “Methodological features of studies”, and “Evaluation in leveraging MBIIs for treating depressive disorders”); and (3) to integrate these empirical and methodological insights to identify strengths, clinical implications, and key challenges for future development of MBIIs in depressive disorders, thereby providing guidance for research design and practical application (Section “4Discussion”). Guided by PRISMA principles61, this paper contributes a systematic review to investigate MBIIs’ impact on depressive symptoms across clinical methodologies and provides a comprehensive analysis of design efficacy and comparative effectiveness. It contributes valuable insights into the field of digital mental health interventions and lays the groundwork for further direction on improving depression care through MBIIs.

Methods

This review followed the PRISMA guidelines61 to systematically assess the clinical applications of immersive technologies in MBIs for depressive disorders. The literature search and selection process was conducted with rigor to ensure comprehensiveness and transparency. This systematic review was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD420251273080).

Search strategy and literature selection

A comprehensive literature search was conducted by the first author on April 29, 2025. The search included all eligible studies published between January 1, 2016, and April 29, 2025. No earlier studies were considered, as this time frame was predefined to capture contemporary research on immersive technologies in mental health. The search included multiple academic databases: PubMed, ProQuest, Cochrane, MEDLINE, CINAHL, and Scopus. Search terms were organized into three conceptual domains: (1) immersive technologies (e.g., “virtual reality”, “augmented reality”, “mixed reality”, “VR”, “AR”, “XR”, “MR”, “metaverse”, “digital twins”), (2) mindfulness-based interventions (e.g., “mindfulness”, “mindfulness-based interventions”, “meditation”, “mindful”), and (3) depressive disorders (e.g., “depressive”, “depression”, “major depressive disorder”, “MDD”, “clinical depression”). Within each domain, synonyms and related terms were combined using the Boolean operator OR, while the three domains were combined using AND to ensure that retrieved studies addressed all core concepts. Database-specific adaptations of search syntax were applied where necessary. Searches were conducted primarily in titles, abstracts, and keywords. In addition to database searches, supplementary manual searches were conducted using Google Scholar to identify potentially relevant studies not indexed in the selected databases, including early-stage, interdisciplinary, or technology-oriented publications. These manual searches primarily involved forward and backward citation tracking of included articles and key reviews. Two rounds of study selection, namely title or abstract screening and full-text screening, were performed by two researchers who independently evaluated each article based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria outlined in Table 1 based on the PICO (population, intervention, comparison, outcome) framework62. In case of any conflicts, the first author made the final decision.

Data collection process

We used Covidence, a web-based systematic review tool, to manage the literature screening process. The initial screening process involved reviewing titles and abstracts, with each reviewer independently determining study inclusion or exclusion criteria as described above. To assess the level of agreement between the reviewers, we calculated the percentage agreement by dividing the number of studies both reviewers agreed on (included or excluded) by the total number of studies reviewed and then multiplying by 100. The percentage agreement was 83% at the title and abstract screening stage, indicating a high level of concordance between the reviewers. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion. Data were extracted from the included studies based on the previous framework of systematic review63,64 and the topic of this paper, focusing on key elements such as subjects of the study (patient versus non-patient, sample size, and demographic characteristics), study design (randomized controlled trials, RCTs versus non-RCTs), type of implementation (intervention by AR, VR, or MR technologies), sessions of intervention (e.g., an 8-week intervention comprising two 45-min sessions per week), comparators (e.g., MBII groups versus mindfulness-based interventions group), and clinical outcomes (e.g., reductions in depressive symptoms, feasibility measured of MBIIs, improvements in mindfulness and quality of life). General information, such as the title of the paper, first author, and year of publication, and answers to the questions listed in Table 2 were extracted.

Data analysis

Qualitative methods

Qualitative approaches were critical for capturing participants’ subjective experiences and intervention acceptability. Data collection included: (1) post-intervention surveys with open-ended questions65 assessing perceived usefulness, enjoyment, and benefits of MBIIs; (2) thematic and textual narrative synthesis to identify emergent patterns from qualitative responses, particularly regarding the impact of MBIIs on well-being dimensions66.

Quantitative methods

Quantitative methods offered objective measures for assessing the effectiveness of MBIIs, including both primary and secondary outcomes. Primary outcomes were assessed on depression or anxiety. These were evaluated using standardized scales including Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-967), Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS68), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI69), Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D70), Behavioral Activation for Depression Scale-Short Form (BADS-SF71), 30-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-3072), Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI73), State-Trait Anxiety Inventory-6 (STAI-674), Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale Depression subscale (HADS-D75), Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-776), and Psychotic Symptom Rating Scales-Delusions (PSYRATS-D77).

Secondary outcomes were assessed across three dimensions: psychological processes, feasibility and clinical adaption, and physiological measures. Psychological processes of change, including motivation, mindfulness, attention control, emotion regulation, self-awareness, and well-being were evaluated using standardized scales including Five Facets Mindfulness Questionnaire Short Form (FFMQ-SF78), Toronto Mindfulness Scale (TMS79), Motivation and Pleasure Scale-Self Rating (MAP-SR80), Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS81), Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS82), Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS83), Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness (MAIA84), Brunel Mood Scale (BRUMS85), Emotional Discomfort Detection Scale (DME86), Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination-III (ACE-III87), and Short Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale (SWEMWBS88). Feasibility and clinical application of MBII programs, encompassing usability, user experience, immersion, and cognitive load were measured through System Usability Scale (SUS89), Virtual Reality Sickness Questionnaire (VRSQ90), Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-1091), Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ92), Igroup Presence Questionnaire (IPQ93), User Experience Questionnaires (UEQ94), and NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX95). Physiological measures96–98 to complement quantitative outcomes included electroencephalogram (EEG), electromyography (EMG), galvanic skin response (GSR), and heart rate variability (HRV).

Evaluation metrics

Key statistical indicators included significance thresholds, measures of central tendency and dispersion, effect size estimates, and regression coefficients to evaluate predictors of intervention outcomes. These metrics assessed the magnitude, precision, and clinical relevance of observed effects while controlling for potential confounders.

Assessment of methodological rigor and study quality

In line with the aims of this review, methodological rigor was assessed using a structured narrative synthesis. This approach is consistent with prior systematic reviews in digital mental health99,100 and human-computer interaction101,102 research, where substantial heterogeneity in study designs, intervention modalities, and outcome measures limits the applicability of standardized bias tools. Specifically, study quality was evaluated across four predefined methodological domains: (1) study design characteristics, including randomized versus non-randomized designs, presence and type of control conditions, and sample size adequacy; (2) intervention specification, including clarity of mindfulness components, immersive technology configurations, session duration, and intervention dose; (3) outcome measurement rigor, including the use of validated clinical scales for depressive symptoms and psychological processes, as well as the integration of objective or physiological measures; and (4) analytic transparency, including reporting of statistical methods, handling of confounders, and completeness of outcome reporting. Rather than generating a composite quality score, these domains were used to identify recurring methodological strengths and limitations across studies. Studies with unvalidated outcome measures, insufficiently described interventions, or inadequate control of confounding factors were excluded during full-text screening. Among included studies, variability in sample size, control conditions, and outcome selection was systematically documented and synthesized to contextualize the strength of the evidence and inform interpretation of findings.

Results

Selection and inclusion of studies

A total of 670 articles were initially identified, with 650 retrieved from database searches and an additional 20 found through manual searches on Google Scholar (Fig. 2). After removing 219 duplicates, 451 articles remained for screening. These articles were reviewed through title and abstract screening based on the eligibility criteria (Table 1), resulting in the selection of 94 articles for full-text review. Subsequently, we carefully read the full text of each article and excluded 57 articles. 22 articles were excluded due to insufficient methodological rigor (i.e, 14 unvalidated outcome measures, 16 articles with unclear intervention descriptions, and 2 articles lacking control for confounding factors); 14 articles were excluded due to the lack of participants with depressive symptoms; 12 articles were excluded since they did not involve mindfulness-related exercises or did not use immersive technologies; 7 articles were excluded since they were merely exploratory or descriptive studies; and 2 articles were excluded because they were not in English. Following a rigorous evaluation, 37 studies were included in the final analysis.

Methodological features of studies

Study design and sample characteristics

As shown in Table 3, 18 of the 37 included studies were RCTs, while the remaining 19 employed non-RCT designs. Non-RCT studies comprised single-blind or double-blind trials as well as single-arm designs. Although RCTs were commonly used, their methodological characteristics varied across studies. For instance, Ritvo et al. employed a parallel-arm RCT with intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses to evaluate an 8-week Mindfulness Virtual Community (MVC) program for undergraduates. However, a concurrent university labor strike introduced confounding stressors, potentially biasing outcomes such as depression (PHQ-9) and anxiety (BAI) scores103.

Sample sizes ≤ 60 participants were prevalent (25 studies, 67.6%), with 12 studies (32.4%) having n ≤ 20. Pilot designs prioritized feasibility over generalizability. Studies such as Senda et al. (n = 15)41 and Van et al. (n = 20)104 prioritized feasibility over generalizability, limiting their statistical power to detect small-to-moderate effects. Rodrigues et al. further highlighted pandemic-related recruitment challenges, with only 44 COVID-19 patients enrolled across four sites, which restricted subgroup analyses in their multicenter trial53.

Across the included studies, participants were broadly categorized into two groups: individuals with clinically diagnosed conditions accompanied by depressive symptoms (n = 1027), including populations such as patients with cancer, schizophrenia, or depressive disorders; and non-clinical participants reporting depressive symptoms (n = 1222), among whom college students and adolescents constituted a substantial proportion. Populations ranged from adolescents (e.g., mean age 17.03105) to elderly cohorts (e.g., mean age 70.73106), with interventions tailored to developmental needs (e.g., gamification for youth versus low-intensity VR for seniors). Cultural specificity was also considered, as seen in studies like Mao et al.107, which incorporated localized content (e.g., income tiers in yuan), and Rowland et al.108, which focused on Indigenous cultural contexts. Moreover, some trials included participants with overlapping diagnoses (e.g., depression, PTSD, and chronic pain41), further complicating the isolation of MBII effects.

However, narrow inclusion criteria (e.g., only two spinal cord injury patients in the study109) limit the extrapolation of findings to broader depressive populations. Furthermore, heterogeneous baselines, such as variability in depression severity (e.g., PHQ-9 scores ranging from mild to severe57), obscure session-response relationships. Additionally, geographic representation showed Western dominance (24 studies, 64.9%), followed by Asian populations (12 studies, 32.4%), with only 1 study (2.7%) from a Global South region. The uneven distribution of the studied population was emphasized.

Implementations of intervention

Across the included trials, interventions exhibited substantial technological heterogeneity. As shown in Table 3, 26 studies used VR-based mindfulness (VRM), 9 studies combined VRM with other clinical approaches (VRM+), 1 study employed AR-based mindfulness mixed with neurofeedback (ARM+), and 1 study utilized MR-based mindfulness (MRM) as the intervention type. Interventions were delivered in two forms: guided and self-guided. For example, Lee et al.110 used HeartMath biofeedback-integrated immersive VRM for real-time emotional coherence monitoring, whereas Van et al.104 relied on self-guided VR mindfulness without feedback. Content customization also varied widely. Mao et al.107 incorporated AI-driven emotion tracking and personalized mindfulness courses, while Habak et al.111 used a fixed MR environment to evoke future-oriented hope.

Sessions of intervention

The sessions of interventions also exhibited considerable variability. Drawing on historical research frameworks in this domain, we conducted a systematic classification of extant studies112–115 based on parameters (e.g., duration) of technology-mediated mindfulness interventions. The studies were categorized into four types (Table 3): 16 studies were brief interventions (BI), where participants experienced only one session (10 min ≤ duration ≤ 45 min), 6 studies were short-term interventions (STI, 1 day ≤ duration ≤ 2 weeks, 10–15 min per session), 6 studies were mid-term interventions (MTI, 2 weeks < duration ≤ 4 weeks, 10–45 min per session), and 9 studies were long-term interventions (LTI, duration > 4 weeks, 10–15 min per session). The interventions ranged from single 10-min sessions51 to 10-week programs116. While shorter sessions (e.g., 4 weeks107,) prioritized feasibility, they may lack sustained therapeutic impact, whereas longer programs risk participant attrition. For example, BI reported immediate anxiolytic effects but showed minimal durability53,117. In contrast, LTI achieved sustained symptom reduction118, yet faced higher attrition due to cognitive burden in depressed populations119. While MTI interventions balanced feasibility and impact108, only LTI induced measurable neurocognitive changes (e.g., increased dlPFC activation on fMRI120). Acute depressive episodes benefited most from BI’s rapid symptom relief (e.g., VR crisis intervention110), whereas chronic depression required LTI’s skill consolidation. Notably, little study systematically explored session-response effects, underscoring a critical gap for future research.

Outcome measures

The reliance on self-reported psychometric scales (e.g., PHQ-9, HADS, FFMQ-SF) across studies introduces potential response biases that may confound outcome interpretation. While subjective measures remain pragmatic for assessing depressive symptoms, only a minority of trials supplemented these with objective biomarkers, such as HRV and GSR, which provide quantifiable physiological correlates of emotional regulation115,117. Furthermore, the temporal scope of most investigations was constrained by short-term intervention (≤4 weeks), with exceptions such as extending assessments to 8 weeks post-intervention59. Even in studies reporting sustained symptom reductions (e.g., demonstrating improvements in the GDS at 2-week follow-up106), the absence of longitudinal data beyond acute phases limits conclusions about durability—a critical gap given the recurrent nature of depression.

Main results

The impact of MBIIs on depressive disorders in all studies was primarily categorized into seven comparators (Fig. 3). Specifically, there were 2 studies comparing MBII groups versus physicotherapeutics groups39,41, 2 studies comparing MBII groups versus exercise groups54,120, 2 studies comparing MBII groups versus psychotherapy groups52,106, 6 studies comparing MBIIs versus multisensory stimulation groups53,57,121–124, 7 studies comparing MBII groups versus MBI groups51,115,117,118,125,126, 9 studies comparing MBII groups versus other control groups (i.e., Treatment as Usual (TAU); Wait List Control (WLC), or physical room)58,103,104,110,116,119,127–129 and 9 studies that did not include a control group (without control groups)105,107–109,111,130–133. MBIIs versus MBIs

Some studies reported that MBIIs were associated with higher engagement and adherence, as well as improvements in attention control, emotion regulation, and self-awareness, compared to conventional MBIs. In an RCT by Navarro et al.118, VR-supported MBIs were reported to be associated with reductions in generalized anxiety and depressive symptoms. The MBI + VR group was reported to have a higher treatment completion rate than the MBI-only group (100% vs. 70%), which may indicate greater engagement with the intervention. Tarrant et al.51 employed qualitative and quantitative methods, including in-depth interviews, focus groups, and standardized scales. Their study reported high usability and immersion of VR combined with MBCT, along with improvements in emotional regulation among participants with depression. Chavez et al.125 utilized psychological testing tools, EMG, and EEG data to report a significant reduction in negative emotions and enhanced attention control and emotion regulation through VR restorative environments. Kumar et al.117 used both qualitative and quantitative methods to assess the impact of VR-based mindfulness on patients with schizophrenia. Their study noted a decrease in depressive and anxiety symptoms, as well as improved emotional states, following VR mindfulness training in this population. In a pilot RCT conducted by Wang et al.115, it was found that VR-guided mindfulness effectively reduced caregiver burden and enhanced psychological well-being within home-based settings, further demonstrating increased accessibility for patients. Holsteg et al.126 assessed the impact of an online mindfulness and CBT intervention on university students. The intervention group showed statistically lower depression and anxiety scores compared with the control group. In addition, the study reported statistically higher mindfulness scores following the intervention. Liu et al.59 compared a VR-enhanced mindfulness and yoga intervention with traditional in-person and blank control groups among 111 postpartum women. Results indicated reductions in depression (EPDS) and anxiety (GAD-7) scores in the VR group. Compared with the control and blank groups, the VR group showed larger symptom reductions (24% for depression and 35% for anxiety). In addition, changes in cognitive control were reported, as reflected by a reduced conflict effect in the emotional Stroop task.

Traditional MBIs have also been reported to reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety in some studies. Navarro et al.118 reported that MBIs alone showed significant improvements in depressive symptoms comparable to those achieved with MBIs combined with VR. Similarly, Kumar et al.117 found that traditional mindfulness interventions improved cognitive functioning and emotion regulation in patients with schizophrenia. These improvements were attributed to enhanced attention control and self-awareness, which are critical for managing depressive symptoms.

MBIIs versus physicotherapeutics

Some studies suggested that MBII, combined with EEG-based measures, was associated with improvements in mood states and changes in brain activity patterns relevant to depressive symptoms. Viczko et al.39 reported that AR meditation improved mood states, with participants experiencing increased happiness and calmness, as well as decreased depression, tension, and fatigue. EEG data revealed increased alpha activity and reduced gamma activity in the frontal regions, suggesting a state of calm-abiding alertness. These interventions also showed the potential to enhance attention control and emotion regulation, although direct measurements were limited. The high engagement levels reported by participants indicate that these interventions are feasible and acceptable for daily use. In contrast, physiotherapeutic approaches such as accel-TMS have been reported to reduce depressive symptoms in some studies, particularly when targeting regions such as the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) and the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC). Senda et al.41 found that accel-TMS targeted to the left dlPFC led to improvements in emotional well-being, depression, anxiety, and PTSD symptoms. The intervention’s rapid response and flexibility in treatment sessions allow for quick adjustments based on individual patient needs.

MBIIs versus exercise

A study conducted by Ritvo et al.103 which compared MBII treatment to standard relaxation exercises in a WLC group and an MVC group, showed a statistically significant reduction in perceived stress (PSS) in the MVC group. However, no significant differences were observed between the WLC group and the MVC group in depression, anxiety, or perceived stress. Burrows et al.54 investigated the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of a combined pre-habilitative VR-based mindfulness (VRM) program and a personalized activity prescription (PARx) in hemodialysis (HD) patients with depressive symptoms. The immersive nature of VRM and the personalized approach of PARx were well-received by participants, resulting in high adherence rates. The VRM + PARx group showed a within-group increase in physical activity energy expenditure, suggesting that the PARx effectively promoted physical activity. However, no between-group differences were observed, indicating that both the VRM + PARx and PARx-alone groups benefited equally from the PARx program.

MBIIs versus psychotherapy

The immersive and personalized nature of VR interventions enhances engagement and adherence, making the approach a promising alternative to traditional psychotherapy. Joanna et al.106 evaluated the impact of VR interventions—specifically mindfulness VR and relaxing music VR—on depressive symptoms compared to a control group receiving standard treatment (40 min of general fitness training and 20 min of health education and psychoeducation). The study found that both VR interventions reduced depression, anxiety, and stress compared to the control group, with mindfulness VR showing the most notable improvements. However, no significant long-term differences were observed at the 2-week and 12-week follow-up points, suggesting that while VR interventions have an immediate impact, their sustained effects require further investigation.

Torres et al.52 investigated the efficacy of a VR-based CBT program compared to a standard psychoeducational intervention based on mindfulness, with a control group receiving standard treatment. The VR-based CBT program reduced depressive symptoms compared to the standard treatment group. Additionally, the VR-based approach may be associated with improvements in attention control, emotion regulation, and self-awareness compared to the psychotherapy group. Physiological measures, such as HRV, indicated better emotional regulation, while self-report questionnaires revealed increased self-awareness among participants in the VR group.

MBIIs versus multisensory stimulation

Lee et al.121 evaluated the effectiveness of a VR app in enhancing mindfulness and positive affect compared to a VR control group viewing natural scenes. The study found a significant increase in state mindfulness and positive affect in the VR-treated group, with no significant changes in negative emotion or arousal. Qualitative feedback indicated that the VR environment was engaging and helped participants direct their attention and regulate emotions. Rodrigues et al.53 compared the effectiveness of therapeutic VR mindfulness training with non-therapeutic VR content in a residential substance use treatment setting. Results suggested reductions in negative affect and increases in positive affect from pre- to post-intervention. Qualitative feedback indicated that participants perceived the VR mindfulness intervention as providing a temporary escape from negative thoughts and supporting self-awareness. Zheng et al.122 compared a VR mindfulness intervention with a treatment group receiving relaxing music through VR and a control group with no intervention. Results suggested reductions in depression, anxiety, and stress in the VR group, whereas the control group showed no notable changes in these symptoms. Kim et al.57 conducted a study comparing the effects of a VR-based meditation intervention with neurofeedback (VR + NF) to an audio-only control group on mood states, including depression. Both groups experienced significant reductions in depression and anxiety levels, but the VR + NF group showed additional improvements in calmness and happiness, suggesting enhanced attention control and emotion regulation. Qualitative feedback indicated that the immersive nature of VR combined with NF contributed to greater engagement and a sense of immersion, potentially leading to increased self-awareness. The high completion rate and lack of adverse events highlighted the feasibility and acceptability of the VR + NF intervention in a healthcare setting. Li et al.123 examined the impact of urban and restorative VR environments on emotional and cognitive recovery using a mixed-methods approach with 10-min VR sessions, physiological data, and questionnaires. The study found significant reductions in negative emotions across all VR groups, with restorative environments particularly enhancing self-efficacy and cognitive recovery. Despite limitations such as the lack of a control group and a short intervention duration, the findings highlight VR’s potential as a therapeutic tool for mental health. Seabrook et al.124 compared VR-based mindfulness (VBM) with a VR control intervention. The VBM group suggested increases in decentering and reductions in delusion-related preoccupation. Although changes in depression scores were not observed, the study indicated potential improvements in emotion regulation and self-awareness.

MBIIs versus other control groups

Here, we systematically examine the utilization of MBIIs against three control conditions across nine rigorous trials: four studies employed standard TAU protocols, three utilized WLCs, and two implemented physical room-based interventions. This methodological diversity allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of MBIIs’ comparative efficacy in both real-world and experimental contexts.

MBIIs versus TAU. Van et al.104 conducted a randomized, double-blind clinical trial comparing a VR mindfulness intervention group with a control group receiving standard care. Results suggested improvements in fatigue, shortness of breath, anxiety, and well-being, whereas no changes were observed in depression. In the control group, improvements were reported for fatigue and anxiety but not for shortness of breath or well-being. Between-group comparisons indicated a trend toward greater improvement in well-being in the VR group. Miwoo et al.116 conducted a single-center RCT to evaluate the effectiveness of VR interventions in reducing anxiety and depression among breast cancer patients before their first chemotherapy dose. Results indicated that the VR intervention group reported greater reductions in anxiety and depression scores compared to the TAU group, with differences reaching statistical significance. The VR group also showed trends toward decreased emotional distress and improvements in coping strategies, suggesting potential enhancements in emotion regulation and attention control. Plencler et al.127 compared TAU with TAU plus mindfulness-based training via VR. Results reported reductions in depression, anxiety, and stress scores in the VR and Zen meditation groups compared to the control group, with some differences reaching statistical significance. Findings from the VR-based Zen meditation group suggested potential improvements in emotion regulation and self-awareness post-intervention. Zhang et al.128 conducted a single-blinded RCT comparing an intervention group (VR-based meditation for 20 min daily for 14 days) with a control group receiving standard care. The results indicated reductions in anxiety levels and improvements in quality of life in the intervention group compared to the control group, with some differences reaching statistical significance. Although depression levels did not differ significantly between groups, the intervention group showed a trend toward reduction.

MBIIs versus WLC. Ahmad et al.119 conducted an RCT comparing three groups: full intervention with video modules, discussion forums, and videoconferences (F-MVC); partial intervention with video modules only (P-MVC); and a WLC group that received no intervention. Results indicated reductions in anxiety levels in the intervention groups compared to the WLC group, while differences in depression scores were not statistically significant. Nonetheless, the intervention groups showed a decreasing trend in depression scores. Ritvo et al.103 evaluated the effectiveness of a VR relaxation tool (VRelax) in reducing negative affective states compared to standard relaxation exercises and a WLC group. Results indicated that both VRelax and standard relaxation exercises led to immediate improvements in negative affective states, with VRelax showing a relatively greater reduction in total negative affective states compared to standard relaxation (change 21.2% versus 16.2%). Qualitative feedback suggested that the immersive nature of VR may have helped participants maintain attention and focus during the relaxation sessions, potentially contributing to improvements in emotion regulation and self-awareness. El Morr et al.58 examined the effectiveness of an MVC group compared to a WLC group. Results indicated a decrease in BDI and BAI scores from pre- to post-measurement in the MVC group, suggesting a potential reduction in depression and anxiety symptoms. The study also reported high user satisfaction with the VR intervention, indicating its acceptability and feasibility in the clinical setting.

MBIIs versus physical room-based interventions. Ilioudi et al.129 compared the effectiveness of immersive VR meditation with a traditional physical calm room setting in reducing depression and anxiety symptoms. The study reported reductions in depression and anxiety for both interventions, with the VR intervention suggesting a potentially greater effect on emotional regulation. Participants in the VR group also indicated higher levels of engagement and immersion. The study further reported that the VR intervention was feasible and acceptable, as reflected by high completion rates and low dropout rates. Lee et al.110 compared the effects of physical and VR calm rooms on psychiatric inpatients. Both interventions were associated with increases in well-being and decreases in heart rate, suggesting a potential positive impact on emotional disturbance. The study also reported that VR calm rooms were feasible and acceptable, indicating their potential as a flexible and accessible alternative to physical calm rooms.

MBIIs without control groups

Nine single-arm studies included in this review reported reductions in depressive and anxiety symptoms and improvements in psychological outcomes. Although these studies lacked control groups, findings suggested that MBIIs may be feasible and provide immediate benefits, particularly in resource-limited or high-risk contexts. Franklin et al.130 reported a significant reduction in depression symptoms, with mean PHQ-9 scores decreasing from 14.93 at baseline to 9.57 post-intervention and 8.49 at the 1-month follow-up. The intervention also reported improvements in anxiety symptoms. Mohamad et al.131 observed a significant within-group reduction in depressive symptoms and an increase in mindfulness, as measured by FFMQ-SF. The VRM program effectively enhanced participants’ present-moment awareness and attention control, contributing to improved emotion regulation. Rowland et al.108 conducted VR mindfulness skills training for patients with spinal cord injuries. Both participants reported reductions in depression and anxiety scores following the VR sessions. The intervention was generally well accepted and appeared feasible from a technical perspective. Miller et al.105 evaluated the impact of a VR-guided meditation application on patient-reported outcomes. The study found significant improvements in anxiety levels and mental health scores. Habak et al.111 assessed a VRM training model in cancer patients. The study reported significant reductions in anxiety and depression scores at 1 week and 4 weeks post-intervention. The VRMT model was highly accepted by participants, with no adverse events reported, underscoring its feasibility and safety. Blackmore et al.132 evaluated a VR-supported mindfulness practice in a multi-phase study. The intervention was reported to have high feasibility and tolerability, with participants generally showing good adherence and engagement. Mao et al.107 used a mixed reality environment to assess the impact of a VR intervention on hopelessness, mood, and well-being. The study found reductions in hopelessness, improvements in positive mood, and increased well-being. Flores et al.109 evaluated a VRM-based intervention in Aboriginal children and young people in out-of-home care. The study reported an improvement in state mindfulness, suggesting enhanced emotion regulation and self-awareness. The intervention was highly accepted and feasible, with high completion rates and positive participant feedback. Ma et al.133 conducted a quasi-experimental study with a single-group pre-post test and 1-week follow-up among 49 Chinese university students with anxiety/depressive symptoms. Results indicated reductions in depression (BDI) and anxiety (BAI) scores, which were maintained at the 1-week follow-up. Improvements in mindfulness and olfactory discrimination were also reported.

Experimental setups

Across the 37 reviewed studies, intervention sessions varied substantially, ranging from brief to long-term formats. This variability highlights gaps in understanding MBII therapeutic dynamics and suggests an unresolved tension between session characteristics and observed effects. The findings indicate that optimal duration and frequency of MBIIs for depressive disorders remain to be clarified. For example, some studies used a single 10-min VR session, which may not be sufficient to produce lasting effects51. In contrast, longer interventions, while potentially more effective, may face challenges in real-world implementation107. This dichotomy underscores a fundamental challenge: brief interventions risk therapeutic insufficiency, whereas long-term interventions may reduce adherence and feasibility. Notably, no study has systematically explored session-effect relationships—a critical omission, given that the neural mechanisms underlying mindfulness may require cumulative exposure12. Additionally, the lack of standardization in sessions of intervention makes it difficult to compare results across studies and draw general conclusions about the effectiveness of MBIIs. The optimal session may vary depending on the severity of depressive symptoms and individual patient characteristics, highlighting the need for personalized approaches to intervention.

The studies reviewed employed a variety of VR and AR platforms, including devices such as Oculus Quest 2 and Meta Quest 2 headsets54,116 and HTC Vive Pro Eye headsets53,126, which differed in levels of immersion, user experience, and accessibility. This variability suggests that the optimal balance between technological sophistication and practical feasibility remains unclear. While more advanced headsets may provide more immersive experiences, simpler and more accessible devices could be preferable for broader implementation, particularly in clinical settings with limited resources. The diversity of consumer-grade versus clinical-grade VR systems may also pose challenges for reproducibility across studies. While low-cost headsets enhance accessibility, their limited tracking fidelity may compromise immersion depth—a key mediator of therapeutic outcomes53. This trade-off demands standardized reporting of immersion metrics (e.g., presence questionnaires, gaze tracking57) alongside cost-benefit analyses in pragmatic trials.

The content of MBIIs varies widely, incorporating guided meditation, mindfulness exercises, CBT components, and even physical activity prescriptions51,110. The diversity in content underscores the need for personalized interventions that address the unique needs and comorbidities of different patient groups. However, this variability also complicates the standardization and comparison of interventions across studies. Integration of MBIIs with other therapeutic approaches has been a trend. While this integration can enhance therapeutic outcomes, it also makes it challenging to isolate the specific effects of mindfulness.

The comparison methods used in the studies reviewed here were diverse. Some studies compared MBIIs to a control group receiving no intervention, others to a waitlist control group, and some to a standard treatment group54,58. The predominance of waitlist controls over active comparators inflates effect sizes by ignoring placebo effects and natural remission. Even when active controls are employed (e.g., VR nature scenes121), inadequate matching of nonspecific factors (e.g., clinician attention) persists. The choice of comparison group may influence study outcomes, as different comparison groups often have varying baseline characteristics and levels of depressive symptoms.

The evaluation metrics used in the studies varied widely. Some studies focused on primary outcomes such as depression, anxiety, and stress, while others emphasized secondary outcomes such as mindfulness, attention control, and emotion regulation57,104,110. Importantly, differences in participant characteristics may partly explain the heterogeneity of reported effects. Studies enrolling individuals with a clinical diagnosis of depressive disorders tended to prioritize symptom reduction as a primary outcome41,54, whereas studies involving participants with subclinical depressive symptoms or elevated mood vulnerability more frequently reported improvements in attentional regulation, emotional awareness, and mindfulness-related capacities58,125. This pattern suggests that MBIIs may exert differential effects across clinical and subclinical populations, with symptom-level changes being more modest and variable in clinically diagnosed samples, and cognitive-affective regulation outcomes appearing more consistently in subclinical or mixed populations.

Across studies, a predominant reliance on self-report scales (e.g., PHQ-9, FFMQ-SF) introduces recall bias and constrains mechanistic interpretation. While clinical diagnosis of depression currently relies on self-report instruments and clinician-administered assessments, several studies39,41,51,117 have explored complementary objective or semi-objective markers, including physiological indices (e.g., HRV), behavioral performance metrics (e.g., sustained attention or cognitive control tasks), and neurophysiological measures (e.g., EEG-derived markers of affective or attentional regulation). These markers are not intended to replace clinical diagnosis but may serve as adjunct indicators of intervention engagement, self-regulatory capacity, or neurophysiological change. Notably, studies integrating multimodal measures occasionally revealed discordance between subjective symptom reports and physiological or behavioral responses57,121,122, underscoring the complexity of interpreting intervention effects. Finally, the substantial variability in evaluation metrics limits cross-study comparability and complicates the synthesis of evidence regarding the effectiveness of MBIIs for depressive disorders137–139.

Experimental results and performance analysis

Some studies suggest that MBIIs may contribute to reductions in depressive symptoms, although outcomes appear to be context-dependent. Clinically meaningful improvements were more frequently observed in high-distress populations. For instance, Zhang et al.128 reported a 37% reduction in PHQ-9 scores among leukemia patients undergoing chemotherapy, while Mao et al.107 observed a 41% decline in HADS-D scores in ovarian cancer patients. Similarly, Lee et al.121 documented a 29% reduction in PANAS scores in patients with psychosis, with effect sizes amplified in those with baseline suicidality. These findings suggest that MBIIs may be particularly effective for individuals with high baseline levels of distress. Conversely, efficacy is attenuated in mild or moderate cases, particularly in student populations. Ritvo et al.103 found no significant differences in PHQ-9 scores versus waitlist controls, suggesting a symptom severity threshold for MBII efficacy. This aligns with neurocognitive models where immersive interventions preferentially engage attention networks compromised under high affective load.

The interpretability of these results is constrained by three critical limitations. First, technological placebo confounds persist, as only 18% of studies included active VR controls (e.g.,120), leaving the specificity of mindfulness effects unproven against equivalent technological interventions. Second, session-response ambiguity remains, with most trials employing fixed-duration sessions that obscure optimal exposure parameters. While Franklin et al.130 showed symptom reduction correlated with session frequency, the lack of session-response data limits the generalizability of findings. Third, attrition bias poses a significant issue, particularly in longitudinal designs. High dropout rates (e.g., 40% in ref. 127) disproportionately affected severe cases, potentially inflating efficacy estimates and undermining the reliability of results.

Improvements in mindfulness, attentional control, and emotion regulation were frequently reported across studies. These enhancements suggest that MBIIs may effectively enhance cognitive and emotional functioning, which could contribute to the observed reductions in depressive symptoms. For instance, El Morr et al.58 demonstrated a significant increase in non-judgmental awareness, with the FFMQ-SF showing a significant increase, while Zheng et al.122 showed improvement in present-moment focus without FFMQ-SF differentiation. However, the interpretability of these findings is undermined by conceptual and methodological inconsistencies. Physiological-subjective mismatches were also observed, such as in Lee et al.110, where HRV improvements were uncorrelated with self-reported calmness, highlighting measurement discordance.

The immersive properties of VR may selectively enhance specific mechanisms, such as attentional anchoring and interoceptive recalibration. For example, Tarrant et al.51 found that 360∘ environments reduced mind-wandering by 42% compared to audio-only controls, and Wang et al.115 reported that biofeedback systems increased body scan accuracy by 29%, suggesting augmented meta-awareness. Despite these promising results, 63% of studies used ad-hoc measures, precluding meta-analysis and limiting the generalizability of the findings. Standardized cognitive batteries, such as the Attention Network Test and Emotional Stroop task, are urgently needed to isolate MBII-specific effects and enhance the robustness of future research.

Discussion

Through a systematic review, this paper has synthesized a taxonomy of the clinical applications of MBIIs for depressive disorders (Fig. 4). In the following sections, this study discusses the strengths of utilizing MBIIs in the context of depressive disorders, their clinical implications, and the challenges that must be addressed to guide their practical application. Finally, it outlines the limitations of the present study.

Strengths of leveraging MBIIs with depressive disorders

Some studies reported that MBIIs, particularly VR-based interventions, were associated with higher engagement and adherence compared with traditional MBIs. For example, one randomized study118 reported higher completion rates in a VR-based MBII condition compared with a conventional MBII control (92.3% vs. 64.7%); and similar completion advantages were also systematically reported across the majority of included studies51,115,125. However, evidence across studies remains heterogeneous, and such differences were not consistently examined or observed in all included trials. Compared to physiotherapeutic interventions like accel-TMS, MBIIs showed similar efficacy in improving mood states and brain activity patterns associated with depression, with the added benefit of being non-invasive and well-accepted by patients.

Furthermore, compared with exercise interventions, some studies reported that MBIIs were associated with greater patient engagement and reductions in perceived stress54,120, although results were not consistent across all trials. This is notable, as exercise, while effective in treating depressive symptoms, relies on different physiological pathways. Additionally, some studies suggested that MBIIs, particularly VR-based CBT, were associated with improvements in attention control and emotion regulation compared to traditional psychotherapy, though evidence remains limited and heterogeneous52,106. However, the question of long-term effects remains uncertain, with only 5 studies tracking outcomes >3 months. Moreover, when compared to multisensory stimulation approaches (e.g., relaxing nature videos121), MBIIs demonstrated a clear edge in enhancing mindfulness and positive affect, underlining their potential to offer more holistic therapeutic benefits.

The immersive quality of VR has been reported to enhance patient engagement by creating controlled, distraction-free environments. This immersive experience may facilitate engagement with mindfulness practices104,125, which is crucial for improving treatment adherence. VR simulations, incorporating 360° visuals and natural sounds, help foster emotional regulation and mindfulness41,59,140, which may provide a temporary reprieve from stressful environments107,122, especially beneficial for those with chronic health conditions or psychological stress54,130. In contrast, traditional MBIs often suffer from low engagement and high dropout rates, particularly among patients with depressive disorders who struggle with passive, non-interactive sessions51,109. VR-based mindfulness programs can address these challenges by offering more engaging and interactive experiences, which may support retention and therapeutic engagement58. Furthermore, VR interventions may support scalability and accessibility, allowing patients to practice mindfulness at home and overcoming logistical barriers typical of in-person therapy119,121.

Implications for guidance of clinical application

The integration of MBIIs into depression treatment suggests the potential value of personalized interventions to address the diverse needs of patients54,107,110. However, the current literature provides limited guidance on frameworks for customizing MBIIs. Our systematic review reveals three fundamental barriers that should be addressed to advance clinical implementation: (1) substantial heterogeneity in intervention sessions (e.g., inconsistent VR hardware and dosing parameters), (2) insufficient long-term efficacy data (with ≤ 6 weeks follow-up in 89% of studies), and (3) limited understanding of optimal personalization strategies for different depression subtypes. Although VR-based therapies have shown potential benefits, variation in tailoring VR content—such as therapeutic scenarios, environmental design, and interaction modes—may be associated with heterogeneity in clinical outcomes. This gap in the literature needs to be addressed to ensure that VR interventions are optimally personalized for diverse patient populations, ranging from those with mild to severe depression55.

Our review also suggests the potential benefits of combining MBIIs with traditional therapeutic modalities, such as CBT and medication58,110,116,119,130, for more comprehensive treatment. The synthesis of mediators (e.g., presence or interactivity using MBIIs) provides actionable targets for these integrated approaches. However, empirical evidence regarding the long-term efficacy of such integrated approaches remains limited. Further studies are needed to examine whether combining VR with conventional therapies may yield improvements compared to standard treatments alone55. If empirical evidence for the benefits of these combinations is insufficient, the complexity of such interventions may hinder their widespread adoption in clinical settings55.

Moreover, VR may help address treatment gaps, particularly among underserved populations125. Future implementation could benefit from balancing standardization—such as consensus guidelines for core protocol elements—with flexibility to accommodate individual needs and evolving technologies. However, the scalability of VR interventions remains a significant concern due to the high costs associated with the technology, including equipment, software development, and clinician training55. Integrating VR into existing healthcare systems requires substantial investment and adaptation, which may present logistical challenges110. Additionally, the risk of discomfort or motion sickness associated with VR107, particularly among individuals with sensory sensitivities, should be considered to minimize adverse user experiences. Long-term trials assessing both clinical outcomes and implementation factors (e.g., cost-effectiveness, user tolerance) may help clarify the sustainable role of VR in depression care.

Current evidence suggests that the therapeutic effects of MBIIs on depressive disorders may be mediated by interconnected neurophysiological, psychological, and contextual factors (Fig. 5). This hypothesis is derived from converging patterns observed across the included studies rather than from direct causal testing. These mediation factors potentially influence the primary or secondary outcomes in the clinical application of MBIIs.

At the neurophysiological level, several MBII studies incorporating biofeedback or physiological monitoring consistently reported concurrent changes in autonomic regulation alongside symptom improvement. For example, biofeedback-integrated interventions demonstrated improvements in HeartMath coherence scores and heart rate variability (HRV), indices inversely associated with sympathetic arousal—a core feature implicated in depressive symptomatology116,117. Although these studies primarily reported correlational associations, the repeated co-occurrence of autonomic modulation and symptom change across trials supports the plausibility of neurophysiological pathways contributing to therapeutic effects. At the psychological level, the mediation hypothesis is informed by both review findings and prior mindfulness research. Several included studies reported improvements in mindfulness, emotion regulation, or attentional control alongside reductions in depressive symptoms. For instance, El Morr et al. explicitly tested partial mediation, showing that increases in mindfulness statistically mediated depressive symptom improvement58. However, most studies examined psychological variables as parallel secondary outcomes rather than formally testing mediation models. In some cases, dissociations were observed: Seabrook et al. reported increased mindfulness without corresponding reductions in negative affect124, suggesting that psychological changes alone may be insufficient to account for clinical effects and may interact with other mediating domains. Contextual and design-related factors emerged as an additional, though under-theorized, mediating layer in the review. Several studies57,121,122 indicated that immersive features—such as environmental presence, interactivity, and multisensory feedback—were associated with enhanced engagement, adherence, or intervention feasibility. While these factors were rarely modeled as mediators, their consistent association with improved acceptability and sustained participation suggests that contextual affordances of immersive technologies may indirectly influence clinical outcomes by shaping intervention exposure and engagement.

Despite these converging observations, the clinical validation of mediation mechanisms remains largely at a descriptive or associative stage. Most studies relied on correlations between primary outcomes (e.g., depressive symptoms) and secondary measures (e.g., mindfulness or physiological indices), without explicitly testing multi-level causal chains. Furthermore, substantial heterogeneity in mediating indices—ranging from standardized scales such as DERS and PANAS to ad-hoc measures—limits cross-study comparability and meta-analytic synthesis. Moderating factors that could interact with mediating pathways are also insufficiently addressed. For example, Plencler et al. identified dual pathways involving symptom alleviation and neurocognitive enhancement in psychotic populations, suggesting subgroup-specific mechanisms127. Similarly, Miller et al. demonstrated age-related differences in engagement, with adolescents responding more favorably to gamified immersive interfaces105. Such findings support the view that mediation processes may vary across developmental, cultural, or diagnostic contexts, yet these moderators are rarely incorporated into analytic models. Future clinical assessments of MBIIs should incorporate formal mediation analyses within a precision medicine framework to clarify how neurophysiological, psychological, and contextual factors interact to influence clinical outcomes. By moving beyond descriptive associations toward explanatory modeling, such studies could illuminate the mechanistic pathways underlying MBII effects on depressive disorders.

Challenges of included studies and future directions

One of the primary challenges in applying mindfulness-based interventions to depressive disorders is the lack of understanding of the underlying mechanisms that contribute to their therapeutic effects. Although immersive technologies such as VR show promise in enhancing mindfulness practice, the physiological and psychological processes that drive their outcomes remain unclear128,130. Future research should explore how MBIIs interact with neural networks involved in mood regulation and cognitive control. Integrating neuroimaging techniques and biomarkers in future studies could help clarify these mechanisms and inform the development of more targeted, personalized interventions for individuals with depressive symptoms110.

Furthermore, despite growing evidence supporting the efficacy of MBIIs, their adoption in clinical settings remains limited due to barriers such as high costs, technological complexity, and a lack of clinician training41. Research should focus not only on the effectiveness of MBIIs but also on improving their accessibility and ease of use for both patients and healthcare providers. This could involve developing lower-cost VR systems, simplifying user interfaces, and creating standardized sessions for clinical use107,122.

A further concern is the uncertainty regarding whether participants truly acquire mindfulness skills through immersive technology121,141. Self-reports and scales may indicate improvements in mindfulness and psychological well-being142,143, but these measures often overlook real-time changes occurring during the intervention143. To address this issue, future research should focus on determining whether participants are actively aware of the phased changes in their psychological processes, such as mindfulness, emotion regulation, and self-awareness, during training.

Several methodological challenges persist in the design of clinical trials54,125, including small sample sizes107,130, short durations109,116, and a lack of control groups128. Future studies should aim to include larger, more diverse sample sizes to improve the generalizability of findings across various settings and populations. Furthermore, studies with longer follow-up periods are necessary to assess the long-term effects of MBIIs on depressive symptoms41,51, as many interventions in the current literature have been limited to follow-up periods of 6 weeks or less103,128. Additionally, the inclusion of well-matched control groups is essential to isolate the effects of immersive technologies from placebo effects or other confounding factors128,141. Variability in participant characteristics and the reliance on self-reported measures introduce potential biases into the findings106,129. Future studies should incorporate objective physiological measures, such as HRV and GSR, to provide more accurate assessments of the effects of MBIIs on depressive symptoms and emotional regulation.

Furthermore, this review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the search strategy was confined to a specific scope, which may have introduced a bias toward published evidence, potentially overlooking relevant unpublished studies or gray literature. Second, a substantial proportion of included studies was identified through supplementary manual searches using Google Scholar via forward and backward citation tracking, enhancing coverage of early-stage, interdisciplinary, and technology-oriented research not fully indexed in major databases. However, reliance on these manual searches may indicate limited sensitivity of the initial database search due to heterogeneous terminology in immersive digital mental health research and could introduce selection bias, reducing reproducibility. Third, while efforts were made to include a wide range of immersion-related terms, the review did not fully address the growing body of literature on game-based mindfulness interventions, particularly serious games. These approaches have gained significant theoretical and empirical support in the mental health field, demonstrating their potential to enhance engagement and therapeutic outcomes144,145. Fourth, the review did not sufficiently address the integration of online and virtual communities in mindfulness interventions. While previous studies have systematically reviewed web-based mindfulness programs146, the potential of integrating virtual spaces, such as multiplayer environments or social VR platforms, remains underexplored. Fifth, given the ongoing debates about the effects of VR on mental health, this review highlights both the benefits and challenges of incorporating immersive technologies into mindfulness practices49,55. While VR has shown promise in improving engagement and adherence, concerns about its scalability, cost, and potential adverse effects, such as motion sickness, remain unresolved. Sixth, this review employed a systematic review method without conducting a meta-analysis. The included studies exhibited substantial heterogeneity in their experimental designs, outcome measures, and participant characteristics, which precluded a meaningful meta-analysis. Conducting a meta-analysis under such conditions could have led to misleading conclusions due to the inability to adequately account for these differences. Finally, the discussion in this review may reflect some bias due to the limited evidence available from the included studies.

In conclusion, this review summarizes the potential of MBIIs for treating depressive disorders. We began by exploring the developmental history of MBIIs and providing essential background knowledge, including fundamental concepts and evaluation metrics. We then organized the performance evaluation of MBIIs, critically analyzing experimental setups and findings. We also outlined the challenges and proposed directions for future MBII research. Importantly, regarding depression-specific outcomes, the reviewed evidence suggests that MBIIs are associated with reductions in depressive symptom severity across both clinically diagnosed depressive disorders and subclinical depressive symptoms, although the magnitude and consistency of effects vary across studies. Several trials reported improvements on standardized depression scales, while others observed symptom reductions primarily in individuals with elevated depressive symptoms rather than formally diagnosed disorders, highlighting potential differences in responsiveness across participant groups. Future research should focus on elucidating underlying neural mechanisms and biomarker analyses, developing standardized intervention protocols, and conducting cost-effectiveness evaluations to support broader clinical application of MBIIs for depressive disorders. By addressing the gaps and challenges identified in this review, researchers and clinicians can work toward developing more effective and accessible interventions, fully realizing the potential of MBIIs to improve mental health outcomes.
