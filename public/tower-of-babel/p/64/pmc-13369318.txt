Interleukin-6 and leukemia inhibitory factor differentially modify embryonic disk composition in post hatching bovine embryos†
Authors: Mary A Oliver, Vitor R G Mercadante, Alan D Ealy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProper formation of the embryonic disk is essential for pregnancy survival. This study examined whether supplementing interleukin-6 (IL6) or leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) influence epiblast (EPI) and hypoblast (HYPO) development as well as other facets of post-hatching bovine embryo development in an extended culture system. A series of three studies were completed where in vitro-produced (IVP) embryos were treated with IL6 or LIF from days 5–12, 5–7, or 7–12 post-fertilization. Survival and hatching were monitored from days 7–12, then embryos were fixed for immunofluorescent assessments. Non-treated IVP embryos were transferred at day 7 and collected on day 12 post-estrus (IVP-ET) for use an in-utero reference for the days 7–12 culture group. Neither IL6 nor LIF affected survival or hatching. Supplementing IL6 from days 5–12 increased EPI cell number and reduced HYPO formation, whereas LIF had no effect. Supplementing IL6 from days 7–12 produced similar responses, and EPI cell numbers matched those of the IVP-ET day 12 embryos. By contrast, supplementing IL6 from days 5–7 had no effects, indicating that IL6 exposure is unnecessary before blastocyst formation but required afterward to elicit these outcomes. In conclusion, IL6 supplementation after blastocyst formation enhances EPI development to levels comparable to in utero embryos, although this may occur at the expense of HYPO formation. It was equally intriguing that LIF did not mimic the effects of IL6 but rather failed to exhibit any noteworthy effects on the EPI and HYPO lineages.Keywords: bovine, blastocyst, embryonic disk, interleukin-6, epiblast, hypoblast

Introduction

Approximately one-third to one-half of pregnancies in lactating dairy and suckling beef cattle are lost within the first month of gestation [1–3]. Pregnancy losses are especially common during the second week of pregnancy, when critical developmental events occur. These include differentiation of the inner cell mass (ICM) into epiblast (EPI) and primitive endoderm (PE) lineages, formation of the hypoblast (HYPO), yolk sac, and finally primitive streak formation and initiation of gastrulation within the embryonic disk (ED) [4–6]. During this period, the blastocyst, commonly termed the conceptus by this timepoint, undergoes profound morphological transitions, progressing from a spherical to an ovoid morphology and ultimately an elongated, filamentous structure by days 14–16 of gestation [7, 8].

A fundamental challenge in studying these developmental milestones is that most of these events cannot be recapitulated outside of the uterus. Developing in vitro-based systems to investigate these processes is essential, especially when considering that post-transfer pregnancy losses occur at a greater incidence among in vitro-produced (IVP) bovine embryos than embryos recovered from superovulated cattle [9–12]. The use of IVP embryos continues to grow in the dairy and beef industries. In 2024, an estimated 2.0 million IVP embryos were produced worldwide from oocytes harvested from genetically elite heifers and cows, which is more than six-fold great than the number of cattle embryos produced by superovulation [13]. The substantial time and financial investment required to produce these embryos underscores the need to better understand how to enhance their developmental competence after they reach the blastocyst stage at days 7–8 post-fertilization.

This work builds upon previous efforts to develop culture-based systems for studying embryo development after its formation at days 7–8 post-fertilization. Earlier studies largely focused on recreating the sequence and timing of conceptus elongation observed in utero, testing approaches such as agarose gel tunnels, agarose layers, collagen gel matrices, and glucose- and serum-rich media [14–17]. These methods often enhanced proliferation and/or survival of the trophectoderm (TE), the lineage responsible the morphological changes during elongation, although the conceptus elongation phenotype has remained difficult to replicate in vitro. Moreover, these systems have failed to generate ED and yolk sac structures that resemble those present in utero. Given that ED integrity is likely essential for pregnancy success, recent advances in culturing bovine embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) have inspired us and others to consider media formulations that can support ED and yolk sac development, allow bovine embryo survival to day 15 and in some cases up to day 23 in vitro [18–22]. However, even these extended culture approaches have produced small, disorganized EDs with incomplete HYPO formation and migration, extensive apoptosis and necrosis within the ED, and an absence of conceptus elongation [18–22].

Our efforts to improve upon these extended embryo culture systems focused on describing whether supplementing interleukin-6 (IL6) or leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) could enhance ED development, specifically EPI and/or HYPO development, during extended culture. Previous work from our laboratory and others has established that IL6 supplementation during the blastocyst stage increases ICM cell numbers, in cattle, mice, and pig blastocysts [23–29]. In cattle, IL6 also increases both EPI and PE cell numbers in day 9 bovine blastocysts, and IL6 was recently identified as necessary for establishing and maintaining a bovine PE stem cell line [30–32]. We also chose to examine LIF because of its well-established roles in maintenance of naïve ESCs [33, 34] and its ability to mimic at least some of the beneficial effects of IL6 on the bovine ICM, EPI, and PE [31, 35]. We focused our analyses to development through day 12 to evaluate how these cytokines influence blastocyst survival, EPI and HYPO development, and overall morphology before the stage when conceptuses normally transition from spherical to ovoid forms. Focusing on day 12 embryos also allowed us to collect age-matched IVP embryos that were transferred to the uterus and flushed on day 12 for direct comparison.

Methods

Animal use and reagents

All animal procedures and experimentation were completed with the approval of the Virginia Tech Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC; Protocol #23-085). Mature Angus-based commercial cows (n = 18) with similar body condition were maintained on pasture and provided ad libitum access to orchard-grass hay and mineral supplement at the Virginia Tech Kentland Beef Center (Blacksburg, VA, USA). The abattoir-derived ovaries were approved for use (Cadaver Protocol #24-238) and were purchased from Brown Packing Inc (Gaffney, SC, USA). All reagents were purchased from Thermo Fisher Inc. (Waltham, MA, USA) unless specified otherwise.

Experimental design

Three separate studies were completed to describe how supplementing IL6 and LIF for different periods before and after blastocyst formation influenced EPI and HYPO development after extended embryo culture. The first study treated embryos from days 5 to 12 with either 20 ng/ml of IL6 or LIF. The second study only applied treatments from days 5 to 7 and then embryos were cultured in N2B27 medium from days 7 to 12 without treatment. In the third study, IL6 and LIF were administered from days 7 to 12 and were compared with non-treated IVP blastocysts that were transferred to recipients at day 7 and recovered at day 12.

In vitro embryo production

In vitro-produced embryos were generated as described previously [36] by harvesting cumulus-oocyte-complexes (COCs), maturing COCs in oocyte maturation medium (TCM-199 containing Earle’s Salts, 10% [v/v] fetal bovine serum (FBS), 25 μg/ml bovine follicle stimulating hormone [Bioniche Animal Health Canada Inc., Belleville, Ontario, Canada], 2 μg/ml estradiol [Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA], 22 μg/ml sodium pyruvate, 1 mM L-alanyl-L-glutamine [Glutamax], 50 ng/ml recombinant human epidermal growth factor, and 25 μg/ml gentamicin sulfate). Fertilization was completed 22–24 h post-maturation by using a pool of semen from three Holstein bulls (1 × 106 spermatozoa/ml fertilization medium) (Select Sires, Plain City, OH, USA). The beginning of fertilization is considered day 0 post-fertilization. After co-incubation with sperm for 14–18 h, presumptive zygotes were denuded by vortexing, then cultured in 45 μl drops of modified synthetic oviduct fluid (SOF) formulation termed SOF bovine embryo 1 (SOFBE1; 20 μg/ml essential amino acids [Sigma-Aldrich], 10 μg/ml nonessential amino acids [Life Technologies], 4 mg/ml fatty acid free bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich), and 25 μg/ml gentamicin sulfate [Life Technologies]) in groups of 25 zygotes/drop overlaid in mineral oil (Cooper Surgical, Trumbull, CT, USA) [37]. Embryos were incubated at 38.5°C in 5% CO2, 5% O2, and 90% N2 in a humidified chamber until day 7 post-fertilization, when blastocyst formation, stage, and quality grading was completed according to the International Embryo Technology Society guidelines [38]. Normal cleavage rates (82.85 ± 1.01%) and day 7 blastocyst rates (24.59 ± 1.67% of presumptive zygotes) were achieved in each study (see Supplemental Table S1 for details).

Extended embryo culture

The extended culture medium used is termed N2B27 (1:1 mix of DMEM/F12:Neurobasal with N2 and B27 supplements, 100 U/ml Penicillin, 100 μg/ml Streptomycin, 10 mM non-essential amino acid mix, and 0.55 mM 2-Mercaptoethanol) [18]. On day 7, grades 1 and 2 early, regular, expanded, and hatching/hatched blastocysts were selected (see Supplemental Table S1 for stage distributions), washed in HEPES-SOF, then N2B27, and groups of 5–10 blastocysts were placed into 500 μl N2B27 medium covered in mineral oil (1–2 wells/treatment; 4–5 replicates/study) in non-adherent 4-well Nunc plates [18]. Blastocysts were moved to new 500 μl of N2B27 medium supplemented with their respective treatment every 48 h. Embryos were cultured until day 12 (5 days in extended culture). Degenerate embryos were identified by being collapsed and containing pyknotic or karyorrhectic nuclei and/or cytoplasmic blebbing. These degenerate embryos were not moved into new medium on days 9 and 11. Viable day 12 embryos were fixed and processed for immunofluorescence as described below.

Cytokine reconstitution and supplementation

Concentrated stocks of recombinant human IL6 and LIF (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) were prepared in SOF base (embryo culture) or DMEM/F12 base (extended culture) stock solution with 1% [w/v] BSA. Stocks were stored in single-use aliquots at −80°C for no more than 3 months. Cytokine stocks were thawed and diluted to 200 ng/ml, and 5 μl of the cytokine was added to 45 μl drops of SOFBE1 for treatment during embryo culture, and 50 μl of the diluted cytokine was added to 450 μl of N2B27 during extended culture to achieve a final concentration of 20 ng/ml and a total volume of 500 μl. Controls received either 5 μl (day 5–7) or 50 μl (all extended culture studies) of carrier only (SOF + 1% BSA (embryo culture); DMEM/F12 + 1% BSA (extended culture).

Embryo transfer and collection

Two replicate embryo transfer (ET) studies were completed (April/May 2024 and January/February 2025). Each replicate study used IVP blastocysts generated using the same methods as described above. At day 7 post-fertilization, groups of 10 regular and expanded blastocysts (grades 1 and 2) were placed into pre-warmed EmCare Holding Medium (ICPbio Reproduction; Spring Valley, WI, USA), loaded into 0.25 ml ET straws (AgTech Inc., Manhattan, KS, USA), placed in a portable incubator (WTA Technologies, College Station, TX, USA), and transferred into the uterine horn ipsilateral to the functional CL. All transfers occurred within 4 h after ET straw loading.

Estrus was synchronized in mature Angus-based beef cows (n = 18) using the 7&7 synchronization protocol [29, 39]. Cows received an intravaginal controlled internal drug releasing device (CIDR; 1.38 g progesterone; Zoetis, Parsippany, NJ, USA), an intramuscular (IM) injection of a prostaglandin F2alpha (PGF2α) agonist (25 mg; Lutalyse; Zoetis, Parsippany, NJ, USA), then received an IM injection of a gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist (100 μg; Cystorelin; Merial, Duluth, GA, USA) 7 days later. After another 7 days, CIDRs were removed and another IM PGF2α agonist injection was provided. Estrus activity was monitored 48 and 66 h after CIDR removal, and cows were given an IM injection of a GnRH agonist 66 h post-CIDR removal. Estrus activity was monitored visually [40]. Cows detected in standing estrus within a 12 h period were used as ET recipients at day 7 post-estrus (n = 18 cows total). Transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS; Ibex EVO II, South Pasadena, FL, USA) was performed to confirm the presence of a functional corpus luteum (CL) immediately before ET. For ET, epidural anesthesia (5–7 ml lidocaine hydrochloride, 2% [w/v] solution; Vedco, St. Joseph, MO, USA) was administered into the lumbosacral foramen, then ET was performed by an experienced technician to transfer blastocysts into the uterine horn ipsilateral to the functional CL.

On day 12 post-estrus (5 days post-ET), recipient cows were provided epidural anesthesia (5–7 ml lidocaine hydrochloride, 2% [w/v] solution) and blastocysts were non-surgically flushed by inserting a Foley catheter into the uterus and flushed with Biolife Advantage Complete Flush Medium (Ag Tech Inc., Manhattan, KS, USA). Embryos were captured using EMCON filters (MAI Animal Health, Elmwood, WI, USA), fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and transported back to the laboratory on ice for further processing.

Immunofluorescence

All antibody host species, concentrations, and vendors are listed in Supplemental Table S2. Day 12 blastocysts (n = 20–42 blastocysts/treatment; 4–5 replicates/experiment) were fixed and processed for EPI and HYPO markers as previously described, with modifications [18]. Embryos were fixed in 4% [w/w] paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, then permeabilized in 0.25% Triton-X for 30 min at room temperature before blocking for 1 h in 10% horse serum at room temperature. Embryos were incubated overnight in antibodies to detect EPI (SOX2), HYPO (SOX17 or GATA6), and TE (CDX2) at 4°C (see Supplemental Table S2 for details). Strong nuclear SOX17 staining was observed in the HYPO cells for embryos cultured from days 7 to 12, but both nuclear and cytoplasmic SOX17 staining was observed in the embryos transferred at day 7 and recovered at day 12 (IVP-ET group). Confirming that these cells were indeed HYPO cells was accomplished by GATA6 staining, which contained a nuclear-localization in both extended cultured and IVP-ET embryos. Also, SOX17 and GATA6 co-localization was observed to verify that either marker was appropriate for detecting the HYPO lineage (Supplemental Figure S1). A subset of non-treated day 12 embryos cultured in N2B27 were stained with isotype control IgG antibodies to serve as negative primary antibody controls (Supplemental Figure S2). Embryos were incubated with the respective secondary Alexa-conjugated antibody (Life Technologies, Supplemental Table S2). Embryos that were not stained for CDX2 were incubated in DAPI for 5 min before washing in PBS-PVP. All embryos were flattened on glass slides with a thin layer of petroleum jelly and imaged with an ECHO Revolve Epifluorescence Microscope and associated ECHO Pro Software (ECHO, San Diego, CA, USA).

Embryo diameter was calculated by averaging of two measurements of each embryo using ECHO Pro Software. The presence or absence of EPI (SOX2+) cells and HYPO cells (SOX17+ for IVP embryo, GATA6+ for IVP-ET embryos) were also determined. The number of EPI and HYPO cells in embryos that contained either lineage was counted using the CellProfiler cell image analysis software (BROAD Institute; Cambridge, MA, USA). The EPI:HYPO cell ratio was then calculated. The degree of HYPO migration along the inner border of the TE from the animal (EPI-adjacent) to vegetal (opposite) pole was calculated by tracing the total area of SOX17+ or GATA6+ cells and dividing it by the area of DAPI+ or CDX2+ cells using ECHO PRO software. Day 12 embryos not containing either lineage were not included in the cell counting or ratio data sets.

In experiment 2, a representative group of day 7 blastocysts (n = 27–33 blastocysts/treatment; 5 replicates) were fixed and stained to quantify ICM and TE cell number using DAPI and CDX2 staining as described above (Supplemental Figure S3) (see Supplemental Table S3 for details on blastocyst stages).

Statistical analyses

Studies were analyzed using the Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLIMMIX) (SAS v9.4, Cary, NC, USA) to examine the effects of supplementing IL6 or LIF on EPI and HYPO cell number, HYPO migration, EPI:HYPO ratio, and embryo diameter. Binary outcomes, including day 7 blastocyst development, day 12 EPI presence, and day 12 HYPO presence were analyzed using a binomial distribution with a logit link function. Study replicate was included as a fixed effect when significance (P ≤ 0.05) and otherwise was treated as a random term. Repeated measures analyses were completed to examine the effects of IL6 and LIF on hatching and survival across time. For this, a logistic regression model using Firth’s penalized likelihood method was employed to assess overall main effects of IL6, LIF, and day. Subsequently, selective days where hatching or survival was less than 100% (i.e., partial response observed) were analyzed using the GLIMMIX procedure with a binomial distribution (days 7–10 for hatching; days 7–12 for survival). The analysis of multiple comparisons was adjusted using Tukey’s method. Statistical significance was declared at P ≤ 0.05.

Results

Supplementing IL6 or LIF prior to and during extended culture in N2B27 medium

Previous work identified that beginning IL6 or LIF supplementation at day 5 post-IVF increases ICM cell numbers in blastocysts at day 8 [26, 35]. This led us to propose that benefits in ED size and organization would be observed by supplementing IL6 or LIF from days 5–7 in SOFBE1 and again from days 7–12 while being cultured in N2B27 (Figure 1). A majority of day 7 blastocysts transferred from SOFBE1 at day 7 into N2B27 medium were categorized as regular or expanded blastocysts (Supplemental Table S1). Representative images of day 12 embryos that were harvested and immunostained are provided (Figure 1A–C). Assessments for hatching (Figure 1D) and visual signs of survival (Figure 1E) were completed daily between days 7 and 12. There were no effects of treatment on hatching or survival within any day, but overall effects of day were observed, with increases in the percentage of hatched blastocysts from days 8 and 9 (P ≤ 0.0001) and days 9 and 10 (P = 0.04) (Figure 1D) and decreases in survival were observed from days 11 and 12 (P ≤ 0.0001) (Figure 1E).

Supplementing IL6 and LIF from days 5–12 alters EPI and HYPO allocation in extended cultured bovine embryos. Embryos were cultured from days 5–7 in standard culture conditions supplemented with 20 ng/ml IL6 or 20 ng/ml LIF and moved to N2B27 medium supplemented with either 20 ng/ml IL6 or 20 ng/ml LIF from days 7–12. Shown are hatching from days 5–12 (Panel A), survival from days 5–12 (Panel B), embryo diameter (Panel C), EPI presence (Panel D), EPI cell number (Panel E), HYPO presence, (Panel F), HYPO migration (Panel G), HYPO cell number (Panel H), and EPI:HYPO ratio (Panel I). Different superscripts indicate statistical differences within each graph (P ≤ 0.05) and shown are means and standard error of the means (SEM). Scale bar = 570 μm.

Several developmental endpoints were examined in embryos that survived to day 12 in culture. No effects of IL6 or LIF were detected when examining embryo diameter (Figure 1F) or the percentage of embryos containing EPI cells (Figure 1H). There was, however, an effect of IL6 supplementation from days 5–12 on EPI cell numbers at day 12, where IL6 treatment increased (P ≤ 0.0001) EPI cell numbers when compared to controls and LIF-supplemented embryos (Figure 1I). No LIF effect was noted for EPI cell numbers. A reduction (P ≤ 0.03) in the percentage of day 12 embryos containing HYPO cells was detected when comparing IL6-supplemented embryos with the controls and LIF-supplemented embryos (Figure 1I). A reduction in the degree of HYPO migration along the inner border of the blastocoel cavity was detected when comparing IL6 with LIF-supplemented embryos (P = 0.02) and when comparing IL6-supplemented with control blastocysts (P = 0.01) (Figure 1J). No differences in HYPO migration were detected between the controls and LIF-supplemented embryos, and no treatment differences in HYPO cell number were detected (Figure 1K). An increase (P ≤ 0.0008) in the EPI:HYPO cell ratio was observed in IL6-supplemented embryos (Figure 1L).

Supplementing IL6 or LIF prior to extended culture in N2B27 medium

A second study determined if supplementing IL6 or LIF only from days 5–7 post-fertilization was sufficient to influence EPI and HYPO development at day 12 (Figure 2). To achieve this, embryos were treated at day 5 with IL6 and LIF while being maintained in their original SOFBE1 culture drops, harvested from each treatment group at day 7, and placed into N2B27 without cytokine supplementation. A subset of blastocysts was collected at day 7 to determine if supplemental IL6 and LIF altered ICM and TE cell number. No treatment differences were observed in blastocyst development, total cell number, TE cell number, ICM cell number, or distribution of blastocyst stages (Supplemental Table S3).

Shown are hatching from days 7–12 (Panel A), survival from days 7–12 (Panel B), embryo diameter (Panel C), EPI presence (Panel D), EPI cell number (Panel E), HYPO presence, (Panel F), HYPO migration (Panel G), HYPO cell number (H), and EPI:HYPO ratio (Panel I). Different superscripts indicate statistical differences within each graph (P ≤ 0.05) and shown are means and standard error of the means (SEM).

As in the previous study, there were no treatment effects on embryo hatching or survival at day 12, but effects of day were detected for embryo hatching or survival, with hatching increasing (P ≤ 0.0001) from days 8 and 9 and survival decreasing (P ≤ 0.0001) from days 11 and 12 (Figure 2A and B). No effects of IL6 or LIF treatment were observed for hatching or survival rates. No effects of IL6 or LIF supplementation were noted for day 12 embryo diameter, the presence and number of EPI cells (Figure 2C–E). There were also no effects of treatment on the presence of HYPO cells and HYPO migration (Figure 2F and G). Although no differences in HYPO cell numbers were detected when comparing the IL6 or LIF-supplemented embryos with the control group, an increase (P = 0.02) in HYPO cell numbers was detected when comparing the IL6 and LIF treatment groups (Figure 2H). This change in HYPO cell number did not affect the EPI: HYPO cell ratio (Figure 2I).

Supplementing IL6 or LIF to embryos in N2B27 medium

The goal of the third experiment was to determine if supplementing IL6 or LIF influences ED development when provided from days 7 to 12, and to describe whether these developmental outcomes at day 12 were compared with IVP embryos that were transferred into recipient cows at day 7 and recovered at day 12 (termed IVP-ET embryos). Representative images that contain the various degrees of embryo sizes within each treatment group at day 12 are provided in Figure 3. The percentage of blastocysts placed into culture from days 7 to 12 that underwent hatching was not affected by treatment although increases in the percentage of hatched blastocysts were observed from days 8 to 9 (P ≤ 0.0001) and 9 to 10 (P = 0.01) but on thereafter (Figure 4A). Embryo survival in culture was also not affected by treatment, but there were effects of day, with decreased survival in all groups from days 10 and 11 (P = 0.04) and days 11 and 12 (P ≤ 0.0001) (Figure 4B).

The various day 12 endpoint measures observed from those embryos cultured from days 7–12 were compared with the IVP-ET embryos harvested from the uteri of recipient cows at day 12. No treatment differences in embryo diameter were detected among the embryos cultured from days 7 to 12, but the diameters of each group of cultured embryos were smaller (P ≤ 0.05) than the day 12 IVP-ET embryos (Figure 4C). No differences were detected among any treatments in the percentage of embryos containing EPI cells (Figure 4D). For those embryos that contained EPI cells, IVP-ET and IL6-supplemented embryos contained equivalent EPI cell numbers, whereas numbers were reduced (P ≤ 0.0001) in the controls and LIF-supplemented embryos (Figure 4E). No differences in the percentage of embryos containing HYPO cells were observed (Figure 4F), but in those embryos that contained HYPO cells, the percentage of HYPO migration along the inner border of the blastocoel cavity was less (P ≤ 0.03) for the IL6-supplemented embryos than the IVP-ET and LIF-supplemented groups (Figure 4G). No differences were observed in HYPO cell number (Figure 4H) and EPI:HYPO ratio (Figure 4I) among any of embryo groups.

Discussion

Advancements in extended embryo culture systems in recent years have allowed for partial ED formation and maintenance [18–22], and this permitted us to explore ED development outside of the uterus, thus limiting confounding effects of uterine secretions and animal variation. The modifications made in these media formulations to permit embryo survival and ED development were selected based on media components used to maintain ES and iPSC cultures [18, 19, 22]. This, indeed, facilitated embryo survival and ED development, although these embryos fail to undergo conceptus elongation. Another limitation exists in maintaining embryo survival in extended culture. Work presented herein and by others observed that a subset of embryos do not survive to day 12 and that embryo survival continues to be compromised if cultures continue after day 12 [18]. A likely explanation is that our system, like others, remains deficient in critical uterine components required for embryo survival. The N2B27 formulation used herein produced fewer embryo losses than a culture medium formulation based on bovine uterine histotroph profiles [19], suggesting that the use of the N2B27 media formation brings us closer to, yet does not fully recapitulate, an ideal system for extended embryo culture. It is interesting that we observed that the proportion of embryos failing to survive to day 12 is roughly similar to the magnitude of IVP embryo losses when transferred at day 7 and recovered at days 14–16 [29, 41]. This could indicate that these extended culture systems may be a very good model for exploring the etiology of pre- and peri-elongation pregnancy losses in cattle.

Arguably, the most notable finding from this work was detecting an increase in day 12 EPI cell numbers following IL6 supplementation in day 12 embryos. Previous work in our laboratory identified IL6-dependent improvements in EPI cell numbers by day 9 when culturing in SOFBE1 medium [30]. It was not surprising that IL6 only produced this positive EPI cell effect when it is supplemented from day 7 onward. Much of our previous work describing the actions of IL6 on ICM development was completed by supplementing IL6 from days 5 to 8 post-IVF [26–29], but it is becoming clear that supplemental IL6 cannot influence ICM cell numbers until after a definitive ICM has formed at the blastocyst stage. This work and work from another set of studies [30] failed to detect any positive effects on the ICM or EPI cells when supplementing IL6 only from days 5–7. We also have recently found that IL6 supplementation from days 5–8 post-IVF has positive effects on ICM cell numbers in expanded/hatched blastocysts but not in regular blastocysts [29, 35]. It usually takes 24–36 h for IVP blastocysts to begin expanding [42], thus the expanded/hatched blastocysts were provided sufficient time for IL6 to exert its positive effects on the ICM, whereas an insufficient amount of exposure time was available to blastocysts that formed later during IVP.

The actions of embryo-derived IL6 are best described in the mouse, where TE-secreted IL6 acts on the ICM to promote development [43]. A similar effect occurs in human blastoids, where TE cells secrete IL6, and this acts through signal transducer and activation of transcription 3 (STAT3) to support primed epiblast-like cell proliferation [44]. There also is evidence in cattle that either embryo-derived IL6 is required for normal embryo development in cattle. Loss-of-function editing of the IL6 signal transducer receptor (IL6ST), the β-receptor subunit shared by most IL6 family members, reduced bovine IVP embryo development, producing fewer blastocysts, and the blastocysts that were generated from this mutation contained smaller, disorganized ICMs [45]. This IL6ST-targeting will prevent actions of any IL6 family members produced by the bovine embryo, so we cannot definitely state that these actions occur solely because of the lack of IL6 action, but it seems reasonable to predict that embryo-derived IL6 is at the very least involved with multiple developmental events occurring before and after blastulation [23].

The lack of effect of IL6 supplementation on HYPO cell numbers observed herein is not consistent with previous work by our laboratory, where IL6 supplementation increased HYPO cell numbers in SOFBE1-cultured blastocysts [30]. A recent report also identified IL6 as a key supplement for the generation and maintenance of bovine-induced extraembryonic endoderm (iXEN), an equivalent cell type to the HYPO cells we are describing [32]. Rather, in this work there were some indications that IL6 supplementation may hinder HYPO development, although we failed to consistently identify a specific endpoint measurement that was altered in both the days 5–12 and days 7–12 studies. Observing the negative effect of supplemental IL6 on the presence of HYPO cells only when IL6 is provided from days 5–12 is intriguing because it may indicate that IL6 can limit and/or delay HYPO emergence if provided early in development. We acknowledge this explanation is highly speculative, and that a more reasonable explanation is that IL6 signaling must occur in the presence of other critical HYPO lineage specifying signals to see a true reflection in how IL6 influences the HYPO lineage. The fibroblast growth factor (FGF)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathway is a key component of HYPO cell determination [46, 47]. Specifically, supplementing FGF2 to bovine blastocyst outgrowths increases HYPO cell lineage expansion [46–49]. Also, successful bovine iXEN cultures contain IL6, FGF4, bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4), a WNT inhibitor (XAV939), and a TGFβ inhibitor (A83-01) [32], thus a prediction is that one or more of these additional signals are be needed for IL6 to exert its true actions on HYPO development. In fact, the large variation in HYPO cell numbers (<100 to >2000 HYPO cells/embryos) observed in these studies suggests that differences in each embryo’s inherent ability to generate HYPO cells and/or promote HYPO migration exist, and these intrinsic signals may include one or more of the signaling systems mention above.

This work provides evidence that LIF supplementation does not have any positive effects on cultured bovine embryos. We and others have described increases, decreases, or no effect of LIF supplementation on IVP bovine blastocyst formation [15, 35, 50, 51], and several reports failed to detect beneficial effects of LIF in extended embryo cultures [19, 52, 53]. With that being said, the current findings are different from our previous work, where we describe that LIF supplementation increased both EPI and HYPO cell numbers in bovine embryos cultured in SOFBE1 medium [31]. There are several interpretations that may explain this differential response to LIF. The SOFBE1 medium is clearly inferior to N2B27 at maintaining embryos in extended culture; therefore, it is quite possible that LIF is acting as a survival factor when blastocysts are cultured in SOFBE1 medium. This could also explain why supplementing IL6 also improved HYPO cell numbers in SOFBE1-cultured blastocysts [30, 31] but had either no effect or a negative effect on HYPO development when supplemented to N2B27 medium. Another possible explanation for the lack of detectable LIF effects is that only a single LIF concentration was tested. This was intentional as it allowed us to compare LIF with IL6 using recombinant proteins that had equivalent biological activities. The previous SOFBE1 work used 15-times greater amounts of LIF to observe positive effects on HYPO cells [31]. However, this previous work used a recombinant bovine protein that was not rigorously tested to ensure high biological potency, so it is not possible to make any firm conclusions on LIF dose requirements, and it certainly seems possible to consider that greater LIF concentration could produce different outcomes in this extended embryo culture system. Nonetheless, it certainly appears that any actions that LIF may have on bovine embryos are vastly overshadowed by the actions of IL6 between days 7–12 of development.

The final study explored whether the strategy of supplementing IL6 or LIF could generate blastocysts that resembled those developing within the uterus between days 7–12. Our primary rationale for using IVP-derived embryos for this in utero work was to minimize variation in embryo source, although we concede that the IVP-ET embryos may not entirely represent day 12 embryos derived entirely in vivo. The most exciting outcome of this extended culture versus in utero development comparison was observing that IL6 supplementation was successful at increasing EPI cell numbers to where they were comparable to the IVP-ET embryos. This is noteworthy given that previous comparisons between cultured embryos with either in vivo-developed or IVP-ET embryos found that cultured embryos lag in their development and have reduced EPI cell numbers [18, 21, 52, 54]. This IL6 supplementation scheme may, therefore, provide a means for reducing the high incidence of small and undetectable EDs that exist in IVP bovine conceptuses as they undergo elongation [6, 9, 10, 55, 56]. We and others have not described whether IL6 supplementation before ET improves post-transfer pregnancy rates, but we have observed benefits of supplementing IL6 on early conceptus development and post-implantation development of the embryo-proper and fetus [27, 29]. Therefore, the mechanism(s) that IL6 uses to improve EPI development could be key to understanding how to improve IVP embryo survival after transfer.

Two concerns were evident when comparing the IL6-supplemented embryos with the in utero-developed embryos. First, although not statistically significant, it is concerning to observe a numerical reduction in HYPO migratory activity and HYPO cell numbers after IL6 exposure during extended culture. This could reflect delays in HYPO development that eventually will adversely affect yolk sac development and promote pregnancy loss [57, 58]. The second concern was observing reductions in embryo diameter in extended culture embryos. Other reports have also observed this outcome [18, 19, 52, 54]. Our speculation is that although the N2B27 enables EPI and HYPO development, it does not provide adequate support for TE cell development. This lack of TE support is also seen in studies where embryos are cultured beyond day 12, where no elongation/filamentation occurs even with culture up to day 23 [18, 19, 22, 52, 54]. This is not surprising given that achieving conceptus elongation in cultured bovine embryos has not been possible, but this and other observations do suggest that promoting ED development in extended cultures is not the only corrective measure needed to achieve elongation during culture. Missing uterine secretory signals must be identified for this to be achieved.

Conclusion

By employing an extended bovine embryo culture system, we were able to uncover a new mechanism of IL6 action on early development of the EPI cell. Ensuring proper EPI cell development is indispensable for pregnancy success, so this finding provides us with new opportunities to explore mechanisms at play for controlling EPI development. We also uncovered a surprising contrary finding, where IL6 potentially compromises HYPO cell development during this pre-conceptus elongation phase of development. This outcome is not consistent with what we have observed in other work, but nonetheless, it indicates that the benefits seen for IL6 on the EPI cell lineage may occur at the expense of HYPO formation. Lastly, there is clear evidence that LIF cannot replicate the actions IL6 at this stage of development.
