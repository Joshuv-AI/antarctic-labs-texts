Stochastic human exposure-risk modelling and environmental health implications of potentially toxic elements in the Mubuku irrigation scheme, Uganda
Authors: Godswill J. Udom, Brian Turyahabwe, Daniel N. Obot, Omoirri M. Aziakpono, Joseph G. Omole, F. Faez Saad, Alisher Abduvokhidov, Uchkun Kutliev, Khursheed Muzammil, Nita-wills G. Udom, Hope Onohuean, Orish E. Orisakwe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Potentially toxic elements (PTEs) contamination of agro-ecosystems poses significant threats to food safety and public health. Study investigated the occurrence, sources, and human health risks of PTEs (As, Pb, Cd, Co, Cr, Hg, Ni, Cu, Fe, Mn, Se, Zn, Th, and V) in irrigation water (n = 1), agricultural soils (n = 9 composite samples), and vegetables (onion, cabbage, and amaranth; n = 36 composite samples) collected in August 2024 from the Mubuku Irrigation Scheme, western Uganda. Elemental concentrations were determined using inductively coupled plasma–optical emission spectroscopy. Health risks were assessed using hazard quotient, hazard index (HI) and total cancer risk (TCR) models, complemented by Monte Carlo simulation (MCS; 10,000 iterations) to address exposure variability and uncertainty. More than 20% of detected PTEs in vegetables exceeded WHO/FAO limits. Arsenic (2.22–3.57 mg/kg), lead (2.02–3.66 mg/kg), and mercury (0.42–0.61 mg/kg) consistently exceeded guideline values (0.1, 0.3, and 0.001 mg/kg, respectively), with amaranth as the highest accumulator. Irrigation water contained elevated As (1.56 mg/L) and Pb (2.73 mg/L). Estimated HI values indicated considerable non-carcinogenic risks (HI > 1). TCR and MCS exceeded acceptable thresholds (10⁻⁶–10⁻⁴), highlighting probable lifetime cancer risks and the need for targeted risk management interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53403-9.

Introduction

Chemical pollution by potentially toxic elements (PTEs) has emerged as a critical global environmental and public health concern, particularly in regions experiencing rapid urbanization, industrial growth, and agricultural intensification1. PTEs are naturally occurring constituents of the Earth’s crust; however, their geochemical cycling and biogeochemical equilibrium have been profoundly disrupted by anthropogenic activities. Owing to their persistence, non-biodegradability, and bioaccumulative behavior, PTEs readily accumulate in environmental matrices such as soils, surface and groundwater, and biota, resulting in long-term ecological degradation and human health risks. It is estimated that more than 200 million people worldwide are exposed to metal(loid) concentrations exceeding World Health Organization (WHO) safety thresholds, primarily through contaminated food and water pathways2.

Globally, PTE contamination arises from both natural and anthropogenic sources; however, human-driven activities—including mining and smelting, industrial emissions, wastewater discharge, fossil fuel combustion, and the extensive application of agrochemicals—are now the dominant contributors3,4. Once released, metal(loid)s persist in environmental compartments and enter food chains through plant uptake, leading to trophic transfer and chronic exposure in human populations. This challenge is particularly acute in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where limited environmental monitoring infrastructure, rapid land-use change, and weak regulatory enforcement exacerbate contamination risks5. In sub-Saharan Africa, irrigation-dependent agricultural systems are especially vulnerable, as they often rely on surface waters impacted by upstream mining, industrial, and urban activities, as well as diffuse agricultural inputs such as fertilizers, pesticides, herbicides, and fungicides6.

At the regional scale, East Africa faces growing pressure from PTE contamination linked to expanding agriculture, mining, and urban development. Irrigated agro-ecosystems in this region serve as critical food baskets for rapidly growing populations, yet they are frequently exposed to contaminated water sources and soils7. Uganda exemplifies this challenge. The country’s agricultural productivity and food security are increasingly threatened by contaminants of concern (CoCs), including toxic metal(loid)s originating from industrial waste streams, agricultural runoff, domestic effluents, artisanal and large-scale mining4,7,8, and atmospheric deposition from vehicular and industrial emissions. Despite the recognized importance of irrigation for crop production, systematic evaluations of metal(loid) contamination and associated human health risks in Ugandan irrigation schemes remain limited.

The Mubuku Irrigation Scheme (MIS), located in Kasese District in western Uganda, represents a high-priority system for environmental health assessment. The scheme is one of Uganda’s most productive agricultural zones, supporting the cultivation of widely consumed vegetables such as onion, cabbage, and amaranth. Irrigation water is primarily sourced from the River Sebwe, which originates in the Rwenzori Mountains and traverses the Kilembe copper mining area—an area historically associated with elevated PTE discharges7. Previous studies have reported elevated metal(loid) concentrations in rivers within the Rwenzori region, including the Mubuku, Nyamwamba, and Rwimi rivers, underscoring their vulnerability and importance as water sources for both irrigation and domestic use4,8. These river systems contribute to the contamination of soils and agricultural produce, thereby increasing the potential for dietary exposure among local communities6,9. Evidence of PTE accumulation has already been documented in fruits and vegetables grown in Kasese, including detectable levels of cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), and lead (Pb) in soils, irrigation water, and edible plant tissues6.

Human exposure to PTEs occurs primarily through ingestion of contaminated food and water, inhalation of contaminated particulates, and dermal contact with polluted media10. Dietary intake, particularly through vegetables, accounts for a substantial proportion (up to 50%) of total metal intake in many populations10. Chronic exposure to toxic metal(loid)s has been linked to a wide range of adverse health outcomes, including neurotoxicity, organ damage, carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, endocrine disruption, and genotoxic effects11–14. Given their long biological half-lives and limited excretion pathways, metal(loid)s can accumulate in critical organs, amplifying health risks over time. Consequently, increasing PTE contamination in food systems has intensified public concern over food safety, nutritional security, and long-term health sustainability13.

Despite the agricultural and socio-economic importance of the MIS, comprehensive data on PTE occurrence, exposure pathways, and human health risks remain scarce. More importantly, no study to date has applied stochastic (probabilistic) risk assessment frameworks to evaluate metal(loid)-related health risks in irrigation-based agro-ecosystems in East Africa, including Uganda. Existing studies have largely relied on conventional deterministic approaches, which generate single-point risk estimates and fail to capture the inherent variability and uncertainty associated with contaminant concentrations, dietary intake rates, and population characteristics15. Such approaches may underestimate or overestimate true population-level risks, limiting their usefulness for evidence-based decision-making.

Recent advances in environmental health risk assessment emphasize the integration of deterministic and probabilistic methods to better characterize uncertainty and variability in exposure scenarios. Monte Carlo simulation (MCS)-based probabilistic models provide risk distributions rather than point estimates, allowing for more realistic evaluation of exposure ranges, high-end risk scenarios, and sensitive population subgroups9,16,17. However, the application of these stochastic tools to irrigation-linked food systems in sub-Saharan Africa remains extremely limited, representing a critical methodological and regional knowledge gap.

Against this backdrop, the present study aims to address this gap by integrating environmental monitoring with deterministic and probabilistic human health risk assessment in the MIS. Specifically, the study: (1) quantified concentrations of selected PTEs in irrigation water, agricultural soils, and commonly consumed vegetables; (2) evaluated non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic health risks for exposed populations using United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) models; (3) applied Monte Carlo simulation to assess variability and uncertainty in exposure and risk estimates; and (4) identified dominant exposure pathways to inform risk mitigation and sustainable management strategies. By introducing a stochastic risk assessment framework to a Ugandan irrigation system, this study provides novel insights and critical baseline data to support risk-informed policy, targeted remediation, and food safety protection in Uganda, other LMICs, and similar agro-ecological settings globally.

Materials and methods

Study area

MIS is located in the Nyamwamba Division of Kasese Municipality, western Uganda, approximately at latitude 00°15′41″ N and longitude 30°07′27″ E (Fig. 1). The scheme covers an estimated area of ~ 2,000 acres and represents one of the most important irrigation-based agricultural systems in the Rwenzori region. The MIS supports intensive cultivation of vegetables and fruits—including cabbage, onions, amaranth, spinach, tomatoes, mangoes, watermelon, pumpkin, eggplant, citrus, banana, and pineapple—supplying food to an estimated population exceeding one million people (of which many are farmers and small business owners) across Kasese, Fort Portal City, Kabarole, Bunyangabu, Bushenyi, Sheema, and Rubirizi districts18.

The scheme is irrigated primarily by water abstracted from the River Sebwe, a tributary originating in the Rwenzori Mountains and flowing through the historically active Kilembe copper–cobalt mining area. The region is characterized by mineralized bedrock, intense weathering, and legacy mine tailings, which together contribute to elevated background levels of metal(loid)s in surface waters and soils. In addition to geogenic inputs, anthropogenic pressures—including artisanal mining residues, agricultural runoff, and urban effluents—further increase the vulnerability of the MIS to potentially toxic element (PTE) contamination. These characteristics make the MIS a suitable case study for assessing irrigation-linked contamination and human health risks.

Sampling technique and collection

The study area was systematically partitioned using a stratified, uniform grid-based sampling design to ensure spatially representative coverage of cultivated zones within the MIS. Sampling locations were selected based on irrigation zones, crop types, and proximity to potential contamination sources. Crop cultivation areas were identified through consultations with MIS management and field reconnaissance. Although sampling was conducted during a single season, it coincided with peak irrigation activity, representing a conservative exposure scenario. At the time of sampling (August 2024), onions, cabbage, and amaranth were the dominant crops and were therefore selected for analysis. The sampling area was georeferenced using a handheld Global Positioning System (GPS), and the cultivated fields were divided into grids. The entire MIS was overlaid with 1 km × 1 km square grids, each further subdivided into four equal cells (500 m × 500 m). A total of 18 grids, comprising 72 cells, were generated across the sampling area. Within each grid, two cells were randomly selected for sampling, resulting in 36 sampling cells. Randomization was performed using a computer-based random number generator implemented via the RAND() function in Microsoft Excel, ensuring unbiased spatial selection. The coordinates of the sampling points were recorded for traceability19. At the crop sites (onions — Site 1; cabbage — Site 2; amaranth — Site 3), a total of 36 composite vegetable samples were collected (i.e., one from each sampling cell). Each composite sample consisted of multiple sub-samples harvested within the same grid cell, pooled to account for within-site variability. Each composite sample consisted of five (5) spatially distributed sub-samples collected within a 10 m × 10 m micro-plot at each sampling cell. The sub-samples were obtained from the four corners and the center of the plot and homogenized thoroughly to form a single representative composite sample. Samples were placed in sterile, air-tight polyethene Ziplock bags and transported under cooled conditions to the laboratory. Following a systematic zig-zag sampling pattern within each cell, soil samples were collected in triplicate from each site at three depth intervals (i.e., 0–5 cm, 6–10 cm, and 11–15 cm), representing topsoil (n = 3), mid-soil (n = 3), and subsoil layers (n = 3), respectively. Samples from each layer were homogenized into composite samples (n = 9). Samples were collected using an acid-cleaned stainless-steel auger and stored in pre-labeled polyethylene bags. Irrigation water (n = 1) was collected from the MIS reservoir, representing the primary point-source abstraction from the River Sebwe. A single sample was collected due to the uniform source and continuous flow of irrigation water during the sampling period. Water was collected into a 250 mL sterile glass bottle pre-treated with 5% HNO₃ and rinsed thoroughly with deionized water20. All samples were collected between 07:00 and 08:00 h to minimize diurnal variability. Administrative permission was obtained from MIS authorities before sampling.

Preparation and digestion of samples

In the laboratory, crop and soil samples were desiccated with an oven (GenLab Limited, UK) regulated at 90–100 °C, and homogenized. Approximately 2 g of each dried sample was digested using a concentrated acid mixture of HNO₃–HCl (3:1, v/v; Sigma Aldrich, USA) following a modified wet digestion protocol21. Digestion was conducted on a Stuart hot plate with magnetic stirrer (SB152; Stuart Scientific, UK) until a clear solution was obtained, indicating complete mineralization. The digest was allowed to cool, then filtered through Whatman 125 mm filter paper and diluted to 50 mL with deionized water. Water samples were acidified with ultrapure HNO₃ (pH < 2) and stored at 4 °C before analysis.

Instrumental analysis of PTEs

Quantification of PTEs was performed using an Agilent 5800 Inductively Coupled Plasma–Optical Emission Spectrometer (ICP-OES; Agilent Technologies, USA). This is a sensitive instrument known to detect contaminants with concentrations ≤ 10 µg/L. Calibration standards (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 2.5, and 5.0 mg/L) were prepared from a NIST-standard reference material (SRM® 2711a). Calibration curves were generated using internal standards. For each PTE, R2 > 0.998 was maintained to ensure linearity and reproducibility. Instrument operating conditions included radiofrequency power of 1450 W, plasma gas flow rate of 15 L/min, auxiliary gas flow rate of 0.2 L/min, equilibration time of 15 s, and read delay of 45 s. The performance of the ICP-OES method was verified based on the obtained limits of detection (LODs) and quantification (LOQs), extraction recoveries, relative standard deviations (RSDs), and linear ranges. The other ICP-OES operating set-up was done according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Element-specific wavelengths were selected to minimize spectral interference22(Table 1), and the results were expressed in ppm (equivalent to mg/kg and mg/L). Analyses were conducted in triplicate at the Institute for Medical Research, Yaba, Lagos, Nigeria.

Quality assurance and control (QA/QC)

Strict QA/QC protocols were followed throughout sampling and analysis. All reagents were of analytical grade, and glassware was acid-washed and rinsed with deionized water. Procedural blanks, duplicate samples, and spiked samples were analyzed alongside field samples. The accuracy and precision of the analysis were assessed using duplicate samples and standard reference material (SRM® 2711a). Recovery rates within ± 15% and RSDs ≤ 5% were considered acceptable. Continuing calibration verification (CCV) standards were analyzed after every 20 samples to verify calibration stability and to avoid interference from residue. Limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were determined at 3 and 10 times the standard deviation of the blanks, respectively. Instrument stability was ensured by a minimum 30 min warm-up period, and analyses were conducted under controlled laboratory temperatures (20–25 °C).

Analysis of data

Descriptive statistics were computed using SPSS (version 26; IBM, USA), with results expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Source identification and apportionment using principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to identify potential sources of PTEs. The dataset included five matrices (cabbage, amaranth, onions, soil, and water) and 14 PTEs. Components with eigenvalues ≥ 1 were retained, and factor loadings were classified as strong (> 0.75), moderate (0.75–0.50), or weak (< 0.50)23. A positive score indicates that the variable’s (PTE’s) concentration rises along the PC axis, whereas a negative score indicates that the metal’s concentration falls along the axis. Conversely, a score close to 0 indicates that the concentration has a poor (linear) relationship with the PC axis. PCA was performed using RStudio24.

Health risk assessment

Health risk assessment (HRA) was performed following both the deterministic and stochastic approaches. The deterministic HRA was done by calculating the chronic daily intake (CDI), target hazard quotient (THQ), hazard index (HI), cancer risk (CR), and total cancer risk (TCR) of carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic metals. The parameters used for the HRA are shown in Table 2. All exposure and risk equations are harmonized with the USEPA (2004–2023) guidance, with assumptions clearly described and referenced (Supplementary file).

Probabilistic cancer risk estimation

To enhance the robustness of risk estimation, a probabilistic Monte Carlo simulation (MCS) framework was implemented using Oracle Crystal Ball (version 11.1.2.4.850). A total of 10,000 iterations were performed to propagate variability and uncertainty in exposure parameters15–17. Input variables were assigned probability distributions based on empirical data and literature values, with selection guided by goodness-of-fit tests (Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Anderson–Darling statistics)15. Lognormal distributions were applied to contaminant concentrations due to their right-skewed nature, while physiological parameters such as body weight and ingestion rates were modeled using normal distributions. Triangular distributions were used where limited data were available (see Table X). Variability (e.g., dietary intake, body weight) and uncertainty (e.g., measurement error, toxicity factors) were explicitly incorporated into the model. The input parameters and their assigned probability distributions used are summarized in Table 3.

Random sampling was conducted for each parameter across iterations, generating a distribution of risk estimates rather than single-point values. Output metrics included percentiles (5th, 50th, and 95th), which represent low-end, median, and high-end exposure scenarios, respectively. Uncertainty propagation was achieved through repeated random sampling of input distributions across all iterations. This approach allows the combined effects of parameter uncertainty and population variability to be reflected in the output risk distributions. The resulting probabilistic outputs therefore provide a more realistic representation of exposure scenarios compared to deterministic estimates. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using rank correlation coefficients to quantify the contribution of each input variable to the variance in cancer risk estimates. The results were visualized using tornado plots, enabling identification of dominant risk drivers.

Results

Concentration of PTEs

Fourteen metal(loid)s were detected across soil, vegetable, and irrigation water samples obtained from the MIS, with concentrations reported consistently in mg/kg (dry weight) for soils and vegetables and mg/L for irrigation water (Table 4). In vegetables, mean concentrations followed crop-specific accumulation patterns. For onions, concentrations increased in the order Cd < Cr < Co < Hg < Se < Ni < Cu < Pb < Zn < As < Fe < Mn < V < Th, while cabbage and amaranth showed similar but distinct hierarchies, with amaranth consistently exhibiting the highest metal burdens. Mean concentrations of As, Pb, and Hg in onions, cabbage, and amaranth ranged from 2.10 to 3.57 mg/kg, 2.02–3.66 mg/kg, and 0.29–0.61 mg/kg, respectively, exceeding WHO/FAO maximum permissible limits (MPLs) of 0.1 mg/kg (As), 0.3 mg/kg (Pb), and 0.001 mg/kg (Hg). Irrigation water showed elevated concentrations of As (1.56 mg/L), Pb (2.73 mg/L), Cu (0.84 mg/L), Co (0.03 mg/L), Mn (0.23 mg/L), V (0.28 mg/L), and Zn (0.23 mg/L), all surpassing WHO/FAO guideline values (i.e., 0.1, 0.065, 0.05, 0.017, 0.02, 0.1 and 0.2 mg/L for As, Pb, Cu, Co, Mn, V, and Zn, respectively) for irrigation water.

Source identification and apportionment of PTEs

PCA provided insight into the potential sources and pathways of PTE contamination (Fig. 2). The PCA eigenvalues, factor loadings, and correlation are presented in Tables S2-S3 (Supplementary file). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 83.2% of the total variance, indicating strong explanatory power. PC1 (49.5%) was dominated by Cd, Cr, Pb, Co, Fe, and Mn, while PC2 (33.7%) was strongly associated with As, Ni, Cu, Zn, V, and Th. While Se concentration was the major contributor to PC3, with a factor loading of 0.925. The PCA Biplot (PC1 vs. PC2) revealed that all metals except Se and Hg had long vector lengths along Dim1 or Dim2 axis, showing a high quality of the two selected PCA dimensions. As regards the type of samples, soil, amaranth, and cabbage were found to be on the positive side of PC1 and PC2 (Fig. 2). The highest scores corresponded to soil and amaranth samples.

Health risk assessment

Non- carcinogenic risks

Tables 5 and 6 show the CDI (oral), THQ, and HI of the PTEs assayed and computed for adult males, females and the geriatric population. From the result, elements with high CDI were Ni, V, Pb, Fe, Zn, and Mn. Also, the result revealed HI values > 1 for all vegetables and all population groups, indicating an unacceptable level of chronic health risk. Amaranth posed the highest risk, with HI values (males – 6.88; females – 6.75; elderly – 5.13), followed by cabbage (males – 2.18; females – 2.34; elderly – 2.23), and onions (males – 2.13; females – 2.30; elderly – 2.19). The dominant contributors were As with THQs ranging from 1.25 to 2.82, V (0.15–2.04), and Hg (0.30–0.76), exceeding safe thresholds.

Carcinogenic risks

Deterministic cancer risk estimates showed that TCRs via oral ingestion exceeded both acceptable (10⁻⁶) and priority (10⁻⁴) thresholds for all vegetables and population groups (Table 7). TCR values ranged from 7.74 × 10⁻⁴ to 2.63 × 10⁻³, indicating a probable lifetime cancer risk associated with long-term vegetable consumption. Furthermore, the TCR following dermal exposure to soil was determined to be 1.18 × 10− 4 and 1.27 × 10− 4 in adult males and females, respectively. These values were above the priority (moderately) and the tolerable risk thresholds. As and Ni were the largest contributors, with individual TCRs for As reaching ~ 2.54 × 10⁻³.

Stochastic (MCS) cancer risk estimation

The MCS results (Table 8, Fig. S1-S2 (Supplementary file)) revealed substantial variability and uncertainty in cancer risk estimates across three exposure scenarios (i.e., best case (5th percentile), median (50th percentile), and worst case (95th percentile) and population subgroups (i.e., adult males, females and geriatrics). Median (50th percentile) and upper-bound (95th percentile) risks for onions ranged from 5.97 × 10⁻⁴ to 9.11 × 10⁻⁴, while cabbage (1.08 × 10− 3 to 1.50 × 10− 3) and amaranth showed markedly higher values. Notably, in the elderly, all cancer risk estimates (5th, 50th and 95th percentile) for cabbage exceeded the acceptable risk range. Amaranth presented the greatest concern, with 95th percentile risks reaching 4.16 × 10− 3, 4.24 × 10− 3, and 1.49 × 10⁻¹ for adult males, females, and the elderly, respectively, indicating extreme vulnerability among high-end consumers. Even the best-case scenario (5th percentile – 2.62 × 10− 3) risks for amaranth exceeded acceptable thresholds, underscoring persistent exposure risk regardless of consumption variability. Sensitivity analysis identified As, Cd, and Ni as the most influential variables driving cancer risk uncertainty (Table 8).

Discussion

This study represents the first documented integration of deterministic and stochastic human health risk assessment approaches to evaluate exposure to PTEs in environmental matrices within the Ugandan context. By integrating stochastic modeling, this work advances beyond descriptive exposure assessment, providing a more robust characterization of uncertainty and population-level variability in health risks within one of Uganda’s most intensively cultivated irrigation schemes. The research pioneers a dual-model approach that is increasingly recommended for environmental health assessments in low- and middle-income countries, where exposure heterogeneity and data uncertainty are often high.

Notably, our findings record a relative abundance of PTEs varied by matrix and crop type, reflecting differences in environmental availability, uptake mechanisms, and source contributions. The elevated concentrations of As, Pb, and Hg in vegetables (cabbage, amaranth, and onions), all exceeding WHO/FAO MPLs (i.e., 0.1, 0.3, and 0.001 mg/kg, respectively), reflect the combined influence of geogenic and anthropogenic sources within the MIS. These exceedances indicate a serious food safety concern, particularly for leafy vegetables such as amaranth. The Kasese region is characterized by metal-rich lithologies associated with the Rwenzori Mountains and historical mining activities4. Weathering of mineralized rocks and dissolution of metal-bearing sulfides can release As, Cu, Ni, and Pb into surface waters and soils, particularly under acidic conditions35,36 common in tropical environments. Geologically, the area is dominated by Pleistocene to recent sediments, alluvium, black soils, and moraines from the Cainozoic era34, and are commonly associated with the presence and potential release of PTEs. These materials act as both sinks and sources for metal(loid)s like Cd, Zn, Pb, Cu, and As, which can be remobilized into the environment through natural weathering or human-induced changes in soil chemistry, such as acidification. Therefore, the elevated concentrations of analyzed PTEs observed in soils and vegetables are partly attributable to geogenic inputs, especially as these lithologies are recognized natural sources of transition metals through progressive chemical weathering, soil formation, and sediment transport processes. Soils in the MIS and Kasese region are predominantly sandy loam and clay loam soils, generally classified as Andosols34. They are typically acidic, with pH values often falling into the strongly acidic range (pH < 5.5) in cultivated and grazing lands. This acidity increases the bioavailability and mobility of geogenic PTEs like As, Cd, Hg, Ni, and Pb, posing a significant risk of accumulation in the food chain. Low soil pH enhances the solubility of these elements, making them more readily available for absorption by crops and other organisms. Irrigation return flows and seasonal flooding further redistribute these geogenic metals across agricultural fields, facilitating their accumulation in soils and crops.

Furthermore, anthropogenic inputs substantially amplify this background burden. The widespread application of inorganic fertilizers, pesticides, and herbicides — often unregulated or applied at excessive rates — introduces additional loads of Pb, As, Cd, and Hg into agricultural soils. Agrochemicals are known to contain metal impurities, including toxic metals37,38, and repeated application leads to gradual enrichment of soils and increased bioavailability to crops. This aligns with the observations of Alengebawy et al39., who reported a direct correlation between the use of pesticides and fertilizers and the contamination of crops with toxic metal(loid)s. These findings are consistent with reports from other African irrigation systems, including studies from Ethiopia40,41, Nigeria9,42,43 and Ghana44,45, which similarly link intensive agrochemical use and contaminated irrigation water to elevated metal levels in vegetables, underscoring the regional nature of this problem. Compared with findings of Sulaiman et al46.., the As and Pb concentrations observed in the present study are markedly higher than the previously reported ranges of 0.19–0.43 mg/kg and 0.03–0.05 mg/kg, respectively, and are clearly above the MPLs set by the WHO and FAO. Furthermore, levels of Cr, Cd, Cu, Ni, Zn, Fe, and Mn in cabbages were higher than those reported by Sultana et al47.., although lower than concentrations reported by Guerra et al48. and Najmi et al49., reflecting site-specific variability influenced by agricultural practices, soil geochemistry, and irrigation sources. Based on these comparisons, therefore, the present study indicates a comparatively greater contamination burden in the MIS.

The disproportionately high metal(loid) concentrations observed in amaranth relative to cabbage and onions highlight important crop-specific bioaccumulation mechanisms. Leafy vegetables such as amaranth possess higher transpiration rates, larger surface areas, and greater metal-binding capacity in leaf tissues, making them particularly efficient accumulators of metals from both soil and irrigation water. This finding aligns with previous studies9,43 showing that leafy vegetables represent a critical exposure pathway for dietary metal intake. The accumulation of metals in edible plant tissues directly links environmental contamination to human exposure through the food chain, especially in subsistence and peri-urban farming systems where dietary diversity is limited. Soil acts as a long-term reservoir for metals, while contaminated irrigation water serves as a continuous input, reinforcing a soil–water–plant transfer cycle. Without intervention, this cycle promotes progressive accumulation and increases chronic exposure risks over time. Thus, the MIS represents not only an agricultural system but also a persistent exposure nexus with implications for food security and public health.

Our findings also revealed that the concentrations of As, Pb, Cu, Mn, and Zn in irrigation water exceeded the MPLs set by the WHO/FAO (i.e., 0.1, 0.065, 0.02 and 0.2 mg/L, respectively). This contamination is likely due to geogenic and anthropogenic sources such as mining and agrochemical runoff. These findings suggest that irrigation water represents a continuous source of PTE input into agricultural soils, reinforcing soil–water–plant transfer processes.

From a toxicological perspective, though legal mining activities at the Kilembe copper mines ended in 1982, their legacy remains. As noted by previous studies38,50,51, mining contributes significantly to metal enrichment in surface waters, which are eventually taken up by crops via irrigation. Similar conclusions were drawn by Rad et al52. and Zamora-Ledezma et al53., who emphasized the contribution of agricultural runoff to metal loading in irrigation systems. Additionally, soil erosion during rainfall events likely facilitates the transport of these metals into surface water bodies, compounding the contamination risk54. The primary water source for the MIS in Kasese is the River Sebwe. This river and its tributaries have elevated concentrations of toxic metals, potentially due to mining and geological weathering. Furthermore, an alternate water source to augment Sebwe for the irrigation scheme is the River Nyamwamba. Both water bodies are directly impacted by mining activities, particularly the Kilembe copper mine. The Nyamwamba River is a major water source in the Kasese district, and the Kilembe mine has been reported to be a major source of toxic metal(loid)s in the river55, impacting water quality and potentially posing environmental and human health risks.

Nevertheless, the PCA source apportionment showed that PC1 was dominated by Cd, Cr, Pb, Co, Fe, and Mn, elements commonly associated with geogenic sources and mining-related activities, particularly sulfide mineral weathering and mine tailings dispersion. This aligns with the geological context of the Rwenzori region and the historical Kilembe copper–cobalt mining operations. PC2 was strongly associated with As, Ni, Cu, Zn, V, and Th, suggesting a mixed anthropogenic–geogenic signature. These elements are frequently linked to agrochemical inputs, vehicular emissions, and irrigation water contamination, as well as natural mineral dissolution. The clustering of soil and amaranth samples along positive PC1 and PC2 axes indicates that soil acts as both a sink and a secondary source of these PTEs, while leafy vegetables efficiently accumulate available PTEs from both soil and water. Similar PCA-based source apportionment studies in the US56, Nigeria57, Cameroon58, and Kenya59 in irrigation systems have attributed Cd–Pb–Cr groupings to mining and lithogenic inputs, while As–Ni–Cu associations are often linked to agricultural inputs and contaminated irrigation water. The present results reinforce these interpretations and highlight the synergistic role of legacy mining and modern agricultural practices in shaping contamination profiles in MIS.

The elevated HI values (HI > 1) across all adult groups suggest a high likelihood of adverse health outcomes, including neurotoxicity, renal impairment, cardiovascular dysfunction, and endocrine disruption, particularly under long-term exposure scenarios. The elderly population exhibited slightly lower HI values due to lower consumption rates, but remains highly vulnerable due to reduced physiological detoxification capacity. Pb and As emerged as the dominant contributors to overall risk, evidenced by their THQs, which consistently exceeded one in all environmental matrices. These findings are consistent with reports from Ethiopia41, Nigeria42,43, Bangladesh47, and Brazil48, where Pb and As were identified as primary drivers of dietary non-cancer risk. Mechanistically, these noxious elements disrupt essential physiological processes by inducing oxidative stress, inhibiting enzyme activity, impairing mitochondrial function, and interfering with endocrine and nervous system signaling. Chronic exposure has been widely associated with cardiovascular dysfunction (e.g., hypertension, conduction disorders, and heart failure), neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., dementia, Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s), renal dysfunction, and a spectrum of reproductive disorders, including infertility and menstrual irregularities38,60,61, while children are particularly susceptible to neurodevelopmental deficits, cognitive impairment, and immune dysregulation62–64. They have also been linked to growth retardation, immunosuppression, cognitive impairment, and multiple cancers in children65–67. Furthermore, Pb exposure has been associated with anemia, delayed speech and language development in toddlers and preschool children, seizures, and reproductive toxicity in adolescents and adults63,66,68]. Prolonged mercury and arsenic exposure pose risks of congenital anomalies, neurodevelopmental disorders, osteoporosis, and chronic kidney disease69–74. These health effects are largely driven by the ability of PTEs to interfere with essential physiological and biochemical pathways (e.g., enzyme inhibition), impair detoxification processes, and generate reactive oxygen species74.

Based on expert opinion, we assert that in clinical practice, the impact of metal exposure on health is often misdiagnosed or entirely missed, especially as the onset of toxicity is insidious, with nonspecific clinical manifestations, and a lack of widespread knowledge of the concept of environmental medicine among most practitioners. For instance, confusion in the elderly is often misdiagnosed as Alzheimer’s disease75. In the geriatric population, an imbalance between cholinergic and dopaminergic stimulations in the striatum causes an array of neurological pathologies, including Parkinson’s disease76. By inactivating or inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, toxic metals (e.g., Hg, Pb, etc.) potentiate the accumulation of acetylcholine, leading to an imbalance in the levels of acetylcholine and dopamine, hyperstimulation of the cholinergic receptors, continuous neurotransmission, and a range of neurotoxic outcomes. In the same vein, Pb is implicated in the elevation of blood pressure74,77. The etiology of numerous diseases is multifactorial, and toxic metals may not be the major causal factors; however, by targeting the enzyme systems and interfering with their physiology, toxic metal(loid)s and other persistent environmental pollutants should be considered key drivers of the etiopathogenesis and progression of several pathological conditions. This is especially so as these metals are known to inhibit the body’s defense and repair mechanisms, disrupt immune response, and cause oxidative stress38,74. The latter is seen in virtually all human pathologies. Therefore, the complex interplay between environmental exposure and clinical toxicology must be given careful attention during the diagnosis of human diseases.

The calculated TCR values further support a concerning level of public health threat. Major contributors to the recorded TCR are As and Ni, with individual TCRs for As reaching ~ 2.24 × 10⁻³, far exceeding the acceptable safety range (1 × 10⁻⁶ – 1 × 10⁻⁴), and consistent with its classification as a Group 1 human carcinogen. Comparable cancer risk magnitudes have been reported in contaminated agricultural systems in Bangladesh47, Brazil48, Malaysia46, China78, Ghana45 and Nigeria9,79,80, reinforcing the global relevance of these findings. Dermal exposure to soil also contributed to cancer risk, with TCR values exceeding tolerable limits (10⁻⁶), particularly among farmers, who are more likely to engage in soil-contact activities. This highlights the importance of considering multiple exposure pathways in agricultural communities.

Compared with the probabilistic cancer risk estimation, some variations in the numerical values of the risk estimates were observed. Regardless, the deterministic risk estimates recorded in this study corroborate the stochastic risk estimates, as both were on the same trend. These findings are consistent with previous MCS-based studies in contaminated food systems9,23,78–80 and demonstrate the superiority of probabilistic methods in capturing real-world exposure heterogeneity. MCS is a well-established probabilistic method that employs stochastic sampling to model uncertainty and variability in complex systems, and was utilized to estimate the excess lifetime cancer risk (ELCR) associated with the consumption of PTE-contaminated vegetables from the MIS. By simulating 10,000 iterations using probabilistic distributions for input parameters, MCS enables the derivation of a risk spectrum rather than a single deterministic value15,16. Importantly, the probabilistic approach captures the wide variability in exposure scenarios, reflecting real-world differences in consumption patterns, body weight, and exposure duration. Such analysis is particularly critical in environmental health contexts, where small variations in exposure assumptions can significantly influence risk estimates17,81. Moreover, MCS addresses potential biases resulting from over- or underestimation of carcinogenic risk, which may lead to either unwarranted mitigation costs or inadequate public health protection. Endorsed by regulatory bodies like the USEPA82 and the National Research Council83, MCS strengthens the transparency, reliability, and defensibility of risk assessment outcomes. Therefore, integrating MCS into this study not only enhances the robustness of the ELCR estimates but also supports evidence-based policy-making aimed at safeguarding public health and environmental integrity.

The convergence of deterministic and probabilistic risk estimates underscores a serious public health concern in the MIS. Chronic exposure to PTEs through staple vegetables may contribute to increased burdens of neurodegenerative diseases, cardiovascular disorders, renal dysfunction, and cancers, particularly among vulnerable groups such as children and the elderly. Given the affordability and dietary centrality of these vegetables, urgent interventions are required, including routine monitoring, soil remediation, regulation of agrochemical inputs, and identification of safer irrigation water sources. Integrating stochastic risk assessment into regulatory frameworks will be critical for evidence-based decision-making in Uganda and similar LMIC contexts.

Implications of the study

Environmental significance

The detection of elevated metal concentrations in irrigation water highlights the critical role of water quality in shaping downstream exposure risks. Legacy contamination from historical mining activities, particularly the Kilembe copper mine, combined with ongoing runoff and erosion, continues to influence metal loads in the River Sebwe and River Nyamwamba. These rivers function as conduits for metal transport into agricultural soils and crops, reinforcing the need for basin-scale pollution management.

From an environmental health perspective, the findings emphasize the vulnerability of irrigation-dependent agro-ecosystems and the urgent need for integrated risk management strategies. Continuous consumption of contaminated vegetables poses a silent but significant public health burden, particularly among low-income populations who rely heavily on locally produced foods. The use of MCS enhances the credibility and policy relevance of the risk estimates by providing a transparent and defensible basis for regulatory decision-making.

Policy takeaway

This study provides quantitative evidence that irrigation-dependent agriculture in the MIS poses unacceptable non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic health risks, with probabilistic cancer risks exceeding international safety thresholds by up to three orders of magnitude, particularly for As, Cd, and Pb. To mitigate these risks, Uganda’s Ministries of Agriculture and Environment should mandate routine monitoring of irrigation water, soils, and agricultural produce, enforce water-quality standards for irrigation, and prioritize low-cost treatment or alternative clean water sources for farming communities. Integrating environmental health risk assessment into national irrigation and food-safety policies, alongside farmer education and agrochemical regulation, is critical to reducing long-term disease burden and safeguarding food security in vulnerable agro-ecosystems.

Implications for clinical practice

The findings of this study have profound implications for clinical practice, particularly in regions where environmental exposure to PTEs is prevalent due to agricultural activities. The elevated concentrations of toxic metals such as arsenic, lead Pb, and mercury (Hg) in commonly consumed vegetables, and the corresponding high hazard indices and cancer risks in the population studied, underscore an urgent need to integrate environmental exposure assessment into routine clinical evaluation. Healthcare providers should routinely include environmental and dietary exposure histories in patient evaluations, especially for populations presenting with nonspecific chronic symptoms such as fatigue, cognitive impairment, hypertension, renal dysfunction, or unexplained reproductive issues. This will enable early detection of potential toxic metal exposure and timely intervention.

Clinicians in high-risk areas should advocate for and utilize biomarkers of exposure (e.g., blood or urine levels of Pb, As, Hg) in diagnostic workups for patients with conditions potentially linked to metal toxicity. This is particularly critical for vulnerable populations such as pregnant women, infants, and patients with chronic non-communicable diseases. Clinical practice must emphasize patient education regarding food safety and the potential risks of consuming vegetables grown in contaminated irrigation zones. Recommendations on dietary modifications, food sourcing, and proper vegetable washing or blanching techniques can help reduce the risk of metal ingestion.

Health professionals should collaborate with public health agencies to support surveillance and reporting of PTE exposure-related diseases. Clinical data can serve as an early warning system for environmental contamination trends, prompting timely public health interventions. Medical practitioners have a pivotal role in advocating for environmental justice and the right to safe food. Their voices can influence policy by highlighting the clinical burden of PTE toxicity and the long-term healthcare costs associated with untreated exposure. By translating environmental research into clinical action, healthcare systems can better protect at-risk populations, prevent environmentally driven disease burdens, and promote health equity in low-resource settings.

Limitations of the Study

The major limitation is the use of USEPA models and threshold values to assess health risk levels in an African context, particularly given the paucity of agreed-upon maximum acceptable limits and reference doses (RfDs) in Uganda and sub-Saharan Africa. While RfDs provide a standardized benchmark for evaluating non-carcinogenic health risks, their application to African populations warrants caution due to demographic, genetic, and environmental differences. These RfDs are typically derived from toxicological studies based on U.S. or Western populations, with assumptions on average body weight, dietary habits, exposure duration, and metabolic rates that may not reflect conditions in sub-Saharan Africa. African populations may also experience higher baseline exposure to environmental pollutants due to differences in infrastructure, occupational patterns, and dietary reliance on locally grown food, which may increase the cumulative toxic burden. Moreover, the default use of a 70 kg adult body weight in RfD calculations may underestimate risk in populations with lower average body mass, such as children and women in rural regions. These physiological and sociocultural variances suggest that region-specific toxicological parameters should be developed to improve the accuracy of health risk assessments in African contexts. Until such context-specific RfDs are available, MCS can help mitigate uncertainty and provide a more realistic risk characterization.

Secondly, the limited temporal sampling represents a potential limitation, as seasonal variability in irrigation inputs and environmental conditions may influence PTE distribution.

Recommendations for further studies

Longitudinal epidemiological studies should be conducted to link chronic exposure to PTEs in various environmental matrices with disease incidence, especially in vulnerable populations like children, pregnant women, and the elderly. Future studies should explore molecular pathways of metal-induced toxicity and interactions with pharmacological agents, particularly drugs metabolized by cytochrome P450 enzymes. Also, further studies should investigate the extent to which PTEs in vegetables are absorbed into the human body under real gastrointestinal conditions. Nevertheless, interventional studies that evaluate the effectiveness of various soil remediation techniques, phytoremediation strategies, and water filtration methods in reducing metal uptake by plants should be carried out. Finally, future studies should expand the geographical scope and replicate this study in other irrigation schemes and agro-ecological zones across Uganda and sub-Saharan Africa to map regional variations in risk.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling quantitative evidence of significant environmental contamination and associated human health risks linked to selected vegetables from the MIS, Uganda. More than 20% of the analyzed PTEs in edible vegetables exceeded WHO/FAO maximum permissible limits, with As, Pb, and Hg consistently surpassing safety thresholds across onions, cabbage, and amaranth. Deterministic health risk assessment revealed HI > 1 for all population groups and vegetables, suggesting a high likelihood of chronic non-carcinogenic effects, with Pb and As accounting for over 60% of total risk contributions.

The stochastic (Monte Carlo) modeling further demonstrated that lifetime cancer risks substantially exceeded acceptable limits (1 × 10⁻⁶–1 × 10⁻⁴) across all exposure scenarios. The highest risks were associated with amaranth consumption, reaching 4.16 × 10⁻³ (adult males), 4.24 × 10⁻³ (adult females), and 1.49 × 10⁻¹ (elderly) under the worst-case scenario. At the 95th percentile, elderly populations exhibited markedly elevated vulnerability, with cancer risks of 5.37 × 10⁻², 6.27 × 10⁻², and 1.49 × 10⁻¹ for onions, cabbage, and amaranth, respectively. Sensitivity analysis identified As, Cd, and Ni as the dominant risk drivers in adults, while As and Ni were the principal contributors in the geriatric population, underscoring age-dependent susceptibility.

Corroboration between the deterministic and stochastic risk estimates confirms that chronic dietary exposure through irrigation-grown vegetables represents a critical and ongoing public health concern in the MIS. To mitigate these risks, routine monitoring of irrigation water, soils, and crops should be institutionalized, alongside the adoption of water treatment technologies or alternative uncontaminated irrigation sources. Policymakers should enforce national irrigation water quality standards, regulate agrochemical inputs, and embed risk-based environmental assessments into agricultural planning frameworks. Additionally, farmer education and community awareness programs are essential to promote safer agronomic practices and reduce food-chain contamination. Collectively, these measures are critical for protecting vulnerable populations, strengthening food safety, and advancing sustainable agriculture in Uganda and other LMICs facing similar irrigation-related environmental health challenges.
