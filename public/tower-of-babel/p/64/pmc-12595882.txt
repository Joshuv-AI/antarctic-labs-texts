The multifaceted roles of non-coding RNAs in cell-cell communication in cardiovascular health and disease
Authors: Danica Jimenez-Gallegos, Francisco Sigcho, Emiliano Vicencio, Raúl Arias-Carrasco, Sergio Lavandero, Valentina Parra, Vinicius Maracaja-Coutinho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-coding RNAs are critical regulators of cell-to-cell communication in the cardiovascular system, playing pivotal roles in heart development, function, and disease progression. Among these, extracellular vesicles, RNA-binding proteins, and lipoprotein complexes have emerged as key carriers, facilitating the paracrine—and occasionally endocrine—transfer of non-coding RNAs. While extensive evidence supports the role of microRNAs in mediating communication between cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells, the involvement of long non-coding RNAs and their carriers in cardiac intercellular signaling remains understudied. This review explores how non-coding RNA carriers protect these molecules from extracellular degradation and ensure targeted delivery to recipient cells, enabling precise modulation of gene expression. Furthermore, we highlight the gaps in understanding the mechanisms of non-coding RNA uptake and their implications, which can be addressed through advanced sequencing technologies, such as single-cell RNA sequencing. These tools provide unparalleled insights into non-coding RNA-mediated communication networks in the heart, shedding light on their roles in maintaining homeostasis and driving disease. Finally, we contrast the well-established microRNA-driven communication mechanisms with the emerging significance of long non-coding RNAs. We also discuss how sequencing advancements transform our understanding of non-coding RNA-based signaling pathways in cardiovascular health and disease.Keywords: Cell-cell communication, Non-coding RNAs, Non-coding RNA carriers, RNA-binding proteins, Long non-coding RNAs, Cardiovascular diseases

Introduction

The cardiovascular system plays a fundamental role in maintaining homeostasis by enabling the exchange of nutrients and gases between tissues, a process driven by heart contractions. This system relies on intricate cell-to-cell communication (CCC) mechanisms to ensure coordinated function throughout the body. A notable feature of CCC in the cardiovascular system is the heart’s dual role as a paracrine and endocrine organ. In paracrine signaling, cardiomyocytes and fibroblasts secrete growth factors facilitating cardiac tissue repair and regeneration [1]. In endocrine signaling, cardiac hormones such as atrial natriuretic peptide and angiotensin II regulate renal function by exerting diuretic and vasodilatory effects. Additionally, endothelial cells lining blood vessels release nitric oxide, which modulates vascular tone and platelet function [2]. These examples highlight the heart’s and vasculature’s critical role in coordinating physiological and pathological processes throughout the organism. Disruptions in these cell and tissue communication pathways can contribute to the onset and progression of cardiovascular diseases (CVD), including hypertension, atherosclerosis, and heart failure (HF).

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), molecules with little to no coding potential, play critical roles in gene regulation and diverse cellular processes [3]. Despite their frequent association as clinical biomarkers, ncRNAs exert significant regulatory functions within the cardiovascular system. While there is extensive literature on microRNAs (miRNAs) as paracrine messengers, their roles as endocrine molecules are less well-documented (Fig. 1) [4, 5]. Recent research has begun to uncover similar endocrine functions for long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) in CCC [6, 7]. For instance, Li et al. highlighted the characterization of ncRNAs as endocrine molecules, positioning them as novel regulators of metabolism across adipose, pancreatic, skeletal, and hepatic tissues, thereby identifying them as primary secretors of ‘RNAkines’ [8]. Despite this growing body of evidence, the field still faces challenges due to the lack of robust methodologies and techniques for comprehensively identifying and characterizing these ncRNAs.

While significant progress has been made in understanding the roles of ncRNAs, the carriers responsible for their transport remain underexplored [8, 9]. This gap hinders a complete characterization of CCC, including the tissue or cell origin, tissue-specificity, receptor cell recognition, uptake mechanisms, and subsequent cellular responses. Building on the emerging evidence of ncRNAs as paracrine and endocrine mediators, this review explores their intricate roles in cardiovascular cell-cell communication. We emphasize the well-documented miRNA carriers and shed light on the relatively unstudied carriers of lncRNAs, providing a broader perspective on their contributions to paracrine and potential endocrine signaling. Additionally, we underscore the pivotal role of advanced transcriptomic technologies in unraveling ncRNA-mediated communication within the cardiovascular system.

Role of miRNAs in cardiac function and pathology

Non-coding RNAs have emerged as key regulators of CCC within the cardiovascular system, playing critical roles in cardiac function and pathology. Among them, miRNAs post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression by binding to target mRNAs, promoting their degradation or inhibiting translation. In contrast, lncRNAs are versatile regulators that act both pre-and post-transcriptionally [10].

miRNAs are a highly abundant class of non-coding RNAs, typically 22 nucleotides long, that play critical roles in gene regulation. Approximately half of all identified miRNAs originate from introns and, less frequently, exons of protein-coding genes (intragenic miRNAs), while the remainder are transcribed independently from their own promoters (intergenic miRNAs) [11, 12]. miRNA-mediated gene regulation relies on the miRNA-induced silencing complex (miRISC), which consists of a guide strand and the Argonaute 2 (AGO2) protein. When perfect complementarity occurs between the miRNA and its mRNA target at miRNA response elements (MREs), AGO2’s endonuclease activity is activated, leading to mRNA cleavage and subsequent degradation or translational repression [13, 14]. AGO2, recognized as a key regulator of miRNA-mediated repression, has also been identified as a miRNA carrier in various studies [15, 16]. In cases of imperfect miRNA: MRE base pairing, AGO2 recruits the GW182 protein, which interacts with effector complexes, including poly(A)-deadening and decapping enzymes, to promote mRNA degradation. These RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) facilitate miRNA regulatory functions and represent valuable tools for investigating ncRNA-mediated mechanisms of CCC.

miRNAs play fundamental roles in cardiac development and function. For instance, miR-1 and miR-133 are essential for cardiac lineage specification [17]. Sabour et al. identified 217 miRNAs differentially expressed during embryonic mouse heart development, underscoring their potential critical roles in these processes. Notably, 51 of the 217 miRNAs are correlated with transcription factors, of which 24 have been directly linked to heart development and function. Among them miR-1a-3p correlates with Foxp1, Gata6, Hand2, Myocd, Rarb, and Srf, while miR-22-5p correlates with Tbx20 and Gata4 [18].

In the context of CVD, miRNAs are implicated in various pathological processes. For example, 273 differentially expressed miRNAs were identified in experimental cardiac hypertrophy, with 33 miRNAs associated explicitly with this process [19]. Notably, miR-30, miR-29, miR-181, and miR-27 families are involved in the development of cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, functioning as hubs in a network composed of both miRNAs and transcription factors. Furthermore, in HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), 152 differentially expressed miRNAs were detected [20]. Some overexpressed miRNAs, such as hsa-miR-21-3p, have also been implicated in other cardiac pathologies, like cardiac fibrosis [21].

Moreover, miRNAs have been extensively studied in a wide range of CVD, including contractility defects, arrhythmias, myocardial infarction (MI), hypertrophy, and inherited cardiomyopathies [22]. They play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of these conditions and are also valuable biomarkers for diagnosis and prognosis. Various strategies are currently under investigation to modulate miRNA activity to treat CVD. Their multifaceted roles in both the pathophysiology and potential treatment of these diseases highlight the exciting prospects of miRNAs in cardiovascular research. Other comprehensive reviews are available for a more in-depth exploration of miRNAs’ involvement in cardiovascular function and disease [23, 24], providing valuable insights into the complex regulatory networks in which miRNAs play central roles.

LncRNAs in cardiac physiology and disease

LncRNAs are a heterogeneous group of transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides that lack protein-coding potential [25, 26]. In humans, it is estimated that approximately 100,000 lncRNAs may be involved in a wide range of biological processes [27]. However, the specific functional roles of most of these transcripts remain largely unexplored. While lncRNAs are generally poorly conserved at the primary sequence level, they exhibit significant conservation at other levels, such as positional and structural conservation [28]. These molecules are primarily associated with the regulation of gene expression and act at various cellular levels by interacting with DNA, RNA, and protein molecules. Their functions include modulating chromatin structure at the epigenomic level [29], influencing transcription and RNA processing by recognizing targets and recruiting protein machinery during splicing/editing events, contributing to RNA stability at the post-transcriptional level, facilitating translation by recruiting necessary cofactors, and regulating protein activity at the post-translational level [30]. Moreover, lncRNAs have been proposed to play a signaling role due to their presence in extracellular vehicles, suggesting that they may be involved in a novel intercellular communication mechanism, which is described in the next section.

One of the key features of lncRNAs is their tissue-specific expression, which allows them to regulate the development of specific tissues, such as the heart [31–34]. For instance, Jiang et al. stratified cardiac-specific lncRNAs based on its expression levels among human tissues. In this study, they identified 2,353 lncRNAs with cardiac-specific expression, 273 lncRNAs enriched in cardiac tissue, and 4,828 lncRNAs exhibiting at least five-fold higher expression in cardiac tissue compared to other tissues [32]. Additionally, the study by Ramos et al.., using a single-cell transcriptomic approach, highlighted the role of lncRNAs as cell-specific markers for different cell types in distinct chambers of the mice heart, showing their potential role in heart development and cell-type functional definition (Fig. 2b) [34]. Examples of key heart-associated lncRNAs are Braveheart (BVHT) and FENDRR. The first one is associated with cardiac lineage and heart development, expressed at earlier stages during the differentiation of mouse embryonic stem cells into cardiomyocytes [35]. Similarly, FENDRR is another lncRNA involved in heart development, playing a role in regulating chromatin status and cardiac gene expression [36].

In addition to their physiological functions, lncRNAs play a significant role in various CVD, including hypertension [37], coronary artery disease [38, 39], MI [40], atrial fibrillation [41], diabetic cardiomyopathy [42], and HF [43]. They modulate several disease-related biological processes, such as cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, apoptosis, necrosis, autophagy, and fibrogenesis [44]. By participating in these processes, lncRNAs contribute to the progression and pathology of various cardiovascular conditions. In the context of HF, for example, Thomas et al. identified 2,085 lncRNAs in HF right ventricle patients, of which 48 showed differential expression compared to healthy subjects [45]. Similarly, Yang et al. identified 18,480 lncRNAs in left ventricle samples, of which 1,249 were differentially expressed in cardiomyopathy [46]. Furthermore, Jiang et al. identified 357 lncRNAs differentially expressed between normal and CVD samples, with cis-regulatory neighboring protein-coding genes associated with calcium ion channel activity [32]. For instance, MIAT has been associated with cardiac hypertrophy by regulating the miR-93/TLR4 axis, contributing to disease progression [47], while the lncRNA H19 acts as a negative regulator of cardiac hypertrophy by inhibiting CaMKIIδ, highlighting its protective role [48]. These findings emphasize the critical involvement of lncRNAs in CVD and underscore their potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. As research continues to uncover the diverse functions and mechanisms of lncRNAs, their clinical implications in cardiovascular health and disease are becoming increasingly evident. It is worth noting that, although thousands of lncRNAs have been identified as associated with CVDs through bulk RNA-seq technologies, their functions remain largely unexplored, especially in the context of newly described extracellular lncRNAs. Investigating their carriers could provide valuable insights into their roles in cell-cell communication, as some have been identified in both lipid membrane and non-lipid membrane carriers, as detailed in the following sections.

Non-coding RNA carriers in cell-to-cell communication

ncRNAs have emerged as key mediators of CCC, primarily due to their ability to be packaged or associated with both lipid and non-lipid membrane carriers. Among lipid-based carriers, the most extensively studied are extracellular vesicles (EVs), followed by non-lipid carriers such as RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and lipoproteins, mainly high-density lipoproteins (HDL), and to a lesser extent, low-density lipoproteins (LDL) and very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL). These carriers have been described as vehicles for ncRNAs, serving primarily to protect RNA molecules from degradation, facilitate subcellular localization, and, particularly in the case of EVs, enable their secretion and uptake by recipient cells, thereby mediating intercellular signaling.

EVs are enriched in the circulation of patients with CVDs compared to healthy individuals and have been recognized both as biomarkers and as potential therapeutic targets [49, 50]. In addition, multi-omics and computational approaches have provided valuable insights for identifying EV-specific protein surface markers and functional cargo [51–53], as well as into transcriptomic profiling for characterizing their RNA content. This includes mRNAs, small RNAs, primarily miRNAs, processed RNAs (e.g., tRNAs and rRNAs), other and functionally relevant lncRNAs [54–56]. The molecular diversity of this ncRNA-based cargo has significantly enhanced our understanding of EV heterogeneity, positioning EVs as highly specific mediators of CCC. However, this same heterogeneity presents challenges in identifying the specific ncRNA cargo involved in CCC and in advancing their precise clinical application [57].

In parallel, lipoproteins have been identified as alternative non-lipid membrane-based ncRNA carriers in circulation, particularly in the form of HDL-miRNA complexes in CVDs [58]. In contrast to EVs, miRNAs are exported and loaded into extracellular HDL particles by metabolically active cells, such as hepatocytes and pancreatic cells [58, 59], as well as immune cells like macrophages and neutrophils [60, 61]. In CVDs associated with hypercholesterolemia and hyperlipidemia, changes in HDL particle abundance affect the availability and functional delivery of specific miRNAs in circulation [62, 63]. The uptake of HDL-miRNA complexes by endothelial recipient cells has been observed [63–65], and is mediated by the scavenger receptor class B type I (SR-BI) [63, 65]. Other lipoprotein subclasses, such as LDL and VLDL, have also been associated with circulating miRNAs; however, evidence linking them to CCC remains limited [66–68].

Furthermore, RBPs represent a non-lipid membrane-based carrier system. In the context of CVDs, RBPs play a critical role in regulating the localization and stability of ncRNAs [16, 69]. In fact, a significant fraction of circulating ncRNAs has been detected bound to RBPs rather than enclosed within lipid membrane structures [16, 70]. In circulation, miRNA–AGO2 complexes represent the most abundant form of circulating miRNAs in healthy individuals [70]. However, the mechanisms by which AGO2 is exported or released into circulation remain unclear. Recent evidence from extracellular RNA-enhanced crosslinking and immunoprecipitation (exRNA-eCLIP) intersection analyses has expanded this landscape by mapping extracellular RBPs and their associated small RNAs across major human biofluids, including plasma and serum, thus revealing additional RBP candidates beyond AGO2 that may contribute to ncRNA stabilization and transport in plasma [71].

Although miRNA carriers are increasingly recognized as mediators in CCC, significant gaps remain in identifying their cellular origin and uptake mechanisms in CVDs. Notably, lncRNAs, due to their versatile functions and high tissue-specificity, may offer valuable clues for elucidating the cellular sources of ncRNAs. Integrating multi-omics data with carrier-specific profiling could therefore enhance our understanding of ncRNA release and uptake mechanisms in CCC, ultimately contributing to the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting intercellular communication in CVDs.

Transport mechanisms of microRNAs and lncRNAs in cell-to-cell communication

ncRNAs involved in CCC have revealed new mechanisms of gene expression regulation between different cell types and across tissues. Paracrine communication, which enables intercellular signaling through the release of biomolecules that are subsequently captured by receptor cells, is the most well-known mode of cell-to-cell ncRNA transmission [6, 72]. However, the latter event is not always clearly demonstrated [73–76]. In contrast, endocrine communication involves a limited number of miRNAs, and even fewer lncRNAs have been identified as participating in these mechanisms [8, 77, 78].

Although many miRNAs and lncRNAs are present in circulation, little is known about their cellular origin and the specific receptor cells they target. Among ncRNA carriers, lipid membrane-based structures, particularly EVs and exosomes, are the most extensively characterized (Fig. 1a) compared to non–lipid membrane carriers involved in CCC, such as RNA-binding proteins and lipoproteins (Fig. 1b) [16, 79]. To date, no functional protein or lipid surface markers have been identified for EVs with tropism toward specific tissues, and the secretome profile of the heart, including its lipid and RNA-binding protein content, remains largely unknown.

Extracellular vesicles: key players in ncRNAs transport

miRNAs

Under pathological conditions, cardiomyocytes secrete EVs mediating paracrine communication and influencing fibroblast gene expression, contributing to cardiac hypertrophy [80–83]. Thus, a pressure-overload model in a miR-378 (+/-) KO mice shows aggravated cardiac fibrosis despite higher miR-378 expression in cardiomyocytes [80]. In experiments with neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (NRVMs) under mechanical stress, miR-378 was shown to regulate metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) and collagen expression in cardiac fibroblasts via EVs. EV uptake by fibroblasts was assessed using fluorescently labeled cardiomyocyte-derived EVs.

Furthermore, in a study using transgenic, viral, and pharmacological overexpression models of miR-30d, mice subjected to MI showed improved ejection fraction, reduced interstitial fibrosis, lower levels of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), and reduced apoptosis after 3 weeks, compared to controls [81]. In this study, hypoxia-induced NRVMs showed increased levels of pre-miRNA-30d, mature miRNA-30d and EVs-associated miR30d, compared to hypoxia-induced neonatal rat cardiac fibroblasts (NRCF). NRCF treated with NRVM-derived EVs overexpressing miRNA-30 showed decreased proliferation and downregulation of integrin α5, a protein subunit involved in cell adhesion and migration, even in the presence of Transforming Growth Factor-β (TGF-β). Both the in vivo and in vitro effects were reversed by administering a locked nucleic acid (LNA) anti-miR-30d, a chemically modified RNA molecule that inhibits specific miRNAs. Remarkably, CCC was demonstrated in the in vivo MI model by tracking EV-mediated transfer using a recombinant cardio-specific Cre-expressing system and a Cre reporter (membrane GFP). This approach allowed the identification of cardiomyocyte-derived EV release, as EVs carried functional Cre protein and activated GFP expression in recipient cells. EV uptake was confirmed by co-localization of Vimentin (a fibroblast marker) and GFP. The number of Vimentin⁺/GFP⁺ cells was significantly increased in MI mice compared to control animals [81].

CCC is also suggested to act in an autocrine manner. In a study of H9c2 myoblasts, miRNA transfer was observed in hypoxic cells and their derived EVs under hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) conditions [82]. Both miRNA-208a and miRNA-208b were highly expressed in these cells and in the isolated EVs. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and apoptosis increased upon incubation with hypoxia-induced EVs derived from H9c2 cells, and were reduced in H9c2 cells overexpressing a mutant version of the RNA-binding protein QKI5 lacking its 3′ untranslated region (3′-UTR) [82]. However, EV uptake with silenced miR208a/b was not fully demonstrated.

In other studies, endothelial CCC has been widely associated with cell-to-cell interactions [84]. In primary neonatal mouse cardiomyocytes (NMCM) exposed to H2O2, miR-19 was highly expressed in exosomes, similar to serum-derived exosomes from acute MI patients and mice [36]. When exosomes from NMCMs were incubated with endothelial cells (HMEC-1), they induced increased apoptosis and reduced cell proliferation. These effects were reversed by inhibiting miR-19 with AMO-19. miR-19 was shown to target hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α), and co-incubation of miR-19 with AMO-19 or shHIF-1α restored cell viability, further supporting the role of exosomes in damage propagation. However, the precise mechanisms of exosome secretion and uptake in this context remain to be fully elucidated [36].

CCC between macrophages and fibroblasts has also been implicated in regulating inflammatory responses [85]. In an MI mouse model, mature miR-155 was upregulated in cardiac fibroblasts and macrophages compared to pri-miR-155. Exosomes from macrophages containing miR-155 inhibited fibroblast proliferation by targeting Sos1 and enhanced inflammation through the regulation of interleukins (IL-1β, IL-6), cytokines (TNF-α), and chemokines (CCL2). Fibroblasts cultured with exosomes from macrophages showed increased miR-155 levels, indicating exosome-mediated miRNA transfer. In vivo, miR-155 knockout mice exhibited improved cardiac function and fibrosis, suggesting a role of macrophage-fibroblast communication in inflammation and tissue remodeling [85].

LncRNAs

LncRNAs have garnered attention for their paracrine role in CCC, mainly through EVs, such as exosomes [7]. Recent research highlights the functional roles of EVs and their lncRNAs cargo in intercellular communication within the cardiovascular system. For instance, Wang and Zhang highlighted the role of exosomes derived from cardiomyocytes under hypoxic conditions in modulating the behavior of cardiac fibroblasts [86]. They found that among the differentially expressed lncRNAs in exosomes isolated from hypoxia-treated cardiomyocytes, AK139128 was upregulated in both the exosomes and the cardiomyocytes themselves. Elevated levels of AK139128 were also detected in cardiac fibroblasts treated with these hypoxic exosomes, leading to increased apoptosis and reduced proliferation, migration, and invasion. Although uptake of dye-labeled exosomes by fibroblasts was confirmed, and the effects were significantly reversed when using exosomes derived from AK139128-silenced hypoxia-treated cardiomyocytes, the decrease in AK139128 expression was observed only in the donor cardiomyocytes, and not in the recipient fibroblasts. Moreover, the precise molecular mechanism of action of AK139128 remains unclear [86]. Similarly, Lin et al. identified high levels of the lncRNA HCG15 in exosomes isolated from the serum of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients, which were positively correlated with serum cardiac troponin levels. Interestingly, exosomes derived from hypoxia-treated cardiomyocytes (AC16 cells) exhibited higher levels of HCG15 compared to the intracellular levels in the same hypoxic cardiomyocytes. These hypoxia-induced exosomes were taken up by AC16 cells and successfully transferred HCG15, resulting in elevated intracellular lncRNA levels, inhibited cell proliferation, increased apoptosis, and upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), mediated via the NF-κB/p65 and p38 MAPK pathways. Notably, exosomes derived from hypoxic AC16 cells transfected with siRNA targeting HCG15 attenuated apoptosis, cytokine production, and HCG15 expression in recipient cells. This contrasts with the previous study, in which the reduction of lncRNA expression under silencing conditions was not measured in recipient cells to confirm its transfer [87].

The macrophage-fibroblast communication previously mentioned mediated by miRNAs [85] has also been observed through mice bone marrow M2-derived cells enriched with circUbe3a in co-cultures with primary cardiac fibroblasts and an MI model [88]. Wang et al. isolated small and large EVs from M2 macrophages and observed that fibroblasts co-cultured with small EVs exhibited a more pronounced myofibroblast phenotype compared to those cultured with large EVs. CircUbe3a expression was significantly upregulated in the small EV fraction compared to the large EV fraction. It was shown to regulate cell proliferation, migration, and myofibroblast-related protein expression, including collagen type I/III and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), by targeting the miR-138-5p/RhoC axis [88]. The pro-fibrogenic effects of circUbe3a were evaluated in a mouse model of MI through gain- and loss-of-function experiments using recombinant adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated circUbe3a transduction. Although overexpression of circUbe3a exacerbated myocardial fibrosis following MI induction, and this effect was reversed under loss-of-function conditions, in vivo CCC mediated by small EVs enriched in circUbe3a was not thoroughly investigated [88].

Several studies have also highlighted the therapeutic potential of EVs derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs). Ding et al. demonstrated that circHIPK3-enriched exosomes, derived from human umbilical cord MSCs and labeled with PKH26, were injected into circHIPK3 knockout (KO) mice and successfully localized to the heart. Treated mice showed improved cardiac function, increased cardiac circHIPK3 levels, longer telomere length, and decreased mRNA and protein levels of p16 and p21. Using RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) and RPISeq (RNA–protein interaction prediction) experiments, the authors showed that circHIPK3 enhances the interaction between HuR and β-TrCP, promoting HuR degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, which in turn leads to reduced p21 expression [89]. Several mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)-derived EVs enriched with lncRNAs, have shown regulation mainly through competitive endogenous mechanisms [90, 91]. Yan et al. found that exosomes derived from adipose tissue MSCs, enriched in lncRNA XIST, can be transferred to atrial tissue, where they mitigate pyroptotic cell death and inflammasome activation in a mouse model of atrial fibrillation. Moreover, XIST-containing EVs were shown to exert these effects by acting as a miR-214-3p sponge, thereby indirectly enhancing Arl2 expression in HL-1 atrial myocytes subjected to fibrillation-inducing conditions [90]. Similarly, Mao et al.. reported that MSC-derived exosomes enriched in lncRNA KLF3-AS1 accumulate in cardiac tissue and attenuate pyroptosis and the inflammatory response in a rat model of myocardial infarction. The cardioprotective effects of KLF3-AS1-enriched exosomes were mediated by sponging miR-138-5p and upregulating Sirt1 expression [91].

The protective effects of MSC-derived exosomal UCA1 were reported by Sun et al., who showed that hypoxia-induced MSC-derived exosomes enriched in UCA1 decreased apoptosis and fibrosis in a rat model of myocardial infarction. These effects were mediated through the miR-873-5p/XIAP axis under hypoxic conditions in H9c2 cells [92]. Furthermore, Li et al. found that H/R conditions decreased lncRNA HCP5 expression in cardiomyocytes, but treatment with bone marrow MSC-derived exosomes restored its expression, exerting an anti-apoptotic effect on these cells. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments demonstrated that exosomal lncRNA HCP5 acts as a potential regulator of IGF-1 by sponging miR-497, thereby mitigating H/R-induced myocardial apoptosis [93]. Zhu et al.. also highlighted the role of MALAT1 in preventing age-related cardiac dysfunction by inhibiting the NF-κB/TNF-α pathway. MALAT1, enriched in exosomes derived from umbilical cord MSCs, has been shown to inhibit the NF-κB/TNF-α pathway, thereby preventing age-induced cardiac dysfunction. These protective effects were reversed upon MALAT1 knockdown [94]. Despite the significant functional roles of lncRNAs carried by MSC-derived EVs, the use of labeled EVs and the mechanisms of uptake remain insufficiently elucidated across studies [74–76]. Thus, Xia et al.. demonstrated that adipose tissue-derived hypoxic MSC-derived exosomes transfer lncRNA MALAT1 to cardiomyocytes, reducing doxorubicin-induced senescence by modulating the miR-92a-3p/ATG4a axis [74]. Zhang et al.. demonstrated that exosomes derived from bone marrow MSCs inhibit oxLDL-induced damage in HUVECs by delivering lncRNA FENDRR, which regulates the miR-28/TEAD1 axis, thereby reducing cell apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation [75].

Notably, exosomal ncRNAs can also contribute to vascular damage. Shyu et al.. demonstrated that high glucose conditions upregulate MALAT1 in macrophage-derived exosomes, which, upon incubation with macrophages under basal conditions, downregulate miR-150-5p and increase resistin protein expression. Moreover, injection of MALAT1-enriched macrophage-derived exosomes promoted vascular injury in diabetic rats through the miR-150-5p/resistin axis. These effects were reversed both in vitro and in vivo by treatment with a MALAT1-targeting LNA-gapmer [76].

Similarly, Chen et al. found that MALAT1-enriched exosomes derived from M1 macrophages exacerbate myocardial dysfunction following cardiac infarction, through internalization of these exosomes by endothelial cells. By sponging miR-25-3p, MALAT1 promoted CD42 expression and activated the MEK/ERK signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting angiogenesis and impairing myocardial regeneration [95]. Oxidative stress also influences EV-mediated communication. Hu et al. showed that ox-LDL treatment upregulates LIPCAR lncRNA in THP-1 cells, which is transferred to HUVECs via exosomes, inducing ROS production, increasing CASP3 expression, and promoting apoptosis by downregulating CDK2/PCNA [96]. Gao et al.. identified that exosomes derived from oxLDL-treated HUVECs aggravated aortic lesion formation in an atherosclerotic mouse model. Incubation of neutrophils with MALAT1-enriched HUVEC-derived exosomes promoted the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) via the RAC1/p38–AKT pathway, thereby exacerbating atherosclerosis [97]. Moreover, Zhang et al. highlighted that exosomes derived from oxLDL-treated HUVECs exhibit high expression of LINC01005, which is transferred to VSMCs. This promotes VSMC proliferation and migration via the miR-128-3p/KLF4 axis [98]. Lastly, emerging studies have suggested novel therapeutic targets for vascular-related conditions. Su et al. demonstrated that endothelial cell-derived exosomes containing LINC00174 alleviate myocardial infarction in a mouse I/R model, in which this lncRNA is typically downregulated. Furthermore, co-culture of H/R-treated cardiomyocytes with these exosomes led to upregulation of LINC00174, which reduced cardiomyocyte apoptosis by suppressing p53 expression through interactions with SRSF1 and by activating the Akt/MAPK pathway [99]. Song et al. reported that adipocyte-derived exosomes containing high levels of SNHG9 mitigate endothelial cell inflammation in HUVECs upon incubation. Mechanistically, SNHG9 exerts its effect by binding to TRADD mRNA, leading to degradation of the complex and thereby alleviating inflammation and apoptosis in endothelial cells [100].

Despite significant advances in understanding the role of EVs in cardiovascular diseases, major challenges remain in the field of CCC [101]. On the one hand, sample handling and the lack of standardized isolation and purification protocols can markedly influence EV yield, purity, and the determination of their molecular cargo composition. These limitations often lead to the co-isolation of non–lipid membrane contaminants, which confound data interpretation and hinder experimental reproducibility [102]. It is essential to adopt recommended protocols and promote broader adherence to reference guidelines, such as those proposed by the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV), to ensure robust, reproducible, and biologically meaningful findings in cardiovascular research [103, 104].

Additionally, a key challenge is the heterogeneity of EVs, which is shaped by their cellular origin and the context of secretion. This complexity complicates the identification of functionally relevant cargo involved in CCC. The issue is particularly evident in the case of lncRNAs, for which a precise and standardized classification is still lacking [105]. An alternative approach to overcome this limitation has been proposed by the Extracellular RNA Communication Consortium (ERCC) through the integration of transcriptomic profiling of ncRNAs with standardized EV isolation and purification protocols. Using deconvolution algorithms, previously applied in tumor heterogeneity analysis [106], along with hierarchical clustering and enrichment profiling of ncRNA cargo across density-fractionated carriers, the ERCC proposed the classification of six distinct “cargo types”. This strategy enabled the characterization of ncRNA profiles associated with both lipid membrane-based carriers (such as low- and high-density vesicles) and non–lipid membrane carriers (including RBPs and lipoproteins) across various biofluids from healthy and disease states [107, 108]. In addition to computational and density fractionation approaches, the authors used a gene set composed of more than 50% intergenic lncRNAs (lincRNAs) to address overlapping issues between density fractions in the identification of distinct cargo types. This strategy highlights the value of lncRNA diversity in delineating carrier heterogeneity, and, when combined with their inherent high tissue specificity; could provide a promising framework for future studies aimed at accurately predicting the cellular origin and recipient in ncRNA-mediated communication.

Lipid-associated lipoproteins

High-density lipoproteins (HDL) can bind and transport miRNAs to specialized cells, influencing gene expression and exerting protective vascular effects [64, 79]. Despite their functional relevance, miRNAs associated with HDL constitute less than 10% of the total circulating miRNAs in plasma from healthy individuals, with miR-223 reported as the most abundant among them [68]. Similarly, another study identified 24 miRNAs associated with HDL in healthy individuals, highlighting miR-142–3p as the most prevalent [67].

In human coronary artery endothelial cells (HCAEC), the levels of pri-miR-223 and pre-miR-223 were initially low. However, incubation with native HDL isolated from healthy donors increased mature miRNA-223 levels. This effect was abolished when HDL was pre-treated with RNase to degrade its RNA cargo, indicating that miRNA-223 was transferred to endothelial cells. Further experiments showed that HDL treatment negatively regulated miRNA-223’s target, ICAM-1, by decreasing its mRNA expression and protein levels, as confirmed by a luciferase assay. Additionally, when HCAEC were treated with a miRNA-223 hairpin inhibitor alongside HDL, ICAM-1 protein levels were not reduced, reinforcing the role of miRNA-223 in this regulatory pathway [64]. Interestingly, HDL-mediated miRNA transport appears to be modulated by diet, with a positive correlation observed between HDL-cholesterol and HDL-miRNA-223 levels following a trans-fatty acid diet [109]. More recently, HDL-mediated miRNA transport has been implicated in vascular complications of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [79]. In T2DM patients with vascular complications, HDL-miRNA-27b-3p and HDL-miRNA-181c-5p were upregulated, while HDL-miRNA-223 levels were downregulated compared to T2DM patients without vascular complications and healthy controls. Functionally, HDL derived from these patients promoted endothelial tubule formation in HCAEC, as assessed by a matrigel tubulogenesis assay, and increased miRNA-181c-5p expression in endothelial cells [79].

Regarding low-density lipoproteins (LDL), miR-223 is also the most abundant transcript bound to LDL, though at lower levels than HDL [68]. No significant differences in LDL-bound miRNA levels were observed in patients with acute coronary syndrome. However, another study identified four miRNAs bound to LDL in healthy individuals, with miR-532–5p being the most prevalent [67]. Similarly, while fewer studies have explored very low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) and miRNAs, 14 miRNAs were identified as potentially associated with VLDL in a recent analysis, with miR-451a being the most abundant [67]. Enrichment analysis of these VLDL-associated miRNAs revealed pathways linked to CVD, highlighting their potential clinical relevance.

Recently, Gluba et al.. reviewed the critical role of lncRNAs in lipid metabolism in the context of CVDs [110]. Among them, LIPTER was described as a lipid-droplet associated lncRNA in T2DM patients, expressed explicitly in cardiomyoctes derived from induced pluripotent stem cells [111]. Metabolomics analysis positioned LIPTER as a key regulator in the lipid droplet size, cytosolic distribution, and uptake of mitochondrial lipids, and consequently, in mitochondrial activity and function.

Moreover, recent studies have shown that lncRNAs can bind to HDL particles in patients with cholesterol metabolism alterations. Scicali et al. evaluated the expression of HDL-associated lncRNAs in individuals with heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HeFH), a genetic risk factor for atherosclerosis. Despite the characteristically high plasma LDL levels in HeFH patients, their HDL fraction was enriched in the lncRNAs HIF1A-AS2, LASER, and LEXIS. Interestingly, LEXIS was found to be significantly upregulated in the HDL fraction of HeFH patients compared to healthy controls [112]. Sallam et al. were pioneers in identifying LEXIS in mice fed a Western diet, where it was found to be upregulated in hepatic tissue compared to mice on a chow diet. In this study, adenoviral overexpression of LEXIS led to a reduction in serum HDL particles and downregulation of genes associated with cholesterol biosynthesis. In primary mouse hepatocytes, LEXIS was characterized as a nuclear lncRNA, and the observed effects were attributed to its interaction with the transcriptional co-factor RALY. In the presence of LEXIS, RALY exhibited reduced occupancy at the promoters of cholesterol biosynthetic genes, suggesting a mechanism for LEXIS-mediated transcriptional regulation [113]. Although there is currently no direct evidence of CCC mediated by lncRNAs associated with lipoproteins, the fact that HDL is the most studied ncRNA carrier suggests that circulating HDL-associated lncRNAs may contribute to protective mechanisms in cardiometabolic diseases.

The study of ncRNA–lipoprotein complexes faces several challenges. Some studies have raised concerns that the role of HDL as an ncRNA carrier may be obscured by overlapping signals from EVs, primarily due to technical limitations in the purification and isolation protocols for both types of carriers [102]. Another unexplored aspect of these non–lipid membrane carriers is the mechanism by which ncRNAs are loaded into circulating lipoproteins, largely due to a limited understanding of RNA–lipid interactions [114, 115]. Similarly, the mechanisms underlying their release and uptake by recipient cells remain poorly understood, leaving significant knowledge gaps in the field [116]. Vickers et al. explored the biogenesis pathways of HDL, identifying the ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) and the exosome secretion pathway mediated by the nSMase2 enzyme as export mechanisms for miRNA-223 in macrophages. Additionally, they identified the scavenger receptor class B type I (SR-BI) as a key uptake receptor for miRNA-223 in kidney cells [58]. Although functional transfer of HDL-associated miRNA-223 to endothelial cells, and subsequent targeting of ICAM-1, has been reported to be mediated by SR-BI [64], increased levels of HDL–miRNA-223 were observed in macrophages treated with an inhibitor of the nSMase2 pathway, while no significant changes were seen in ABCA1-overexpressing cells, contrary to expectations [58]. Additionally, transcriptomic analysis of the HDL plasma fraction from SR-BI knockout (SR-BI–KO) mice revealed a minimal impact on the small RNA profile of HDL, including miRNAs, suggesting that SR-BI deficiency does not significantly alter the overall small RNA cargo associated with HDL [116]. These gaps in our understanding of HDL as an ncRNA carrier in CCC suggest that complementary multi-omics approaches could provide deeper insights into the mechanisms of ncRNA loading into lipoproteins, as well as their release by donor cells and uptake by recipient cells. Finally, the recently reported role of LEXIS highlights the need to expand CCC research beyond miRNAs to include lncRNAs, particularly in the context of cardiovascular diseases associated with hyperlipidemia and hypercholesterolemia.

RNA-binding proteins

In the context of CVDs, circulating miRNA–AGO2 complexes have been identified; however, their cellular origin remains unclear, and their role in endocrine-like communication has not been fully explored [16, 117]. For instance, in a remote I/R preconditioning mouse model, microarray analysis identified miRNA-144, which was found at increased levels in the heart, liver, plasma, and also in human plasma following remote I/R preconditioning. This miRNA has been shown to exert cardioprotective effects by improving hemodynamic parameters and reducing infarct size [16]. However, although elevated miRNA-144 levels were detected in cardiac tissue, its enrichment in AGO2 complexes from serum supernatant fractions suggests a systemic transport mechanism. Additionally, miRNA-144 was detected in serum exosome pellets, but no definitive evidence was provided for its involvement in CCC [16]. Moreover, the clinical significance of AGO2-mediated miRNA transport is underscored by a study examining circulating miRNA-126 bound to AGO2 in the context of vascular dysfunction, particularly in patients with acute or chronic kidney disease. In this study, miRNA-126–AGO2 complex levels were responsive to treatment and varied according to disease severity, highlighting their potential as dynamic biomarkers for disease progression and therapeutic response [117].

Regarding RBPs and lncRNAs, a review by Ding et al. [118] highlights the binding interactions between RBPs and lncRNAs as a key regulatory mechanism that influences lncRNA expression, cellular localization, and even target gene regulation in atherosclerotic diseases. Some insights into lncRNA enrichment in plasma RBPs have been explored in healthy subjects, although several challenges remain. As previously mentioned, Murillo et al. identified different extracellular RNA carriers using bioinformatic tools and density fractionation, which they categorized as “cargo types”. In this study, AGO2-positive fractions, as expected, were primarily enriched in miRNAs. Interestingly, these fractions also contained a notable abundance (~ 50%) of lncRNAs. However, the study also predicted a shared ncRNA cargo profile between AGO2-associated fractions and uncharacterized vesicular fractions, suggesting potential overlap or co-packaging mechanisms that are yet to be fully understood [107].

Although AGO2-positive fractions were enriched in both miRNAs and lncRNAs, the overlap with uncharacterized vesicular fractions and HDL marker–positive fractions complicates the assignment of specificity to AGO2 as an exclusive ncRNA carrier, thereby hindering a clear understanding of its functional role in circulation. Moreover, the absence of standardized protocols for isolating and characterizing RBP–ncRNA complexes remains a significant technical challenge, limiting reproducibility across studies [107, 119]. To date, studies have not determined whether RBP–lncRNA complexes represent biologically active carriers or are merely by-products of cellular degradation. For example, Murillo et al. reported that ultracentrifugation pellets, classified as uncharacterized vesicular fractions, shared ncRNA profiles with AGO2-predicted fractions, further blurring the distinction between functional carriers and nonspecific RNA remnants [107]. Similarly, Rodosthenous et al.. evaluated the efficiency of long RNA transcript detection, including protein-coding RNAs, lncRNAs, other ncRNAs, and pseudogenes, in both ‘EV-enriched’ and “non-EV-enriched” fractions. Among long RNAs, lncRNAs were the most abundant transcripts in the EV-enriched fraction. However, this fraction also contained markers for AGO2 and HDL, complicating the interpretation of RNA origin. In fact, the fraction with the highest AGO2 levels also shared EV and HDL markers, posing a critical obstacle. As a result, the AGO2-positive fraction was excluded from further transcriptomic sequencing and analysis [119]. While AGO2–miRNA complexes are abundant in plasma, a major obstacle is whether they result from active secretion or passive cellular release [120]. Moreover, the absence of known ligands or receptors for AGO2 on recipient cells limits our ability to define target specificity in intercellular communication. Together, these challenges underscore the need for improved isolation techniques, functional validation assays, and integrative multi-omics approaches. Addressing these limitations is essential to elucidate the biological relevance of RBPs in intercellular RNA signaling in CVDs.

Platelets

Platelets are cell fragments derived from megakaryocytes, primarily responsible for regulating hemostasis. Despite lacking a nucleus, platelets possess mRNAs and miRNAs, enabling protein synthesis. Over 490 miRNAs have been identified in circulating platelets in the plasma of healthy individuals, with the let-7 family being the most abundant [121]. As previously mentioned, circulating miRNAs are predominantly transported via EVs. However, platelet-like particles have also been shown to interact with THP-1 and HUVEC cells, transferring RNA species such as globins (HGB1, HBA1, HBE1) and mitochondrial genes (SOD2, MRPS15) [122]. Nonetheless, the study did not confirm whether platelets transfer miRNAs to these target cells. Alternatively, it was suggested that miRNAs may circulate within platelets themselves to reach recipient cells. In support of this, some studies have proposed that activated platelets release miRNAs via small, membrane-bound microparticles, which can influence gene expression in endothelial cells [123, 124].

Further, in a more recent study [125], an increase in specific miRNAs (miR-30b-5p, miR-30c-5p, miR-103a-5p, miR-140-3p, miR-185-5p, and miR-221-3p) was observed in the serum of patients with coronary artery disease (CAD). The same study demonstrated that platelets derived from CAD patients and activated in vitro released these miRNAs, which were linked to the regulation of atherosclerosis and cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Additionally, another study highlighted the role of platelet-derived miRNAs (miR-27b-3p, miR-222-3p, and miR-335-5p) enriched in the infarcted area improves cardiac function and structure following I/R injury [126]. These miRNAs were associated with cardiac remodeling in response to hypoxia, cellular stress responses, angiogenesis, and fibrosis. Moreover, platelet reactivity has also been implicated in ischemic stroke; these exhibited higher reactivity compared to those from healthy individuals. These patients also showed elevated levels of platelet-derived extracellular vesicles and increased plasma levels of miR-19a-3p, which correlated with greater stroke severity [127].

One major obstacle in studying platelets as ncRNA carriers is the difficulty in discriminating between the direct transfer of ncRNAs from activated platelets and their delivery via EVs. While platelet-derived microparticles (PMPs) have been shown to transfer miR-223 to endothelial and smooth muscle cells [123, 128], the specificity and physiological relevance of this mechanism remain incompletely understood. In addition, the profile of platelet-derived miRNA release is highly stimulus-dependent (e.g., thrombin, collagen), and pathological conditions such as CAD or stroke can markedly alter their ncRNA content [129]. The mechanisms of cellular uptake of platelet-derived ncRNAs—including whether they are internalized via endocytosis, direct membrane fusion, or receptor-mediated interactions, remain largely uncharacterized, which limits the functional interpretation of current findings [124, 130]. Finally, the lack of in vivo tracing tools specific for platelet-derived ncRNAs represents a critical limitation. Addressing these challenges will require integrated experimental approaches, including advanced tracking technologies, selective PMP isolation, and robust functional validation in physiologically relevant models.

RNA sequencing approaches for the study of ncRNA-mediated communication in the heart

Understanding the complex mechanisms of ncRNA-mediated communication in the heart remains a significant challenge, particularly in identifying how recipient cells take up these molecules and the functional implications of this process. Emerging RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) technologies provide powerful tools to address these gaps, offering depth and precision to study ncRNA expression, transport, and intercellular signaling. This session discusses the utility of bulk RNA-seq and single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) in elucidating ncRNA-driven communication networks. While bulk RNA-seq has advanced our knowledge of miRNA and lncRNA expression patterns in cardiac tissues, single-cell RNA-seq enables the resolution of cell-specific ncRNA dynamics, uncovering their distinct roles in maintaining homeostasis and contributing to disease progression. Finally, we emphasize how these sequencing advancements transform our understanding of ncRNA-based signaling pathways, shedding light on the emerging significance of lncRNAs and their interplay with well-characterized miRNA-driven mechanisms in cardiovascular health and disease.

Bulk RNA-seq for the study of circulating miRNA and lncRNA in cardiovascular diseases and heart failure.

Bulk RNA-seq has significantly advanced our understanding of miRNA and lncRNA expression patterns in cardiac tissues and circulation (Fig. 2a). By pooling RNA from tissue samples or plasma, bulk RNA-seq identifies global changes in ncRNA expression under different physiological and pathological conditions along different periods, such as in HF and ischemic injury. However, this approach lacks cell-specific resolution, limiting the ability to pinpoint ncRNAs’ exact cellular source or uptake mechanisms.

Circulating miRNAs have been extensively studied in CVD [131] and HF [132], often as biomarkers for diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic purposes. While many studies mainly focus on ‘free’ miRNAs in plasma, serum, or blood, their endocrine roles have been suggested without fully addressing their transport mechanisms [133]. The first study using RNA-seq to analyze miRNAs in plasma was performed on healthy subjects [134]. Since then, few studies have applied this method to investigate circulating miRNAs in patients with HF. In one such study, bulk RNA-seq was used to evaluate miRNA expression in plasma samples from cohorts of patients with varying HF severity (10 healthy controls, 10 acute HF, and 10 chronic HF patients) [135]. This discovery phase revealed 15 miRNAs downregulated four-fold compared to healthy controls. These levels gradually increased from Acute Heart Failure (AHF) admission to AHF 6-month follow-up and Congestive Heart Failure (CHF) patients. Among the miRNAs identified, miR-18b-5p, miR-30e-5p, miR-423-5p, and miR-652-3p were notable, with miR-18b-5p and miR-652-3p predictive of 6-month mortality. miR-423-5p, on the other hand, emerged as a biomarker of treatment progression in hypertension-induced HF [136].

A subsequent study investigated a cohort of 32 post-MI HF patients and 16 controls with stable angina and no significant coronary lesions, determining the expression levels of miR-20a-5p, miR-340-5p, and miR-7i-5p in plasma using RNA-seq and RT-PCR validation [137]. The tissue origin of these circulating miRNAs was explored using a rat post-MI HF model, where plasma and myocardial samples were analyzed at 4, 8, and 12 weeks. At 4 weeks, only miR-7i-5p was upregulated in plasma and downregulated in the myocardium, suggesting an inverse relationship. At 8 weeks, miR-20a-5p, miR-340-5p, and miR-7i-5p were upregulated in plasma and downregulated in myocardial tissue. MiR-20a-5p and miR-340-5p were upregulated in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), while only miR-20a-5p showed elevated exosome levels. These findings highlight proportional changes in expression between myocardium and blood samples as a potential approach to identifying active secretion of ncRNAs [138]. However, these studies did not determine evidence of myocardial-specific secretion or mechanisms involving ncRNA transport carriers.

Several studies have identified lncRNAs in plasma exosomes and plenty ‘free’ circulating lncRNAs from patients with CVDs, defining them as potential biomarkers for cardiovascular diseases [6, 43, 139–141]. Among them, LIPCAR and H19 have been highlighted [140], with H19 playing a role in regulating the cardiac damage response [142]. Another well-characterized lncRNA is MIAT, which was described by Chen et al. as upregulated in EVs from the serum of patients with atrial fibrillation. This transcript is thought to promote atrial fibrosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress in atrial myocytes by regulating the miR-485-5p/CXCL10 axis [141].

However, identifying circulating lncRNAs with no clinical potential remains an unexplored field that could be studied with new technologies (Fig. 1b). Li et al. identified 117 dysregulated lncRNAs in exosomes from the plasma of patients with arteriovenous malformations. A ceRNA regulatory network constructed revealed significant pathways involved in Rap 1, Ras and MAPK signaling, and platelet activation that could be involved in this pathology [143]. The study by Raboul et al. explored the expression pattern of circulating EVs in patients with CAD and identified that the lncRNA PUNISHER was significantly upregulated, suggesting a potential role in regulating the angiogenic function of endothelial cells [144]. Additionally, lncRNAs derived from PBMCs have been reported to be enriched in EVs, as well as in potentially resident cardiac white blood cells [145, 146]. Boeckel et al. determined that Heat2 is enriched in circulating eosinophils as well as in cardiac-resident eosinophils from HF patients, where it plays a role in regulating the cells’ transmigratory capacity [145]. CircNPHP4, enriched in monocyte-derived EVs in CAD, regulates monocyte adhesion to human coronary artery endothelial cells (HCAECs) by sponging miR-1231 in HCAECs, thereby modulating the EGFR/PI3K/Akt signaling pathway [146]. A possible endocrine role for lncRNAs has been previously proposed; however, there is still insufficient evidence to support this mechanism. Further methodological development is needed to advance our understanding of circulating lncRNAs and their potential systemic functions [6, 138].

While bulk RNA-seq has expanded our knowledge of miRNA and lncRNA expression changes in cardiac pathology, it provides limited insight into CCC mechanisms. The inability to resolve cell-specific expression patterns or identify active secretion pathways underscores the need for complementary approaches, such as single-cell RNA-seq, to further unravel ncRNA-mediated intercellular signaling in cardiovascular health and disease.

Single-cell RNA-seq for unraveling gene expression profiles in the heart tissue

scRNA-seq has revolutionized our ability to analyze gene expression in cardiovascular tissues, providing unprecedented insights into cellular heterogeneity and dynamic regulatory networks [34, 147, 148]. By enabling the transcriptomic profiling of individual cells, scRNA-seq has facilitated the identification of unique cell types and states during adaptation processes in development (Fig. 2b) and disease, significantly advancing our understanding of CVD mechanisms [149]. Recent studies have leveraged this technology to map CCC networks and elucidate the roles of ncRNAs in these interactions [150–153].

Cellular crosstalk is central to CVD repair, regeneration, and disease progression. While traditional studies focused on protein-based signaling, scRNA-seq has highlighted the pivotal roles of ncRNAs in CCC. For instance, scRNA-seq studies of murine cardiac tissue identified fibroblasts as highly interconnected cells based on ligand-receptor pair correlations, underscoring their central role in the cardiac microenvironment communication, besides revealing sex-specific differences in these interactions [154]. In the context of MI, novel trajectories determined fibroblast subpopulations involved in fibrosis. Farbeni et al. characterized over 30,000 cells in post-MI mouse hearts, uncovering fibroblast subpopulations with distinct profibrotic and antifibrotic profiles, as well as CCC by weighted edges based on ligand-receptor expression levels in target cell populations. In this study, the largest and most significant outbound connections were seen in myofibroblasts, and the inbound connections were seen in endothelial cells [155]. These insights extend to miRNAs and lncRNAs. For example, miRNAs act as regulators of receptor-mediated signaling pathways, modulating both intracellular and intercellular processes crucial for disease progression [156]. On the other hand, scRNA-seq has emerged as a valuable tool for identifying lncRNAs, due to its discrete expression levels and their increased cell-specificity compared to protein-coding genes (PCG) [33]. In this context, FIXER was identified as a novel myofibroblast-specific lncRNA in post-MI mouse hearts and conserved in humans with therapeutic potential by regulating heart remodeling and fibrosis (Fig. 2c). Several cis-regulated PCGs were correlated to FIXER expression levels, as expected among them collagens and, interestingly, cardiac lineages associated transcription factors [157].

A growing field of research focuses on the potential roles of competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) in CCC. These ceRNAs could effectively modulate the availability of miRNAs to target ligands, receptors, or other regulatory molecules involved in CCC networks. For instance, ceRNAs may regulate EV cargo to influence receptor-ligand dynamics, thereby impacting signaling cascades in recipient cells, or even directly regulating the availability of a ligand or receptor [158]. This regulatory mechanism offers a novel layer of complexity in understanding CCC, suggesting that ceRNA networks could directly modulate the interactions between ligands or receptors central to cardiac communication.

Emerging studies have also integrated multi-omics approaches with scRNA-seq to deepen our understanding of cardiac biology. For instance, combining scRNA-seq with chromatin accessibility assays has elucidated transcription factor activity driving cellular transitions in cardiac development and injury responses. Integrating with spatial transcriptomics provides spatial context, enabling more accurate interaction inferences [159]. Together, these findings could underscore the intricate complexity of CCC in cardiac tissues, highlighting ncRNAs as central mediators. Advances in scRNA-seq, combined with multi-omics and functional analyses, continue to refine our understanding of cardiovascular pathophysiology and unveil novel targets for therapeutic intervention.

Despite its transformative potential, scRNA-seq presents both technical advantages and important limitations that must be considered when studying CCC in cardiovascular tissues. One of scRNA-seq greatest strengths lies in resolving cellular heterogeneity and uncovering rare subpopulations. This is particularly valuable in contexts involving cell differentiation, such as embryonic development or experimental models of pluripotent stem cells, and in endothelial or fibroblasts differentiation processes that are highly relevant in CVDs. This offers unparalleled resolution of gene expression dynamics, including ncRNAs with discrete and cell-specific patterns, particularly for lncRNAs which may contribute to a more precise resolution of cellular heterogeneity within subpopulations [34].

However, scRNA-seq faces intrinsic challenges when analyzing ncRNAs, including technical noise, dropout events, and low capture efficiency, particularly for low-abundance transcripts such as many lncRNAs and miRNAs [160, 161]. These limitations may hinder the comprehensive characterization of regulatory RNA molecules that are central to CCC. Another major technical challenge for scRNA-seq in cardiac tissue is the large size of cardiomyocytes [162], which limits the efficiency of sequencing at the whole-cell level. As a result, single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) has become the preferred approach in this context [163]. However, since most studies on CCC focus on post-transcriptional mechanisms involving ncRNAs, nuclear RNA may not adequately capture these processes. Therefore, elucidating the role of cardiomyocytes in CCC using single-cell approaches from a post-transcriptional ncRNA perspective remains a significant challenge [164]. In addition, most scRNA-seq protocols lack spatial context and do not provide information on cellular localization, which is essential for making accurate predictions about CCC pathways in complex tissues such as the heart [165–167]. To address these challenges, the integration of scRNA-seq with spatial transcriptomics or CLIP-seq, to explore the potential role of RBPs in CCC, as well as the use of functional validation approaches, is crucial. Emerging multi-omics strategies and improved computational pipelines [71, 107, 168, 169] are helping to overcome current limitations, enabling a more accurate reconstruction of CCC networks and providing transcriptomic insights that may inform future therapeutic strategies for CVDs.

Conclusions and future perspectives

The study of ncRNAs has the potential to revolutionize our understanding of CCC in the cardiovascular system, shedding light on their pivotal roles in both physiological homeostasis and disease. This review highlights the dual role of ncRNAs, with miRNAs being well-established as key mediators of cardiovascular CCC, influencing processes such as cardiac remodeling, fibrosis, and immune responses. LncRNAs are emerging as equally essential players, mainly through their involvement in EV-mediated signaling, acting as ceRNAs, and potentially modulating ligand-receptor dynamics. The discovery of ceRNA networks adds a new layer of complexity to our understanding of cardiac communication, emphasizing their potential to regulate CCC by sequestering or regulating key signaling molecules. Both miRNAs and lncRNAs play central roles in this regulatory architecture: miRNAs silence specific mRNA targets, thereby dampening signaling cascades, while lncRNAs can function as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), sequestering miRNAs and thus indirectly modulating gene expression in recipient cells. These molecular decoys and inhibitors shape the signaling landscape between cardiac and non-cardiac cells, adding functionally relevant layers to CCC networks. Integrating these regulatory interactions into CCC models may enhance the precision of inferred networks and better reflect the dynamic interplay between cell populations, presenting significant opportunities for further investigation [16, 79, 126, 158]. Despite these advancements, considerable gaps remain in understanding the mechanisms governing ncRNA transport, uptake, and intracellular processing. Questions about the tissue or cellular origins of circulating ncRNAs [144, 145], their specificity for recipient cells, and the dynamic modulation of their cargo by external stimuli remain unanswered. Addressing these challenges requires integrating functional validation studies, spatial and temporal transcriptomics, and innovative technologies to capture the dynamic nature of ncRNA-mediated CCC fully. In conclusion, ncRNA research in cardiovascular health and disease is at a transformative juncture. Emerging technologies and interdisciplinary approaches are poised to bridge existing knowledge gaps, paving the way for ncRNA-based diagnostics and therapeutics. By unraveling the full potential of ncRNAs in CCC, we can advance toward novel strategies for managing cardiovascular diseases, offering hope for precision medicine and improved patient outcomes.
