Gut microbiota‐targeted interventions for depression in adolescents and young adults: Mechanisms, evidence strength and clinical strategies—A narrative review
Authors: Hanchen Hou, Yinan Li, Wanting Tang, Yiwen Gao, Yuanyuan Tian, Fei Chen, Lige Zhang, Jushuang Zhang, Xiaoxia Xu, Yao Li, Yi Li, Qingshan Wang, Lingli Liu, Teng Teng, Hao Wu, Xinyu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDepression in adolescents and young adults is common, associated with substantial functional impairment and characterised by limited treatment efficacy. The microbiota–gut–brain (MGB) axis has emerged as a potential therapeutic target for depression. This narrative review synthesises preclinical and clinical evidence on a range of MGB axis interventions aimed at alleviating depressive symptoms in youth, including probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, postbiotics, faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) and lifestyle strategies such as dietary modification and structured exercise. In animal models, these interventions consistently produce antidepressant effects, accompanied by reduced inflammatory signalling, normalisation of hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal axis activity and upregulation of neurotrophic and serotonergic pathways. In humans, particularly among younger cohorts, the evidence is heterogeneous. Some probiotic or synbiotic regimens have yielded modest improvements in depressive symptoms in preliminary trials, whereas stand‐alone prebiotics have shown inconsistent or null effects; clinical evidence for postbiotics and FMT remains preliminary. Lifestyle interventions that target the MGB axis, such as Mediterranean‐style diets and structured exercise programmes, have been associated with improved mood and, in some studies, reductions in inflammatory biomarkers. Compared with studies in adults, research in this population remains limited by small sample sizes, greater methodological heterogeneity and less consistent findings and also suggests the presence of age‐specific pathways. Current evidence indicates that interventions targeting the MGB axis should be approached cautiously and considered only as adjunctive strategies for the treatment of depression in this population. Future work requires rigorously designed, strain‐ and protocol‐specific clinical trials with standardised procedures, careful safety monitoring and biomarker‐guided personalised approaches.Keywords: faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), interventions, major depressive disorder (MDD), microbiota–gut–brain (MGB) axis

INTRODUCTION

Depression is a prevalent and disabling mood disorder characterised by persistent low mood and anhedonia, frequently comorbid with medical conditions and associated with a markedly elevated suicide risk. 1 Over the past two decades, the global burden of major depressive disorder (MDD) has risen substantially, with prevalence increasing by more than 50% and the steepest relative rise observed in individuals aged 20–24 years. 2 Current data indicate that approximately one third of adolescents aged 10–19 years report clinically significant depressive symptoms. 3 Despite the availability of psychotherapy, pharmacotherapy, somatic interventions and lifestyle modification, the supporting evidence has been derived predominantly from adult populations with MDD. 4 In adolescents (12–18 years) and young adults (19–35 years), treatment effects are often attenuated and may even diverge from adult findings. 5 Acute‐phase pharmacotherapy yields remission in only 23%–37% of young patients 6 ; relapse occurs in nearly half of patients after discontinuation. 7 Moreover, available data suggest that fluoxetine monotherapy 8 or fluoxetine combined with cognitive‐behavioural therapy 9 constitutes the most efficacious treatment for MDD in adolescents and young adults, yet its overall therapeutic effect remains modest. Collectively, these observations highlight the urgent need for more effective, acceptable and age‐appropriate treatment strategies for depression in young people.

To identify more effective treatments, clarifying the pathophysiology of MDD is essential. 10 Converging evidence implicates disturbances across multiple biological systems, including monoaminergic dysregulation, an imbalance between glutamatergic and γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA)‐mediated signalling with impaired synaptic plasticity, dysregulation of the hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal (HPA) axis, immune and inflammatory activation with alterations in the tryptophan‐to‐kynurenine pathway, dysfunction of large‐scale neural networks, circadian disruption and perturbations within the microbiota–gut–brain (MGB) axis. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Previous evidence suggests that targeting the MGB axis is a viable therapeutic strategy across multiple diseases, particularly in gastroenterology and oncology. Recent landmark preclinical work shows that the microbiota can modulate behavioural and physiological abnormalities relevant to neurodevelopmental disorders through four interconnected routes. First, immune and inflammatory cascades, including modulation of microglia and astroglia and communication via the vagus nerve, shape neural activity. 15 Second, metabolite pathways act as signalling modules. Short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs) support glial homoeostasis and the integrity of the blood–brain barrier, whereas microbiota‐induced changes in tryptophan metabolism generate kynurenine and indole derivatives that perturb glutamatergic transmission and synaptic plasticity. 16 Third, neural pathways relay gut signals to brainstem and limbic circuits through vagal afferents. 17 Fourth, reciprocal interactions between the microbiota and the HPA axis couple peripheral stress responses to central regulation. 18 Together, these routes provide a mechanistic framework that links alterations in the gut microbiota to central nervous system function and mood regulation.

Over the past decade, several interventions targeting the MGB axis have been investigated for the treatment of MDD, with a particular focus on adolescents and young adults. These interventions, including probiotics, prebiotics, postbiotics, synbiotics, faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), antibiotics and antidepressants, as well as lifestyle modifications such as exercise and dietary adjustments, have shown potential in alleviating depressive symptoms. These strategies operate through a complex interplay of neuroendocrine regulation, immune modulation, restoration of microbial diversity and production of key microbial metabolites, thereby influencing both the gut microbiota and central nervous system function (figure 1). Probiotics and prebiotics modulate microbial composition and function, and certain probiotic strains have shown preliminary signals of antidepressant efficacy through MGB mechanisms. Examples include high‐fibre diets, prebiotic diets, strain‐specific probiotics, postbiotics, modulation of tryptophan–kynurenine or bile‐acid receptor signalling, physiological vagal neuromodulation and exploratory FMT. 19

Taken together, the current evidence remains limited and heterogeneous, precluding firm conclusions about which MGB‐targeted interventions may be most relevant for depression in adolescents and young adults. This review synthesises preclinical and clinical evidence on strategies that modulate the MGB axis in depression, including dietary interventions, exercise, probiotics, prebiotics, postbiotics, synbiotics, FMT and other exploratory microbiota‐related approaches, with a particular focus on adolescents and young adults. We searched literature indexed in PubMed, Embase and Web of Science from database inception to 25 March 2025, with priority given to developmentally appropriate rodent studies and human studies focusing on adolescents and young adults, whereas selected mixed‐age studies were included when directly relevant to the scope of this review. Studies were included based on their contribution to clarifying therapeutic mechanisms, the consistency of current findings and the clinical implications of MGB‐targeted interventions in this population. By integrating evidence on microbial composition, metabolite profiles, inflammatory signalling and related neurobiological processes, we aim to delineate key mechanistic pathways, identify interventions warranting more rigorous evaluation and highlight outcome domains of particular relevance for future trials and clinical translation.

MICROBIOTA DYSBIOSIS IN YOUTH DEPRESSION

The developmental stage of an individual is a critical determinant of both gut microbiota composition and the functional dynamics of the MGB axis. As individuals develop from childhood through adolescence into adulthood, the gut microbiota undergoes significant changes that are closely linked to immune maturation and the regulation of mood and stress. During early childhood, a bifidobacteria‐dominated microbiota evolves rapidly, converging towards an adult‐like configuration by around 3 years of age, which plays a crucial role in immune system maturation and health. As individuals transition into adolescence, hormonal shifts are accompanied by significant changes in the microbiota, including increased production of SCFAs and expansion of taxa such as Clostridiales and Ruminococcaceae, reflecting maturation of the gut ecosystem. Adolescents also tend to display lower gut α‐diversity and a higher abundance of Bacteroidetes, which has been associated with increased vulnerability to mood disorders, particularly depression (table 1). 20 , 21

Adolescence represents a critical phase of neurodevelopment and gut microbiota maturation. During this period, the gut microbiota is highly dynamic and is influenced not only by developmental changes but also by external factors such as diet, stress and substance use. The HPA axis is highly reactive to stress, increasing susceptibility to the effects of microbial dysbiosis. Consistent with this, adolescents with depression often display an altered balance of Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, which correlates with increased inflammation and depressive symptoms. 22 , 23 This underscores the importance of early interventions targeting microbial imbalances in adolescents to mitigate long‐term mental health risks.

In young adults aged under 35 years, although the gut microbiota stabilises compared to that in adolescents, it remains susceptible to environmental stressors such as academic pressure, work‐related stress and lifestyle factors. This age group exhibits a more mature neurobiological state, with a comparatively less developmentally labile and, on average, a less stress‐reactive HPA axis than that of adolescents. 24 However, stress‐related dysbiosis may still disrupt emotional regulation. Research has demonstrated that specific probiotic strains, such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, are associated with improved mood regulation and reduced anxiety, 25 suggesting that interventions targeting microbial imbalances may help restore emotional stability and reduce stress‐induced symptoms in this age group. 26

For adults aged 36 years and older, the gut microbiota remains relatively stable, but age‐related declines in core commensals and increases in rarer taxa are linked to reduced stress resilience and increased inflammation. 27 Although core neurobiological systems supporting cognitive and emotional regulation are generally more established, ageing‐related microbial imbalances can still exacerbate depressive symptoms. In this context, interventions designed to maintain microbiota diversity and address age‐associated dysbiosis may be particularly important. Studies suggest that probiotics and prebiotics can help maintain a healthy gut microbiota, potentially improving both mental health and stress resilience in older adults. 28 , 29 Thus, interventions focusing on microbial balance, particularly those addressing age‐related changes, could enhance mental health outcomes in adults.

Collectively, these age‐related differences in microbiota composition and neurodevelopment highlight the need for age‐specific MGB axis‐targeted interventions for depression in adolescents and young adults. This developmental heterogeneity argues against a uniform intervention model and instead supports an age‐stratified framework in which microbiota‐directed strategies are considered in relation to gut microbial maturation, microbial stability and diversity, and stress‐related HPA axis and inflammatory profiles. 30 , 31 , 32

PROBIOTICS

Probiotics are defined by the Food and Agriculture Organization and World Health Organization as live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host. 33 To date, the United States Food and Drug Administration has approved two live biotherapeutic products containing viable microorganisms for the prevention of recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection: REBYOTA (fecal microbiota, live‐jslm) 34 and VOWST (fecal microbiota spores, live‐brpk). 35 Most other probiotics in the United States are regulated as dietary supplements rather than as drugs. 36 In China, the National Medical Products Administration has long authorised multiple live bacterial and microecological preparations, most of which are available over the counter, for gastrointestinal conditions associated with intestinal dysbiosis such as diarrhoea, abdominal bloating and constipation. Examples include triple‐viable Bifidobacterium formulations (Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus and Enterococcus) 37 and dual‐viable Bacillus subtilis preparations (B. subtilis and Enterococcus faecium). 38 These regulatory precedents support the safety and efficacy of probiotic medicinal products for gastrointestinal indications and provide a rationale for evaluating their potential in psychiatric disorders.

Preclinical studies

Preclinical evidence indicates that probiotics exert small‐to‐moderate adjunctive antidepressant and anxiolytic effects, and their efficacy is shaped by strain specificity, dose, duration, concomitant therapies (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [SSRIs]) and interindividual variability. Evidence relevant to adolescent populations is largely limited to rodent models, highlighting Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium as candidate genera (table 2). 60 , 61 , 62 Specifically, Lactobacillus plantarum 90sk and Bifidobacterium adolescentis 150 produced antidepressant‐like effects comparable to those of fluoxetine in mice 50 ; L. plantarum PS128 and the newly isolated L. plantarum strains 286 and 81 also showed benefits, though the underlying mechanisms remain to be clarified. 39 , 49 Furthermore, Lactobacillus reuteri NK33 and B. adolescentis NK98 alleviate stress‐induced depressive‐like behaviours accompanied by reductions in interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and corticosterone (CORT). 42

Mechanistically, chronic stress activates the HPA axis and elevates glucocorticoids, increasing intestinal permeability and LPS translocation and inducing proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL‐6), collectively driving depressive pathophysiology. 63 , 64 , 65 Probiotics may counteract this cascade by strengthening mucosal barrier integrity and suppressing neuroimmune activation. In stressed rodents, L. plantarum LP3, Lactobacillus rhamnosus LR5, Bifidobacterium lactis BL3 and B. breve BR3 normalised elevated CORT 47 ; Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis CCFM687 reduced both CORT and tumour necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) and improved epithelial barrier function 43 ; a mixture of Lactobacillus helveticus R0052, L. plantarum R1012 and B. longum R0175 decreased TNF‐α, interferon‐γ (IFN‐γ) and indoleamine 2,3‐dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), yielding antidepressant‐like effects. 40 Given that IDO1 is induced by inflammatory signals (e.g., TNF‐α, IL‐6 and IFN‐γ) and that its hyperactivity correlates with depression severity, 66 , 67 attenuation of this pathway offers a plausible immune–metabolic mechanism. Overall, specific strains within Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium constitute the main sources of current psychobiotic signals.

Clinical studies

Most clinical trials have investigated probiotic strains from the Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium genera (table 2). In a randomised controlled trial (RCT), 3 weeks of treatment with L. plantarum JYLP‐326 reduced depressive and anxiety symptoms in exam‐stressed students compared with placebo. 59 In young adults with insomnia, 30 days of L. plantarum PS128 improved sleep quality and lowered depressive symptoms and fatigue. 56 Among medical students during their 8‐week exam period, Lactobacillus casei Shirota attenuated cortisol surges and reduced stress‐related somatic symptoms compared with placebo. 52 In contrast, L. rhamnosus JB‐1 showed no advantage over placebo in an 8‐week crossover study in healthy young adults, with no significant effects on mood, cognition, stress reactivity or inflammatory markers. 53 In college students with MDD, an 8‐week four‐strain regimen (Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus and Bacillus spp.) improved cognitive function with reductions in cortisol and interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β). 58 An eight‐strain regimen (B. bifidum W23, B. lactis W52, Lactobacillus acidophilus W37, Lactobacillus brevis W63, L. casei W56, Lactobacillus salivarius W24 and Lactococcus lactis W19/W58) reduced cognitive reactivity to sad mood on the Leiden Index of Depression Sensitivity‐Revised Scale compared with placebo after 4 weeks of continuous treatment. 51 In another double‐blind, placebo‐controlled RCT involving 58 healthy adults, 4 weeks of a multispecies probiotic (Ecologic® BARRIER) attenuated stress‐related working‐memory impairments and modulated prefrontal activation. 54 Similarly, a 6‐week multispecies probiotic supplement (Lactobacillus fermentum LF16, L. rhamnosus LR06, L. plantarum LP01 and B. longum BL04) improved self‐reported sleep quality and mood in stressed adults. 55 Nonetheless, negative findings have also been reported: A 28‐day four‐strain regimen (L. casei, B. breve, Lactococcus lactis and Streptococcus thermophilus) did not significantly change anxiety or stress levels compared with placebo. 57

Probiotics also induce structural remodelling of the gut microbiome. Across animal and human studies, probiotic interventions do not consistently increase alpha‐diversity. 68 In contrast, beta‐diversity, defined as the overall community configuration, 45 frequently shifts towards patterns observed in healthy or control groups, indicating structural reorientation rather than a simple gain in richness. At the phylum level, studies commonly report an increased Firmicutes‐to‐Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio, 45 higher relative abundance of Bacteroidetes and lower relative abundance of Proteobacteria. At the genus or species level, stress paradigms and exam‐stressed cohorts 59 often show increases in Bifidobacterium and, in some contexts, Prevotella, accompanied by reductions in Bacteroides and Roseburia, whereas Lactobacillus is sometimes unchanged. In chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) mouse models, multistrain combinations of lactobacilli and bifidobacteria often restore stress‐induced dysbiosis towards a control‐like profile. Overall, the most reproducible findings are beta‐diversity reorientation and directional shifts in key taxa, with effect size and direction contingent on the specific strain, dose and duration of intervention. 69

Taken together, evidence from both preclinical and clinical studies suggests that L. plantarum PS128 and L. rhamnosus JB‐1 are among the most frequently studied probiotic strains in adolescents and young adults, although the strength and reproducibility of the evidence remain strain‐specific and uneven. Specifically, L. plantarum PS128 has shown promise in alleviating depressive and anxiety symptoms. 39 , 56 Evidence for L. rhamnosus JB‐1 is more equivocal; although it remains a biologically plausible candidate strain, the available findings do not yet support a definitive claim of consistent benefit for mood regulation or anxiety reduction. 46 , 53 However, the limited number of high‐quality studies specifically focusing on this age group, coupled with significant heterogeneity in probiotic formulations, dosing regimens and treatment durations, makes it difficult to definitively establish the optimal probiotic strains, dosages and treatment protocols.

PREBIOTICS AND POSTBIOTICS

Prebiotics are nondigestible substrates selectively utilised by host microorganisms to confer a health benefit (e.g., fructooligosaccharides from the traditional Chinese herb Morinda officinalis). 70 Postbiotics are inactivated microorganisms or their components that exert beneficial effects on the host (e.g., the SCFA sodium butyrate and heat‐killed Lactobacillus strains). 71 Both strategies are being investigated as adjunctive approaches for alleviating mood symptoms in adolescents and young adults (table 3).

Prebiotics

Preclinical studies

Preclinical evidence indicates that fructooligosaccharides derived from M. officinalis remodel the gut microbiota and attenuate depressive‐like phenotypes in stress‐exposed rats, accompanied by reductions in CORT levels. 72

Clinical studies

Human evidence in the target age range remains limited and preliminary. In a double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial involving healthy females aged 18–25 years, a 4‐week course of galactooligosaccharide supplementation reduced trait anxiety in participants with high baseline anxiety and increased faecal Bifidobacterium levels, though no significant effects on state anxiety or depressed mood scores were observed at the overall group level. 74 Conversely, a prospective cohort study of exam‐stressed medical students found no significant protective association between the intake of fermented foods or food‐derived prebiotics and subsequent cognitive or affective outcomes. 75 Narrative and scoping reviews highlight the biological plausibility of prebiotic interventions, for example, through SCFA production and immune–neuroendocrine modulation, while emphasising the need for adequately powered RCTs to establish efficacy. 76

Postbiotics

Preclinical studies

Preclinical evidence supports the potential of postbiotic strategies for mood regulation. The bacterial metabolite sodium butyrate has been shown to abolish LPS‐induced depressive‐like behaviours in mice and attenuate hippocampal microglial activation, as indicated by reduced Iba1 expression. 73 In addition, several heat‐killed bacterial strains have demonstrated antidepressant‐ and anxiolytic‐like effects in rodent stress models. For instance, supplementation with heat‐killed Enterococcus faecalis EC‐12 altered neurotransmitter receptor gene expression in the prefrontal cortex and alleviated anxiety‐like behaviours in mice. 77 Similarly, heat‐killed Lactobacillus preparations have been reported to modify gut microbiota composition and influence host behaviour. 78 Moreover, heat‐inactivated L. helveticus MCC1848 improved social interaction and sucrose preference in mice subjected to subchronic social defeat stress. 79 Taken together, these findings support a biologically plausible role for postbiotics in mood regulation, although the evidence to date is limited to preclinical models.

Clinical studies

At present, no clinical studies have evaluated postbiotic interventions in adolescents and young adults with MDD. The efficacy, safety and clinical applicability of postbiotic strategies in youth remain uncertain and must be established in rigorously designed trials incorporating developmentally sensitive outcome measures and mechanistic endpoints.

DAILY DIET

Accumulating evidence indicates that microbiota‐supportive dietary patterns can alleviate depressive symptoms, partly by reshaping the gut microbiome and modulating gut–brain signalling. 80 , 81 , 82 Accordingly, dietary intervention is more appropriately conceptualised as an upstream, modifiable influence on the MGB axis rather than as a discrete mechanistic category. 83 , 84 , 85 , 86 Specific plant‐derived polysaccharides and fermentable fibres, such as fractions derived from Dendrobium officinale, have been shown to restructure microbial communities, enhance the generation of neuroactive metabolites and SCFAs and exert both anti‐inflammatory and neurotrophic effects. 87 , 88 , 89 Because adolescence and early adulthood are periods of heightened microbiome plasticity, individuals in these developmental stages may be particularly responsive to dietary strategies targeting the MGB axis. 87 , 90 Study design and intervention parameters are summarised in table 4.

Preclinical studies

No animal models have directly tested daily dietary interventions for youth depression to date, but related experimental studies provide important insights. Alcohol‐soluble polysaccharides from D. officinale flowers reduced depressive‐like behaviours in mice through microbiota modulation and neurotrophic signalling. 89 Supplementation with SCFAs reversed depression‐ and anxiety‐like phenotypes in faecal transplantation models of treatment‐resistant depression. 88 Similarly, microbiota‐accessible carbohydrates prevented neuroinflammation and cognitive decline in diet‐induced obese mice, underscoring the relevance of immune and metabolic pathways in diet–microbiota–brain interactions. 97 In parallel, dietary n–3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (fish oil) improved depressive‐like behaviours and mitigated gut dysbiosis in a CUMS rat model, whereas olive oil did not confer comparable behavioural benefits. 93

Clinical studies

Clinical evidence broadly supports the preclinical findings, confirming that diet quality rapidly and reproducibly shapes the gut microbiome and has been linked to biological processes implicated in depression. 80 , 97 In an RCT, young males with MDD who followed a Mediterranean‐style diet for 12 weeks exhibited significantly greater improvement in depressive symptoms and quality of life compared with controls. 81 Similarly, a brief 3‐week Mediterranean‐style dietary intervention in young adults with elevated depressive symptoms and poor diet quality led to significantly greater improvements in depressive symptom ratings than those in controls maintaining their habitual diet, with benefits maintained at 3‐month follow‐up; notably, greater mood improvements were closely correlated with higher dietary adherence. 96 Observational studies support these findings, showing that higher overall diet quality is associated with fewer depressive symptoms, whereas greater intake of added sugars predicts increased risks of depression and anxiety. 98

In addition to global diet quality, specific nutrients show meaningful associations with depression risk. Individuals with depression consume less dietary fibre than controls, and higher fibre intake is linked to a substantially lower risk of depression. 99 Diets rich in omega‐3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as eicosapentaenoic acid and docosahexaenoic acid, show antidepressant effects in some patients, although outcomes vary by dose and formulation. 86 Polyphenol‐rich foods, including berries and green tea, are metabolised by gut microbes into bioactive compounds that influence inflammatory, oxidative and neurotransmitter‐related pathways. 84 These findings are consistent with evidence implicating oxidative and nitrosative stress in depression. 83 Furthermore, updated nutritional guidelines emphasise the Mediterranean diet as a model for promoting both metabolic and mental health. 82 Reviews highlight dietary modulation as a promising adjunctive approach for depression management. 80

In summary, dietary strategies that emphasise fibre, omega‐3 fatty acids and polyphenol‐rich foods may support gut microbial health and reduce depressive symptoms.

Exercise

Structured exercise also alleviates depressive symptoms, partly through modulation of gut microbiota and gut–brain signalling. 100 , 101 , 102 , 103 Exercise contributes to intestinal homoeostasis and enhances microbial diversity. 92 , 102 , 103 Accordingly, structured exercise should be regarded as a modifiable behavioural intervention within the broader MGB axis framework, rather than as a discrete mechanistic pathway. 103 , 104 As adolescence and early adulthood are periods of heightened microbiome plasticity, individuals in these developmental stages may be particularly responsive to exercise‐induced gut–brain modulation. 90 Study design and participant characteristics are summarised in table 4.

Synergistic effects are observed when exercise is combined with dietary interventions. In animal studies, aerobic training added to a polysaccharide‐rich diet enhanced beneficial bacterial taxa and promoted the production of neuroactive metabolites, producing greater antidepressant effects than either intervention alone. 105 Similarly, in translational contexts, combining regular exercise with a diet rich in fibre and polyphenols may optimise both gut microbial composition and host metabolic and inflammatory pathways relevant to depression. 85 , 105

Preclinical studies

Across diverse rodent stress models, exercise reduces depressive‐like behaviours while simultaneously remodelling both gut microbiota and neural plasticity markers. 92 In CUMS paradigms, 4 weeks of moderate‐intensity swimming altered microbial profiles, enriching taxa such as Desulfovibrio and Streptococcus, and improved depression‐related behaviours. 92 Similarly, 3 weeks of voluntary wheel running restored hippocampal cytoarchitecture, increased brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and reduced IL‐6, consistent with enhanced neuroimmune function. 91 Comparable findings across other aerobic regimens suggest a generalisable effect of exercise on the MGB axis, 102 , 103 with behavioural improvements consistently paralleling these biological changes. 91

Studies at the neural circuit level provide additional mechanistic insight. One pathway links peripheral sensory input from dorsal root ganglia to the dorsal raphe nucleus, which projects to the medial prefrontal cortex, thereby increasing central serotonin release and mitigating stress‐induced behaviours. 106 Exercise also reshapes gut microbial composition, enriching taxa such as Coprococcus and Blautia, which are associated with greater tryptophan availability and enhanced serotonergic tone. 107 , 108 Moreover, certain commensal bacteria (e.g., Bacteroides spp.) produce GABA, a neurotransmitter that may mediate postbiotic effects on central excitability and mood. 109 Most rodent studies to date are relatively short term (3–4 weeks), which strengthens causal inference regarding exercise‐induced microbiota–brain interactions but leaves the durability of antidepressant effects uncertain. 91 , 92

Clinical studies

Clinical trials and meta‐analyses consistently support exercise as an effective adjunctive strategy for depression in adolescents and young adults. 94 , 95 , 110 A systematic review and network meta‐analysis confirmed that exercise interventions significantly reduce depressive symptoms in this age group, with aerobic protocols ranking among the most effective. 110 In an RCT, adolescents with subthreshold depression who participated in a 12‐week school‐based aerobic exercise (AE) programme showed greater reductions in depressive symptoms than those in psychoeducational controls, alongside favourable changes in gut microbiota. 105 Among young females, 8 weeks of high‐intensity interval or sprint interval training reduced subclinical depressive symptoms. 94 In college‐aged adults, 6 weeks of moderate continuous AE lowered TNF‐α levels and improved mood relative to sedentary controls. 95 Reviews emphasise that exercise reduces both depressive symptoms and inflammation, with exercise intensity emerging as a critical determinant of outcomes and adherence. 101

Mechanistic studies highlight BDNF as a key mediator of exercise benefits: astrocytic overexpression of BDNF in the hippocampus promotes local neurogenesis and elicits anxiolytic‐like effects. 111 BDNF also supports memory and synaptic plasticity under both healthy and pathological conditions. 112 Translational work extends external validity beyond younger populations, as exercise interventions in older adults with mild cognitive impairment have improved both cognition and mood. 113 Overall, moderate‐intensity exercise regimens appear to provide the most favourable balance between efficacy and tolerability in adolescents and young adults. 94 , 95

OTHERS

Faecal microbiota transplantation

In recurrent C. difficile infection, FMT has proven efficacy and is recommended by most guidelines for prevention 114 ; in irritable bowel syndrome and inflammatory bowel disease, some RCTs suggest that it may induce remission, though it remains exploratory overall. 115 FMT has an acceptable safety profile, with no serious adverse events reported in the available evidence to date. 116 In psychiatric disorders, particularly MDD, this approach remains at an early exploratory stage.

Preclinical evidence from depression models suggests that FMT from healthy donors may exert antidepressant‐like effects. 117 , 118 For example, Rao et al. reported that a 5‐day course of FMT administered to chronically stressed rats significantly reduced immobility in the forced swim test and increased sucrose preference relative to untreated controls. 118 These behavioural improvements coincided with restoration of gut barrier integrity and attenuation of neuroinflammation, as evidenced by increased expression of intestinal tight junction proteins (zonula occludens‐1 and occludin) and reduced levels of brain inflammasome components, such as NOD‐, LRR‐ and pyrin domain‐containing protein 3 (NLRP3), caspase‐1 and IL‐1β in FMT‐treated rats. 118 Likewise, Marcondes Avila et al. found that FMT normalised stress‐elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines (IL‐6 and TNF‐α) and oxidative markers in the hippocampus, suggesting that gut‐to‐brain signalling, in part via the vagus nerve, mediates these benefits. 117

Clinical observations provide preliminary support for these preclinical findings. 119 , 120 In a recent case report, two patients with treatment‐resistant MDD experienced marked symptom relief after 4 weeks of adjunctive FMT, with one patient's improvement persisting for up to 8 weeks postintervention. 119 Similarly, an open‐label study in patients with irritable bowel syndrome and related functional gastrointestinal disorders reported significant reductions in depressive and anxiety scores after FMT. 120 Although these clinical observations are uncontrolled and anecdotal, they are consistent with the hypothesis that microbiome modulation can influence mood.

Overall, available evidence suggests that FMT may ameliorate depressive symptoms by correcting dysbiosis‐associated pathways. 117 , 118 , 119 , 120 , 121 By repopulating the gut with beneficial microorganisms and their metabolites, FMT may strengthen gut barrier function, promote the production of neuroactive compounds and suppress intestinal and neuroinflammation. 117 , 118 The studies summarised in table S1 support the mechanistic plausibility of FMT as an MGB axis‐targeted intervention for depression, 117 , 118 although the current clinical evidence remains preliminary and largely uncontrolled. 119 , 120 These preliminary findings highlight FMT as an exploratory but promising strategy warranting further investigation.

Synbiotics

Synbiotics are defined as combinations of probiotics (live microorganisms) and prebiotics (substrates selectively utilised by host microorganisms) that confer health benefits on the host. The prebiotic component enhances the survival and activity of the probiotic strain, thereby amplifying therapeutic effects. 122 Recent studies indicate that synbiotic interventions can alleviate depressive symptoms through mechanisms involving the microbiota–metabolism–neuroimmune triad, partly by regulating intestinal microbial composition. 123 , 124

In a double‐blind, placebo‐controlled RCT, adjunctive synbiotic therapy led to greater reductions in Hamilton Depression Rating Scale scores at Week 6 compared with placebo, and this difference remained significant at Week 10 after adjustment for sex, age and body mass index. 125 Evidence from systematic reviews and meta‐analyses further indicates that gut microbiota‐targeted interventions improve depressive symptoms relative to controls, supporting the use of synbiotics. 126 A quantitative synthesis of 50 RCTs (across 62 datasets) reported significant overall effects of probiotic, prebiotic and synbiotic interventions on depressive symptoms, although considerable heterogeneity was observed. An additional evidence synthesis of 24 observational and 19 interventional studies suggests that, over 4–9 weeks, probiotic and synbiotic interventions produce greater reductions in depressive symptoms compared with placebo, whereas prebiotics show no consistent effect. 127 However, uncertainties remain regarding optimal strain selection, dosage and timing of administration, particularly in combination with antidepressants. 128 Taken together, the evidence suggests that synbiotics may serve as a promising adjunctive strategy for depression, although optimal regimens, integration with conventional care and clinical implementation parameters remain undefined.

Antidepressants

Antidepressants are pharmacological agents used to treat depressive symptoms such as low mood and feelings of worthlessness. First‐generation antidepressants, including tricyclic antidepressants and monoamine oxidase inhibitors, primarily act by inhibiting the reuptake of serotonin and noradrenaline or by suppressing monoamine oxidase activity. Second‐generation agents were developed for greater target selectivity, modulating specific neurotransmitter transporters or receptors, 129 , 130 which reduces off‐target interactions and may therefore mitigate adverse effects. 131

Beyond their central monoaminergic actions, antidepressants may also influence the gut microbiota, indirectly contributing to clinical improvement. 132 SSRIs and other agents have been observed to modify gut microbial composition, increasing the relative abundance of potentially beneficial taxa while reducing pathogenic taxa. These shifts have been linked with reduced systemic inflammation and enhanced intestinal barrier integrity, suggesting that antidepressants may exert part of their therapeutic benefit via the MGB axis. 133 Supporting this perspective, exploratory evidence from animal models indicates that microbiota‐related pathways may mediate antidepressant effects. For instance, a recent study demonstrated that the purine nucleoside inosine significantly alleviated depressive‐like and anxiety‐like behaviours in adolescent mice exposed to CUMS, in parallel with distinct alterations in gut microbiota composition, including reductions in Muribaculaceae abundance, which was positively associated with depressive phenotypes. 134

Taken together, these findings suggest that the microbiota may act as both a mediator and a potential target of antidepressant therapy. 135 , 136 Nevertheless, caution is warranted: human evidence remains limited, and microbiota alterations induced by antidepressants may reflect nonspecific drug effects rather than causal therapeutic mechanisms. At present, available human evidence remains insufficient to disentangle these relationships or to determine whether microbiota modulation can be translated into optimised treatment strategies for depression.

Antibiotics

Antibiotics are widely used to prevent and treat bacterial infections, acting through bactericidal or bacteriostatic mechanisms that disrupt key bacterial processes such as cell wall biosynthesis, protein synthesis and DNA replication. Beyond their antimicrobial effects, antibiotics also modulate the gut microbiota, which may, in turn, influence depression and anxiety. 137 Antibiotic exposure can substantially alter gut microbiota composition, leading to downstream effects on mood and cognition via the MGB axis. Broad‐spectrum antibiotics can deplete commensal gut bacteria and precipitate dysbiosis, which has been associated with increased anxiety‐like and depressive‐like behaviours. 138 Antibiotic overuse can lead to persistent dysbiosis that may exacerbate psychiatric symptoms. 139 These effects may reflect the role of the gut microbiota in regulating neurotransmitter synthesis, modulating immune responses and influencing HPA axis function. 140 Conversely, rational, targeted antibiotic use may reshape the microbiota and improve symptoms of depression and anxiety 138 ; proposed mechanisms include attenuation of inflammation, restoration of intestinal barrier integrity and normalisation of neurotransmitter biosynthesis. Overall, while mechanistically intriguing, antibiotics remain an exploratory concept rather than a viable therapeutic option for depression in current clinical practice.

LIMITATIONS

This review has several limitations in both the evidence base and methodology. First, the available clinical data in youth are limited in quantity and quality. Several MGB axis‐based interventions have been tested only in small or heterogeneous trials, often extrapolated from adult studies, which weakens confidence in their efficacy for younger populations. Postbiotic strategies remain largely supported by preclinical findings; human studies are needed to establish their safety and antidepressant potential. 141 Likewise, evidence for FMT in mood disorders is preliminary, consisting mostly of open‐label studies, case series or animal experiments; no sufficiently powered paediatric trials have yet confirmed its efficacy or long‐term safety in depression. 142

Furthermore, lifestyle‐based interventions that engage the MGB axis, such as diet and exercise, face inherent design and practical challenges. Although healthier dietary patterns and structured exercise programmes have been associated with mood improvements, these interventions cannot be double‐blinded, and their specific mechanisms are difficult to isolate. Additionally, most such studies typically involve short treatment durations and subclinical samples, and their outcomes may partly reflect general health benefits or placebo expectancy rather than purely microbiome‐mediated effects. The lack of standardised outcome measures across trials (e.g., varying depression scales, different microbiome or inflammatory endpoints) further complicates direct comparisons. Most trials to date have short follow‐up periods, so the durability of mood improvements and microbiome shifts remains unclear.

Finally, this narrative review itself is subject to methodological constraints. We did not perform a formal systematic search or meta‐analysis, introducing potential selection bias and preventing quantitative synthesis of effect sizes. The breadth of interventions covered, ranging from probiotics and dietary supplements to exercise and FMT, and the diversity of outcomes reported make it difficult to draw firm conclusions or identify which specific MGB axis modifications are most clinically relevant. Moreover, developmental differences in both depression pathophysiology and gut microbiome composition between adolescents and adults may limit the generalisability of adult findings to youth. In summary, these limitations constrain causal inference and reduce certainty regarding whether and how targeting the gut–brain axis can translate into reliable clinical benefits in adolescents and young adults.

CONCLUSION

Converging evidence indicates that interventions targeting the MGB axis can mitigate depressive symptoms in adolescents and young adults, although the magnitude of these effects is smaller and their consistency is more heterogeneous than in adults. The intervention modalities are broadly similar across age groups, including probiotics, prebiotics, postbiotics, dietary strategies, exercise and FMT, yet their outcomes diverge, underscoring the importance of developmental stage as a determinant of efficacy and safety. Mechanistically, these interventions converge on immune, endocrine and neuroplasticity pathways. In terms of neurobiological differences, adolescents undergo distinct developmental processes compared with young adults, particularly in aspects such as synaptic pruning, prefrontal‐limbic circuit maturation and HPA axis reactivity. These processes may render adolescents more sensitive to interventions targeting the MGB axis, although direct clinical evidence remains limited. The dynamic neuroinflammatory changes and neurotrophic signalling in adolescents may result in different therapeutic responses to MGB‐targeted interventions compared to those in young adults, whose neurobiological systems are more stabilised after maturation. Therefore, understanding how age‐specific developmental processes influence the efficacy of MGB‐targeted interventions is essential for tailoring effective treatments for both populations. Consistent findings include attenuated systemic and central inflammation, partial recalibration of HPA axis activity and enhanced neurotrophic and serotonergic signalling. Restoration of microbial balance, enrichment of SCFAs and reinforcement of barrier integrity are also frequently reported. Emerging evidence further implicates vagal signalling and tryptophan–kynurenine metabolism as potential mediators. Although biologically coherent, these mechanisms require rigorous causal validation and careful delineation of age‐specific windows of responsiveness.

From a clinical perspective, MGB axis interventions should be regarded as adjunctive rather than replacement strategies. Their favourable tolerability and noninvasive nature render them particularly relevant for younger populations, in whom conventional treatments are often constrained by adverse effects, poor adherence or stigma. When integrated with established therapies, these approaches may expand treatment options and address unmet clinical needs in this population. However, for MGB axis interventions to be effectively applied in clinical practice, they must be adapted to various clinical environments, including primary care settings, psychiatric hospitals and adolescent mental health centres, with clear protocols defining appropriate dosing, treatment duration and patient selection. Noninvasive approaches such as probiotics and dietary strategies may be more readily incorporated into routine care, whereas more intensive approaches such as FMT should remain investigational, particularly given the limited clinical evidence in younger populations. Long‐term safety surveillance and ethical safeguards, including informed consent and monitoring of developmental effects, are essential, particularly for adolescents.

Across intervention categories, the evidence profile remains uneven. Although probiotics constitute the most extensively investigated intervention category in adolescents and young adults, the available evidence remains constrained by limited sample sizes, findings that vary by strain and methodological heterogeneity, supporting an overall moderate evidence rating. Dietary interventions and structured exercise are also supported by moderate evidence, reflecting a broader, albeit still methodologically constrained, body of preclinical and clinical research. By contrast, the evidence for stand‐alone prebiotics remains low, whereas support for synbiotics is very low because direct clinical data in adolescents and young adults remain scarce. Evidence for postbiotics and FMT likewise remains low, as current conclusions rely predominantly on preclinical studies and preliminary human observations rather than adequately powered controlled trials in this population.

Future research should prioritise multicentre RCTs with adequate power across key developmental stages, particularly focusing on adolescents and young adults. To better understand how age‐specific developmental differences influence the MGB axis, it is essential to standardise outcome measures for each stage, accounting for variability in microbiota composition, symptom presentation, placebo responsiveness and developmental vulnerability. Neurobiological and psychosocial differences between adolescents and young adults may contribute to differential responses to MGB‐targeted interventions. Adolescents, owing to ongoing neurobiological changes, may be more susceptible to microbial imbalances, whereas young adults may show relatively greater neurobiological stability. Future studies should incorporate microbiome, metabolomic and inflammatory biomarkers to better elucidate therapeutic mechanisms. For FMT in particular, rigorous donor screening, process standardisation and long‐term safety surveillance are essential before wider clinical use. Personalised strategies guided by biological signatures and combined with lifestyle interventions may further enhance efficacy and scalability.

In conclusion, MGB‐targeted interventions represent safe, biologically grounded and clinically relevant complements to established therapies. However, evidence in adolescents and young adults remains weaker than that in adults, and differences at mechanistic and phenotypic levels warrant further clarification. Anxiety symptoms also merit attention in this context. Improvements in anxiety‐related outcomes have been described in studies of probiotics, 39 , 41 , 42 , 46 , 48 prebiotics, 74 , 75 dietary interventions, 93 , 96 structured exercise 92 , 95 and FMT. 117 Such observations suggest that some MGB axis‐related mechanisms and intervention strategies relevant to depression may also have implications for anxiety in adolescents and young adults. Nevertheless, the current evidence remains limited and heterogeneous and does not yet permit firm conclusions. Moreover, even within the adolescent and young adult cohort, developmental heterogeneity may engender differential responses to MGB‐targeted interventions, a possibility that remains poorly characterised. Recognising outcome differences across developmental stages and advancing rigorous, age‐specific research are crucial for translating the biological rationale into verifiable and reproducible clinical benefits for adolescents and young adults.
