Explainable machine learning for early prediction and anatomical classification of pulmonary embolism in the emergency department
Authors: Maryam Panahi, Mohanna Fallah Ghadi, Toktam Dehghani, Fatemeh Mahdizadeh, Raheleh Ghouchan Nezhad Noor Nia, Negar Saleh, Saeid Eslami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary thromboembolism (PTE) is a life‑threatening condition that requires prompt and accurate evaluation in the emergency department (ED). Standardized clinical scoring systems, including the Wells and revised Geneva scores, form the cornerstone of initial risk stratification but have limited specificity, leading to unnecessary D‑dimer testing and frequent overuse of CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA). This study aimed to develop explainable machine‑learning (XML) models as a complementary decision‑support layer following initial clinical assessment, with the dual goals of improving PTE prediction and providing an early, non‑imaging‑based indication of clot location.MethodsClinical and paraclinical data from 472 ED patients with suspected PTE were collected across three centers of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (2022–2024) using structured forms aligned with Wells/Geneva criteria. CTPA served as the reference standard for diagnosis and anatomical classification. We developed classical ML models, ensemble algorithms such as Extra Trees, and hybrid stacking pipelines. Explainable AI (XAI) using SHAP values quantified global and patientlevel feature contributions. The XML models were designed to operate after Wells/Geneva-based triage: supporting D-dimer decisions in low-risk patients and refining post-triage risk estimation in intermediate - and high-risk patients to help reduce avoidable CTPA utilization.ResultsIn this intermediate‑ to high‑probability cohort, all patients were classified as PTE‑suspect by Wells/Geneva scoring, whereas only 24% were confirmed positive on CTPA. ML models demonstrated improved discrimination, with Extra Trees achieving the best performance (accuracy 0.82, sensitivity 0.69, specificity 0.86, AUC 0.83). Among PTE‑positive cases, the model achieved an AUC of 0.77 for central emboli and 0.67 for peripheral emboli, with an overall anatomical classification accuracy of 63%.ConclusionsThe proposed XML models offer a transparent, clinically aligned framework that augments existing scoring systems, enhancing diagnostic efficiency and reducing unnecessary imaging.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12873-026-01602-y.Keywords: Pulmonary embolism (PE), Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), Machine learning (ML), CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA), Emergency department (ED)

Introduction

Pulmonary Thromboembolism (PTE) is an acute and life-threatening condition responsible for a significant percentage of out-of-hospital and in-hospital mortality and serious complications in emergency departments, particularly in patients over 50 years old [1, 2]. Nearly half of the patients with thrombosis in the veins of the legs or pelvis develop pulmonary embolism, many of whom are initially asymptomatic [3, 4]. The six-month mortality rate in the elderly population with PTE can increase by up to 20% [5, 6]. Since a significant portion of PTE-related mortality occurs within the first hours after the onset of the illness [7], rapid diagnosis and timely initiation of treatment are absolutely critical for saving patients’ lives and improving clinical outcomes.

Pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE) frequently manifests with nonspecific symptoms, most notably dyspnea and chest discomfort, which can easily mimic a wide range of pulmonary or cardiac conditions [8]. To initiate diagnostic evaluation, clinicians routinely employ established clinical prediction tools such as the Wells and Revised Geneva scores. These instruments synthesize key symptoms, physical findings, and patient risk factors to determine the pretest likelihood of PTE. Current evidence-based diagnostic pathways recommend first estimating this probability using the aforementioned scores, followed by D-dimer measurement and/or computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA), as appropriate. In individuals classified as having low or intermediate probability, PTE can generally be ruled out when the D-dimer concentration is ≤500 µg/L or below an age-adjusted threshold [9, 10]. Conversely, patients with a high pretest probability or elevated D-dimer levels should undergo CTPA [11]. Thus, the Wells and Revised Geneva scores remain fundamental for directing selective CTPA utilization, with CTPA continuing to serve as the gold standard for diagnosis.

However, numerous studies have shown that, due to their low specificity, these scoring systems yield many false positives [12]. This results in overutilization of the diagnostic gold standard, CT Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA) [13, 14]. Despite its high accuracy and sensitivity, CTPA requires advanced equipment, skilled specialists, and sufficient time for performance and interpretation [15–17]. Imposing this workload, especially in busy emergency departments and resource-limited centers, leads to delays in diagnosis and patient overcrowding as well as exacerbating health inequities [15]. Local data also indicate that standard scoring systems lack sufficient specificity in our centers, misidentifying a high volume of suspected patients.

In such circumstances, Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML)-based Clinical Decision Support Systems (CDSS) are emerging as novel and rapid tools to enhance diagnostic accuracy and facilitate triage [18–20]. ML algorithms can analyze large volumes of multi-faceted data (demographic, vital signs, paraclinical, and physical examination) to identify more complex patterns than linear scoring systems and optimally stratify high-risk patients [21]. However, existing local and global studies have predominantly focused on single-center data or small populations, and the main challenge is the trustworthiness of these models, which can be addressed using Explainable AI (XAI) methods [22, 23]. Therefore, the present study was designed to develop and validate explainable ML models as complementary to existing scores to improve the accuracy of predicting PTE occurrence and reduce unnecessary CTPAs, and to determine the anatomical location of the clot (central and peripheral).

Methodology

The present study was designed to develop and validate explainable machine‑learning (XML) models using clinical and paraclinical data from patients evaluated across three emergency departments. Importantly, the aim of the proposed models is not to replace standard diagnostic scoring systems for PTE, but rather to serve as a complementary decision-support layer following initial clinical risk stratification. In current clinical practice, all patients with suspected PTE undergo standardized assessment using the Wells and revised Geneva scores as a first step. Based on these established scores, low‑risk patients are recommended to undergo D‑dimer testing, and intermediate‑ and high‑risk patients are typically referred for CTPA. Our proposed XML framework is intended to be integrated after this initial standardized evaluation. Specifically, for low‑risk patients, the model supports decision-making regarding whether D‑dimer testing is necessary. For intermediate- and high‑risk patients, the model provides an additional layer of risk estimation that may help avoid unnecessary CTPAs while maintaining patient safety. The objective of this study is therefore to improve the accuracy and efficiency of PTE triage while minimizing avoidable imaging. The results are expected to support the development of a locally optimized CDSS that enhances diagnostic safety, reduces ED workload, and accelerates the management of suspected PTE, without bypassing or substituting existing evidence‑based scoring systems. Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted on data from patients presenting to three main emergency centers affiliated with Mashhad University of Medical Sciences. The collected data included patients who presented to these centers between 2022 and 2024 and were suspected of having PTE. The data were collected specifically for the AI application in a structured format based on clinical scoring systems (Wells and Geneva). The study’s primary objective is to reduce unnecessary CTPAs.

In accordance with standards for reporting diagnostic accuracy studies, we followed the STARD guidelines to ensure transparency and reproducibility in evaluating our ML models for PTE prediction.

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for the study consisted of patients who [1] were diagnosed with suspected PTE based on Wells or Geneva scores [2], underwent CTPA to confirm the diagnosis of PTE, and [3] had all required data, including demographic information, medical history, physical examination findings, vital signs, and paraclinical results, entirely recorded in their file.

Conversely, the exclusion criteria were [1] patients with incomplete medical histories or incomplete clinical and paraclinical data [2], patients who did not undergo CTPA or whose imaging quality was insufficient for diagnostic confirmation by a radiologist [3], patients lacking essential information for training ML models, such as age or vital signs, and [4] cases where the CT pulmonary angiography was requested based on clinical file correlation for a reason other than pulmonary embolism.

Target variables in the study

CT angiographies with a definitive report from faculty radiologists were included in the study. Based on the final CTPA report by radiology specialists, two separate output variables were defined as model labels [1]: Diagnosis of presence (Positive) or absence (Negative) PTE. [2] Anatomical classification: Central (including Saddle Emboli and emboli in the right or left main pulmonary artery branches (RT/LT Main Branch Emboli)) and Peripheral (Lobar/Segmental), including Lobar Emboli and Segmental Emboli.

Data preprocessing

In the initial step, descriptive statistics were performed for all variables. The association between qualitative variables and PTE presence was assessed using the Chi-square test. The statistical significance level was set at 0.05 or lower in all analyses. Subsequently, the raw patient data, after extraction from the imaging system and clinical records, underwent review, cleaning, and preparation. For standardization, missing values were handled, and categorical variables were incorporated into the ML models via binary encoding.

To enhance model capability and address the limited sample size for certain individual clinical features, we constructed three composite engineered variables using binary encoding. All underlying features were encoded as 1 (present) or 0 (absent). Each composite variable was generated using a logical OR operation, such that the final variable was assigned a value of 1 if at least one of its constituent features was present. Accordingly, the Overall_DVT variable served as a comprehensive indicator of deep vein thrombosis and combined localized tenderness of the deep venous system, upper limb swelling, entire leg swelling, calf swelling, and documented evidence of a DVT. The Overall_Recently_Bedridden variable reflected recent immobility status by aggregating the presence of recently being bedridden, paralysis, paresis, recent immobilization, or recent surgery/immobilization. Finally, the Overall_Lung_auscultation variable captured abnormal respiratory findings by combining decreased respiratory sounds in the right and left lungs or at the lung bases, as well as wheezing and bilateral rales.

Given the imbalanced data (355 patients without pulmonary thromboembolism and 117 patients with PTE), resampling methods were considered a statistical necessity, as models would otherwise overfit to the majority class and exhibit reduced accuracy in identifying patients with PTE. To address this challenge, a range of resampling approaches were evaluated, including over-sampling methods such as Random Over-Sampling (ROS), Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling Technique (SMOTE), Borderline-SMOTE, and ADASYN; under-sampling methods like Random Under-Sampling (RUS) and Tomek Links; and finally, hybrid methods such as SMOTE-Tomek and SMOTE-ENN. Among these methods, the SMOTE-Tomek algorithm achieved the best performance by combining synthetic data generation for the minority class (affected patients) with removing ambiguous samples from the majority class.

Model development

A multi‑stage modeling pipeline was implemented to integrate diverse machine‑learning families (linear, kernel‑based, neural, tree‑based, and gradient‑boosting methods) to capture heterogeneous patterns in PTE‑related clinical data. Classical baseline models included Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and a shallow Artificial Neural Network (ANN). These models provided interpretable and well‑established reference baselines. To enhance predictive performance for tabular clinical data, several ensemble learners were additionally employed, including Random Forest (RF), Extra Trees, XGBoost, and CatBoost. To leverage the complementary strengths of these ensemble models, a hybrid stacking architecture was developed as the final predictive framework. In this configuration, the out‑of‑fold predictions generated by the base learners (RF, Extra Trees, XGBoost, and CatBoost) were used as meta‑features for a Logistic Regression model acting as the meta‑learner. The complete list of implemented algorithms and their configurations is provided in Table S1 (Supplementary Material).

Model evaluation

The dataset was divided into training (80%) and test (20%) subsets using stratified sampling to maintain class balance. All hyperparameter optimization and resampling procedures were performed exclusively within the training subset, and the test set remained untouched and was reserved for final evaluation. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using a hybrid strategy that combines Random Search for broad parameter exploration and Grid Search for fine-grained refinement. The search space included variations in the number of estimators, tree depth, minimum samples per node, learning rate, and regularization parameters, depending on the algorithm. Model selection and tuning were embedded in a stratified 5-fold crossvalidation pipeline to ensure robust performance estimation while strictly avoiding data leakage. The complete hyperparameter ranges and final selected configurations are reported in Table S2 of the Supplementary Material.

To evaluate the performance of the developed models in predicting the occurrence and anatomical location of PTE, a comprehensive set of standard ML metrics was used. These metrics included Accuracy, Sensitivity/Recall, Specificity, Precision/PPV, F1-score, and the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC). The calculation formulas for these metrics (based on TP, TN, FP, FN values in the confusion matrix) are provided in Table S3 of the Supplementary Material.

Explainable models

To ensure that the ML outputs could be interpreted by physicians in a busy emergency setting, we used SHAPbased explanations that translate model predictions into clinically familiar reasoning patterns. Rather than providing raw visualizations, SHAP values were converted into structured summaries displaying: (i) the most influential features for each prediction, (ii) whether each feature increased or decreased the probability of PTE, and (iii) the magnitude of this effect. This approach mirrors bedside clinical assessment and allows the model to operate as an interpretable decisionsupport layer rather than a black box.

Comparison with clinical scoring systems (Wells and Geneva)

One of the most well-known scoring systems for estimating the probability of pulmonary embolism is the Wells clinical prediction rule (Wells Score), which combines clinical findings and underlying risk factors to estimate the likelihood of pulmonary embolism. A key point in this score is the physician’s clinical judgment in determining the probability of PE. The final score categorizes patients into three probability levels (low, moderate, and high). The Revised Geneva Score was also introduced as a completely objective scoring system to reduce bias arising from physicians’ subjective judgment in the Wells Score. This model relies more on quantitative and clinical data and is less dependent on diagnostic probability, thereby offering greater potential for standardization. Table S4 in the Supplementary Material illustrates how scoring is performed using the Wells and Revised Geneva Scores, based on the variables studied in this research. To more accurately assess the effectiveness and clinical value of the proposed models, the performance of the ML algorithms was compared with that of the Wells and Revised Geneva scoring systems for predicting pulmonary embolism.

Results

Model implementation environment

The predictive models were implemented, trained, and evaluated using Python and specialized ML libraries, including Scikit-learn, Numpy, CatBoost, SHAP, and PyCaret. The project source code is available at the following address to ensure reproducibility and future review: https://github.com/smartdxgen/Explainable-Artificial-Intelligence-for-Pulmonary-Embolism-Risk-Assessment

Descriptive data analysis

The descriptive analysis of the study population revealed that of the 472 patients suspected of pulmonary embolism who were screened using the Wells and Geneva clinical scoring systems, only 117 (24.8%) received a positive CT angiography diagnosis of pulmonary embolism. This relatively low prevalence rate in the suspected population underscores the importance of more effective filtering and accurate diagnostic tools in the emergency department. The gender distribution in the total population was almost balanced (Female 53%). There were four pregnant women, indicating few cases and a lack of samples in this area.

Correlation of the study population’s features with the target feature evaluation

Analysis of clinical and demographic characteristics between the PTE-positive and PTE-negative subgroups showed that the most prominent clinical factors associated with an increased probability of PTE, with statistically significant differences (p-value < 0.05), were: Pleuritic chest pain, syncope, and sudden onset of symptoms. Regarding risk factors and medical history, variables such as being recently bedridden, having a history of previous deep vein thrombosis, undergoing recent surgery or Immobilization, and hormone use were significantly more prevalent in the PTE-positive group. Physical examination findings related to DVT, including localized tenderness and the entire leg swollen, also showed a strong correlation with the final PTE diagnosis. Finally, vital signs, including tachycardia (PR > 100), tachypnea (RR > 20), and systolic blood pressure below 100, were clearly significant indicators of physiological disturbance in PTE-positive patients. A comparison of clinical and paraclinical variables between the PTE-positive and PTE-negative subgroups revealed significant differences in certain variables, as detailed in Table 1.

Evaluation of pairwise correlation of features in the study population

Analysis of pairwise feature correlations revealed that some variables showed strong, significant correlations. Because the data were categorical, the Chi-square test was used to assess feature correlations. Figure S1 in the Supplementary Material displays the p-values from the Chi-square test. p-values less than 0.05 are displayed in a darker color and larger p-values in a lighter color, clearly indicating significant and non-significant relationships between each pair of features. Previously, deep vein thrombosis showed a strong correlation with Overall_DVT, suggesting shared clinical information regarding the patient’s venous status. Additionally, PR > 100 and RR > 20 were significantly correlated, indicating a co-occurrence of cardiorespiratory dysfunction in patients with PTE. On the other hand, active cancer and hormone use had a significant association, which may be due to underlying conditions or related treatments. Additionally, the sudden onset of symptoms was correlated with an alternative diagnosis and PR > 100, suggesting that these features facilitate rapid diagnosis and symptom severity.

Evaluation of feature distribution in patients with pulmonary thromboembolism

In the PTE-positive subgroup (n = 117), patients had a nearly equal gender distribution (Female = 51.3%) with a slight predominance of individuals under 50 years old (54.7%). The most important clinical findings in this group indicated a focus on severe cardiac and pulmonary dysfunction; dyspnea with a very high prevalence (94.9%), sudden onset of symptoms in 76.9%, and signs of hemodynamic dysfunction such as low oxygen saturation (<95%) in 87.2%, tachycardia (PR > 100) in 71%, and tachypnea (RR > 20) in 61.5% were the most prominent findings. Other symptoms included pleuritic chest pain in 30% and syncope in 17% of patients. Also, clinical histories related to thromboembolism included recent immobilization in 58%, findings of DVT in 37%, and active malignancy in 24%.

Regarding the anatomical location of the embolism in CTPA examinations, the prevalence of segmental emboli (87.2%) was higher compared to other types (embolism in the right main pulmonary branch (34.2%) or left (29.1%)). This distribution indicates a predominance of peripheral emboli over higher-risk, life-threatening emboli such as saddle embolism (6.8%).

The distribution of pleural effusion was examined in terms of anatomical orientation, and a tendency towards right-sided effusion (23.9%) was predominantly observed. However, the statistical test results indicated that the presence of pleural effusion on the right, left, or both sides was not significantly associated with PTE diagnosis in the studied sample.

Evaluation of ML models in early prediction of pulmonary thromboembolism

A broad spectrum of machine learning algorithms (classical models (LR, SVM, ANN), ensemble methods (RF, Extra Trees, XGBoost, CatBoost, AdaBoost), and a hybrid Stacking model) was evaluated using standardized performance metrics (Fig. 3). Consistent with prior EDbased diagnostic studies, ensemble learning techniques demonstrated clear superiority over classical algorithms. Among them, the Extra Trees model achieved the most favorable overall performance, with the highest F1score (0.65), a robust AUC (0.83), and acceptable accuracy (0.82) (Figure S2.1 in Supplementary Material). Clinically, the model yielded a balanced diagnostic profile characterized by high specificity (0.86) and moderate but reliable sensitivity (0.69). This balance is particularly relevant for optimizing CTPA utilization by reducing unnecessary imaging while maintaining adequate detection of clinically significant PTE.

Comparison of the performance of ML models in early diagnosis of PTE

While Extra Trees outperformed other methods, the comparative analysis offers clinically relevant nuances. The XGBoost model achieved strong accuracy (0.80) and the highest specificity (0.87), making it powerful for minimizing false positives. However, its lower sensitivity (0.60) highlights a greater risk of missed diagnoses. The classical SVM model provided competitive baseline performance (accuracy: 0.81; MCC: 0.48) but lacked the discriminative strength of ensemble models (AUC: 0.79). Conversely, logistic regression demonstrated high sensitivity (0.80) but markedly low precision (0.43), resulting in limited overall reliability (MCC: 0.39). Calibration analysis of the Extra Trees model (Figure S2.2 in Supplementary Material) demonstrated acceptable probability estimation, with the calibration curve aligning closely with the ideal diagonal. Minor overprediction at high-probability levels and conservative estimates at low-probability ranges were observed, suggesting opportunities for further refinement.

Comparison of ML model performance with Wells and Geneva scoring methods

All patients included in this study underwent standardized clinical assessment using the Wells and Revised Geneva scores before imaging and were classified as intermediate‑ or high‑probability. By design, both scoring systems channel such patients toward definitive imaging. Consequently, within this pre‑selected, high‑risk test cohort (n = 142), the scoring systems flagged virtually all patients as PTE‑suspect. In this restricted context, the confusion matrix counts for Wells/Geneva are structurally constrained to true positives (TP = 35, 24%) and false positives (FP = 117, 76%), with TN = 0 and FN = 0. This lack of specificity does not reflect poor intrinsic performance; rather, it is the expected statistical behavior when applying pretest‑probability tools to a sample already enriched for suspected PTE.

In contrast, the ML model provides incremental diagnostic value by recovering specificity within this high‑risk cohort. For the Extra Trees classifier on the same test set (n = 142), the confusion matrix was: TN = 92, TP = 24, FN = 11, FP = 15 (Figure S3). These counts correspond to a sensitivity of 68.6% and a specificity of 85.9%, indicating that the model correctly identified most PTE‑positive cases while reclassifying a substantial proportion of PTE‑negative patients as lower probability. Specifically, approximately 72% of patients without PTE in this high‑risk test set were correctly identified as TN, potentially allowing clinicians to reconsider the immediate need for CTPA in select cases.

Explainable Extra Trees model

SHAP analysis demonstrated how the Extra Trees model integrates acute symptoms, vital signs, and predisposing risk factors in a manner consistent with real‑world ED reasoning. Although “sudden onset of symptoms” is not specific to PTE, within this intermediate‑ to high‑probability cohort, it functioned as a synergistic risk amplifier, increasing the predicted probability of PTE primarily when accompanied by tachypnea, tachycardia, or recent immobilization. This pattern mirrors clinical practice, in which an abrupt onset heightens suspicion only when embedded within a compatible physiological and risk‑factor profile.

Figure 4. a shows that sudden symptom onset had the highest mean absolute SHAP value (0.0946), making it the most influential global predictor. Vital sign abnormalities (tachypnea [RR > 20] and tachycardia [PR > 100]) ranked next, reflecting the model’s reliance on indicators of cardiopulmonary strain. Established risk factors such as recent immobilization and prior deep‑vein thrombosis (Overall_DVT) also contributed substantially, in line with known epidemiological pathways. Moderately influential variables included the presence of an alternative diagnosis, systolic blood pressure < 100 mmHg, and age > 50 years, each shifting predictions in clinically intuitive directions. In contrast, the history of COPD, fever, and syncope exhibited minimal global influence. Figure 4.b (Beeswarm plot) illustrates how specific feature values shift individual predictions: higher values for variables such as sudden onset, elevated respiratory rate, elevated heart rate, bedridden status, and previous DVT increase predicted risk, whereas COPD history lowers it. Predicting the location of pulmonary thromboembolism

In addition to predicting the presence of pulmonary thromboembolism, the study explored the feasibility of estimating embolus location using clinical and paraclinical variables. Emboli were categorized into two clinically relevant groups: central (saddle or main pulmonary artery involvement), typically associated with greater hemodynamic risk, and peripheral (lobar, segmental, or subsegmental branches).

The ExtraTrees classifier, trained with SMOTE, Tomek links, and class weighting to address class imbalance, achieved an accuracy and F1 score of 0.63 on the test set. Confusionmatrix analysis showed that 61% of central emboli and 65% of peripheral emboli were correctly identified, indicating moderate discriminative ability. Featureimportance analysis (Figure S4 in the Supplementary Material) revealed that the most influential predictors were: alternative diagnosis, history of deep vein thrombosis, pleuritic chest pain, abnormal lung auscultation, recent immobilization, and respiratory rate above 20 breaths per minute. These findings demonstrate that, while anatomical prediction based solely on clinical variables is challenging, the model can capture clinically meaningful patterns that differentiate central from peripheral emboli.

Discussion

This study highlights a persistent structural challenge in emergency medicine: the systematic overclassification of patients as suspected PTE when using conventional clinical scoring systems such as the Wells and Revised Geneva scores. In our cohort of 472 patients already deemed intermediate-to-high probability by these tools, nearly three-quarters (75.2%) were ultimately PTE-negative on CTPA. This high proportion of negative imaging does not reflect a failure of the scoring systems but rather their intentional design to prioritize sensitivity and avoid missed diagnoses in acute settings. Within this context, the Extra Trees model operates as a posttriage, adjunctive decisionsupport tool. Its balanced diagnostic performance (AUC 0.83, accuracy 0.82, sensitivity 0.69, specificity 0.86) provides additional risk discrimination within an already highrisk population, and its high specificity suggests that a meaningful subset of patients may be safely reclassified as lower likelihood for PTE and thereby reducing unnecessary CTPA use while maintaining detection of clinically significant disease.

Beyond binary prediction of PTE, the framework evaluated whether readily obtainable clinical and paraclinical variables could offer early insight into clot anatomy. The model differentiated central from peripheral emboli with approximately 63% accuracy (AUC 0.67–0.77), demonstrating moderate discriminative ability. Although inadequate for guiding invasive interventions such as systemic thrombolysis or embolectomy, this capability offers a preliminary, non-imaging-based stratification layer that may support early decision-making—particularly in high-volume or resource-limited EDs where imaging delays are common. Because central emboli are more likely to be associated with hemodynamic compromise, even a coarse probabilistic estimate of clot location can help prioritize monitoring intensity and accelerate diagnostic workflows. Nevertheless, definitive anatomical classification must remain imagingbased, and further refinement incorporating laboratory biomarkers and physiologic measures is needed before such models can influence timecritical therapeutic decisions.

A comparative analysis with the existing literature underscores the distinct contribution of this study (Table 2). Despite relying solely on bedside ED variables, our model performed comparably to far larger inpatient or ICU cohorts, such as those reported by Ryan et al. (AUC 0.85, N = 60,297) [29] and Rapuri et al. (AUC ≈0.83, N = 164,383) [29]. Differences from laboratory-dominant models, such as Liu et al. (AUC 0.99) [3], primarily reflect the lower signal-to-noise characteristics of real-time ED data rather than algorithmic inferiority. Importantly, our cohort reflects a realistic ED PTE prevalence (~25%), avoiding the extreme imbalance observed in studies such as Ben Yehuda et al. (prevalence ~4%) [24], which required heavy oversampling and consequently carries calibration limitations. These factors position our work among the few AI systems developed and validated specifically for the diagnostic inflection point, where ED clinicians first consider PTE and must decide on low or high-resource imaging.

Two features of our model represent noteworthy advancements over prior studies. First, the use of SHAPbased explainability at both global and patient levels enables clinicians to interrogate nonlinear interactions that conventional scores and many ML models obscure. Second, prediction of central versus peripheral clot location using nonimaging data adds clinically meaningful contextualization, offering an early indicator of severity and the potential need for escalation. Previous ML research has predominantly focused on inpatients, patients with confirmed PTE deterioration, or ICU monitoring, leaving ED triage relatively unexplored. By providing interpretable, EDspecific decision support before CTPA, our model contributes to the development of transparent, clinically compatible AI tools suited to real-world emergency care.

Limitations and future work

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The modest sample size (n = 472) and recruitment from three centers may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability, particularly across EDs with differing workflows and patient demographics. Certain high‑risk subgroups, such as pregnant patients or individuals with atypical presentations, remain underrepresented. Dynamic laboratory biomarkers (including D‑dimer and troponin) were not routinely available in this cohort because local practice directed intermediate- to high-probability patients directly to CTPA; their absence likely constrained predictive performance, as prior studies have demonstrated improved accuracy when such variables are incorporated. Although SMOTE‑Tomek methods were applied to mitigate class imbalance, synthetic resampling can still introduce calibration drift, so performance estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Regarding anatomical prediction, it is important to emphasize that early localization does not currently alter initial anticoagulation management. In our cohort, no patients underwent emergent catheter‑directed thrombectomy or surgical embolectomy. Thus, localization should be viewed as a preliminary severity indicator rather than a determinant of reperfusion therapy. Most importantly, the absence of external prospective validation limits confidence in transportability across heterogeneous institutions and electronic environments. Future work should include multicenter external validation, integration of laboratory biomarkers, and real‑time evaluation within clinical workflows to determine operational impact on CTPA utilization, decision‑making efficiency, and patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the Extra Trees model offers a robust and interpretable machine learning framework for early prediction of PTE and preliminary anatomical localization among emergency department patients already pre-screened as intermediate-to-high probability by conventional scoring systems. Using only routinely collected clinical and demographic variables, the model achieved strong diagnostic performance (AUC 0.83, accuracy 0.82) and showed the potential to improve risk stratification within this highrisk cohort. Importantly, the model also demonstrated moderate capability in estimating clot location (central vs. peripheral), offering early signals that may support preliminary risk assessment, but not replace imaging. SHAPbased interpretability confirmed that the dominant predictors identified by the model align with established pathophysiological pathways, reinforcing its clinical face validity. Overall, this work provides a methodological foundation for developing explainable MLbased decisionsupport tools for emergency PTE assessment. However, prospective, multicenter validation and integration with laboratory biomarkers and electronic health records will be essential before the model can be translated into routine clinical practice.
