Ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis in periodontitis: Biological insights and therapeutic advances
Authors: Yangan Chen, Fumiao Lu, Zicong Zhang, Xin Li, Rongxin Zhang, Tengyi Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPeriodontitis is a severe global public health issue associated with the development and exacerbation of various systemic diseases, underscoring the need for further research and clinical attention. Ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis have garnered attention as prominent domains of investigation in the medical field; however, their exact roles in the pathogenesis of periodontitis are not yet fully understood. This review aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of current research on ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis in periodontal disease, while exploring the roles of redox homeostasis, lipid metabolism, and iron metabolism in linking ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis in the context of periodontitis. Furthermore, we propose that macrophage reprogramming may act as a unifying mechanism that integrates these three factors in the disease process, clarifying the potential interconnection between macrophage efferocytosis and ferroptosis during periodontitis. This article systematically summarizes the molecular mechanisms that govern ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis in periodontitis, offering innovative theoretical perspectives on its pathogenesis and identifying potential therapeutic targets for intervention.KEYWORDS: Periodontitis, ferroptosis, macrophage efferocytosis, macrophage reprogramming

Introduction

Periodontitis, characterized by periodontal inflammation and alveolar bone loss, is a multifactorial infectious disease initiated by pathogenic oral bacteria, which ultimately results in progressive destruction of the periodontium. Globally, over 1 billion individuals suffer from severe periodontitis, with projections indicating that this number may reach 1.5 billion by 2050 [1]. Among these, approximately 660 million patients are expected to experience tooth loss. This trend underscores the emergence of periodontitis as a significant global public health issue affecting individuals across all to suggest that periodontitis is not only a major oral health concern but also exerts critical influence on the initiation and progression of numerous systemic diseases [2–8] (Figure 1). Despite ongoing research, the precise underlying mechanisms of periodontitis remain inadequately elucidated. Consequently, it is essential to investigate the pathogenesis of periodontitis to develop safe and effective treatments for this disease process.

Periodontal disease and associated comorbidities.

In recent years, ferroptosis, a novel form of programmed cell death (PCD) characterized by an iron-dependent oxidative imbalance in the intracellular microenvironment, has attracted significant attention in the medical field. Ferroptosis is predominantly initiated by the dysfunction of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), which is a critical regulator of intracellular redox homeostasis [9]. Iron homeostasis is essential for the redox cycle reactions required for normal biological functions, while intracellular iron overload can stimulate the generation of significant amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation via the Fenton reaction. Therefore, ferroptosis acts as a risk factor for diverse inflammatory diseases that exacerbates inflammatory responses and injury expansion [10].

Efficient clearance of apoptotic cells by macrophages and other phagocytes, a process termed efferocytosis, is recognized as a crucial process for maintaining cellular homeostasis under normal physiological conditions and for restoring homeostasis following infection or injury [11]. Multiple previous studies have pointed out the pivotal role of apoptosis and efferocytosis in the progression and resolution of inflammation [12–14]. A critical resolution mechanism of inflammation is neutrophil depletion in the inflamed tissue through spontaneous neutrophil apoptosis and reduced neutrophil infiltration, followed by the elimination of apoptotic neutrophils via macrophage efferocytosis. Research has demonstrated that effective efferocytosis by M2-like macrophages (anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotype) prevents the release of inflammatory cytokines, indicating that efferocytosis is a brake to inflammatory response [15]. Macrophage efferocytosis dysfunction has been reported to be closely related to the occurrence and development of various inflammatory diseases [16–19].

While numerous studies have previously revealed the involvement of ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis in various pathological conditions, their precise roles in the etiology of periodontitis remain to be fully elucidated. Therefore, this review aims to systematically and comprehensively synthesize current knowledge on ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis within periodontal inflammation and investigate the roles of redox homeostasis, lipid metabolism and iron metabolism in linking ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis during periodontitis. Furthermore, there may exist a potential unifying mechanism linking ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis in periodontitis, a relationship that has not been previously investigated. We suppose that macrophage reprogramming may serve as a common mechanism connecting these two processes during periodontitis. An enhanced comprehension of these pathogenic mechanisms may inform the development of novel therapeutic approaches to periodontitis.

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis in periodontitis

Previous research has demonstrated a strong positive correlation between the elevated iron levels and the pathogenesis and severity of periodontitis, indicating that iron dyshomeostasis has been associated with periodontitis [20–23]. Iron overload induced by periodontal pathogenic bacteria can activate a reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst in periodontium and further increase susceptibility to pathogenic bacterial infection, ultimately leading to ferroptosis and subsequent damage to the periodontium [23–28]. Bioinformatics research has also been widely used to predict the putative connections between periodontitis and ferroptosis. Ferroptosis-related genes seem to be involved in periodontitis pathogenesis via regulating immune microenvironment and cellular metabolism, potentially providing novel avenues for the diagnosis and treatment of periodontitis [29–36]. However, the exact molecular mechanisms through which ferroptosis is implicated in the etiology and progression of periodontitis remain insufficiently understood.

Recently, increasing evidence has revealed an association between periodontitis and ferroptosis, suggesting that ferroptosis may contribute to the pathogenesis of periodontitis of periodontal inflammation (Table 1) [37–43]. Therefore, ferroptosis intervention may be an effective strategy to attenuate periodontitis, and several ferroptosis inhibitors have been shown to exert significant anti-inflammatory effects on periodontitis both in vitro and in vivo (Table 2) [44–50]. Additionally, ferroptosis in the course of periodontitis may be a contributing factor to various systemic health conditions. Yao et al. underscored the critical roles of Porphyromonas gingivalis (P.g) in driving ferroptosis in hepatocytes, exacerbating alcoholic liver disease and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease in murine models [51,52]. Similarly, periodontal pathogens could aggravate chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in murine models via inducing ferroptosis in lung tissue [53]. Indoxyl sulfate in the gingival crevicular fluid from mice with chronic kidney disease was demonstrated to promote alveolar bone resorption through ferroptosis via blocking the SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway [54].

The interconnection between ferroptosis and dysregulation of metal homeostasis in periodontitis

Metal homeostasis plays an essential role in various fundamental biological processes, including cellular metabolism and energy production, oxygen transport and hemoglobin function, genetic material synthesis, cellular signaling, and enzyme catalysis and cofactor functionality. Previous scientific literature emphasizes the critical relationship between metal homeostasis and the pathogenesis, progression, and therapeutic management of periodontitis, highlighting the determinant role of metal ions, such as iron, copper, calcium, zinc, potassium, and manganese, in both the development and treatment of the disease [55–59]. A substantial body of evidence from basic and clinical research has elucidated a striking convergence between periodontitis and ferroptosis in the pathophysiological characteristics, such as oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation [60–63]. Given the presence of similar characteristic markers involved in ferroptosis and periodontitis, such as iron and ROS, we suppose that iron homeostatic imbalance mediated by periodontal pathogens potentially represents a unifying mechanism between ferroptosis and periodontitis. A positive feedback loop may exist between periodontal pathogens and iron concentration during periodontitis. Periodontal pathogens contribute to iron overload by degrading ferritin, which ultimately disrupts the balance of the oxidation-antioxidant system within the periodontium. Conversely, elevated intracellular iron concentrations may heighten susceptibility of periodontal tissue to bacterial infection and to oxidative damage [20,23,26,27,64,65]. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that increased intracellular copper levels may play a crucial role in regulating ferroptosis. Elesclomol-induced mitochondrial copper overload triggered ROS accumulation and ferroptosis activation in colorectal cancer cells by degrading copper-transporting ATPase 1 [66]. Copper-dependent autophagic degradation of GPX4 drove ferroptosis in a pancreatic cancer mouse model via the direct interaction between copper and GPX4 cysteines [67]. Notably, significantly elevated levels of copper were observed in the saliva and serum of patients with periodontitis, indicating a disruption in copper homeostasis under periodontal inflammation. Further, a positive correlation was identified between copper levels and the periodontal index [55,68]. Liu et al. also revealed that the intracellular copper levels exhibited a threefold increase in P.g LPS-treated HGFs compared to untreated controls [69]. However, the complex immunomodulatory role of copper complicates the understanding of pathogen-host interactions [70,71]. Invading microbes must acquire sufficient copper from the host environment to colonize and establish infection through essential cuproenzymes, whereas copper overload can be lethal to bacteria through producing ROS and degrading essential bacterial enzymes. We hypothesize that a self-reinforcing cycle of ferroptosis and copper overload with significant proinflammatory potential may emerge from the depletion of glutathione (GSH), a copper/iron chelator, during periodontal pathogen attacks. Iron overload induced by pathogens leads to ferroptosis through GSH reduction and mitochondrial impairment, resulting in excessive intracellular copper in periodontal tissues. Copper overload triggers the aggregation of lipoylated proteins, loss of iron-sulfur cluster proteins, ultimately inhibiting tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and liberating free Fe3+. The interaction between Fe3+ and GSH can further inhibit the GSH/GPX4 pathway by converting GSH to oxidized GSH (GSSG) and supplying ample Fe2+ for the Fenton reaction, thereby intensifying ferroptosis in periodontal tissue. Given this, the host immunomodulation response to periodontal infection may alter metal homeostasis and promote oxidative stress in periodontal tissues, potentially accelerating ferroptosis and periodontitis progression.

The maintenance of metal homeostasis is also vital for mitochondrial function. The proper functioning of the mitochondrial electron transport chain and numerous other mitochondrial processes are known to be significantly influenced by iron and copper within the mitochondria. A growing body of literature supports the observation that mitochondrial dysfunction is an important component of periodontitis pathogenesis. Fluctuations in mitochondrial function are critical signaling events and directly reflect how cells respond to infections by periodontal pathogens. Various forms of mitochondrial dysfunction, such as oxidative stress, mitophagy, mitochondria-mediated apoptosis, and metabolic disorders, are frequently observed in periodontal disease [72–77]. Disruption of mitochondrial metal homeostasis can also aggravate oxidative damage through mitochondrial ROS overgeneration and trigger ferroptosis [78,79]. Conversely, ROS can exacerbate mitochondrial damage, forming a vicious cycle [80]. Therefore, periodontal infection-induced disruption of trace metal homeostasis may constitute a significant factor contributing to excessive ROS generation and mitochondrial dysfunction in the periodontium, ultimately inducing ferroptosis and aggravating periodontal tissue destruction (Figure 2).

Potential therapeutic strategies against periodontitis via ferroptosis inhibition

Based on these findings, strategies targeting restoration of iron and copper homeostasis represent a critical advancement in periodontitis therapy, offering novel therapeutic avenues. The copper/iron chelator GSH may be a promising treatment target for mitigating periodontal tissue damage through the maintenance of metal homeostasis, which has also been shown in Tables 1 and 2 [39,42,45,50]. GSH serves as a critical enzymatic cofactor that facilitates the catalytic activation of GPX4, which can effectively inhibit lipid peroxidation and ROS accumulation, thereby preventing ferroptosis during periodontitis progression [9,81,82].

Antioxidant therapies are also expected to become effective treatment options against ferroptosis in periodontitis. Several natural antioxidant agents have been identified as promising strategies to combat oxidative damage associated with periodontitis. For instance, quercetin has been shown to reduce oxidative damage in periodontal ligament cells (PDLCs) by NRF2 activation, while also attenuating alveolar bone loss in murine periodontitis models [83]. Resveratrol can prevent alveolar bone loss in a periodontitis rat model by reducing the production of circulating ROS through the NRF2/heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) pathway [84]. Furthermore, the development of nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems has opened up new gate for antioxidant treatment in periodontitis. A multifunctional controlled-release drug delivery system, created by encapsulating self-assembled nanoparticles within a hydrogel matrix, exhibited excellent antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties in RAW 264.7 cells. In the experimental diabetic periodontitis rat models, it effectively alleviated periodontal tissue inflammation and curtailed alveolar bone loss by minimizing inflammatory infiltration and collagen degradation [85]. Similarly, zeolitic imidazolate framework 8 (ZIF-8)-based nanoparticles, in conjunction with minocycline hydrochloride, synergistically has been demonstrated to enhance resistance to inflammation and oxidative stress during periodontitis through sustained release of minocycline hydrochloride with anti-inflammatory and antibacterial properties, alongside the generation of zinc ions with pro-antioxidant effects during degradation [86]. Manganese-doped self-assembling Prussian blue nanoparticles, crosslinked with negatively charged human serum albumin, exhibited the capacity to scavenge various ROS, including superoxide anion, free hydroxyl radicals, singlet oxygen and hydrogen peroxide both in vitro and in vivo, thereby alleviating periodontal inflammation and alveolar bone loss [87].

To conclude, ferroptosis is considered a potential risk factor for periodontitis and related systemic diseases. However, Saikosaponin A has been demonstrated to mitigate osteoclastogenesis and alveolar bone loss in experimental periodontitis rat models through the induction of ferroptosis in osteoclasts via the NRF2/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway, suggesting the protective function of ferroptosis against alveolar bone resorption [88]. Ferroptosis may exhibit differential regulatory roles during periodontitis, largely contingent upon the specific cell type undergoing ferroptosis. Notably, plenty of studies have found that other types of PCD, such as apoptosis, pyroptosis and cuproptosis, are involved in the periodontal inflammatory response [69,89–93]. Due to the molecular similarity between ferroptosis and other PCD pathways, their specific correlations in the pathogenesis of periodontal disease have not yet been fully clarified. Besides, the instability of animal models and disparities in the local microenvironment between animal models and humans have led to the general perception that the anti-periodontitis effect of ferroptosis inhibitors, as observed in animal studies, may not be as reproducible in human clinical trials. Therefore, there remains limited insight into the application of ferroptosis intervention as a therapeutic strategy for periodontitis, warranting further investigation.

Macrophage efferocytosis

Macrophage efferocytosis and inflammation resolution

Macrophage efferocytosis in vivo consists of three sequential phases: recognition of apoptotic cells, binding of apoptotic cells, and subsequent engulfment and degradation of apoptotic cells [94]. A dynamic balance between macrophages efferocytosis and apoptosis plays vital roles in fundamental cellular processes that are necessary for development, differentiation, and tissue homeostasis [95]. Sustained macrophage efferocytosis has been shown to inhibit secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines while upregulating expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines, facilitating inflammation resolution and tissue homeostasis [13,16,96]. According to different functions and phenotypes under various circumstances, macrophages can be divided into pro-inflammatory M1-like macrophages and anti-inflammatory M2-like macrophages. It is important to note that the process of efferocytosis, where macrophages engulf apoptotic cells, is primarily carried out by M2-like macrophages and is critical for timely resolution of inflammation [14]. Current research suggests that there are two major pathways for the crosstalk between macrophage efferocytosis and inflammation resolution. On the one hand, the microenvironment established by apoptotic cells drives macrophages into an anti-inflammatory phenotype and results in an increased secretion of pro-resolving cytokines. Apoptotic cells create a transient and localized immunosuppressive environment to prevent unwanted immune responses, which inhibit the immune cell recruitment to inflammatory sites through neutralizing inflammatory chemokines via elevated CCR5 expression [97]. The soluble substances secreted by apoptotic cells, including anti-inflammatory cytokines like TGF-β and IL-10, pro-resolving factors such as annexin-A1 and thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1), and cellular metabolites like spermidine, may promote the differentiation of macrophages to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and enhance the capacity for sustained efferocytosis [98–102]. Moreover, “find-me” and “eat-me” signals emitted by apoptotic cells can draw macrophages to the location of cell death, exhibiting immunomodulatory effects that may induce M2 macrophage polarization [103]. On the other hand, the regulated engulfment of apoptotic cell remnants by macrophages through efferocytosis is required to avert exacerbated autoimmune responses and to facilitate the resolution of inflammation. The degradation of apoptotic cells by macrophage efferocytosis is accompanied by massive release of proteins, nucleotides, carbohydrates, and lipids into macrophage metabolism and energy cycles. These materials result in a significant accumulation of cellular components in macrophages, which need to either be transformed and recycled by macrophages or excreted to prevent further accumulation, thereby regulating the transition of macrophages to an anti-inflammatory phenotype [104,105]. Overall, macrophage efferocytosis plays a crucial role in maintaining homeostasis by clearing apoptotic cells and promoting M2 macrophage polarization, which in turn suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines and enhances anti-inflammatory mediators.

Macrophage efferocytosis in periodontitis

Neutrophils, as a critical part of innate immunity for host defense, are initially recruited to the infected site during periodontitis, where they combat invading microorganisms through phagocytosis and ameliorate inflammation before undergoing apoptosis [106]. Upon invasion by periodontal pathogens, the resident immune cells of the periodontium, including macrophages and mast cells, identify these pathogens and initiate the transendothelial migration of neutrophils into the gingival sulcus through traversing the gingival epithelium-capillary interface, thereby establishing an immune barrier against biofilm-associated infections. As neutrophils migrate toward sites of inflammation, they rapidly eliminate invading microorganisms and manage the inflammatory response by neutrophil degranulation, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase-dependent ROS generation, antimicrobial peptide production, and neutrophil extracellular trap formation [107]. Following the ingestion and elimination of periodontal pathogens, neutrophils undergo apoptosis and are predominantly cleared by macrophage efferocytosis, thereby maintaining tissue homeostasis and preventing an excessive immune response. In the context of periodontitis, macrophages demonstrate significant plasticity in response to various stimuli. The excessive activation of pro-inflammatory macrophages can result in inflammation and the destruction of periodontal tissues. Macrophage efferocytosis of apoptotic neutrophils can establish an anti-inflammatory microenvironment, which regulates the polarization of macrophages toward a pro-resolution phenotype. When fatty acids from apoptotic neutrophils undergo mitochondrial oxidation, there is a shift in the oxidized nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)/reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) ratio, which promotes the release of anti-inflammatory mediators by macrophages [108]. Additionally, putrescine produced during protein metabolism aids in Rac1 activation, thereby enhancing the phagocytic capacity of macrophages [109]. Lactic acid generated from the fermentation of sugars by macrophages can bolster their own efferocytosis activity while simultaneously regulating neighboring macrophages to exert anti-inflammatory effects [110,111]. The protective role of macrophage efferocytosis during periodontitis has been confirmed by multiple independent studies (Table 3) [112–115]. Consequently, the prompt removal of apoptotic neutrophils from an inflammatory periodontal environment, prior to their transition to secondary necrosis, is essential to prevent the continual release of pro-inflammatory and immunogenic intracellular contents that could induce periodontal tissue damage and exacerbate periodontitis.

The interplay between regulatory T cells (Tregs) and macrophage efferocytosis in periodontitis

Tregs, a specialized subset of CD4+ T lymphocytes, are widely recognized as the primary effectors of immune tolerance, which play a crucial role in maintaining immune homeostasis and promoting tissue regeneration. Elevated Tregs have been observed in periodontitis conditions, whereas functional inhibition of Tregs has been reported to aggravate disease progression in experimental animal models [116,117]. Numerous studies indicate that Tregs can contribute to the amelioration of periodontal inflammation through multiple mechanisms, including the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as transforming TGF-β and IL-10, the release of proteins associated with tissue repair processes, and the modulation of inflammatory cell metabolism [118]. The imbalance between T helper 17 (Th17) cells and Tregs is a significant aspect of immune dysregulation in periodontitis. Okui et al. demonstrated that both Th17 and Treg cells were more abundant in periodontitis lesions compared to gingivitis, indicating a shift in immune cell dynamics during disease progression and highlighting the pathogenetic significance of Tregs [119]. Additionally, various therapeutic strategies targeting the Th17/Treg axis have been developed to alleviate immune disorders in periodontitis, including all-trans retinoic acid, subcutaneous immunization with P.g, IL-35, exosomal microRNA-155-5p derived from periodontal ligament stem cells, and Eldecalcitol [120–124].

Considerable evidence has demonstrated a complex interplay between Tregs and macrophage efferocytosis in modulating inflammation resolution during periodontitis.

Stem cell therapy fosters the differentiation of naive CD4+ T cells into Tregs and facilitates the polarization of macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This therapeutic approach restores the dysregulated balance between Th17 cells and Tregs in inflamed periodontal tissues while augmenting macrophage efferocytosis, ultimately promoting periodontal tissue repair and regeneration [125,126]. Similarly, the role of GAS6, a key homeostatic immunological regulator, has also been emphasized in the interplay between Tregs and macrophage efferocytosis. GAS6 is involved in promoting the clearance of apoptotic cells and maintaining Treg balance, which is crucial for preventing microbial dysbiosis and subsequent periodontitis [125]. In addition to these findings, the relationship between Tregs and macrophage efferocytosis is characterized by a mutual enhancement, synergistically potentiating the anti-inflammatory effects in periodontitis [127]. Macrophage efferocytosis not only facilitates the differentiation of CD4+ T cells to Tregs and proliferation of Tregs but also stimulates the production and secretion of TGF-β, an essential cytokine for Treg cell induction [128,129]. Conversely, Tregs have been shown to secrete IL-13 to promote macrophage efferocytosis through a regulatory transcellular signaling pathway that enhances apoptotic cell internalization, such as IL-10 production and Rac1 activation, creating a positive feedback loop [130]. This bidirectional interaction highlights the cooperative role of Tregs and macrophage efferocytosis in the efficient clearance of apoptotic neutrophils and the resolution of inflammation, underscoring their joint immunoregulatory functions in periodontitis.

Potential therapeutic strategies against periodontitis via restoring macrophage efferocytosis

The underlying causes of anomalies in macrophage efferocytosis can be classified into two primary categories: aberrations in neutrophil recruitment and dysregulation of efferocytosis activity.

The relationship between neutrophil recruitment and periodontitis has garnered significant attention in recent literature. Impaired macrophage efferocytosis resulting from a deficiency in neutrophil recruitment is closely associated with periodontal tissue damage. Leukocyte adhesion deficiency-type 1 (LAD1) is a genetic disorder characterized by impaired recruitment of neutrophils to peripheral tissues, resulting from mutations in the CD18 subunit of β2 integrins. This condition leads to an inability to generate anti-inflammatory signals due to defective efferocytosis, ultimately resulting in overactivation of the IL-23/IL-17 axis. Elevated levels of IL-23 and IL-17 in the periodontium directly contribute to periodontal tissue damage, thereby explaining the rapid progression of gingivitis and periodontitis in LAD1 patients [131]. Kajikawa et al. further reported that neutrophil transplantation or synthetic agonist-induced activation of efferocytosis-related receptors, such as liver X receptor (LXR) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR), could inhibit IL-23/IL-17 expression and mitigate periodontitis by promoting macrophage efferocytosis in the CD18−/− murine periodontitis models, suggesting LXR and PPAR as potential therapeutic targets for treating periodontitis in LAD1 patients [132]. Moreover, an integral myeloid-specific IFN-1 pathway has been shown to be capable of protecting against osteoclastogenesis by significantly increasing IL-17-mediated neutrophil recruitment in the gingiva, which improved alveolar bone resorption in murine models of periodontitis [133]. Therefore, future therapeutic strategies for periodontitis should focus on restoring macrophage efferocytosis by regulating neutrophil recruitment. This could be achieved by targeting the dysregulation of the IL-23/IL-17 axis and harnessing the therapeutic potential of LXR and PPAR agonists (for example, GW3965 and GW0742). Additionally, interventions aimed at enhancing the myeloid-specific IFN-1 pathway could present innovative strategies for reducing periodontal tissue destruction and improving clinical outcomes in periodontitis patients, particularly in those with underlying immune deficiencies such as LAD1.

Persistent accumulation of neutrophils in the periodontium, stemming from dysregulated macrophage efferocytosis, represents a substantial contributor to periodontal tissue destruction. A class of endogenous lipid-derived molecules with potent anti-inflammatory properties termed specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) have been recognized as critical players in the inflammation resolution via regulating the activity of macrophage efferocytosis [134]. Notably, recent years have witnessed numerous studies investigating the application of SPMs in the treatment of periodontitis. SPMs generated during the acute inflammatory phase exhibit significant pro-resolution and restorative functions within periodontal tissues. For example, lipoxin A4 (LXA4) could suppress LPS-induced inflammation through the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88)/nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway in PDLCs, while a significant reduction in its relative content levels has been observed in the saliva of patients with periodontitis [135–137]. These findings suggest that the development of periodontitis may be linked to insufficient macrophage efferocytosis due to low L×A4levels. Similarly, during non-surgical periodontal therapy, dietary supplementation with high-dose of precursors of SPMs, such as eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), in the patients with severe periodontitis may have potential therapeutic benefits in the management of periodontitis. This therapeutic effect was observed through a reduction in salivary levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, accompanied by a shift toward elevated levels of anti-inflammatory IL-10 and pro-regenerative fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) [138–140]. Further investigations also revealed that multiple SPMs associated with macrophage efferocytosis might confer protection against periodontitis, such as resolvin D1/D2/E1/E2, maresin-1 and maresin-2 [141–146]. Therefore, enhancing macrophage efferocytosis through SPM supplementation may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of periodontitis. Additionally, the periodontal pathogen P.g has been reported to induce apoptosis in smooth muscle cells, which may indirectly affect macrophage efferocytosis through the activation of the TLR2 pathway, suggesting that oral pathogens not only contribute to periodontal tissue destruction but also impair the ability of macrophages to clear apoptotic cells [147]. Antibacterial therapy targeting periodontal pathogens may also serve as an effective strategy to enhance macrophage efferocytosis and alleviate periodontitis. Similar results were achieved in other independent studies through enhancing macrophage efferocytosis, showing the significant anti-inflammatory effects on periodontitis both in vitro and in vivo (Table 4) [148–152]. These findings above collectively indicated the protective role of macrophage efferocytosis during periodontitis and the therapeutic potential of the augmentation of macrophage efferocytosis for periodontal inflammation.

In conclusion, effective macrophage efferocytosis is a critical biological process in the inflammation resolution and tissue repair during periodontitis, involving a highly regulated mechanism that encompasses recognition, binding, and subsequent digestion of apoptotic cells by macrophages (Figure 3). Impairments in this intricate process serve as a key pathogenic factor contributing to the progression and persistence of periodontal inflammation. The investigation into the mechanisms and functional roles of macrophage efferocytosis in periodontitis could potentially translate into novel therapeutic approaches. Future research efforts should prioritize the development of periodontitis treatments aimed at augmenting macrophage efferocytosis, including strategies such as SPM supplementation, antimicrobial therapy, and the maintenance of neutrophil homeostasis. However, research on the signaling molecules involved in macrophage efferocytosis in the pathological progression of periodontitis remains limited, such as the interplay between macrophage reprogramming and efferocytosis, and the effect of macrophage life cycle on its efferocytosis activity. A thorough examination of the mechanisms by which macrophages execute efferocytosis could pave the way for developing novel therapeutic strategies that might prove beneficial in specific targeted therapy for periodontitis in the future. Additionally, the treatment related to macrophage efferocytosis in periodontitis is predominantly confined to cell and animal models, with little development being made in the clinical context. It is crucial to conduct further research to investigate how regulating macrophage efferocytosis affects susceptibility to periodontitis and to assess the safety of this approach in clinical settings. Finally, whether distinct forms of PCD modulate the functional activity of macrophage efferocytosis in resolving inflammation during periodontitis remains to be thoroughly investigated. A comprehensive understanding of the interplay between PCD modalities and efferocytic function could provide novel insights into the pathogenesis of periodontitis and illuminate potential therapeutic targets, thus warranting further research.

The protective role of macrophage efferocytosis in periodontitis. As ubiquitous and indispensable components of innate immunity, neutrophils are the first leukocytes recruited to the infected site in periodontitis, where they combat invading periodontal pathogens through the mechanism of phagocytosis, thereby mitigating inflammatory responses. However, certain periodontal pathogens have developed strategies to evade neutrophil-mediated immune clearance. Subsequent to the ingestion and elimination of periodontal pathogens, neutrophils undergo programmed apoptosis, primarily being cleared by macrophage efferocytosis, a process that maintains tissue homeostasis and prevents an exaggerated immune response. Rapid clearance of apoptotic neutrophils from the inflamed periodontium, before their progression to secondary necrosis, represents a critical step in preventing the persistent release of pro-inflammatory and immunogenic intracellular contents, which can otherwise exacerbate periodontal tissue damage. Macrophage efferocytosis of apoptotic neutrophils can establish an anti-inflammatory microenvironment through inducing M2 macrophage polarization and secreting IL-10 and TGF-β. Additionally, a complex crosstalk between the Th17/Treg pathway and macrophage efferocytosis has been demonstrated in the resolution of inflammation in periodontitis. On the one hand, macrophage efferocytosis decreases the RANKL/OPG ratio through inhibiting the IL-23/IL-17 axis, ultimately reducing osteoclast differentiation and alveolar bone resorption. On the other hand, macrophage efferocytosis promotes the differentiation of CD4+ T cells to Tregs and stimulates TGF-β-mediated Treg cell induction. These Tregs can release immunosuppressive cytokines, including IL-10 and TGF-β, to ameliorate periodontal inflammation and tissue damage. Conversely, Tregs augment macrophage efferocytosis via the IL-13/IL-10/Rac1 axis, creating a positive feedback loop. Enhancing macrophage efferocytosis is a potential therapeutic strategy for periodontitis treatment through a variety of therapeutic interventions, such as SPM supplementation, LXR and PPAR agonists, activation of the myeloid-specific IFN-1 pathway, and antibacterial therapy targeting periodontal pathogens.

The crosstalk between ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis in periodontitis

Recently, the intricate relationship between cell clearance and cell death has emerged as a burgeoning and fascinating field of investigation in various physiological and pathological cellular processes. While the two separate processes, ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis, are characterized by distinct cellular mechanisms, there is growing evidence of their certain potential connections and interdependencies that have already been identified. Pan et al. demonstrated that ferroptotic mesenchymal stem cells can protect mice against sepsis by promoting macrophage efferocytosis, underscoring the therapeutic potential of the complex interplay between macrophage efferocytosis and ferroptosis [153]. In this section, we present a comprehensive overview of the potential interplay between macrophage efferocytosis and ferroptosis in the periodontitis pathogenic mechanisms.

Redox homeostasis

Ferroptosis can result in an imbalance between oxidants and antioxidant defense systems, ultimately playing a critical regulatory role in the development and progression of periodontitis. Oxidative stress is also a crucial pathological factor that contributes considerably to the dysfunction of macrophage efferocytosis. MERTK, the essential efferocytosis receptor expressed on macrophages, was subjected to proteolytic cleavage when exposed to specific pro-inflammatory factors like ROS, thereby rendering it dysfunctional [154]. Activating the 90 kDa ribosomal S6 kinase (p90RSK)-NRF2 signaling pathway has been shown to robustly inhibit macrophage efferocytosis by generating mitochondrial-derived ROS [155]. Conversely, apoptotic cell membrane-coated antioxidant nanozymes have emerged as potent inducers of macrophage efferocytosis by enhancing macrophage recognition of “eat-me” signals while simultaneously scavenging intracellular excessive ROS [156]. Baicalin also increased macrophage efferocytosis by acting as an antioxidant via blocking the RhoA/Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK) pathway and regulating macrophage polarization from M1 to M2 phenotypes, thereby facilitating inflammation resolution [152]. Similar results were obtained through the activation of immune response gene 1 (IRG1)/NRF2/T-cell immunoglobulin domain and mucin domain 4 (TIM4) pathway [157]. Together, these findings suggest that oxidative stress induced by ferroptosis may have a suppressive impact on macrophage efferocytosis in the context of periodontitis.

Impaired macrophage efferocytosis is closely associated with an inflammatory periodontal environment that is typically characterized by the release of numerous pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. The inflammatory factors have been shown to cause increased ROS production, while ROS production further exacerbates the inflammatory response [158–160]. Furthermore, the surge in ROS levels results in the GSH depletion and a compromised antioxidant defense system, which may hinder the effective elimination of lipid peroxides and increase susceptibility to ferroptosis during periodontitis [161]. Interestingly, macrophage efferocytosis has also been found to trigger increased ROS production and contribute to oxidative stress in macrophages, which potentially augment cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis via the promotion of lipid peroxidation and destruction of antioxidant defense systems [162,163]. Therefore, macrophage efferocytosis may regulate the susceptibility of periodontal cells to ferroptosis by a bidirectional effect on cellular oxidative stress in periodontitis.

Lipid metabolism

Macrophage efferocytosis necessitates a highly dynamic remodeling of cellular lipids to enable the recognition, binding, and subsequent engulfment of apoptotic cells. This mechanism entails the translocation of phosphatidylserine to the exofacial surface of the apoptotic cell membrane, directing macrophages to identify and engulf apoptotic cells effectively through the “eat-me” signal. Notably, dysregulations in lipid metabolism can also impact the vulnerability of cells to ferroptosis primarily owing to specific lipids that exhibit a high propensity for peroxidation, consequently contributing to the exacerbation of ferroptosis. A growing evidence body of evidence suggests that certain lipids may be central to interplay between the crosstalk between ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis. Previous studies have revealed that omega-3-enriched lipid emulsions enhanced macrophage efferocytosis and phagocytosis through the involvement of 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX), 12-LOX and 15-LOX during murine polymicrobial sepsis, while lipid peroxidation mediated by 5/12/15-LOX has been identified as a critical factor that directly contributes to the ferroptosis process [140,164–166]. Similarly, supplementation of n-3-polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) promoted effective autophagy activation in macrophages, thereby promoting the excretion of cholesterol levels and improving the activity of macrophage efferocytosis [167]. Excessive PUFA accumulation also represents a critical marker of ferroptosis, since PUFAs are highly prone to lipid peroxidation and result in elevated MDA levels [168].

Empirical evidence has shown that periodontal infection significantly alters lipid levels in both the gingival crevicular fluid and serum during periodontal inflammation, and this alteration simultaneously serves as the critical linkage between periodontitis and systemic diseases [169]. Given the close association between lipid metabolism and periodontitis, we speculate that the role of lipid metabolism in the context of periodontitis is multifaceted, not only affecting ferroptosis pathways but also influencing periodontal inflammation resolution mediated by macrophage efferocytosis. The modulation of lipid metabolism through pharmacological interventions may provide a novel therapeutic avenue for promoting resolution of inflammation and enhancing tissue repair in periodontal disease. SPMs derived from PUFAs, particularly n-3 and n-6 PUFAs, are integral to the resolution of endogenous inflammation, whose protective role against periodontitis has been previously discussed in this article [134–146]. It is noteworthy that these mediators have also been identified to counteract the pro-inflammatory effects of ferroptosis under various pathological conditions. Maresin1 suppressed ferroptosis via stimulating the NRF2/SLC7A11/GPX4 axis, thereby protecting against sepsis-induced acute liver injury and kidney injury [170,171]. Lipoxin A4-mediated ferroptosis inhibition was observed in alveolar type II epithelial cells via NRF2 activation, while Lipoxin A4 treatment attenuated lung ischemia-reperfusion injury in murine models [172]. Consequently, SPM supplement may be a promising therapeutic approach for periodontitis treatment by promoting macrophage efferocytosis and inhibiting ferroptosis.

Iron metabolism

Recent studies have highlighted the intricate relationship between iron metabolism and macrophage function, particularly in the context of efferocytosis. Iron overload has proven to be able to impair macrophage efferocytosis by inducing mitochondrial and lysosomal dysfunction, thus accelerating plaque rupture in atherosclerosis [173]. Research has found that SIRT3 knockdown downregulates the levels of efferocytosis receptor MERTK and triggers disrupted mitochondrial iron homeostasis, accompanied by pro-inflammatory cytokines production in cardiac macrophages, whilst an iron chelator deferoxamine can reverse the abovementioned phenomenon through improving macrophage efferocytosis and alleviating mitochondrial iron overload [174]. Additionally, macrophage efferocytosis of eryptotic erythrocytes facilitated hematoma clearance and neurological recovery during intracerebral hemorrhage by reducing iron deposition in the brain and decreasing macrophage proinflammatory phenotype via the AXL/MERTK axis [175]. Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), known as a key regulator of ferroptosis via degrading heme into free iron that disrupts the iron homeostasis, has also been demonstrated to enhance the engulfment and anti-inflammatory responses in macrophages during efferocytosis [176].

As mentioned above, emerging evidence suggests a potential positive feedback loop between periodontal pathogens and iron levels during periodontitis progression [20,23,26,27,64,65]. We suppose that dysregulation of iron metabolism can disrupt the precise balance between macrophage efferocytosis and ferroptosis, leading to pathological consequences in the periodontium. Elucidating how iron metabolism influences macrophage efferocytosis efficiency and the susceptibility to ferroptosis can facilitate the identification of novel therapeutic targets and intervention strategies for periodontitis treatment.

The potential unifying mechanism linking ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis in periodontitis——macrophage reprogramming

The phenotypic transformation of macrophages has been identified as a crucial factor in the advancement of inflammatory diseases. The damage to periodontal tissue during periodontitis is predominantly ascribed to the innate immune response to periodontal pathogens, with an increasing amount of evidence highlighting the role of M1 macrophages in promoting osteoclast differentiation in the periodontal inflammatory microenvironment. In contrast, M2 macrophages are recognized as essential cells in maintaining periodontal tissue homeostasis, which have been implicated in tolerance induction processes that mitigate inappropriately excessive host immune responses stimulated by the pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) pathway [177]. Macrophage efferocytosis is a critical process in the resolution of inflammation, facilitating the transition of macrophages from a pro-inflammatory phenotype to a pro-resolving phenotype, a process referred to as macrophage reprogramming. This transition is characterized by a decreased synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines and an increase production of pro-resolving mediators. Recent studies have found that macrophage reprogramming is crucial in the progression of periodontal damage, highlighting the potential to regulate macrophage reprogramming to develop innovative and effective therapeutic approaches for periodontitis [113,178]. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that ferroptosis-related signaling pathways are closely associated with macrophage reprogramming, particularly those involved in iron metabolism, redox homeostasis, and lipid metabolism, indicating that macrophage reprogramming may be a potential unifying mechanism linking ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis in periodontitis.

Macrophage reprogramming is intricately connected to the accumulation of key metabolites involved in macrophage behavior and functionality, which are carefully orchestrated across multiple metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis, TCA cycle, iron metabolism, and lipid metabolism. Pro-inflammatory macrophages and pro-resolving macrophages have been proven to exert distinct iron handling capabilities to influence their roles in immune responses and tissue homeostasis by regulating iron storage and recycling. M1 macrophages promote iron retention by mediating iron efflux, characterized by diminished expression of ferroportin, CD163, and HO-1, while exhibiting elevated levels of ferritin, hepcidin and transferrin receptor 1, thereby depriving pathogens and non-self-cells of the iron essential for their proliferation [179]. Besides, multiple M1 macrophage-associated markers, such as IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) have the potential to foster an inflammatory environment conducive to ferroptosis through disrupting iron homeostasis and enhancing lipid peroxidation [180–183]. M2 macrophages demonstrate a more pronounced iron release phenotype by facilitating iron exportation and suppressing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines via opposite alterations in ferroportin, CD163, HO-1, ferritin, hepcidin, and transferrin receptor 1, as previously documented. Conversely, the iron content under various conditions significantly influences macrophage polarization, with macrophages typically exhibiting an inclination toward M1 polarization in iron-replete environments, whereas iron-deficient environments tend to promote M2 polarization [179]. However, iron treatment has been confirmed to increase M2 polarization of macrophages both in vitro and in vivo, indicating a dual role of iron in macrophage polarization and the heterogeneous phenotypes of macrophages [184].

The mutual interaction between lipid metabolism and macrophage reprogramming also constitutes an important area of investigation, with significant implications under various pathological conditions. Dysregulation in fatty acid biosynthesis may induce divergent phenotypic profiles of macrophages. Research has shown that saturated fatty acids promote M1 macrophage polarization, whereas polyunsaturated fatty acids drive M2 macrophage polarization. The modulation of PPAR-γ activity has the potential to influence macrophage M1/M2 polarization induced by lipids, consequently impacting inflammation and lipid metabolism in hepatocytes. Specifically, PPAR-γ agonists were shown to induce lipid-induced M1-polarized macrophages to shift toward an M2-polarized phenotype, whereas PPAR-γ antagonists exerted an opposing effect [185]. Similarly, free fatty acid receptors 1 (FFAR1) and FFAR4 agonists reduced fatty lipid accumulation and drove macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype via activating PPAR-γ in RAW264.7 cells, thereby improving ulcerative colitis in mice [186,187]. Furthermore, microRNA-33, a key regulator of fatty acid biogenesis and oxidation, fostered the development of the M1-like macrophage phenotype, while microRNA-33 inhibition in hypercholesterolemic murine models induced a macrophage metabolic reprogramming toward the M2 phenotype by enhancing fatty acid oxidation, ultimately reducing the atherosclerosis progression [188]. The transcriptional regulators of lipid metabolism, sterol regulatory element-binding proteins and LXRs, have also been proven to play distinct roles in promoting the polarization of macrophages toward M1 and M2 phenotypes, respectively, by modulating cholesterol homeostasis and lipid biosynthesis within macrophages [189,190]. Further investigation has confirmed the promotive effect of N-acetyltransferase 10 on macrophage M2 polarization via enhancing lipid metabolism [191]. The influence of macrophage reprogramming on lipid metabolism is equally crucial. The administration of crocin inhibited lipogenesis and mitigated the inflammation in a rat model of coronary atherosclerosis by promoting the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype [192]. Similar results were obtained in a type 2 diabetes mellitus mouse model [193]. Conversely, M1 macrophages were found to markedly enhance lipid synthesis and accumulation in primary hepatocytes through the activation of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway [185].

As mentioned above, disorders of iron and lipid metabolism can influence cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis, primarily due to elevated oxidative stress driven by iron overload and an increased tendency for the peroxidation of specific lipids. Moreover, periodontal infection has been proven to induce a disruption of iron and lipid homeostasis and promote macrophage reprogramming within periodontal inflammatory microenvironment, indicating that alterations in iron metabolism and lipid metabolism, in association with macrophage reprogramming, may be strongly linked to the initiation and progression of ferroptosis in the context of periodontitis [20,23,26,27,64,65,169,194].

Generally, ROS overproduction can induce ferroptosis in macrophages, particularly M1 phenotypes. ROS were found to be generated by M1 macrophages through the NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) pathway to assist in eliminating pathogens during immunological processes [195]. These ROS in turn facilitated macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype, resulting in an elevated release of various inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and iNOS [196]. ROS-mediated inflammatory microenvironment may foster conditions favorable to ferroptosis by disrupting iron homeostasis and promoting lipid peroxidation [180–183,197]. Therefore, reduction of ROS levels has been demonstrated to be an effective strategy to inhibit ferroptosis by facilitating a shift in macrophage polarization from the M1-like to the M2-like phenotype [198–201]. During the development of periodontitis, neutrophils are recruited and accumulate at the sites of inflammation to combat invading pathogens and to inhibit the inflammatory response by multiple antibacterial mechanisms, such as substantial ROS generation [107]. ROS exert potent antibacterial effects via eliminating periodontal pathogens and activating complementary defense mechanisms against infections, whereas ROS overproduction can stimulate osteoclast differentiation and maturation to trigger periodontal tissue degradation and alveolar bone resorption [202,203]. Excessive ROS levels have also been shown to drive M1 macrophage polarization during periodontitis through activating NF-κB signaling pathway, which further enhance the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and potentially increase cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis [204]. Research found that macrophages tended to polarize toward the M1 phenotype via excessive ROS production under hyperglycemic conditions, which contributed to the exacerbation of diabetic periodontitis. Conversely, ROS scavengers alleviated this condition by suppressing M1 macrophages and promoting the transformation toward M2 macrophages [197]. However, previous findings revealed that M1 macrophages exhibited greater resistance to pharmacologically induced ferroptosis compared to M2 macrophages. iNOS depletion rendered M1 macrophages more sensitive, while nitric oxide (NO) enhanced the resistance of M2 macrophages to ferroptosis [205].

Overall, lipid metabolism, iron metabolism, and redox homeostasis collectively underscore the possible crosstalk between macrophage efferocytosis and ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of periodontitis. In this context, macrophage reprogramming may act as a unifying mechanism that integrates these three factors in the disease process, clarifying the potential interconnection between macrophage efferocytosis and ferroptosis during periodontitis (Figure 4). Subsequent to the invasion of periodontal tissues by periodontal pathogens, the balance of cytokines in the periodontal microenvironment is significantly perturbed, accompanied with disorder of lipid metabolism, iron metabolism, and redox homeostasis, as a result of the disruption of resident microbiota equilibrium. Dysregulation in lipid, iron, and redox metabolism triggers ferroptosis and potently drives M1 macrophage polarization, whereas macrophage efferocytosis effectively reverses this condition, facilitating macrophage reprogramming from M1 to M2 phenotypes in the periodontal inflammatory microenvironment. Recent studies have found that targeting macrophage reprogramming may contribute to the resolution of periodontal tissue injury through the precise regulation of macrophage activation and functional activity, which potentially offers novel insight into periodontitis treatment. Developmental endothelial locus-1 (DEL-1)-mediated macrophage efferocytosis has been demonstrated to trigger LXR-dependent macrophage reprogramming toward a pro-resolving and tissue-reparative phenotype, thereby promoting the effective resolution of periodontal inflammation [113]. Besides, comparable therapeutic outcomes were achieved by the administration of M2 macrophage-derived exosomes, chitosan-P407-PNIPAM scaffold multi-crosslinked hydrogel, apabetalone, or quercetin in the treatment of periodontitis [178,206–208].

The potential interconnection between macrophage efferocytosis and ferroptosis during periodontitis. Following the invasion of periodontal tissues by pathogenic pathogens, the equilibrium of cytokines within the periodontal microenvironment is significantly disrupted, leading to disturbances in lipid metabolism, iron metabolism, and redox homeostasis. This dysregulation in lipid, iron, and redox metabolism initiates ferroptosis and strongly promotes the polarization of M1 macrophages. Conversely, macrophage efferocytosis effectively reverses this state, aiding in the reprogramming of macrophages from M1 to M2 phenotypes during periodontitis. Lipid metabolism, iron metabolism, and redox homeostasis collectively highlight the potential crosstalk between macrophage efferocytosis and ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of periodontitis. Macrophage reprogramming may serve as a unifying mechanism that integrates these three factors within the disease process, elucidating the potential interconnection between macrophage efferocytosis and ferroptosis in the context of periodontitis.

Conclusion and outlook

To summarize, the significant roles of ferroptosis and macrophage efferocytosis in the pathogenesis of periodontitis have been demonstrated, which has garnered considerable scientific attention in the field of periodontal research. While the direct causal relationship between macrophage efferocytosis and ferroptosis is not yet fully elucidated, we propose several potential associations in periodontitis, including redox homeostasis, lipid metabolism, and iron metabolism. Macrophage reprogramming represents a potential unifying mechanism underlying the complex pathophysiological interplay between macrophage efferocytosis and ferroptosis during periodontitis. Regulation of macrophage reprogramming may present efficacious therapeutic strategies for mitigating periodontal injury by suppressing ferroptosis and facilitating macrophage efferocytosis. Future investigations are crucial to address the following biological issues. The divergence between animal models and human biology has led to the belief that the therapeutic effects of ferroptosis inhibitors or efferocytosis agonists observed in preclinical research may not be consistently replicated in human studies. The therapeutic effects of inhibiting ferroptosis and enhancing macrophage efferocytic activity on the colonization and translocation of pathogenic microorganisms during periodontitis require more in-depth future research. Furthermore, the relationships between macrophage efferocytosis and other forms of PCD in the development of periodontitis are still not fully elucidated. Targeting their shared pathways may produce synergistic therapeutic effects, thereby providing innovative combinatorial strategies to impede the progression of periodontitis.
