The effectiveness of self-management intervention on quality of life and health outcomes in patients with non-communicable diseases: a systematic review and meta-analysis of the current empirical evidence (2019–2024)
Authors: Suebsarn Ruksakulpiwat, Chontira Riangkam, Roongnapa Khampang, Chantira Chiaranai, Naruebeth Koson, Nway Eint Chei, Roshani Shrestha, Manassawee Srimoragot, Thitipong Tankumpuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-management empowers non-communicable disease (NCDs) patients to improve health, enhance quality of life (QoL), and reduce adverse outcomes. This study aims to synthesize empirical evidence on the effectiveness of self-management interventions in improving QoL and health outcomes among patients with NCDs.MethodsFollowing the PRISMA guidelines, a systematic search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, CINAHL, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and OVID databases was conducted to identify relevant studies published between 2019 and 2024. Studies were included if they evaluated the impact of self-management interventions on QoL among adults with NCDs using randomized controlled trials or experimental designs. Risk of bias was assessed using the RoB 2 and ROBINS-I tools. A random-effects meta-analysis was performed to calculate pooled standardized mean differences using Hedges’ g. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic. Sensitivity analyses and publication bias assessments were also conducted.ResultsEleven studies involving 1,591 participants were included in the systematic review, and 10 studies were eligible for the meta-analysis. The pooled analysis demonstrated that self-management interventions significantly improved QoL compared with standard care (Hedges’ g = 0.58; 95% CI: 0.34–0.81). Moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 69.3%). Subgroup analysis indicated that intervention effects varied by country income level, with larger effect sizes observed in upper-middle-income countries. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the findings. Funnel plot inspection and Egger’s test indicated no statistically significant evidence of publication bias.ConclusionSelf-management interventions are associated with significant improvements in QoL among individuals with NCDs. These findings highlight the importance of integrating structured self-management programs into chronic disease care. Given the observed heterogeneity across studies, future research should focus on standardized intervention components, rigorous study designs, and evaluation across diverse healthcare settings to better understand the long-term effectiveness of self-management strategies.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13690-026-02014-x.Keywords: Meta-analysis, Non-communicable diseases, Quality of life, Self-management, Systematic review

Background

As reported by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including stroke, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, chronic respiratory conditions, and cancers, are among the leading causes of mortality and disability worldwide [1]. Moreover, NCDs account for approximately 74% of all deaths worldwide, with a disproportionately high burden in low- and middle-income countries [1]. The increasing prevalence of NCDs is primarily attributed to aging populations, urbanization, and the widespread adoption of unhealthy lifestyle behaviors, such as poor diet, physical inactivity, tobacco use, and harmful alcohol consumption [2, 3]. As chronic conditions, NCDs impose a long-term burden on individuals, families, and healthcare systems, demanding sustainable management strategies that address both clinical outcomes and quality of life (QoL) [3–5].

Living with NCDs profoundly affects patients’ physical, emotional, and social well-being. Beyond the clinical manifestations, individuals often experience functional impairments, psychosocial challenges, and limitations in spiritual well-being, all of which contribute to a decline in QoL [6–8]. For instance, individuals with type 2 diabetes may experience fatigue and mobility challenges [9, 10], while those with cardiovascular disease often report individual and caregiver burden, emotional distress and limitations in social engagement [11, 12]. Evidence suggests that adults with NCDs are more likely to report lower life satisfaction and higher levels of depression compared to those without chronic conditions. One study involving 854 adults found that individuals with diabetes, hypertension, and obesity reported significantly poorer QoL outcomes than those without these conditions. Specifically, they were less likely to express satisfaction with their health and scored lower in the physical health domain of QoL [6]. These findings highlight the importance of prioritizing QoL enhancement as a core objective in the comprehensive management of NCDs.

To address these challenges, self-management interventions have become essential components of chronic disease care. These interventions empower patients to actively participate in managing their health by developing skills in health awareness, self-care, symptom management, medication adherence, lifestyle modification, and problem-solving [13–17]. Grounded in behavioral theories and health education models, self-management programs are typically facilitated by healthcare professionals such as nurses, dietitians, or trained community workers. For instance, a systematic review highlighted that most educational interventions were led by health professionals, including dietitians, nurses, clinical pharmacists, physicians, physiotherapists, and community health workers [18]. These programs may be implemented through various formats, including face-to-face sessions, group workshops, telehealth services, or digital platforms [18]. Overall, self-management has been recognized as an essential strategy to improve patient outcomes and promote sustainable healthcare practices.

A growing body of research suggests that self-management interventions can significantly enhance QoL among adults with NCDs. Evidence indicates that participants in structured self-management programs are associated with enhanced physical functioning, emotional well-being, and disease-related coping abilities [19, 20]. However, the magnitude and consistency of these benefits vary depending on factors such as disease severity, intervention intensity, duration, delivery format, and participant characteristics. Furthermore, many existing studies have been conducted within single-disease frameworks or confined to specific geographic regions, resulting in limited generalizability. These gaps highlight the need for further research to understand the broader impact of self-management on QoL across diverse adult and older populations with NCDs [21].

Given the global burden of NCDs and the growing emphasis on patient-centered care, a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence is warranted. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate the effect of self-management interventions on QoL among adults with NCDs, drawing on studies conducted across multiple settings and disease types. By consolidating recent findings, the review seeks to identify best practices for enhancing QoL and to inform the design and implementation of effective self-management strategies in diverse populations. To capture this contemporary evidence, the review deliberately focused on studies published between 2019 and 2024. This five-year window was chosen because self-management has shifted substantially over recent years, with the rapid uptake of digital and mobile health technologies, the disruption and subsequent reconfiguration of chronic disease care during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, and the publication of updated NCD management guidelines. Restricting the search to this period, therefore, ensured that the synthesised interventions reflected current models of care and present-day healthcare technologies, rather than approaches that may now be outdated.

Objective

To systematically review and synthesize current empirical evidence on the effectiveness of self-management interventions in improving QoL and other health-related outcomes in individuals with NCDs.

Methods

Identify relevant studies

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [22], including presenting a flow diagram outlining the process of literature identification, screening, exclusion, and inclusion. A systematic search was conducted across six electronic databases—PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, CINAHL, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and OVID—to identify relevant studies published between 2019 and 2024 that investigated the effect of self-management interventions on QoL among individuals with NCDs. The search strategy utilized a combination of keywords and Boolean operators: ((NCD OR Non-communicable chronic diseases OR Non-infectious diseases OR Disease, non-communicable) AND (Self-care OR Care, Self-OR Self-Care OR Self-management OR Self-care activities OR Self-care strategies) AND (Quality of Life OR QoL OR Health-Related Quality of Life OR HRQOL)). To ensure comprehensive coverage, the reference lists of included articles were also manually screened for additional relevant studies. All identified references were stored and managed using EndNote X7.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts of all retrieved records were initially screened for relevance. Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were then reviewed to assess their suitability for inclusion. Predefined inclusion criteria were applied to ensure that only studies directly aligned with the review objective were retained. Conversely, exclusion criteria were used to eliminate studies that did not meet the scope or focus of the review (Supplementary Table 1).

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form (Supplementary Data 1) was developed to systematically organize and document the characteristics of each included study. For each study, key information was extracted, including full reference, year of publication, country of origin, and study setting. Characteristics of the target population were also recorded, such as diagnostic criteria, sample size for both intervention and control groups, mean age, and the proportion of female participants. Additionally, the study’s aim, research design, and the type of healthcare provider involved in delivering the intervention were noted. Detailed descriptions of the self-management intervention were collected, including the delivery format (e.g., face-to-face, online), frequency of sessions, and overall duration of the intervention. The QoL measurement tools used in each study were recorded, along with the primary outcomes, overall conclusions, study limitations, and suggested implications for future research. All data were extracted independently by two reviewers using the standardized chart, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer to ensure accuracy and completeness.

Risk of bias assessment

To assess the methodological quality of the included studies, we employed two validated tools developed by Cochrane: The Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool and the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool. The RoB 2 tool [23] was used to evaluate the risk of bias in randomized controlled trials (RCTs), covering five key domains: bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Each domain was judged as having low risk, some concerns, or high risk of bias.

For non-randomized studies of interventions (NRSIs), the ROBINS-I tool was applied [24]. This tool assesses bias across seven domains, including confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported results. Each study was assigned an overall risk rating of low, moderate, serious, or critical risk of bias. All assessments were independently conducted by two reviewers. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer to ensure accuracy and consistency in the evaluation.

Data synthesis for systematic review

Data from the included studies were synthesized using the convergent integrated approach, as outlined in the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for systematic reviews [25]. This approach involved integrating findings across studies by identifying key intervention-related themes associated with quality of life (QoL) and other health outcomes. A thematic analysis technique, commonly used in qualitative evidence synthesis, was employed to categorize and interpret patterns emerging from the extracted data. The thematic analysis was conducted through a manual coding process. Two reviewers independently reviewed the extracted data, generated initial codes based on intervention characteristics and outcomes, and subsequently grouped related codes into broader themes. Any discrepancies in coding were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. This process enabled the identification of common intervention components, delivery approaches, and outcome-related themes across studies, thereby enhancing the interpretability and comparability of heterogeneous self-management interventions.

Statistical analysis for meta-analysis

A two-step approach was employed for data analysis. First, a narrative synthesis was conducted to summarize the study characteristics, including interventions, populations, and outcomes. Baseline and post-intervention means, standard deviations, and sample sizes were extracted for both intervention and control groups. Change scores were calculated as the difference between post-intervention and baseline values. When the standard deviation of the change score was not reported, it was estimated from baseline and post-intervention standard deviations using formulas recommended in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [26], assuming a correlation coefficient of 0.5. Studies with multiple intervention arms were combined by calculating pooled sample sizes, means, and standard deviations to avoid double-counting participants. The direction of quality-of-life scales was harmonized prior to analysis; scores from the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ) and the Diabetes-Specific Quality of Life Scale (DSQLS) were reversed by multiplying by − 1 so that higher scores consistently indicated better quality of life. Change scores and their corresponding standard deviations were pooled in the meta-analysis to account for potential baseline differences between groups. Effect sizes were calculated as standardized mean differences using Hedges’ g, which applies a small-sample bias correction and is recommended for meta-analyses including trials with relatively small sample sizes. Effect size calculation rules were provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, the I² statistic, meta-regression, and subgroup analyses. An I² value of 75% or higher was interpreted as substantial heterogeneity. To evaluate the robustness of the results, a sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method was conducted. A random-effects model was applied to account for between-study variation. Publication bias was examined through visual inspection of funnel plots, Egger’s test, and contour-enhanced funnel plots. All statistical analyses were conducted using the meta command in Stata 18 (StataCorp, 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).

Results

Search results

Following the PRISMA guidelines [27], a total of 2,907 records were initially identified through electronic databases, including PubMed and Medline (n = 226), Scopus (n = 580), CINAHL (n = 140), Web of Science (n = 75), ScienceDirect (n = 328), and OVID (n = 1,558). After removing 78 duplicate records, 2,829 records remained for title and abstract screening. During the screening phase, 2,818 records were excluded based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eleven full-text reports were sought for retrieval and subsequently assessed for eligibility. No reports were excluded during the eligibility assessment. As a result, 11 studies were included in both the systematic review and the meta-analysis [28–38] (Fig. 1).

Description of included studies

The characteristics of the included studies are presented in Supplementary Table 3. Most of these studies were published in 2021 (N = 3, 27.3%). Two studies were published in each of the years 2019, 2021, and 2022 (N = 2, 18.2% per year), while one study was published in both 2020 and 2024 (N = 1, 9.1% for each year). In terms of geographic distribution, Iran accounted for the largest number of studies (N = 3, 27.3%). The remaining studies were each conducted in different countries, including China, Egypt, Malawi, Spain, Thailand, Turkey, the United Kingdom, and the United States of America (N = 1, 9.1% per country).

Regarding study design, six were RCTs (N = 6, 56.6%) and five were quasi-experimental studies (N = 5, 45.5%). The sample sizes varied across studies: six studies enrolled between 1 and 100 participants (N = 6, 54.6%), four included between 101 and 300 (N = 4, 36.4%), and one study had more than 300 participants (N = 1, 9.1%).

Healthcare providers involved in the included studies were varied, with one study reporting the involvement of more than one provider type. Nurses were the most commonly involved healthcare providers (N = 3, 27.3%). Other providers included: a certified instructor and healthcare worker (N = 1, 9.1%), clinical psychologist (N = 1, 9.1%), community health volunteer (CHVs) (N = 1, 9.1%), general practitioner trained in CBT (N = 1, 9.1%), physician (N = 1, 9.1%), social worker (N = 1, 9.1%), specialist kidney physical therapist (N = 1, 9.1%), trained primary care nurse (N = 1, 9.1%), and a trained health professional with expertise in diabetes education (N = 1, 9.1%). Two studies did not specify the type of healthcare provider involved (N = 2, 18.2%).

The quality appraisal of the included studies

A total of six RCTs were assessed using the RoB 2 tool, while NRSIs were evaluated using the ROBINS-I tool (Fig. 2). Most RCTs demonstrated a low risk of bias across the assessed domains. However, several studies presented some concerns in the domains related to deviations from intended interventions and the selection of reported results. Notably, Streltsov et al. (2022) and Zuo et al. (2020) showed minor concerns in one or more domains; however, none were rated as having a high overall risk of bias. Among the NRSIs, four out of five were rated as having a moderate overall risk of bias. One study, by Angwenyi et al. (2021), was assessed as having a serious risk of bias, primarily due to confounding and classification of interventions. Most of the remaining domains across these studies were judged as having low or moderate risk (Fig. 2a and c). Bar charts (Fig. 2b and d) present a visual summary of domain-specific risk-of-bias judgments for all RCTs and NRSIs. Overall, these findings suggest stronger methodological rigor among the RCTs, whereas the NRSIs exhibited greater variability, particularly regarding control for confounding factors and clarity in intervention classification. The visualizations of domain-level and overall risk-of-bias assessments were generated using the robvis tool [39], an open-source R-based web application developed to support transparent evidence synthesis. These risk-of-bias assessments were considered when interpreting the meta-analysis findings. Specifically, greater caution was applied when interpreting pooled estimates derived from studies with moderate or serious risk of bias, particularly the non-randomized studies, as these designs may be more susceptible to confounding and methodological limitations. Using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework [40], the overall certainty of evidence for the primary outcome (quality of life) was judged to be low. This rating reflects substantial heterogeneity across studies, inclusion of non-randomized study designs with moderate risk of bias, and potential small-study effects identified in the publication bias assessment.

The effect of self-management intervention on quality of life and health outcomes

We synthesized the findings from 11 included studies and found that self-management interventions consistently enhance QoL and health outcomes across various chronic conditions, including diabetes, heart failure, epilepsy, cancer, and mental health disorders. Interventions that incorporate education, physical activity, digital tools (e.g., weblogs), and psychological support (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy [CBT]) yield significant benefits. For instance, diabetes self-management education (DSME) was associated with reductions in blood glucose levels and stress, along with improved QoL [34], while nurse-led health coaching was found to temporarily increase patient activation [33].

Digital platforms such as weblog-based education [36] and the Kidney BEAM program [31] were found to improve metabolic indicators (e.g., glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c]) and mental health-related QoL, respectively. In-person interventions like Home-Based Self-Management and Cognitive Training Changes Lives (HOBSCOTCH) program for individuals with epilepsy, demonstrated sustained improvements in QoL [37]. Theory-driven models, such as Erickson’s Modeling and Role-Modeling Theory, applied to cancer care [35], supported long-term enhancement in QoL. Additionally, interventions combining cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) with exercise showed positive effects on sleep quality, glycemic control, and diabetes-specific QoL [38]. While outcomes varied depending on the mode of delivery (e.g., virtual versus in-person), the overall evidence highlights self-management as a key strategy for symptom reduction, strengthening self-efficacy, and advancing patient-centered care across chronic disease populations. However, the magnitude and consistency of intervention effects varied across studies, likely reflecting differences in study design, target populations, intervention components, delivery formats, and duration of follow-up. All information about each included study can be found in Supplementary Data 1.

Analytical findings of meta-analysis

Effect of self-care management on quality of life

A total of 10 studies were included in the meta-analysis. Of these, seven were RCTs and three were quasi-experimental studies. The analysis included 1,097 patients, 564 in the intervention group and 533 in the standard care group. Self-care management interventions were associated with a moderate improvement in quality of life compared with usual care. The pooled standardized mean difference using Hedges’ g was 0.58 (95% CI 0.34 to 0.81; 95% prediction interval − 0.178 to 1.332) under a random-effects model, indicating a statistically significant positive effect in favor of the intervention. However, the prediction interval suggests that the true effect may vary substantially across different populations or settings, ranging from a small negative effect to a large positive effect. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 69.3%, τ² = 0.09), indicating variability in effect sizes between trials. Although most studies showed effects favoring self-care management, several confidence intervals crossed the null value. Overall, these findings suggest that self-care management programs may improve quality of life, but the magnitude of benefit may vary across populations and intervention contexts (Fig. 3).

In subgroup analysis, self-care management interventions improved quality of life across different country income levels, although the magnitude of effect varied by context. In high-income countries, the pooled effect size was Hedges’ g = 0.26 (95% CI 0.09 to 0.44) with no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%), indicating a small but statistically significant improvement in quality of life. In upper-middle-income countries, a larger effect was observed (Hedges’ g = 0.78, 95% CI 0.54 to 1.02), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 43.4%). Only one study from low- and lower-middle-income countries was available, showing a positive but non-significant effect (Hedges’ g = 0.61, 95% CI − 0.08 to 1.31). The test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (Qb = 11.83, p < 0.001), suggesting that the effect of self-care management on quality of life may vary by country income level (Fig. 4).

Our analysis revealed moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity, as indicated by a high I² statistic. To explore potential sources of this variability, we conducted subgroup analyses and meta-regression using relevant moderators. Country income level emerged as a statistically significant contributor to the observed heterogeneity, whereas other variables were not significantly associated with variation in effect sizes. However, with only 10 included studies, the subgroup analyses were likely underpowered to detect differences between groups. Consequently, the absence of significant associations for other moderators may reflect limited statistical power rather than a true lack of effect (Supplementary Data 2).

Sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out analysis indicated that no single study significantly altered the overall effect size. The pooled effect size ranged from Hedges’ g = 0.51 to 0.64 across all iterations, suggesting the results are robust and not driven by any one study (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. 2) showed a generally symmetrical distribution of studies around the pooled effect estimate, suggesting no clear evidence of publication bias or small-study effects. The contour-enhanced funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. 3) showed a generally symmetrical distribution of studies around the pooled effect estimate, with most studies located within the non-significant regions of the plot. There was no clear pattern of missing studies in statistically significant areas, suggesting that publication bias is unlikely to explain the observed asymmetry. Egger’s regression test showed no evidence of small-study effects (β = 2.11, SE = 1.77, z = 1.19, p = 0.23), suggesting no statistically significant indication of publication bias. However, this result must be viewed cautiously because only a few studies were included.

Discussion

Findings of data synthesis

Self-management interventions play a crucial role in enhancing the QoL among individuals living with NCDs. Evidence from the present study indicates that these interventions are particularly effective in improving health outcomes when delivered by nurses. This observation aligns with findings from prior research. For instance, Pulgar et al. (2022) reported that nurse-led interventions in self-care and disease management were associated with reductions in premature mortality and significant improvements in QoL among patients with NCDs [41]. Similarly, Lee et al. (2022) demonstrated that nurse-delivered telehealth interventions focusing on self-management education and health behavior modification resulted in positive outcomes in patients’ QoL [42]. The success of nurse-led interventions may be attributed to nurses’ emphasis on holistic, person-centered care, as well as their specialized competencies in behavioral counseling, patient education, and facilitation of technology-assisted self-management strategies [42–44]. These competencies are grounded in established theoretical frameworks, including Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory, which emphasizes self-efficacy as a key driver of behavior change [45], and the Chronic Care Model [46], which highlights the importance of proactive, patient-centered interactions between informed patients and prepared healthcare teams in achieving optimal self-management outcomes. Building on this foundation, nurses are increasingly leveraging digital health technologies as an extension of their self-management support role, further amplifying the reach and impact of these interventions.

Recent condition-specific evidence further supports this role; for example, a systematic review by Dollaku et al. (2025) reported that structured educational interventions significantly improved self-care behaviors among patients after cardiac valve replacement, highlighting the critical contribution of nurses as facilitators of patient education and self-management support [47]. Nurses are uniquely positioned to establish trusting and supportive relationships with patients, enabling them to tailor interventions to individual needs and sustain long-term engagement in self-management practices [48]. These findings underscore the importance of integrating nurses as central facilitators of self-management programs across clinical, community, and telehealth settings. Integrating nurse-led approaches can significantly enhance the QoL for individuals with NCDs and contribute to improved health outcomes at a population level.

Digital health innovations, from web-based learning modules [49] to targeted programs such as the Kidney BEAM initiative [50], have shown notable improvements in metabolic indicators like HbA1c and mental health–related quality of life among individuals with chronic conditions. These platforms support self-management by combining patient education, physical activity, and psychosocial support, thereby promoting healthier behaviors and better clinical outcomes. Evidence from recent systematic reviews and clinical trials indicates that mobile health (mHealth) tools, including smartphone applications, SMS reminders, telemonitoring systems, and interactive digital platforms, effectively improve patient engagement, medication adherence, and control of clinical markers in NCDs such as diabetes and hypertension [51–54]. However, the widespread adoption of digital health innovations is not without challenges. Key limitations include inequitable access to technology among older adults, individuals with low digital literacy, and those in low-resource settings, which may exacerbate existing health disparities [55]. Furthermore, concerns regarding data privacy, cybersecurity, and the sustainability of digital platforms pose additional barriers to long-term implementation [56]. The heterogeneity in intervention design, outcome measures, and follow-up periods across studies also limits the comparability of findings and the generalizability of conclusions. These challenges underscore the need for inclusive design, robust evaluation frameworks, and policy-level support to ensure that digital health tools can be equitably and effectively integrated into chronic disease self-management programs.

Nurse-led digital interventions have also demonstrated efficacy in managing blood pressure and enhancing patient empowerment, underscoring the vital role of nurses in delivering telehealth services [57–59]. In diabetes care, telehealth systems offer tailored educational content and ongoing support, contributing to improved glycemic control and emotional well-being [60, 61]. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of technology-based self-management interventions to improve health outcomes and quality of life in individuals with NCDs. Future research should explore long-term effectiveness, cost-efficiency, AI-driven personalization, and strategies to enhance equitable access and digital literacy.

Findings of meta-analysis

The meta-analysis synthesized data from 10 studies and yielded a pooled effect size supporting the efficacy of self-management interventions in enhancing QoL among individuals with NCDs. The results revealed a moderate and statistically significant effect (Hedges’ g = 0.58, 95% CI 0.34 to 0.81; 95% prediction interval − 0.178 to 1.332), highlighting a meaningful advantage of self-care interventions over standard care. These findings are consistent with previous research underscoring the value of structured self-management programs in improving patient-reported outcomes among individuals with chronic conditions [62–64]. However, the practical interpretation of this pooled effect size should be considered cautiously, as the included studies encompassed diverse NCD populations and heterogeneous intervention approaches. Therefore, the pooled estimate should be viewed as a general indication of benefit, while the effectiveness of self-management interventions may vary depending on specific conditions and intervention components.

Despite the encouraging effect size, the analysis revealed moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity (I² = 69.3%), suggesting considerable variability across study designs, populations, interventions, and outcome measures [65, 66]. Despite additional subgroup analyses, heterogeneity remained substantial across most models. We found that differences in country income level may partly explain the observed heterogeneity, as healthcare resources, access to services, patient education, and social determinants of health vary substantially across settings and may influence the effectiveness of self-care interventions and subsequent quality-of-life outcomes. Another potential source of heterogeneity is that interventions aimed at modifying health behaviors and improving quality of life are not always identical or tightly controlled, and individual responses to these behavioral interventions can also vary significantly [67]. This level of heterogeneity highlights several important directions for future research. First, future studies should adopt standardized intervention protocols and outcome measurement tools to enhance comparability across trials [65]. Second, researchers should investigate the extent to which disease-specific factors, such as illness severity, comorbidity burden, and disease duration, moderate the effectiveness of self-management interventions on QoL. Third, we hypothesize that intervention intensity and delivery format, whether face-to-face, digital, or hybrid, may significantly influence effect sizes, and future trials should be designed to directly compare these modalities. Finally, large-scale, multicenter RCTs with longer follow-up periods are needed to better understand the sustainability of self-management intervention effects across diverse populations and healthcare settings.

Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method confirmed that no single study disproportionately influenced the overall effect size, supporting the robustness of the findings. Overall, this meta-analysis affirms the beneficial role of self-management interventions in improving QoL among individuals with NCDs, while also highlighting the need for more consistent methodological approaches and transparent reporting to reduce heterogeneity and bias in future research.

Study limitations

Despite the rigorous methodology employed in this review, its limitations should be acknowledged and interpreted in context. First, the inclusion criteria were restricted to studies published in English between 2019 and 2024. While this approach ensured the use of up-to-date evidence and facilitated consistent interpretation, it may have excluded relevant studies published in other languages or outside the designated time frame. However, the selected period was intended to ensure the relevance of included interventions to contemporary clinical practices and healthcare technologies.

Additionally, this systematic review was not prospectively registered in an international registry such as PROSPERO. Although registration is recommended to enhance methodological transparency and reduce the risk of reporting bias, it is not mandatory for conducting systematic reviews. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, the study was conducted and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and all supporting data are provided in the Supplementary Data files.

Second, moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity was observed among the included studies. This variability likely reflects differences in study populations, intervention formats, delivery settings, and QoL measurement tools. Although subgroup and meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, no significant moderators were identified. Nevertheless, a random-effects model was used to account for between-study variation, and sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the findings.

Third, the methodological quality of included studies varied. While RCTs generally showed a low risk of bias, NRSIs exhibited moderate to serious risk of bias, particularly in domains such as confounding and intervention classification. This reflects the inherent design limitations of quasi-experimental studies, which, while valuable for real-world settings, often lack random allocation and control over confounders. Finally, publication bias was indicated through asymmetry in the funnel plot and a statistically significant Egger’s test. This suggests that smaller studies with positive findings may have been preferentially published. However, the interpretation of Egger’s test should be approached with caution, as publication bias tests may be unstable when applied to meta-analyses with a relatively small number of studies and may also reflect underlying heterogeneity rather than true publication bias. However, the trim-and-fill analysis did not identify any missing studies, and the pooled effect size remained stable, which provides reassurance regarding the overall reliability of the results. Nevertheless, the absence of imputed studies in trim-and-fill analysis does not completely rule out the possibility of selective reporting or other forms of bias.

In sum, although several methodological and analytical limitations were present, the systematic approach used in study selection, risk of bias assessment, and synthesis, combined with the application of rigorous statistical techniques, supports the validity of the overall findings. These limitations highlight areas for improvement in future research and underscore the need for more methodologically robust, diverse, and longitudinal studies to further examine the long-term impact of self-management interventions on quality of life in individuals with NCDs.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide compelling evidence that self-management interventions significantly improve QoL among individuals with NCDs. Despite moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity and indications of publication bias, sensitivity analyses confirmed the overall stability and reliability of the findings. However, the results should be interpreted with caution due to variability in study designs, intervention components, and outcome measurements across the included studies. These results support the value of incorporating self-management strategies into chronic care models, with nurses serving as key facilitators in delivering holistic, patient-centered interventions. To further strengthen the evidence base, future research should prioritize methodologically rigorous study designs, standardization of outcome measures, and evaluation of scalable and diverse delivery formats. Ultimately, self-management interventions represent a promising pathway for improving chronic disease outcomes and promoting the sustainability of healthcare systems.
