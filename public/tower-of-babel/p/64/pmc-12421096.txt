Comparison of structure-function correlation among IMO visual function analyser and Humphrey field analyser
Authors: Joyce Kang, Rafaella Nascimento e Silva, Julia Anne Kim, Dolly Chang, Yihao Li, Chen Chen, Yan Zhao, Sofia De Arrigunaga, Sandra E Freeman, Tobias Elze, Michael M Lin, David S Friedman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo compare the structure-function correlations between optical coherence tomography (OCT) thickness parameters and the corresponding sectorial mean sensitivity (MS) values obtained with IMO visual function analyser (IMOvifa) and Humphrey field analyser (HFA) perimeters.DesignProspective cross-sectional study.MethodsGlaucoma suspect, glaucoma and ocular hypertension patients underwent IMOvifa 24-2 Ambient Interactive Zippy Estimated Sequential Testing, HFA 24-2 Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm-Standard and OCT tests within 12 weeks. Based on the Garway-Heath map, the sensitivity points were divided in corresponding OCT peripapillary retinal nerve fibre layer (pRNFL) sectors, and also the central 10° points in corresponding ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer thickness over the macula. The structure-function correlations were analysed by Spearman’s rank coefficient and compared by Steiger’s test.Results57 eyes of 57 patients (mean age 59.2 years, 61.4% female) with reliable field and adequate OCT strength were included in this analysis. IMOvifa had significantly shorter testing time (3.7 vs 5.3 mins), lower mean deviation (difference −0.6 dB) and greater visual field index (difference +1.0%) compared with HFA (p<0.001 for all). There was moderate to strong correlation between IMOvifa and HFA MS sectors (p<0.001 for all). IMOvifa had significant correlations in the inferior (I, r=0.46), superior (S, r=0.45), temporal (T, r=0.28) pRNFL sectors and in the I (r=0.63) and S (r=0.53) mGCPIPL hemispheres (p≤0.03 for all). There was no significant difference in the strength of the correlation of IMOvifa versus HFA results with OCT parameters (p≥0.06).ConclusionsThe IMOvifa test took less time and resulted in slightly less severe defects than HFA. MS in all sectors was moderately to strongly correlated. Function-structure correlations were similar when using either perimeter.Keywords: Optic Nerve, Glaucoma, Diagnostic tests/Investigation, Prospective Studies, Field of vision

The combined use of functional and structural assessments, including optical coherence tomography, is crucial for comprehensive glaucoma evaluation.

Introduction

Visual field (VF) examination using standard automated perimetry (SAP) is presently the gold standard for glaucoma assessment.1 However, SAP is patient-dependent, with intertest variability that presents challenges for tracking VF progression in glaucomatous eyes.2 3 Prior studies have pointed to the variability of reliability indices and point-wise sensitivity values even in the most commonly used perimeters, including the Humphrey field analyser (HFA; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, California, USA).2,7

Frequent testing may help overcome inherent testing variability and detect progression earlier.8 9 Obtaining a sufficient number of reliable VF examinations after initial diagnosis can establish a good baseline and help detect those who are rapidly progressing.10 Some have recommended a minimum of six VF examinations in the first 2 years postdiagnosis.10 However, performing this many tests in the office is challenging given resource constraints.

Portable VF testing offers a potentially more efficient alternative that would allow for more frequent testing and improved monitoring of glaucoma.11 12 There is a gap in the literature regarding these automated perimeters with few comparisons to standard SAP like the HFA and Octopus (Haag-Streit, Bern, Switzerland), another commonly used VF testing device.13 IMO smart visual function analyser (IMOvifa; CREWT Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan) is a portable perimeter which performs VF testing under non-occlusion conditions and without requirement of a dark room.14,17

In addition to VF assessment, structural optic nerve damage identification is critical for glaucoma diagnosis and monitoring.18 Given the complementary role of structural and functional assessments in detecting disease-related changes, it is generally agreed that both structural and functional tests should be used for the best possible evaluation of the disease damage.19 Optical coherence tomography (OCT) plays a vital role in this approach by providing quantitative and objective measurements of the optic nerve head. It effectively captures structural changes, including retinal nerve fibre layer (RNFL) thinning and macular ganglion cell layer thinning, thereby complementing the functional insights gained from VF testing.18,20

Structure-function (S-F) models have been used to assess the relative effectiveness of structural and functional testing in identifying glaucomatous damage. The purpose of this study is to assess this relationship by comparing the IMOvifa to clinic-based tests (HFA, OCT). This study assessed the S-F correlation between OCT thickness parameters and the corresponding sectorial mean sensitivity (MS) values obtained with IMOvifa and HFA perimeters in glaucoma patients and suspects.

Methods

This observational, cross-sectional study was conducted at Massachusetts Eye and Ear (MEE; Boston, Massachusetts, USA) to evaluate the S-F correlations between VF sensitivities, as measured by IMOvifa 24-2 Ambient Interactive Zippy Estimated Sequential Testing (AIZE) and HFA 24-2 Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm (SITA) Standard programme, and OCT parameters, as assessed by Cirrus HD-OCT (Carl Zeiss Medite, Dublin, California, USA).

Study participants

Inclusion criteria included patients in the MEE glaucoma clinic between 18 and 80 years of age who had a diagnosis of known glaucoma, suspected glaucoma or ocular hypertension (OHTN); a best-corrected visual acuity of 20/40 or better; and an HFA mean deviation (MD) of −15 decibels (dB) or better in at least one eye. In addition, participants had to be able to perform reliable Humphrey visual field (HVF) testing (defined as false-positive (FP) and false-negative (FN) rates of ≤20% on their most recent VF), understand English (written and voice-over instructions for VF tests were all supplied in English) and score ≥7 on the Six-Item Cognitive Impairment Test.21 Medical charts were reviewed by two fellowship-trained glaucoma specialists (MML and DSF), and diagnoses were based on comprehensive eye exams, VF testing and OCT imaging. If a participant had two eyes that met the inclusion criteria, they were given the option to enrol both eyes in the study. Participants who had systemic or ocular disease other than glaucoma that could interfere with VF results or had undergone intraocular surgery within 90 days of the study were excluded.

Procedure

Patients came to the clinic for a designated study visit and underwent VF examinations with both the IMOvifa and HFA, along with OCT imaging. The only exception was that if the patient had an HFA and/or OCT test obtained as part of routine clinical care within the previous 12 weeks, they did not have to repeat the test, and this recent test was used in the analyses. The order of the tests, that is, HVF first or IMOvifa, was randomised for patients who did not have a reliable HVF (FP and FN rates of ≤20%) obtained in the past 12 weeks.

Device

The specifications and features of the IMOvifa were extracted from the device’s manual unless otherwise stated.22 The system consists of a headset (200×180×120 mm, 850 g) mounted to a tabletop stand, a patient response button, a test controller laptop and a cloud-based server (figure 1). The IMOvifa can measure a pupillary distance of 52–78 mm and has magnetised spherical and cylindrical corrective lenses capable of correcting –12 dioptres (D) to +6D of sphere and up to 3D of cylinder to adjust for a test-taker’s refractive error. The device uses completely isolated optical systems to conduct independent stimulus presentation and pupil monitoring for each eye. The optical system employs a wide-angle lens that can measure the VF up to 30° from the fovea and allows for the test to be administered under non-occlusion conditions. The IMOvifa test was performed binocularly (both eyes open) throughout the entire examination, whereas one eye was occluded for the HFA VF exam. IMOvifa software uses the AIZE algorithm, a derivative of the adaptive Bayesian Zippy Estimated Sequential Testing algorithm.23 The 24-2 pattern of this AIZE algorithm was used in the study, which presents stimuli at 54 points spanning 24° from fixation at 6° intervals. Stimulus size is expressed in sizes I–V independently of the placement of the 24-2 magnitude points, and the VF test generally uses size III, which has a visual angle of 0.431° in diameter. This threshold procedure records retinal sensitivities in the range of 0–50 dB. The HFA has a background brightness of 31.5 asb and a maximum brightness of 10 000 asb. IMOvifa uses a special LCD backlight to mimic these specifications, having a background luminance accuracy of 31.4 asb±20%, as detailed in the device manual. Participants took the IMOvifa VF test in a moderately dim environment, as the IMOvifa allows for VF testing without a dark room, in contrast to SAP.

Data analysis

HFA SITA 24-2 sensitivity values were recorded for 52 points. While the 24-2 Standard test algorithm has 54 test locations, we excluded the two locations closest to the blind spot as they do not have normative values, leaving a total of 52 points.24 Based on the Garway-Heath map, these 52 sensitivity points were divided into regions by the corresponding OCT peripapillary RNFL (pRNFL) sectors, and also by the central 10° points corresponding to ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer thickness over the macula (mGCIPL) (figure 2).25 26 The peripapillary OCT sectors consisted of four sectors: inferior, nasal, superior and temporal. The macular sectors included the superior and inferior sectors. For this study, the sensitivity points were averaged after converting to linear scale and converted again to dB.27 The S-F correlations were analysed by Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient and compared using Steiger’s test on a web tool.28 JMP V.16.1.0 was used for the analyses. The analysis only included eyes that performed HFA and IMOvifa exams with reliability indices of fixation loss (FL) ≤33%, FN ≤20%, FP ≤20% and OCT strength signal ≥7. Online supplemental table 1 shows the exclusion due to low-reliability indexes for both devices. If both eyes met the inclusion criteria, one eye was randomly selected per patient to adjust for intereye correlation. We generated the random selection using a random sequence generating algorithm.29

Results

Out of 120 eyes imaged, 23 (19%) were excluded due to low-reliability parameters in at least one of the tests. After the selection of one eye per patient, a total of 57 eyes of 57 patients with reliable VF results and adequate OCT signal strength were included in this analysis; 36 (63.2%) had glaucoma, with only 6 (10.5%) having moderate to severe glaucoma, defined as the MD lower than −6 dB. The mean age was 59.2 years (range, 25–79 years), 35 (61.4%) patients were female, and average HFA MD was −2.3±3.1 dB. 31 patients had the order of their HVF and IMOvifa tests effectively randomised, while the remaining 26 patients had a reliable HVF obtained in the past 12 weeks and thus did not require randomisation.

IMOvifa had significantly shorter test time duration (3.7 vs 5.3 min), less severe MD (difference +0.6 dB) and better VF index (VFI, difference +1.0%) values as compared with HFA (p<0.001 for all, table 1). Both the IMOvifa and HFA had moderate to strong correlation when compared with OCT using sectorial analysis (p<0.001 for all, figure 3). IMOvifa had significant correlations in the inferior (I, r=0.50), superior (S, r=0.45) and temporal (T, r=0.27) pRNFL sectors and in the I (r=0.63) and S (r=0.53) mGCPIPL hemispheres (p≤0.046 for all, table 2). HFA had significant correlations in the inferior (I, r=0.52) and superior (S, r=0.39) pRNFL sectors and in the I (r=0.52) and S (r=0.38) mGCPIPL hemispheres (p≤0.004 for all, table 2, online supplemental figure 1). There was no significant difference in the correlation strength of IMOvifa versus HFA with OCT parameters (p≥0.05, table 2, online supplemental figure 2).

Moreover, the effects of OCT thickness on VF sensitivity were quantified by regression models in the linear-linear scale. In general, the magnitude of effects is comparable between IMOvifa and HFA. For example, a 1-unit increase in superior pRNFL is associated with a 7.5-unit increase in HFA and a 6.8-unit increase in IMOvifa in the linear scale (online supplemental figures 1 and 2). The regression models are then transformed back into the original decibel scale, shown in online supplemental figures 3 and 4. Bland-Altman analysis was performed to assess agreement in cluster-average sensitivity between HFA and IMOvifa across the six macular sectors (online supplemental figure 5). The mean differences were small, with the majority of data points falling within the 95% limits of agreement across all sectors. To further evaluate measurement agreement at a more localised level, pointwise Bland-Altman analysis was conducted, providing a higher-resolution comparison of sensitivity differences across individual test locations (online supplemental figure 6). The analysis showed better agreement at higher (>20 dB) and lower (<10 dB) sensitivity values, while variability increased in the mid-range (10–15 dB).

Discussion

Test results using the IMOvifa perimeter correlate well with those from the widely used HFA and demonstrated similar S-F relationships after the exclusion of cases with low-reliability parameters (online supplemental table 1). However, it is important to note that the similarity of the correlations between these two devices does not necessarily indicate that this portable VF device performs similarly to standard testing. Correlations alone cannot confirm measurement agreement between the two devices, and further analysis is needed to evaluate the agreement in specific measurement outcomes. Despite these limitations, IMOvifa’s shorter test time and potential portability could allow for more frequent testing, either at home or in testing locations, which might allow for more rapid detection of VF worsening.

Our previous study examined the same patient cohort, with the only distinction being the present study included one eye per patient. The findings of our study with 133 eyes of 79 participants reported a lack of significant difference in the MD between IMOvifa and HFA.17 However, we found notable differences in specific point sensitivities between the IMOvifa and HFA, suggesting that a difference in MD calculation method between the devices may explain the non-significant difference in MD. Nishida et al’s study similarly reported comparable MD values between IMOvifa and HFA, but in addition, reported a strong correlation for the VFI while pointing to different area under the receiver operating characteristic curve diagnostic accuracies for it.15 The algorithm to calculate VFI in both devices may not be the same. The authors hypothesised that this difference might be caused by the higher threshold sensitivities in the central points in IMOvifa, the region that is weighted more in the VFI calculus.15 Regardless of the absolute values measured, both the IMOvifa and HFA can be used to monitor progression by calculating changes compared with their own baselines. Additional studies for IMOvifa are needed to determine short-term and long-term reproducibility in order to have better estimates of variability in testing so that thresholds for ‘change’ can be established.

Our sectoral analyses found moderate to strong correlation between RNFL thickness and IMOvifa testing results. The four points corresponding to the nasal OCT quadrant, which are adjacent to the blind spot, had lower correlation. These points have been shown to be more variable in our previous point-by-point analysis, as shown by significant differences when comparing the results from the inferotemporal portion of the HFA to those of the IMOvifa.15 Nishida et al similarly reported an absolute error of 1 dB in these same points between the HFA and IMOvifa and attributed the error to a rim artefact caused by IMOvifa. The authors point to the use of a prototype device of IMOvifa that did not have a chin rest as a contributing reason to the lack of stable fixation; it is important to note that our study also used a version of the device without a chin rest.15

Bland-Altman analysis provided insights into measurement agreement between IMOvifa and HFA. While cluster-average analysis (online supplemental figure 5) showed small mean differences across macular sectors, a pointwise analysis (online supplemental figure 6) revealed greater variability, particularly at mid-range (10–15 dB) where glaucomatous damage is more likely. Agreement was more consistent at higher (>20 dB) and lower (<10 dB) sensitivity values. This pattern suggests measurement differences may be more pronounced in areas of partial structural loss, potentially due to thresholding differences or variability in damaged regions. These findings underscore the value of assessing both regional and localised agreement for a more comprehensive comparison of perimetric devices.

The inclusion of OCT as an independent reference in our study serves as a strength, as it allows for an objective measure of structural changes that complements VF testing. By providing quantitative measurements such as RNFL and macular ganglion cell layer thinning, OCT enabled a robust assessment of the S-F correlations for both IMOvifa and HFA perimeters. While OCT is not inherently superior to VF testing, its integration into this study highlights the value of combining structural and functional assessments to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of glaucomatous damage. This approach underscores the complementary strengths of these modalities in the management and diagnosis of glaucoma.

AIZE, the test programme used by the IMOvifa device, speeds up test time while providing comparable test results to traditional SAP. Implications of this include improved clinical efficiency, the ability to conduct more frequent tests and reduced burden on patients and clinic staff. The pupil tracking function in IMOvifa that automatically corrects for stray fixation may allow for better fixation stability than HFA. This capability also reduces the burden on ophthalmic technicians who typically guide patients through the examination. In addition, findings from a recent study highlight that binocular fixation may be more stable than that found in the monocular condition.30 This suggests that the binocular random testing employed by the IMOvifa may result in less fixation variability as compared with the monocular fixation test used by HFA. The shorter testing time, potentially more comfortable positioning, and binocular testing may contribute to patient preference for the IMOvifa over HFA. Our previous study of the same cohort of patients reported that over 70% of participants preferred IMOvifa over HFA, with 72% reporting a ‘very good’ overall user experience.31 While the IMOvifa offers relative portability, allowing for more flexible testing environments, it is not intended for conventional home-monitoring where patients independently purchase and use the device over a prolonged period of time. The practical limitations, including cost and size, make such usage untenable. Instead, the ability to offer portable VF testing could enhance patient care by enabling more regular assessments outside traditional clinical environments, such as in care homes or other supervised environments. The positive feedback from patients further supports its value in glaucoma management, suggesting that the benefits of more frequent and comfortable monitoring may outweigh the challenges posed by its size and cost.31

It is important to note that there remains uncertainty about which statistical model best captures the link between structural and functional loss in glaucoma.32 The present study used the Hood method, which uses a linear conversion that relates RNFL thickness to losses in SAP sensitivity.32 33 The strength (R2) of the S-F correlations from the fit of the Hood model varies in literature, with some studies reporting weak-to-moderate S-F correlations (R2 values ranging from 0.037 to 0.314 using the Cirrus, p<0.001), while others show stronger correlations (R2 values of 0.623 using the Stratus and 0.588 using the scanning laser polarimeter, p<0.001).32 There are several explanations for this variation, including the choice of perimeters and RNFL measuring devices, inclusion of different patients at different disease stages, exam variability and age-related structural loss.32 Furthermore, refractive error-associated biases can affect the images and their quality, as indicated by the signal strength.34 The lens holder in HFA can also cause artefacts, leading to incorrectly reported peripheral scotomas.

Our study is subject to several limitations. First, we included patients with mild pre-perimetric glaucoma or OHTN, who had normal HFA exams, as well as glaucoma suspects, who also presented without any VF defects. The S-F correlation may be weaker in this study as VF defects typically occur after the onset of RGC loss, and stronger correlations may occur later in the disease process.27 Additionally, the relatively small sample size may have limited our ability to detect statistically significant differences, raising the possibility of a type II error. This should be considered when interpreting the absence of significant differences in correlation coefficients between the two VF devices. Furthermore, the study was composed of relatively younger age glaucoma patients, and the exact performance for each age range requires further investigation.

Additionally, our inclusion criteria required an MD ≥ −15 dB in at least one eye. This criterion was chosen to ensure that the investigational device could accurately detect moderate VF loss, which is critical for the early diagnosis and monitoring of glaucoma. This approach was intended to reduce variability and improve the reliability of the data collected. However, this may have resulted in a distribution of defects skewed towards the earlier stages of glaucoma, potentially affecting the generalisability of our findings to patients with more advanced disease. Future studies should include a broader range of defect severity to better assess the performance of the IMOvifa device across different stages of glaucoma.

In our study, we excluded cases based on both FPs and FLs to ensure the accuracy of the test results. Although FPs have the most significant influence on VF reliability compared with other metrics,24 33 FL was also considered critical, especially given IMOvifa’s eye-tracking technology, which ensures more accurate fixation monitoring—a feature absent in HVF. To maintain consistent criteria across both devices and ensure a fair comparison, cases with significant FL were excluded. Notably, only eight eyes could have potentially been included in the analysis if the FL criterion had not been applied, as six of the excluded eyes had fellow eyes already represented in the dataset, and five had other low-reliability parameters. While this suggests that the impact of the overall dataset would have been minimal, this approach may have led to the exclusion of valid data, reduced sample size and potential selection bias, impacting the generalisability of our findings. In addition, it is possible that IMOvifa could have exhibited stronger S-F correlations in patients with unreliable fixation, particularly given its eye-tracking capabilities, an area that may warrant investigation in future studies.

Regarding the study protocol, there was up to a 12-week difference between the three exams; for some patients, the HFA and/or OCT were performed at a regularly scheduled clinic visit outside of the study visit when the IMOvifa exam was performed. Although progression of disease is not expected during this short time period in this population of clinically stable patients who were under glaucoma specialist care, it is possible that the difference in timing of these exams may have affected the S-F correlations. Additionally, while test order was randomised for patients without a prior reliable HFA within the past 12 weeks, this was not applied uniformly to all patients, introducing potential variability in test performance. Differences in test sequencing could have influenced patient responses due to fatigue, learning effects or adaptation to testing conditions. Standardised randomisation in future studies could mitigate these factors and further refine the evaluation of S-F relationships.

Another potential limitation is that wearing face masks during testing due to the COVID-19 pandemic may have caused a new type of VF artefact.35 Recent studies have shown that mask use can lead to defects in the inferior VF and/or fogging of the perimeter’s trial lens, impacting the accuracy of VF reports.35 While steps were taken to minimise these artefacts, such as ensuring masks did not obstruct the VF by adjusting their position when necessary, their potential influence on results cannot be entirely ruled out. Lastly, there are differences between the HFA and IMOvifa parameters to consider, with different algorithms and threshold test strategies.14 15 17 While a few studies have highlighted strong correlations between the parameters and sensitivity of the HFA and IMOvifa, we found significant differences in specific point sensitivity values that may suggest differences between the devices’ normative databases and in their respective calculations of MD.17

In conclusion, this study found that in this cohort of relatively healthy participants, the IMOvifa and HFA had similar S-F correlations, suggesting that the IMOvifa might be a viable alternative to the HFA, given their comparable performance. Further larger-scale and longitudinal studies are necessary to validate the longitudinal diagnostic capability of IMOvifa.
