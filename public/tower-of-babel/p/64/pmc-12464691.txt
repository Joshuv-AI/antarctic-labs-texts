Epileptogenic zone-targeting nanoparticle drug delivery system for antiseizure medication
Authors: Honglu Ping, Qi Zhang, Xiaojie Chen, Yi Wang, Di Wu, Jianhong Wang, Jun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder affecting over 65 million people worldwide, characterized by abnormal excessive excitation of neuronal networks. Traditional antiseizure medications (ASMs), such as phenytoin (PHT), have limited therapeutic efficacy due to poor blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration and off-target toxicity. To address these issues, this study proposes an electrically responsive closed-loop system drug delivery system (DDS) using PHT as a model drug. The system utilizes electro-responsive nanoparticles made from polypyrrole (PPY), which can contract in response to electrical stimulation, allowing for on-demand drug release during epileptic discharges. Additionally, the excessive activation of tryptophan metabolism in people with epilepsy leads to tryptophan (Trp) depletion, promoting its uptake in the epileptogenic zone and creating a "metabolic trapping" effect. Meanwhile, hyaluronic acid (HA) binds to CD44 receptors, enhancing nanoparticle retention at the lesion, thereby creating a "targeted delivery-extended retention" effect. This non-invasive epileptogenic zone-targeting PPY-HA-PHT-Trp DDS may enhance ASM effect and have potential applications in clinical settings.Keywords: Epilepsy, Nano-drug delivery system, Polypyrrole, Electro-responsive drug release, Biosafety

Introduction

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder affecting over 65 million people worldwide [1,2]. Its pathophysiological basis originates from the abnormal excessive excitation of neuronal networks. Antiseizure medications (ASMs) represent the mainstay of epilepsy treatment [1,2]. The significant challenge of traditional ASMs is the poor penetration of these drugs across the blood-brain barrier (BBB), with only trace amounts of free drug reaching the brain [3]. Furthermore, ASMs typically have a broad systemic distribution, which may lead to peripheral toxicities, such as hepatotoxicity and bone marrow suppression [2,4,5]. Simultaneously, the broad inhibitory effects of these drugs on ion channels can induce dose-dependent central nervous system (CNS) adverse effects, including drowsiness, cognitive impairment, and sedation [[6], [7], [8]]. For example, phenytoin (PHT), a typical sodium channel blocker, is effective in controlling focal seizures by inhibiting voltage-gated sodium channels [9,10]. However, due to its intolerable side effects (such as gingival hyperplasia and arrhythmias) [11,12], PHT has been excluded from first-line treatments for epilepsy in modern clinical practice. Therefore, achieving targeted drug accumulation at epileptogenic zones without increasing the systemic drug exposure remains a key challenge in precision epilepsy therapy. Physically enhancing the concentration of ASMs within epileptic foci has been proven to effectively reduce seizure frequency, showing promise as a treatment for drug-resistant epilepsy. However, this delivery method is invasive, requires the localization of the epileptogenic zone, and is only applicable to patients with focal epilepsy [13].

Recent advancements in drug delivery system (DDS) offer promising new approaches to address these challenges. Our prior study developed an electro responsive DDS, PPY-PDA-PHT-ANG [14]. Polypyrrole (PPY) is a conductive polymer that can dynamically adjust its shape and size under electrical stimulation, which is the ability of electrical response [15,16]. This system enabled sustained, on-demand ASM release triggered by epileptiform discharges. However, PPY-PDA-PHT-ANG [14] requires the near-infrared photothermal response to open the BBB in order to achieve maximum instantaneous therapeutic effect during epileptic seizures. Due to the unpredictability of seizures, the approach remains passive in its therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, how to further enhance the accumulation of electro-responsive DDS in epileptogenic zone during interictal periods and wait for the occurrence of abnormal neuronal discharges is the key to turning this passive approach into an active strategy.

Within epileptogenic zones, the abnormal consumption of tryptophan (Trp) via the kynurenine pathway leads to a significant increase in Trp uptake [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. Furthermore, this phenomenon of increased targeting of nanoparticles to epileptic foci after Trp modification has been reported [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. Elevated CD44 expression has been consistently observed in epileptogenic zone across multiple animal models of epilepsy [[29], [30], [31], [32]] and epilepsy patients [33]. Based on this, we developed a nano DDS to maximizly utilize the pathological changes in the epileptogenic zone through targeted delivery, drug retention, and responsive release, thus matching the spatiotemporal characteristics of epileptic seizures(see Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6 J mice (5–6 weeks) were used in these studies. C57BL/6 J mice were group housed at 18–23 °C with 40 %–60 % humidity in a 12-h light-dark cycle (light on 8:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m.) and permitted access to food and water. All mice were housed in a 12-h light-dark cycle (light on 8:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m.) environment. Mice were single housed after surgery and for the remainder of the experiments. All efforts were made to minimize the number and suffering of the animals.

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85-23, revised 1996) and were approved by Fudan University animal welfare and ethics committee. The application approval number is 2020 Huashan Hospital JS-149. All experimental tests were conducted during the light cycle.

Synthesis and material characterization of electrically responsive nanoparticles

Chemicals and materials were presented in the supplementary materials. Electrically responsive nanoparticles were synthesized via a “one-pot” method. Briefly, 30/60/100/200 mg hyaluronic acid (HA) and 5 mg PHT were dissolved in 10 ml distilled water under vigorous stirring at room temperature. Subsequently, 36 μl pyrrole monomer was added under continuous stirring, followed by ultrasonication to ensure homogeneous dispersion. Polymerization was initiated by dropwise addition of 50 mg ammonium persulfate in an ice-water bath. The reaction mixture was stirred in the dark for 12 h. The resulting black product was washed three times with ddH2O to remove unreacted monomers and free PHT molecules, then resuspended in distilled water for further use. Due to the presence of HA, nanoparticles are negatively charged. The surface functionalization of nanoparticles is achieved through electrostatic attraction between positively charged Trp-PEG2k-NH2 and nanoparticles. Through an EDC/NHS chemical reaction, amide bonds are formed between the carboxyl groups of HA and the amino groups of Trp-PEG2k-NH2 to complete the surface functionalization of nanoparticles. The reaction time is 24 h [34,35]. The resulting black product was washed three times with ddH2O to remove unreacted monomers. Material characterization was presented in the supplementary materials.

In vitro electrically triggered release evaluation

Electrically responsive drug release was assessed in PBS buffer using two parallel platinum electrodes (1 cm2 × 1 cm2) under varying current intensities (200–500 μA) and durations (0.5–10 min). Prior to testing, a high-concentration nanoparticle solution was deposited onto the cathode surface and dried in the dark. Electrodes were then immersed in buffer (10 ml), and DC voltage stimulation was applied. At designated time points, 200 μl aliquots were withdrawn, and PHT concentration was quantified by HPLC to evaluate the effects of current intensity and duration on drug release.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay and BBB penetration assay

To evaluate the cellular compatibility, the bEnd.3 cell line was cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS, penicillin (100 IU ml−1), and streptomycin sulfate (100 mg ml−1) [DMEM for bEnd.3 cell culture was additionally supplemented with NaHCO3 (1.5 mg ml−1)]. The bEnd.3 cell line was purchased from ZeYe Biological Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The cell lines were cultured in a 5 % CO2 incubator at 37 °C. All cellular experiments were performed by using cells in the logarithmic growth phase. Cytotoxicity of nanoparticles toward cerebrovascular endothelial cells (bEnd.3) was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. BBB Penetration Assay was presented in the supplementary materials.

Kainic acid (KA)-induced chronic spontaneous epilepsy model

Male C57bl/6 mice (n = 40) weighing between 22 and 26 g were surgically prepared. All surgical procedures were performed under general anesthesia (1.5 % isoflurane in O2) and stereotaxic guidance. C57BL/6 J mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and immobilized in a stereotaxic apparatus. KA (0.5 mg ml−1, 0.5 μl), a glutamatergic excitotoxin, was microinjected into the dorsal hippocampal CA1 region (AP: −2.0 mm; ML: −1.3 mm; V: −1.6 mm). After KA injection, mice were monitored for status epilepticus (SE). Only mice that developed SE were used in subsequent experiments.

Western blot analysis and immunohistochemistry

For the KA-induced epilepsy mouse model, a subset of mice was perfused with saline via the heart to collect the brains (hippocampi). The brains were then divided to prepare homogenates for Western blot analysis. The following primary antibody was used for protein detection: anti-CD44 antibody (30854-1-AP, 1:500, Proteintech, biotinylated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, immunoreaction time), biotinylated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:1000, Beyotime). For immunofluorescence staining, other rats were perfused with saline and 4 % paraformaldehyde, and their brains were used to prepare frozen coronal sections (20 μm) for immunofluorescence. The following primary and secondary antibodies were used: anti-GFAP antibody (1:1000, ab7260, Abcam), goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488 (ab150077, 1:1000, Abcam), goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 555 (ab150114, 1:1000, Abcam).

Drug effects on seizure-like events

All surgical procedures were performed under general anesthesia (1.5 % isoflurane in O2) and stereotaxic guidance. After a two-month recovery period, helical electrodes were implanted into the CA3 region (AP: −2.9 mm; ML: −3.1 mm; V: −3.1 mm) for electroencephalography (EEG) monitoring. Post-surgical recovery (7 days) preceded baseline EEG recording (8 h/day for 3 days) following intraperitoneal (i.p.) saline injection. Only mice with detectable seizures were given subsequent treatment. Mice exhibiting spontaneous seizures were divided into two groups (n = 5/group) and administered either free PHT (10 mg kg−1) or PPY-HA-PHT nanoparticles (10 mg kg−1 PHT equivalent) i.p. for 5 days, with EEG recorded daily. Generalized tonic-clonic seizures (GS) were defined as continuous rhythmic spike-wave discharges (>30 s duration, amplitude ≥3 × baseline, frequency >2 Hz). FS was defined as a paroxysmal event that continued more than 10 s and had an average amplitude of three times versus baseline and frequency of >2 Hz.

In vivo targeting evaluation

The near-infrared fluorescent dye Cy5.5 was conjugated to PPY-HA-PHT-Trp nanoparticles or PEGylated Trp (Trp-PEG). Cy5.5 labeling efficiency was quantified via a Tecan Spark microplate reader to standardize dye loading (1.2 μg/mg nanoparticles). KA-induced chronic epileptic mice were divided into two groups (n = 3/group): one group received intraperitoneal injections of PPY-HA-PHT-Trp-Cy5.5 (5 mg/kg nanoparticles), while the control group received equivalent doses of Trp-PEG-Cy5.5; an additional normal control group (healthy mice, n = 3) was injected with PPY-HA-PHT-Trp-Cy5.5 at the same dose. Whole-body fluorescence imaging was performed using an IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer) at 12, 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 h post-injection (excitation/emission: 675/695 nm, exposure: 500 ms). Targeting specificity was quantified by calculating the brain-to-systemic fluorescence ratio and epileptic focus-to-whole brain ratio, with epileptogenic zone confirmed by MRI-histopathology co-registration.

Rotarod test and open field test

KA-treated mice (n = 5/group) were administered saline, free PHT, or nanoparticles via i.p. injection. After 24 h, mice were acclimatized to the testing room for 1 h. The rotarod was accelerated from 0 to 40 rpm over 2 min, then maintained at 40 rpm for 3 min. Eight trials were conducted with 30-min intervals (1 h between trials 4 and 5). Latency to fall was recorded. Mice (n = 5/group) were injected as above and tested 24 h later. Each mouse was placed in a black acrylic chamber (45 × 45 × 45 cm) for 15 min. Total distance traveled and average speed were recorded. Chambers were cleaned between trials.

Blood biochemistry and histopathology

Biosafety of the treatment were assessed by blood biochemistry and histopathology. Mice (n = 5/group) received daily i.p. injections for 7 days. Blood was collected via cardiac puncture under isoflurane anesthesia. After overnight refrigeration (4 °C), samples were centrifuged (4000 rpm, 10 min) for analysis of total protein, albumin, globulin, total bilirubin, ALT, AST, and BUN. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) were paraffin-embedded, sectioned (4 μm), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Images were acquired using a Thunder Virtual Slide Scanner.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SD, and a two-tailed p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. One-way ANOVA with Dunn's multiple comparisons was used to compare the frequency and duration of seizures in the spontaneous seizure model.

Results

Optimization and characterization of PPY-HA-PHT nanoparticles

The HA, pyrrole, and PHT molecules interacted via hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and π-π interactions, forming mixed drug-loaded nanoparticles (PPY-HA-PHT). To screen for the optimal HA and pyrrole mass ratio, the HA content in the PPY-HA-PHT nanocomposites was grouped from 30 mg to 200 mg. The average particle size of the nanoparticles decreased from 199.8 nm at 30 mg HA to 50.8 nm at 200 mg HA (Fig. 2A). The impedance spectra showed that, compared to the other two groups, the nanoparticles in the 100 mg HA group exhibited lower charge transfer resistance and better conductivity (Fig. 2B). As the HA content increased, the conductivity significantly decreased at 200 mg (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). As shown in Fig. 2D, the device simulates epileptic seizures in vitro and the electrical response ability of nanoparticles. With increased HA content, drug release significantly increased during the 10 min, 200 μA electrical stimulation (Fig. 2E). The 100 mg HA content group of PPY-HA-PHT nanoparticles exhibited the highest drug release, but no significant difference in PHT release was observed between the 100 mg and 200 mg HA groups. As HA content increased, the PPY-HA-PHT particle size decreased, which, despite the reduction in volume, increased the surface area, potentially enhancing drug loading.

To explore the possible mechanisms of this improvement, molecular models were established for PPY, consisting of three consecutive pyrrole molecules, binding with PHT molecules in the presence or absence of HA (Fig. 2F and G). For the composite system of PPY and PHT, the intermolecular binding energy was −19 kcal/mol. However, after the introduction of HA, the binding energy of the PPY-PHT-HA composite system increased to −28 kcal/mol. Considering the nanoparticle size, drug release under electrical stimulation, and electro-responsive ability, we ultimately selected the PPY-HA-PHT system with 100 mg HA content as our final choice.

Processing and performance characterization of the selected nanoparticles

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Fig. 2H) revealed that the PPY-HA-PHT-Trp nanoparticles had a rough surface, with an average particle size of approximately 86 nm, spherical shape, and uniform distribution. The drug loading capacity was determined to be 30.4 % by calculating the drug concentration in the supernatant after synthesis. The weight loss curve of PPY-HA-PHT showed a significant decrease in weight around 350 °C, which corresponds to the decomposition temperature of PHT, confirming the successful synthesis of drug-loaded hybrid polymer nanoparticles (Fig. 2I). The proton NMR spectrum of Trp-PEG2k-NH2 (Fig. S1) showed the proton peak of the PEG amine at 7.77 ppm. In summary, an amide bond formation occurred between Trp and PEG. Zeta potential measurements showed that, after modification, the potential shifted from −38 mV to near neutral, indicating successful surface binding (Fig. 2J). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. S2) confirmed the copolymerization of PPY and HA. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis of the nanoparticles' hydrodynamic diameter for 30 days (Fig. 2K) showed that the nanoparticles exhibited excellent dispersion stability in serum-containing media. The total drug release under 5 A current stimulation for 24 h was set as 100 %. Electrical stimulation at 200 μA and 500 μA, similar to extracellular electrical potentials, was used to assess the drug release performance. After 10 min of stimulation at 200 μA and 500 μA, the release rates of PHT were 35 % and 59 %, respectively (Fig. 2F). About 10 % of the drug was released within the first 30 s of electrical stimulation, indicating a rapid release potential during epileptic seizures. While treated with an “on-off” cycle of electric stimulus, the drugs were released accordingly at “on” state and showed negligible leaking at “off” state (Fig. 2M). Cytotoxicity results confirmed that nanoparticles up to a concentration of 1 mg/ml did not affect the survival rate of bEnd.3 cells (Fig. 2N). The integrity of the endothelial monolayer was monitored using transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER). After seven days of culture, the TEER reached a peak value of 200 Ω-cm2, meeting the requirements for in vitro BBB assessment. The permeation efficiency over time is shown in Fig. S3.

Practicability verification of targeted therapy

Quantitative Western blot analysis revealed significantly elevated CD44 levels in the epileptogenic hippocampal region compared to non-epileptic brain areas (Fig. 3A and B, P < 0.01). This upregulation was further confirmed by longitudinal immunofluorescence studies, demonstrating progressive CD44 overexpression in lesion areas at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months post-epileptogenesis (Fig. 3C–D, P < 0.05 for all time points vs. controls).

In vivo distribution via live fluorescence imaging

Fluorescence imaging conducted in KA-induced chronic spontaneous epilepsy mice (C57BL/6, n = 3/group) and healthy control mice (n = 3/group) revealed distinct pharmacokinetic profiles (Fig. 4A). In the epileptic model group, mice receiving nano showed a prolonged nanoparticle retention time in comparison to the healthy control group after 48 h (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4B). However, no significant difference in retention time was observed between the epileptic mice injected with Trp-PEG and the normal control mice injected with nano (P > 0.05).

The distribution of Trp-PEG and nano in KA mice differed, with nano showing a greater tendency to remain in the brain. After 48 h, the proportion of brain fluorescence relative to total body fluorescence increased over time. The brain fluorescence ratio in epileptic mice was significantly higher compared to Trp-PEG (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4C), indicating that CD44 expression in the epileptic region facilitated the retention of HA-doped DDS in the brain. Notably, 24 h after injection, the accumulation of Trp-PEG in the brain of epileptic mice was higher than that of nano in the brain of healthy control mice. However, this advantage rapidly diminished after 24 h. Therefore, we chose to image the brain at 24 h (Fig. 4D). The results revealed significantly higher fluorescence values in the brains of model mice compared to the normal group, whether injected with nano or Trp-PEG (P < 0.001/P < 0.01) (Fig. 4E), with the model mice injected with nano showing the highest fluorescence values (P < 0.05). Further brain slices showed that compared with normal mice, after administering the same dose of fluorescently labeled nano, the fluorescence in the epileptogenic zone of epileptic mice was higher (P < 0.001), indicating that nano has unique targeting properties for epileptogenic zone. Compared with Trp-PEG, nano showed stronger fluorescence in the epileptogenic zone of epileptic mice (P < 0.05). The fluorescence intensity of epileptogenic zone in the Trp-PEG group was higher than that in the normal group of mice treated with nano (Fig. 4F).

Effect of PPY-HA-PHT-Trp on seizure-like event incidence

We recorded three-day EEGs in mice to establish baseline measurements for epilepsy treatment (Fig. 5A). Only mice exhibiting GS proceeded to subsequent therapy. During the 3-day treatment period, both the frequency and duration of GS and FS decreased in all mice (Fig. 5B–I). The average seizure frequency decreased from a baseline mean of 3.8 to 1.4 seizures (Fig. 5B). In the free PHT administration group, the average seizure frequency decreased from 3.6 to 2.2 seizures (Fig. 5F). Compared with free PHT, the PHT delivered via the DDS demonstrated significantly stronger antiepileptic efficacy during the 3-day treatment period, with particularly pronounced effectiveness in controlling GS. Following treatment discontinuation, the DDS-delivered PHT group showed only a minimal increase in GS frequency (mean increase: 0.2 seizures) over the subsequent three days, with no statistically significant difference compared to the treatment period (Fig. 5B). Conversely, the free PHT group exhibited an increase from 2.2 to 3.6 seizures (Fig. 5F). Notably, one mouse in the free PHT group even exceeded its baseline seizure frequency due to abrupt withdrawal. EEG recordings from the hippocampal CA3 region (Fig. 5J) showed that PPY-HA-PHT-Trp significantly reduced the severity of seizures, whereas the PHT group exhibited no significant differences before, during, or after treatment.

Effect of PPY-HA-PHT-Trp on motor function

Mice treated with the PPY-HA-PHT-Trp exhibited significant improvements in both total distance (Fig. 5L, P < 0.05) and average velocity (Fig. 5M, P < 0.05) compared to the free PHT group. No statistically significant differences were observed between the PHT group and saline controls. However, the rotarod test, which evaluates motor coordination, balance, and endurance, revealed no significant differences in latency to fall among the three groups (Fig. 5N).

Biosafety assessments

After exposure to hyaluronidase, the nanoparticles underwent structural loosening and volume expansion (Fig. 6A and B), followed by fragmentation (Fig. 6C). These findings confirm the successful incorporation of HA into the PPY matrix and that the resulting nanoparticles possess biodegradability.

Blood chemistry analysis showed no significant differences in hematological parameters among the groups, including total protein, albumin, globulin, total bilirubin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (Fig. 6D). Histological examination of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, was performed using HE staining (Fig. 6E). No significant organ damage was observed in the PHT and nano treatment groups compared to the saline group.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully developed an intelligent closed-loop drug DDS using PPY-HA-PHT-Trp nanoparticles for the treatment of chronic spontaneous epilepsy. The results demonstrate the advantages of this electric-responsive DDS in terms of targeted delivery to the epileptogenic zone, prolonged drug retention, enhanced ASM effect and favorable biosafety.

First, PHT was used as a model drug, and it was encapsulated during the copolymerization process of HA and pyrrole at a specific mass ratio. As the HA concentration increased, the average particle size of the nanoparticles decreased, indicating that the HA chains had a stabilizing effect and further confirming the successful doping of HA. This phenomenon may be related to the steric hindrance and electrostatic repulsion of HA, which prevent newly generated PPY particles from colliding and aggregating with each other, and disperse and restrict the growth of PPY in a smaller space [36,37]. In vitro electrically triggered release evaluation provided a comprehensive response of the drug-loading performance and electro-responsive ability of the nanoparticles. The results showed that the HA-to-PPY mass ratio had a significant impact on the drug release under electrical stimulation. Additionally, simplified molecular docking simulations were conducted. After the introduction of HA, which is rich in hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, it was observed that the carbonyl oxygen of PHT could form hydrogen bonds with the hydroxyl groups of both PPY and HA. This firmly attracted PHT, and HA could also form hydrogen bonds, further enhancing the intermolecular interactions and its attraction to PHT. The binding energy of the PPY-HA-PHT composite system increased to −28 kcal/mol. HA content alters nanoparticle size, binding energy, and electro-responsive ability [38,39]. About 10 % of the drug was released within the first 30 s of electrical stimulation and the leakage of drug in PPY-HA-PHT-Trp was much higher when electrified than when not electrified, indicating a rapid release potential during epileptic seizures.

The spatiotemporal characteristics of CD44 overexpression in epileptic mice indicate its potential as an enduring molecular marker for epileptogenic tissue throughout disease progression, consistent with previous research [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. CD44 may play a key role in the pathogenesis of epilepsy through glial activation or extracellular matrix remodeling [32]. The temporal stability of CD44 expression is particularly noteworthy, as it addresses a major challenge in epilepsy treatment—the need for targets that remain consistently present during chronic disease phases.

In terms of pharmacokinetics, PPY-HA-PHT-Trp nanoparticles demonstrated prolonged retention times in the epileptogenic zone compared to free PHT or Trp-PEG. The accumulation of Trp-PEG in the brain of epileptic mice was higher than that of nano in the brain of healthy control mice only at 24 h after injection, possibly due to LAT1-mediated compensatory tryptophan uptake under seizure-induced metabolic stress. The absence of prolonged retention time in the epileptic mice injected with Trp-PEG suggests that the long retention of the nanoparticle DDS in epileptic mice may be associated with the CD44-targeting mechanism, indicating a dual targeting mechanism of tryptophan and HA, which is more conducive to nano targeting and staying in epileptogenic zone. EEG results confirm that PPY-HA-PHT-Trp enhances the inhibitory effect of PHT on chronic spontaneous epilepsy not only enhanced efficacy during treatment but notably prolonged suppression after drug withdrawal. While free PHT showed only transient effects, likely due to its rapid metabolism and clearance, the use of CD44-targeted nanoparticles ensured that the drug was retained in the epileptogenic zone for a longer duration. This prolonged effect is crucial for managing chronic epilepsy, where sustained drug levels are needed to prevent recurrent seizures.

Considering that the broad inhibitory effects of ASMs on neuronal excitability are key contributors to the central adverse reactions of PHT, systemic PHT exposure may suppress generalized neuronal activity, leading to reduced locomotor function. We also examined the effect of both PPY-HA-PHT-Trp and systemic ASM delivery on motor coordination using the open-field and rotarod test. The longer movement distance and higher movement speed both reflect the therapeutic effect of nano DDS. Moreover, the in vivo biosafety studies confirmed the safety and biodegradability of the PPY-HA-PHT-Trp nanoparticles, with no significant impact on general activity, neuromuscular coordination, or organ health. The absence of liver and kidney toxicity, along with the lack of adverse effects on blood parameters, supports the biocompatibility of this DDS. The complete absence of adverse effects with DDS also suggests that even higher local doses could be safely used to further increase the therapeutic window of these drugs. This is a critical consideration for the clinical of any DDS, especially for long-term treatments such as epilepsy management.

By fully utilizing the pathophysiology of epileptogenic zone, PPY-HA-PHT-Trp has improved the active targeting ability of ASM to epileptogenic zone. Previous materials focused primarily on physical properties, while we integrated the pathophysiological characteristics of epilepsy into the design of this nanomaterial [14]. Currently, there are plans to increase the concentration of ASM in epileptogenic zone. Implantable DDS in the brain require surgical implantation, which is highly invasive and carries a risk of infection in daily life [13,40]. This increases the burden on patients. Secondly, the retention rate of DDS within the epileptogenic zone is prolonged during the interval between epileptic seizures. In contrast to previous studies that required the use of near-infrared photothermal therapy to open the BBB in order to increase instantaneous concentration within the epileptogenic zone, our nanoparticles are pre trapped in the epileptogenic zone, waiting for rapid drug release during abnormal electrophysiological activity, increasing the possibility of long-term patient compliance. This DDS better aligns with the clinical characteristics of sudden onset and cessation of epileptic seizures.

However, some drawbacks of the DDS remained to be settled. To conform to clinical application, a patient-friendly pathway of administration for long-term medication is desired such as oral administration of convenience. This study was conducted exclusively in male mice. Future research should validate the efficacy of this therapy in female animals to comprehensively assess potential sex differences.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that by improving the synthesis method of electrically responsive materials, PPY-HA-PHT-Trp nanoparticles synthesized using HA and modified with Trp can enhance the ability of delivering ASM to epileptogenic zone, prolong ASM residence time in epileptogenic zone, and improve the safety of electrically responsive DDS. Our findings lay a foundation for the development of personalized, long-term treatments for epilepsy, with potential applications in both preclinical and clinical settings.
