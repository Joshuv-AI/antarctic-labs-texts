KNC nanozyme repairs hypoxia ischemia brain damage through ALOX12 mediated lipid peroxidation inhibition
Authors: Xiaohua Dong, Dongya Jiang, Shuhan Chen, Ji Tan, Jing Zhao, Xingdan Liu, Ziyi Lu, Kelvin W.K. Yeung, Yun Liao, Xuanyong Liu, Liping Ouyang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hypoxia-Ischemia Brain Damage (HIBD) results in a widespread neuronal damage and permanent brain tissue injury due to the severe reactive oxygen species (ROS) boost induced neuroinflammation. In this work, a K doped N-C based nanozyme was fabricated for scavenging ROS. KNC could impair ROS production and M1 polarization in microglia. Beneficial from these contents, the brain damage was mitigated in HIBD rats, which was proved by the increased regional blood flow, decreased pro-inflammatory microglia and astrocyte activation. The learning and memory capabilities were restored after applying with KNC post HIBD, which was ascribed to the diminished HI-induced dendritic spine loss in hippocampal regions. RNA-seq revealed that decreased ALOX12 expression is one of the clues of neuronal protection. KNC could combine with ALOX12 and further inhibit the lipid peroxidation. These two clues obtain KNC with superior ability of inhibition of ROS boost induced brain damage post HIBD. This nanozyme provided a potential strategies and new idea of HIBD therapy.

Introduction

Hypoxia-Ischemia Brain Damage (HIBD) resulting from perinatal asphyxia, placental blood flow abnormalities, or other factors leads to inadequate oxygen supply to the fetus or newborn [1]. The pathogenesis of HIBD involves a series of complex pathological processes, including oxidative stress triggered by initial ischemia-reperfusion, disruption of calcium ion homeostasis, excitotoxicity, inflammation, and blood-brain barrier dysfunction [2]. These processes collectively result in widespread neuronal damage and permanent brain tissue injury. Despite recent advances in the diagnosis and treatment of HIBD, such as hypothermia therapy and pharmacological interventions [3,4], further research is needed to develop more effective treatments and early intervention strategies.

HI-induced brain damage induces subsequent neuroinflammation, which results in long-term neurological disabilities and neurodevelopmental impairments [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. Microglia, the immune-surveillance glial cells in the brain, is the most related cells that participates in neuroinflammation. Post HIBD, much reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced and calculated around of the damaged tissue, which is highly activated to M1. The produced inflammatory mediators by M1, such as IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and chemokines, exacerbate neuronal damage through multiple pathways [10,11]. Consequently, it is an effective strategies to prevent consequent damage and repair neural functions post HIBD by efficient ROS scavenging.

Accumulating evidence demonstrates that ROS generation and lipid peroxidation represent hallmark features of microglial activation and neurodegenerative pathologies [12]. Critically, lipid peroxidation triggers ROS production, generating 4-HNE which subsequently induces ER stress, mitochondrial damage, and microglial pro-inflammatory activation. This cascade forms a self-amplifying loop with ROS generation, promoting neuroinflammation and driving neurodegeneration [13,14]. ALOX12 (Arachidonate 12-lipoxygenase), as a member of the lipoxygenase family, plays a pivotal role in mediating both pro-oxidant and pro-inflammatory effects [15]. Specifically, ALOX12 and its metabolite 12-HETE directly mediate microglial activation, exacerbating inflammatory responses and impairing neurological function [16]. Notably, the ALOX12-12-HETE axis also plays a critical role in hepatic and pulmonary ischemia-reperfusion injury. Moreover, in spinal cord injury models, ALOX12 knockdown suppresses neuroinflammation and apoptosis, consequently attenuating neurotrauma [17]. However, whether ALOX12 mediates HI-induced neuroinflammation in HIBD remains to be determined.

Enzyme is the natural molecules that maintain homeostasis of ROS via redox [18]. For example, superoxide dismutase (SOD) catalyzes O2·−to O2, catalase catalyzes H2O2 to O2 [19], and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) catalyzes glutathione and hydrogen peroxide to oxidized glutathione and O2 [20]. Even though there are several enzyme based strategies, such as orgotein, and abselen, are developed, the allergic reactions, low reaction constants and catalytic efficiency limit the application of natural enzyme in vivo [[21], [22], [23]]. Nano-enzyme is the new emerged materials that could play effect instead of enzyme, has the characteristics of high catalytic efficiency, stability, economy and large-scale preparation [24]. Nano-enzyme has been widely researched in several applications, such as bio-imaging, anti-oxidation, anti-bacteria-disease therapy, bio-sensing and so on [[25], [26], [27]]. N-C based non-metal nanozyme exhibits superior catalytic efficiency due to that nitrogen may induce new active sites in the nanozyme structure, regulates the electronic structure of the nanozyme, adjust the band structure of the nanozyme and promote electron transfer to facilitate the catalytic reaction, and changes the interaction between the nanozyme and the substrate, enhancing the adsorption capacity of the substrate near the catalytic center [28]. It is worthy to be noted that the metal adding would further improve the catalytic efficiency via dissociating metal ions to increase the active N-C sites, such as Fe-Nx-C [29]. Herewith, it is worth to explore metallic based N-C nanozyme.

In this work, a novel M-Nx-C nanozyme (KNC) is established as a potent neuroprotective agent with multimodal mechanisms against HIBD. Dual catalase/superoxide dismutase-mimicking activities and exceptional stability are demonstrated in KNC, enabling effective ROS scavenging in pathological microenvironments. Neuroinflammation is attenuated through suppression of microglial M1 polarization and subsequent astrocyte A1 activation by KNC, thereby preserving synaptic integrity and neuronal viability. A novel therapeutic axis is identified through integrated transcriptomic and functional analyses, wherein ALOX12 is directly bound and inhibited by KNC, leading to abrogation of lipid peroxidation-driven ROS amplification loops. The ALOX12-12-HETE signaling cascade is disrupted through this targeted mechanism, resulting in mitigation of acute neuronal apoptosis and restoration of long-term cognitive function in HIBD models. KNC is positioned as a pioneering nanozyme that concurrently addresses oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and metabolic dysregulation, presenting a promising translational strategy for neonatal brain injury and related neurodegenerative disorders.

Experimental section

Materials preparation and characterization

Materials preparation: Formamide was placed in a reactor at 200 °C for 24 h, then centrifuged and dried. The obtained powder was ground and mixed with potassium chloride, then transferred to a tube furnace, heated to 700 °C under argon atmosphere, and held for 2 h. The obtained product was washed and dried to obtain sample KNC.

Characterization: The morphology of the prepared samples was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Talos F200X G2, USA). The phase composition of the samples was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8 advance, Bruker, Germany). Raman spectroscopy (Raman, LabRam HR800, Horiba Jobin Yvon, France) was used to detect the Raman vibration peaks of the samples. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA) was used to characterize the elemental composition and chemical state of the sample.

Catalase-like (CAT-like) activity: The time-dependent H2O2 consumption of KNC in the reaction system was determined by colorimetry. The reaction system was as follows: 1 mL PBS, 3 mM H2O2, 100 μg/mL sample. After mixing well, incubate at 37 °C. After the reaction, the mixture of 200 μL was placed on a 96-well plate and reacted with 20 μL 1 % Ti(SO4)2 for 5 min. The absorbance value of the mixture at 405 nm was determined. The formula for calculating H2O2 removal effect is as follows:H2O2scavengingeffect(%)=(Ap−An)−(A−An)(Ap−An)×100%In which, A, An and Ap are the absorbance values of the treated sample, negative control and positive control at 405 nm, respectively.

SOD-like activity: The superoxide dismutase-like activity of KNC was determined using the total superoxide dismutase detection kit WST-8 (Beyotime, Shanghai) according to the instructions. 50 μg/mL KNC was added into the working solution and left for 15 min. The absorbance of the mixed solution at 450 nm was measured using a multifunctional enzyme marker. SOD-like activity is calculated by the following formula:SOD‐likeactivity(%)=(Bp−Bn)−(B−Bn)(Bp−Bn)×100%In which, B, Bn and Bp are the absorbance values of the treated sample, negative control and positive control at 450 nm, respectively.

In vitro experiments

Cell line and treatment: Mouse microglia cell line BV2 (Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd, ZQ0397) was maintained in DMEM (Gibco, 11965–092) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10099141C) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Servicebio, G4003-100 ML). To activate M1 type microglia, cells were treated with 100 ng/mL LPS (Sigma, L2880) for either 1 or 4 days before proceeding with further experiments.

ROS staining: ROS staining was performed as we previously reported. Briefly, BV2 cells were plated in transwell plates with 1 × 105 cells per well. BV2 cells were treated with LPS and subsequently co-cultured with KNC at concentrations of 2, 4, and 8 ng/mL for 1 and 4 days, or the cells were treated with LPS, LPS combined with 8 ng/mL KNC, or a combination of 8 ng/mL KNC and 100 nM 12-HETE. ROS levels were measured using the Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0033S) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, BV2 cells were incubated with 10 μM 2,7-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe at 37 °C for 30 min in a dark incubator. The ROS staining was immediately visualized and captured using the confocal microscope. (Leica, SP8, Germany).

Microglia phenotype detection: BV2 cells were seeded into transwell plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. LPS-induced BV2 cells, combined with KNC-treated BV2 cells, were cultured for 1 and 4 days. The cells were then fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, followed by treatment with 0.1 % Triton X-100. Next, blocking was performed using 5 % normal serum to reduce non-specific binding. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies anti-iNOS (1:200, 22226–1-AP, Rabbit, Proteintech) and anti-Arg1 (1:200, NB100-59740, Goat, NOVUS). Subsequently, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies CoraLite488-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:250, SA00013-2, Goat, Proteintech) and Cy3-conjugated Donkey Anti-Goat IgG (H + L) (1:300, GB21404, Donkey, Servicebio) for 2 h. Finally, the cells were mounted with a mounting solution containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (P0131, Beyotime). The fluorescence images were observed and captured using the confocal microscope (Leica, SP8, Germany).

Quantitative RT-qPCR:Cortex brain tissues from the Sham, HIBD, and HIBD groups treated with KNC were collected 3 days post-surgery. Total RNA was isolated from the cortex brain tissue using RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (R701-01, Vazyme) following the instructions of manufacturer. RNA concentrations were determined with the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. mRNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Perfect Real Time) (RR036A, Takara) according to the provided protocol. The qPCR was performed as previously described. Briefly, qPCR was carried out on an ABI StepOne Plus instrument using PowerUP SYBR Green Master Mix (A25742, ThermoFisher) with ALOX12 as the target gene and β-Actin as the reference gene. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Western blot: The cortex brain tissue of injured side were collected at 3 days post-surgery. Proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with phosphatase inhibitors. The lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined with a BCA protein assay (Thermo Scientific, #23225). Equivalent amounts of protein (10 μg per sample) were loaded onto a 10 % Tris-Glycine PAGE for separation and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked for 2 h at room temperature with BSA. Following this, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies. The membranes were then washed three times with TBST and subsequently incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Finally, the membranes were visualized using the Tanon 6200 imaging system (Tanno, Shanghai). The primary antibodies of ALOX12 (1:1000, rabbit, A14703, ABclonal), β-Actin (1:20000, mouse, 66009-1-Ig, Proteintech), and the secondary antibodies of HRP-conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:5000, SA00001-2, Proteintech), HRP-conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (1:5000, SA00001-1, Proteintech) were used.

Cellular Thermal Shift assay (CETSA):The CETSA was conducted as previously described (35). For the intact cell assay, BV2 cells were exposed to 8 ng/mL KNC or vehicle (ultrapure water) for 1 h. After treatment, the cells were collected and thoroughly washed with PBS to remove any residual KNC. Each cell suspension was placed into separate 0.2 mL PCR tubes. The samples were then subjected to denaturation at various temperatures on a PCR machine for 3 min. Subsequently, the cells were frozen and thawed twice using liquid nitrogen. After centrifugation, the supernatants were analyzed by Western blot.

Lipid peroxidation detection: BV2 cells were seeded in transwell plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. The cells were treated with LPS, LPS combined with 8 ng/mL KNC, or a combination of 8 ng/mL KNC and 100 nM 12-HETE. Or cells treated with LPS, and LPS combined with KNC, ML355, or KNC and ALOX12. Lipid peroxidation levels were assessed using the BODIPY 581/591C11 probe (Thermo Fisher, D3861), which exhibits a shift in emission from red to green upon oxidation by lipid peroxides, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, BV2 cells were incubated with 2 nM BODIPY 581/591C11 at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. The lipid peroxidation staining was analyzed using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope equipped with 488 nm and 560 nm lasers.

NLRP3 detection: BV2 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. Following LPS stimulation, cells were treated for 24 h under various conditions: 1) LPS with 8 ng/mL KNC, 10 μM ML355, or 8 ng/mL KNC plus ALOX12; 2) LPS with KCl (50 nM, 100 nM, 200 nM, or 50 mM). Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100, and blocked with 5 % normal serum. Primary antibody incubation used rabbit anti-NLRP3 (1:300, GB114320-100, Servicebio) overnight at 4 °C, followed by CoraLite488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:250, SA00013-2, Proteintech) for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI-containing mounting medium (P0131, Beyotime), and images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Leica SP8, Germany).

RNA-seq

RNA Extraction and Library Construction: Hippocampal tissue from HIBD and KNC-treated HIBD rats was collected 72 h post-surgery. Total RNA was extracted and RNA purity and the RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Transcriptome libraries were prepared with the VAHTS Universal V5 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit. Transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

RNA Sequencing and Differential Gene Expression Analysis:The libraries were sequenced using the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Approximately 47257434 raw reads were obtained for each sample. The raw reads in fastq format were processed using fastp software to remove low-quality reads, resulting in clean reads for subsequent data analysis. HISAT2 software was used for aligning the reads to the reference genome, and gene expression levels (FPKM) were calculated. The read counts for each gene were obtained using HTSeq-count. R (v 3.2.0) was used to plot the gene counts to assess the biological replicates of the samples.

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis was performed using DESeq2 software. Genes with a q-value <0.05 and a fold change >2 or <0.5 were defined as DEGs. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed based on the hypergeometric distribution algorithm to identify significantly enriched functional terms using R (v3.2.0). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA software. Predefined gene sets were used, and genes were ranked according to their differential expression between the two sample classes. The analysis tested whether the predefined gene sets were enriched at the top or bottom of this ranked list.

Potassium ion Release: To assess potassium ion (K+) release kinetics, 8 μg KNC was dissolved in 10 mL ultrapure water with gentle vortexing, followed by incubation at 37 °C. Supernatants were collected at specified time points (days 1, 4, 7, and 14) and quantified for K+ concentrations via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; Agilent 7800, Agilent Technologies, USA). Potassium ion quantification was performed by ICP-MS under optimized conditions: Plasma/auxiliary/carrier/makeup gas flows at 15/1/1/1 L/min, He collision cell gas at 5 mL/min (0.09–0.13 MPa). Daily performance validation confirmed instrument sensitivity prior to batch analysis including CalBlk, multi-level CalStd, FQBlk, and samples. Elemental concentrations were derived from Rh-IS corrected calibration curves generated in Agilent MassHunter.

Pharmacological Blocks of K+ efflux: BV2 cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C under a humidified 5 % CO2 atmosphere. For pharmacological inhibition of K+ efflux, LPS-stimulated BV2 cells were treated with 50 nM, 100 nM, 200 nM, or 50 mM KCl (GC102003, Servicebio, China) for 24 h.

In vivo experiments

Hypoxic-Ischemic Brain Damage model in neonatal rats: Seven-day-old male SD rats were used to establish the HIBD model using the modified Rice-Vannucci method. Briefly, P7 SD rats were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the right common carotid artery (CCA) was exposed and ligated with a double-layer suture (7-0 thread). After ligation, the rat pups were returned to their mothers for 2 h. Subsequently, the pups were placed in a hypoxic chamber with 8 % O2 and 92 % N2 at 37 °C for 2 h to induce hypoxia-ischemia. In the sham group, rats underwent exposure of the right CCA without ligation or subsequent ischemia. In the KNC treatment group, rat pups received intraventricular injections of KNC at concentrations of 2 ng/mL, 4 ng/mL, and 8 ng/mL immediately after surgery. The rats were sacrificed 72 h after the hypoxia-ischemia insult, and their brain tissues were collected for subsequent experiments. All animal surgical experiments and procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tongren Hospital (Approval No. A2024-028-01).

Cerebral Infarction detection: The brains of the sham, HIBD, and KNC-treated HIBD rats were harvested 72 h post-surgery and photographed with the camera. The brains were then sectioned into 2 mm coronal slices and stained with a 2 % solution of 2, 3, 5-Triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC, Coolaber, China) for 30 min. Subsequently, the stained brain slices from each group were arranged in a row and photographed. The red-stained areas represented non-ischemic tissue, while the white-stained areas indicated ischemic necrosis.

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining: Brain tissue samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h. The fixed tissues were then dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin at 60 °C overnight. Paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 4–6 μm and mounted on slides. The sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol. The slices were then stained with hematoxylin for 3–5 min, differentiated in hydrochloric acid ethanol, rinsed in running tap water, and blued in ammonia water for 1 min. The sections were subsequently stained with eosin. Following staining, the sections were dehydrated and cleared through a graded series of ethanol and xylene. Finally, the sections were mounted with a neutral resin mounting medium, air-dried, and observed and captured under the Pannoramic Scan Pannoramic SCAN II (3DHISTECH, Hungary).

Nissl staining: The paraffin-embedded brain slices were used for Nissl staining. The slices were stained with 0.1 % cresyl violet solution for 20–30 min, then gently rinsed with 70 % ethanol. Next, the sections were dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol, cleared with xylene, and mounted with a neutral resin medium. The Nissl staining images were obtained by the Pannoramic Scan Pannoramic SCAN II (3DHISTECH, Hungary).

Immunofluorescence (IF):The rat brain tissue was removed and immediately fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 24 h. After fixation, the tissue was washed with PBS, then dehydrated through a series of graded alcohols (100 %, 95 %, 70 %), and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded brain tissue was sectioned into 5 μm thick slices and mounted onto microscope slides. The sections were subsequently immersed in xylene and graded alcohols (100 %, 95 %, 70 %), and finally washed with PBS. Antigen retrieval was performed, followed by blocking with BSA at room temperature for 30 min to 1 h. Primary antibodies were then applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with PBS, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. Following PBS washes, DAPI staining was performed, and the slides were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium. Finally, the sections were observed and imaged using a confocal microscope (Lecia, SP8, Germany). The primary antibody of were NeuN (1:200, mouse, GB15138, Servicebio), GFAP (1:500, rabbit, GB11096, Servicebio), IBA1(1:500, rabbit, GB11096, Servicebio), and CD86 (1:200, mouse, nbp2-25208, Novus), and ALOX12 (1:200, rabbit, A14703, ABclonal) were used, and the secondary antibodies CY3-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:300, GB21303, Servicebio), Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (1:400, GB25301, Servicebio), CY3-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (1:400, GB21401, Servicebio), and Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:400, GB25303, Servicebio) were used.

Morris water maze (MWM) test: The Morris water maze test was conducted on one-month-old rats in the sham group, HIBD group, and the KNC-treated HIBD group to assess the learning and memory capabilities of the experimental animals. In this test, the animals were required to locate a submerged platform in a black circular pool with a diameter of 140 cm and a height of 50 cm. The pool was situated in a controlled environment, shielded from noise and light, with the water depth set 1 cm higher than the movable platform. The pool was divided into four equal quadrants and the water was colored black with non-toxic ink. During the 5-day training period, the rats were placed into the water from the starting point of the maze, and their time, path, and behavior in locating the platform were recorded. After the training period, the platform was removed, and placing the rats in the water maze to observe their swimming time and path in the area where the platform was previously located.

Y-maze test: At 20 days post-surgery, the Y-maze test was conducted to evaluate the spatial and short-term memory abilities of rats from the sham-operated, HIBD, and KNC-treated HIBD groups. The Y-maze apparatus was constructed from black acrylic, with each arm arranged at a 120-degree angle to the others. The maze arms were labeled as the "starting arm," "other arm," and "new arm." In the initial phase of the test, rats were placed at the end of the starting arm and allowed 5 min to explore both the starting and other arms, while access to the new arm was restricted by a plastic barrier. Following this 5-min exploration period, the rats were returned to their home cages, and the Y-maze apparatus was cleaned with 70 % ethanol to remove any scent traces that could influence subsequent tests. One hour later, the rats were reintroduced to the starting arm and given another 5 min to explore all three arms. Throughout the test, the rats' movements were recorded using a video tracking system. The data on the frequency and latency of entries into the new arm were then analyzed statistically.

Golgi staining:After the behavioral tests were completed, the rats were euthanized, and their brains were extracted and sectioned into slices of 5–10 mm thickness. These tissue slices were then immersed in Golgi stain fixing solution (Servicebio, G1069-30 ML, China) in a ventilated environment at room temperature. The slices were subsequently transferred to Golgi staining solution (Servicebio, G1069-1, China) and stored in a cool, ventilated area at 26 °C for 14 days in complete darkness. Then, the tissue was treated with a tissue treatment solution (Servicebio, G1069-3, China) for 1 h, and then placed at 4 °C in the dark for an additional 3 days. The brain tissue was further sliced into 60 μm sections and treated again with the solution. The sections were then rinsed with ultra-pure water, developed in Golgi developer solution (Servicebio, G1069-2, China) for 30 min, and excess water was removed from the slides. The slides were mounted using glycerin gelatin (Servicebio, G1402, China) and imaged using digital scanning under white light (3DHISTECH, Hungary).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Unpaired Student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multiple group comparisons. Data are presented as mean ± SD, with error bars representing SD in all figures. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization and the ROS scavenging property of KNC

The HRTEM image and SAED pattern of KNC were shown in Fig. 1a. The morphology of KNC was irregular, and the size was about 200–500 nm. SAED pattern showed that KNC was an amorphous structure. HAADF image and EDS mapping displayed K, C, N, and O elements evenly distributed in KNC nanoparticles (Fig. 1b). DLS and zeta potential analysis in PBS revealed that KNC possesses a hydrodynamic diameter of 660.18 nm (PDI = 0.325) and a zeta potential of −28.34 ± 2.42 mV, indicating moderate colloidal stability in physiological conditions (Fig. S1a and b). After 7 days of incubation in PBS, the particle size increased only slightly to 734.75 nm with a reduced PDI of 0.264, suggesting good dispersion stability over time (Fig. S1c). Fig. S2a showed the XRD pattern of KNC with a characteristic peak of about 25.8°. Compared with the reported characteristic peak of FeNC, its characteristic peak shifted to the left, indicating that the lattice constant may be reduced because K replaced the position of Fe atoms [30,31]. Fig. S2b showed the Raman spectrum of KNC. The D and G peaks of C atoms appeared at 1348 cm−1 and 1552 cm−1, respectively, with a peak intensity ratio of 1.11, indicating that the C atom crystals had more defects in KNC. Figure S2c and Fig. 1c–e showed the XPS spectra of KNC and the high-resolution spectra of C, N, and K elements. The atoms percent of C, N, O, and K in KNC were 73.89 %, 20.86 %, 4.59 %, and 0.66 %, respectively. The high-resolution XPS spectrum of C1s showed that the C atom had bonds with C, O, and N. To experimentally validate the functional benefits of K+ doping, a comparative study was conducted by using the Ti(SO4)2 colorimetric assay to quantify the CAT-like activity (Fig. 1g). KNC (95.38 ± 0.32 %) significantly outperforms undoped NC (4.25 ± 0.84 %) and other representative nanozymes such as Fe-N-C (83.73 ± 0.21 %), MnO2 (14.95 ± 0.68 %), and Prussian Blue (29.62 ± 0.08 %). Michaelis-Menten kinetic analysis showed that KNC exhibits a Km of 8.10 mM and a Vmax of 1.68 × 10−6 M s−1, indicating a moderate substrate affinity and appreciable catalytic efficiency (Fig. 1h and i). Importantly, one of the most remarkable advantages of KNC over natural enzymes lies in its exceptional stability under harsh conditions (Fig. 1j). To assess environmental robustness, the CAT-like activity of KNC under varying pH and temperature conditions relevant to brain injury was examined (Fig. S3a and b), showing high activity even under acidic or elevated-temperature conditions. In addition, KNC exhibits a SOD-like activity of 75.54 ± 0.68 %, confirming its dual antioxidant enzyme-mimicking properties (Fig. 1k).

Biocompatibility of KNC

To evaluate the biocompatibility of KNC, developmental toxicity was assessed in zebrafish embryos. Embryos exposed to 2, 4, or 8 ng/mL KNC exhibited no morphological abnormalities through 48–72 h (Fig. S4a–b). Moreover, KNC treatment has no adverse effects on survival rates (48 h), hatching efficiency (72 h), and heart rate (72 h) of zebrafish larvae comparable to untreated controls (Fig. S4c–d). These results confirm the favorable biosafety profile of KNC within its therapeutic concentration range.

KNC reduced LPS-induced M1 polarization of microglia

To evaluate the effects of KNC on microglial responses, ROS activity and mitochondrial function were measured and the phenotype of BV2 cells was characterized. The DCFH-DA probe was utilized to measure ROS levels in BV2 cells. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in ROS generation in the LPS-treated groups co-incubated with 2, 4, and 8 ng/mL KNC (p < 0.01, p < 0.001, and p < 0.01), compared to the LPS-induced BV2 cells at 1 day (Fig. 2a–c). Similarly, ROS levels were markedly reduced in BV2 cells treated with 2, 4, and 8 ng/mL KNC (p < 0.01, p < 0.01, and p < 0.0001), compared to the levels observed in LPS-treated BV2 cells at 4 days. Notably, the ROS levels exhibited a concentration-dependent decrease with increasing KNC concentrations at 4 days (Fig. 2b and c). A positive feedback loop exists between the production of ROS and the polarization of microglia to the M1 phenotype. Elevated oxidative stress activates microglia to the M1 phenotype, leading to increased ROS production. Conversely, iNOS expression was significantly reduced in BV2 cells treated with 2, 4, and 8 ng/mL KNC (p < 0.001, p < 0.0001, and p < 0.0001) compared to LPS-treated cells (Fig. 2d and e). However, only the group treated with 8 ng/mL KNC exhibited a significant reduction (p < 0.001) in iNOS expression at 4 days (Fig. 2f and g).

To determine whether KNC modulates microglial polarization, we assessed inflammatory mediators through qPCR, ELISA, and Western blot analyses. In LPS-stimulated BV2 cells, KNC (4 and 8 ng/mL) significantly downregulated mRNA expression of Tnf-α, Il-1β, and Il-6 (Fig. S6a). Consistently, ELISA revealed attenuation of pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion (TNF-α, IL-1β) with concurrent upregulation of anti-inflammatory IL-10 (Fig. S6b). Western blot analysis further confirmed suppression of LPS-induced TNF-α and IL-6 protein expression by KNC at these concentrations (Fig. S6c–d).

Activated microglia promoted astrocyte activation, which further exacerbated neural injury by increasing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and disrupting neuronal function. To evaluate the impact of KNC on microglial secretions and their effect on the phenotypic transformation of astrocytes, primary astrocytes were treated with conditioned media from microglia. The results demonstrated that exposure of microglia to LPS resulted in conditioned media that strongly induced the differentiation of astrocytes into the A1 phenotype, as evidenced by elevated expression of compliment C3 (C3, marker of A1) and GFAP at both 1 day and 4 days. Conversely, when microglia were simultaneously exposed to both LPS and KNC, the conditioned media did not induce the A1 astrocyte phenotype at both 1 day and 4 days (Fig. S7). KNC inhibited neuroinflammation independent of K+ efflux regulation

Given that KNC could release K+, and considering the crucial role of K+ efflux in microglial pro-inflammatory activation, we investigated whether the anti-inflammatory effect of KNC operates via K+ efflux modulation or direct ALOX12 targeting by assessing NLRP3 expression. 4 - AP was used to block voltage - gated potassium (Kv) channels. As shown in Fig. S12a and b, compared with the LPS group, the relative intensity of NLRP3 in the LPS + KNC group was significantly reduced, suggesting that KNC can inhibit LPS - induced NLRP3 expression. When Kv channels were blocked by 4 - AP, the relative intensity of NLRP3 was also decreased compared with the LPS group. Moreover, in the LPS + KNC +4 - AP group, the relative intensity of NLRP3 was similar to that in the LPS +4 - AP and LPS + KNC groups (Fig. S12a and b).

To further explore whether K+ efflux is involved in LPS - induced neuroinflammation, the KCl was used to maintenance of intracellular potassium ion homeostasis. The result shown that low KCl concentrations (50–200 nM, equivalent to ≤14.9 mg/L) failed to suppress LPS-induced NLRP3 activation, whereas 50 mM KCl (3727.5 mg/L) significantly inhibited NLRP3 upregulation (Fig. 9e–f), which was consistent with prior reports [32]. Critically, ICP-MS quantification revealed that the extracellular K+ concentration released upon KNC treatment was merely 16.9 ng/L over the 14-day period. This value is far below the established threshold of 14.9 mg/L, which has been proven ineffective for NLRP3 inhibition (Fig. 9g). These results demonstrate tha KNC's anti-inflammatory action is not mediated through K+ efflux regulation, implicating direct ALOX12 targeting as its primary mechanism. Taken together, KNC plays a critical anti-inflammatory role through the dual mechanism of ROS scavenging and ALOX12 inhibition (Fig. 9h).

Discussions

ROS production post HIBD is the chief culprit of neural damage, which leads to a serious consequence [19]. After HIBD, microglia polarizes to M1 subtype and produces ROS through DAMP. Complex 1 is the main source of ROS due to the high ratio of NADPH/NADP+ [33,34]. Then almost of the superoxide converted to H2O2. H2O2 produced by complex 1 is soluble in both water and lipid, easily penetrates into the unsaturated region of the membrane, leading to lipid peroxidation mediated apoptosis. H2O2 is converted to the strong oxidizing hydroxyl radical (HO•), which can easily snatch electrons from the double bond of the fatty acid chain (RH), forming the fatty acid radical (R•). Then R• reacts with O2 to form ROOH, which is further oxidized to ROO• by ROS due to the close distance of original ROS produced point on complex 1 and the nearest membrane fatty acid chain double bond. This positive loop induces severe ROS boost, which leads to microglia polarization and damage of lipid membrane. Consequently, scavenging ROS producing by H2O2 and inhibiting lipid peroxidation is a potential effective strategy to repair HIBD.

Metal-nitrogen coordination structure (M-Nx) have become a potential catalyst for mimicking enzymes because of their catalytic center structure and biocatalysts activity similar to that of natural metalloproteinases, such as the production of (ROS) catalyzed by peroxidase and oxidase. Based on the theory calculation and experiments verification, metal-nitrogen-carbon nanomaterials (M-Nx-C) are one types of oxidation mimicking enzymes [35], in which M could be Mn [36], Cu [37,38], Co [39], Fe [40,41] and Pt [42], and so on. Among of them, Fe-N-C nano-enzyme exhibits superior catalase-like activity capable, which was verified by both calculation and experiments [41,43]. As the metals has more than one electron in the outer shell, the coordinate bonds form between metal and N-C, which provides more active sites for substrate. Take Fe-N-C as an example: There are two types of active sites, Fe-Nx and N-C, that could strengthen H2O2 catalysis. Trace superoxide anions are generated in the catalytic process and the unstable Fe-NX in Fe-N-C is induced to dissociate into Fe ions. When the free Fe ions are far away from the catalyst, more N-C active sites are activated due to the decrease of Fe-NX active site content in the oxidation process catalyzed by ascorbic acid, which directly leads to the increase of hydrogen peroxide yield [28]. Metal based enzymes with different metallic monatomic centers but similar physicochemical and coordination structures exhibit different catalytic activity, substrate selectivity, kinetics and ROS products. In this work, we found that K doping N-C exhibits superior capable in H2O2 catalysis and ROS scavenging. K could not form coordination bond with N-C due to that there is only one electron in the outer shell. The catalysis effect of KNC may be most ascribed to the active N-C sites.

While traditional small-molecule antioxidants such as edaravone, N-acetylcysteine (NAC), and melatonin have shown some efficacy in mitigating oxidative stress and neuronal injury, they often suffer from rapid clearance, poor blood-brain barrier permeability, and limited ability to scavenge multiple reactive oxygen species (ROS) simultaneously [44,45]. In contrast, our KNC nanozyme offers a unique combination of CAT-like and SOD-like activity, robust performance under acidic and high-ROS conditions, and structural stability, which enables prolonged antioxidant function in the brain microenvironment. For instance, unlike NAC and edaravone that primarily target hydroxyl radicals or specific ROS pathways, KNC is capable of decomposing hydrogen peroxide and neutralizing superoxide radicals with high efficiency (CAT-like activity: 95.38 ± 0.32 %; SOD-like activity: 75.54 ± 0.68 %). Additionally, we benchmarked KNC against other representative nanozymes (e.g., Fe-N-C, MnO2, and Prussian Blue), demonstrating superior or comparable antioxidant capacity.

Emerging evidence has established the critical role of intracellular ion homeostasis, particularly potassium (K+) dynamics, in regulating inflammatory responses and immune activation. Notably, intracellular K+ efflux has been identified as a key trigger for NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and subsequent IL-1β secretion [46,47]. Our transcriptomic analysis demonstrated significant downregulation of NLRP3 expression in KNC-treated HIBD models. Subsequent mechanistic investigations revealed that although KNC can release K+, its anti-inflammatory effect operates independently of K+ efflux modulation. Using the Kv channel blocker 4-AP, we observed that inhibition of K+ efflux did not attenuate the suppressive effect of KNC on NLRP3 expression. Furthermore, experiments modulating extracellular K+ levels confirmed that the concentration of K+ released by KNC (5.73 ng/L) was far below the threshold required for NLRP3 inhibition (14.9 mg/L). These results clearly indicate that KNC inhibits neuroinflammation through a K+ efflux-independent mechanism. These findings strongly suggest that the modulatory effect of KNC on NLRP3-mediated inflammatory pathways is primarily mediated through direct ALOX12 targeting rather than altered K+ homeostasis.

Peroxidation is the other clue of microglia polarization. ROS reacts with phospholipids, membrane receptors, and polyunsaturated fatty acids in biofilms to produce lipid peroxidation products, such as 4-hydroxynonenoic acid (HNE) and HETE (hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid), which are the pro-inflammatory lipid mediators to active M1 microglia [[48], [49], [50], [51], [52], [53]]. In which, ALOX12 is the key enzyme that catalyzes arachidonic acid, one of the phospholipids in membrane, to HETE [54,55]. In this work, it is proved that KNC can inhibit the expression of ALOX12 on the one hand, and KNC can bind to ALOX12 on the other hand. The above two reasons synergically lead to functional inhibition of ALOX12. Beneficial of ROS scavenging and ALOX12 inhibition, microglia tend to polarize to M2 (Fig. 9). The learning and memory abilities of the HIBD mice were naturally restored with KNC treatment.

Conclusions

ROS boost is the most key cause of serious consequence post HIBD. In this work, KNC nanozyme was fabricated and the results show that KNC could effectively scavenge H2O2 and O2·, which obtain its superior ability of ROS scavenging in microglia. As the results of ROS scavenging, microglia was tend to polarized to M2. In vivo experiments showed that KNC could increase regional blood flow, decreased IBA1 positive microglial and GFAP positive area, and prevented dendritic spine loss post HIBD. The learning and memory capabilities were also restored after applying with KNC. The mechanism analysis found that KNC inhibits ROS boost through inhibiting lipid peroxidation. On the one hand, KNC decreased the gene and protein expression of ALOX12 expression; on the other hand, KNC could combine with ALOX12. These two clues obtain KNC with superior ability of inhibition of ROS boost induced brain damage post HIBD. In summary, KNC restored HIBD induced brain damage through scavenging ROS and inhibiting lipid peroxidation, which provided a potential strategies and new idea of HIBD therapy.
