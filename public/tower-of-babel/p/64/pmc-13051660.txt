Ischemic cerebellar infarction due to isolated anterior inferior cerebellar artery dissection mimicking peripheral otological symptoms: illustrative case
Authors: Shin Nemoto, Ririko Takeda, Yutaro Jinushi, Yuya Ogura, Makoto Nakane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDIschemic stroke caused by anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) dissection is rare, and cases limited to cerebellar infarction without brainstem involvement are particularly uncommon. AICA territory ischemia may initially present with audiovestibular symptoms, posing a diagnostic challenge by mimicking peripheral otological disorders.OBSERVATIONSA 32-year-old woman presented with sudden sensorineural hearing loss, vertigo, and peripheral facial nerve palsy. A peripheral otological disorder was initially suspected; however, careful neurological examination revealed marked unsteadiness and torsional nystagmus, prompting brain MRI. Imaging demonstrated a focal cerebellar infarction without brainstem involvement. Follow-up CT angiography and digital subtraction angiography identified an isolated dissection of the proximal segment of the AICA. Despite improvement in facial nerve palsy and gait disturbance, the sensorineural hearing loss persisted.LESSONSPeripheral otological symptoms do not exclude central vascular disease. Isolated AICA dissection may present without brainstem infarction and be occult on initial MRA; serial vascular imaging is mandatory when neurological findings are atypical. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE2695

Sudden sensorineural hearing loss and vertigo are common clinical symptoms typically encountered in emergency or otolaryngology settings. While anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) territory infarction typically presents with classic brainstem and cerebellar signs as originally described by Adams,1 isolated audiovestibular loss can occur as the sole clinical manifestation.2 Sensorineural hearing loss has been reported in up to 92% of patients with AICA territory infarction, primarily due to the high metabolic demand and lack of collateral supply in the internal auditory artery territory.3 Such presentations frequently mimic peripheral labyrinthine disorders like Ramsay Hunt syndrome, creating a significant diagnostic pitfall.4 Although the underlying mechanisms for AICA stroke are most commonly atherosclerotic or embolic, spontaneous isolated dissection of the AICA is an exceptionally rare etiology.5 We present a case of isolated AICA dissection in a 32-year-old woman whose symptoms were initially misidentified as a peripheral otological disorder, providing a clinical lesson on the value of meticulous neurological evaluation and follow-up imaging.

Illustrative Case

A 32-year-old woman with a medical history notable only for childhood asthma and no regular medications presented for medical attention.

On the evening of symptom onset, she developed sudden right-sided hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo, and unsteadiness, and visited the emergency department later that night. Noncontrast head CT showed no apparent abnormalities, and she was discharged home.

On the following day, she consulted a local otolaryngology clinic, where right-sided sensorineural hearing loss and right peripheral facial nerve palsy were identified. She was referred to our otolaryngology department with a suspected diagnosis of sudden sensorineural hearing loss or Ramsay Hunt syndrome.

At admission, pure-tone audiometry demonstrated severe sensorineural hearing loss in the right ear, with a mean hearing level of 63.3 dB calculated using the four-frequency method (500 Hz, 1 kHz, 2 kHz, and 4 kHz), while hearing in the left ear was within the normal range (18.4 dB). No vesicular lesions were observed on the auricle, but the patient reported pain behind the right ear. Virological testing was negative.

Based on these findings, a peripheral otological disorder was considered as the initial diagnosis, and high-dose steroid therapy (methylprednisolone 200 mg/day) was initiated after admission. Neurological examination revealed mild right peripheral facial nerve palsy, but no obvious brainstem symptoms, such as dysarthria, dysphagia, or tongue deviation, were observed. However, because marked unsteadiness and prominent torsional nystagmus were noted on examination, a CNS lesion was suspected, and brain MRI was performed immediately.

MRI revealed a focal area of hyperintensity on diffusion-weighted imaging with corresponding low apparent diffusion coefficient values in the posterior superior aspect of the right cerebellar hemisphere, consistent with cerebellar infarction (Fig. 1A and B). No abnormal signal changes were observed in the pons or middle cerebellar peduncle, and no brainstem lesions were detected on fluid-attenuated inversion recovery or T2*-weighted images. MR angiography (MRA) did not demonstrate any definite vascular abnormalities, and no findings suggestive of arterial dissection were identified at that time (Fig. 1C). Because the initial MRA was negative, our clinical reasoning initially prioritized the investigation of other embolic sources, such as cardioembolism, and we proceeded with noninvasive evaluations in a stepwise manner. Antithrombotic therapy with argatroban, aspirin, and a statin was initiated. The patient received dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) for the 1st month, which was subsequently transitioned to single antiplatelet therapy for long-term maintenance. Three days after symptom onset, 3D CT angiography (CTA) revealed dissecting changes with a pearl-and-string appearance in the proximal segment of the right AICA (Fig. 2A). The dissected segment was located proximal to the porus acusticus internus. Subsequent digital subtraction angiography (DSA) confirmed the same findings (Fig. 2B and C). No dissection was observed in the vertebral or basilar arteries, and a diagnosis of ischemic cerebellar infarction due to isolated AICA dissection was established. During hospitalization, the patient’s facial nerve palsy and gait unsteadiness gradually improved, and she was discharged home ambulatory on hospital day 13 with a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 1. Follow-up MRA performed prior to discharge demonstrated persistent irregularity of the proximal right AICA (Fig. 3A and B). At the 12-month follow-up, MRA showed marked improvement of the previously observed irregularity with normalization of the arterial contour, suggesting spontaneous healing of the dissected lumen (Fig. 3C and D). This atypical presentation can be explained by the branching pattern of the AICA and the anatomical characteristics of the dissection site. In this patient, the dissection was located proximal to the porus acusticus internus, a location that likely caused selective hypoperfusion of the labyrinthine artery territory.6 Because the labyrinthine artery is an end artery lacking collateral circulation, ischemia of the inner ear may occur before the development of brainstem infarction.6 The irreversible severe sensorineural hearing loss observed in this case supports this hemodynamic mechanism3 and is consistent with previous histopathological findings demonstrating loss of cochlear sensory epithelium following AICA occlusion.7

The peripheral facial nerve palsy observed in this patient may also be explained by ischemia of the microcirculation within the facial nerve canal, namely the vasa nervorum.6 Preservation of the pontine parenchyma, including the facial nerve nucleus, further supports a peripheral rather than central mechanism for the facial nerve palsy.8,9

Notably, no vascular abnormality was identified on MRA at symptom onset, and the dissection was detected only on subsequent CTA and DSA performed several days later. Given the small caliber of the AICA, isolated dissections may be difficult to detect on initial noninvasive imaging, particularly with 1.5T MRA, as the lesion may fall below its spatial resolution limits. Follow-up MRA performed 12 months after discharge demonstrated improvement of the previously observed vascular irregularity with normalization of the arterial contour, strongly supporting arterial dissection as the underlying pathology in this case.

Lessons

This case highlights a critical diagnostic pitfall: sudden hearing loss and facial palsy can represent the initial presentation of isolated AICA dissection even in the absence of brainstem infarction. Because the AICA is a small-caliber artery and early dissection changes may be below the spatial resolution of routine MRA, initial noninvasive vascular imaging can yield false-negative results.

Careful neurological examination for subtle central signs, such as truncal ataxia or torsional nystagmus, is therefore essential. When such atypical findings are present, repeated vascular imaging should be performed to avoid misdiagnosis as a purely peripheral otological disorder.
