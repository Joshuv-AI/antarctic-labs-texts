Calcified liver metastases from medullary thyroid carcinoma in a Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 2A patient
Authors: Chun Lang Tang, Fung Him Ng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Calcified liver lesions encompass a broad differential diagnosis, with calcified hepatic granulomas being the most frequently encountered. We report a rare case of calcified hepatic metastases that were initially misinterpreted as hepatic granulomas.A 51-year-old patient with known Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 2A had a history of medullary thyroid carcinoma treated surgically with total thyroidectomy and bilateral neck dissection in 2005. Serial CT scans obtained between 2010 to 2025 showed a few progressively enlarging calcified hepatic lesions that were initially misinterpreted as calcified hepatic granulomas. Due to persistently elevated tumor markers, a 18F-DOPA PET/CT was performed, which demonstrated increased radiotracer uptake of these lesions, suggesting they are metabolically active hepatic metastasis. MRI with Gadoxetate disodium also showed finding suggestive of hypervascular hepatic metastases. Diagnostic laparoscopy and histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of calcified hepatic metastasis from medullary thyroid carcinoma.This case emphasizes that hepatic calcifications, though often presumed benign, may occasionally represent other underlying pathologies such as metastatic disease. We hope to describe the imaging features of calcified hepatic metastases from medullary thyroid carcinoma, and the differentials of calcified hepatic lesions.

Introduction

The liver is one of the most common sites of metastases [1]. The most common primary tumors to metastasize to the liver include colorectum, pancreas, stomach, breast, and lung [2]. It is rare for thyroid cancer to metastasize to the liver, with a reported frequency of about 0.5% [3].

Medullary thyroid carcinoma accounts for 1% to 2% of thyroid cancers in the United States [4]. It arises from parafollicular C cells of the thyroid, which come from the neural crest. Hence, it is essentially a neuroendocrine tumor [5]. Germline mutations that activate the RET (Rearranged during Transfection) gene are reported in 88-95% of hereditary MTC, and 40-50% of sporadic MTCs [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]].

Medullary Thyroid Carcinoma are sporadic in 75% of cases and familial in 25% of cases (eg, from MEN2A or MEN2B syndromes, or familial MTC (FMTC). At the time of diagnosis of medullary thyroid carcinoma, 13% of patients already have distant metastases. The most common sites of metastases include the liver, lung, mediastinum, and bone [5]. These cases usually show elevated calcitonin levels above 150 pg/mL or 43.8 pmol/L [11,12].

We demonstrate a case of typical imaging features of calcified hepatic metastases arising from medullary thyroid carcinoma. Medullary thyroid carcinoma liver metastases can be calcified and hypervascular with uptake on 18F-DOPA PET/CT, which is demonstrated in our case [4].

Case presentation

A 51-year-old male patient with known Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 2A (MEN2A) underwent total thyroidectomy and modified radical neck dissection for medullary thyroid carcinoma in 2005. He also underwent left adrenalectomy for left adrenal pheochromocytoma in 2004, and right adrenalectomy for right pheochromocytoma in 2017.

However, follow-up all along shows persistently elevated calcitonin up to 3347 pmol/L. Neck CT and DMSA study all along shows no local recurrence. Up to 2025, no source of recurrence or cause of elevated tumor marker was identified.

It was only after prolonged negative workup for persistent elevated CEA and rising calcitonin trend that an 18F-DOPA PET/CT was performed in 2025, which suggests hypermetabolic calcified hepatic metastases from medullary thyroid carcinoma.

MRI with hepatobiliary specific agent (Gadoxetate disodium) also suggests hypervascular hepatic metastases from medullary thyroid carcinoma. This was confirmed by diagnostic laparoscopy and frozen section.

Retrospective review of CT images shows hepatic calcifications which show progression in size and number. These were calcified hepatic metastases but were misdiagnosed as calcified benign granulomas since 2010.

Management

The patient subsequently underwent diagnostic laparoscopy in 2025, which shows multiple nodules in both lobes of the liver. The largest ones at segment 7 contain calcification. Follow up

The patient is currently undergoing follow up in oncology in Hong Kong and considering systemic targeted therapy with a RET inhibitor.

Discussion

There are no large studies published regarding the nature of medullary thyroid carcinoma liver metastases [5]. However, they are generally hypervascular, similar to other neuroendocrine tumors.

On CT, they typically appear hypoattenuating on non-enhanced images and demonstrate early arterial phase enhancement with washout in the portal venous phase [2,5,13]. On MRI, they are hypointense on T1-weighted and hyperintense on T2-weighted sequences, with a similar dynamic contrast enhancement pattern as CT [2,5,13]. On delayed phase using extra-cellular contrast agents in MRI, 25% of patients with liver metastasis from neuroendocrine tumors (including medullary thyroid carcinoma) may show peripheral washout sign [4]. It is defined as an enhancing liver lesion with peripheral rim of hypoenhancement relative to its center and surrounding liver parenchyma.

Additionally, some metastatic liver lesions from medullary thyroid carcinoma can appear small and discrete (as in our case) [5]. The metastatic liver lesions may calcify because medullary thyroid carcinoma arises from parafollicular C cells that produce calcitonin and are prone to dystrophic calcification [5]. Calcifications can also become more apparent or develop after treatment, and the liver may show post-treatment pseudocirrhosis-like changes [2,14].

The treatment for medullary thyroid carcinoma liver metastases depends on its extent. Surgery or local therapy for example radiofrequency ablation can be performed for surgically resectable liver lesions. However, medullary thyroid carcinoma liver metastases are often multifocal, making this impossible [5]. Chemoembolization is another therapy that can be useful if the main metastatic disease load is in the liver [15]. Systemic therapies such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors is also an option for patients with unresectable locoregional diseases, those with distant metastases and those with symptomatic or progressive disease as defined by the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) [5,16].

Differential diagnosis

While liver calcification is frequently dismissed as a benign finding, the differential diagnosis for calcified hepatic lesions is broad and requires distinguishing between infectious, benign, and malignant etiologies:•Infection: Healed granulomas (from prior TB, histoplasmosis or sarcoidosis) represent the most common cause, typically presenting as small, discrete calcification involving the entire lesion. Alternatively, hydatid cysts may display curvilinear, ringlike, or dense calcification. Fascioliasis are often subcapsular in location and may be associated with nodular hepatic parenchymal calcifications [17].•Benign hepatic tumors: Hepatic cysts can uncommonly present with wall calcifications. 10-20% of hemangiomas contain calcifications, typically in areas of central fibrosis. Hepatocellular adenomas rarely show eccentric, dystrophic calcifications [17].•Malignant primary tumors: Fibrolamellar hepatocellular carcinomas can show calcifications within a central stellate scar. Other primary malignancies that may manifest with calcifications include standard hepatocellular carcinoma, hemangioendothelioma and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [17].•Calcified hepatic metastases: Metastases from medullary thyroid carcinoma can present as small, discrete calcifications as described above. Other calcifying metastases include primary mucin-secreting GI tract or ovarian tumors, as well as metastatic osteosarcoma and chondrosarcoma; these can present with amorphous, stippled, punctate, or granular calcifications [17].

Conclusion

It is often tempting to dismiss liver calcification as benign calcified granulomas. However, the list of calcified hepatic lesions is long. Radiologists should recognize the wide range of differential diagnoses for calcified hepatic lesions and maintain a high index of suspicion for calcified metastases, particularly in patients with relevant clinical and oncologic history.
