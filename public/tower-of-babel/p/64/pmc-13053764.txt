A machine learning-based model to predict multi-time-point prognosis for acute-on-chronic hepatitis B liver failure
Authors: Jieyang Jin, Zhong Liu, Mei Liao, Ting Zhang, Jingming Liu, Yudi Luo, Kai Yang, Minhua Lu, Jie Zeng, Xin Chen, Jie Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur prognostic model and mobile application enable multi-time-point prognostic evaluation for patients with acute-on-chronic hepatitis B liver failure, thereby improving patient care and facilitating clinical decision-making for liver transplantation.Subject areas: health sciences, medicine, medical specialty, health informatics, internal medicine, hepatology

Introduction

Acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) presents as a sudden and severe illness characterized by high mortality rates.1,2,3 For patients in critical condition, liver transplantation remains the only effective treatment option. However, several factors—including organ availability, illness severity, and social challenges—can hinder access to transplantation for many patients. Therefore, it’s essential to allocate liver resources judiciously on basis of the patient’s condition.3 If a patient is too ill and spontaneous recovery seems unlikely, the decision to proceed with liver transplantation must be made promptly, as optimal treatment window is a mere 7 days.3,4,5,6,7

Accurate prognosis prediction for ACLF is crucial for guiding decisions regarding liver transplantation and improving survival rates. Specifically, short-term (30-day) and mid-term (90-day) prognoses are vital for clinicians to determine urgency of transplantation.8 For severely ill patients, early transplantation can significantly lower mortality rates.9 Once a patient stabilizes, clinicians should monitor for potential relapses or complications post-discharge, as these factors can also influence long-term survival.1,10 Consequently, understanding long-term (1-year) prognosis is important for tailoring follow-up strategies, with frequent check-ups potentially increasing survival rates for high-risk patients.11,12,13,14,15

Currently, various clinical scores exist to evaluate prognosis of ACLF, but most of these focus solely on short- or mid-term outcomes and are primarily applicable to inpatients.16,17,18,19 Few models, such as CLIF-C ACLF score,19 provide multi-time point predictions; however, data required for these scores can be challenging to obtain for outpatients. This situation highlights the urgent need for a more accessible prognostic model with fewer, easier-to-collect indicators, especially those tailored for those who have been discharged. Moreover, most existing prognostic methods are designed for doctors, leaving a gap in resources for patients and their families to understand potential clinical outcomes. Effective communication between doctors and ACLF patients/families is also important. An online prognostic tool could address these challenges, facilitating better follow-up during and after hospitalization. However, there are no reports of such tool.

Ultrasound elastography has emerged as a widely used, quantitative technology that is relatively simple to master. For sonographers or radiologists with experience in abdominal ultrasound, only 50–100 elastography examinations are needed to become proficient.20 This technique plays a pivotal role in monitoring liver disease over time. Clinical and radiological guidelines recognize the value of elastography in diagnosing and ruling out liver fibrosis and cirrhosis.20,21,22,23,24,25,26 Our previous studies demonstrated that two-dimensional shear wave elastography (2D-SWE) holds significant promise in assessing outcomes for ACLF, reinforcing its potential as a valuable tool in clinical practice.27,28

The purpose of this study was to establish a prognostic model based on elastography and serological parameters through machine learning to predict prognosis in ACLF patients at multiple time points within 1 year. Furthermore, mobile applications are more practical than complex prognostic models, especially for outpatients. Therefore, we attempted to design a mobile application with a prognostic model for ACLF, regardless of whether they are hospitalized. Patients/families only need to input examination indicators, and physicians can determine the patients’ condition in real time and accurately predict occurrence of serious adverse outcomes.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 590 ACLF patients met inclusion criteria, and 564 were eventually enrolled (418 in training cohort, 71 in validation cohort, and 75 in testing cohort) (Figure 1). Among all the ACLF patients, 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year transplant-free survival rates were 74.5% (420/564), 57.6% (325/564), and 51.6% (291/564), respectively (Figure S1). There were 99 (23.7%), 177 (42.3%), and 206 (49.3%) ACLF patients in training cohort who deteriorated within 30 days, 90 days, and 1 year, respectively. There were 24 (33.8%), 33 (46.5%), and 36 (50.7%) patients in validation cohort, as well as 21 (28.0%), 29 (38.7%), and 31 (41.3%) patients in testing cohort. Correlation analysis and feature selection

By analyzing correlation between serological parameters and outcomes of training cohort, we found that total bilirubin (TB), prothrombin time (PT), prothrombin activity (PTA), and international normalized ratio (INR) correlated well with all of 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year outcomes, with correlation coefficients greater than 0.2 (p < 0.01; Figures 3A–3C). In addition, Na level was individually correlated with 30-day outcome, with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.2 (p < 0.01, Figure 3A), whereas correlation between Na level and survival decreased with increasing prognostic time. In contrast, platelet count (PLT) count was individually correlated with 1-year outcome, with a correlation coefficient value greater than 0.2 (p < 0.01, Figure 3C), and correlation between PLT count and survival increased with increasing prognostic time. On the basis of the above analysis, TB, PT, PTA, INR plus Na and PLT were chosen as the candidate features for model construction. Since redundancy may exist between the candidate features, an interparameter correlation analysis29 was performed to remove features with relatively larger correlations. Through the latter, we found that PT was positively correlated with INR, whereas PTA was negatively correlated with INR, both with an absolute correlation coefficient of approximately 1 (Figure 3D). Thus, PT and PTA were removed from the candidate feature set, leaving TB, INR, Na, and PLT count as the final serological parameters. Parallel to the above analysis, we also investigated correlations of 2D-SWE with survival outcomes as well as correlations of 2D-SWE with serological parameters and found that 2D-SWE was highly correlated with all of 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year outcomes, with correlation coefficients greater than 0.2 (p < 0.01; Figures 3A–3C), whereas no serological parameters were correlated with 2D-SWE, with correlation coefficients greater than 0.4, suggesting good complementation between 2D-SWE and serological parameters. On basis of the above analysis, TB, INR, Na, PLT, and 2D-SWE were ultimately chosen as features for machine learning model development. For the final selected features, 2D-SWE exhibited correlations with TB, INR, Na, and PLT, with Spearman correlation coefficients of 0.31, 0.20, −0.23, and −0.17, respectively (all |r| < 0.4); TB was correlated with INR, Na, and PLT, with correlation coefficients of 0.30, −0.10, and −0.15, respectively (all |r| < 0.4); INR showed negative correlations with Na and PLT, with correlation coefficients of −0.12 and −0.24, respectively (both |r| < 0.3); The correlation between Na and PLT was weak (r = 0.08). Collectively, these results indicate that the selected features exhibit good independence, with no strong pairwise correlations (i.e., |r| < 0.4).

Establishment and validation of the prognostic model

On the basis of the meaningful indicators mentioned above, we established a prognostic model, machine learning model with data integration of serum parameters and 2D shear wave elastography (Serum-SWE-ML), through machine learning with random forest classifiers. In predicting 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year outcomes in training cohort, Serum-SWE-ML achieved area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC) values of 0.859 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.822–0.891), 0.894 (0.861–0.922), and 0.918 (0.888–0.943), respectively, which were significantly greater than those obtained by model for end-stage liver disease (MELD), model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) combined with sodium (Na), (MELD-Na), or model for end-stage liver disease combined with shear wave elastography (MELD-SWE) (AUC ranges: 0.760–0.786, 0.789–0.821, and 0.788–0.824, respectively, for 30-day, 90-day and 1-year outcome predictions; p < 0.001). For predictions in validation cohort, Serum-SWE-ML achieved AUC values of 0.924 (0.843–0.977) [30-day], 0.872 (0.773–0.940) [90-day], and 0.878 (0.773–0.940) [1-year], which were significantly greater than those of other models (AUC ranges: 0.844–0.873, 0.798–0.826, and 0.798–0.835 for 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year survival predictions, respectively; p < 0.05). In the testing cohort, Serum-SWE-ML achieved AUC values of 0.870 (0.768–0.934) [30-day], 0.879 (0.784–0.944) [90-day], and 0.896 (0.801–0.953) [1-year], which were also significantly better than those obtained by MELD, MELD-Na, or MELD-SWE (AUC ranges: 0.806–0.858, 0.828–0.864, and 0.804–0.854, respectively, for 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year outcome predictions; p < 0.05). Development of the mobile application

In daily clinical work, it is difficult for physicians to perform complex model calculations. If this calculation step can be simplified, it will increase convenience for physicians. To avoid complex calculations, we designed a mobile application (http://uat25.helpfooter.com/#/) (Figure 5A) in which physicians need only to input prognostic indicators (TB, Na, PLT, INR, and 2D-SWE) to obtain 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year clinical outcomes of patients. The cutoff values of Serum-SWE-ML for predicting the 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year outcome of ACLF were 22.7%, 44.2%, and 45.6%, respectively (see Section 8 of the supplemental information for the assessment of the cutoff values). If baseline Serum-SWE-ML value was less than or equal to 22.7%, 44.2% or 45.6%, the patients were predicted to recover within 30 days, 90 days or 1 year, and were considered low-risk group. If Serum-SWE-ML value was greater than 22.7%, 44.2% or 45.6%, the patients’ condition was expected to deteriorate at corresponding time point, and they were considered high-risk group. We applied the mobile application to testing cohort, and accuracies of 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year prognoses were 0.870 (0.768–0.934), 0.879 (0.784–0.944), and 0.896 (0.801–0.953), respectively (Figure 5B).

Model calibration and net benefits

Figure 6 shows observed and predicted probabilities of deterioration at 30 days, 90 days, and 1 year in terms of quartiles of Serum-SWE-ML predictions in training, validation, and testing cohorts. Predicted and observed probabilities of deterioration were similar across all the quartiles of Serum-SWE-ML predictions at multiple time points in validation cohort (30 days: overall predicted 0.293 vs. overall observed 0.338, Hosmer-Lemeshow χ2 = 12.6, p = 0.125; 90 days: 0.500 vs. 0.465, χ2 = 7.3, p = 0.499; 1 year: 0.571 vs. 0.507, χ2 = 6.2, p = 0.620), as well as in testing cohort (30 days: overall predicted 0. 311 vs. overall observed 0. 280, Hosmer-Lemeshow χ2 = 12.1, p = 0.097; 90-day: 0. 450 vs. 0. 387, χ2 = 11.7, p = 0.109; 1-year: 0. 503 vs. 0. 413, χ2 = 11.1, p = 0.136). Predicted and observed probabilities were also compared in training cohort, with similar findings: observed and estimated probabilities of deterioration were comparable at most of the study time points, including 90-day (overall predicted 0.426 vs. overall observed 0.423, Hosmer-Lemeshow χ2 = 11.7, p = 0.164), and 1- year (0.495 vs. 0.493, χ2 = 11.8, and p = 0.162), except for 30 days (0.241 vs. 0.237, Hosmer-Lemeshow χ2 = 16.5, p = 0.035). These results demonstrated stable ability of Serum-SWE-ML in predicting prognosis under different stages of deterioration risk. To further evaluate the calibration performance of the prognostic model, we generated the locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOESS)-smoothed calibration curves of the model in predicting 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year outcome across various cohorts (Figure 7), and reported the optimism-corrected calibration metrics with 95% CI (Table 4). On the validation cohort, Serum-SWE-ML achieved Brier scores of 0.140 (95% CI: 0.096, 0.181), 0.145 (0.105, 0.188), and 0.148 (0.107, 0.194) in predicting 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year outcome, respectively, while the corresponding calibration slopes were 1.569 (1.181, 2.136), 1.170 (0.900, 1.352), and 1.053 (0.830, 1.273). On the testing cohort, Serum-SWE-ML achieved Brier scores of 0.143 (0.095, 0.194), 0.141 (0.105, 0.179), and 0.138 (0.100, 0.185) in predicting 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year outcome, respectively, while the corresponding calibration slopes were 1.720 (1.261, 2.131), 1.190 (0.956, 1.428), and 1.163 (0.884, 1.378). The calibration curves and slope statistics suggested that the model’s overall calibration performance was relatively better in predicting the 90-day and 1-year outcome compared to that in the 30-day, highlighting the greater challenge associated with short-term (30-day) outcome prediction. This challenge was further evidenced by the net benefit curves (Figure 7, bottom row), where the net benefit in 30-day outcome prediction was relatively worse than that in 90-day and 1-year outcome predictions. Despite this challenge, Serum-SWE-ML demonstrated promising clinical utility across a broad range of decision thresholds in each outcome prediction.

Model interpretation

Figure 8 (top row) depicts the mean absolute Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) values—along with their corresponding confidence intervals—for the Serum-SWE-ML model in predicting positive prediction (deterioration) of various outcomes (30-day, 90-day, and 1-year) on the entire cohort. For 30-day outcome prediction, the feature importance ranking (from highest to lowest) was: INR, shear wave elphyastogra (SWE), Na, TB, and PLT. In contrast, the rankings for 90-day and 1-year outcome predictions were consistent: INR, TB, SWE, PLT, and Na. Notably, INR emerged as the most influential feature across all the three outcome predictions. From 30-day to 1-year outcome predictions, the relative importance of INR and PLT showed a gradual increase. SWE and TB exhibited higher relative importance for 90-day and 1-year predictions compared to that of the 30-day. Conversely, Na was less weighted in the 90-day and 1-year predictions than in the 30-day prediction. Detailed quantitative data on feature importance (including exact mean absolute SHAP values and confidence interval ranges) are summarized in Table 5. SHAP beeswarm plots illustrating the detailed distribution of SHAP values for each feature are presented in the bottom row of Figure 8. Overall, INR, SWE, and TB were positively correlated with positive outcome predictions (30-day, 90-day, and 1-year), whereas Na and PLT exhibited a negative correlation with these positive predictions. It could be noted higher values of INR, SWE, and TB were associated with an increased likelihood of positive predictions—this trend was particularly pronounced for INR and SWE. These insights derived from the SHAP value distribution analysis align well with the patterns observed in the partial dependence plots presented in Figure 9. For local interpretation, Figure 10 depicts the waterfall plots of Serum-SWE-ML in predicting the outcomes of an ACLF patient with Na = 141.8 mmol/L, INR = 2.46, TB = 419.73 μmol/L, SWE = 47.5 kPa, and PLT = 62 × 109/L (follow-up endpoint: 79 days). In predicting the 30-day outcome (Figure 10A), the baseline mean probability of disease deterioration across the entire training dataset was 0.245; PLT exerted no modulatory effect on this baseline probability; SWE then increased the baseline probability by approximately 0.01, followed by a 0.01 increase induced by TB. Afterward, INR reduced the updated probability by around 0.03, and Na ultimately decreased the newly adjusted probability by approximately 0.05, resulting in a final predicted probability of 0.193. This value was below the 30-day prediction cutoff of 0.227, indicating a negative prediction for 30-day deterioration of the patient. The model’s 90-day and 1-year outcome predictions (Figures 10B and 10C) could be interpreted in the same manner as of 30-day prediction. Such individual-level model interpretation may assist clinicians in comprehending and trusting the prognostic outputs of the model.

Performances in patient subgroups

To evaluate the performances of Serum-SWE-ML across patient subgroups, we first merged the validation and testing cohorts into a single combined cohort—this expansion of sample size ensured more reliable subgroup-level analysis. We then stratified this merged cohort into paired subgroups based on eight factors as follows: (1) age (≤45 vs. >45 years); (2) gender (male vs. female); (3) TB; ≤400 vs. >400 μmol/L); (4) serum sodium (Na; ≤135 vs. >135 mmol/L); (5) ultrasound device (SuperSonic Imagine Aixplorer vs. Canon Aplio i900); (6) sonographer (operator 1 vs. operator 2); (7) scanning time period (stratified independently for each device: time period 1 vs. time period 2); and (8) liver transplantation (transplantation included vs. transplantation excluded). We systematically assessed the prognostic performance of Serum-SWE-ML in each subgroup, with simultaneous comparisons to three conventional prognostic models (MELD, MELD-Na, and MELD-SWE). For 30-day outcome prediction, Serum-SWE-ML demonstrated relatively consistent performances across (Na) (AUC: 0.891 vs. 0.898), scanning time with the SuperSonic Imagine Aixplorer (AUC: 0.931 vs. 0.932), scanning time with the Canon Aplio i900 (AUC: 0.908 vs. 0.927), and liver transplantation (AUC: 0.896 vs. 0.894). In contrast, large performance variations were observed across age (AUC: 0.946 vs. 0.846), gender (AUC: 0.882 vs. 0.952), (TB) (AUC: 0.962 vs. 0.863), ultrasound device type (AUC: 0.924 vs. 0.870), and sonographer (AUC: 0.923 vs. 0.852). For 90-day and 1-year outcome predictions, Serum-SWE-ML maintained relatively consistent performances across most subgroups, including age (AUC: 0.943 vs. 0.816; 0.954 vs. 0.833), gender (AUC: 0.871 vs. 0.854; 0.884 vs. 0.848), TB (AUC: 0.886 vs. 0.848; 0.866 vs. 0.879), Na (AUC: 0.872 vs. 0.864; 0.868 vs. 0.872), ultrasound device type (AUC: 0.872 vs. 0.879; 0.878 vs. 0.896), sonographer (AUC: 0.875 vs. 0.875; 0.888 vs. 0.896), and liver transplantation (AUC: 0.874 vs. 0.850; 0.885 vs. 0.867), while large performance variations were only observed across age (AUC: 0.943 vs. 0.816; 0.954 vs. 0.833), scanning time with the SuperSonic Imagine Aixplorer (AUC: 0.816 vs. 0.924; 0.854 vs. 0.909), and scanning time with the Canon Aplio i900 (AUC: 0.923 vs. 0.837; 0.900 vs. 0.881). Collectively, Serum-SWE-ML showed better performance consistency in predicting 90-day and 1-year outcomes than that of 30-day. The relatively large performance variation for 30-day outcome prediction is likely due to the class imbalance in training of the 30-day outcome prediction model. Notably, although there were fluctuations for the performances of Serum-SWE-ML between paired subgroups, the model demonstrated superior prognostic performances relative to conventional models across most stratifications, suggesting its potential as a valuable alternative in clinical practice. Due to space constraints, more detailed subgroup-specific results are presented in the supplemental information (Section 10).

Discussion

Prognosis of ACLF has become a focal point in global medical research. In this study, advanced machine learning techniques were employed to develop a prognostic model that incorporates various meaningful indicators, including 2D-SWE. This model assesses the clinical outcomes of ACLF at multiple time points: 30 days (short-term), 90 days (medium-term), and 1 year (long-term). A mobile application was also created to facilitate real-time, rapid, and accurate prognostic assessments for patients.

Indicators used in this innovative model include both serological markers and 2D-SWE. At present, clinical models are mainly used in clinical practice to evaluate prognosis of ACLF16,17,18; most of these models include TB and INR, which are also included in our model. Previous studies have shown that presence and number of complications are related to the mortality of ACLF.8,19 Results of our study also supported this conclusion. Additionally, our study supported that liver and coagulation complications are closely linked to ACLF prognosis. Our findings revealed that Na level was highly correlated with 30-day outcome, whereas correlation of Na level with survival decreased with increasing prognostic time. In contrast, PLT count was highly correlated with 1-year outcome, whereas correlation of PLT count with survival increased with increasing prognostic time. Notably, 2D-SWE provides real-time assessments of liver stiffness, offering biomechanical insights that complement functional data from serological tests. Our earlier studies established that among various ultrasound (US) parameters, 2D-SWE is particularly relevant for predicting ACLF outcomes,27 and this study further reinforces the value of 2D-SWE by including it in the final prognostic model. This investigation represents the first effort to create machine learning models that combine 2D-SWE and serological parameters for prediction of multi-time-point prognoses in ACLF.

By evaluating prognosis of ACLF at various time intervals, this study highlights the importance of addressing short- and medium-term outcomes. Early consideration for liver transplantation is crucial for patients with severe conditions and high mortality risk.1 For those on the waiting list, timely decisions regarding liver support therapies can maximize therapeutic benefits.3 Attention to medium- and long-term outcomes is also essential, as studies indicate that approximately 30% of patients may remain in ACLF status.3 During this period, it is vital for patients to actively manage their primary liver disease, reverse early stage cirrhosis, treat ACLF-related complications, and prevent recurrence. In this case, clinicians should focus on two specific patient groups. First, ACLF patients hospitalized with high mortality risk, particularly those patients with poor short-to mid-term prognoses, should be included. Individualized treatment plans are necessary for these patients, especially if they are in critical condition. Timely evaluation and potential transplantation can increase survival rates. Second group includes discharged high-risk patients, who may exhibit poor mid-to long-term prognoses. Continuous follow-up and close monitoring are essential for these individuals. Research indicates that incidence of post-transplant complications is particularly high for patients with severe conditions, necessitating ongoing attention to their recovery.11,15,30 While ensuring survival, clinicians must also consider the quality of life of these patients.

The clinical significance of this study lies in development of a prognostic model, Serum-SWE-ML. By integrating clinically relevant indicators through machine learning, Serum-SWE-ML demonstrated promising discriminative performances, with the following AUC values in predicting 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year outcomes across different cohorts: 0.859 (0.822–0.891), 0.894 (0.861–0.922), and 0.918 (0.888–0.943) in the training cohort; 0.924 (0.843–0.977), 0.872 (0.773–0.940), and 0.878 (0.773–0.940) in the validation cohort; and 0.870 (0.768–0.934), 0.879 (0.784–0.944), and 0.896 (0.801–0.953) in the testing cohort, respectively. Notably, the 30-day AUC of the model in the training cohort was lower than that in the validation and testing cohorts. This phenomenon can be attributed to several data-driven and methodological factors that are consistent with prognostic modeling practices as follows (1) Random variation in cohort characteristics: the validation cohort, while independent, may have exhibited subtle differences in baseline feature distributions that align better with the 30-day outcome prediction pattern captured by the model. This random variation is inherent in clinical datasets, especially with moderate sample sizes. (2) Mitigation of overfitting in cross-validation: during model training (via 5-fold cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning), we controlled for overfitting (e.g., grid search with cross-validation and RF’s inherent bagging property). This ensured the model generalized well to unseen data. (3) Lower event rate in the training set for 30-day outcomes: the 30-day event rate was slightly lower in the training set compared to the validation set, which could marginally reduce the training AUC (as binary models are more sensitive to event rate imbalance in the training data). The Youden index derived optimal cutoff (used in both cohorts) alleviated this impact but did not fully eliminate the subtle difference in performance.

In the present study, we built three independent binary classifiers for predicting the 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year outcomes, respectively. Instead of binary classifiers, time-to-event algorithms, such as the Cox proportional hazards model (CoxPHM),31 can be used to perform prognostic predictions across multiple time points. In this respect, we constructed two CoxPHM models: one incorporating the selected features (TB, Na, PLT, INR, and SWE), designated CoxPHM-SF, and another including all features (patient baseline characteristics presented in Table 1, excluding MELD, MELD-Na, and MELD-SWE), designated CoxPHM-AF. We then compared the performances of these two models along with that of Serum-SWE-ML, in terms of various quantitative metrics including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, AUC, and mean time-dependent AUC (mTAUC). Due to page limitations, the corresponding results are summarized in Table S8. As evidenced by the AUC values: CoxPHM-AF exhibited superior predictive performance compared with CoxPHM-SF (AUC ranges: 0.822–0.862 vs. 0.777–0.850 for the training cohort; 0.876–0.908 vs. 0.867–0.901 for the validation cohort; 0.854–0.880 vs. 0.847–0.867 for the testing cohort). Meanwhile, CoxPHM-AF slightly outperformed Serum-SWE-ML in predicting the 90-day outcome on the validation cohort (AUC: 0.876 [95% CI: 0.773–0.940] vs. 0.872 [95% CI: 0.773–0.940]), the 1-year outcome on the validation cohort (AUC: 0.887 [95% CI: 0.790–0.950] vs. 0.878 [95% CI: 0.773–0.940]), and the 30-day outcome on the testing cohort (AUC: 0.880 [95% CI: 0.784–0.944] vs. 0.870 [95% CI: 0.768–0.934]), whereas CoxPHM-AF demonstrated inferior predictive performance relative to Serum-SWE-ML for the other predictions. DeLong test revealed no significant differences in the AUC values between CoxPHM-AF and Serum-SWE-ML during validation and testing (AUC ranges: 0.876–0.908, 0.854–0.880 vs. 0.872–0.924, 0.870–0.896; all p values >0.2). From the perspective of mTAUC, CoxPHM-AF also showed better performances compared to CoxPHM-SF (mTAUC ranges: 0.822–0.842 vs. 0.761–0.803 on the training cohort, 0.800–0.826 vs. 0.792–0.818 on the validation cohort, 0.816–0.851 vs. 0.796–0.838 on the testing cohort). Moreover, the performances of Serum-SWE-ML were comparable to those of CoxPHM-AF for most cases (mTAUC ranges: 0.829–0.841 vs. 0.822–0.842 in predicting various outcomes on the training cohort; 0.824–0.852 vs. 0.800–0.823 in predicting 30-day and 90-day outcomes on the validation cohort; 0.831–0.842 vs. 0.816–0.851 in predicting various outcomes on the testing cohort), except for predicting the 1-year outcome on the validation cohort (mTAUC: 0.905 vs. 0.826). The time-dependent AUC curves for Serum-SWE-ML and coxPHM-based models in predicting 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year outcomes on various cohorts are presented in the supplemental information (Figure S16).These results validated the feasibility of using binary classifiers for prognostic predictions across multiple time points.

The serological indicators included in the Serum-SWE-ML model are commonly used in clinical practice, making them highly accessible and clinically practical. Although the global adoption of 2D-SWE could be further improved, it plays a crucial role in prognostic assessment for ACLF patients. Our model integrates liver function information represented by serological indicators and biomechanical information provided by 2D-SWE, enabling a more comprehensive prognostic evaluation for ACLF patients. Moreover, the prognostic accuracy of our model surpasses that of commonly used clinical models. Furthermore, we innovatively translated this model into a mobile application, enhancing its practicality in daily clinical practice. Clinicians can simply input ultrasound elastography and serological test results to obtain immediate prognostic assessments, facilitating real-time communication between physicians and patients and thereby improving ACLF management.

Limitations of the study

Despite its contributions, this study has limitations. This retrospective analysis focused exclusively on patients with ACLF caused by hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, which may affect the generalizability of the model. Owing to limited sample size, we did not stratify the patients according to severity. Additionally, our model did not compare with clinical models such as COSSH-ACLFs, COSSH-ACLFIIs or CLIF-C ACLF, etc. In this case, we anticipate that future international prospective studies will be conducted and will include patients with ACLF resulting from a wider range of etiologies and compare with more clinical models to further explore the disease progression and prognosis of ACLF. Also, we acknowledge that the feasibility of each scoring system varies across different countries and regions due to differences in healthcare costs and medical infrastructure. As for our model, the incorporation of 2D-SWE may have some impact on clinical practicality. In future studies, we also aim to explore more easily obtainable and clinically significant indicators to enhance prognostic evaluation for ACLF patients.

Experimental model and study participant details

This was a dual-center retrospective cohort study. Patients were screened at two hospital centers, including the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University (SYSU-TAH) and Lingnan Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University (SYSU-LNH), from 1 November 2013 to 31 March 2021 (Table 1). Inclusion criteria were meeting the ACLF criteria of APASL guidelines and caused by hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. Exclusion criteria were failure of 2D-SWE detection, missing data of 2D-SWE or serological parameters, or caused by other etiologies (e.g., alcoholic liver disease). Date of ACLF diagnosis was considered as the starting date of follow-up. All patients in our study were monitored for at least 30 days. Each patient was given standard medical treatments.3 The clinical outcomes, namely, the recovery/steadiness or deterioration of each patient at 30 days, 90 days and 1 year, were recorded. Patients with resolution, improvement, or a steady condition were considered recovering/steady patients. Deterioration patients were those who died or underwent liver transplantation during follow-up period. ACLF patients successfully enrolled at SYSU-TAH formed the training cohort for model derivation, while those enrolled at SYSU-LNH were stratified into validation and testing cohorts according to the diagnostic time and ultrasound devices used for 2D-SWE examinations, to evaluate the performance of the models derived from the training cohort and that of the mobile application, respectively (see Figure 1 and Methods S1). Our study adheres to the EvaluAtion of Radiomics research (CLEAR) checklist. All procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional and national) and with Helsinki Declaration of 1975. This study was approved by the local medical ethics committee of the hospital (II2023-077-02), and written informed consent was obtained from each patient.

Method details

Data collection

At baseline, 2D-SWE examinations were performed by two operators, both with more than 500 2D-SWE examinations and 2 years of ultrasound experience, using Aixplorer US system (SuperSonic Imagine) with a convex probe (SC6–1, 1–6 MHz) and Aplio i900 US system (Canon Medical System) with a convex probe (i8CX1, 1–8 MHz). 2D-SWE measurement protocol has been described in our previous studies27 and is also recommended by European Federation of Societies for Ultrasound in Medicine and Biology guidelines20 and World Federation for Ultrasound in Medicine and Biology guidelines22 (refer to Methods S1 for more details). The following serological data were collected from each patient: aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), albumin (ALB),(TB), gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total cholesterol (CHE), serum (Na), serum creatinine (Cr), hemoglobin (HGB), white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet (PLT) count, prothrombin time (PT), prothrombin activity (PTA), and international normalized ratio (INR). Serological data and 2D-SWE data were obtained within 3 days.

Model development

Using the data from the training cohort, four prognostic predictive models, including three previously reported models (MELD,16,17 MELD-Na,18 and MELD-SWE,27 detailed definitions of which were provided in Methods S1), and a prognostic model, namely Serum-SWE-ML, were developed. Serum-SWE-ML model was developed by machine learning with data integration of serological parameters and 2D-SWE, with survival outcomes as data label (binary values: 0 for recovery/steadiness, 1 for deterioration). This machine learning procedure consisted of two major steps: feature selection (Figure 2, step 2) and model derivation (Figure 2, step 3). For feature selection, correlation analysis was conducted, including correlation analysis between patients’ serological parameters and data labels via Spearman method,32,33 followed by interfeature correlation analysis between each pair of serological parameters, such that serological parameters that correlated well with clinical outcomes but with minimum interfeature correlations could be screened out. In the following model construction step, three random forest (RF) classifiers34 were trained to predict clinical outcomes corresponding to 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year survival outcomes. The data input to RF classifiers were the screened serological parameters combined with 2D-SWE, which were unified for all the predictions. The optimal hyperparameters within RF classifiers were identified via grid searching method. More details on model development including feature/classifier selection, hyperparameter tuning of the selected classifier are provided in supplemental information. The resulted models together with a subset of anonymized medical data were available at https://github.com/binhuang15/ACLFapp. The data and code associated with this study have been deposited in the Zenodo repository (ACLFapp v1.0.0) with the https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18752099.

Model evaluation and online deployment

Predictive performances of various models were evaluated and compared on both training and validation cohorts (Figure 2, Step 4) in terms of a set of evaluation metrics, including accuracy (ACC), sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPE), positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and area under the ROC curve (AUC). Quantitative metrics of ACC, SEN, SPE, PPV, and NPV in predicting 30-day, 90-day and 1-year outcomes were calculated on basis of the optimal cutoff point identified on ROC curves of training cohort by maximizing the Youden index of curve data. After comparing the performance of various models, the most effective predictive model was identified. Subsequently, the calibration, net clinical benefits, and sub-group performances of the best model were evaluated across various cohorts. Moreover, a survival analysis was conducted to validate the model’s predictive performance for the target outcomes. During survival analysis, patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups on basis of the optimal cutoff points, and then survival probability curves (Kaplan‒Meier) were generated for different groups of patients. To leverage the most effective model for assisting physicians and helping ACLF patients, the model was deployed online to create a smartphone application (Figure 2, Step 5). Predictive performance of smartphone application was assessed on testing cohort using the same metrics as those employed for other cohorts (Figure 2, Step 6).

Model interpretation

To unpack the predictive mechanism of the prognostic model, we employed SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)35—a widely recognized explainable AI tool based on game theory—to analyze the feature importance across the entire cohort. Mean absolute SHAP values, together with their corresponding confidence intervals, were calculated to quantify the overall contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions. Furthermore, SHAP beeswarm plots were generated to visualize the distribution of SHAP values for all features across the entire cohort, while waterfall plots were generated to visualize the influence of each feature to the model’s prediction on an individual level. In addition, partial dependence analysis36 was performed to examine the monotonicity of the relationships between individual features and the model’s output by marginalizing the interference of other features.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical tests were performed via SPSS (version 26.0), MATLAB (version 2023a) or MedCalc software (version 11.2). Quantitative patient data are expressed herein as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), which were compared via Mann‒Whitney U test. Qualitative patient data are expressed as numbers with percentages and were compared via Fisher’s test orχ2 test. ROC curves and AUCs were used to describe predictive value of MELD, MELD-Na, MELD-SWE and the prognostic model. Next, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for quantitative evaluation metrics via Clopper–Person method.37 DeLong test38 and log rank test39 were used to compare ROC curves. Calibration of the prognostic model was assessed by comparing observed risk and average probability of deterioration at each time point predicted by the model. Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to assess corresponding goodness-of-fit. Furthermore, to comprehensively evaluate the calibration performance of the our prognostic model, we generated the LOESS (locally weighted scatterplot smoothing40)-smoothed calibration curves for the model in predicting the 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year outcome across various cohorts, and reported the calibration metrics—including optimism-corrected Brier score, calibration slope and calibration intercept with 95% CI (derived via 1,000 bootstrap resamples). Additionally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to generate net benefit curves for assessing the model’s clinical utility. Transplantation-free survival probability curves (Kaplan–Meier) were constructed for different groups and compared via log rank test. p values were two-sided, and a value less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance.
