Controlling the Field: Memory, Labor, and Ethics in Oral Histories of Brazilian Human Genetics**
Authors: Rosanna Dent, Ana Carolina Vimieiro Gomes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article examines how oral histories of twentieth‐century human genetics in Brazil reveal the politics of memory of fieldwork. Through a comparative analysis of interviews with prominent geneticist Francisco M. Salzano and technician Girley V. Simões, who worked with him for most of his career, this study explores the narrative strategies each employed to establish their historical accounts. Attending reflexively to the oral history encounters, the analysis examines how each narrator negotiates professional identity and moral legitimacy in light of changing ethical norms surrounding research with Indigenous communities. Simões's vivid recollections foreground invisible forms of technical and logistical labor, offering him the opportunity to recast his position as one of active knowledge‐making. Salzano's controlled and diplomatic accounts, by contrast, illustrate how the senior scientist curated memory to stabilize his professional legacy and defend disciplinary ethics in the wake of controversy. Contrasting Salzano and Simões's approaches to describing their shared experiences drives home the complex social and political realities of narrating fieldwork, as well as the political valences of remembering and documenting these histories in the present.Keywords: oral history, fieldwork, human genetics, controversy, Brazil, labor

Introduction

Field stories are a genre of their own. They draw in the listener, create suspense, and invite empathy. They can provoke laughter or evoke disgust. For historians of science, field stories can unearth nuances of scientific labor and logistics, colonial relationships, collaborations, personal and affective experience, and the messy, enthralling ethical issues raised by science in practice. 1 However, like any other primary source, they are produced by actors with specific goals and audiences in mind and reflect ongoing tensions over what should be remembered and forgotten. Memory and recollection have special resonance in the biomedical and human sciences (such as anthropology), where firsthand accounts of fieldwork establish a researcher's familiarity with the messy ethics of studying science in practice. 2

Interviewing actors about their field stories adds another layer of complexity to the politics of memory. An established corpus of oral history research highlights that interviews are not passive channels for recollection, but active processes whereby interviewees curate their past and negotiate their identities in relation to the present. 3 Historian Paul Merchant recently emphasized that scholars have drawn on oral history to document twentieth and twenty‐first‐century science to great success, but have been less deliberate in thinking about oral history as a genre of scientists’ self‐representations. 4 As Lillian Hoddeson writes, historians need to pay attention to “how a person's memories are shaped and reshaped to support his present self‐image.” 5 Oral histories shed light on the interviewees’ self‐representations as knowledge producers. They give interviewees an opportunity to reshape their past to suit new political, ethical, and professional contexts. This makes them a particularly interesting resource for interpreting the history of science as informed by personal and present concerns.

The mid‐twentieth century is a particularly interesting moment to explore these reflexive dynamics. Postwar funding, colonial administration, and technical assistance programs sent fieldworkers from Europe and North America to all corners of the globe, even as decolonization, civil rights movements, and major reconfigurations of geopolitics denaturalized these endeavors. The result was often heightened reflexivity and self‐consciousness. Jeremy Vetter has recently observed that fieldworkers increasingly “developed not only experiential knowledge but also their own folklore surrounding their practices.” 6 For instance, Etienne Benson argues that US Survey geologists and geomorphologists self‐consciously used humor and satire to situate their increasingly bureaucratic, quantitative, and laboratory‐oriented field in relation to older nineteenth century ideals of heroic fieldwork. Specific ways of describing the past thus consolidate professional identity in the present. As Benson puts it, we “need to attend to those moments when our historical actors seem gleefully aware that they are players on a stage—putting on their best costumes, exaggerating their gestures for comic effect, and generally making it clear to their audiences that they, too, are in on the joke.” 7

This self‐consciousness, interesting and relevant in its own right, is a major challenge in oral history. In her research on scientific fieldwork in the Portuguese empire, Cláudia Castelo reflects on how scientist‐interviewees worked to control their narratives. In their oral histories and the aftermath, they engaged in detailed editing before authorizing transcripts and reformulated their accounts to be increasingly similar to familial written records of colonial fieldwork. Castelo even describes facing an “interviewee‐dominated relationship” that, although empathetic, limited the interviewer‐interviewee flow in her oral history work. However, with her analysis, these narratives still offer insight into scientific work that supplements the written record in essential ways: they elucidate felt experiences of science in practice, bring labor dynamics to the fore, and help make clear the political valences of ostensibly apolitical scientific investigation. 8

In this essay, we build on these insights regarding the folklore, performance, and control of fieldwork narratives through the comparison of two oral histories of Brazilian genetics research in the twentieth century. Rosanna Dent recorded these interviews between 2012 and 2014 in the context of doctoral research. They document the memories of Professor Francisco M. Salzano (1928–2018) and Girley V. Simões (1936–2021) of their work on human genetics in the Department of Genetics at the Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul (UFRGS) in Porto Alegre, Brazil. Salzano was a professor and an often‐noted founder of human genetics in Brazil. Simões was a laboratory and field technician, driver, and general assistant. The two men conducted genetics fieldwork together beginning in the late 1950s. Comparing their two accounts of the same work illuminates the contested terrain of the politics of memorialization in field disciplines.

Ideas about the role of genetic and biomedical research with Indigenous groups transformed over the latter part of the twentieth century and the first decade of the twenty‐first. Scientists’ belief in genetics as a form of universal human knowledge and their assumption that highly trained physicians and biomedical researchers with good intentions would inevitably benefit humans more generally by studying Indigenous people were increasingly challenged. 9 Indigenous activists and scientists, anthropologists, and others contested the notion that Indigenous people could be used as a stand‐in for humanity of the distant past, and raised sharp critiques of common genetics practices and classifications in dynamic conversation with evolving ethical norms around key issues such as informed consent. 10 Much as decolonization of the Portuguese empire transformed Castelo's interlocutors’ stories of fieldwork, shifts in the politics and ethics of genetics motivated new forms of recollection for our narrators. The politics and performance of memory in human genetics become more salient when listening to Simões and Salzano in parallel: although they participated in the same research, their different positionalities produce contrasting stories that reveal their different preoccupations with the past.

Salzano and Simões's narratives juxtapose professor and technician, but neither's position is simple. While some might imagine the Brazilian geneticist as a subaltern actor within a global science where funding and the flow of biosamples skew heavily to North America and Europe, within Brazil, Salzano was also an elite actor with a prestigious position in a federal university. Geneticists’ access to Indigenous populations for study within Brazil relied on infrastructures of internal colonialism and government support for nation‐building through science, dynamics that made Salzano's work possible. 11 Within this matrix, Simões held an essential but often underacknowledged position as technician, even as he positioned himself within the hierarchy of knowledge producers and expressed views that replicated worn and derogatory tropes of Indigenous peoples. Neither “studying up” nor “studying down” captures the complex social positions of the narrators, nor of our own positionalities as historians in relationship to them. Rather, we explore how these shifting positionalities and changing political contexts motivated Simões and Salzano's specific recollective strategies to solidify their professional credentials for their imagined audiences. We argue that contrasting Salzano and Simões's approaches to describing their shared experiences drives home the complex social and political realities of narrating fieldwork, as well as the political valences of remembering and documenting these histories in the present.

Situating the Oral History Encounter

In this section, we reflect on how our own positionalities colored our encounter during the interviews. Before we begin, a little more background on Salzano and Simões is warranted. Salzano and Simões's careers were intimately linked, with Simões securing his position in the early days of Salzano's sixty‐three‐year career at UFRGS, but retiring two decades before the end of Salzano's career. Among the most famous and influential geneticists of his generation, Salzano was a public figure, highly decorated for his work on the history of the peopling of the Americas and on the science of human evolution more broadly. 12 He had an international circulation and a global network facilitated by his knowledge of and access to Indigenous peoples in Brazil. 13 Salzano was also known as a generous and rigorous teacher, mentor, and colleague, who, despite his outsized presence in the field, maintained a certain modesty in relation to his own contributions.

Simões's labor was central to Salzano's success, helping to sustain his work in both laboratory and field. Both men independently described their relationship as one of collegiality, bound by trust and respect. Salzano explained their relationship to historian Naida Menezes in an early oral history in 2009, saying, “there was one [technician] who was very close to me, Girley Simões, because he did all the laboratory work, a person of excellent technical and individual ability, and he also accompanied me on all the field work. So we traveled all over Brazil together studying Indigenous groups. He and I formed a very close friendship.” 14 Simões recognized himself as central to Salzano's work, as “the person who did things for him.” 15 He also praised Salzano warmly, noting Salzano's flexibility during the rigors of travel and fieldwork, “Doctor Salzano is an extraordinary person [uma pessoa assim fora de série].” Both expressed that their shared experiences in the field shaped their appreciation and respect for one another. Both understood the other as central to their careers. As such, they were ideal candidates for a microcomparative oral history.

Rosanna Dent's Perspective

Simões and Salzano each narrated their own vision of themselves as historical actors. But as with any interview, their understandings of my interests and my questions and responses also shaped our exchanges. They knew that with their permission, these oral histories would become part of the public record.

One of the controversies that animated major threads of Salzano's narrative was also what provided an opening for my work with him. In the year 2000, Salzano's recently deceased collaborator James V. Neel was embroiled in the furor following the publication of Patrick Tierney's journalistic exposé Darkness in El Dorado. The book rocked the fields of anthropology and human genetics with grave allegations of unethical research among Yanomami communities. 16 Tierney reserved some of his most aggressive attacks for Neel's actions during a measles outbreak in 1968, accusing Neel of vaccinating with an outdated, dangerous vaccine, and implying he had purposely spread measles to study Yanomami immunity. These and some of Tierny's other gross misinterpretations have since been debunked. 17 Nevertheless, the work raised relevant critiques that still deserve attention today. 18

Historian of science Susan Lindee was among the scholars to weigh in on the controversy. She drew on her extensive expertise on Neel's career and archived papers to offer careful empirical analysis that undermined some of Tierney's most damning allegations. 19 A decade later, it was Lindee who suggested I investigate Neel's relationship with Salzano. Soon after reading the two geneticists’ archived correspondence, I traveled to meet Salzano and explore whether his scholarship on population genetics would become the topic for my doctoral research.

When he first welcomed me to Porto Alegre in 2012, Salzano expressed his great appreciation for Lindee's empirically grounded approach to Tierney's allegations. From the beginning, I credited his willingness to work with me to his experiences with Lindee. 20 He gave me wide latitude to explore his filing cabinets and sat for a series of six interviews between 90 and 120 min each.

Salzano was gracious in deferring to me as the expert in the room, even as it was my first time recording oral history. I had little training, having read a few influential works in Latin American oral history and pored over a practical guide. 21 My inexperience shows in the recordings as I failed to leave ample pauses that might have prompted Salzano to continue or expand on his line of thinking, and posed compound questions that I would later learn to minimize. These compound questions complicate interpretation, as it is not always evident which topics Salzano avoided of his own volition. But some dynamics became clear. Salzano's first recording had long responses, where he described becoming a scientist and how he fell in love with the field of genetics. He told stories of fieldwork that mirrored expeditionary tales. These answers closely resemble his previous interviews and writings. 22 As the interviews continued, his responses about fieldwork became much more succinct. As I would try to ask the same thing several ways, he often did not have much to say, and I struggled to draw him out.

My experience with Simões had a very different texture. It took time, effort, and help to find him, and I was delighted when I was finally able to meet him. I opened our conversation about conducting the recordings from the position that his labor had been essential to Salzano's career, and that his story would constitute an important part of the history of the field and lab work that built the discipline of human population genetics in Brazil. Simões, who had never recorded an oral history before, seemed immediately pleased to be invited to participate, and as we describe below quickly relaxed into enthusiastic, long, flowing answers about his experience, particularly of fieldwork. Just as actively as Salzano, Simões made use of the interviews to shape his narratives around the labor politics of his role, and to assert his position as contributing to knowledge production through more than just grit and tacit skill.

It was not until 2019 that Vimieiro and I began working together to create an accessible online collection that included Salzano and Simões's oral histories. Listening to the oral histories again, this time with a team of graduate and undergraduate students, raised new sets of questions and curiosities about the narratives and how to interpret them within a history of science framework.

Ana Carolina Vimieiro Gomes’ Perspective

I never met Francisco Salzano. I only knew him from the historiography of human population genetics, and due to the importance of his work on South American Indigenous populations. Nor did I meet Girley Simões. I learned the story of his life as a technician and his trajectory as Salzano's right‐hand man when I listened to his oral histories. I never visited the Salzano's laboratory, and had no exposure to the department atmosphere, scientific culture, or Salzano's papers. I had previous experience in oral history, having collaboratively collected dozens of oral histories with professors from the Institute of Biological Science of the Federal University of Minas Gerais. But that project was explicitly aimed at celebrating the fiftieth anniversary of the Institute, and interviews were used only as sources of useful information to tell an institutional history. Our goal for this project was very different. Dent and I agreed that we wanted the interviews to be accessible even outside the institution, and so we developed a website open not just to geneticists and historians, but also to the broader public.

It felt awkward to join this project with the oral histories already completed. I did not participate in the historical fieldwork that produced the narratives. I cannot offer an (auto) ethnographic account of the texture of interviews, or complement the audio recordings with memories of gesture and expression. I felt this lack, even as my distance allowed me to read the oral histories differently. My position outside of the relationship of the interview provided a complementary perspective. The first step involved trying to assess the backstage of the interview's practice, that is, “how, when, why, and who made the construction of such oral sources possible.” 23 It implied an effort to build the historical context of the sources’ production and learn more about who narrated, who interviewed, and to reflect on the factors that conditioned the interviewee's memories, their biography, institutional positions, professional status, and social worlds. Listening carefully was essential. These historical tasks were complemented by my engagement with the practical work of processing, systematizing, and coding each interview to make them readable and audible for other people. We worked with existing online oral history tools to put the transcripts together with the audio recordings, thus providing access to the orality of the narratives. The results are now available in an interactive platform, hogenetica.org, where anyone can listen to, read, and browse the oral histories by keyword and theme. 24 Collaborative work on the interviews helped us to perceive new lines of analysis, denaturalizing aspects of the narratives that were familiar to Dent.

Analyzing the Politics of Memory Through Comparative Oral History

We now turn from the context of the interviews’ production to think about how these dynamics intersected with existing politics around memory in human genetics. Salzano's diplomatic narration reflected decades of engaging with the construction of his discipline's history and of learning to craft his recollections to fit evolving ethical norms and standard practices. By contrast, Simões's energy and enthusiasm were focused on what was for him an unusual opportunity to record his lived experience and, through this, valorize his labor. His oral narratives are enriched by details on his skills and the troublesome and invisible roles often entrusted to technicians, 25 but through them, he deliberately positioned himself as a protagonist in Salzano's fieldwork endeavors. Each narrator brought concerns about his position and an agenda relating to how his work and contributions would be judged, whether in terms of ethical action or historical impact and recognition.

We begin with an exploration of Simões's narratives as an acknowledgment of our own interest in invisibilized labor. His ebullient and expansive storytelling reinforces the point that oral history holds the potential not only to fill in details missing from the archival record but also to help historians of science understand how less elite actors make moral meaning out of their participation in scientific work. 26 His narrative reveals the field in that it brings the materiality and lived experience to the fore; he worked to dramatically demonstrate the field as a site that upended traditional hierarchies, uncovering his own authority in scientific work and teaching. Salzano's narratives, on the other hand, offer an object lesson in the retrospective crafting of memory in relation to controversy and ethical debate. The utmost care he practiced in what he disclosed regarding his actions and the actions of his colleagues highlights the oral history encounter as a site of control in which the dynamic of the interview itself may be as informative for analyzing history in the context of contemporary concerns as for informing missing details from the archive.

Revealing the Field: Subverting Labor Hierarchies in Science

Girley Simões loved to tell a good story, usually about his truck. Yet in our listening, his heroic narratives of fieldwork have a different flavor than those of explorers of yore. 27 His accounts are inflected with a consciousness of class and hierarchies of training. They lean into the ironies of differing priorities and logics between himself and the recognized scientists he accompanied. He described himself as the common man, without a fancy degree or high technical qualifications, but with the salt‐of‐the‐earth problem‐solving of the mechanic. In his telling, it was he who made possible the slightly outrageous realities of fieldwork for a wide range of professors and students. Through countless encounters with mud, flaming motors, and rats and gnats, endlessly long days, and the help of strangers, Simões figured himself as the person whose definitive actions and strategic choices shaped the trajectory of scientific work. His narrative became a space to make a claim for recognition for years of labor that left only traces in the acknowledgement sections of published papers, but that were essential for training many who would go on to become prominent geneticists. Simões appeared in his own story, desiring to make visible his work, contributions, and sacrifice. 28

As he narrated, Simões firmly positioned himself as the protagonist. Oral historians pay close attention to the moment in a recording where the narrator relaxes and finds their footing in their narrative. For Simões, the break came in his response to Dent's first question about his academic or professional training. His response began stiffly, as if he felt he had to admit to not having a higher degree. He went on to explain how he had competed for his position in Porto Alegre against candidates with far more experience: “Now there were those people who had already worked in clinical laboratories, so they had a bit more experience with handling [samples]. And in this case, I didn't. But that's exactly what they didn't want.” It was at this moment, as he fumbled his words and laughed, that Simões caught his footing to exclaim, “What they wanted was [someone] to start from zero! So I started with Dr. Salzano in 1958. It was just me and him in the lab.” 29 Simões emphasized the surprise that his lack of training made him perfect for the position. He figured himself as part of the origin story of Salzano's work, as a blank slate who could be trained into the stalwart foundation of the human genetics laboratory.

Dent's questions to Simões skewed to the topic of fieldwork, reflecting her interest in interactions between genetics teams and the human subjects who made their research possible. But it also allowed Simões to speak to his ideal imagined audience: practically oriented people interested in so‐called real work on the ground. As such, he offered story after story about his work driving, which offered him the opportunity to highlight the fundamental nature of his labor for sustaining scientific inquiry. He described the earliest fieldwork in Kaingang communities in western Rio Grande do Sul, saying, “The trips were no joke. […] I made odysseys in that pick‐up truck.” He laughed, “I would get into the middle of this mud, and sometimes there were holes from huge trucks. […] I'd sometimes fly into the air and hit my head on the roof.” These descriptions helped underline Simões's claims to competence: “Look, I had some close calls, and thank God, I never hit anyone, and no one ever hit me. I never had even the smallest accident. Nothing. Understand?” 30 Time and again in his recounting, Simões's nerve and skill saved the day, transforming his role as a driver, which he made sure to note was never an official or remunerated duty, into that of a heroic facilitator.

In his extensive discussion of getting to the field and home again, Simões not only constructed a narrative that centered his contributions and made sense of years of effort and sacrifice as a contribution to science, but his narratives also help to underline the kinds of invisibilized labor, and class politics, that made mid‐century human genetics possible. Simões relished sharing moments where he saved or surprised his traveling companions, proving himself as the more knowledgeable or competent party. Many of these stories focused on his interactions with Salzano's students. Reflecting on his work with Israel Roisenberg on hemophilia, for example, Simões recounted how even after a long day of driving, he would help the physician‐turned‐doctoral‐candidate construct genealogies. One day, Simões, with a sharp eye for detail, noticed that the names on two distant parts of the family tree matched up. He described Israel's incredulity and his own reaction: “So I said to him, ‘Let me see the name of this person here. I'm going to go look for this person. I'm going'. And I got there, and it turned out, they were sisters, but for forty years they had lost contact, and they didn't even know they were living in the same municipality!” 31 Simões's observation resulted in his reuniting the women, who had been separated when young and had assumed one another dead.

In other moments, when the geneticists he accompanied doubted his skill, Simões proved himself behind the wheel, changing a flat tire, and extinguishing an engine fire just in time. In his narrative, he claimed experiences that few others had, a kind of lived expertise that came from his work rather than his studiousness and formal training. This extended to his daily life: at the doctor's office, as the technician went to reassure Simões before drawing his blood with a vacutainer, Simões remembered, “I looked at him and said, ‘If you only knew! I think I was the first Brazilian to use one of those.’” 32

In another favorite story that Simões repeated in each of his two interviews, the technician taught a graduate student a lesson on receiving hospitality. Simões accompanied Salzano and doctoral student Fernando José da Rocha (1939–2020) to Kayapo territory in the mid 1960s. As was often the case, Simões was charged with preparing meals for the research team: “Rocha was a spoiled rich kid [filhinho de papai]. We'd go to hotels, restaurants, and he only wanted eggs, steak, and French fries.” Simões gave the graduate student a hard time, warning that he would not be serving anything like that in the field. Rocha subsisted on packets of crackers and water, Simões explained, until one afternoon when they were sitting by the edge of the river. “I saw the chief of the Indians. Like this, with a walking stick [Simões mimes hunching over] […] but already old. He came with his hand like this. [Simões mimes holding something in the palm of his hand.] He came, came, and passed by me and went in Fernando's direction. Do you know what he had in his hand? An egg, fried. He brought it in his dirty hand. […] And Fernando: ‘Não.“[I said,] Don't do that, young man. Take it! Take it and eat it! You're looking at the chief of the village, lad'!” 33

Simões savored this story, which stayed very alive in his memory, and apparently also in Rocha's. While the graduate student would go on to get his PhD, become a professor, and eventually chair the department, Simões emphasized that Rocha never forgot what he claimed had been a joke at his expense, orchestrated by Simões. But in Simões's telling, although he did not express this explicitly, he was teaching fundamental social skills of fieldwork, insisting Rocha show respect by accepting the leader's gift, however unpalatable. He seemed to imply it was his practical skill, and his own positionality as a grounded, working‐class man, that allowed him to relate to the social realities of the people who participated in their scientific research. While Simões was only three years older than Rocha, he had already acquired ten years’ experience in fieldwork that, in his memory, gave him authority and collegiality across academic hierarchies. While humorous, Simões's stories are more than just jokes. As a technician, driver, and research assistant, he focused on moments when he outshone those with more traditional credentials, of a higher socioeconomic class. The oral history afforded him a space to document his labor, and emphasize that he brought more to the research enterprise than logistical support or simple laboratory work. The conspiratorial dynamic of Dent's interest only served to encourage these stories.

Moreover, Simões's position in relation to the research field meant that he was less concerned with how his account would resonate with broader developments in the discipline of human genetics. Indeed, he had been retired since 1989, 34 and had not been called upon to respond to ethical controversies or criticized for his role in research with Indigenous populations. This translated into relatively uncensored descriptions of the Kayapó leader that at times reinforced pejorative stereotypes. He offered unflattering anecdotes about Indigenous communities, describing bodily smells, filth, or, in one instance, commenting on clouds of gnats that swarmed around community members. Unlike Salzano, as we shall see below, he did not shy away from descriptions that were politically incorrect by local standards. At the same time, Simões praised attributes of Indigenous groups that resonated with his own working‐class background. He commended Indigenous subjects for being stronger and more fearless than Brazilians, whom he referred to as “civilizados”: “It's better to work with Indians than with civilizados. It's the civilizado who is annoying [chato]. You go and, ‘it hurts!’ and I don't know what else. I've had men faint right in front of me because they are afraid of a needle. And among the Indians, children, they watch you and laugh! [Simões laughed.] As if it were the most natural thing in the world. Understand? There was no problem. It was easy, fast, fast, to collect material.” 35 When listened to in their entirety, Simões's narratives brought together discourses about the generosity, resilience, and strength of his Indigenous hosts with frank descriptions of what he found unpleasant, to create a narrative that was self‐consciously honest, practical, and unafraid to “tell things as they were.”

Controlling the Field: Managing Fieldwork Controversies in Oral History

The quality and process of the interview experience with Salzano diverged from Dent's conversations with Simões, such that it raises a series of questions about the very different ways that Salzano exercised control over his narrative. Salzano was not a reticent participant. He sat for a series of interviews with a great deal of patience, interest, and goodwill. However, while he offered long responses and told stories about his work with little prompting in his first recording, in subsequent recordings, Dent asked many more questions and Salzano offered succinct responses with little elaboration, no free association, and few tangents. This shift was not one that Dent sought, and we interpret it to be a result of Salzano's practiced responses and tendency to draw on structuring logics that he had used in previous written and spoken accounts. The politics of representing fieldwork in the aftermath of controversy were marked by Salzano's restraint and redirection at some moments, and by his placement of key stories and commentaries in defense of his discipline at others.

Like Simões, Salzano's narration was influenced by his position as a senior leader in human genetics. Unlike Simões, who subverted labor hierarchies to valorize his hidden contributions, Salzano's scientific legacy was assured. Instead, it was the ethics and rigor of his work that he defended through his interviews. In the narrations of his career and particularly his fieldwork, Salzano consistently responded to a series of controversies and ethical debates that had played out in the final decades of his career. He also reproduced and strengthened his articulations of what he saw as the ethical character and the universalist imperative of human genetics research. The ethics of his research was not the exclusive focus of his narrations of fieldwork, but it surfaced repeatedly. Salzano knew that ethical oversight of research and the history of regulatory rules were among Dent's interests, and he used the space of the interviews to rearticulate lines of discourse that he had developed in prior work, written and spoken, about ethical questions at the heart of human genetics as a field and set of practices.

Like Simões, on a few occasions in his first interview, Salzano offered dramatic anecdotes about what he experienced during fieldwork, mostly about occasions when things went wrong. When asked by Dent to describe the landscape of Mato Grosso, the state where A'uwẽ (Xavante) communities live, Salzano described a trip with Simões through the semi‐arid Central Plateau as a “drama” and an “odyssey.” The pair went to Mato Grosso with a plan to drive to an A'uwẽ community and conduct their study. Then, the precious blood samples were to be picked up by their colleague, but the trip went awry when the aircraft did not arrive. The race back to the lab culminated in a heroic episode where Salzano managed to get help from a village some kilometers away to save the day. 36 Salzano, not unlike Simões, found a familiar format in travel stories. Yet where Simões's odyssey served to highlight his physical competence and skill, Salzano's account demonstrated his willingness to go above and beyond to keep the biological samples from spoiling and maintain scientific rigor.

Passages of Salzano's first recording with Dent corresponded closely with accounts that he had penned or recounted previously. Salzano repeated frequently, including both in his first interview with Dent, 37 and in an obituary for his postdoctoral mentor, a story of Neel's futile insistence on bringing a generator for joint work in A'uwẽ (Xavante) territory. 38 Emphasizing that “Jim had the philosophy that ‘one should be prepared for any eventuality that may occur in the field,’” 39 Salzano went on in both accounts to expound on the inconvenience and massive weight of the generator and the time wasted to try, unsuccessfully, to get it to work. Other stories from the first interview followed this model, corresponding closely with prior accounts. This is a tendency that many oral historians have observed in which those who are famous in their fields repeat versions of history that they themselves have shaped over time through their writing and multiple interviews. 40 A key aspect of a productive oral history process according to this literature, is to break through the practiced, established account, to encounter a more spontaneous telling. Although Dent was aware of the alignments of Salzano's various histories, she found it extremely difficult to draw him out to describe his time in the field differently or reflect on his life history in a way that would surprise him and enrich his own sense of his historical position.

Salzano's account is also telling for its careful curation in relation to changing ideas about the ethics of Indigenous biosampling. In the wake of controversy and critiques of human genetics research, the interviews provided a space for Salzano to revisit his account and offer new details in his and his colleagues’ defense. He was well aware that his accounts would be read against social scientific critiques of his discipline. 41 His strategy was to avoid recounting potentially controversial episodes while providing contrasting anecdotes of humanitarian behavior.

For example, Salzano did not deny or avoid mentioning his work with Neel on their first trip to Yanomami territory, an object of Tierney's attacks. However, when mentioned, he quickly shifted to speaking about other joint work, traveling down the Amazon on the research vessel the Alpha Helix, redirecting the conversation multiple times. 42 The details Salzano included about work with Yanomami avoided any direct mention of the people or study procedures. When Dent inquired further about Neel's role in the Yanomami field work, Salzano replied: “The first one we did in Brazil. And with all the necessary ethical precautions.” He then immediately changed the subject, continuing: “At that time, there was also a question of resistance toward Americans, foreigners in general. So, for the Alpha Helix, a Brazilian researcher from INPA [National Institute of Amazonian Research] was even assigned to monitor whether we were behaving well.” Even when Dent insisted, “And when you went to work in the field with the Yanomama” Salzano redirected to the timeline, offering few details about the work: “The Yanomama was before Alpha Helix.” 43 Salzano's claim that he and Neel took “all the necessary ethical precautions” suggests that he saw questions about Neel as indirect questions about ethical practice, which may have caused him to withhold further details about fieldwork.

While many of his descriptions of interactions with Indigenous subjects were succinct or framed as limited, Salzano took time to repeat heroic stories about Neel that challenged Tierney's representations. These accounts worked to establish the ethics, humanitarian behavior, and affective relations that informed their joint fieldwork. Salzano was acutely aware that ethical regimes had changed over time, but he also asserted the ethical basis for their work in the years before Institutional Review Boards and Ethics Committees. Describing Neel in the field he offered what he called “a shocking episode” of a child who was nearly abandoned and suffering a dire infection: “When we arrived, she was practically lost to a worm, a worm called ‘bicho‐de‐pé’ here [jigger flea, Tunga penetrans].” Knowing she would be unable to survive the aggressive medications used to treat the subcutaneous infection, Salzano explained, “during the whole time that we were there, Neel would pick up the child, and he started removing [the worms] with tweezers, one by one. He did this so naturally. These are things that don't appear in scientific publications, but it was very important for the survival of that child.” 44 Throughout his career, Salzano held that the medical attention that the researchers offered during their fieldwork in Indigenous communities was an essential aspect of giving back to participants. 45 Neel's actions as a physician were a key part of what Salzano saw as the ethical foundation of their work.

In comparison to Simões, Salzano offered little detail about the routine of fieldwork even when directly asked. Rather than sharing personal impressions or memories of the intimate contact of genealogies, medical exams, and anthropometric measurement, he delivered a narrative of social organization and ritual life:

DENT: And what was it like … what time did you wake up? How many hours did you work a day? What did you eat there? What was the interaction like with … with the people in the study, with the Xavante? Did they … how did they relate to the team? How did they relate?

SALZANO: At that time, they were still very isolated, with little nonindigenous influence. They are structured in age groups. So it was very common for certain age groups to get together and dance, in a circle, at different times during the day and even at night. Then, from time to time, they would get together and serenade us in the evening with their typical songs. The work started more or less at the time they got up, which was around nine o'clock. And … it went on for most of the afternoon. 46

Salzano stopped there, narrating distance from A'uwẽ (Xavante) subjects and thereby limiting the risk of opening a view into the nuances of power relations in the fieldwork.

In a later interview, Salzano went even further in his control over his narrative by ignoring Dent's questions about the field, changing the topic to publications. When Dent asked how his first field experience in A'uwẽ territory compared to his second, Salzano pulled out a journal issue and directed attention to the results instead of the process of the fieldwork: “So, this here is a copy of the work, the first one from ‘64 as you said… So they include the people who did fieldwork there. Now, in ‘67 we published a whole set of articles, ten in number...” Rather than following Salzano's interest, Dent asked again about who accompanied him to the field, hoping to draw out memories of the second trip to Mato Grosso. Salzano responded by opening the journal: “Let me show you the whole series … [page] 394 … ‘67 … One, two, three, four, five, six, seven. Seven [articles] appeared in this special issue, and then two or three others, which were more medical in nature, appeared in other journals.” 47 While the publications offered a way to remember who had traveled with him on a trip that had happened decades earlier, they also relieved him from commenting on field interactions.

Salzano was aware of his agency over narratives about polemic aspects of his past scientific investigations. He had worked for more than sixty years as a scientist. He lived through key moments in the history of human genetics: moments of optimism about its promises and others of intense social and bioethical contestation of its procedures. His careful curation of discussions about fieldwork shows how he sought to preserve his scientific legacy from ethical controversy in the oral history process. Where Dent looked to move past the published record, an aim often centered in rationales for oral history of science, Salzano made recourse to his published accounts and scientific products, anchoring his narrative in the written record to minimize the risk of new critiques. 48 He knew that controversial happenings of the past help make up human population genetics in the present, and he shaped the memories he offered accordingly.

Conclusion

Speaking about twentieth‐century human genetics fieldwork in the twenty‐first century is a complex process, in which affect, political valences, contradictions, controversies, and ethical debates do not fit easily into heroic models. 49 This essay's primary contribution lies in its comparative analysis of interviews with a geneticist and his technician. Reading these accounts together makes clear the centrality of memorialization within oral histories of science and shows how contrasting narrative strategies regarding shared events reveal divergent stakes and audiences of each account. As Lynn Abrams notes, oral histories are narrative constructions actively created for specific imagined audiences; they emerge from the interactions of the interviewee with themself, with the interviewer, and with sociocultural discourses of the past and the present. 50 For Simões, who occupied a role peripheral to the formal discipline of human genetics, the interview was an opportunity to subvert labor hierarchies and place himself at the center of fieldwork through a language of working‐class pragmatism. His approach to the interview illuminated details of field practices, including the complicated local dynamics of knowledge production and transit, with its specific division of labor (and its subversion) and its power relations. 51 On the other hand, Salzano's position as a decorated researcher and his awareness of the ethical controversies plaguing his discipline led to a very different type of controlled recollection about his relationships with the Indigenous groups he studied. Salzano made his interviews with Dent into an opportunity to stabilize ready‐made narratives about controversial issues. Even when experiences overlapped, these different agendas generated contrasting memories, such as when Simões and Salzano both referred to their travels as odysseys to make distinct points. For Simões, overcoming the challenges was proof of his problem‐solving capabilities; for Salzano, his willingness to engage in physical work in addition to mental labor signaled his deep commitment to scientific rigor.

These processes of memorialization also intersected with the dynamic of the interviewer‐interviewee relationship in ways that both enriched and limited the epistemic value of our oral histories. Simões and Salzano both responded to Dent's stated interests, in invisible labor, human subjects’ experience, and larger‐scale ethical debates and regulatory practices, as they controlled their discourse about field research. While Dent was able to use her stated investment in understanding and representing Simões's contributions to facilitate a conspiratorial dynamic that encouraged greater recollection about Simões's subjective field experiences, Salzano's familiarity with both the process and political implications of speaking about research with Indigenous communities precluded a similar candidness, even as he was a generous and patient interviewee. Our study of microcomparative oral history thus highlights that historians must be aware that their actors are already part of existing cultures of memory within their professional disciplines. A historian enters this epistemic terrain from a particular position, place, and point in time. By being reflexive about oral histories as sources shaped by a range of forces, historians can reveal the unspoken politics of memory that are essential to the field disciplines.
