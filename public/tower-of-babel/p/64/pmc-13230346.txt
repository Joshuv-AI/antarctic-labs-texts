IER3 as an RFX5 transcriptional co-activator promotes hepatocellular carcinoma progression via AKR1B10-mediated p53 transcriptional regulation
Authors: Xin Chen, Qing Zhang, Nuobei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) exhibits profound molecular heterogeneity, which complicates prognosis and therapy. Identifying key molecular subtypes and their driving oncogenes is crucial for developing targeted strategies. This study aimed to delineate chemokine-based HCC subtypes and investigate the functional role and mechanism of a critical identified driver, Immediate Early Response 3 (IER3). Methods. Bioinformatic analysis of public datasets was performed for molecular subtyping, differential expression, survival, and pathway enrichment. Clinical relevance was validated via immunohistochemistry on a tissue microarray containing 48 paired HCC/adjacent tissues. In vitro functional assays (CCK-8, colony formation, flow cytometry, wound healing, and Transwell) were conducted in SMMC-7721 cells following siRNA-mediated knockdown. Mechanistic studies employed dual-luciferase reporter, co-immunoprecipitation, qPCR, and western blot to delineate the IER3/RFX5/AKR1B10/p53 axis. Results. Unsupervised clustering based on chemokine expression stratified HCC into two subtypes: an immune-active subtype (Cluster 1) enriched in interferon-γ signaling and a matrix-remodeling subtype (Cluster 2). Prognostic analysis further refined three patient clusters, with the aggressive Subtype C exhibiting worst survival (HR = 3.78, p < 0.001) and significant IER3 upregulation. Clinical samples confirmed IER3 protein overexpression correlated with advanced grade (p < 0.001). In vitro, IER3 knockdown suppressed proliferation, colony formation, migration/invasion, and induced apoptosis. Mechanistically, IER3 functioned as a transcriptional coactivator for RFX5, forming a complex that upregulated AKR1B10, which subsequently mediated the suppression of the tumor suppressor p53. Conclusion. This study defines a chemokine-based duality in HCC and identifies IER3 as a key driver of the aggressive subtype. Our findings indicate that IER3 contributes to HCC malignancy through the novel IER3/RFX5-AKR1B10-p53 axis in vitro, positioning it as a potential therapeutic target warranting further in vivo investigation. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) exhibits profound molecular heterogeneity, complicating prognosis and therapy [1, 2]. e cellular composition and functional state of the tumor microenvironment (TME), particularly immune cell infiltration and matrix remodeling, are core factors determining HCC progression and treatment response [3, 4]. Chemokines and their receptor networks are the core communication system coordinating the directional migration, spatial localization, and functional polarization of various cell types (including lymphocytes, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, etc.) within the TME [5]. Different chemokine expression profiles can precisely depict unique immune contexts, CXCL9/10-enriched subtypes exhibit T-cell exhaustion through PD-L1 upregulation, whereas CCL20/CXCL8-dominant clusters promote fibrotic niche formation via IL6 and VEGFA signaling, indicating dual chemokine axes involved in HCC pathogenesis [6, 7]. We hypothesized that chemokine-based subtyping could identify patient subgroups with distinct biological behaviors and serve as a discovery platform to pinpoint critical oncogenic drivers linked to aggressive immune-microenvironment contexts. Among genes upregulated in aggressive HCC subtypes, Immediate Early Response 3 (IER3) emerged as a top candidate.

IER3, a stress-responsive nuclear protein, modulates apoptosis and proliferation in various cancers but lacks defined transcriptional partners or mechanisms [8]. Its chromatin-binding properties suggest it may function as a transcriptional co-regulator. Regulatory Factor X5 (RFX5), traditionally known for its role in MHC class II gene regulation, has also been implicated in metabolic reprogramming through interaction with PGC-1α [9, 10]. However, its collaboration with stress-responsive cofactors in HCC remains unexplored. IER3’s chromatin-binding properties suggest it may function as a co-regulator with DNA-binding transcription factors such as RFX5 [11]. The inactivation of the p53 tumor suppressor pathway is a central event in HCC pathogenesis. We observed that knockdown of IER3 significantly upregulates p53 mRNA levels, indicating that IER3 may negatively regulate p53 transcriptional activity. AKR1B10 is an aldehyde-ketone reductase that can inhibit p53 transcription, thereby promoting tumor development [12, 13].

Building on these insights, we hypothesized that IER3 cooperates with RFX5 to transactivate AKR1B10 expression, thereby suppressing p53-mediated tumor suppression and driving HCC progression. Through integrated multi-omics profiling, functional genomics, and clinical validation, this study systematically deciphers chemokine-defined molecular subtypes with prognostic relevance, identifies IER3 as a critical driver of aggressive HCC phenotypes, mechanistically resolves the IER3-RFX5-AKR1B10-p53 transcriptional axis, and establishes a mechanistic rationale for targeting this axis in HCC, providing critical insights into both the molecular pathogenesis and potential therapeutic strategies for liver cancer.

Materials and methods

Clinical cohorts and multi-omics data acquisition

Transcriptomic data and clinical annotations for 374 HCC patients were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA-LIHC, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). An independent validation cohort (GSE14520, n = 242) was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (Accession: GSE14520, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Chemokine-related genes were curated from Reactome (R-HSA-380108) and MSigDB (C5: GOBP). Bulk RNA-seq raw counts were normalized using DESeq2 (v1.34.0). To remove potential batch effects from multi-center data, we applied the ComBat algorithm implemented in the sva package (v3.44.0) in R. The batch correction was performed with parametric adjustment mode, using the “batch” variable (derived from the original data source: TCGA vs. GSE14520) as the only covariate. Empirical Bayes estimates were used to adjust for batch effects while preserving biological variation. Batch effect removal was validated by principal component analysis (PCA) before and after correction, showing improved mixing of batches in the first two principal components (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Additionally, we performed a κ-nearest neighbor (KNN) batch effect test (k = 5, 100 permutations), which confirmed a significant reduction in batch separation after correction (p < 0.001 for pre-correction batch association vs. p = 0.32 post-correction).

Molecular subtyping and functional enrichment

Consensus clustering was performed using ConsensusClusterPlus (v1.62.0) with Euclidean distance and Ward’s linkage, iterated 1,000 times for k = 2–5. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified via DESeq2 (|log2FC| >1, FDR < 0.05). GO/KEGG enrichment used clusterProfiler (v4.4.4) with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Gene-pathway networks were visualized in Cytoscape (v3.9.1) with edge-weighted force-directed layout.

Prognostic modeling and immune microenvironment

Univariate Cox regression (survival v3.3-1) screened survival-associated genes (p < 0.05). Immune cell infiltration was quantified via CIBERSORTx (LM22 signature, 1,000 permutations). Treg infiltration differences were validated by multiplex IHC (CD4 + FOXP3+ cells/mm²) using Vectra Polaris (PerkinElmer). PD-L1 expression was assessed via H-score (0–300) on archival FFPE sections.

Cell culture and reagents

The human hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell line SMMC7721, Huh7and normal hepatocyte cell line LO2 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. SMMC-7721 cells were selected for this study as they are known to express wild-type p53 [14, 15], allowing us to investigate the regulatory mechanisms of p53 transcriptional suppression rather than mutational inactivation. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays

For Co-IP experiments, SMMC7721 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime) containing protease inhibitors (Roche). Cell lysates were incubated with anti-RFX5 antibody (Abcam, ab9255) or IgG control (Santa Cruz, sc-2027) overnight at 4 °C, followed by protein A/G agarose beads (Thermo Fisher) for 2 h. Beads were washed three times with lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted with 2× SDS loading buffer. Immunoprecipitated proteins were analyzed by Western blot using anti-IER3 antibody (Abcam, ab65152).

GST pull-down assays

Recombinant GST-RFX5 protein was expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) and purified using glutathione-Sepharose 4B beads (GE Healthcare). HCC cell lysates were incubated with GST-RFX5 or GST control for 4 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed extensively, and bound proteins were analyzed by Western blot using anti-IER3 antibody.

RNA interference and overexpression

IER3, RFX5, and AKR1B10 knockdown was performed using siRNA oligonucleotides synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The target sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. A non-targeting scrambled siRNA (siNC) was used as negative control. Cells were transfected with 50 nM siRNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qPCR and Western blot at 48 h post-transfection.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara). qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio 6 Flex system (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche). Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and probed with primary antibodies against IER3, RFX5, p53, AKR1B10, and GAPDH (detailed information including supplier, catalog number, clone ID, and dilution is provided in Supplementary Table S2). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech) were used, and signals were detected using ECL substrate (Millipore).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

ChIP assays were performed using the Magna ChIP Kit (Millipore). SMMC7721 cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde, and chromatin was sheared by sonication. Lysates were immunoprecipitated with anti-RFX5 or anti-IER3 antibodies. Precipitated DNA was analyzed by qPCR using primers specific to the AKR1B10 promoter region (−832/−745 bp). The primer sequences were: Forward: 5’-TGAGGCAGGAGAATTGCTTGA-3’, Reverse: 5’-CTCGCTCTGTCGCCCAG-3’. Negative Control Primers (Targeting Exon 5, non-promoter): Forward: 5’- GATGCAGGATATCGGCACAT-3’, Reverse: 5’- TTCTCCTTGATGGCTTCCCC-3’.

These primers amplify a 112 bp fragment spanning the predicted RFX5 binding site. Primer specificity was confirmed by melting curve analysis and agarose gel electrophoresis. qPCR conditions: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s. Enrichment was calculated as percentage of input DNA using the 2^(-ΔCt) method.

Dual luciferase reporter assays

The AKR1B10 promoter region (−1500/+100 bp) was cloned into the pGL3-Basic vector (Promega). SMMC7721 cells were co-transfected with the reporter plasmid and pRL-TK (Renilla luciferase control) using Lipofectamine 3000. Luciferase activity was measured 48 h post-transfection using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega).

Cell proliferation and apoptosis assays

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Dojindo). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Apoptosis was analyzed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences) and flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II). For apoptosis analysis, cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection, washed with cold PBS, and stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI according to the manufacturer’s protocol (BD Biosciences). Flow cytometry was performed on a BD FACSCanto II system, and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8). The gating strategy was as follows: cells were first gated on FSC-A/SSC-A to exclude debris, then on FSC-H/FSC-A to exclude doublets. Quadrant gates were set based on unstained and single-stained controls. Apoptotic cells were quantified as the percentage of Annexin V-positive cells (early apoptotic: Annexin V+/PI-; late apoptotic: Annexin V+/PI+). All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates. For colony formation assays, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well and cultured for 10–14 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and manually counted under a light microscope. Only colonies containing > 50 cells were scored. Data are presented as mean colony counts ± SD from three independent experiments, each performed in duplicate wells.

Migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion were evaluated using Transwell chambers (Corning) with or without Matrigel coating. Cells were seeded in serum-free medium, and migrated/invaded cells were stained with crystal violet and quantified.

Clinical specimen analysis

Forty-eight HCC tissue samples and paired adjacent normal tissues were obtained from Guilin Fanpu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. with informed consent and ethical approval (No. Fanpu 2018-23). IER3, RFX5, and AKR1B10 expression levels were analyzed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and qPCR.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc.). Comparisons between two groups were assessed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences were evaluated by the log-rank test, with hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated using a univariate Cox proportional hazards model. Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients were used to assess correlations between continuous variables. Exact p-values are reported in the figures and text. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Chemokine-based molecular subtyping reveals immune-ECM remodeling duality in HCC

Based on the ensemble analysis of the TCGA-HCC and GSE14520 datasets, after batch correction, PCA showed that batch effects were eliminated (Figure S1A). Through chemokine expression profile clustering, HCC was divided into two subtypes (Cluster 1/2) (Figure S1B). Differential expression analysis (|logFC| >1, p < 0.05) identified 103 significant genes, including 72 upregulated genes in Cluster 1 (CXCL9, CXCL10) and 31 elevated in Cluster 2 (CCL20, CXCL8) (Fig. 1A). GO analysis demonstrated Cluster 1 enrichment in leukocyte chemotaxis and interferon-γ response, while Cluster 2 downregulated extracellular matrix organization pathways (Fig. 1B). KEGG pathway analysis further revealed Cluster 1 dominance in cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions versus Cluster 2 enrichment in oncogenic viral infection pathways (Fig. 1C). Network analysis identified two functional hubs: CXCL9/10-driven immune activation via MHC II complexes (HLA-DRA/DRB1), and CCL20/CXCL8-mediated profibrotic signaling through VEGFA/IL6 axes (Fig. 1D). In summary, based on chemokine expression, we established two basic biological subtypes (1: immune activity; 2: matrix remodeling). Next, we sought to determine whether these subtypes and their associated genes could improve patient prognosis.

Prognostic clustering identifies an aggressive subtype enriched with C2 features and driven by IER3

To translate the biological subtypes into a prognostic framework, we first identified survival-associated genes among those differentially expressed between Clusters Cluster 1/2 and from known HCC prognostic signatures. Univariate Cox regression pinpointed 21 such genes (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Consensus clustering stratified HCC patients into three subtypes (A-C) with distinct outcomes: Subtype C showed worst prognosis (median OS 3.6 years vs. 6.8 years in Subtype A, HR = 3.78, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B-C). Differential expression analysis revealed IER3 as the most upregulated gene in Subtype C, along with S100A9 and MSC (Fig. 2D). Subtype C correlated with advanced TNM staging (72% T3-T4) and immunosuppressive TME (2.8-fold Treg infiltration increase) (Fig. 2E). Seven candidate genes (IER3, S100A9, SPP1, etc.) showed strong positive correlation with PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway activity (r = 0.69, p < 0.001) and PD-L1 expression (r = 0.53, p = 0.008). Given that IER3 was the most significantly upregulated gene in the immunosuppressive and aggressive Subtype C (Fig. 2D, E), and its reported roles in stress response and potential immune modulation, it was selected as the primary candidate for subsequent functional and mechanistic investigation.

IER3 is highly expressed in hepatocellular carcinoma and promotes oncogenic phenotypes in vitro

Screening of seven candidate genes by qPCR revealed distinct expression patterns in HCC cell lines. Among them, IER3 was consistently and significantly upregulated across all tested cell lines (Fig. 3A-G). In SMMC-7721 cells, IER3 mRNA level was increased by 33.8-fold (95% CI: 4.1–6.3) with p = 0.0012 (unpaired t-test). To evaluate the clinical relevance of IER3, immunohistochemical analysis was performed on a tissue microarray comprising 48 paired HCC and adjacent non-tumor tissues, with one normal liver sample as an internal control. IER3 protein expression was significantly elevated in HCC tissues compared to matched paracancerous tissues, and higher IER3 levels were associated with advanced histological grade (Fig. 3H-J). Functional investigations were conducted using siRNA-mediated knockdown of IER3. The functional role of IER3 in supporting HCC cell phenotypes associated with tumor progression was confirmed in multiple HCC cell lines: In SMMC-7721 cells, which efficiently reduced its mRNA expression (Fig. 4A), loss of IER3 significantly suppressed cell proliferation (CCK-8 assay, Fig. 4B) and markedly impaired colony-forming ability (p < 0.05; Fig. 4C-D). Furthermore, IER3 knockdown induced cell cycle arrest and promoted apoptosis, as demonstrated by flow cytometry (Fig. 4E-F). The role of IER3 in HCC cell motility was also examined. Silencing IER3 strongly inhibited cell migration in wound healing assays (Fig. 5A) and significantly reduced both migratory and invasive capacities in Transwell assays (p < 0.01; Fig. 5B-C). Consistently, in Huh7 cells, IER3 knockdown similarly inhibited proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion, and promoted apoptosis (Supplementary Figs. S2-S3).

IER3/RFX5 complex promotes HCC via AKR1B10-mediated p53 suppression

To elucidate the transcriptional mechanism underlying IER3’s oncogenic function, we investigated its interaction with the transcription factor RFX5. Dual-luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that IER3 is required for RFX5-activated transcription, supporting its role as a transcriptional coactivator (Fig. 6A). This physical interaction was confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation, which showed reciprocal pulldown of endogenous IER3 and RFX5 proteins (Fig. 6B).

We next identified Aldo-Keto Reductase Family 1 Member B10 (AKR1B10) as a key downstream target of this complex. Knockdown of either IER3 or RFX5 significantly reduced both mRNA and protein levels of AKR1B10, whereas their co-expression produced a synergistic upregulation (Fig. 6C-I). Notably, IER3 knockdown did not affect RFX5 protein stability (Fig. 6D), reinforcing its function as a coactivator rather than a stability regulator.

Downstream functional studies established a critical role for AKR1B10 in negatively regulating the tumor suppressor p53. Knockdown of AKR1B10 significantly increased p53 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels, while its overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 7A-E). Rescue experiments confirmed the linearity of this regulatory axis: the suppression of p53 observed upon IER3 overexpression was effectively reversed by concomitant AKR1B10 knockdown. Similarly, reintroduction of AKR1B10 in RFX5-overexpressing cells restored p53 to baseline levels (Fig. 7F). These findings collectively demonstrate that the IER3/RFX5 transcriptional complex promotes HCC progression, at least in part, by upregulating AKR1B10 to suppress p53 expression. To further strengthen the clinical relevance of our findings, we analyzed the TCGA-LIHC cohort (PanCancer Atlas). Single-gene survival analysis revealed that high expression of IER3, RFX5, or AKR1B10 was each significantly associated with poorer overall survival (Supplementary Fig. S4A-C). Moreover, a composite risk score integrating all three genes demonstrated the strongest prognostic stratification, with patients in the high-risk group exhibiting markedly shorter survival (p = 0.00074; Supplementary Fig. S4D). These clinical observations are consistent with our in vitro mechanistic findings and support the potential utility of the IER3/RFX5/AKR1B10 axis as a prognostic signature in HCC.

Discussion

Our study identifies a previously uncharacterized oncogenic axis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) wherein IER3 functions as a transcriptional coactivator of RFX5, driving oncogenic cellular phenotypes in vitro via AKR1B10-mediated suppression of p53. These findings suggest a mechanism that may contribute to tumor progression in vivo, though this remains to be experimentally validated. This mechanistic insight emerges from a comprehensive integration of molecular subtyping, clinical cohort analysis, and functional validation in vitro, offering a new framework for understanding immune-escape and metabolic reprogramming in aggressive HCC subtypes.

Through chemokine-driven molecular subtyping, we identified two biologically distinct clusters. The rationale for utilizing chemokines as the basis for stratification, rather than broader transcriptomic signatures, lies in their role as primary regulators of the immune landscape. Our results confirm that this chemokine-centric approach effectively captures the complexity of the HCC microenvironment: Cluster 1, enriched in CXCL9/10, reflects an immune-hot phenotype driven by T-cell recruitment, whereas Cluster 2, dominated by CCL20/CXCL8, represents a matrix-remodeling phenotype. This validates that chemokine profiles can serve as robust proxies for broader immune and stromal states while offering specific mechanistic targets for intervention [16, 17].

IER3 has been implicated in the depletion of T cells, enhancing the immune evasion capabilities of tumors and further promoting tumorigenesis [18]. Functionally, IER3 silencing impaired HCC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and enhanced apoptosis, underscoring its role as a driver of malignant progression. These findings align with previous reports of IER3’s anti-apoptotic function in other cancer types through modulation of MAPK/ERK and NF-κB signaling [8, 19]. Notably, we identified a direct physical and functional interaction between IER3 and RFX5, which are co-enriched in HCC tissues and function synergistically to drive transcriptional activation of AKR1B10—a gene with established roles in metabolic reprogramming, chemoresistance, and immune suppression [20, 21]. Co-immunoprecipitation and dual-luciferase assays confirmed that IER3 acts as a coactivator rather than a stabilizer of RFX5, enhancing its transcriptional output without affecting RFX5 protein abundance. These results expand the functional repertoire of RFX5, traditionally associated with MHC II gene regulation, into a broader oncogenic context involving metabolic modulation and p53 repression.

The downstream effects of the IER3–RFX5–AKR1B10 axis are particularly striking. AKR1B10 knockdown restored p53 expression at both transcript and protein levels, while its overexpression abrogated p53 function. Rescue experiments demonstrated that the p53-suppressive effects of IER3 and RFX5 were dependent on intact AKR1B10, establishing this pathway as a coherent and targetable transcriptional circuit. These findings provide a molecular explanation for how inflammation-induced transcriptional rewiring in HCC can lead to p53 inactivation in the absence of TP53 mutation, a phenomenon frequently observed in HBV-related liver cancer [22, 23]. It is important to clarify that while our data demonstrate AKR1B10 modulates p53 expression at both mRNA and protein levels, they do not establish AKR1B10 as a direct transcriptional repressor of the p53 gene. The observed increase in p53 mRNA upon AKR1B10 knockdown could result from indirect mechanisms, such as altered p53 mRNA stability, regulation by upstream transcription factors, or feedback loops involving p53 target genes. Elucidating the precise molecular mechanism by which AKR1B10 influences p53 expression—whether direct or indirect—will require additional biochemical and chromatin-based studies. It is important to note that the detailed mechanistic dissection of the IER3/RFX5/AKR1B10/p53 axis was primarily established in SMMC-7721 cells, which harbor wild-type TP53. While key phenotypic observations (proliferation, migration, apoptosis) were validated in Huh7 cells (which carry mutant p53), the specific transcriptional regulatory mechanism—wherein IER3 functions as an RFX5 coactivator to upregulate AKR1B10 and subsequently suppress p53—was delineated in a wild-type p53 context. This suggests that the axis represents a non-mutational mechanism for inactivating functional p53, and may be particularly relevant in the 30–40% of HCCs that retain wild-type TP53. However, the extent to which this mechanism operates in p53-mutant HCC cells, or whether alternative downstream effectors are engaged in that context, remains to be fully elucidated. Future studies investigating the correlation between IER3/RFX5/AKR1B10 axis activation and TP53 mutation status in larger clinical cohorts will further refine its prognostic and therapeutic significance.

Clinically, this axis offers intriguing therapeutic potential. The IER3high/RFX5nuclear+/AKR1B10high signature showed a correlation with patient outcomes in our exploratory cohort, suggesting that it may be a relevant pathway in aggressive tumors, particularly those that are immune-cold and Treg-enriched. Given that AKR1B10 expression correlates with immune checkpoint molecules, such as PD-L1 [12], this axis may also represent a rational candidate for future studies investigating predictors of response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) or MDM2 inhibitors. However, we emphasize that these clinical observations are preliminary and require rigorous validation in large, independent, and prospectively designed cohorts.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while we have validated the oncogenic functions of IER3 across multiple HCC cell lines (SMMC-7721 and Huh7) and established a detailed mechanistic axis, the current study lacks in vivo validation using animal models. Subcutaneous and orthotopic xenograft experiments, as well as metastatic colonization assays, are essential to definitively establish the tumor-promoting role of the IER3/RFX5/AKR1B10 axis in a physiologically relevant context. Therefore, while our in vitro and bioinformatic analyses strongly suggest a tumor-promoting function, conclusions regarding its direct impact on tumor growth and metastasis in living organisms should be considered preliminary until confirmed by future in vivo studies. Second, although we demonstrate that the IER3/RFX5 complex directly binds to the AKR1B10 promoter and activates its transcription, the precise chromatin remodeling events or additional co-factors involved in this process remain to be fully elucidated. Third, the clinical correlation analysis, while supportive, is based on bulk transcriptomic data; spatial transcriptomics or multiplex IHC would provide higher-resolution insight into the cellular localization of this axis within the tumor microenvironment. Addressing these limitations in future studies will further solidify the translational potential of targeting this pathway in HCC. Finally, while our bioinformatic analysis linked IER3 to an immunosuppressive TME, the current study lacks direct experimental validation of its immunomodulatory functions. Investigating how the IER3/RFX5/AKR1B10 axis influences the composition and function of immune cells within the HCC microenvironment is a crucial avenue for future research that will be addressed in subsequent in vivo studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals a novel transcriptional axis linking IER3 and RFX5 to AKR1B10-dependent suppression of wild-type p53, which in vitro drives cellular programs associated with malignancy in a subset of aggressive HCCs that retain functional p53. These insights advance our understanding of liver cancer biology and suggest that strategies targeting this pathway may hold promise, though future in vivo studies are necessary to establish its therapeutic relevance. These insights not only advance our understanding of liver cancer biology but also suggests that strategies targeting this pathway may hold promise to counter resistance and improve therapeutic outcomes, particularly in tumors where p53 is inactivated through non-mutational mechanisms.
