Comparison of a Risk Calculator With Frailty Indices in Patients Undergoing Lung Cancer Resection
Authors: Dominic J. Vitello, Charles D. Logan, Norah N. Zaza, Kelly R. Bates, Ryan Jacobs, Joseph Feinglass, Ryan P. Merkow, David J. Bentrem
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionWhile frailty has gained attention for its utility in risk stratification, no studies have directly compared them to existing risk calculators. The objective of this study was to compare the risk stratification of the American College of Surgeons Surgical Risk Calculator (ACS‐SRC), the Revised Risk Analysis Index (RAI‐rev), and the Modified Frailty Index (5‐mFI). The primary outcomes were 30‐day postoperative morbidity, 30‐day postoperative mortality, unplanned readmission, unplanned reoperation, and discharge disposition other than home.MethodsPatients undergoing anatomic lung resection for primary, nonsmall cell lung cancer were identified within the ACS National Quality Improvement Program (ACS NSQIP) database. Tools were compared for discrimination in the primary outcomes.Results9663 patients undergoing anatomic lung resection for cancer between 2012 and 2014 were included. The cohort was 53.1% female. Median age at diagnosis was 67 (IQR 59–74) years. Perioperative morbidity and mortality rates were 10.9% (n = 1048) and 1.6% (n = 158). Rates of 30‐day postoperative unplanned readmission and reoperation were 7.5% (n = 725) and 4.8% (n = 468). The ACS‐SRC had the highest discrimination for all measured outcomes, as measured by the area under the receiver operating curve (AUC) and corresponding confidence interval (95% CI). This included perioperative mortality (AUC 0.74, 95% CI 0.71–0.78), compared to RAI‐rev (AUC 0.66, 95% CI 0.62–0.69) and 5‐mFI (AUC 0.61, 95% CI 0.57–0.65; p < 0.001). The RAI‐rev and 5‐mFI had similar discrimination for all measured outcomes.ConclusionACS‐SRC was the perioperative risk stratification tool with the highest predictive discrimination for adverse, 30‐day, postoperative events for patients with cancer treated with anatomic lung resection.

Introduction

Risk stratification is an essential component of preoperative planning for cancer surgery and is fundamental in deciding which treatment options are offered to which patients. The knowledge of a patient's risks may be the difference between choosing curative cancer surgery or nonsurgical alternatives. Surgeons and other proceduralists often rely on clinical training, experience, and judgment to estimate a patient's risk. Though clinical judgment is key and reasonably accurate, it remains a subjective instrument, vulnerable to bias [1]. Age is a canonical example of a method by which clinicians estimate risk; however, a patient's so‐called “physiologic age” is often viewed as a more valuable parameter [2, 3, 4]. The prospect of assessing physiologic age, and relatedly objectifying perioperative risk estimation, has led to the development of various clinical tools. Among these tools are risk calculators and frailty indices. Though numerous in all, the number of calculators that are freely accessible, utilize routinely available perioperative patient data, and accurately assess risk are relatively few. Notable among these is the American College of Surgeons Surgical Risk Calculator (ACS‐SRC) [5]. The ACS‐SRC is able to provide estimates for a variety of 30‐day postoperative outcomes, in addition to composite 30‐day postoperative morbidity and mortality [6]. This information is especially important for goals of care in complex cases, such as patients with advanced age, comorbid conditions, and cancer [5, 7].

Convergently, frailty has been introduced as an additional element in the domain of perioperative risk stratification. Frailty was initially developed by physicians treating geriatric patients and purposed for predicting an increased risk of mortality in 6 months to 5 years [8, 9]. This concept has been adapted to the perioperative setting, with the subsequent development of numerous frailty indices. One study identified 67 frailty instruments within the literature [10]. These available indices are heterogeneous and carry limitations particularly for patients diagnosed with cancer, which by itself does not confer frailty [9, 10, 11]. Among those that have been validated for surgical populations, generally, are the risk analysis index‐revised (RAI‐rev) and the 5‐factor modified frailty index (5‐mFI).

Further investigation is needed to compare the predictive ability of frailty indices in many surgical patient subpopulations [12, 13]. The majority of studies to date have only been single armed, demonstrating the predictive ability of a single index, or its most recent iteration, in a wide array of surgical patients [9, 11, 12, 14, 15]. There also have been studies that have shown reduced predictive ability in certain patient populations or for certain surgical procedures [16, 17]. Despite these limitations, frailty assessment for all surgical patients is being incorporated by some health systems. The objective of this study was to compare predictive ability of the RAI‐rev, 5‐mFI, and the ACS‐SRC for discrimination in perioperative adverse outcomes.

Methods

Data Source and Population

The study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board. Before conducting the study, the STROBE guidelines were reviewed to ensure that proper reporting practices would be observed [18]. This was a retrospective cohort study using the ACS NSQIP database to compare the discrimination of the RAV‐rev, 5‐mFI, and ACS‐SRC. The ACS NSQIP database is a large, prospectively maintained, national registry of operative cases performed at participating hospitals. Trained individuals collect over 150 variables from a sample of patient records including demographics, preoperative comorbidities, operative characteristics, and postoperative events. Audits occur regularly to verify data integrity and reliability. Patients were identified within the 2012, 2013, and 2014 ACS NSQIP files by previously reported ICD‐9, ICD‐10, and CPT codes corresponding to anatomic lung resection for non‐small cell lung cancer [7, 19, 20, 21]. Starting on January 1, 2015, variables within the ACS NSQIP database necessary for calculating the RAI‐rev were removed. Therefore, the years used in this study represent the most contemporary data available for calculating the RAI‐rev and for comparing it to other methods of risk stratification in this population.

Patient Characteristics

Demographic information including patient age, sex, race and ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), functional status, smoking status, and preoperative comorbidities were collected from the ACS NSQIP database. Race and ethnicity were analyzed as reported in the database, categorized as American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian American or Pacific Islander, African American, non‐Hispanic White, and unknown or not reported. Ethnicity was categorized as Hispanic or non‐Hispanic. Outcomes of interest collected were 30‐day postoperative composite morbidity, 30‐day postoperative mortality, 30‐day postoperative readmission, and 30‐day postoperative reoperation. Discharge disposition other than home was also collected.

Stratification Tool Implementation

American College of Surgeons Surgical Risk Calculator

For the study years, the ACS‐SRC included the following variables: procedure, age, sex, functional status, emergency case status, ASA class, steroid use, ascites within 30 days, sepsis within 48 h, ventilator dependency, disseminated cancer, diabetes mellitus, hypertension requiring medication, congestive heart failure, dyspnea, smoking within 1 year, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease requiring dialysis, acute renal failure, and patient BMI. The probability of 30‐day postoperative composite morbidity and 30‐day postoperative mortality are calculated by the ACS using a regression‐based model and stored within the database as the MORBPROB and MORTPROB fields respectively. Patients with a missing MORBPROB or MORTPROB or both were excluded from the analysis.

Frailty Indices

The RAI‐rev is scored on a weighted scale based on responses to 14 survey items including age, sex, weight loss, loss of appetite, chronic kidney disease, congestive heart failure, shortness of breath, living arrangement, activities of daily living, and presence of malignancy. The 5‐mFI is scored based on hypertension requiring medication, diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or a diagnosis of pneumonia within 30 days, functional status, and congestive heart failure. Fields within the database were used to calculate the RAI‐rev and 5‐mFI indices for each included patient. Patients with a missing RAI‐rev of 5‐mFI or both were excluded from the analysis.

Outcomes

Fields within the ACS NSQIP database pertaining to 30‐day postoperative complications were collected. This included complications such as surgical site infection, pneumonia, unplanned intubation, pulmonary embolism, unplanned readmission, unplanned reoperation, and death among others. Discharge destination was also collected. The outcomes of interest were 30‐day postoperative composite morbidity, 30‐day postoperative mortality, 30‐day postoperative unplanned readmission, 30‐day postoperative unplanned reoperation, and discharge disposition other than home. The outcomes were considered binary and modeled as such. Each outcome corresponds directly to a field within the ACS NSQIP database with the exception of 30‐day postoperative composite morbidity. For this study, 30‐day postoperative composite morbidity was defined as the presence of any complication contained within the database, exclusive of the other outcomes of interest. Patients with missing outcomes of interest were excluded from respective comparative analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Logistic regression was used to model the prediction of the outcomes of interest by the ACS‐SRC, RAI‐rev, and 5‐mFI. When modeling mortality, the covariate for the ACS‐SRC was the MORTPROB field. When modeling all other outcomes of interest, the covariate for the ACS‐SRC was the MORBPROB field. The frailty indices were used in a similar fashion to model the outcomes of interest. The discrimination threshold for each risk stratification tool was varied. In doing so, the true positive rate of the risk stratification tool in predicting the recorded outcome in the ACS NSQIP database was plotted as a function of the false positive rate to generate a receiver operating curve. For each risk stratification tool and each outcome, the area under the receiver operating curve (AUC) with a corresponding 95% confidence interval (95% CI) was taken to determine each tool's discrimination. The resultant AUCs were compared using DeLong's test. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.01. All analyses were performed with Stata SE Version 18.0, College Station, TX.

Results

Characteristics of the National Patient Cohort

There were 9718 patients identified within the database as having undergone anatomic lung resection for non‐small cell lung cancer. After excluding patients with incomplete frailty scores (n = 55, 0.57%), 9663 patients were included in the final analyses (Table 1). The cohort was 53.14% female with a median age at diagnosis of 67 (interquartile range [IQR] 59–74) years. Among the cohort, 81.4% were non‐Hispanic White, 6.25% were non‐Hispanic Black, 3.5% were Asian American or Pacific Islander, 3.5% were Hispanic, and 0.3% were Native American or Alaska Native. Smoking within 1 year of surgery was reported by 33.4% of patients. By year, 29.6% were diagnosed in 2012, 33.2% in 2013, and 37.2% in 2014. Cardiothoracic surgeons performed 89.0% and general surgeons performed 11.0% of the operations (Table 1).

Risk Stratification Tools and Outcomes of Interest

Median (IQR) morbidity and mortality rates predicted by the ASC‐SRC were 10.0% (7.46–13.57) and 0.82% (0.42–1.52) respectively. The median (IQR) RAI‐rev score and 5‐mFI scores were 23 (19–27) and 0.20 (0.00–0.40) respectively. Observed 30‐day postoperative morbidity and mortality rates were 10.9% (n = 1048) and 1.6% (n = 158), respectively. Within 30 days of the index operation, 7.5% (n = 725) of patients had unplanned readmissions and 4.8% (n = 468) had unplanned reoperations. The postoperative discharge disposition was not to home for 8.1% of patients (Table 2). By categorization based on the most recent calibration of the RAI‐rev, most patients had an RAI‐rev score of less than 25 (6411, 61.0%) (Table 3). With the exception of frailty scores 25–29, each other risk category within the RAI‐rev more frequently predicted a higher mortality than the ACS‐SRC (Table 3). Discussion

It was found that the ACS‐SRC outperformed both the RAI and 5‐mFI in discrimination of all outcomes of interest for patients undergoing anatomic lung resection for primary nonsmall cell lung cancer. Numerous studies report the value of these indices, particularly the RAI‐rev. The body of literature surrounding frailty and perioperative risk stratification has led to calls for the expansion of its use, even to make frailty assessment universal [22]. However, frailty indices, such as the RAI‐rev, have limitations for patients with cancer by overweighing the contribution of cancer to the assessment of frailty and thereby overestimating perioperative risk for cancer surgery.

The results show a propensity by the RAI‐rev to provide high relative estimates for postoperative adverse outcomes. This is seen in Table 3, where the predicted mortality by the RAI‐rev and the ACS‐SRC were compared. The rows represent the different categories of predicted mortality by frailty score range based on the most recent calibration of the RAI‐rev [11]. The columns “RAI‐rev > ACS‐SRC” and “RAI‐rev < ACS‐SRC” illustrate the proportions of patients in each frailty category that had RAI‐rev‐predicted mortalities that were higher or lower than the ACS‐SRC respectively. For all frailty categories, the RAI‐rev estimated a higher mortality probability than the ACS‐SRC, except for patients with scores 25–29. To convey this visually, predicted mortality by the ACS‐SRC and RAI‐rev were plotted on the left and right of Figure 1, connected by a thin line to form a paired plot. The large number of up‐sloping lines emphasizes the tendency for the RAI‐rev to predict higher risks.

The RAI‐rev was developed as an adaptation of the Minimum Data Set Mortality Risk Index‐Revised [9]. To attempt to account for the influence of malignancy on physiologic age, the RAI‐rev considers active cancer or a history of cancer as a deficit, reflected as an increase in a patient's frailty score. The cancer scores for the RAI‐rev were created by regression analyses from a large database of patients in the Veterans Affairs Nebraska‐Western Iowa system. Patients with surrogate codes for a diagnosis or history of cancer were analyzed. These surrogate diagnoses identified specifically patients with disseminated cancer, patients receiving neoadjuvant radiotherapy, and patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy [9, 12]. Unfortunately, these codes would not appear to have strong positive selection for a generalizable, surgical oncology population. The codes seem to target a subset of patients with the most advanced disease. Patients who would be treated with adjuvant therapy, have localized cancer, or are in posttreatment surveillance seem not to have been included in the regression analysis used to create the RAI‐rev [9]. In all, the RAI‐rev score adjustments for cancer were not developed based on the average surgical cancer patient. As a result, the RAI‐rev overestimates postoperative risks for surgical cancer patients. Future improvements to the RAI‐rev's performance in this setting may include recalibration based on a more heterogeneous surgical cancer patient population.

The 5‐mFI was, similarly, derived from a previously existing index. In this case, the original index was the 70‐item Canada Study of Health and Aging Frailty Index. This index was used to develop the 11‐factor modified Frailty Index. However, due to changes in available variables in large, prospectively maintained databases and ensuing challenges with validation, the 5‐mFI was created as an adapted form of the 11‐factor modified Frailty Index. The 5‐mFI was then validated using the ACS NSQIP database [14]. The 5‐mFI does not specifically address the issue of a current or prior history of cancer. However, the 5‐mFI performed relatively similarly to the RAI‐rev. This is demonstrated in the AUCs summarized in Table 4. The table shows statistically significantly superior discrimination by the ACS‐SRC. For this reason, if preoperative risk stratification is desired for lung cancer patients, the ASC‐SRC should be used in favor of frailty indices based on discrimination alone [23, 24].

Limitations

This study should be interpreted in the context of its limitations. Limitations of data inaccuracy exist as they do for any database. As these data are prospectively maintained by trained abstractors, however, some of the ascertainment bias may be mitigated.

While this study has demonstrated superior discrimination of the ACS‐SRC for these 30‐day, perioperative outcomes, it does not interface with the body of published work demonstrating frailty as a predictor of outcomes beyond the 30‐day, perioperative period. In the context of a perioperative risk stratification tool, this may be viewed as appropriate [7, 19, 25]. However, 90‐ and 180‐day outcomes are of reasonable interest. While the ACS‐SRC was designed for 30‐day outcomes, areas of future study may consider its comparison with frailty indices for these longer‐term outcomes.

Lastly, calendar years 2012, 2013, and 2014 were used for the analysis, the most recent years in which calculating the RAI‐rev using the methods in this study was possible. Based on the methods used in this study, the age of the data alone is unlikely to influence the conclusions. It should be noted that the ACS‐SRC has, itself, actually been improved in the interim. However, in this study, the predictive ability of the ACS‐SRC as it existed in those years, was compared to the most contemporary iterations of the RAI‐rev and the 5‐mFI. In all, were it possible to repeat this study with more recent data, the conclusions drawn from the analysis would be unlikely to change.

Conclusions

The ACS‐SRC had superior discrimination for all adverse postoperative outcomes of interest, including 30‐day morbidity, mortality, unplanned reoperation, unplanned readmission, and discharge disposition other than home compared with the RAI‐rev and 5‐mFI for patients with cancer treated with anatomic lung resection.

Synopsis

Frailty indices have been adapted for objective perioperative risk stratification, but their performance has not been compared to pre‐existing tools for cancer patients. In this retrospective cohort study of 9663 patients undergoing lung resection for cancer, the American College of Surgeons Surgical Risk Calculator had statistically significantly superior discrimination for all measured outcomes. Perioperative, objective risk stratification for lung cancer patients by the American College of Surgeons Surgical Risk Calculator is superior to commonly used frailty indices.
