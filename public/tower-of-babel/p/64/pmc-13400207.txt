TMC7 promotes colorectal cancer metastasis via inhibition of PKN2 and regulation of hypoxia-adaptive responses
Authors: Yun Zhu, Anni Luo, Xing Liu, Yongxin Zhang, Xiangzhao Li, Qingqing Zhang, Yi Lei, Yanqi Li, Yang Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•TMC7 is significantly upregulated in colorectal cancer, with the highest expression level in metastatic lesions of CRC.•High TMC7 expression predicts poor prognosis, severe liver metastasis and unsatisfactory immunotherapy/chemotherapy response in CRC.•A novel TMC7–PKN2–HIF1α axis drives CRC metastasis via hypoxia adaptation and Warburg effect-mediated metabolic reprogramming.•TMC7 inhibits PKN2 to facilitate HIF1α nuclear translocation and activate glycolysis, supporting CRC tumor survival.•TMC7 serves as a novel therapeutic target to block hypoxia-induced metastasis and metabolic plasticity in metastatic CRC.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading global malignancy [1]. Metastasis is common in CRC, with the liver being the most frequent target organ. Liver metastasis occurs in about 15% of patients with CRC at diagnosis and in 25–30% during treatment [2]. Despite receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radical surgery, approximately 40% of patients develop liver metastases [3]. Approximately 75% of patients with liver metastases are ineligible for resection. Moreover, these metastases exhibit resistance to chemotherapy, leading to therapeutic challenges. The 5-year survival rate under palliative care is only about 5% [2].

Metastatic colon cancer lesions display a significantly hypoxic microenvironment compared to primary tumors. Hypoxia drives tumor metastasis through multiple molecular pathways, including epigenetic transcriptional regulation, metabolic reprogramming, and stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) [4]. Notably, HIF-1α acts as a central regulatory factor, with increased expression observed in patients with metastatic CRC, where its overexpression is strongly associated with poor prognosis [5].

HIF-1α upregulates vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression, promoting tumor angiogenesis and thereby enhancing oxygen and nutrient supply [6]. Concurrently, it induces the expression of key glycolytic enzymes, significantly increases glucose uptake and glycolytic activity in tumor cells, and drives a metabolic shift toward the Warburg effect [7,8]. The resulting accumulation of lactate not only acidifies the tumor microenvironment and suppresses the anti-tumor functions of T cells and natural killer cells, but also promotes extracellular matrix degradation, facilitating tumor cell invasion and distant metastasis [9].

Transmembrane channel-like protein 7 (TMC7), a member of the transmembrane channel-like gene family, remains poorly characterized in tumorigenesis. Our previous study first identified TMC7 promotes proliferation and invasion of pancreatic tumor cells; and high TMC7 expression is associated with poor patient prognosis [10]. Protein kinase N2 (PKN2), a member of the protein kinase N family, plays a critical role in regulating various biological processes in tumor cells. Our previous studies have demonstrated that PKN2 inhibits the differentiation of tumor-associated macrophages into the M2 phenotype, and suppresses the in vivo proliferation of colon cancer cells [11]. Furthermore, PKN2 has been shown to reduce the nuclear accumulation and transcriptional activity of HIF1α, leading to the downregulation of its downstream target genes [12]. However, the role and clinical relevance of TMC7 in colon cancer remain unclear; moreover, its involvement with PKN2 in metastasis and in tumor cell adaptation to hypoxia requires elucidation.

In this study, we identify TMC7 as a novel promoter of CRC metastasis. We demonstrate that TMC7 drives the hypoxic adaptive response and the Warburg effect in CRC cells by regulating PKN2. These findings unveil a new mechanistic pathway for CRC progression and establish the TMC7/PKN2 axis as a promising therapeutic target to suppress metastasis.

Methods

Patients

We included specimens from 80 patients with CRC liver metastasis diagnosed and treated at Nanfang Hospital between June 2018 and February 2026, comprising primary tumor tissue, paired adjacent non-cancerous tissue, and liver metastatic tissue. Inclusion criteria comprised: histopathologically confirmed colorectal adenocarcinoma with liver metastasis; availability of all three types of tissue samples; and complete clinical follow-up and prognostic data. The median follow-up time for patients was 46 months. Among them, 27 patients received oxaliplatin-based neoadjuvant therapy prior to resection of the primary tumor, and 39 received neoadjuvant therapy before liver metastasis resection or biopsy. PET-CT scans were performed in 37 patients at the time of liver metastasis diagnosis, and their SUV values were included in the analysis. Baseline clinical characteristics of the patients are detailed in the Supplementary Table S1.

Isolation and culture of tumor-derived colorectal cancer cells

Human colorectal tumor specimens were obtained from patients undergoing resection or biopsy at Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University. All patients provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital.

Fresh tissues were rinsed with PBS, then minced into 1–2 mm³ fragments, and incubated in digestive enzyme solution for 1–3 h. After digestion, cells were gently pipetted, and filtered through a 70 μm strainer into a 50 mL Falcon tube. The suspension was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min, resuspended in complete medium, seeded into culture dishes, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After 24–48 h, non-adherent cells and debris were removed. Medium was replaced every 2–3 days. Subculture was performed at 80 - 90% confluency to obtain human tumor-derived colorectal cancer (HU-CRC) cells. The short tandem repeat profiling results of HU-CRC cells are provided in the Supplementary File S1.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded tissues from the patient were used for IHC analysis. IHC staining was performed as described [11]. Staining was independently assessed by two experienced pathologists. The score was based on the integrated staining intensity and the proportion of positive cells. Staining intensity was scored as follows: 0= no color; 1= yellow; 2 =light brown; and 3= dark brown. The proportion of positive tumor cells (number of positively labeled tumor cells / number of total tumor cells) was scored as follows: 0, positive cells <10%; 1, 10%- 40% positive cells; 2, 40%- 70% positive cells; and 3, positive cells >70%. The final score was determined by adding the staining intensity score and average proportion of positive cells score. All the percentages/numbers of positive cells were expressed as the average of six randomly selected microscopic fields. Anti-PKN2, anti-HIF1α (Abcam), and anti-TMC7 (Thermofisher) antibodies were used. Mouse IgG monoclonal-isotype control and rabbit IgG polyclonal-Isotype control (Abcam, Cell Signaling & Santa Cruz) were used as negative controls. Patients were divided into high and low TMC7 expression subgroups using the median IHC score as the cutoff value.

Cell culture and transfection

The human colon cancer cell lines HCT116 and SW480 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. Cells were transfected with plasmids or shRNA using the protocol described in our previous study [11,13]. Sequences of the shRNAs are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Cultures were maintained under standard aerobic conditions (AC) with 5% CO2, or separately cultured in an anaerobic chamber (ANC) gassed with a mixture of 85% N2, 10% H₂, and 5% CO2 to establish hypoxic experimental conditions. The HIF-1α inhibitor PX-478 (MCE) was used at a working concentration of 20 μM. For Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays, cells were treated for 24, 48, and 72 h. For transwell migration assays, the treatment duration was 48 h.

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from TMC7-overexpression (OE) and negative control (NC) HCT116 cells (n = 3) using TRIzol reagent (Thermofisher). RNA integrity was assessed with the Agilent 2000. mRNA was enriched using mRNA capture beads and fragmented by heat and metal ions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized with random hexamers, followed by second-strand synthesis. Double-stranded cDNA was purified with DNA Clean Beads, then subjected to end repair, poly A-tailing, and adapter ligation. Size selection was performed using DNA clean beads, followed by PCR amplification and purification to generate the final library. Insert size was checked on the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and concentration was quantified using the ABI StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR system. Libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina platform with pair end 150 bp reads. Clean reads were obtained after filtering, aligned to the reference genome, and used for gene expression quantification. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by statistical analysis, followed by Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis.

Immunoprecipitation

Total protein was isolated following the manufacturer's instructions. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Beijing). Western blot analysis and immunoprecipitation were conducted as previously reported [13]. Primary antibodies targeting TMC7 (Thermofisher) and flag (Cell Signaling) were used. The precipitated proteins were subjected to kinase activity assay.

The kinase activity assay

PKN2 activity was assessed using the Universal Kinase Activity Kit (R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer's instructions, as described previously [11]. KK-CROSSTIDE was used as the substrate and incubated with the kit reaction system at 37 °C for 10 min. Absorbance (OD) at 620 nm was measured for each group, and all OD values were corrected by subtracting the background signal from the negative control (detection buffer only). All assays were independently repeated three times.

Animal studies using a mouse model of colon cancer metastasis

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital. A lung metastasis model was developed in nude mice following a previously reported protocol [14]. Pretreated HU-CRC cells were injected into mice via the tail vein. After four weeks, part of the mice were euthanized. Lung tissues were serially sectioned and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining to evaluate metastatic nodules. The remaining mice were monitored to generate the survival curve.

Extracellular acidification rate measurement (ECAR)

Cells were seeded at 1 × 10⁴ cells/well in XFp plates, incubated overnight, and then switched to assay medium for 1 h at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator. Glucose (100 mM), oligomycin (10 μM), and 2-DG (500 mM) were loaded into ports A, B, and C of the XFp sensor cartridge, respectively. ECAR was measured using a Seahorse XFp Analyzer (Seahorse Biosciences).

Oxygen consumption rate measurement (OCR)

Cells were seeded in XFp culture plates and cultured overnight. The next day, cells were incubated with assay medium containing 2.0 mM L-glutamine, 10 mM glucose, and 1.0 mM sodium pyruvate for 1 h at 37 °C in a CO₂-free incubator. OCR was measured using an XF96 Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience) after sequential treatment with oligomycin (1 µM), FCCP (1 µM), and rotenone (1 µM).

Lactate production and glucose consumption assay

Lactic acid concentrations in culture supernatants were quantified with a commercial Lactate Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Glucose consumption in culture medium was determined using a Glucose Uptake Assay Kit (Abacm). All data were normalized to viable cell number, and all experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates.

Transwell

HCT116 and SW480 cells were treated as described and then seeded into Matrigel-coated Transwell inserts for a 24-hour incubation period. The Transwell invasion assay was carried out following the protocol. The number of cells that migrated through the membrane was subsequently quantified.

CCK-8 assay

Pretreated cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C under AC or ANC. CCK-8 reagent (DingGuo Bio) was then added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek).

qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from colon cancer cell lines treated with Trizol reagent. The qPCR was performed as previously described [11]. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S3. ΔCt values were normalized to the viable cell count of each corresponding sample prior to harvest. The data were normalized to the geometric mean of GAPDH and calculated as 2−ΔΔCT. All experiments were independently repeated three times.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining of pretreated cells was performed as described before [14]. Antibody against HIF1α (Abcam) were used. Images were taken with a Nikon Eclipse E400 fluorescence microscope.

The cancer genome atlas (TCGA) data analysis

Transcriptomic data (RNA-seq raw counts), corresponding clinical information, protein data, and somatic mutation data for Colon Adenocarcinoma (COAD) were downloaded from TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Normal colon tissue transcriptome data were obtained from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project (https://www.gtexportal.org). This study integrated and analyzed a total of 1412 samples from TCGA-COAD and GTEx.

Online bioinformatics tools were utilized to analyze the potential functions and signaling pathways of the identified DEGs. GO enrichment analysis, conducted through the DAVID platform (https://david.ncifcrf.gov), was employed to determine the biological roles linked to these DEGs. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied to identify enriched terms.

Pan-cancer analysis of TMC7 expression and microsatellite instability (MSI) correlation

Pan-cancer correlation analysis was performed using the TCGA plot R package to investigate the association between TMC7 expression and MSI across 33 cancer types. Transcriptomic data and MSI scores from TCGA database were used. MSI scores, calculated using the MANTIS algorithm, reflect MSI degree per sample. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used to assess the correlation with TMC7 expression.

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) analysis

TMB was calculated as the total number of non-synonymous mutations per sample divided by the exome size (38 Mb). TMC7 expression group differences in TMB were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis between COAD tumor samples and normal colon tissues was performed using the raw counts matrix. Genes with |log2 fold change| > 1 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were deemed significant and visualized in a volcano plot. TMC7 expression levels were compared between tumor and normal groups using FPKM values and visualized with ggplot2 (v3.5.0).

Somatic mutation landscape analysis

Somatic mutation data for COAD were acquired from TCGA in Mutation Annotation Format. The maftools R package (v2.20.0) was utilized to analyze the data, generating oncoplots (waterfall plots) to display the overall mutational landscape and the most frequently mutated genes.

Drug sensitivity prediction

The oncoPredict R package (v1.2.0) and the GDSC2 database were employed to predict the half-maximal inhibitory concentration for chemotherapeutic and targeted drugs in TCGA-COAD samples.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyzes were performed with GraphPad Prism (v6.0). Data from at least three independent experiments were presented as mean ± SEM. Differences in continuous variables between control and treatment groups were evaluated using Student's t-test or paired t-test. Survival outcomes were analyzed by the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Spearman's correlation assessed associations between PKN2 expression and HIF1α or standardized uptake value (SUV). Data were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test to examine associations between categorical variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

TMC7 is upregulated in CRC and correlates with tumor hypoxia

Analysis of TCGA COAD data revealed that TMC7 is significantly upregulated in CRC tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1A), with expression levels further elevated in metastatic compared to non-metastatic tumors (Fig. 1B). The expression differences of TMC family members in CRC tissues and normal tissues are shown in Supplementary Table S4. The DEGs associated with TMC7 in tumor and normal tissues are summarized in Supplementary Fig. S1. Mutation site analysis indicated a low mutation frequency of TMC7 in colon cancer (Fig. 1C). The DEGs between patients with high and low expression of TMC7 in tumor tissues are shown in Fig. 1D. Drug response prediction analyses showed that the TMC7 high-expression group exhibits decreased sensitivity to chemotherapy agents (docetaxel, gemcitabine, camptothecin) and targeted therapies (erlotinib, entospletinib) (Fig. 1E & Supplementary Fig. S2). GO enrichment analysis demonstrated that TMC7 is primarily involved in oxygen transport and is enriched in molecular functions of oxygen carrier activity and oxygen binding (Fig. 1D). The high-expression group exhibited significantly lower TMB than the low-expression group (Supplementary Fig. S3). Furthermore, pan-cancer database analysis revealed a negative correlation between TMC7 expression and MSI scores specifically in colon cancer, suggesting that patients with elevated TMC7 levels may have decreased responsiveness to immunotherapy (Supplementary Fig. S4). TMC7 facilitates CRC metastasis via enhanced hypoxia adaptation

Analysis of paired tissue specimens from patients with colon cancer liver metastasis showed that the expression of TMC7 was low in normal colon tissue and was significantly higher in liver metastasis compared to primary colorectal tumors (Fig. 2A). Patients with high TMC7 expression in liver metastasis exhibited a significantly greater number of metastatic nodules compared to those with low expression (Fig. 2B). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that the overall survival time in the high-expression group was notably shorter than that in the low-expression group (Median OS, TMC7-High 31.9 months vs TMC7-Low 80.1 months, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). Further analysis of liver metastasis patterns demonstrated a marked difference between the two groups (Fig. 2D). For in vivo validation, we derived TMC7-OE and NC HU-CRC cell lines from primary human CRC cells isolated from surgical specimens, and established lung metastasis models in nude mice by tail vein injection. TMC7-overexpressing mice formed substantially more lung metastatic nodules and had significantly reduced survival compared with controls (Fig. 2E, F). Next, we investigated the effects of TMC7 on the proliferation and invasion of colon cancer cells under hypoxia in vitro. Under normoxia, TMC7-OE and control cells showed comparable proliferation, whereas hypoxic conditions markedly boosted proliferation of TMC7-OE cells, as determined by CCK-8 assay (Fig. 2G, H). Transwell invasion assays revealed that TMC7-OE enhanced invasive capacity under hypoxia, while TMC7 knockdown reduced invasiveness (Fig. 2I). These data suggest TMC7 promotes metastasis and facilitates tumor cell adaptation to hypoxia.

TMC7 enhances the activity of HIF1α and inhibits the activity of PKN2

To investigate how TMC7 regulates hypoxia adaptation in CRC, we analyzed TMC7 expression and 18F-FDG SUV values in CRC liver metastases. High TMC7 expression correlated with increased average SUV and max SUV, suggesting increased glucose uptake (Fig. 3A). RNA-seq analysis showed 1401 upregulated and 1294 downregulated genes in TMC7-OE colon cancer cells, with significant enrichment of HIF1α-regulating genes such as PLCG2, MAP2K1, and PIK3R1 (Fig. 3B). qPCR validation confirmed the top DEGs, consistent with RNA-seq results (Supplementary Fig. S5). KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment in critical cancer-related pathways, including HIF-1 signaling, nuclear-cytoplasmic transport, and ubiquitin-mediated degradation (Fig. 3C). Immunofluorescence analysis indicated that under hypoxia, HIF1α was mainly distributed in the cytoplasm in the control group, while in the TMC7 OE group, nuclear localization of HIF1α was significantly enhanced, and the cytoplasmic signal concomitantly reduced (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, both glucose consumption and lactate production were also significantly higher in the TMC7-OE group (p < 0.001; Fig. 3E, F). We next investigated the regulatory relationship between TMC7 and PKN2. Analysis of TMC7 transcript and protein levels in colon cancer cells after PKN2 overexpression, dominant-negative inactivation, or control treatment revealed that PKN2 did not significantly regulate TMC7 expression (not shown). However, TMC7 -OE significantly suppressed PKN2 kinase activity in a plasmid dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3G), whereas TMC7 knockdown enhanced PKN2 activity (Fig. 3H). These findings indicate that TMC7 promotes nuclear translocation of HIF1α and negatively regulates PKN2 activation.

PKN2 suppresses the hypoxia-adaptive response in colon cancer cells

We examined PKN2 expression in primary tumors and matched liver metastases from CRC patients. PKN2 expression was significantly lower in liver metastases than in primary tumors (Fig. 4A). Moreover, patients with higher PKN2 expression in metastases had fewer metastatic nodules (Fig. 4B). Liver metastases with high PKN2 showed reduced HIF1α and SUV levels, whereas those with low PKN2 exhibited elevated HIF1α and SUV (Fig. 4C). PKN2 expression negatively correlated with average SUV, indicating reduced glucose uptake in PKN2-high lesions (Fig. 4D). To explore functional consequences, we compared proliferation of PKN2-overexpressing and control CRC cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. PKN2 suppresses the Warburg effect in colon cancer cells

To further elucidate the role of PKN2 in modulating HIF-1α-mediated Warburg effect, we performed a series of metabolic analyses. As shown in Fig. 5A–E, PKN2 knockdown significantly increased ECAR, reduced OCR, and elevated both glucose consumption and lactate production in colon cancer cells, collectively indicating enhanced Warburg effect. Conversely, PKN2 overexpression significantly downregulated mRNA levels of PDK1 and GLUT1 (Fig. 5F) and reduced glucose consumption and lactate production (Fig. 5G, H), indicating suppression of the Warburg effect. TMC7 suppresses PKN2 to inhibit HIF1α activation and the Warburg effect

Having established that PKN2 inhibits the Warburg effect in CRC cells, we next investigated whether TMC7 promotes this metabolic shift and facilitates CRC metastasis by suppressing PKN2. We established stable Hu-CRC cell lines overexpressing TMC7 (TMC7-OE), co-overexpressing TMC7 and PKN2 (TMC7&PKN2-OE), or harboring empty vector (NC), and generated a nude mouse lung metastasis model. TMC7-OE mice developed a greater number of lung metastases than NC controls, whereas TMC7&PKN2-OE mice exhibited fewer metastases and prolonged survival compared with TMC7-OE mice (Fig. 6A, B). Functional assays further supported these in vivo findings. CCK-8 and Transwell assays demonstrated that TMC7&PKN2-OE cells exhibited reduced proliferation and invasion relative to TMC7-OE cells (Fig. 6C–E). These findings indicate that TMC7 enhances CRC cell aggressiveness by inhibiting PKN2, and ectopic PKN2 co-expression restores its tumor-suppressive effects. TMC7&PKN2-OE cells showed lower glucose consumption and lactate production than TMC7-OE cells (Fig. 6F, G), along with downregulated mRNA levels of glycolytic genes GLUT1 and PDK1 (Fig. 6H, I). Moreover, the TMC7&PKN2-OE cells showed impaired nuclear accumulation of HIF1α compared with TMC7-OE group (Fig. 6J, K). Discussion

Elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying CRC metastasis is critical for developing effective therapeutic strategies. In this study, we provide the first evidence that TMC7 drives metastatic progression by targeting PKN2. Specifically, TMC7 exerts pro-metastatic effect by suppressing PKN2 activity, thereby enhancing HIF1α activation and hypoxic adaptation.

Metastatic colonization in target organs requires tumor cells to adapt to a microenvironment distinct from the primary site [15]. In liver metastatic cells, both HIF-1α activity and its downstream target PDK1 are upregulated [16]. By enhancing hypoxia adaptation, colon cancer cells maintain ADP production to meet energy demands and increase metastatic potential; blocking this response can induce hypoxic cancer cell death in metastases [17]. Notably, anti-angiogenic therapy can promote liver metastasis by triggering hypoxia adaptation in primary tumors [18]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying hypoxia adaptation in colon cancer remain unclear.

TMC genes play pivotal roles in the carcinogenesis, proliferation, and cell cycle regulation of various human cancers. However, the functional characterization of TMC members in oncogenesis remains largely unexplored. Emerging evidence has revealed that TMC6 and TMC8 are implicated in cervical cancer [19] and squamous cell carcinoma [20], respectively. TMC5 has been shown to promote prostate cancer cell proliferation by modulating the cell cycle [21]. The TMC gene family has also been reported as a promising set of biomarkers for prognosis and predictors of immunotherapeutic response, with potential roles varying across different cancer types [22,23].

TMC7 remains poorly characterized in cancer. A systematic investigation of TMC7 in cancers is necessary to elucidate its roles in tumorigenesis and to evaluate its clinical therapeutic potential. Our previous study identified TMC7 as a prognostic marker in pancreatic cancer [10]. In the present work, we show that TMC7 is aberrantly overexpressed in CRC, and high TMC7 levels correlate with increased metastatic burden and elevated tumor glucose uptake. High-TMC7 tumors data show lower TMB in high-TMC7 cases, and pan-cancer analysis reveals a negative correlation between TMC7 expression and MSI scores. Since high TMB and MSI-H are key predictors of favorable immunotherapy response [24,25], we propose that high-TMC7 tumors may respond poorly to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Drug sensitivity predictions further suggest reduced efficacy of chemotherapy agents including docetaxel and gemcitabine, as well as certain targeted therapies, in TMC7-high cases. Moreover, under hypoxia, TMC7 enhanced proliferation and invasion of colon cancer cells in vitro and promoted lung metastasis in murine models. Together, this study establishes the abnormal expression of TMC7 in CRC and its association with metastasis, metabolic dysregulation, and hypoxia-driven tumor progression.

The Warburg effect promotes CRC development by altering energy metabolism to support tumor growth. Under hypoxic conditions, it enhances resistance to necroptosis through pyruvate-mediated clearance of mitochondrial free radicals [26], and also enhances resistance to cytotoxic drugs and immune responses [27]. HIF-1α serves as a central transcription factor driving glycolysis. Upon nuclear translocation, HIF-1α binds hypoxia response elements in the promoters of glycolytic genes, leading to overexpression of glycolytic transporters and enzymes such as GLUT1 and PDK1 [28,29].

This study demonstrates that TMC7 promotes CRC cell proliferation and invasion in a hypoxia-specific manner—significantly enhancing these phenotypes under hypoxic, but not normoxic, conditions—indicating its functional dependence on the hypoxic microenvironment. Transcriptomic analysis revealed significant enrichment of the HIF-1α signaling pathway in TMC7-OE cells. In vitro experiments confirmed that TMC7 enhances HIF-1α nuclear translocation. Furthermore, TMC7 increases glucose consumption and lactate production, suggesting it activates HIF-1α to drive the Warburg effect, thereby supporting hypoxic adaptation and enhancing CRC cell survival and metastatic capacity.

We further show that TMC7 exerts these effects by inhibiting PKN2 activation, which in turn promotes HIF-1α activity and Warburg effect. Protein kinases drive tumor proliferation and invasion via multiple mechanisms. PKC enhances cancer cell migration by activating Rho, inducing extracellular matrix-degrading proteases, and triggering epithelial-mesenchymal transition. It also promotes immune evasion by upregulating PD-L1 [30]. PKD2 drives stromal remodeling, immunosuppression, and metastasis by regulating HIF-1α, Snail, β-catenin, and PD-L1 [31]. PKN2 exhibits context-dependent functions across cancers: in pancreatic cancer, it suppresses metastasis by activating tumor-associated fibroblasts [32]; in esophageal cancer, PKN2 is highly expressed in PMN-MDSCs, where it promotes fatty acid oxidation and inhibits T-cell activity, contributing to immunosuppression [33]. These findings highlight that PKN2 function is shaped by tumor type, cellular context, and microenvironmental cues, potentially acting as either an oncogene or a tumor suppressor. Currently, the research on PKN2 in colon cancer is limited. Our previous study found that patients with high expression of PKN2 in colon cancer tissues have better prognosis; PKN2 inhibits the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages to M2 type by blocking the secretion of IL-4/IL-10 [11]; PKN2 inhibits VEGFA and bFGF-mediated angiogenesis by targeting HIF-1α in colon cancer [12]. Another study reported that PKN2 knockdown can enhance the vitality of CRC cells, induce G0/G1 phase arrest and apoptosis [34]. Importantly, this study first reveals a previously unrecognized role of PKN2: suppression of hypoxia tolerance and the Warburg effect in colon cancer cells, ultimately inhibiting liver metastasis. Mechanistically, PKN2 overexpression reduces HIF-1α nuclear accumulation, downregulates downstream target genes, and consequently inhibits glucose uptake and glycolysis.

The upstream regulation of PKN2 has been partially characterized, with evidence indicating its activation through binding to Rho GTPases [35]. This study is the first to demonstrate that TMC7 suppresses PKN2 kinase activity in a dose-dependent manner. By alleviating PKN2-mediated inhibition of HIF-1α nuclear translocation and glycolytic gene expression, TMC7 promotes HIF-1α nuclear accumulation and activation, thereby enhancing the Warburg effect and driving CRC metastasis. Notably, overexpression of PKN2 partially reverses this pro-metastatic phenotype, underscoring its functional antagonism of TMC7.

Our study has some limitations. The current lung metastasis model cannot fully recapitulate the entire metastatic cascade—particularly early steps such as local invasion and intravasation. In addition, in vivo experiments lacked complementary TMC7 knockdown models to confirm the observed phenotypes. Future studies employing orthotopic implantation of TMC7-knockdown colon cancer cells or spontaneous metastasis models will provide complementary insights to further validate TMC7’s role across the full metastatic process. Additionally, future work will investigate whether targeting TMC7 suppresses colon cancer metastasis and improves tumor response to chemotherapy and immunotherapy, with further clinical validation to determine whether TMC7 serves as a prognostic and therapeutic response biomarker. Moreover, the precise mechanism by which the TMC7/PKN2 axis modulates HIF-1α remains incompletely defined and requires further investigation.

In conclusion, this study is the first to systematically elucidate the molecular mechanism by which TMC7 promotes CRC metastasis through suppression of PKN2 activity, leading to enhanced hypoxia-adaptive response and activation of the Warburg effect. The newly identified TMC7-PKN2-HIF1α regulatory axis offers a promising framework for developing targeted therapies against CRC metastasis. This work not only advances our understanding of the molecular basis of CRC metastasis but also provides critical theoretical support and novel targets for intervention.
