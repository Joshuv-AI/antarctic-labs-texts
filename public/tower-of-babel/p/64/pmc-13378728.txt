Multi-omic modelling of body mass index response to a dietary weight loss intervention
Authors: Emily N. Yeo, Ashley W. Scadden, Zachary T. Caterer, Kristen J. Sutton, Iain R. Konigsberg, Joanne B. Cole, Elizabeth M. Litkowski, Zhaoxing Pan, Catherine A. Lozupone, Danielle M. Ostendorf, Paul S. MacLean, Edward L. Melanson, Daniel H. Bessesen, Sarah J. Borengasser, Ethan M. Lange, Victoria A. Catenacci, Maggie A. Stanislawski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObesity is a multifactorial condition, and there is wide heterogeneity in responses to weight loss interventions. Although it remains challenging, modeling responses to weight loss interventions can help tailor treatments, increase weight loss success, or improve our understanding of underlying pathophysiology. We leveraged multi-omic (genetics; gut microbiota: taxonomy, inferred gene pathways and metabolite dynamics; blood metabolomics) and clinical data (e.g., lipids, blood glucose) from a 12-month behavioral weight loss trial of adults (n = 150) with overweight/obesity, to forecast longitudinal body mass index (BMI) and BMI change (ΔBMI) using Mixed Effects Random Forests (MERF) and GLMM-Lasso. Across modeling approaches and outcomes, routinely available clinical variables and blood metabolomics consistently improved prediction over basic demographics, and metabolomics added value beyond clinical information. Across models, the combined omic risk score most improved models of longitudinal BMI trajectories, explaining 20.5–26.0% marginal variance (R2m), whereas metabolomic risk scores most improved BMI change prediction (R2m = 52.9–59.3%). Gut microbial taxonomy and inferred gene pathways offered modest but significant gains for some models and outcomes, while metabolite dynamics consistently failed to enhance performance. The most important features in the models included insulin, glycoprotein acetyls, lipoprotein sizes, and certain amino acids, aligning with known inflammatory and metabolic mechanisms. These findings support that select blood-based biomarkers correlate with individual responses to weight loss efforts.

Introduction

Obesity is a rising global health concern, with individual responses to behavioral weight loss efforts varying widely across individuals, 1 primarily due to complex physiological, psychological, and environmental factors that are not yet fully understood. 2-8 Elevated Body Mass Index (BMI) levels have been shown to increase risk for chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease (CVD), certain cancer types, and metabolic syndrome (MetS). 1 , 9-12 Even marginal (5–10%) weight loss has multiple health benefits, including decreasing blood pressure, improving glycemia, reducing the need for medications, and enhancing quality of life. 11 , 13-18 Nonetheless, achieving and maintaining successful weight loss can be challenging for many. 2 , 4 , 5 , 19 Thus, identifying personalized factors that associate with weight loss response to diet and lifestyle interventions, and improving our understanding of the varying physiological reasons that may impact heterogeneity of response could have profound benefits for the development of personalized or more effective weight management strategies. Given the many biological and environmental influences on complex traits such as BMI and weight loss, the use of multi-omic data analyzes offers a unique opportunity to uncover underlying contributors towards obesity and individual responses to weight loss, potentially acting as biomarkers for different subgroups of weight loss responders.

Recently, in addition to individual clinical data, genetic, gut microbial, and blood metabolomic signatures have been shown to explain differences in weight loss trajectories. 6 , 8 , 20-28 Integrating these various biological data types through multi-omic approaches has not only demonstrated novel insights into the physiological processes involved in weight loss, but has also shown significant potential to enhance modeling accuracy for metabolic phenotypes. 22 , 29 , 30 For example, Piening et al. profiled genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and microbiomics during controlled weight gain and loss, revealing inflammatory and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy signatures, some persisting post-loss, and individual biomarker stability linked to insulin resistance. 31

However, this study did not build models of individuals undergoing weight loss interventions or compare omic layers for prospective BMI change. Additionally, Watanabe et al. utilized blood multi-omics to model BMI for 1,277 adults that outperformed BMI in reflecting metabolic health and validated them externally, but did not develop longitudinal prediction models or quantify the incremental benefit beyond clinical metrics. 30 Prior work has examined additive value across omics in other disease contexts. 30 , 32-34 For example, Arehart and Sterrett et al. integrated various longitudinal omic data layers into poly-omic risk scores to more accurately model inflammatory bowel disease diagnoses. 34 However, no study has applied such frameworks to model longitudinal BMI response to a weight loss intervention.

Therefore, using data from a 12-month randomized behavioral weight-loss interventional trial, we developed and evaluated omic risk scores of longitudinal BMI and BMI change (ΔBMI). We analyzed multiple omic data types, including blood metabolomics, stool microbial taxonomy, inferred microbial pathways and microbial metabolite dynamics, and genetic risk of obesity (in the form of a genetic risk score, GRS). We assessed incremental value of these omic risk scores both against a basic demographic benchmark (age, sex, diet, and intervention group) and a benchmark composed of clinical variables (routine blood markers of cardiometabolic heath, such as cholesterol). We used two complementary longitudinal modeling approaches to develop the omic risk scores that have distinct underlying assumptions: GLMM-Lasso, which assumes linear relationships, and mixed-effects random forests (MERF), which allows for nonlinear, non-parametric relationships and interactions among features. 35-37 This work extends prior multi-omic BMI studies by (i) developing longitudinal omic risk scores during an intervention, (ii) directly benchmarking omic risk scores against demographic and clinical metrics, and (iii) evaluating multiple microbiome data types alongside metabolomics and genetics with longitudinal mixed-effects models.

Materials/subjects and methods

Study details

The data for this project is from a study conducted at the University of Colorado Anschutz, Daily Caloric Restriction vs. Intermittent Fasting Trial (DRIFT), a 1-year randomized trial which compared weight loss generated by either Daily Caloric Restriction (DCR) or 4:3 Intermittent Fasting (IMF) in healthy adults (N = 165), aged 18–65 years, with overweight or obesity (BMI 27–46) (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03411356; registered 26 January 2018). 38 , 39 Details of the study protocol are outlined extensively in Ostendorf et al. 2022. 38

Both groups were assigned an equivalent weekly energy deficit target of 34.3% from baseline maintenance requirements. The DCR group received a daily caloric prescription to achieve this deficit, while the IMF group followed a 4:3 intermittent fasting regimen, with three non-consecutive fast days per week (80% energy restriction) and ad libitum intake on non-fasting days. Both groups received recommendations for healthy eating, were prescribed a progressive exercise program targeting 300 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity by 6 months and participated in structured, group-based behavioral support sessions (weekly for 16 weeks, then biweekly for the remainder of the 12-month intervention). Clinical measures were collected at baseline, 6, and 12 months, including anthropometrics and serum laboratory values related to cardiometabolic health (e.g., fasting lipids, insulin, glucose, and hemoglobin A1c), as well blood and stool samples.

Omic data collection & preparation

Clinical data

Participant outcome measures collected at each timepoint were recorded on detailed data forms. Details on the collection and handling of survey data, anthropometry measures, clinical assessments, and blood and stool samples have been outlined previously. 38 From this, de-identified participant data and outcome measures underwent quality control prior to incorporation. This involved removal of non-consenting individuals from the analysis, addressing potential data entry errors, and normalization (z-score) where necessary. Participants without sequenced stool samples at baseline, or BMI metrics at baseline, 6-months and 12-months (N = 8) were removed from the analysis. Although clinical data is not usually referred to as omic data, it is included in the mention of “omic layers” in this paper. All data analysis was conducted in R using version 2023.12.1 + 402, except when otherwise noted.

Genetic data

Genomic DNA was isolated using a column-based method according to the manufacturer’s instruction and genotyping of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) from previously collected whole blood samples was completed in two batches. For the initial batch (n = 62), DNA extraction was carried out using the QIAamp Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD), and DNA concentration and purity were assessed using both a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE). Genotyping for this first batch was completed using the Illumina Multi-Ethnic Global Array (MEGA) v2.0 BeadChip.

For the second genotyping batch (N = 90), thawed samples were aliquoted for DNA extraction using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) and protocols. 40 Briefly, EDTA whole blood was treated with proteinase K, RNase A, and the provided cell lysis buffer, Buffer AL. The solution underwent serval wash steps with ethanol and the provided AW1 and AW2 buffers using a DNeasy Mini Spin column, with the flow-through being discarded at each step. DNA was eluted off the column, in two sequential 10-minute incubations with 50 μL of Buffer AE, to increase the final yield. Purity was assessed using a Nanodrop (Thermofisher), and DNA concentration was measured by a Qubit Fluorometer (Life Technologies). The DNA samples were normalized to 50 ng/µL prior to storage at −20 °C. Samples were shipped to the Oklahoma Medical Research Foundation (OMRF) for sequencing using the Global Screening Array (v3.0) protocols. 41

All genotyping data underwent quality control processing in PLINK (2.0). 42 Briefly, control sample sequences, single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with missing genotype call rates of 0.1, and individuals with missingness greater than 10% were removed. Palindromic SNPs were removed prior to imputation via the TOPMed Imputation Server with minimac 4.0. 43-45 SNPs requiring reversal, as indicated by imputation output, were flipped using PLINK prior to final imputation. To remove SNPs that are more susceptible to genotyping errors, those with minor allele frequencies below 1% were removed. Imputation quality was assessed using squared correlation coefficients, with a minimum r-squared value of 0.3, and sliding window of 1000 kilobases.

BMI genetic risk score

We computed a GRS for the DRIFT participants using the R package bigsnpr (1.12.18). 46 , 47 The GRS included 97 genetic variants based on a study by Locke et al. (European; n = 322154 (95%), Non-European; n = 17072 (5%)). 46 A risk score based on a larger set of BMI-associated SNPs (n = 2.1million) from 238,944 individuals (99% European; Polygenic Score Catalog: PGS000027), 48 , 49 was also investigated. 8 However, this score did not align as well with the participants baseline BMI as the GRS composed from the 97 variants, and thus the simpler GRS was used going forward.

Blood metabolomics

Fasted serum samples were collected by venipuncture and processed at each timepoint as previously described. 38 The serum samples underwent a freeze/thaw cycle, with 350 μL of serum aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. The serum metabolic data was processed by Nightingale Health platform, which utilizes high-throughput proton nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) to infer concentrations of 250 metabolites, including lipids, lipoproteins, amino acids, and glycolysis-related metabolites. 50-52 Prior to incorporation into BMI and ΔBMI models, preprocessing was conducted using the caret (7.0-1) R package. After centering, scaling, near-zero variance filtering, and correlation-based filtering (threshold = 0.99), 156 features were removed. 53 Additionally, 53 more metabolites were discarded for either extreme frequency skew (top value ≥ 95 × second) or insufficient uniqueness (<10% unique; n = 16). The resulting dataset was thus standardized and filtered to retain an informative, non-redundant set of 78 metabolites. 54 , 55

Gut microbial taxonomy

Details regarding stool collection and extraction using DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen Catalog # 47014) were outlined previously. 56 , 57 Library preparations and sequencing was done using the Illumina Amplicon Protocols of the V4 variable region of the 16S rRNA gene. 58 The resulting sequencing reads went through quality control filtering and trimming to a uniform length using Qiime2 (2023.2) software. 59 Data sequences were de-noised using DADA2 with default parameters, then taxonomically classified using the Greengenes2 2022-10 database. 60 , 61 To balance bias aversion with sequence inclusion, a standardization of 3407 sequences per sample was set.

Amplicon sequence variant (ASV) count data from 16S rRNA gene sequencing was processed using the phyloseq package (1.46.0). 62 ASVs that were observed fewer than three times in over 90% of samples were removed (n = 2070 taxa), followed by filtering based on variability (n = 9 with coefficient of variation <1.0). Counts were then aggregated to the genus level (n = 179), and genera with zero abundance in ≥80% of samples were excluded (n = 47). Data were transformed using the centered log-ratio (CLR) method to account for compositionality. 63 , 64 Post-transformation, features with high collinearity (Pearson’s r > 0.95) were removed using the caret package (n = 11). 53 Additional filtering parameters included a frequency ratio threshold (95/5) for near-zero variance variables, a uniqueness cut-off (10%), and a minimum of 15 unique observed values per feature as described further by Kuhn. 53 The final dataset, comprising 121 genus-level taxa, was evaluated for normality, skewness, and sample integrity prior to analysis.

Gut microbial functional genes data

Functional annotation of ASVs with sets of KEGG orthologs (KOs) and associated pathways was achieved using the bioinformatic tool Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States (PICRUST) (2.5.2). 65-68 To enhance model accuracy, we employed the stratified option, which allows for more detailed functional profiling by accounting for the phylogenetic relationships among sequences.

The predicted abundances of 390 metabolic pathways were filtered to retain only those present in more than 20% of samples (n = 311) prior to CLR transformations. 63 , 64 Post-filtering, features with high collinearity (Pearson’s r > 0.90) were removed using the caret package. 53 The same additional filtering parameters for microbial taxonomy data described above was applied to the pathway features. The final dataset, comprising 127 pathways, was included in subsequent analyzes.

Microbial metabolic flux data

Microbially-inferred metabolite dynamics were estimated using a flux balance analysis framework in MICOM run via Qiime2 (2024.2.0), with a pre-built western diet categorization as described by authors. 59 , 69 This diet was chosen because it was believed to resemble initial diets of the study participants. While MICOM can incorporate alternative or personalized diets, this is currently not a standardized option in the software. Sample-specific relative abundance and taxonomic assignments were combined with genome-scale metabolic models from the AGORA 2 collection to build community models for each sample, following the MICOM workflow. 69-71 Taxa with a low relative abundance < 0.01% were excluded prior to model building. The model was then executed to predict metabolite flux distributions, with the exchange flux bounds in the medium specified in mmol gDW−1 h−1. 72 Community growth and fluxes were estimated using cooperative tradeoff flux balance analysis, which imposes a tradeoff between maximal community biomass production and more even growth across individual taxa. 69 Tradeoff analyzes were first run using ‎`MICOM tradeoff` to determine a range in which most taxa had positive growth; based on these results, a tradeoff value of 0.6 was selected for all subsequent simulations. Only fluctuating metabolites detected in more than 20% of samples were retained (n = 120). Flux values were z-score normalized across samples, and filtered to remove those with near-zero variance (n = 12) and high collinearity (n = 55 with Pearson’s r > 0.85), resulting in 53 metabolite fluxes included in downstream analyzes. 53

Integrating omic layers

To facilitate longitudinal analyzes of multi-omic data across three timepoints (baseline, 6 months, and 12 months), clinical metadata and GRS data were merged sequentially with processed metabolomics, taxa (CLR-transformed genus-level abundances), KO pathway abundances, and MICOM-predicted metabolic fluxes. Any subject with >80% missing data across features was removed (n = 1). Features with missingness post-merge (<17%) were imputed using the missForest algorithm. 73

Construction of omic risk scores

Because they capture different sources of variability, we examined two primary outcomes of interest, longitudinal BMI and longitudinal changes in BMI. For each set of omic features, models were constructed for each of the outcomes: longitudinal BMI, using repeated BMI measurements at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months, and ΔBMI, defined as changes in BMI from baseline to 6 months and from 6 to 12 months. In both frameworks, omic features were modeled longitudinally, including the feature value for longitudinal BMI models or interval-specific change in the feature for ΔBMI models. To avoid overfitting, samples from 38 participants (~25%) were set aside to serve as the validation set and used to test and evaluate models trained exclusively on the training set of 112 participants (~75%). To avoid imposing researcher bias, the train and test split was implemented at random. Model performance assessments were carried out on the testing set.

To account for and compare the impact of differing model structures and assumptions, two longitudinal modeling approaches were applied for each outcome (BMI and ΔBMI): Mixed Effects Random Forest (MERF), implemented in Python (version 3.10.18), and a Lasso-type regularization method described below.

The MERF algorithm (merf 1.0) allows for the possibility of non-parametric distributions and non-linear relationships among variables within a longitudinal random forest framework, incorporating both fixed and random effects. 35 , 74 BMI or ΔBMI was modeled as the response variable, with fixed effects for the omic features and random effects for subject and timepoint. Fixed effects variables were either the variables collected at baseline, 6, and 12 months for BMI models, or the calculated difference (if numeric) of each fixed effect for the two time windows for the ΔBMI models (baseline-6 months, 6–12 months). Hyperparameter tuning and cross-validation were used to systematically assess multiple parameter combinations for the MERF model, and the final models for BMI or ΔBMI in each omic dataset used the combination of parameters yielding the lowest mean squared prediction error in the training dataset. To interpret the contribution of each omic feature to the models, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were computed for the best-performing MERF models in the testing datasets for each omic layer. 75 Feature importance was visualized using SHAP (0.48.0) summary plots (also known as beeswarm plots), which depict the distribution and magnitude of SHAP values for each feature across all samples, enabling assessment of the direction/nature of association between the model features and the outcome.

Second, we used a Lasso-type regularization method for high-dimensional generalized linear mixed models (GlmmLasso), implemented in the R package glmmLasso (1.6.3), for each omic dataset, which assumes parametric distributions and linear relationships between independent variables and outcomes. 37 , 76 As in the MERF models, BMI or ΔBMI was modeled as a function of the omic features, with random intercepts for subject and time period. To identify an optimal regularization parameter (λ), models were fit across a grid of λ values (λ = 10 to 100, incremented by 1) and recorded the number of non-zero coefficients at each value. The “elbow point” of the resulting sparsity curve, representing a balance between model complexity and feature retention, was determined using the Kneedle algorithm and used to select the final λ. 77 A final GlmmLasso model for each omic was then fit using the optimal λ for each dataset. Feature selection results, including the subset of retained predictors and their estimated effect sizes, were extracted from each final model.

For each omic feature, MERF and GlmmLasso models of BMI and ΔBMI were fit on the training data, with the final tuned models applied to the held-out testing data to generate predicted BMI or ΔBMI values, which served as “risk scores” for each set of predictors. Thus, the score represents the fitted value from the full model (including all retained predictors under the model’s regularization/random-effects structure), rather than a manually constructed weighted sum of selected features outside the model. Risk scores were made for each model type (MERF or GlmmLasso) and BMI outcome combination including the following features:

B asic Risk Score: age, sex, and diet intervention group. This served as a benchmark for the other scores.

Clinical Risk Score: routine clinical variables: high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), fasting insulin, and fasting glucose.

Genetic Risk Score (GRS): the participant’s GRS for BMI.

Taxonomic Risk Score: CLR-transformed gut microbial taxa.

Pathway Risk Score: CLR-transformed microbial metabolic pathways inferred from functional annotation based on taxonomy.

MICOM Risk Score: predicted community-scale microbial metabolic fluxes, estimated via MICOM.

Metabolite Risk Score: circulating blood metabolite concentrations.

Combined Risk Score: all clinical, GRS, taxonomic, pathway, MICOM, and metabolomic features.

First, the clinical and omic risk scores were compared to the Basic Risk Score, and then the omic scores were compared to the Clinical Risk Score. For the comparisons to the Clinical Risk Score, adjustments were made to both the clinical and combined scores. Basic features were included in the clinical risk score (called Clinical + Risk Score), and clinical features were removed from the combined risk score (called Combined* Risk Score).

Comparison of omic risk scores to basic risk scores

To assess the incremental contribution of each omic risk score to BMI and ΔBMI models, linear mixed-effects models were fit, with random intercepts for within-subject correlations, using the lmer function from the lme4 (1.1-37) R package. 78 Models were constructed for each of the risk scores described above: a baseline model with only the basic risk score and seven extended models, each with the addition of one risk score (clinical, GRS, taxa, pathway, MICOM, metabolomics, or combined). For each model, the marginal R2, R2M (variance explained by fixed effects), and conditional R2, R2C (variance explained by the entire model, including both fixed and random effects), was computed using the MuMIn package (1.48.4). 79 Nested model comparisons were conducted to determine whether the inclusion of each omic risk score significantly improved model fit relative to the baseline model via analysis of variance (ANOVA) likelihood ratio tests, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Comparison of omic risk scores to clinical + risk scores

While basic scores provide a simplified benchmark for evaluating the contribution of the other scores to the models, a more comprehensive clinical risk score (clinical+) was also evaluated as a comparator. Following the same modeling framework described above, each omic-specific risk score (GRS, taxa, pathway, MICOM, metabolomics, or combined*) was evaluated for its added model contribution relative to the clinical + risk score. This comparison offers a more clinically meaningful benchmark than the basic score.

Model stability over time

In order to evaluate the performance stability of each omic risk score over time, we applied the trained models to each separate timepoint of the test set and compared model R2 values.

Results

Participant characteristics and BMI change

Overall participant characteristics across timepoints are shown in Table 1. Participants were primarily female (73%), with a mean BMI of 34.25 kg/m2 at baseline and age of 41.93 years. Average BMI significantly changed over the course of the intervention, with a mean decrease in BMI occurring in the first 6 months (mean change −2.47 kg/m2, SE = 0.19, p < 0.0001), and slight mean BMI increases in the 6–12 month period (mean change +0.21 kg/m2, SE = 0.20, p = 0.30). Substantial between-subject variability in BMI was observed (Supplementary Figure 1). No significant differences in basic participant characteristics existed between individuals in the testing and training datasets at any time point (Supplementary Table 1).

Performance of longitudinal BMI risk scores

Comparison of risk scores to basic risk scores

Several of the MERF-based risk scores significantly improved models of longitudinal BMI over the basic score (which included just age, sex and diet group) in the testing data (Figure 1a ; R2M = 0.077): clinical score (R2M = 0.194, p < 0.001), GRS (R2M = 0.139, p < 0.01), pathway score (R2M = 0.088, p < 0.01), metabolomics score (R2M = 0.156, p < 0.001), and the combined score (R2M = 0.205, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 2a). For the GlmmLasso-based scores, only the clinical (R2M = 0.176, p < 0.001) and metabolomics (R2M = 0.129, p < 0.01) scores significantly improved longitudinal BMI models compared to the basic score alone (R2M = 0.093, Figure 1c ; Supplementary Table 2c).

Comparison of omic risk scores to clinical+ scores

We next evaluated whether omic scores could improve the clinical+ score, which included the basic and clinical features (HDL, LDL, HbA1c, HOMA-IR, fasting insulin, and fasting glucose). Compared to the clinical+ score alone (R2M = 0.191), the MERF metabolomics (R2M = 0.189, p = 0.043) and combined* (R2M = 178, p = 0.049) risk scores provided additional model benefit (Figure 1b; Supplementary Table 2b). For the GlmmLasso-based scores, the GRS (R2M = 0.220, p < 0.01) and the metabolomic risk score (R2M = 0.254, p < 0.01) improved upon the clinical+ risk score (R2M = 0.254) (Figure 1d; Supplementary Table 2 d).

Model stability over time

Trained MERF and GlmmLasso models were also applied to test data at individual timepoints, with the predictions becoming the omic risk score for that timepoint. Nested linear models were used to compare (using R2 values) these timepoint-specific scores to the clinical socre for that timepoint alone (Supplementary Table 4). Overarchingly, omic risk scores performed worst in the first timepoint, and better thereafter (Supplementary Table 4a−c),except for the GlmmLasso ΔBMI score, which predominantly had better R2 values in the first 0–6 month time interval (Supplementary Table 4d).

Discussion

This study implemented longitudinal modeling procedures, specifically MERF and GlmmLasso, that leverage data from genetics, metabolomics, the gut microbiome, and clinical parameters to create individual risk scores of both BMI and ΔBMI. We identified which omic layers, as well as the specific features therein, that improved models of BMI outcomes over the course of a behavioral weight loss intervention compared to standard basic individual characteristics (i.e., age, sex, diet group) or to clinical information (e.g., insulin, cholesterol).

Prior studies have found age and sex to be associated with changes in BMI during weight loss interventions. 5 , 28 , 80 , 81 As these are readily accessible variables, they formulated the basic model for comparison of the clinical and omic risk scores. Clinical variables related to dyslipidemia and insulin resistance, including fasting insulin, triglycerides, and HDL cholesterol, substantially improved models of BMI and ΔBMI beyond basic demographic characteristics. Among these, insulin and triglycerides consistently demonstrated the strongest associations across modeling approaches, whereas HDL contributed more modestly. This pattern aligns with SHAP-based interpretations of random forest models, which indicated that greater increases in insulin and triglycerides were associated with higher BMI or reduced weight loss, whereas lower or decreasing levels of these markers were associated with greater BMI reduction. 82-84

Our results also showed the additive value of metabolomics scores above basic and clinical scores for BMI and ΔBMI outcomes, across both MERF and GlmmLasso modeling techniques. Furthermore, there was considerable overlap between top metabolomic features across the two modeling approaches, indicating robustness in the findings. These features included glycoprotein acetyls, cholesterol and lipid parameters, amino acids such as phenylalanine, tyrosine, glutamine and glycine, as well as pyruvate. These metabolomic features are biologically feasible. For instance, higher glycoprotein acetyls were associated with increased BMI and BMI gains in our results, and these are widely recognized as markers of systemic inflammation and have been previously associated with overweight. 85-87 Similarly, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), a type of omega-3 fatty acid that associated with lower BMI and BMI loss in our results, has previously shown promise in mitigating obesity-related issues through its impact on inflammation, fat metabolism, and insulin resistance. 88 , 89 Lastly, previous studies have also highlighted the associations between lipid and cholesterol sizes and concentrations and metabolic health, consistent with our results. 90-94

These findings are supportive of prior studies that demonstrated insulin resistance and hypertriglyceridemia are major determinants of weight loss responsiveness during lifestyle interventions. 95 , 96 Individuals with elevated insulin levels often exhibit impaired lipolysis and reduced metabolic flexibility, limiting the mobilization of stored fat despite caloric restriction or increased physical activity. 97-99 Similarly, elevated triglycerides reflect hepatic lipid overproduction and altered lipid clearance, processes that have been associated with diminished weight loss success. 96 , 100-102 Taken together, the strongest associated features of BMI and BMI change across clinical and metabolomic layers point toward metabolic flexibility, insulin sensitivity, and lipid handling as central mechanisms underlying successful weight loss during behavioral intervention.

In addition to metabolomics scores, gut microbial taxonomy and pathway risk scores improved models of ΔBMI compared to clinical risk scores alone; gut microbial gene pathway scores for longitudinal BMI likewise improved upon the basic risk scores. However, the microbial metabolic flux rates (MICOM scores) did not improve accuracy of models of either outcome or modeling type. While this could reflect a true biological effect, methodological constraints may also explain the weak association between MICOM risk scores and BMI outcomes. In particular, the model assumed a Western diet when simulating community metabolite dynamics that may not reflect participants’ true dietary intake, especially if eating habits were modified in response to intervention guidance. Additionally, the gut microbiome is among the most variable biological systems measured, exhibiting substantial intra-individual fluctuation over time. Our limited sample size may be inadequate to capture biologically meaningful microbiome patterns that unfold during a dietary intervention. Furthermore, the gut microbial gene pathways and metabolite flux rates were inferred from microbial taxonomy assigned from 16S rRNA sequencing regions using tools that rely on reference databases, it is possible that the estimated values are imprecise, and direct measurement could more accurately predict BMI outcomes. 68 , 69 Lastly, the DRIFT trial included two distinct dietary strategies, which may have differentially shaped the gut microbiome over time. Diet modality is a well-established driver of microbial composition, and strategy-specific microbial responses could have introduced heterogeneity that diluted detectable signals when both groups were modeled together. While the sample size precluded formal stratified analyzes by intervention arm, particularly given the high dimensionality of microbiome data, the MERF framework may have partially captured non-linear interactions between microbial features and intervention group through its ensemble structure. Future studies with larger cohorts should explicitly test whether dietary strategy moderates the predictive value of omic biomarkers for weight loss outcomes.

Our results highlight numerous taxa that have previously been associated with BMI and metabolism as important predictors of ΔBMI. For instance, the genus Dorea, part of the Firmicutes phylum, has been shown to ferment dietary carbohydrates into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which have beneficial effects on weight management. 103-105 SCFAs like butyrate play a key role in promoting energy metabolism and fat oxidation, possibly contributing to weight loss. 106 , 107 Other genera seen as predictors, including Eubacterium and Faecalicacillus, are also implicated in SCFA production and have been found to enhance gut microbial diversity and metabolic health, potentially leading to lower body fat accumulation. 108 , 109 In the BMI GlmmLasso combined risk scores, increases in the abundance of the genus Dorea was associated with greater BMI gain rather than BMI loss. However, this result was also reported in a prior caloric restriction randomized trial by Sowah et al (2022). 110 In that study, elevated Dorea levels at baseline were predictive of participants having more difficulty losing excess weight. 110

Many of the top features in the combined omic risk scores (which included features from all the omic datasets), have also been shown to be involved in metabolic mechanisms that influence body mass regulation. As described above, glycoprotein acetyl content was a selected marker of both BMI and ΔBMI combined scores. As was insulin, L-fucose and Dephospho-CoA (Dephospho-Coenzyme A). Insulin is a key hormone involved in glucose homeostasis and lipid metabolism. 92 Elevated insulin levels often indicate insulin resistance, a condition frequently observed in individuals with increased BMI. 21 , 111 , 112 In line with this, our results indicate higher increases of insulin and L-fucose predict higher BMI or BMI gains. Indole-3-acetate concentrations showed pronounced associations in two combined omic risk scores with increases correlated with BMI loss. This is again consistent with prior literature, which has found indole-3-acetate to be beneficial for reducing inflammation and improving glycemic control. 113-116

These findings are broadly consistent with, and extend, prior multi-omic studies of weight loss response. Siebert et al. previously identified baseline metabolomic and microbial predictors of short-term weight loss in DRIFT, the behavioral intervention in our study, and the prominence of insulin-related and lipid features in the current study aligns with those earlier findings while extending them across a full 12-month longitudinal trajectory. 22 Similarly, Watanabe et al. demonstrated that blood multi-omic signatures improve upon BMI as a reflection of metabolic health; our results complement this by showing that metabolomic risk scores add predictive value specifically during an active weight loss intervention, beyond routinely available clinical markers. 30 With respect to the gut microbiome, Jian et al. likewise found that baseline microbial composition predicted body fat change during dietary intervention, consistent with the modest but significant taxonomic contributions observed here. 20 Taken together, the convergence of insulin resistance, inflammatory markers such as glycoprotein acetyls, and specific lipid parameters across independent studies and methodological approaches strengthens confidence that these represent genuine biological correlates of weight loss responsiveness rather than study-specific findings.

This study has several limitations. The gut microbial sequencing was done using 16S rRNA sequencing, which has less taxonomic coverage and depth of taxonomic resolution than metagenomic sequencing and lacks direct functional profiling. 117 , 118 To address these shortcomings, functional pathway and metabolic flux data were inferred from taxonomy, though these approaches are likewise not ideal compared to direct measurement. 119 Furthermore, MICOM simulations were conducted using a predefined Western diet medium, which may not accurately represent participants’ actual dietary intake during a structured weight-loss intervention. This modeling assumption may partially account for the limited additional value of inferred gut microbial metabolite dynamics.

While two modeling methodologies with distinct underlying assumptions were employed to create BMI and ΔBMI scores (GlmmLasso and MERF), other machine learning approaches may offer alternative insights or improved predictive performance. The models incorporated diverse biological data types, but it remains possible that unmeasured or excluded variables may better explain individual variability in BMI responses. In addition, longitudinal mixed-effects models were used to relate changes in biological features to BMI and ΔBMI trajectories, however, reverse causality cannot be excluded, whereby improvements in metabolic markers reflect downstream consequences of weight loss rather than upstream drivers. Accordingly, some features that strongly predicted BMI change, particularly clinical and metabolomic markers related to insulin resistance and lipid metabolism, could be interpreted as indicators of underlying metabolic state. While these features are biologically plausible and strongly predictive of BMI change, they likely represent correlates of weight loss response rather than causal determinants and may both influence and be influenced by weight loss itself. As such, the translational potential of these findings warrants careful consideration. The models presented here are not yet suitable for direct clinical application. They were developed in a modest-sized single-site cohort, have not been externally validated, and rely on omic data types that are not yet part of routine clinical care. Rather, the primary value of this work lies in improving our biological understanding of why individuals vary in their BMI trajectories during behavioral weight loss intervention, and in identifying biomarkers that may reflect underlying metabolic states associated with differential response. Looking further ahead, the framework developed here, integrating multi-omic risk scores to predict longitudinal disease-relevant outcomes, synthesizes multiple biological variables into a clinically actionable prediction. With replication in larger, more diverse cohorts and as omic profiling becomes more accessible, approaches of this kind hold promise for informing more personalized weight management strategies.

A further limitation is the use of BMI as the primary outcome measure. While BMI is widely used in clinical and research settings and remains the standard metric for diagnosing obesity and evaluating lifestyle intervention response, it has recognized shortcomings as a surrogate for adiposity and metabolic health. BMI can misclassify individuals with high lean mass as overweight, and BMI-based obesity definitions demonstrate high specificity but lower sensitivity for identifying excess body fat, with variation across ethnic groups. Additionally, BMI does not distinguish between metabolically healthy and unhealthy obesity subgroups, which represent meaningfully different physiological states. In the present study, BMI was selected as the primary outcome given its alignment with the BMI-derived genetic risk score, its clinical interpretability, and its widespread use as an endpoint in behavioral weight loss trials; furthermore, the cohort's self-selection into a weight loss trial reduce the likelihood of systematic misclassification. Future work incorporating direct measures of body composition, such as DXA-derived fat mass or lean mass indices, would provide a more nuanced characterization of intervention response.

Lastly, our results are reflective of the relatively small cohort of participants included. Our training/testing split of the data makes our findings more robust than just internal cross-validation in the whole cohort, but the small sample is not ideal for statistical power - particularly for omic profiles that are more variable across populations, like the microbiome. Additionally, normalization and preprocessing was not feasible in the training set alone given the sample size, and the potential for data leakage is plausible. Replication in larger, more diverse cohorts would improve the generalizability of the results across populations, which is important for translation into clinical or public health applications.

This study assessed the utility of diverse datasets for the prediction of BMI responses to a behavioral weight loss intervention and identified key predictive features from each dataset using two modeling approaches. We found that risk scores based on blood metabolomics and routine clinical variables consistently improved models of BMI and ΔBMI. Taxonomic and microbial pathway scores also added value in some cases, but estimated flux of metabolites through microbial metabolic networks did not. This work adds to a growing body of research that uses risk scores to synthesize and integrate multi-omic data. These types of risk scores have the potential to improve our understanding of clinical responsiveness or to design more effective and personalized interventions.
