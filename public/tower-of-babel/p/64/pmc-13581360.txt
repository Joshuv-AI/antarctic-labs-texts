Target-controlled infusion achieves smoother autonomic modulation than manual bolus during propofol induction: Evidence from wavelet-based spectral analysis
Authors: Jing-Yang Liou, Kun-Hui Chen, Chen Lin, Hsin-Yi Huang, Chien-Kun Ting, Men-Tzung Lo, Wen-Kuei Chang, Hsin-Yi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Anesthesia induction involves rapid autonomic shifts that may compromise hemodynamic stability. Propofol administered via target-controlled infusion (TCI) produces a more gradual onset, whereas rapid manual bolus (MB) administration often triggers abrupt autonomic disturbances. Thus, the propofol delivery method is critical to autonomic stability; however, conventional monitoring lacks sufficient temporal resolution to capture transient fluctuations during early induction. To address this gap, we compared TCI and MB using high-temporal-resolution wavelet-based spectral analysis (WBSA) of heart rate variability (HRV) and pulse photoplethysmography amplitude (PPGA) to characterize real-time autonomic dynamics.Methods:This prospective, single-center observational study included adult patients undergoing elective surgery who received propofol induction via TCI or MB administered over 10 seconds. HRV and PPGA were continuously recorded and analyzed using WBSA. High-frequency power and low-to-high frequency ratio assessed parasympathetic tone and sympathovagal balance, while PPGA served as a surrogate for peripheral sympathetic tone. Simulated effect-site concentrations (CeP) were calculated to examine relationships between concentration dynamics and autonomic responses.Results:Forty-four patients were analyzed. Compared with MB, TCI produced significantly more gradual and attenuated changes in high-frequency, low-frequency, and low-to-high frequency ratio (all p < 0.05), along with slower and smaller increases in PPGA, indicating better preservation of autonomic balance and milder peripheral sympatholysis. Simulated CeP reached 5.5 μg·mL−1 at 117 seconds with TCI vs 36 seconds with MB. Greater transient autonomic disturbance was associated with a faster rise in CeP, rather than absolute CeP values, indicating that delivery mode and rate were primary determinants of autonomic modulation.Conclusion:TCI provided smoother autonomic modulation than did MB during the physiologically unstable induction phase. By enabling real-time detection of short-lived fluctuations, WBSA revealed mechanistic differences not captured by conventional analyses, supporting TCI as a potentially more stable and physiologically balanced induction strategy, particularly for patients at risk of peri-induction instability.Keywords: Anesthesia, Heart rate variability, Manual bolus, Propofol, Target-controlled infusion

1. INTRODUCTION

Anesthetic agents alter autonomic function, affecting heart rate, blood pressure, and vascular tone. Excessive intraoperative autonomic fluctuations can worsen outcomes, particularly in vulnerable patients.1,2 Among all phases of anesthesia, induction is the most unstable, characterized by rapid transitions in consciousness and systemic physiology that may trigger abrupt autonomic disturbances. Despite its clinical importance, autonomic modulation during induction remains poorly characterized because most studies rely on conventional heart rate variability (HRV) metrics or single timepoint assessments that fail to capture transient, non-stationary dynamics.3–7

Propofol, a commonly used intravenous anesthetic, exerts complex effects on autonomic regulation. It typically suppresses parasympathetic activity and variably inhibits sympathetic tone,8–11 reflected in HRV indices, including reduced high-frequency power and elevated low-to-high frequency ratio (LHR), indicating altered sympathovagal balance. However, prior studies largely focus on longer post-induction periods and overlook rapid short-term changes that may be clinically relevant. During induction, the statistical properties of electrocardiogram-derived HRV signals, including their spectral content, can change rapidly, violating the stationarity assumption underlying conventional Fourier-based HRV analysis, which relies on time-averaged frequency components.3 Wavelet-based spectral analysis (WBSA) overcomes this limitation by preserving both temporal and frequency resolution, enabling real-time detection of rapid autonomic changes.4 As such, WBSA is well-suited for assessing instability during the early phase of anesthetic induction.

In clinical practice, propofol is administered either as a manual bolus (MB) or via target-controlled infusion (TCI). Rapid bolus administration, as used in this protocol, ensures a fast onset but may cause abrupt autonomic disturbances from sudden effect-site concentration (CeP) peaks. In contrast, TCI delivers propofol more gradually and may reduce hemodynamic swings.12,13 Despite their widespread use, the real-time autonomic effects of these approaches during induction remain unclear. To address this gap, we conducted a prospective study comparing MB and TCI using WBSA of HRV and pulse photoplethysmography amplitude (PPGA). PPGA reflects peripheral sympathetic tone, with vasoconstriction reducing pulse amplitude and sympathetic withdrawal increasing PPGA.14 In this framework, PPGA represents sympathetic activity, high-frequency power indicates parasympathetic tone, and LHR represents sympathovagal balance. We hypothesized that TCI would provide smoother autonomic modulation than MB during the unstable induction phase, reflected by more gradual temporal changes and smaller fluctuations in HRV-derived indices (high-frequency power and LHR) and PPGA, and that WBSA would detect transient fluctuations missed by conventional analyses.

2. METHODS

2.1. Patient selection

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital (No. 2019-03-003CC) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03954262, 2019/03). Sixty patients with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I to II, scheduled for elective surgery under general anesthesia with propofol induction (TCI or MB), were prospectively enrolled. Exclusion criteria included preoperative sedatives or opioid use within 1 week, emergency surgery, and renal, hepatic, cardiac, or respiratory dysfunction. All participants provided written informed consent.

2.2. Anesthesia management and data acquisition

Patients were preoxygenated with 100% oxygen via face mask for 2 to 3 minutes in the supine position. To minimize respiratory influences on HRV, patients were instructed to breathe in synchrony with a metronome set at 10 breaths/min (0.167 Hz) and to refrain from speaking.

Propofol (Propofol-Lipuro 1%; B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) was administered via a 20-G forearm IV catheter using one of the following protocols:

TCI group: Propofol was delivered using a TCI system (Injectomat TIVA Agilia; Fresenius Kabi, Bad Homburg, Germany) with an initial target CeP of 4 to 6 µg/mL based on the Schnider model.15

TCI group: Propofol was delivered using a TCI system (Injectomat TIVA Agilia; Fresenius Kabi, Bad Homburg, Germany) with an initial target CeP of 4 to 6 µg/mL based on the Schnider model.15

MB group: Propofol was administered as a rapid MB at 1.5 to 2.5 mg/kg over 10 seconds.

MB group: Propofol was administered as a rapid MB at 1.5 to 2.5 mg/kg over 10 seconds.

Group allocation was determined by equipment availability and the attending anesthesiologist’s clinical judgment; no formal randomization, allocation concealment, or blinding procedures were implemented. Following loss of consciousness, patients were mask-ventilated with 100% oxygen (tidal volume 6-8 mL/kg, respiratory rate 10 breaths/min) to maintain normocapnia. No external stimuli other than airway maneuvers were applied. Continuous electrocardiography and pulse oximetry signals were recorded using a multichannel polygraph system (Embla N7000; Natus, Pleasanton, CA) at a sampling rate of 1024 Hz for offline HRV and PPGA analyses.

2.3. Continuous wavelet analysis of autonomic temporal changes

Autonomic responses during early induction were analyzed using a 2-min window following propofol administration. The electrocardiogram was band-pass filtered (0.5-40 Hz) to eliminate noise and baseline drift. R-peaks were detected using a custom algorithm and manually verified by a trained technician. Only normal-to-normal, arrhythmia-free intervals were included. These intervals were resampled at 8 Hz (0.125-second intervals) using cubic spline interpolation to generate an evenly spaced time series. High- and low-frequency power bands were computed every second by convolving the resampled signals with a Morlet wavelet.

2.4. Beat-to-beat PPGA analysis

Beat-to-beat data were extracted from the pulse oximetry waveform, and second-by-second mean PPGA was calculated as a surrogate marker of sympathetic tone.14,16

2.5. Data normalization and analysis

Min–max normalization was applied to reduce interindividual variability in signal dynamic range. Unlike percentage change from baseline, this method captures the full range of within-subject fluctuations, enabling comparison of temporal response patterns across individuals. 2.6. Pharmacokinetic simulations

CePs were calculated using the Schnider model implemented in the Tivatrainer software (version 9.1; EuroSIVA, Leiden, the Netherlands).15

2.7. Data analysis

Normality of age, height, weight, and body mass index in the TCI and MB groups was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Intergroup comparisons were performed using two-tailed t tests for continuous variables and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Autonomic responses over time were evaluated using a nonlinear mixed-effects model, with interindividual variability modeled separately from intraindividual changes. Polynomial regression was applied, and nonlinearity was considered significant when cubic or quadratic terms contributed significantly to the model (t > 3.84).

3.2. HRV analyses

The TCI group exhibited gradual, attenuated changes in autonomic indices during induction. Low- and high-frequency power (Fig. 2A, B) declined more linearly, and LHR (Fig. 2C) remained relatively stable, indicating better preservation of parasympathetic tone and sympathovagal balance. In contrast, the MB group showed rapid reductions in low- and high-frequency power with a marked LHR increase, consistent with parasympathetic withdrawal and sympathetic dominance. 3.3. PPGA analysis

Mixed-effects regression analysis of PPGA showed that the TCI group maintained a gradual increase in normalized PPGA (Fig. 2D), indicating attenuated sympathetic inhibition and reduced autonomic disturbance. In contrast, the MB group demonstrated a steeper PPGA rise, consistent with greater peripheral sympatholysis.

3.4. Pharmacokinetic simulation and HRV correlation

Simulated CeP profiles (Fig. 3) demonstrated distinct temporal patterns between groups. In the TCI group, CeP increased gradually and remained relatively stable, whereas in the MB group, CeP rose more rapidly, reaching comparable concentrations earlier and with greater temporal variability. Specifically, CeP reached 5.5 µg·mL−1 at 36 seconds in the MB group, compared with 117 seconds in the TCI group.

4. DISCUSSION

In this prospective study, propofol induction via TCI produced smoother autonomic modulation than MB, as reflected by HRV and PPGA. TCI better preserved high-frequency power, maintained a more stable LHR, and induced a slower, more gradual PPGA increase, indicating preserved parasympathetic tone, balanced sympathovagal modulation, and attenuated peripheral sympatholysis. In contrast, MB caused larger autonomic fluctuations, including elevated LHR, greater high-frequency suppression, and an earlier, steeper PPGA rise. Importantly, these differences were not attributable to absolute CePs but were temporally associated with differences in the rate of propofol delivery during induction.

HRV monitoring during anesthetic induction provides critical insights into autonomic stability and patient safety. Because induction involves rapid autonomic shifts immediately after drug administration, high–temporal-resolution monitoring is essential to capture short-lived events. Conventional Fourier-based HRV methods average signals over time and therefore miss transient changes. In contrast, WBSA preserves time–frequency resolution, enabling detection of dynamic alterations in HRV and PPGA during this unstable phase.17,18

Propofol induces marked autonomic changes. In this study, although both MB and TCI reduced normalized low and high-frequency power, the decline in high-frequency power—indicating parasympathetic withdrawal—was significantly smaller with TCI. Likewise, the increase in normalized LHR—reflecting sympathetic predominance—was less pronounced with TCI. This pattern paralleled heart rate responses: the TCI group maintained relative stability, whereas the MB group exhibited a transient heart rate spike followed by a cubic-pattern decline converging with TCI values at approximately 120 seconds. Given that HRV primarily reflects parasympathetic modulation and is less sensitive to sympathetic activity,19 the greater high-frequency suppression and LHR elevation with MB are consistent with vagolytic effects and transient sympathetic predominance.18 Previous studies comparing MB with plasma concentration–targeted TCI reported minimal hemodynamic differences20 or modest heart rate reductions with TCI (72 vs 68 beats/min).21 However, these studies did not specify bolus speed or duration and relied on single post-induction heart rate measurements. Earlier investigations mainly focused on heart rate and blood pressure, without a detailed evaluation of autonomic components. Therefore, differences in bolus speed and the lack of high–temporal-resolution monitoring may explain why prior studies reported minimal hemodynamic differences, whereas we observed pronounced, short-lived autonomic fluctuations during the early induction phase. In contrast, continuous high–temporal-resolution monitoring in our study revealed pronounced, short-lived autonomic and heart rate fluctuations during MB induction, underscoring the value of WBSA for capturing dynamic autonomic changes.

Our findings are consistent with prior evidence that propofol exerts both vagolytic and sympatholytic effects. Reported responses include transient tachycardia and peripheral α-adrenergic blockade,10 reductions in cardiac parasympathetic tone dependent on hypnotic depth,3 and decreased sympathetic nerve activity in peroneal nerve recordings.22 Dose- and age-dependent variations have also been described: in older adults (>65 years), induction doses of 0.5 or 1.5 mg·kg−1 reduce high-frequency power and increase LHR,11 whereas during conscious sedation, propofol decreases LHR and increases high-frequency power.23 More recently, a comparative trial showed that propofol increased low-frequency power and decreased high-frequency power, shifting toward sympathetic predominance, whereas remimazolam preserved autonomic balance.24

PPGA serves as a surrogate marker of peripheral sympathetic tone, with lower values indicating vasoconstriction.16 Robinson et al10 demonstrated that propofol causes an early decline in arterial pressure preceding a significant PPGA rise—supporting the view that this increase reflects peripheral adrenergic blockade rather than a compensatory reflex. In our study, MB induction produced an abrupt PPGA increase consistent with rapid sympatholysis and vasodilation, whereas TCI elicited a more gradual and modest rise. These findings suggest that propofol’s parasympatholytic and sympatholytic effects are more pronounced with MB, while TCI provides smoother autonomic modulation.

The calculated CeP profiles showed that patients in the TCI group reached target concentrations more slowly than those in the MB group (Fig. 3). When both groups achieved a CeP of 5.5 µg·mL−1, autonomic changes remained more pronounced with MB, suggesting that the rate of CeP increase contributed to these differences. This interpretation is supported by animal studies showing greater blood pressure reductions with rapid propofol injection,25 and human studies in which higher administration rates (400, 600, and 800 mL·h−1) produced progressively larger decreases in mean arterial pressure (8.2, 9.8, and 11.4 mmHg, respectively),26 with similar trends reported in older patients.27 Notably, these studies found no heart rate differences between rates, likely due to reliance on intermittent rather than continuous heart rate measurements.

Overall, our findings indicate that TCI provides a more stable autonomic profile than MB during the physiologically unstable induction phase. In patients at elevated risk of peri-induction instability, TCI may offer a more physiologically balanced approach to propofol administration by attenuating parasympathetic withdrawal, limiting sympathetic predominance, and reducing peripheral sympatholysis.

This study has several limitations. First, our analysis was restricted to the early phase of anesthetic induction. Although clinically relevant, this focus does not capture longer-term autonomic effects. We targeted this period because rapid, subtle fluctuations immediately after drug administration are rarely described, despite their potential clinical importance. Second, only the Schnider pharmacokinetic model was used for TCI, and results may differ from other models. Third, as this was an observational study, propofol dosing in the MB group reflected routine clinical practice, with doses determined by the attending anesthesiologist rather than a fixed protocol. Although this could theoretically influence autonomic responses, dose variability within the MB group was limited (mean 2.06 ± 0.13 mg/kg), making it unlikely to be a major confounder; therefore, bolus dose was not included as an independent variable. While our primary analyses focused on autonomic responses at matched simulated CePs to reduce confounding from dose and administration rate, a formal dose–response analysis was not performed. Consequently, subtle dose-dependent autonomic effects cannot be completely excluded and warrant further investigation in future studies using controlled incremental dosing or concentration–autonomic response modeling. Although the study was not powered to draw definitive conclusions about the concentration–HRV relationship, it provides evidence that the rate of concentration change, rather than absolute dose, may be important. Additionally, electroencephalography-derived measures of anesthetic depth were not incorporated. Finally, the non-randomized design, with group allocation based on equipment availability and anesthesiologist discretion, introduces potential selection bias. The inclusion of relatively healthy patients with low ASA scores may further limit generalizability to higher-risk populations or those with baseline autonomic dysfunction.

In conclusion, propofol induction via TCI resulted in smoother and more controlled autonomic modulation than MB, characterized by attenuated parasympathetic withdrawal, reduced sympathetic predominance, and milder peripheral sympatholysis. These effects were reflected in smaller HRV and PPGA fluctuations during the early induction phase. By enabling real-time detection of transient autonomic shifts, WBSA provided insights into peri-induction autonomic physiology that conventional methods cannot capture. For patients at increased risk of peri-induction instability, TCI may offer a more physiologically balanced induction strategy. Future studies should examine autonomic modulation with TCI and MB in higher-risk populations and across different pharmacokinetic models.
