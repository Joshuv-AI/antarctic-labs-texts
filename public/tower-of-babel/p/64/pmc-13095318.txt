Assessment tools for frailty in elderly adults: A scoping review
Authors: Danjuan Ye, Jingyi Zhao, Shaomei Cui, Lixia Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:As global population aging intensifies, health issues among the elderly are receiving increasing attention. Frailty, a syndrome characterized by age-related decline across multiple systems, has become a central concern in geriatric medicine. Early screening and assessment of frailty are crucial, yet practitioners lack a systematic and comprehensive understanding of assessment tools due to constraints imposed by cultural contexts, healthcare resources, and population characteristics.Methods:A scoping review was conducted following the 5-stage methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley. A systematic search was performed using a combination of subject terms and free terms across 4 databases: PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Web of Science Core Collection. The search period covered from the establishment of each database to June 27, 2024, with gray literature excluded. Two authors independently screened the literature based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, and discrepancies were resolved by team consensus. Data from included studies were extracted into tables, and results were collated through descriptive overview and thematic synthesis. The review report adhered to the standards outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Scrutiny Reviews guidelines. The study protocol was officially filed with the Open Science Framework.Results:A total of 18,141 documents were retrieved, and 27 studies involving 14 types of frailty assessment tools were ultimately included. Analysis revealed a wide variety of assessment tools currently available, with significant differences in the design of assessment dimensions and usage scenarios between tools. The Fried Frailty Phenotype and Tilburg Frailty Indicator are 2 commonly used assessment tools. The selection of accurate and effective frailty screening tools by assessors is crucial for improving the identification of frail individuals and facilitating intervention.Conclusion:When selecting frailty assessment tools for elderly adults, it is essential to comprehensively consider factors such as the demographic characteristics of the target population, the resources available within the clinical setting, and the performance of the tools themselves. Currently, there remains a lack of globally standardized criteria for frailty assessment. Future research should focus on integrating existing evidence to develop multidimensional assessment tools tailored to diverse elderly adult populations, thereby enabling dynamic evaluation.

1. Introduction

The condition of frailty is gaining international attention as the population of older adults rises globally. Frailty is recognized as an age-related clinical syndrome for which no universally accepted definition currently exists. All older adults are at risk of developing frailty, characterized by the decline of multiple physiological systems and increased susceptibility to stressors.[1] It affects the quality of life of the elderly population, significantly increasing the risk of adverse health outcomes such as falls, depression, and cognitive impairment, while also being positively correlated with key public health indicators such as hospitalization rates and mortality rates. It is the most serious public health challenge facing aging societies in the 21st century.[2]

As the population ages, frailty is becoming increasingly common. A report covering 62 countries worldwide indicates that the prevalence of frailty exhibits an age-graded pattern. Among community-dwelling individuals, the prevalence of frailty is 11% among those aged 50 to 59, approximately 10% among those aged 65 and older, rising to 25% to 50% among those aged 85 and older, and reaching 51% among those aged 90 and older.[3] Elderly adults in acute care hospitals and nursing homes, people in low- and middle-income countries, and socially disadvantaged populations are at higher risk of frailty.[4] For personalized, patient-centered management of frail elderly patients, it is particularly important to accurately screen and identify frail individuals.[5]

Although scholars at home and abroad have developed many frailty assessment tools over the past 30 years, assessors still lack a globally standardized assessment strategy for tool selection due to factors such as cultural adaptability, health resource allocation, and applicable populations.[6] The diversity of assessment tools has led to confusion in clinical practice, and the value of their assessment results for precise intervention and health management is also worth considering.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically synthesize existing evidence concerning assessment tools for frailty in older adults, their applicable scenarios, and conditions. Furthermore, this review endeavors to identify gaps in the current literature and propose directions for future research to enhance the identification and management of frailty risk in older adults, ultimately improving health outcomes and quality of life among this population.

2. Methods

2.1. Research design

We used the 5 steps outlined by Arksey and O’Malley[7] for defining the scope of a scoping review, including identifying the research question; searching for and identifying relevant studies; selecting relevant studies; charting the data; and organizing, summarizing, and reporting the results to guide this review. Additionally, our primary objective was to comprehensively map the existing literature, identify key concepts, and examine the application scenarios and assessment purposes when selecting assessment tools, as well as the characteristics of the assessment subjects. The results of the review were reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Scrutiny Reviews guidelines.[8] The scoping review protocol has been published in the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/hg4qz/) registration.

2.2. Research question

Based on the scoping review title and identified objectives, a scope review was conducted to answer the research questions: What assessment tools are available for assessing frailty in older adults? and What are the conditions for using assessment tools?

2.3. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for literature as follows: the research subjects were elderly patients, including elderly inpatients, community-dwelling elderly adults, and residents of care homes; primary literature concerning the development, testing, revision, localization, and application of frailty assessment tools for elderly adults; study types encompassing cross-sectional, longitudinal, cohort, and case-control studies; and information sources comprising academic publications in peer-reviewed journals. Exclusion criteria were as follows: duplicates; absence of full-text versions; conference abstracts, editorials, letters, and books; and articles not explicitly evaluating frailty assessment tools. The literature screening process involved automated methods to identify duplicate articles. Additionally, 2 authors independently assessed the titles, abstracts, and full texts of identified articles. Any discrepancies in their assessments were resolved through consensus within the research team.

2.4. Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across 4 databases, namely PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Web of Science Core Collection. The search period ranged from the establishment of the databases to June 27, 2024. A combination of subject terms and free-text keywords was used for the search. Taking PubMed as an example, the search terms were “Frailty,” “Assessment,” “Tool,” and “aged.” The literature search was conducted independently by 2 experienced researchers. The final search was conducted in June 2024. The search strategy for PubMed is provided in Table 1 as an example.

2.5. Search outcomes

Two researchers searched the identified databases, yielding 18,141 articles from 4 databases (PubMed = 4642, Scopus = 3355, Embase = 5913, Web of Science = 4231). All data were imported into EndNote X9.3.3, and 5343 duplicate records were removed. Abstracts were read for screening and data extraction, with 324 articles included for review. After full-text screening, 27 articles were ultimately included. The screening process is shown in Figure 1.

2.6. Bias risk (quality) assessment

Although quality assessment is not mandatory for scoping reviews, it was incorporated to enhance the credibility of our findings, in line with recommendations by Daudt et al.[9] Two independent reviewers (DJY and JYZ) assessed the methodological quality of the included instruments using the Consensus-Based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments bias risk checklist.[10] This evaluation encompassed the following domains: internal consistency, reliability, measurement error, content validity, structural validity, construct validity, translation, and responsiveness. Employing the minimum score principle and adhering to predefined criteria, the instruments were rated as “excellent,” “good,” “fair,” or “poor” by researchers.

2.7. Data extraction and synthesis

The full-text versions of collected articles were imported into EndNote for management, and a data extraction form was created to assist in addressing the research questions, including an assessment tool, target population, applicable scenarios, scale items, professional and equipment requirements for assessment, and assessment duration. Two authors independently extracted the relevant information, and disagreements were negotiated by the research team. The data were compared and summarized once the extraction was completed.

3. Results

3.1. Literature search and selection process

The database search yielded 18,141 articles, of which 4642 were from PubMed; 3355 were from Scopus; 5913 were from Embase; and 4231 were from Web of Science. After removing duplicate articles, 12,798 articles remained for further screening. Following the recommendations of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Scrutiny Reviews, the Programme of Research on the Integration of Services for the Maintenance of Autonomy flowchart describes how articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded, as shown in Figure 1. All study titles and abstracts were screened, resulting in the exclusion of 12,474 studies, leaving 324 for full-text review. After reviewing all studies deemed eligible for the review, we excluded 12 articles for which full-text access was unavailable; 79 studies that did not focus on the application of frailty assessment tools in elderly patients; 87 review articles; and 119 articles comparing 2 or more assessment tools.

3.2. Characteristics of sources of evidence

Of the 27 studies included in the final review, all were designed to validate the reliability and validity of frailty assessment tools and to conduct cross-cultural validation, with 13 being cross-sectional studies,[11–23] 8 methodological validation studies,[24–31] 3 prospective cohort studies,[21,32,33] and 2 observational studies.[26,30] The literature included spans from 2016 to 2024, and based on the country of publication, 6 studies were conducted in Turkey,[11,15,22–24,26] 5 studies were conducted in China,[17,18,33–35] 3 of them were in Iran,[20,31,36] Poland,[12] Denmark,[13] Brazil,[25] Portugal,[14] Ethiopia,[16] Greece,[27] Sweden,[19] Malaysia,[28] Spain,[29] Thailand,[37] France,[21] and Singapore,[30] with 1 report each. A total of 14 assessment tools were reported, namely the Comprehensive Frailty Assessment Instrument Plus (CFAI-Plus) scale,[36] Edmonton Frailty Scale (EFS),[12,24] FRAIL scale,[17,23,29,33] Frailty Groupe Iso-Ressource Evaluation (FRAGIRE) scale,[21] Fried Frailty Phenotype (FFP),[22] Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI),[26,34] HALFT scale,[25] Pictorial Fit-Frail Scale (PFFS),[27,28] PRISMA-7 scale,[13,30] Reported Edmonton Frailty Scale (REFS),[37] SARC-F questionnaire,[11] Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS),[14,15,32,35] Social Frailty Scale (SFS-8),[31] and Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI).[16,18–20] The application of tools is guided by clinical outcomes, with 78.6% of frailty tools incorporating morbidity, mortality, and 30-day mortality into their core outcome measures. Among them, the CFS[14,15,32,35] and the PRISMA-7 scale[13,30] perform exceptionally well in predicting mortality and healthcare resource utilization. 3.3. Characteristics of frailty assessment tools included in the literature

3.3.1. Basic characteristics of frailty assessment tools

All assessment tools included in the study were able to determine the presence of frailty, but the assessment results of 4 frailty assessment tools (CFAI-Plus scale, HALFT scale, PRISMA-7 scale, SARC-F questionnaire) did not report the degree of frailty. Among the 14 frailty assessment tools, the number of items in the HALFT scale was not explicitly stated in the original literature. The CFS and PFFS scales, due to their unique design, did not include traditional items and were therefore excluded from the comparison of item counts. The remaining 11 assessment tools had scale item counts ranging from 5 to 19 items. These include patients’ demographic characteristics, nutritional status, activity tolerance, cognitive function, walking ability, fall risk, disease status, frequency of social activities, emotional state, and some laboratory test data.

3.3.2. Conditions and requirements for frailty assessment

There are 4 frailty assessment tools that require special instruments and equipment. The FRAIL series of scales require a gait speed measurement tool, the FRAGIRE scale requires functional assessment equipment such as a grip strength meter and a timer, the FFP assessment requires a grip strength meter and a weighing scale, and the PFFS assessment requires visual aids such as pictorial assessment materials. Six frailty assessment tools – EFS, FRAIL scale, FFP, REFS, SARC-F questionnaire, and CFS – must be administered by researchers who have undergone specialized training or by professional clinical healthcare personnel. When using the FFP for assessment, biological indicators must be obtained, and assessors are required to calculate the results based on these indicators. By contrast, only 5 assessment tools (GFI, HALFT scale, PRISMA-7 scale, SFS-8, and TFI) do not require specialized equipment or professional qualifications to complete the assessment. These assessments can be conducted by ordinary community workers and volunteers.

3.3.3. Timing and duration of frailty assessment

This review mentions 8 frailty assessment tools (CFAI-Plus scale, FFRAIL scale, GFI, HALFT scale, PFFS, PRISMA-7 scale, SFS-8, and TFI). Among these, the FFRAIL scale, GFI, and HALFT scales, as with the SARC-F questionnaire and CFS, may also be employed for frailty assessment during hospital admissions or outpatient visits. Three frailty assessment tools (EFS, REFS, and FFP) are frequently employed in clinical preoperative assessments. Two frailty assessment tools (PRISMA-7 scale and CFS) were reported in the literature to have measurement times <1 minute. Five frailty assessment tools (EFS, FRAIL series scales, PFFS, SARC-F questionnaire, and TFI) took <5 minutes to complete. With the exception of the FRAGIRE scale, which required approximately 45 minutes, most assessment tools took between 5 and 15 minutes to administer. 4. Discussion

This study systematically identified and evaluated 14 frailty assessment tools used in elderly populations through a systematic literature review, aiming to clarify the characteristics and current application status of tools in the field of geriatric frailty assessment. The findings revealed significant heterogeneity in tool design and differentiated application scenarios within this field, providing evidence-based guidance for tool selection in clinical practice and research.

This study identified significant heterogeneity among different frailty assessment tools for older adults, primarily manifested in the structure of items and the dimensions assessed. The CFAI-Plus scale, EFS, GFI, and TFI achieve a multidimensional comprehensive evaluation of frailty by integrating assessments of physiological function, psychological state, nutrition, and social support. Among these, the TFI assessment scale has been identified in this study as a frequently employed tool by scholars. It has undergone cross-cultural adaptation by researchers from multiple countries, including China, Sweden, Ethiopia, and Iran.

TFI is a questionnaire tool developed in 2010 by Dutch researchers Gobbens et al.[38] It is primarily designed to assess self-reported frailty status among older adults in community settings. The questionnaire comprises 2 sections. The first covers factors influencing aging, life experiences, demographic characteristics, and chronic disease status. The second section addresses 3 dimensions of frailty: physical, social, and socio-emotional. This includes weight loss, walking difficulties, deteriorating health, balance problems, visual impairment, hearing loss, reduced grip strength, fatigue, solitary living, social support, interpersonal relationships, diminished cognitive function, anxiety disorders, depressive symptoms, and inadequate coping abilities. The total score for part 2 ranges from 0 to 15 points, with a score of 5 or above indicating frailty. Higher scores denote more pronounced frailty. The Kappa coefficient reached 0.79, whilst the Cronbach α coefficient value was 0.73. Dong et al[18] employed the Chinese version of the TFI to assess the condition of 917 community-dwelling elderly individuals aged over 65 in a specific region of China. The internal consistency reliability was found to be good (Cronbach alpha coefficient = 0.71). Test-retest reliability was high (correlation coefficient r = 0.88). The study found that this tool effectively predicts adverse health outcomes and healthcare resource utilization, facilitating a deeper understanding of health resource consumption among community-dwelling older adults. It enables rapid implementation of effective interventions to mitigate the adverse consequences of aging.

By contrast, the FRAIL scale, FFP, and the SARC-F questionnaire are unidimensional, focusing solely on physiological indicators without incorporating psychological or social evaluations. This difference in assessment tool design reflects varying requirements for social research and clinical management evaluations across distinct elderly populations. For instance, a study examining preoperative frailty assessment in patients undergoing radical cystectomy highlighted that physiology-based evaluation methods are most useful for this cohort, with the FFP.[39] It is noteworthy that despite marked differences in the structure of assessment items and evaluation dimensions across various assessment tools, their findings consistently indicate an elevated risk of adverse health outcomes such as postoperative reoperation rates, readmission rates, and mortality.[40] This suggests that frailty itself constitutes a key independent risk factor, rendering the assessment results from different tools universally valuable as early warning indicators.

This review found that the suitability of assessment tools is highly dependent on specific demographic characteristics and application contexts. This is not only reflected in the design philosophy, such as the PFFS employing visualized items to effectively overcome textual barriers and reduce assessment requirements for patients with cognitive impairment or low literacy levels, thereby demonstrating inclusivity towards populations with low health literacy, which aligns with research by Ysea-Hill et al.[41] This is also reflected in assessment methodologies, such as the CFS, which relies on clinicians’ holistic observations and experiential judgements, rendering it more suitable for dynamic evaluations of intensive care units or rehabilitation medicine departments.[42] In resource-constrained emergency settings, the PRISMA-7 scale comprising just 7 items and taking under 1 minute, serves as an efficient screening tool. It rapidly identifies frailty in elderly emergency patients and holds predictive value for individual 28-day mortality risk and intensive care unit admission.[43]

In terms of assessor qualifications, at least 6 tools (EFS, FRAIL scale, FFP, REFS, SARC-F questionnaire, and CFS) must be administered by researchers who have undergone standardized training or by clinically qualified healthcare professionals. For instance, the REFS requires healthcare professionals to complete frailty assessments by synthesizing information provided by patients or their families.[37] Meanwhile, the FFP necessitates strict adherence to measured values for its 5 biomarkers (body weight, grip strength, walking speed, fatigue, and physical activity), demanding high operational standardization.[22] The CFS relies on the clinician’s observation and experience regarding the patient’s overall condition, necessitating assessment by an experienced clinician.[14,32] Concurrently, 5 frailty assessment tools (GFI, HALFT scale, PRISMA-7 scale, SFS-8, and TFI) were identified that require no specialist training for administration. They can be administered by family members, community workers, or volunteers and are frequently employed for screening frailty risk among elderly individuals in community settings.[44]

This review identified studies from 15 countries examining frailty assessment tools for elderly adults, covering a broad geographical scope. This indicates increasing global engagement in managing frailty among the elderly. However, it warrants consideration that frailty is a dynamic condition, with only 2 assessment tools (FRAGIRE scale and the SFS-8) mentioning the timing for reassessment. For individuals identified as being in the early stages of frailty or already frail during initial screening, dynamic monitoring of their progression and evaluation of intervention outcomes are required. Concurrently, for those not showing signs of frailty at initial assessment, strategies for regular dynamic evaluation should be established to enable early detection and intervention. Continuous, effective monitoring holds significant reference value for clinical interventions.[45] However, at present, most existing assessment tools lack clearly defined reevaluation timings for the dynamic monitoring of frailty risk. In light of this, it is imperative to further develop, validate, and disseminate sustainable frailty assessment tools suitable for elderly populations across diverse clinical settings, cultural contexts, and health statuses. This will enable the identification of individuals at potential frailty risk, facilitate dynamic assessment of frailty severity, and inform the formulation of appropriate follow-up strategies. Such measures will enhance the capacity for precise, continuous frailty management, ultimately advancing the goal of healthy aging.

Frailty in elderly adults stands as a pivotal issue within contemporary geriatric medicine and public health. The development and application of frailty assessment tools are evolving towards greater diversity and refinement. In practice, selecting and implementing assessment tools necessitates comprehensive consideration of multiple factors, including the specific demographic characteristics of the population being assessed, the clinical setting and resource allocation, as well as the inherent performance characteristics of the tool itself. Nevertheless, despite the expanding array of assessment tools, there remains no universally recognized, universally applicable “gold standard” assessment instrument. This limitation impedes the comparability of research findings across studies, the standardization of clinical pathways, and the formulation of consistent public health strategies. Future research should integrate existing evidence to design multidimensional assessment tools tailored to diverse elderly populations, enabling dynamic evaluation.

5. Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. First, although a nonexclusive approach was adopted and 4 databases were searched, some publications may have been missed. Second, the search was limited to English, which may have resulted in the omission of sources published in other languages. Excluding non-English studies may introduce language bias, as research from non-English-speaking regions or countries may be overlooked. This could result in missing a significant portion of relevant research, including valuable contributions and insights critical to a comprehensive understanding of this topic. Due to the nature of the study scope, the results cannot be generalized, and despite efforts to review the references of the studies, data on the validation of the tools used may have been overlooked.

6. Conclusion

The development and application of frailty assessment tools for the elderly are influenced by a combination of factors, including national healthcare resource allocation, regional cultural environments, and the heterogeneity of tool design. The diverse designs of existing tools are a necessary response to complex clinical scenarios (such as emergency screening, community health management, and research stratification), but they also highlight the contradiction between the lack of a gold standard and the need for universality. In the future, it is hoped that technological advancements will enable more precise assessments, provide evidence-based support for individualized health management through multi-scenario adaptation, and optimize public health resource allocation, ultimately reducing frailty-related morbidity and disease burden among the elderly population. This process not only requires technological innovation and academic collaboration but also policy support and the deep integration of multidisciplinary teams to address the global challenge of aging.
