S100A9 deletion ameliorates acute lung injury by attenuating excessive neutrophil activation via activating the NRF2/HO-1 signaling axis
Authors: Shuang Yin, Xinyu Hu, Hao Cai, Ruoxuan Jiang, Jiali Li, Yan Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute lung injury (ALI) is a complex and life-threatening condition. In severe cases, ALI can progress to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), which is associated with high mortality. It is characterized by diffuse pulmonary parenchymal inflammation and refractory hypoxemia. Neutrophils, as pivotal immune cells in the lungs, play a critical role in the development and progression of ALI. This study aimed to determine whether S100A9 deletion alleviates LPS-induced ALI by modulating neutrophil activation through the NRF2/HO-1 signaling axis.MethodsWe established an ALI model using LPS in both wild-type (WT) and S100A9 gene knockout (KO) mice to assess pulmonary inflammation and oxidative stress. Lung injury severity, inflammation, oxidative stress status, neutrophil accumulation, and NET formation were evaluated in vivo, and bone marrow-derived primary neutrophils were exposed to LPS in vitro to examine their activation characteristics and antioxidant capacity.ResultsS100A9 KO significantly ameliorated lung tissue injury, edema, and inflammatory cell infiltration, and improved mouse survival rates. Mechanistically, S100A9 knockout alleviates acute lung injury by activating the NRF2 signaling pathway, which in turn downregulates neutrophil activation and the production of NETs.ConclusionOur findings indicate that S100A9 deletion attenuates neutrophil activation and oxidative stress and may alleviate LPS-induced ALI through activation of the NRF2/HO-1 signaling pathway.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03675-6.

Introduction

Acute Lung Injury (ALI) and its more severe form, Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS), represent a spectrum of inflammatory pulmonary disorders, present with respiratory distress, pulmonary edema, impaired gas exchange, systemic inflammatory response, hypoxemia, and reduced lung compliance [1, 2]. Epidemiological data show that ALI/ARDS affects approximately 3 million patients globally each year, with a mortality rate as high as 35%–50% [3, 4]. Survivors often experience long-term sequelae such as pulmonary fibrosis and cognitive dysfunction, significantly impairing their quality of life [5]. Furthermore, the treatment of ALI/ARDS consumes substantial medical resources, prolongs hospital stays, and imposes a considerable economic burden on families and society, posing a particularly severe challenge to healthcare systems in low- and middle-income countries [6, 7]. Recent research emphasizes the importance of early identification of high-risk populations, optimizing respiratory support strategies, and implementing targeted immunomodulatory therapies to improve patient prognosis and alleviate the associated healthcare burden [7].

Neutrophils, a crucial subset of myeloid leukocytes, serve as central effector cells in acute inflammatory responses and constitute the first line of innate immune defense against pathogen invasion [8]. During the early stages of infection or tissue injury, neutrophils are rapidly recruited to the site of inflammation, where they play a crucial role in phagocytosing and eliminating pathogens [9]. However, their overactivation and dysregulated inflammatory response are considered one of the core pathological mechanisms leading to alveolar-capillary barrier disruption, pulmonary edema, and respiratory failure [10]. Upon activation, neutrophils release various proteolytic enzymes, including elastase, and matrix metalloproteinases, these enzymes directly degrade the extracellular matrix and epithelial tight junction proteins in lung tissue, compromising alveolar structural integrity and thereby exacerbating the pathological progression of ALI [11, 12]. Activated neutrophils also release neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which directly damage endothelial/epithelial cells and contribute to a vicious cycle of inflammation and thrombosis [13]. Concurrently, the respiratory burst in neutrophils generates substantial amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can induce lipid peroxidation, protein and DNA damage, ultimately contributing to severe oxidative stress [14, 15]. The early stage of ARDS is characterized by a sharp increase in peripheral blood neutrophil counts, often accompanied by depletion of the bone marrow storage pool and the release of immature neutrophils into the circulation, which further drives inflammatory imbalance [16]. Furthermore, neutrophils can promote the polarization of alveolar macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype by secreting inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β and IL-10 [17]. In turn, these activated macrophages release chemokines, including CXCL8, which recruit additional neutrophils to the lung tissue. This interaction establishes a self-amplifying inflammatory loop that persistently drives the pathological progression of ALI/ARDS [18].

The S100A9 protein, a Ca²⁺-binding member of the S100 family, serves as a key regulator of neutrophil-mediated inflammation. Typically forming a heterodimer with S100A8, the S100A8/A9 complex functions as a candidate biomarker for diagnosing, monitoring, and predicting therapeutic responses in inflammatory diseases, and is jointly involved in multiple biological processes such as apoptosis and inflammatory responses [19]. In the field of pulmonary disease, evidence-based research suggests that S100A9 plays a significant regulatory role in ALI. Single-cell RNA sequencing analyses of ALI murine models and ARDS patients have revealed distinct differentially expressed gene profiles in both pulmonary and extrapulmonary injury models, with S100A9 emerging as one of the key differentially expressed genes [20]. S100A8 and S100A9 are predominantly expressed in neutrophils and monocytes, collectively constituting approximately 40% of the total cytosolic protein content in neutrophils [21]. S100A9 contributes to disease pathogenesis by mediating aberrant neutrophil activation and amplifying the inflammatory cascade [22]. Elevated expression levels of S100A9 are often positively correlated with disease progression and severity, suggesting a driving role in inflammation and pathogenesis [23]. Under infectious or inflammatory conditions, S100A9 contributes to early pathological processes by promoting leukocyte chemotaxis and upregulating the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines [24].

Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) is a master transcriptional regulator that enables cells to respond to oxidative stress and xenobiotic insults [25]. Under physiological conditions, NRF2 binds to its cytoplasmic inhibitor KEAP1 and is constitutively degraded via the ubiquitination pathway. When cells are exposed to oxidative stress or electrophilic stimuli, a conformational change in KEAP1 occurs, leading to the dissociation of NRF2 and its subsequent translocation to the nucleus [26]. Within the nucleus, NRF2 binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs) in the promoter regions of its downstream target genes, initiating the transcription of a battery of Phase II detoxifying enzymes and antioxidant proteins [27]. Among these, heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) is a key downstream effector of NRF2. It exerts potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic functions by catalyzing the degradation of heme to generate carbon monoxide, biliverdin, and free iron [28]. Studies have demonstrated that in LPS-induced ALI models, pharmacological activation or genetic enhancement of the NRF2/HO-1 signaling pathway can significantly mitigate pulmonary oxidative damage, suppress the pro-inflammatory cytokine storm, and protect the integrity of the alveolar-capillary barrier, thereby exerting a definitive lung-protective effect [29]. In inflammatory states such as ALI, excessively produced ROS and inflammatory mediators can disrupt NRF2 activation through various mechanisms-for instance, by promoting its degradation or inhibiting its nuclear translocation. This creates a vicious cycle wherein oxidative stress actively suppresses the antioxidant defense system [30, 31].

Although previous studies suggest that inhibiting S100A9 can mitigate inflammation in various models and that NRF2 activation is protective, whether S100A9 modulates LPS-ALI specifically by regulating neutrophil activation via the NRF2/HO-1 signaling axis has not been systematically elucidated or directly demonstrated. In this study, we used an S100A9-deficient mouse model to investigate the role of S100A9 in neutrophil activation and LPS-induced ALI. Furthermore, by employing a combination of in vivo and in vitro experimental systems, we assessed the pathological contribution of S100A9 to the initiation and progression of ALI.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Cat. No. L2880) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Anti-myeloperoxidase (MPO, Cat. No. ab208670) and anti-citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3, Cat. No. ab281584) antibodies were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Antibodies against interleukin-6 (IL-6, Cat. No. 6087), Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1, Cat. No. 5266), and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1, Cat. No. 5393) were purchased from Abmart (Shanghai, China). The anti-Ly6G antibody (Cat. No. 14-9668-82) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Antibodies against nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2, Cat. No. 16396) and S100A9 (Cat. No. 26992) were obtained from Proteintech Group (Wuhan, China). The anti-tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, Cat. No. BA0131) antibody was purchased from Boster Biological Technology (Wuhan, China). The BCA Protein Assay Kit (Cat. No. 20201ES76) was purchased from Epizyme Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). ELISA kits for IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were obtained from Ruixin Biotechnology (Quanzhou, China). Assay kits for malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) were purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology (Beijing, China). Neutrophils were isolated using a Mouse Neutrophil Isolation Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Primary neutrophils were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Clark Bioscience, USA) and incubated in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ at 37 °C.

LPS-induced ALI of mice

All experimental procedures performed in this study were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (Approval No. LLSC 20242517). Male C57BL/6 mice (weighing 20–25 g) were supplied by the Animal Experiment Center of Anhui Medical University. The S100A9-deficient (S100A9−/−) mice used in this study were generated on a C57BL/6 genetic background by targeted disruption of the mrp14 gene, and were generously provided by Dr. Shen Shichun. The mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility maintained at 18–22 °C with a standard 12-hour light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. Following a one-week acclimatization period, the experiments were initiated. The experimental animals were randomly allocated into control and ALI model groups, specifically: C57BL/6 control, S100A9−/− control, C57BL/6 ALI, and S100A9−/− ALI groups. Following established protocols, ALI was induced by intratracheal instillation of LPS at a dose of 5 mg/kg in the ALI groups. The control groups received an equivalent volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For histological and biochemical analyses, five mice were included in each group (n = 5). Mice were euthanized 24 h after LPS challenge, after which blood samples were collected and centrifuged to obtain serum, and lung tissues were harvested for subsequent analyses [32]. For survival analysis, a separate cohort of mice was used, including ten mice per group (n = 10) in the C57BL/6 ALI and S100A9−/− ALI groups. Following LPS administration, mice were monitored for survival for up to 150 h, and survival data were recorded to generate Kaplan–Meier survival curves.

Lung edema measurement

Pulmonary edema was evaluated by measuring lung water content. Immediately after euthanasia, lung tissues were rapidly excised and gently blotted with filter paper to remove excess surface blood and fluid. The tissues were immediately weighed to obtain the wet weight. The samples were subsequently placed in a drying oven at 60 °C for 48 h until a constant weight was achieved, after which the dry weight was recorded. The lung water content was calculated using the following formula: Water content (%) = (wet weight − dry weight) / wet weight × 100%. For comparison, the wet-to-dry (W/D) weight ratio, which is commonly used as an indicator of pulmonary edema, can be calculated as wet weight / dry weight. In addition, the lung index was calculated to further evaluate lung injury. The lung index was determined as the ratio of lung weight to body weight using the following formula: Lung index (%) = Lung weight / Body weight × 100%.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and lung injury scoring

Immediately after euthanasia, the right lower lung lobe was collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Following this, the tissue underwent gradient dehydration, clearing, and paraffin embedding. Sections of 3 μm thickness were prepared using a microtome and stained with H&E for the assessment of histopathological changes. To objectively assess the severity of lung injury, a semi-quantitative lung injury scoring system was applied based on a previously described method [33]. Histological evaluation was performed under a Leica upright light microscope at 400× magnification. Five histopathological features were assessed: (1) neutrophil infiltration in the alveolar space; (2) neutrophil infiltration in the interstitial space; (3) hyaline membrane formation; (4) proteinaceous debris in the alveolar space; (5) alveolar septal thickening. Each parameter was scored on a scale of 0–2 according to severity: Neutrophils in alveolar space: 0 = none; 1 = 1–5 cells per high-power field (HPF); 2 = > 5 cells per HPF. Neutrophils in interstitial space: 0 = none; 1 = 1–5 cells per HPF; 2 = > 5 cells per HPF. Hyaline membranes: 0 = none; 1 = one membrane; 2 = more than one membrane. Proteinaceous debris in alveolar space: 0 = none; 1 = one focus; 2 = more than one focus. Alveolar septal thickening: 0 = < 2× normal thickness; 1 = 2–4× normal thickness; 2 = > 4× normal thickness. For each sample, multiple randomly selected non-overlapping high-power fields (HPFs) were examined. The final lung injury score was calculated using the following weighted formula: Lung injury score = [(20 × A) + (14 × B) + (7 × C) + (7 × D) + (2 × E)] / (number of fields × 100). where A–E correspond to the scores of the five parameters described above. The resulting value represents a continuous variable, with higher scores indicating more severe lung injury.

Preparation of Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF) and analysis of total protein concentration and inflammatory cell counts

Mice were anesthetized, and a small incision was made in the trachea. Lungs were lavaged by instilling 3 mL of ice-cold PBS through the incision. After gentle chest massage, the fluid was aspirated. This lavage procedure was repeated three times, and a total of approximately 1.5 mL of fluid was collected into sterile EP tubes. The collected fluid was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting cell pellet and BALF supernatant were collected separately. Total cell counts were determined microscopically using a hemocytometer, and the protein concentration in the BALF supernatant was measured using a BCA assay kit.

ELISA

Following anesthesia, blood was collected from the mice and centrifuged to obtain serum. Lung tissue samples were simultaneously harvested and homogenized. The homogenates were then centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected for subsequent analysis. The concentrations of the inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in both serum and lung tissue homogenates were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, according to the manufacturers’ instructions. For lung tissue samples, cytokine levels were normalized to the total protein content of each homogenate to account for variations in tissue processing. The protein concentration of each sample was determined using a BCA protein assay kit, and cytokine levels were expressed as pg/mg protein. All experimental data were subjected to statistical analysis.

Measurement of Glutathione (GSH), Malondialdehyde (MDA), and Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) levels

Following the manufacturers’ protocols precisely, the GSH content, MDA concentration, and SOD activity in the samples were determined using commercial assay kits for GSH, MDA, and SOD, respectively.

Flow cytometry analysis

Following euthanasia, peripheral blood and bone marrow samples were collected and processed into single-cell suspensions. An appropriate number of cells were aliquoted into flow cytometry tubes and sequentially stained with fluorescently conjugated anti-mouse Ly6G and anti-mouse CD11b antibodies. The stained cells were analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), and data were processed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA). Neutrophils were defined as Ly6G⁺CD11b⁺ cells, and the percentage of Ly6G⁺CD11b⁺ cells within the total leukocyte population was used as the primary readout for quantitative analysis. The gating strategy involved the initial selection of leukocytes based on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) characteristics, followed by the identification of CD11b⁺Ly6G⁺ neutrophils. Quantitative analysis was performed using data obtained from multiple independent biological replicates, and representative plots were selected to illustrate the overall distribution patterns observed in each experimental group.

Isolation and preparation of mouse neutrophils

Bone marrow-derived primary neutrophils were isolated from 6-8-week-old WT C57BL/6 mice and S100A9 KO mice, respectively. Following cervical dislocation, the entire mouse was disinfected by immersion in 75% ethanol for 3 min. The bilateral femurs and tibias were then dissected, and the attached muscle and connective tissue were removed. The bone ends were cut off, and the bone marrow cavities were flushed with ice-cold PBS until the bones appeared white. The collected bone marrow flush was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer, centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in PBS and carefully layered onto an equal volume of mouse lymphocyte separation medium. The gradient was centrifuged at 750 × g for 30 min at room temperature with the lowest acceleration and deceleration settings. The cloudy intermediate cell layer was carefully aspirated and washed twice with PBS. Red blood cells were lysed using an erythrocyte lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation and removal of the supernatant. Finally, the cell pellet was resuspended in RPMI-1640 complete medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were seeded onto coverslips placed in 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well. The experimental group was treated with medium containing 1.5 ug/mL LPS for 6 h, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS. After treatment, the cells were collected for subsequent experiments [34].

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded mouse lung tissue blocks were sectioned at 4 μm thickness and mounted onto slides. After deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through a graded ethanol series, antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating the sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Sections were then blocked with goat serum blocking solution for 30 min at room temperature. Following PBS washes, 50 µL of primary antibody was applied, and the slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. After another wash step, a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody was added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used as the chromogen for color development. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min, followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series, clearing in xylene, and mounting with neutral resin. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with a 40× objective lens. Under microscopic examination, positive staining was identified as brownish-yellow or brown granular signals, while cell nuclei were counterstained blue.

Immunofluorescence staining of cells and lung tissue

Primary neutrophils in culture were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 20 min. After washing with PBS, the cells were blocked with goat serum blocking solution at room temperature for 30 min. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with the primary antibody diluted (1:200) in antibody dilution buffer (Biosharp Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After three washes with PBS, the samples were incubated with the appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody diluted (1:500) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Finally, the cells were washed with PBST and stained with DAPI for 30 min at room temperature to label the nuclei. Additionally, paraffin-embedded mouse lung tissue blocks were sectioned at 4 μm thickness and mounted onto slides. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (95 °C for 30 min). The subsequent immunofluorescence staining steps were identical to those described for the cell samples. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with a 40× objective lens, and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Western blot assay

Total protein was extracted from lung tissue or primary neutrophils using RIPA lysis buffer. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Epizyme Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein (5–20 µg per lane) were separated by SDS–PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes for immunoblot analysis. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST at room temperature for 1.5 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-IL-6, anti-TNF-α, anti-MPO, anti-CitH3, anti-Ly6G, anti-S100A9, anti-Nrf2, anti-HO-1, anti-Keap1, anti-β-tublin, and anti-β-actin. The primary antibodies were diluted (1:1000) in primary antibody dilution buffer (Biosharp Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a Bio-Rad chemiluminescence imaging system. Band intensities were analyzed densitometrically using ImageJ software, and protein expression levels were expressed as relative grayscale values, normalized to the corresponding loading control. Results are presented as fold changes.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Data independence was ensured through rigorous experimental design, specifically by the random allocation of mice to experimental groups and the blinded collection of samples; therefore, no statistical test for independence was required. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variances among groups was evaluated using Levene’s test. For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s *t*-test when data met the assumptions of normal distribution and homogeneity of variance. If these parametric assumptions were violated, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was applied. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for data that were normally distributed and exhibited homogeneous variances. In cases where the assumption of homogeneity of variance was not met, Welch’s ANOVA with Games–Howell post hoc correction was employed. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A *p* value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Detailed information regarding sample sizes and experimental replicates is provided in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Reduced susceptibility of S100A9 knockout mice to LPS-induced ALI

Compared to control mice that received intratracheal PBS, C57BL/6 mice in the ALI group exhibited severe pathological alterations characteristic of ALI. Histological examination revealed extensive alveolar damage, including pronounced alveolar wall thickening, interstitial edema, hemorrhage, and pronounced infiltration of inflammatory cells, predominantly neutrophils. In contrast, S100A9 KO mice subjected to ALI showed markedly improved lung architecture, with significantly attenuated alveolar destruction and reduced inflammatory cell accumulation (Fig. 1A). Semi-quantitative histological scoring further confirmed that lung injury severity was significantly reduced in S100A9 KO mice compared with WT ALI mice (Fig. 1B). The lung wet-to-dry weight ratio (an indicator of pulmonary edema) was significantly increased in WT ALI mice compared to controls. This increase was markedly attenuated in S100A9 KO mice (Fig. 1C). Similarly, the lung index and lung water content ratio were significantly elevated in WT ALI mice, and these metrics also decreased following S100A9 deletion (Fig. 1D, E). Furthermore, following LPS challenge, S100A9 KO mice exhibited a significantly higher survival rate compared with WT mice during the 7-day observation period (Fig. 1F). A schematic diagram of the LPS-induced ALI mouse model is shown in Fig. 1G. These data indicate that S100A9 KO mice exhibit a reduced response to LPS-induced ALI, characterized by improved histopathology, reduced pulmonary edema, and enhanced survival.

Reduced pulmonary inflammatory cell infiltration in S100A9 KO mice in an ALI model

To assess the pulmonary inflammatory response, we first analyzed the BALF. The total protein concentration in BALF, a marker of vascular permeability and alveolar leakage, was significantly elevated in WT ALI mice. In contrast, this elevation was markedly attenuated in S100A9 KO mice (Fig. 2A). Consistent with this, the total cell count in BALF, reflecting overall leukocyte infiltration into the alveolar space, was also significantly increased in WT ALI mice. This increase was significantly reduced in S100A9 KO mice (Fig. 2B). We further examined the levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines. Western blot analysis of lung tissue revealed significant upregulation of IL-6 and TNF-α protein expression in WT ALI mice. This upregulation was significantly suppressed in S100A9 KO mice (Fig. 2 C-E). Simultaneously, we used ELISA to measure the concentrations of inflammatory cytokines in mouse serum and lung tissue homogenates. The results showed that IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β levels were all sharply elevated in WT ALI mice. Notably, S100A9 deficiency led to a significant reduction in the secretion of all three cytokines (Fig. 2F). These data indicate that, in the ALI model, S100A9 KO mice exhibit reduced alveolar barrier disruption, accompanied by decreased recruitment of inflammatory cells and lower production of key pro-inflammatory mediators.

Reduced systemic and pulmonary neutrophil activation in S100A9 KO mice in the ALI model

To clarify the relationship between S100A9 expression and neutrophil infiltration, we performed immunofluorescence co-staining on lung tissue sections (Fig. 3A). In LPS-challenged WT mice, S100A9 showed significant co-localization with the neutrophil marker Ly6G, indicating that infiltrating neutrophils are a primary source of S100A9 in inflamed lung tissue. This co-localization was substantially reduced in S100A9 KO mice. Semiquantitative analysis of S100A9 and Ly6G co-localization is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1A. We further investigated whether S100A9 regulates NET formation. Immunofluorescence co-staining for MPO and CitH3, hallmark components of NETs, revealed extensive NETosis areas in the lungs of WT ALI mice. In contrast, MPO⁺CitH3⁺ positive areas were significantly reduced in the lungs of S100A9 KO mice (Fig. 3B). Quantitative fluorescence analysis of MPO and CitH3 is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1B. This finding was quantitatively corroborated by immunohistochemistry, which demonstrated a significant reduction in the positively stained areas for Ly6G, MPO, and CitH3 in the lungs of KO mice (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, at the protein level, Western blot analysis of lung tissue homogenates confirmed that S100A9, Ly6G, MPO, and CitH3 protein expression was upregulated in wild-type ALI mice. Consistent with the histological data, the expression of Ly6G, MPO, and CitH3 was also significantly reduced. Notably, S100A9 protein was barely detectable in the WT control group, which is consistent with the extremely low basal expression of S100A9 in normal lung tissue under physiological conditions (Fig. 3D-H). In the LPS-induced ALI model, the number of peripheral blood neutrophils in S100A9 KO mice was significantly lower than in WT mice. Conversely, the neutrophil count in the bone marrow of KO mice was significantly higher than that in WT mice (Fig. 3I-M). These results indicate that, in the ALI model, S100A9 KO mice exhibit reduced neutrophil activation both systemically and in the lungs, as evidenced by decreased pulmonary neutrophil infiltration, reduced NETosis, and lower expression of neutrophil activation markers.

S100A9 KO activates the pulmonary NRF2/HO1 antioxidant pathway

To investigate whether the effect of S100A9 deficiency involves antioxidative stress responses, we first examined the subcellular localization of NRF2, a master regulator of the antioxidant response. Immunofluorescence co-staining of lung tissue revealed that in LPS-challenged WT mice, NRF2 expression was low and its co-localization with the neutrophil marker Ly6G was limited. In contrast, lung tissue from S100A9 KO mice exhibited significant nuclear translocation of NRF2, and this activated NRF2 was frequently observed within Ly6G-positive neutrophils (Fig. 4A).This result was further corroborated by immunohistochemistry, which showed a significant increase in nuclear NRF2-positive staining within the pulmonary parenchyma of S100A9 KO ALI mice compared to their wild-type ALI counterparts (Fig. 4B). Western blot analysis further confirmed that, compared to WT ALI mice, S100A9 KO ALI mice exhibited decreased KEAP1 protein levels and a significant upregulation of NRF2 and its key downstream effector HO-1 in lung tissue (Figs. 4 C-F). These findings demonstrate that S100A9 deficiency is associated with a robust activation of the NRF2–HO-1 antioxidant axis in the lungs during LPS-induced ALI. This enhanced NRF2 nuclear translocation and downstream signaling suggest that the protective phenotype observed in S100A9 KO mice is, at least in part, mediated by strengthened antioxidant defenses and redox homeostasis in the injured lung.

Reduced oxidative stress in S100A9 KO mice in the ALI model

To assess the functional consequences of NRF2 pathway activation, we evaluated oxidative stress. Dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescence staining of lung sections revealed a marked increase in ROS generation in the lungs of WT ALI mice, which was significantly attenuated in S100A9 KO mice (Fig. 5A). Consistent with this, biochemical assays of lung tissue homogenates and plasma demonstrated that, compared to WT controls, S100A9 KO mice maintained significantly higher levels of the antioxidant GSH and greater SOD activity, along with a significantly lower content of the lipid peroxidation product MDA (Fig. 5B). These results indicate that S100A9 deficiency activates the NRF2/HO-1 antioxidant axis, promotes the nuclear translocation of NRF2 within infiltrating neutrophils, and upregulates downstream protective genes. Taken together, these findings indicate that S100A9 KO mice display a substantially reduced oxidative stress burden during ALI, accompanied by enhanced antioxidant defenses in the lung.

Neutrophils derived from S100A9 KO mice exhibit an attenuated activation response to LPS stimulation

To clarify the direct role of the S100A9 protein in neutrophil activation, we stimulated primary neutrophils isolated from WT and S100A9 KO mice with LPS (1.5 µg/mL) and assessed the impact of S100A9 deficiency on NETosis. Immunofluorescence co-staining for MPO and CitH3 revealed that LPS stimulation triggered robust NET formation in WT neutrophils, as evidenced by extensive extracellular co-localization of these markers. In contrast, under the same conditions, neutrophils derived from S100A9 KO mice exhibited a significantly attenuated NETotic response (Fig. 6A). Concurrently, we analyzed the expression of activation and NETosis markers by Western blot. LPS stimulation led to significant increases in the protein levels of MPO, the surface activation marker Ly6G, and CitH3 in WT neutrophils. This upregulation was markedly suppressed in S100A9 KO neutrophils (Figs. 6B-E). These results indicate that S100A9 deficiency intrinsically impairs the ability of neutrophils to upregulate activation markers and execute NETosis in response to LPS stimulation.

S100A9 KO in neutrophils enhances the NRF2/HO-1 signaling pathway

To investigate whether the antioxidative effects observed in lung tissue were at least partially due to intrinsic alterations in neutrophils, we first examined the subcellular localization of NRF2 in primary neutrophils. Immunofluorescence co-staining for the neutrophil marker Ly6G and NRF2 revealed that after LPS stimulation, WT neutrophils exhibited only a modest increase in NRF2 nuclear translocation. In stark contrast, neutrophils derived from S100A9 KO mice demonstrated pronounced intranuclear accumulation of NRF2, co-localized with Ly6G, indicating activation of the NRF2 pathway within S100A9-deficient neutrophils (Fig. 7A). We proceeded to quantify the expression of key NRF2 pathway molecules via Western blot. In S100A9 KO neutrophils, LPS stimulation led to a significant reduction in the cytoplasmic inhibitor KEAP1, coupled with increased levels of total NRF2 and its downstream effector HO-1. Specifically, we observed a pronounced decrease in KEAP1 protein, a substantial rise in total NRF2, and a marked upregulation of HO-1 expression following LPS challenge in S100A9 KO neutrophils (Figs. 7B-E). These findings indicate that genetic deletion of S100A9 within neutrophils enhances the NRF2-mediated antioxidant response.

Discussion

ALI is a clinical syndrome triggered by various pulmonary or extrapulmonary factors, including severe infection, trauma, and shock. It is pathologically characterized by diffuse alveolar epithelial and vascular endothelial damage [35], exhibits high morbidity and mortality rates [36], and currently lacks specific therapeutic intervention [37]. In recent years, the role of the damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) molecule S100A9 in ALI has gained increasing attention [38]. Studies have shown that S100A9 drives excessive neutrophil activation and the release of substantial reactive oxygen species, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress and pulmonary tissue damage [39]. However, whether neutrophils can initiate an effective intrinsic antioxidant defense system during activation remains unclear. Therefore, we conducted in vitro and in vivo studies to investigate the protective effect of S100A9 KO on ALI.

The LPS-induced mouse ALI model is widely employed for studying the pathophysiological mechanisms of ALI/ARDS. Furthermore, C57BL/6 background mice are commonly used for genetic engineering, providing a valuable platform for studying specific gene functions [40]. Given that pharmacological inhibition and antibody-mediated blockade of S100A9 have shown therapeutic efficacy in other inflammatory disease models, our findings provide mechanistic support for targeting S100A9 as a feasible strategy in ALI [41]. This study utilized C57BL/6 mice and S100A9 knockout mice on a C57BL/6 genetic background by establishing an ALI mouse model via intratracheal LPS instillation [42]. Compared with WT mice, S100A9 knockout significantly improved survival rates following LPS challenge and markedly alleviated lung histopathological damage, pulmonary edema, and the extent of inflammatory cell infiltration. These results indicate that inhibiting S100A9 expression effectively mitigates the severity of ALI, demonstrating a clear lung-protective effect.

The pathophysiology of ALI involves a complex interplay of various target and effector cells. Among these, neutrophils, as core components of the innate immune system, are the primary innate immune cells responsible for inducing host tissue damage [43]. During the early stages of inflammation, the number of neutrophils in the peripheral blood often increases rapidly and significantly [44]. Neutrophils, however, do not kill bacteria autonomously. Instead, they undergo activation—a process involving degranulation that releases their granular contents into the extracellular milieu. This release promotes neutrophil infiltration and amplifies inflammation [45]. Following activation, neutrophils exert their cytotoxic and immune effects through at least three principal mechanisms: phagocytosis, degranulation, and the formation of NETs. These actions primarily facilitate bacterial lysis, degrade the extracellular matrix via elastase, and promote the release of inflammatory factors from epithelial cells, among other effects [46]. Previous studies have shown that NETs can exacerbate alveolar-capillary barrier damage, while targeted clearance or inhibition of NET formation reduces lung tissue injury and mortality in experimental animals [47]. Myeloperoxidase (MPO) as a hallmark enzyme of neutrophil activation, is released not only as a free enzyme that catalyzes the generation of potent oxidants like hypochlorous acid, but also as an integral component of NETs through its complexation with DNA. This dual role amplifies tissue damage and the inflammatory cascade [48]. Clinical studies have confirmed that plasma MPO-DNA levels are significantly elevated in critically ill patients with ARDS caused by sepsis and other etiologies, serving as an independent predictor of disease severity and 28-day mortality [49].

Previous studies have shown that intracellular S100A9 in neutrophils regulates ROS generation and contributes to NET formation [22]. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrates that ROS production in lung tissue is markedly reduced in S100A9 KO mice during acute lung injury. S100A9 can be passively secreted through various mechanisms, including via NETs. On one hand, it activates and induces neutrophil activation, modulates the number and activity of infiltrating neutrophils, and promotes the release of inflammatory molecules to sustain and amplify the inflammatory response [50]. On the other hand, S100A9 can trigger leukocyte adhesion and recruitment through NETs. Inhibition of S100A9 reduces NET formation in septic lung injury, significantly attenuates LPS-induced inflammation and apoptosis, decreases the secretion and transcription of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL−1β, and directly blocks the pathological progression of ALI [51, 52]. Notably, in our study, the reduction in IL−6 and TNF-α levels following S100A9 knockout exhibited similar trends in both lung tissue and serum. IL−6 and TNF-α are canonical pro-inflammatory cytokines whose expression is tightly regulated by transcription factors such as NF-κB and AP−1, which can be activated downstream of TLR4 signaling [53]. The convergent downregulation of both cytokines in S100A9-deficient mice likely reflects the suppression of these common upstream pathways. However, it is important to note that IL−6 and TNF-α are also subject to divergent regulatory mechanisms, including differential promoter architecture, cell-type-specific expression, and post-transcriptional control via RNA-binding proteins or microRNAs [54–56]. Combining in vitro and in vivo experiments, this study demonstrates that S100A9 KO alleviates lung tissue damage by attenuating neutrophil over-aggregation and oxidative burst. This protective effect is achieved through the downregulation of neutrophil activation and subsequent NETosis. While S100A8 and S100A9 typically function as a heterodimer, we focused on S100A9 due to its prominent role as a key effector in neutrophil-driven ALI pathology [57]. It is important to note that S100A9 deletion is known to cause destabilization of the S100A8 protein [58]. Therefore, the protective effects observed in our S100A9 KO model are likely attributable to the functional loss of the entire S100A8/A9 complex rather than S100A9 alone. This combined effect reinforces the potential of targeting the S100A8/A9 heterodimer as a therapeutic strategy for ALI.

S100A9 primarily mediates its pro-inflammatory effects by interacting with the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE). This interaction promotes leukocyte recruitment and inflammatory responses [59]. Following its binding to receptors such as TLR4, S100A9 activates NADPH oxidase, leading to the generation of ROS [60]. These ROS oxidize key cysteine residues on KEAP1, inducing a conformational change that impairs its ability to target NRF2 for degradation. This process consequently results in the indirect activation of the NRF2 pathways [61]. Our findings reveal that S100A9 acts as a key negative regulator of NRF2 in the inflammatory setting. In WT ALI mice, S100A9 upregulation contributes to NRF2 dysfunction, overriding the compensatory ROS-mediated NRF2 induction. In S100A9 KO mice, this direct suppression is relieved, resulting in enhanced NRF2 expression and nuclear translocation. Although ROS is a classical trigger for NRF2 activation, the regulation of NRF2 is multifactorial and can also be influenced by inflammatory signaling pathways [62]. Therefore, the increased NRF2 expression observed in S100A9-deficient mice may reflect an adaptive enhancement of antioxidant defense following the attenuation of inflammatory stress. Prior research indicates that S100A9 inhibitors target the AMPK/NRF2/HO-1 signaling axis. By promoting NRF2 nuclear translocation, they drive HO-1 expression, which counteracts ROS generation and ultimately suppresses NET formation [63]. Conversely, impairment of NRF2 signaling leads to ROS accumulation. This, in turn, triggers neutrophil apoptosis while simultaneously inhibiting NETosis [64]. In this study, we further demonstrated that S100A9 KO significantly upregulates the Nrf2/HO−1 signaling pathway within neutrophils following ALI.

Despite these findings, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, only male C57BL/6 mice were used in the experiments. Although this choice was based on conventional laboratory considerations such as relatively stable hormone levels and relatively uniform body size, it may not fully reflect the potential sex-specific regulatory effects of S100A9 in females. Future studies incorporating both sexes will be necessary to better understand potential gender-related differences in S100A9-mediated immune regulation during ALI. Second, the present study primarily relied on a genetic knockout model to investigate the role of S100A9 in ALI. Although this approach provides mechanistic insight into the biological function of S100A9, the therapeutic efficacy of pharmacological S100A9 inhibitors has not yet been validated in wild-type animals. Further studies using specific S100A9 inhibitors will therefore be important to evaluate the translational potential of targeting this pathway in ALI/ARDS.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that S100A9 deficiency significantly alleviates LPS-induced acute lung injury by suppressing neutrophil overactivation and reducing oxidative stress. Mechanistically, S100A9 knockout enhances the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant signaling pathway, thereby limiting ROS production and NET formation. These findings highlight a previously underappreciated role of S100A9 in regulating neutrophil-mediated inflammatory and oxidative responses during ALI. Collectively, our results suggest that targeting S100A9 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of ALI/ARDS.
