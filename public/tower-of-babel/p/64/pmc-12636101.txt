Anthropometric and Body-Composition Changes in Severely Acute Malnourished Children (12–59 Months) Post-Nutritional Rehabilitation: A Follow-Up Study
Authors: Shivani Rohatgi, Sukhneet Suri, Praveen Kumar, Rajesh Kumar Sinha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMalnutrition affects one in three hospitalized children and is linked to negative clinical outcomes and long-term developmental issues. Therefore, this study aimed to assess body composition indices among children with severe acute malnutrition (SAM) and their association with anthropometry.MethodsThis observational follow-up study investigated medical complications among children with SAM (aged, 12–59 months) who were admitted to the nutrition rehabilitation centre at a tertiary care hospital in India. Data was collected at three timelines: on admission (baseline), at discharge (midline), and upon completing all five follow-up visits over a period of 12 weeks after treatment (endline).ResultsOf the 130 enrolled children, 96.2% (n=125) were discharged and 73% (n=95) completed the study. The fat mass scores exhibited a consistent improvement with time, with the mean (standard deviation [SD]) rising from 0.7 (0.7) at baseline to 0.8 (0.7) and 1.4 (0.8) at the midline and endline, respectively. Similarly, the fat-free mass (FFM) scores showed steady progress, with the mean (SD) increasing from 6.2 (1.4) at baseline to 6.3 (1.5) and 6.9 (1.7) at the midline and endline, respectively. The weight-for-height also demonstrated an upward trend, with the mean (SD) increasing from −3.3 (0.0) at baseline to −2.6 (0.7) and −1.8 (0.9) at the midline and endline, respectively. Significant improvements were observed in other anthropometric and body composition parameters, except in the FFM index. Comparison of cured and non-cured children revealed better parameter values in the former than the latter.ConclusionIntegration of body composition and anthropometric measurements into routine healthcare for managing children with SAM can enhance treatment outcomes and reduce relapse rates.Keywords: Body composition, Severe acute malnutrition, Anthropometry, Fat mass, Fat free mass

INTRODUCTION

Moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) and severe acute malnutrition (SAM) are significant causes of child mortality in low- and middle-income nations [1]. Malnutrition affects about one in three hospitalized children and leads to negative clinical outcomes and development issues following hospitalization [2]. Children with MAM and SAM spend an average of 1.3 and 1.6 days longer in the hospital, respectively, compared with well-nourished children. They also experience higher infections rates, delayed wound healing, immune dysfunction, and increased mortality [3].

Given the developmental origins of adult diseases, monitoring child growth from conception to 2 years of age is crucial for predicting future health risks [4]. While interventions for acute malnutrition have improved survival rates, the focus is on ensuring that these children reach their full physical and cognitive potential during the forthcoming year of life [5].

While non-invasive methods such as the measurement of height, weight, and skinfold thickness can help screen the nutritional status of children, advanced techniques such as bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) and body density measurements offer additional insights [6,7].

The World Health Organisation (WHO) traditionally categorized undernourished children as “wasted” (low weight for height) or “stunted” (low height for age), but this binary classification is now considered to have limited scope [8]. A more nuanced approach that involves assessing the fat mass (FM) and fat-free mass (FFM) is essential for understanding survival in this population. Muscle mass, which is essential for immune function, often deteriorates during undernutrition, thereby increasing the risk of mortality; in contrast, FM provides energy and produces leptin, which plays a key role in immune responses. As more children survive malnutrition, the focus of research has shifted towards understanding the long-term effects of malnutrition on body composition [5,9].

Although studies have shown that children with SAM gain both FFM and FM during treatment, the specific types of weight gain have not been investigated. Wibaek et al. [10] found that half of the weight gained in healthy infants during accretion is FFM. Kangas et al. [11] reported that while children with SAM gained 45% FFM and 55% FM during treatment, they still exhibited lower weight and body mass compared with community controls. Additionally, studies have demonstrated links between anthropometric measurements and body composition in children with SAM [12].

Research also indicates a link between stunting and increased FM, which may lead to obesity and chronic diseases later in life [13,14,15,16,17,18,19]. Bwakura-Dangarembizi et al. [20] reported that low lean and fat mass at discharge were significant predictors of mortality in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-positive children with SAM.

This study aimed to investigate the changes in anthropometric parameters and body composition indices during the nutritional rehabilitation of children with SAM (12–59 months old) admitted in a tertiary care hospital, and to explore the relationship among undernutrition, changes in the nutritional status, and body composition during the course of treatment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Eligibility criteria

Children with SAM aged 12–59 months admitted and managed at the Nutrition Rehabilitation Centre (NRC) in a tertiary care hospital at Delhi were included in the study. The study protocol was approved by the appropriate ethics review board of the institution. The inclusion criteria were as follows: resident of Delhi National Capital Region, and weight-for-height standard deviation score <−3 (weight-for-height [WHZ] <−3 standard deviation [SD]) and/or the presence of bilateral pitting edema. Critically ill children with SAM and with known congenital anomalies, or those who experienced secondary complications (e.g., celiac disease, tuberculosis, or HIV) were excluded from the study. Children were enrolled after confirming their eligibility for the study and written informed consent was obtained from their caregivers. The study was conducted in three phases, viz at admission (baseline), at discharge (midline), and upon completing all five follow-up visits over a period of 12 weeks after treatment (endline).

Outcomes

We compared the overall, age-group-wise, and sex-wise mean anthropometric and body-composition indices across three time-points: at baseline, midline, and endline. We also compared the anthropometric parameters and body composition indices of cured and non-cured children during the 12-week study period. Cured was defined as WHZ ≥−2 with absence of bilateral pitting edema, whereas non-cured was defined as WHZ <−2.

Calculation of sample size

The sample size was determined to be 106 using a 95% confidence level, with a 7.5% anticipated prevalence of severe wasting in children in India [21] and absolute precision of 5%. The final sample consisted of 130 participants to accommodate 20% of the participants potentially dropping out [22].

Data collection and quality checks

Socio-demographic information such as age, biological sex, and socio-economic status of the family was recorded at admission. Anthropometric measurements (such as weight, length/height, and mid-upper arm circumference) and body composition indices (including FFM and FM) were assessed for all the study participants at baseline, midline, and endline.

Anthropometric measurements were precisely obtained using specific standardized tools. The weight was recorded twice on a 20-kg scale with an accuracy of ±5 g. A third measurement was taken if the difference between the first two readings exceeded 10 g. For children aged 12–23 months, the length of the child was measured and recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm using an infantometer; for those aged 24–59 months, the height was measured using a stadiometer and recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. If the difference between two length/height readings exceeded 0.7 cm, then a third reading was taken, and the average of the two closest readings was used as the final length/height measurement. The mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) was also taken using a non-stretchable tape with 0.1-cm graduations and color-coded for different ranges. The standard deviation scores (Z-scores) for weight-for-age (WAZ), height-for-age (HAZ), WHZ, body mass index (BMIZ), and MUAC (MUACZ) were computed using WHO growth reference data. Additionally, bilateral pitting edema was assessed by pressing both feet for at least 3 s, and coded as either mild (+), moderate (++), or severe (+++).

The body composition was analyzed using BIA. The FFM and FM were assessed. The BIA device was portable and used the tetra polar method to analyze body composition, with one electrode placed on each arm and foot. The machine measured the impedance (resistance) to the electrical signal as it travelled through the body. Based on the impedance, the machine calculated the muscle mass, fat mass, and water content in the body. Body composition estimates were obtained in the lying/sitting position using adhesives. During the measurements, the children wore only light clothing and the electrodes were attached to the skin using adhesive to ensure optimal contact.

The FFM and FM were used as primary indicators of body composition. Given the variation in age among the children in this study, the body composition parameters were adjusted for length/height to calculate the FFM Index (FFMI) and FM Index (FMI). The FFMI and FMI were subsequently used as the primary outcomes for body composition analysis [12]. For quality assurance, 10% of the data was randomly selected and rechecked by senior experts.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented. The overall, age-group-wise, and sex-wise means for anthropometry and body composition indices at baseline, midline, and endline were compared using two-way analysis of variance. Independent sample t-tests were used to compare the anthropometric parameters and body composition indices between cured and non-cured children at discharge. All the statistical tests were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Co.), and the statistical significance threshold was set as p<0.05.

Ethical approval and consent

This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the scientific committees of Vivekanand College, University of Delhi (VC/2021/2262) and Lady Hardinge Medical College, University of Delhi (LHMC/IEC/2021/03/67). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or caregivers of the children.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics and outcomes

Of the enrolled children with SAM (n=130), 49.2% (n=64) were male and 50.8% (n=66) were female. Approximately 66.9% (n=87) were 12–23 months old, whereas 33.1% (n=43) were 24–59 months old. We found that 5.3% of the children had edematous malnutrition. At discharge, the study cohort comprised 125 children, with near-equal distribution of males (n=63, 50.4%) and females (n=62, 49.6%). Of these, three children (2.3%) were identified as dropouts, all of whom were female. Additionally, two children (1.5%) died, with an even sex distribution of one male and one female.

Seventy-six percent (n=95) of the children completed the 12-week study, including the five follow-up visits. During the follow-up period, 24.0% (n=30) of the children dropped out owing to migration or death. Four children (3.2%) died during treatment, with a higher proportion of males (n=3, 75.0%) than females (n=1, 25.0%).

Relationship between anthropometric indicators and body composition parameters during treatment and follow-up

The average weight gain of children was 4.1±15.4 g/kg/day at discharge from hospital (n=125), and 2.0±1.1 g/kg/day upon completion of the fifth follow-up visit (n=95). The average length of stay at the hospital was 10 days at the midline (n=125) and 110 days at the endline (n=95). Sixty-one (64.2%) children were discharged as cured whereas 34 (35.7%) were discharged as not-cured [23].

We compared the anthropometric indicators (WAZ, HAZ, WHZ, MUACZ, and BMIZ) and body composition parameters (FM, FFM, FMI, and FFMI) across different time points—at baseline, midline, and endline. The observed variations in the WAZ, HAZ, WHZ, MUACZ, BMIZ, FMI, FM, and FFM at these time points highlight a significant (p<0.001) positive shift in the mean z-scores of different anthropometric parameters (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated children with SAM and associated medical complications identified using WHZ <−3SD and/or presence of bilateral pitting edema. The patients were admitted and managed at the NRC of a tertiary care hospital. In this study, we assessed the changes in the means of anthropometric indicators and body composition parameters of the study population at three time points: baseline, midline, and endline. The findings revealed a positive change in the overall, age-group-wise, and sex-wise mean anthropometric indicators and body composition parameters across time-points. Additionally, significant improvements in various anthropometric and body composition parameters were noted in the cured children compared with those who were not cured.

According to a study conducted in Ethiopia, increases in the FFM rather than FM during infancy are associated with improved linear growth in childhood [24]. Similarly, research from Burkina Faso revealed that children treated for MAM with lipid-based nutrient supplements gained more lean mass than fat mass after treatment. This finding challenges the commonly held belief that weight gain from such interventions is primarily owing to increased body fat [25].

A multicentric study conducted in India [12] found that children with SAM and severe stunting had significantly lower FFM than those with SAM but without severe stunting. This indicates a relationship between reduced FFM and impaired linear growth. The study also reported no significant difference in the FM between children with SAM who were severely stunted and those with SAM alone [12]. These findings were similar to those of several other studies that observed deficits in the FFM in children with stunting, often with a relative preservation of FM [26,27]. A similar study conducted in Cambodia observed that a decrease in the FFM was closely related to the severity of linear growth retardation. In contrast, the FM did not show a significant correlation with the degree of growth retardation. The research suggested that tissue accumulation was directly proportional to linear growth, with the FFM being the most notably impacted among: stunted boys −6.6 kg; stunted girls 7.9 kg; non-stunted boys 12.3 kg; non-stunted girls 11.9 kg [28].

Generally, studies on stunted children consistently reveal deficits in the FFM. However, the causal relationship between stunting and subsequent adiposity remains unclear and warrants further investigation. There is limited understanding of the relative significance of the FFM and FM in the survival of children with SAM. Over time, reduced FFM is identified as a risk factor for non-communicable diseases. Despite challenges in the research methods, there is an increasing interest in this field as the focus of research has now shifted from merely ensuring short-term survival to fostering long-term health and development in children.

Regular monitoring of body composition allows for timely treatment adjustments, which can reduce hospital stays and prevent relapses. Although there is an initial investment required for equipment and training, these costs are likely to be offset by the long-term benefits of more effective and targeted treatments. Better management of body composition is associated with improved long-term health outcomes, including better physical and cognitive development and a reduced risk of chronic diseases in adulthood.

Policy decisions based on body composition data can help standardize care practices, and training of healthcare providers can improve their diagnostic and treatment skills. Ensuring broad access to advanced diagnostic tools and addressing implementation barriers through appropriate funding and support is essential. Additionally, integrating body composition parameters into routine assessments promotes further research and development, leading to refined and more effective management strategies for SAM.

Study strengths

The key strength of this study lies in its large sample size, allowing for more robust and accurate findings. Beyond traditional anthropometric measurements, the study utilized BIA to gather quantitative data on body composition indices, viz FFM and FM.

Incorporating body composition assessments including the FFM and FM into the management protocols for children with SAM offers significant advantages. This approach enhances the diagnostic accuracy by providing a deeper, more nuanced understanding of the nutritional health of children with SAM compared with traditional methods involving only the WHZ and MUACZ. Data on body composition during and after recovery of children with SAM in India is scant. With more detailed body composition data, clinicians can implement personalized nutritional interventions that are tailored to the unique needs of each child, ultimately leading to improved recovery rates and better treatment outcomes. This focus on body composition could play a pivotal role in refining SAM management strategies and improving the long-term health of these children.

Study limitations

Although the study included a large sample of children with SAM and medical complications, allowing for a longitudinal follow-up study design, the dropout rate was notable and may have affected the accuracy of the findings. Moreover, the results may not fully represent all children with SAM, particularly those without medical complications. While BIA provided valuable insights into body composition parameters, the accuracy of the technique may be impacted by factors such as hydration status, which could affect measurements in severely malnourished children. Additionally, the lack of a control group or comparison with children receiving standard care, limits the ability to draw firm conclusions about the effectiveness of body composition-based interventions in improving recovery outcomes.

Conclusions

Current nutrition programs monitor the changes in anthropometry as a key indicator of recovery in children with SAM and associated medical complications. Our study provides the data concerning the changes in body composition parameters of children with SAM during recovery and nutrition rehabilitation. This data suggests that body composition measurements at discharge and at completion of the 12-weeks treatment using tools such as BIA can effectively predict the risk of mortality and hospital readmission. It can also provide valuable information about more patient-centric treatment regimens.

The integration of BIA and anthropometric measurements into routine health evaluations at discharge and follow-up offers a promising approach to identify children at greater risk for poor outcomes. This improved monitoring enables more targeted and personalized interventions aimed at enhancing fat and lean mass recovery, potentially improving overall health outcomes and reducing readmission rates.

Looking forward, incorporating body composition assessments into clinical practice can lead to more effective management strategies, ensuring better recovery and long-term health for children with SAM and associated medical complications. Developing comprehensive recovery programs that include regular body composition evaluation could play a crucial role in optimizing patient outcomes and reducing future healthcare burdens.
