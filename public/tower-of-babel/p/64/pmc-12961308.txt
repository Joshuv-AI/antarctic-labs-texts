An implanted lennula approach to measure neural Ca2+ responses in the awake marmoset visual cortex
Authors: Soo Hyun Park, Stephany A. Nti, Madeline Marcelle, Elena M. Esch, Kuan Hong Wang, Afonso C. Silva, David A. Leopold
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a novel approach for endoscopic calcium imaging in the marmoset extrastriate visual cortex, combining a chronically implanted microprism lens with an integrated injection cannula. This method achieved stable, longitudinal imaging of visually responsive GCaMP-expressing neurons over multiple weeks. Using a head-mounted miniscope in two subjects, we repeatedly presented naturalistic visual stimuli and longitudinally tracked the activity of more than one hundred neurons across sessions within an 800-μm2 field of view. Stably monitored neurons in cortical areas V3 and MT responded reliably to visual images and movies over periods up to 3 weeks. Cells having different visual response preferences were spatially intermixed. These results demonstrate the feasibility of longitudinal calcium imaging in the primate visual cortex using a single-surgery, integrated lens-cannula approach, and provide initial insights into single neuron response stability and diversity to naturalistic stimuli in mid-level cortical visual areas.

Introduction

The primate extrastriate visual cortex contains neurons specialized for complex shapes, movements, and scenes (Conway, 2018; Kornblith et al., 2013; Parker & Newsome, 1998), with functionally defined regions specialized for biologically relevant stimuli, such as faces and bodies (Popivanov et al., 2014; Tsao et al., 2006). Investigating the spatial organization and temporal stability of neural responses is challenging using conventional electrophysiology methods, particularly in primate extrastriate cortical areas. For one, assessing the spatial layout of response selectivity typically requires multiple electrode penetrations entering the cortex at different locations. The strategy of serial acute penetrations is then incompatible with longitudinal tracking of individual cells across sessions. Finally, in the best-studied primate model, the macaque, functionally important areas such as face and body patches are hidden within curved sulci, making spatial mapping more difficult. Understanding how the visual responses of individual neurons vary over space and time requires an approach to longitudinally monitor a spatially organized population of isolated cells over multiple sessions.

Calcium imaging technologies such as the GCaMP fluorescent calcium indicator offer one viable approach to spatial and temporal monitoring of single-cell activity in primates (Chen et al., 2013; Lin & Schnitzer, 2016). In vivo calcium imaging has previously been used to study neural response selectivity in awake primates, most often employing two-photon excitation through chronic cranial windows (Ebina et al., 2018; Garg et al., 2019; Li et al., 2017; Pattadkal et al., 2024; Santisakultarm et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Recently, miniaturized microscopes with endoscopic lenses, initially developed for experiments in freely-moving mice (Ghosh et al., 2011), have been adapted for measuring optical activity in primates (Bollimunta et al., 2021; Kondo et al., 2018). Chronically implanted gradient index (GRIN) lenses allow for visualization of cortical activity, with a field of view that can be parallel to the pial surface using a straight lens or can cover cortical layers using a 90° microprism (Andermann et al., 2013; Chia & Levene, 2009a, 2009b). The stable field of view further allows for the longitudinal assessment of individual neuron response selectivity, following spatial registration across imaging sessions (Deitch et al., 2021; Marks & Goard, 2021; Xia et al., 2021).

Here, we demonstrate the use of a novel chronic calcium imaging GRIN lens assembly in the marmoset that yields longitudinal calcium imaging of single-unit responses in the extrastriate visual cortex. The approach uses an integrated microprism lens-cannula (henceforth, “lennula”), where a single surgical procedure enables both chronic optical access to neurons and postsurgical viral targeting to the optical field of view. We implanted the lennula at two extrastriate sites, V3 and MT, that lie on the cortical surface of the marmoset cortex, unlike in the macaque where they are buried within sulci. We longitudinally recorded calcium responses of single neurons to movies and other visual stimuli using a head-mounted miniscope. We repeated the same stimulus sets across multiple imaging sessions spanning up to 26 days. Our primary goals were to (1) explore whether individual neurons could be longitudinally tracked across multiple weeks, (2) assess the temporal dynamics and reliability of single-neuron responses to complex visual events, and (3) assess the spatial organization of stimulus preferences within local neuronal populations. We found that individual neurons showed reliable, time-locked responses to different movie content; however, neighboring neurons exhibited minimal spatial functional organization to the stimuli we tested. Together, our findings demonstrate the feasibility of the chronically implanted lennula assembly to assess spatial and temporal properties of single cell activity in a local cortical population.

Methods

Experimental model overview

Two healthy common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus; one male and one female) were involved in this study. The marmosets were 3.5- and 6.5-year-old, weighing 390–540 grams. All experiments were conducted in compliance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Headpost implantation

Surgical procedures were performed under aseptic conditions. Marmosets fasted overnight and were administered with Alfaxalone (4 mg/kg), diazepam (1 mg/kg), Metacam (0.10 mg/kg), Excede (20 mg/kg), and subcutaneous fluid (3 cc’s) intramuscularly. Local anesthetic (Bupivacaine) was injected into the scalp along the midline to reduce potential pain from the midline incision. Marmosets were then placed in a stereotaxic frame with anesthesia maintained using inhaled 1%–3% isoflurane (1.5%–2.5% in oxygen) through a customized face mask with a palate bar. Peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), heart rate, and rectal temperature were constantly monitored. Throughout the procedure, end-tidal CO2, blood oxygen saturation, and heart rate were maintained within physiological ranges. The skin was cleaned and disinfected with an antiseptic solution and prepared for sterile surgery. Sterile drapes covered the animal except for a small fenestration on top of the head, which was covered by an adhesive, sterile betadine sheet. Using sterile techniques, we performed a midline skin incision to expose the skull and retracted temporalis muscle. Using stereotaxic coordinates, the headpost was placed 15 mm anterior to the interaural line and tilted 20 degrees anteriorly to ensure that the bottom of the headpost was flush with the skull. Headposts were secured to the skull first by applying Copalite®, an antimicrobial dental varnish, directly beneath the headpost, followed by a biocompatible dental cement (C&B Metabond, Parkell; C380), which was added in layers until it covered the base of the implant up to the lower third of the headpost. After curing, the skin was sutured around the headpost. The marmosets were given time to recover before returning to their homecage; they were orally administered Meloxicam (0.10 mg/kg) and monitored frequently for a couple of days.

Acclimation

To restrict whole-body motion, marmosets were placed in a prone position within a semi-cylindrical cradle, secured by a back piece affixed to the rim with screws. The cradle was designed to ensure both stability and comfort and had been successfully used in previous studies (Hung et al., 2015a, 2015b). A heating pad lined the cradle to maintain body temperature. Marmosets were acclimated to rest in the cradle while wearing a jacket or vest that could be attached to the setup. This design allowed free movement of the head, neck, forearms, and tail, enabling the animal to adjust its posture as needed.

The initial training involved gradually increasing durations, starting with 15-minute sessions and extending progressively in subsequent sessions. Throughout acclimation, animals received positive reinforcement via fluid and food rewards, and their behavioral state was closely monitored. Because individual animals varied in their adaptation to the setup, reinforcement strategies and session durations were adjusted accordingly to ensure a smooth transition. Within approximately 3 weeks (15 training sessions), both marmosets A and B were able to remain in the body restraint comfortably for the full duration required for experiments (~3 hours) while accepting rewards.

Following successful acclimation to the body restraint, we introduced head fixation training. This process began with brief, gentle touches or light pressure applied with a cotton tip to the implanted headpost while the animal rested in the cradle. Once the animal became accustomed to the headpost contact, we gradually secured the headpost using a holder, positioning it at a comfortable height and optimal position to minimize head movement. As with the cradle acclimation, head fixation duration was incrementally increased, beginning with 5-minute sessions while providing positive reinforcement and monitoring the animal’s comfort level. For both marmosets, full acclimation to head fixation was achieved in approximately 2 weeks.

During the final part of head fixation training, the animals were introduced to the environment of the calcium imaging experiment, including exposure to the stimulus display, visual stimulation, and liquid reward delivery via a tube. This step ensured that they were fully acclimated to the complete experimental setup before data collection began.

Lennula manufacturing

The main components of the lennula implant consisted of an outer cannula and inner plug made out of fused silica capillary tubing (Molex PolymicroTM), a PRISM lens probe (Inscopix; Bruker), and custom-designed collars (Fig. 1A). We used fused silica tubing in two different thicknesses for the outer cannula and inner plug. The outer cannula was prepared by cutting a thicker fused silica tubing (Part # 106815-0025; outer diameter: 355 µm, inner diameter: 176.2 µm) to an 8-mm length using a diamond blade pen. One end of the outer cannula was beveled over a 1-mm length using fine sandpaper to create a smooth, tapered opening. The inner plug was fabricated from thinner fused silica tubing (Part # 106815-0015; outer diameter: 151.1 µm, inner diameter: 50.7 µm) and cut to ~8 mm in length. A small droplet of cyanoacrylate was applied and dried on one end to close the outer cannula when inserted.

A custom-designed 3D-printed collar (Formlabs, Form 2 & Form 3 printers, black resin) was used to secure the implant components. The collar had two intertwined slots: a larger slot (1.3 mm diameter) for the PRISM lens probe and a smaller slot (0.6 mm diameter) for the outer cannula.

To assemble the lennula, the outer cannula and PRISM lens probe were inserted into their respective slots in the collar, positioning them approximately 0.5–1 mm below the top of the lens and cannula. Once affixed securely with cyanoacrylate, the inner plug was carefully inserted into the outer cannula under a microscope to ensure precise alignment and sealing. In the final assembled implant, the beveled end of the outer cannula extended ~0.5 mm above the 1-mm microprism at the tip of the lens (Fig. 1A–B).

Lennula implantation

To implant the lennula, marmosets were sedated and placed into a stereotaxic frame. Anesthetic and aseptic conditions were maintained as outlined above (see Section 2.2). During preparation, we placed the lennula implant wrapped in lens paper and submerged it in a sterile 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tube filled with alcohol for at least 15 minutes to disinfect. The procedure was performed at high magnification using a digital microscope (AM4115ZTF Dino-Lite Edge, AnMo Electronics Corporation) to allow for detailed visualization of the targeted area. Once we retracted the temporalis muscle, we made a circular craniotomy at the predetermined stereotaxic coordinates using an electric drill with a 2.7-mm diameter trephine burr (item No.18004-27, Fine Science Tools). Then, we made an incision through the tissue with a surgical blade to create a pathway for the insertion of the lennula, following the previously documented surgical protocol of inserting the PRISM probe (Bollimunta et al., 2021; Kondo et al., 2018). Specifically, we attached a 10-0 surgical blade to a stereotaxic electrode holder (Model 1771-A-Mod Electrode Holder with Straight Clamp, David KOPF Instruments) and positioned it parallel to the rostrocaudal axis to align with the flat side of the prism component of the lennula. Then, we advanced the blade into the tissue through the dura 1.5 mm in depth, then moved it rostrocaudally 1.2 mm in one direction and next in the opposite direction to create a square pathway for the PRISM part of the probe. Any blood from the incision was thoroughly irrigated using saline. After retracting the blade, we inserted the lennula held at its collar by a stereotaxic holder (XCF Stereotaxic Cannula Holder, Thorlabs) slowly, roughly 100 µm per minute, through the pre-made incision. Once the end of the lennula (i.e., the tip of the prism) reached the desired depth of 2–2.5 mm from the pia, we applied dental cement (C&B Metabond, Parkell) around the collar to secure the lennula to the skull. Once the dental cement cured, we released the stereotaxic holder. To protect the lennula after the surgery, we secured a rectangular 3D-printed chamber with a cap on the skull with dental cement around the lennula. After closing the chamber cap with screws, the excess skin was sutured around the chamber. After the surgery, we orally administered Meloxicam (0.10 mg/kg) and returned the marmosets to the home cage.

Viral injection

Two weeks after implant surgery, we delivered a viral construct via the lennula to express GCaMP to the cells in the imaging field. The viral injection procedure was conducted under anesthesia to ensure stable injection and delivery of the virus. We mixed three viral constructs in equal volumes (1:1:1): two constructs for the tetracycline-inducible (Tet-Off) virus system containing GCaMP6f (rAAV2/9-Thy1S-tTA; 2.65 x 1013 GC/ml and rAAV2/9-TRE3-GCAMP6f; 3.54 x 1013 GC (genome copies)/ml), and one to tag the nucleus of GABAergic neurons with red fluorescence (rAAV2/9-mDlx-NLS-mRuby2), which is not discussed further in the present study. pAAV_Thy1StTA (Addgene plasmid #97411; http://n2t.net/addgene:97411; RRID:Addgene_97411) and pAAVTREGCaMP6f (Addgene plasmid #97410; http://n2t.net/addgene:97410; RRID:Addgene_97410) were gifts from Tetsuo Yamamori. Both plasmid for Tet-Off GCaMP6f were packaged by Vigene Biosciences (now Charles River). 5 µl of each construct was mixed into a test tube over ice for a total volume of 15 µl. The mixture was homogenized by gentle triturate. We connected a sterilized 34-gauge needle (small hub RN, point 4, 10 mm length, Hamilton) to a sterilized Hamilton syringe (701RN syringe 10uL, Hamilton) and backfilled it with mineral oil using a flexible needle (MicroFil flexible needle, World Precision Instruments). Anesthesia was induced with Alfaxalone (4 mg/kg) and diazepam (1 mg/kg) given i.m. When induction was complete, the marmoset was placed into the semi-cylindrical cradle affixed to a breadboard next to a square bar to hold stereotaxic arms. The head was held still by the headpost with the airway cleared, and the body was covered with a warming blanket (Bair Hugger) to maintain the body temperature. Isoflurane anesthesia was maintained (1–3%) using a 3D-printed facemask, with the animal breathing spontaneously without the need for intubation. The facemask contained two small openings, one near the nostril and another through a hollow palate bar placed in the animal’s mouth, that allowed access to expired air during the procedure. Using a small animal physiology monitoring device (SomnoSuite®, Kent Scientific Corportation), we monitored breathing patterns and isoflurane levels via a small tube connected to the mask’s opening, as well as the body temperature via a temperature probe and SpO2 via a pulse oximeter. Once the animal was stable, we cleaned and disinfected the outside of the chamber and surrounding dental cement using betadine solution and alcohol three times alternating. We placed a sterile drape over the animal’s body and a sterile gauze around the chamber to provide additional barriers. We attached the syringe loaded with mineral oil to the stereotaxic arm of the pump (Pump 11 Elite, Harvard Apparatus) and then, using the pump, front-loaded 2.5 µl of the pre-mixed viral construct that had been dispensed onto sterilized parafilm. Once the syringe was prepared, we opened the chamber cap and evaluated the lennula using a digital microscope (AM4115ZTF Dino-Lite Edge, AnMo Electronics Corporation) at high magnification. Then, we covered the top surface of the lens with a piece of sterilized lens paper to prevent any scratches, and removed the inner plug from the outer cannula using forceps. Using the stereotaxic arm holding the syringe, we aligned the needle tip to the outer cannula and then went down slowly. A stopper was placed on the needle in advance to limit its depth of penetration and prevent it from advancing too deeply into the brain tissue (Fig. 1C, viral injection). The stopper was made out of fused silica of the same dimension as the outer cannula and effectively located the needle tip in the middle of the prism. Once the needle was in place, we dispensed 2 µl of the viral construct at a flow rate of 50nl/min and we waited approximately 15 minutes afterward to prevent backflow. After 15 minutes, we slowly retracted the needle and inserted a sterile inner plug into the outer cannula to block the open route to the brain. The chamber cap was screwed back on and marmosets were recovered from anesthesia; then, they returned to their home cage where they were monitored regularly.

Miniscope baseplate installation

After the virus was expressed (typically 4–5 weeks after the injection), we installed the baseplate above our implant following previous protocols (Kondo et al., 2018; Resendez et al., 2016). Marmosets were brought into the experimental room and injected with diazepam (1 mg/kg) to achieve muscle relaxation and light sedation for approximately 30–45 minutes. Marmosets were placed into the semi-cylindrical cradle in a prone position, with head and whole-body motion restrained, and covered with a far-infrared warming pad (RT-0502, Kent Scientific Corporation). The chamber cap was detached and the top of the lens surface was cleaned with lint-free lens papers and alcohol. Using a stereotaxic holder, the miniscope attached to the baseplate was adjusted until an optimal focal distance was achieved, enabling clear visualization of strong calcium signals. These signals indicated the appropriate alignment between the implanted lens and the objective lens of the miniscope. To compensate for potential shrinkage of the dental cement (C&B Metabond, Parkell; C380), the miniscope was carefully moved slightly upward, increasing the focal distance. To prevent light passage through or from the miniscope and implant, a black powder dye was incorporated into the dental cement. The mixture was meticulously applied around the baseplate, and the miniscope was removed as necessary during the process. After the dental cement had fully cured, the baseplate cap was secured in place. From this stage onward, the baseplate cap was used exclusively, replacing the larger chamber cap.

Visual stimulus and trial structure

All of the visual stimulation trials were controlled by NIMH MonkeyLogic (Hwang et al., 2019). For a given daily session, we performed 4–6 brief image presentation runs and 3–4 runs for each video, with 2-minute resting-state runs alternating with visual stimulation runs (Fig. 2A). A typical daily session resulted in 40–60 trials per image category (8–12 trials per individual image), 3–6 trials (or repetitions) for movie 1 and movie 2, and 10-minute resting state. Only movie presentation runs were analyzed in the present paper.

For the movie presentation, we presented a 2-minute movie containing six 20-second blocks of video clips continuously for 2 minutes. Each block showed a different content: human faces, marmoset faces, marmoset bodies, action camera videos (optic flow), scenes, and object motion (rigid-body motion). The images in Figure 2A under “MOV” indicate the contents in each 20-second block. The “marmoset bodies” block contained clips where marmoset bodies were shown without faces (e.g., a marmoset was looking away or turning its back to the camera). By using different sets of clips and mixing the order of the blocks, we created two such 2-minute videos (“movie 1” and “movie 2”) and presented them multiple times. Due to copyright reasons, we display mock stimulus images in Figures 2, 4, and 5, created from third-party images available under the Creative Commons open license. The original stimulus images used in the experiments can be provided upon request.

In vivo calcium imaging

The marmoset was positioned in a prone posture on a semi-cylindrical cradle designed for gentle restraint during the experiment. A plastic upper piece connected to the animal through a customized vest (SKU PJ 00 M (Primate Jacket), Lomir Biomedical Inc.) was secured to the sides of the cradle using plastic screws, lightly holding the animal’s back in place, similar to the previous setup for MRI scans (Hung et al., 2015b). This setup allowed the animal to move its tail and limbs freely within the confines of the cradle while maintaining stable positioning for the procedure. Then, we stabilized the head via the headpost. Once the animal’s body and head were stabilized, we unscrewed the baseplate cap to expose the lennula. Under the digital microscope (AM4115ZTF Dino-Lite Edge, AnMo Electronics Corporation), we examined and cleaned the lens surface using lens paper and alcohol through an opening on the baseplate. The animal cradle was then positioned on a platform in front of the display to ensure consistent placement across sessions. Calcium imaging was performed using the nVista system (Inscopix, Inc.). Specifically, we docked the miniaturized microscope (nVista 2.0) to the baseplate on the animal’s head, and the images from the microscope were transferred by a cable to the data acquisition system (DAQ), and recorded. At the beginning of each session, we captured a snapshot of the field of view or recorded a video for several seconds without any visual stimulus on the display. The single snapshot or an averaged frame of several seconds was later used to co-register all the sessions. For each paradigm (Fig. 2A; IMG, MOV, and RS), we acquired GCaMP signals at 10 Hz (LED power 70%, exposure 100 ms, and gain 1). Acquisition parameters for calcium imaging were controlled by Inscopix Data Acquisition Software (IDAS; Inscopix, Inc.). The calcium imaging and visual stimulation were synchronized by sending TTL pulses corresponding to each nVista frame from the DAQ to the NIMH MonkeyLogic toolbox. When each run starts, NIMH MonkeyLogic sent a TTL pulse to start the calcium imaging (recording) of that run. Using the Eyelink eye-tracking system (Eyelink 1000 Plus, SR Research), monocular horizontal and vertical gaze position, and pupil size were measured. Marmosets were water-restricted overnight before each imaging session in the mornings (maximum 16 hours of restriction) and received diluted juice rewards through a syringe pump (KDS 100 Legacy Syringe Pump, Kd Scientific) during the imaging sessions. Marmosets were rewarded with fruit-flavored juice at an interval ranging from 4 to 6 seconds throughout the run. After the experiment, the animals had unlimited access to water in the home cage during daytime.

Measurement of doxycycline dosage dependency on GCaMP expression

To determine appropriate dosage of doxycycline (Dox) regulation in marmosets that can be safely and reliably used in the calcium imaging field, we measured the down regulating effects of doxycycline on a tetracycline-inducible expression system of GCaMP expression over time (Sadakane et al., 2015). Two different dosages of doxycycline (Sigma Aldrich, Cat # D9891) were administered orally to both marmosets by mixing the drug into marshmallows. A high dosage (3 mg/kg followed by 2 mg/kg, for a total of 5 mg/kg) was given over 2 consecutive days, while a low dosage (2 mg/kg) was administered on a single day. Over a period of 35 days, imaging sessions were conducted daily, with one to five imaging runs per day, each lasting approximately 2 minutes. Imaging was performed while the animals were awake and resting in the dark room. Images were acquired at 10 Hz using a constant set of acquisition parameters (LED power 80%, exposure 100 ms, and gain 1), controlled by Inscopix Data Acquisition Software. After spatial downsampling by a factor of four, average fluorescence was computed for 1) each region of interest (ROI) and 2) the entire field of view (Supplementary Fig. S1). To assess the time course of downregulation across ROIs and areas, relative fluorescence (%) was calculated by normalizing each day’s fluorescence value to the fluorescence level measured on the day of Dox administration.

Ex-vivo structural MRI

Following perfusion with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), the animal’s head was decapitated and stored in PFA at 4 °C for several days. A high-resolution T2-weighted structural MRI scan was performed ex vivo on the decapitated head. Prior to imaging, the sample was thoroughly rinsed with PBS to remove excess fixative and reduce background signal. The sample was then wrapped with a sticky plastic wrap (Press’n Seal® Cling Film, Glad, US) to prevent any liquid from dripping during the scan. The wrapped sample was immobilized in a custom-designed holder with ear bars and a mask (mouthpiece) to minimize susceptibility artifacts during scanning. The ear bars were hollow and filled with water so that the ear canal and anterior-posterior zero position could be visualized in the MR images. We used an oval, single-loop surface coil, the major axis of which was approximately 5 cm and able to cover the top of the head from front to back. Ex-vivo structural MRI scans were acquired using a horizontal 7T/30 cm MRI scanner and ParaVision 6.0.1 (Bruker Corp., Billerica, USA). T2-weighted images were obtained using a 3D RARE sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 900 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, field of view = 38.4 mm × 32 mm, voxel resolution = 200 µm isotropic, matrix size = 192 × 160, slices = 128, RARE factor = 8, number of averages = 4. We repeated these six and nine times in marmoset A and B, respectively. To achieve a higher signal-to-noise ratio, we averaged the repeated scans and generated a grand average. The grand-average was used for detailed structural analysis and evaluation of the brain as well as localizing the implanted lennula in the present study. Note that the lennula method is fully compatible with standard in vivo structural MRI, which can be acquired either before or after implantation without interfering with chronic imaging, longitudinal tracking, or studies of plasticity. In vivo structural imaging provides sufficient anatomical information to localize the implant and register imaging sites to atlas-based cortical maps.

Data analysis

Spatial registration of anatomical images and face-selective activation to the MBM template

High-resolution T2-weighted volumes from each marmoset (200 µm isotropic; acquisition parameters as described above) were registered to the Marmoset Brain Mapping (MBM) v3.0.1 T2-weighted template (Liu et al., 2021) with AFNI’s animal_warper. The pipeline applied sequential rigid-body and affine alignment followed by a non-linear SyN warp (ANTs), producing native-to-template and template-to-native deformation fields at the template resolution (200 µm). we resampled a publicly available face-selective activation map (faces > scrambled faces T-statistics; file group_faces_v_scrambled_A-B_Tstat.nii.gz, downloaded from the accompanying Git repository of the original study, Schaeffer et al. (2020)) into the MBM template space. The inverse warp was then applied so the contrast could be overlaid on each animal’s native T2-weighted image to visualize the lennula track (Supplementary Fig. S6). The Paxinos cortical parcellation distributed with the template was transferred into native space with nearest-neighbor interpolation to preserve discrete area labels, allowing the location of lennula to be assigned to cortical areas (Fig. 1D). This common-space framework enabled direct comparison of our implant locations with established face-selective regions and cortical areas from the atlas.

Preprocessing and source extraction

All data analyses were performed using MATLAB (MathWorks, MA) software, with a set of customized codes in combination with publicly available toolboxes. We first preprocessed each run and then concatenated all the frames from the runs for a given daily session to extract sources. The preprocessing pipeline begins by correcting defective pixels, spatially cropping the FOV, and downsampling each imaging frame by a factor of 4. This reduces data size while maintaining sufficient resolution and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Next, spatial band-pass filtering is applied to the frames with cutoff frequencies of 0.005 pixel-1 (low) and 0.5 pixel-1 (high). Following spatial filtering, brain motion artifacts caused by respiration and head-unrestrained behavior are corrected by applying a rigid translation algorithm 1) to each run first using the 1st frame of the run as a reference image (Thevenaz et al., 1998), then 2) to entire daily session (across runs) by using the 1st frame of the 1st run as a reference image. The motion-corrected frames from each run were then concatenated: on average, this included four 5-minute-long flashing image runs, eight 2-mintue-long continuous video runs, and five 2-minute-long resting state runs, corresponding to more than 60-minute-worth of data. Cell segmentation and calcium activity trace extraction are performed on the motion-corrected frames using the CNMF-E algorithm (Zhou et al., 2018), which models the imaging data as a combination of the spatiotemporal activity of all cells, time-varying background, and noise. Each cell is defined by its spatial footprint, representing its shape and location. Some cells that showed unusual shapes or abnormal calcium signals were excluded. The accepted cells and their activity traces are then exported to MATLAB for further analysis.

Registration of sources across days

Across multiple days, we longitudinally tracked the cells by computing the translational shifts between the FOVs from each day and applied the shifts to the cells’ spatial footprint from each day. For the registration of FOVs over days, we used the reference frame (the 1st frame of each session) from each day, to which all the following frames of the day had been aligned during preprocessing. These reference images contained spatial landmarks that were stable and common across multiple days, such as the boundary of the lens and blood vessels. A rigid-body registration algorithm (NoRMCorre) was used to calculate the translational shifts in pixels for each FOV of each animal. The calculated shifts were then uniformly applied to all sources from a given daily session, allowing all the cells to be in the common space.

We evaluated each neuron to determine if it was the same cell across sessions. This is done in the pixel space of the FOV, that is, a two-dimensional matrix. To do this, we first loaded the spatial component (neuron.A) from each session, then applied spatial smoothing (medfilt2.m) and a threshold of 0.2 (20% from the peak). Then, we brought all the cells’ spatial components to the same space by applying the translation shifts calculated above. To determine the correspondence between the sources from different daily sessions, from source to source, we compare its spatial component to one of all the other sources. We assigned sources to the same cell if the overlapping spatial components were within 2 pixels from the centers of the source. Once going through all the sources from one session, moving to the next session, and repeated the procedure with remaining sources that had not been assigned with the previous session. Through this procedure, we could achieve 337 cells in V3 and 171 cells in MT tracked longitudinally, with varying number of detected sessions (Fig. 3D and 3J).

Time series normalization and sorting

From the concatenated videos from a given daily session, each source had a long time series spanning the entire session of multiple different runs in the order of actual experiments. We first split this concatenated time series from each session back into separate time series for individual runs. Then, we processed each run’s time course using timing information (timestamps) recorded from the NIMH MonkeyLogic. In this process, we used the recorded TTL pulses at the start of imaging as well as at each imaging frame from the miniscope camera to align the stimulus events and the calcium signal. In the case of flashing image runs, timing for the stimulus onset and stimulus offset was used to parse the time series further for each trial, from 1-second before to 4-seconds after the stimulus onset. In the case of continuous video runs, we used the recorded timing for the stimulus onset and took the time series from the start and the end of the 2-minute video presentation. In the case of resting state runs, we used the recorded timing for the imaging onset (from the camera) and took the entire time series until the end, the duration of which was already adjusted by experimental design. To compare overall response dynamics across stimulus conditions, we quantified calcium signal fluctuations during movie viewing (MOV) and static image presentation (IMG). For each neuron, we computed the variance of the raw calcium signal (C_raw from CNMF-E) over the duration of each run, separately for MOV and IMG conditions. Variance was used as a simple, model-free measure of response dynamics that captures the overall magnitude of activity fluctuations without assuming a specific response structure or baseline and visualized for the V3 population for session 1 in Figure 2C.

Video response analyses

We first excluded cells that had less than 8 viewings of movie 1, which corresponded to 3–4 daily sessions, focused only on 157 cells and 71 cells for V3 and MT, respectively. For each cell, we first averaged the 2-minute time courses across trials. Then, we performed the principal component analysis on the population time courses to capture any common co-fluctuation and evaluate the diversity of the population (Supplementary Fig. S5). In addition, to summarize the diversity of movie-driven response profiles, we applied k-means clustering to the population response time courses. Clustering was performed using the kmeans function in MATLAB (Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox) with a squared Euclidean distance metric. For each value of K, the clustering procedure was repeated 1000 times with different initializations to ensure stability. We evaluated values of K ranging from 2 to 20. For each K, we quantified the proportion of variance explained by the clustering, defined as the between-cluster sum of squares relative to the total sum of squares (Supplementary Fig. S4A and S4C). As expected, the explained variance increased asymptotically with increasing k, without a clear elbow or point of concavity. To further assess the marginal contribution of additional clusters, we computed the incremental gain in explained variance between successive values of K (i.e., the difference in explained variance between K − 1 and K, Supplementary Fig. S4B and S4D). Because the clustering was intended as a descriptive visualization of response heterogeneity rather than an attempt to identify discrete functional classes, K = 5 was selected as a representative value that provided an interpretable summary of the population response structure in both V3 and MT. The same cluster assignments were subsequently used to visualize responses to a second movie stimulus (movie 2; Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S3).

Results

We recorded a total of 508 neurons from two animals (337 in marmoset A and 171 in marmoset B). Following a 3-week acclimation to the testing room and body restraint, each animal received a head-post implant and was trained to tolerate head restraint and look at the on-screen stimuli in exchange for juice rewards (see Section 2). Once the animals met behavioral criteria, we implanted the lennula assembly in a single surgery. Following viral delivery of GCaMP, the animals were tested in awake imaging sessions where they repeatedly viewed short movie sequences. We recorded single cell responses over 26 days from area V3 (marmoset A) and over 23 days from area MT (marmoset B). In the following sections, we provide a detailed description of the lennula implantation, viral injection, data acquisition, and cross-session registration. We demonstrate that neurons in both extrastriate areas exhibit selective, reliable and stable GCaMP responses to complex visual stimuli.

Lennula implantation and viral injection for endoscopic calcium imaging

Successful microendoscopic calcium imaging depends on good expression of genetically encoded calcium indicators (GECIs) within the imaged field. The lennula provided optimal localization of GECI expression within the imaging field of view and eliminated the need for two surgeries. In each cortical area of interest, surgical implantation of the lennula was followed by a minor postsurgical procedure, during which we injected the GCaMP-containing virus (Fig. 1, see Section 2).

The lennula comprised three parts: 1) a conventional prism probe consisting of a microprism at the end of a GRIN relay lens, 2) a fused-silica cannula yoked to the lens, with its end directly above the imaging field of view, at the flat end of the prism, and 3) a 3D-printed holder near the top attached to both the lens and cannula (Fig. 1A–B, see Section 2). The surgical procedure followed previously established methods (Bollimunta et al., 2021; Kondo et al., 2018; Schaeffer et al., 2020). Briefly, we first made a cut perpendicular to the pia into the cortical tissue before slowly advancing the prism portion of the lennula into areas V3 and MT (Fig. 1C–D, see Section 2). Following implantation, a tightly fitting inner plug was advanced into the cannula component of the lennula to block entry of external material (Fig. 1A). A 3D-printed chamber with a cap was attached to the skull around the lens (inset in Fig. 1C, “implantation”) to protect the lens until baseplate installation.

Approximately 2 weeks following the surgery, we delivered the adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector containing a tetracycline-inducible GCaMP6f (TET-Off system from Sadakane et al. (2015)) via the cannula to the imaging field of view in a minor procedure under anesthesia (Fig. 1C, “virus injection”; see Section 2). Under aseptic conditions, the inner plug inside the cannula was briefly removed. Following the injection, it was replaced with a new, sterilized plug. The positioning of the cannula, stopping just above the top of the prism lens, ensured that the injected virus, hence the GCaMP expression, was concentrated in the imaging FOV.

Following the viral injection procedure, we tracked the emergence of GCaMP fluorescent expression over the subsequent days and weeks using the miniaturized microscope. During each post-surgical training sessions, we positioned the miniscope over the implanted lennula, initially using a stereotaxic arm in the awake, head-fixed animal (Resendez et al., 2016). Beginning 3–4 weeks following the viral injection, bright fluorescent signals became detectable from the lens, indicating GCaMP expression. Once we detect substantial fluorescent signals, we used adhesive to fix a baseplate above the implanted lennula at the optimal distance (Fig. 1C, “baseplate installation”; (Resendez et al., 2016)). This plate served to hold the miniaturized microscope at the proper focal distance during all subsequent sessions. This stable and fixed construction allowed us to monitor neural GCaMP activity from the same depth and FOV each day, facilitating registration across sessions. Following a brief period of stabilization (1–2 days), a series of regular in vivo imaging sessions began (Fig. 1C, “in vivo imaging”).

Using the TET-Off GCaMP construct (Sadakane et al., 2015), we were able to regulate GCaMP expression levels through oral administration of doxycycline (Dox) (Supplementary Fig. S1, see Section 2). In pilot experiments (Supplementary Fig. S1), we determined the appropriate dosage of Dox to safely and reliably downregulate the TET-Off system in marmosets. Two different dosages of Dox were mixed with marshmallows and administered orally. Measuring GCaMP activity across the FOV on subsequent days revealed a systematic dose-dependent down-regulation of GCaMP expression, where even a single oral administration of 2 mg/kg dose of Dox led to a marked suppression (Supplementary Fig. S1). These findings allowed us to regulate the GCaMP with the aim of avoiding long-term damage to the cells caused by overexpression (Bollimunta et al., 2021; Kondo et al., 2018; Sadakane et al., 2015).

Imaging neural responses to visual stimuli in the behaving marmoset

We next measured the Ca2+ responses of individual neurons. We focused analysis on neural responses to blocks of brief movies, which were repeated during each session and interleaved with other conditions (Fig. 2A; see Section 2). Each 2-minute continuous movie was composed of six 20-second segments, drawn from a distinct content category (human faces, marmoset faces, marmoset bodies, scenes, moving objects, or optic flow). Animals freely viewed the stimulus content and were required only to maintain their gaze on the screen to receive periodic juice rewards (see Section 2). Sessions typically lasted 2 hours.

During each recording session, neurons were isolated and localized based on separable, time-varying changes in fluorescent calcium signal at distinct sites across FOV. To extract the response time courses for each cell, the constrained nonnegative matrix factorization method for analyzing microendoscopic data (CNMF-E) (Zhou et al., 2018) was applied across all conditions. Each day, we identified between 60 and 130 unique and reliable isolated sources corresponding to individual neurons (Fig. 2B, see Section 2).

Across conditions, neurons throughout the FOV exhibited diverse response profiles (Fig. 2B–C). Some neurons respond more to movies than images, while other neurons showed similar level of responses or stronger responses to images.

Utilizing the chronically implanted lens, we tracked the responses of neurons across multiple days of recording sessions. Because the daily positioning of the miniaturized microscope onto the baseplate introduces small shifts in the imaging plane, we developed a method for spatial co-registration of neurons across sessions for longitudinal tracking of activity (Fig. 3A–B and 3G–H). Specifically, we registered a reference image of the FOV acquired at the start of the first session to similar reference images acquired at the start of each subsequent day. After pilot testing, we optimized co-registration through a single, translational rigid-body transformation based on stable anatomical landmarks (e.g., blood vessels, lens edge; see Section 2) (Fig. 3B–C, 3 H–I). This approach was robust and avoided the potentially confounding contribution of time-varying cell fluorescence (i.e. activity) on spatial alignment.

After bringing the FOVs from all sessions into alignment, we compared the spatial footprints of each individual neuron across sessions. Focusing on the central 20% of each neuron’s spatial extent, we considered two neurons to be the same if their centers were within 2 × 2 pixels (6.25 μm × 6.25 μm). This procedure identified 337 unique neurons in V3 (Fig. 3D) and 171 in MT (Fig. 3J). The longitudinal tracking of individual neurons ranged between 1 and 26 days, with 51 neurons in V3 and 40 neurons in MT being tracked continuously for over 21 days (Fig. 3E, 3K). This capacity to monitor unique neurons across sessions allowed us both to assess the consistency of response selectivity over multiple days and to accrue functional responses to the same movie collected over multiple days.

A subset of neurons in both regions exhibited high response consistency both within and across sessions, despite the free viewing of the animals, the complex nature of the stimuli, and retinotopically limited receptive field sizes of neurons of the two areas. Many neurons showed a high response consistency across sessions, particularly in V3 (Fig. 4, see also Supplementary Fig. S2 for data from MT). For these stable neurons, the response dynamics varied: in some cases manifest as transient, time-locked responses to isolated events, and in others as more sustained activation. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of longitudinal calcium imaging of visually driven responses in extrastriate cortex and suggest that responses to naturalistic stimuli in retinotopic extrastriate areas can be stable across days and weeks.

Functional response diversity and spatial organization

The conspicuous diversity of visual response profiles across the local neural population (e.g., Fig. 4A) prompted us to analyze and compare the response profiles across different neurons, again focusing on responses to the naturalistic movies. To this end, we used K-means clustering to group neurons in each area based on the similarity of their responses to movie 1. We selected neurons that were successfully tracked across at least 3 daily sessions and recorded for at least eight repetitions of the same naturalistic movie (157 cells in area V3 and 71 cells in area MT). After computing movie-driven average response across trials for each neuron, we applied K-means clustering to each population (see Section 2, see Supplementary Fig. S4 for the explained variance across a range of K values for both V3 and MT populations). This analysis provided a compact and interpretable summary of the heterogeneous populations in both V3 and MT by computing subpopulations of neurons that shared characteristic time courses. In Figure 5, we showcased one example case of K = 5 to highlight the heterogeneous movie-driven responses and their weak spatial organization over the imaged cortical tissue (see Supplementary Fig. S3 for corresponding responses to movie 2).

In both areas, functional subpopulations exhibited distinctive peak responses at different time points, which sometimes matched the movie epochs (Fig. 5A–B, 5D–E). For example, groups of V3 neurons responded most strongly to epochs containing human faces (Groups 1 and 2), or to marmoset faces (Group 4) (Fig. 5A–B). Groups of MT neurons responded also to the epochs containing human faces (Groups 1, 3, and 4) along with the epochs of optic flow and moving objects (Fig. 5D–E). V3 showed a higher proportion of neurons with sustained visual responses compared to MT, where responses to movie content were more transient. In both areas, one subpopulation (Group 3 in V3 and Group 2 in MT) responded strongest at the beginning of the run, regardless of the order of movie epochs, perhaps reflecting a higher level of overarching visual adaptation in these neurons (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S3).

Finally, we asked whether the neural subpopulations showing similar responses were spatial aggregated within the FOV, as might be expected from the functional segregation observed in some extrastriate cortical visual areas (Albright, 1984; DeAngelis & Newsome, 1999; Fujita et al., 1992; Hatanaka et al., 2022; Roe et al., 2012). To this end, we marked neurons in their native spatial positions according to their functional subpopulation identity (Fig. 5C and 5F; see also results of principal component analysis in Supplementary Fig. S5). This mapping revealed only minimal evidence for spatial clustering. Some similarly responsive units appeared aggregated. However, many neurons with similar response properties were dispersed broadly across the FOV and neurons with different response properties were spatially intermixed. These results demonstrate the feasibility to systematically study laminar and tangential functional organization in the awake marmoset extrastriate visual cortex.

Discussion

The present study is among the first to apply the miniscope technology to observe high-level visual responses in the primate visual cortex (Oguchi et al., 2021). Other previous studies using miniaturized endoscopy in marmosets (Kondo et al., 2018) and macaques (Bollimunta et al., 2021) focused on motor systems. In examining the responses to naturalistic movie stimuli, we found that neurons in the marmoset extrastriate visual cortex were driven reliably by the movie content during free viewing, and that these reliable responses were evident across multiple recording sessions. In some ways, these findings match those obtained with chronic single-unit electrophysiological recordings in the face-selective extrastriate visual areas of the macaque (McMahon et al., 2014), which similarly showed consistent stimulus selectivity across recording sessions.

We focused on the methodological advantanges posed by the implanted lennula assembly and head-mounted miniscope for studying the extrastriate cortex of the awake marmoset. The lennula assembly enabled direct delivery of the calcium indicator to the imaging field, improving targeting and eliminating the need for multiple craniotomies. While not demonstrated here, it also allows for local introduction of other viral and pharmacological agents for various neuroscience manipulations, such as tracing anatomical connections, manipulating physiological activity, or altering local gene expression. The implanted lens gave rise to fixed recordings over several weeks, allowing for longitudinal monitoring of individual cells. Here, we demonstrate that this can be used to evaluate the response stability and spatial functional organization of extrastriate neurons. Such stable recordings, together with experimenter control over GCaMP expression levels, suggest that this method may be of particular use for studying aspects of cortical functional organization that may change over time, as in during learning and development.

Neuronal responses to naturalistic movies

The neurons we recorded in marmoset V3 and MT exhibited consistent responses to naturalistic movie stimuli across days, often showing their highest responses to epochs containing faces. This sensitivity to faces may reflect the broad range of areas in the marmoset brain that respond categorically more to faces than to other stimuli (Schaeffer et al., 2020; Supplementary Fig. S6). Many neurons, particularly those in V3, showed consistent response time courses to repeated viewings of the same movie, even when recordings were separated by weeks. Such stability aligns with previous studies of movie-driven responses in the macaque inferotemporal cortex (McMahon et al., 2015; Park et al., 2022) and mouse visual cortex (Marks & Goard, 2021; Xia et al., 2021). How high-level feature selectivity interacts with spatial receptive field selectivity of mid-level extrastriate areas in the primate is presently unclear. One possibility is that extrastriate neurons participate in multiple modes of visual representation, exhibiting both visual field sensitivity and more general categorical selectivity. Other modulatory cognitive factors, such as attention or emotion evoked by movie content, could also play a role. Disentangling these contributions to high-level cortical responses will benefit from the longitudinal recordings that allow for systematically testing these and other variables.

An unexpected observation was that a significant subset of neurons in both extrastriate areas (Group 3 in V3 and Group 2 in MT) exhibited broad and ostensibly unselective movie responses that adapted over the first 30–40 seconds (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S3). Following this adaptation, the neurons maintained relatively constant response level for the remainder of the movie with minimal content-specific visual responses. Importantly, this response pattern was retained when the order of the movie content was shuffled. Nonspecific response decreases or adaptation in the extrastriate cortex have been reported in previous studies, particularly with brief stimulus presentations repeated over trials, and can sometimes elapse over several tens of seconds, though this time scale has been measured primarily using fMRI (Gardner et al., 2005). Prolonged sensory adaptation in the extrastriate cortex may possibly originate from certain cell classes in the retina or early processing stages in the brain (Koyano et al., 2021; Muller et al., 2009). Alternatively, such apparent adaptation could be due to higher-level cognitive factors related to attention, mnemonic processes, or simply the passage of time. Future studies manipulating the temporal structure of visual input will be crucial for further understanding the mechanisms underlying this nonspecific adaptation.

Limitations of the present study

One limitation of the current study lies in the technical challenges of longitudinal registration of cells, particularly with single photon imaging using a miniaturized microscope. The inherent constraints of single photon imaging, such as lower spatial resolution and potential distortions in the FOV, and the slight differences in the acquisition FOV due to the installation of the miniscope on the baseplate can make it difficult to reliably track the same cells across sessions. The recordings were also carried out in awake, behaving subjects, which added spatial disruptions that could be compensated for, but not perfectly. These challenges naturally limit the precision of long-term analyses of neuronal dynamics. The day-to-day shifts were partially offset by our conservative co-registration approach, which provided assurance that a sizeable subpopulation of neurons could be tracked across recording sessions. Another limitation is the restricted sample size of the present study, with a relatively small sampling of two different portions of extrastriate cortex in two subjects. In the present study, we have not placed emphasis on the differences between areas, focusing on shared features and simply reporting observed differences. In a similar vein, we were careful not to overstate the spatial organization of functionally related neurons within each FOV. Despite these limitations, the present study demonstrated notable visual stimulus responses, categorical selectivity, and response consistency both within and across sessions. The use of calcium fluorescence imaging to link response selectivity profiles, neural dynamics, longitudinal changes, and spatial layout in the primate brain is an exciting avenue for future exploration.
