Effect of Saurauia vulcani leaf extract on spleen morphometric and histopathological changes in rats infected with Salmonella typhimurium
Authors: Samuel Manutur Simanjuntak, Evita Mayasari, Lokot Donna Lubis, Sana Noreen, Yunita Sari Pane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Typhoid-like systemic infection caused by Salmonella species can induce immune responses affecting lymphoid organs such as the spleen. Saurauia vulcani (pirdot) is a traditional medicinal plant known to contain bioactive compounds with potential immunomodulatory properties. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of S. vulcani leaf extract on spleen morphometry and histopathology in rats infected with Salmonella typhimurium. Thirty-two male Wistar rats were randomly divided into four groups (n = 8): normal control (K1), negative control infected with S. typhimurium (K2), positive control treated with chloramphenicol (K3), and treatment group receiving S. vulcani extract (K4). The extract was administered orally at a dose of 500 mg/kg body weight. Spleen weight, spleen volume (cm3), and histological features were evaluated. Data were analyzed using nonparametric tests and presented as medians. Infection increased spleen weight and volume compared to the normal control. The treatment group showed a decreasing trend in spleen weight and volume compared to the negative control; however, the differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Histological observations indicated improved preservation of splenic architecture in the treatment group compared to infected controls. S. vulcani leaf extract showed a tendency to maintain spleen morphology during S. typhimurium infection; however, no statistically significant effect was observed.

INTRODUCTION

Systemic infection caused by Salmonella typhimurium is widely used as an experimental model to study host immune responses.[1] Infection can induce splenomegaly and structural alterations in lymphoid organs due to immune activation.[2] The spleen plays a central role in filtering pathogens and regulating immune responses.[34] During infection, proliferation of lymphocytes and macrophages occurs, especially in the white pulp region, reflecting immune activation.[5] During systemic infection, the spleen often undergoes structural and functional alterations due to immune activation and inflammatory processes.

Saurauia vulcani (pirdot) is traditionally used in Indonesia and contains flavonoids and phenolic compounds with antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties.[6] Previous studies suggest that these compounds can enhance immune responses and improve lymphoid organ structure.[78]

Natural products have been widely investigated as potential immunomodulators. One plant traditionally used in Indonesia is S. vulcani, locally known as pirdot.[9] Previous studies reported that pirdot leaves contain bioactive compounds such as flavonoids and phenolic compounds that possess antioxidant and immunomodulatory activities.[10]

Flavonoids may stimulate immune responses by enhancing the production of interleukin-2 (IL-2), which promotes lymphocyte proliferation and activation. IL-2 also stimulates B lymphocytes and natural killer cells through cytokine signaling mediated by T helper cells.[11] As an immunostimulant, pirdot leaf extract has been reported to increase antibody production and IL-2 levels in experimental animals.[12] Administration of ethanol extract of pirdot leaves also increased lymphocyte counts and improved lymphoid follicle structure in the spleen.[13]

Despite these findings, studies evaluating the effects of S. vulcani on immune organs during bacterial infection are still limited. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of S. vulcani leaf extract on spleen morphology and histology in rats infected with S. typhimurium.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental animals

Thirty-two healthy male Wistar rats (Rattus norvegicus), weighing 180–220 g, were used in this study. The animals were acclimatized for 1 week under standard laboratory conditions with ad libitum access to food and water.[14]

All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (Approval number: 747/KEPK/USU/2022).

In vitro viable counts

To determine the appropriate infection dose, serial dilutions of the bacterial inoculum were prepared, and turbidity was adjusted to optical densities (OD₆₀₀) of 0.8, 0.4, 0.2, and 0.1 using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer.

A volume of 0.1 mL from each dilution was plated onto plate count agar using the pour plate technique and incubated at 37°C for 18–24 h.[15] Colony-forming units (CFU) were subsequently counted to estimate the number of viable bacteria in the inoculum. Based on triplicate measurements, the viable count of S. typhimurium at OD₆₀₀ = 0.1 was determined to be 2.1 × 107 CFU/mL.

Survival test of Salmonella typhimurium infection in rats

A preliminary experiment was conducted to determine the optimal infection dose. Eight rats were randomly divided into four groups (n = 2 per group) and intraperitoneally injected with different concentrations of bacterial suspension.

Rats administered with 2.1 × 107 CFU/mL survived up to 15 days postinfection. In contrast, rats injected with 6 × 107 CFU/mL died on day 8, while those receiving doses higher than 6 × 107 CFU/mL died within 2 days. Based on these observations, a dose of 2.1 × 107 CFU/mL was selected for subsequent experiments.

Histology sampling and staining

On day 15 postinfection, all rats were euthanized using an overdose of a ketamine/xylazine anesthetic cocktail administered intraperitoneally (ketamine 100 mg/kg body weight and xylazine 10 mg/kg body weight).[1617]

Euthanasia was performed in accordance with the guidelines of the American Veterinary Medical Association to ensure humane treatment and minimize animal suffering.[18] This procedure facilitated the safe collection of spleen tissues for subsequent histological examination.

Sample size and sampling technique

A total of 32 rats were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 8 per group).[19]

Preparation of plant extract

Leaves of S. vulcani were collected from the natural forest of Sintong Marnipi Village and processed to obtain an ethanol extract at the laboratory facilities of Universitas Sumatera Utara. The leaves were air-dried for 7–14 days until a constant weight and yellowish-brown color were achieved. The dried leaves were then pulverized into a fine powder and stored at room temperature in a closed container.

A total of 500 g of leaf powder was extracted using the maceration method with 5 L of 96% ethanol. The mixture was soaked for 5 days with occasional stirring, followed by filtration using Whatman filter paper. The residue was re-macerated in fresh ethanol for an additional 2 days and filtered again.

The combined filtrates were concentrated using a rotary evaporator and further dried using a water bath to obtain a crude ethanol extract.

Before administration, the extract was suspended in 0.5% carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC). The CMC solution was prepared by dissolving 1000 mg of CMC-Na powder in 200 mL of warm distilled water under continuous stirring until a homogeneous solution was obtained. Subsequently, 11.2 mg of concentrated extract was mixed with 150 mL of the CMC solution and homogenized.

The treatment group received the extract orally at a dose of 500 mg/kg body weight.

Preparation of bacterial inoculum

S. typhimurium ATCC 14028 (Culti-Loops QC Organisms, Thermo Scientific) was cultured on blood agar plates using the streak plate method according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 18–24 h to obtain bacteria in the exponential growth phase.

Bacterial colonies were collected using a sterile 10 µL inoculating loop and suspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline in an Erlenmeyer flask. The suspension was homogenized using a magnetic stirrer and used immediately for subsequent procedures.[15]

Infection procedure

Rats in the experimental groups were inoculated with S. typhimurium suspension via the intraperitoneal route at the predetermined dose. This infection model was used to induce a systemic immune response, particularly affecting splenic tissue.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, while homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test.

Since the data were not normally distributed and/or not homogeneous, nonparametric analysis was performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by the Mann–Whitney post hoc test. Data were presented as median and interquartile range, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Histological observations

Histological examination of spleen tissue in the normal control group showed well-defined white pulp and red pulp structures. In contrast, the infected group exhibited disruption of splenic architecture, including depletion of white pulp and vascular congestion. The treatment group demonstrated improved splenic structure compared to the infected group.

DISCUSSION

Infection with S. typhimurium induces systemic immune responses that affect lymphoid organs such as the spleen.[20] As an important immune organ, the spleen filters pathogens and facilitates immune cell activation during infection.[67]

In this study, rats infected with S. typhimurium exhibited increased spleen weight and volume compared to normal controls, indicating splenomegaly. This condition is commonly associated with immune cell proliferation and inflammatory infiltration during bacterial infection.[21]

The infection dose used in this study (2.1 × 107 CFU/mL) was higher than that used in previous mouse studies (1 × 106 CFU/mL)[22] but lower than another study using 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL.[23] Differences in infection doses may be influenced by bacterial strain, animal condition, and inoculation route.

Histological findings showed severe disruption of the white pulp in infected rats. White pulp contains lymphoid follicles involved in antigen presentation and lymphocyte activation. Infection with Salmonella stimulates recruitment of immune cells within splenic tissue, resulting in structural changes associated with host defense.[2425]

In this study showed that administration of S. vulcani extract appeared to improve splenic morphology in infected rats. The treatment group showed more organized splenic architecture compared with the negative control group (see Figure 1). These findings suggest that the extract may help maintain immune organ integrity during infection.

The observed effects may be related to bioactive compounds present in pirdot leaves. Previous studies reported that S. vulcani contains flavonoids and phenolic compounds with antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties.[26272829] These compounds can regulate inflammatory responses and reduce oxidative stress during infection.[30]

Flavonoids are also known to stimulate splenocyte proliferation and enhance immune responses.[31] They are increase macrophage activity, lysosomal enzyme production, and nitric oxide synthesis, which contribute to enhanced humoral and cellular immunity.[32] Furthermore, flavonoids can activate CD4+ T cells and stimulate interferon-γ production to control intracellular bacterial replication.[33] Our study demonstrated improved spleen histopathology in the S. vulcani (Pirdot) treatment group, although some parameters did not reach statistical significance. This may be due to biological variability or limited sample size. Further studies are needed to investigate the molecular mechanisms and identify the specific bioactive compounds responsible for these effects.[34]

CONCLUSION

S. vulcani leaf extract tended to preserve spleen morphometric parameters and histopathological features in rats infected with S. typimurium, with observable differences compared to the infected group; however, these effects were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Therefore, while these findings suggest a potential immunomodulatory effect, further studies are warranted to confirm its immunomodulatory activity.
