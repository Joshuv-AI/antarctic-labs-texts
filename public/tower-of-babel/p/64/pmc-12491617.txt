Noise-augmented contrastive learning with attention for knowledge-aware collaborative recommendation
Authors: Wanyi Gu, Hua Xu, Xiang Peng, Ning Rao, Huali Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Knowledge graph (KG) plays an increasingly important role in recommender systems. Recently, Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) and Graph Attention Network (GAT) based model has gradually become the theme of Collaborative Knowledge Graph (CKG). However, recommender systems encounter long-tail distributions in large-scale graphs. The inherent data sparsity concentrates relationships within few entities, generating uneven embedding distributions. Contrastive Learning (CL) counters data sparsity in recommender systems by extracting general representations from raw data, enhancing long-tail distribution handling. Nonetheless, traditional graph augmentation techniques have proven to be of limited use in CL-based recommendations. Accordingly, this paper proposes Noise Augmentations Knowledge Graph Attention Contrastive Learning method (NA-KGACL) for Recommendation. The proposed method establishes a multi-level contrastive framework by integrating CL with Knowledge-GAT, where node representations are refined via projection heads and shuffled batch normalization. Additionally, it replaces ineffective graph augmentation methods with a simple yet powerful noise-augmented algorithm to generate contrastive learning views. Experimental results on three large-scale and real-world datasets indicate that NA-KGACL improves the learned representations, which results in increased recommendation accuracy and more efficient training.

Introduction

The widespread success of recommendation systems has facilitated their application across various web applications, including search software, e-commerce systems and web news1,2. Recommendation systems serve a critical function in content platforms by utilizing historical user data to predict user preferences. The essence of recommendation systems lies in their ability to anticipate user choices based on past behavior. Leveraging Knowledge Graph (KG) techniques to extract relationships within prior information is an effective approach in recommendation systems3,4. With advancements in graph neural network technology, the Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT) has proven effective in constructing Collaborative Knowledge Graph (CKG) between users and items5.

Nevertheless, the vast quantities of graph data in knowledge graphs often lead to a long-tailed distribution, where a significant portion of relationships are concentrated in a small subset of entity nodes6,7. Contrastive Learning (CL) efficiently tackles the issue of data sparsity by learning general representations from unlabeled raw data. Its application to recommendation systems has led to significant advancements, as demonstrated by recent studies8,9. The emergence of CL-based recommendation methods has attracted considerable attention and yielded promising results. Empirical evidence shows that CL promotes more even distribution of representations across various types of embeddings, which facilitates the enhancement of long-tail items10,11.

Although some studies have begun to apply Contrast Learning algorithm to solve long-tail problems in graph data12, these methods are often not ill-suited for KG. This is attributed to the distinctive organization of knowledge graph data, which is structured into triplets (head entity, relation, tail entity). While this triplet structure inherently reduces redundancy13, the sheer volume of KG data combined with the complexity of modeling long-tail distributions across heterogeneous graph elements presents a significant challenge.

In CL-based recommendation systems, the primary approach to enhancing representations typically involves introducing structural perturbations in the graph, such as randomly deleting edges or nodes14. However, the iterative generation of these graph augmentations, coupled with the need to repeatedly construct adjacency matrices, incurs substantial computational overhead during training15. This paper focuses on exploring more efficient alternative approaches to graph augmentation that maintain effective representation learning while reducing computational costs.

Considering the limitations of the existing solutions, developing a recommendation system that leverages higher-order information in KG and establishes a multi-level contrastive learning framework. is crucial. Therefore, we propose the Noise Augmentations Knowledge Graph Attention Contrastive Learning method (NA-KGACL) for recommendation. This method applies CL algorithms to Graph Attention Network (GAT)16, in order to enhance data representation and recommendation effectiveness. By utilizing simple yet efficient noise-based embedding augmentations to generate contrastive samples, NA-KGACL mitigates unnecessary computational overhead associated with structural graph perturbations.

Related work

Recommendation models

To predict user preferences based on user behavior data, a considerable body of research has been dedicated to Collaborative Filtering (CF)16,17. The core objective of CF is to predict whether a user will like a particular item. CF techniques are broadly categorized intouser-based CF (userCF) and item-based CF (itemCF). Among these, itemCF is particularly suitable for applications with relatively stable interest patterns, such as e-commerce platforms and video recommendation systems. Building upon the co-occurrence matrix in CF, Matrix Factorization (MF) methods were subsequently developed18. These techniques decompose the co-occurrence matrix generated by collaborative filtering to obtain denser latent vectors representing users and items. This approach uncovers latent interests and implicit features of both users and items, thereby mitigating the limitations of CF associated with data sparsity.

Although CF and MF methods are effective and widely applicable, they fail to effectively model side information19, which results in suboptimal performance in sparse scenarios characterized by limited user-item interactions20,21. Subsequent studies address this limitation by introducing Supervised Learning (SL) models, such as Factorization Machines (FM)22 and Neural Factorization Machines (NFM)23, which incorporate additional features beyond user-item interactions. While demonstrating strong performance, a key limitation lies in treating each interaction as an independent instance, thus neglecting potential connections between interactions. The emergence of Graph Neural Network (GNN)24–27 has shown remarkable potential in modeling user behavior data28, leading to their widespread adoption in recommender systems. Recommendation models leveraging GNN have achieved superior performance across various scenarios compared to earlier recommendation methods. Among the many variants of GNN, Graph Convolutional Network (GCN)29,30 has been studied by a large number of scholars and driving many recommendation models, such as LightGCN31, ConceptGCN32. Despite variations in implementation details, these GCN-based models share a common mechanism: the layer-by-layer aggregation of information from neighboring nodes within the user-item interaction graph33,34.

The concept of KGAT introduced in5 builds upon GCN by incorporating GAT to capture higher-order dependencies in recommendation systems. Through recursive propagation of embeddings from neighbor nodes—including users, items, and entities, the node representations can be improved. Moreover, an attention mechanism assigns differential weights to neighboring nodes, inspiring subsequent models, such as KGCR35, DS-KGAT36. Wu. Z. proposes a novel recommendation model called KGAT-AX, which aggregates information through multi-layer interaction information propagation, enhancing its generalization ability. It also learns the reasoning relationships of each entity to aggregate information from neighboring entities37. Wang, X et al. propose leveraging auxiliary item knowledge to mine the underlying intentions behind user-item interactions. They introduced a knowledge graph-based intent network model named KGIN, which models each intent as an attentive combination of KG relations. This approach encourages the independence of different intents, aiming to achieve better model performance and interpretability38.

Long-tail distribution

The long tail distribution originates from the long tail effect, which is the most prominent characteristic of a power law distribution39,40. Even though the probability of larger values occurring is very low, the long extension of its tail can still have a significant impact on the overall behavior. In the context of recommendation systems, this manifests as data sparsity, where a few entities hold the majority of relationships, leading to uneven embedding distributions. Many scholars have dedicated their research to long tail distributions. Yin, H. et al. propose an efficient absorption time algorithm to help users find their preferred long tail items. This paper introduced two entropy-bias absorption cost algorithms to distinguish changes in different user-item rating pairs41. Liu, S. et al. propose novel network architecture called TailNet42. This architecture classifies items into short-head and long-tail categories based on click frequency. Then, based on user preferences, it determines the user’s preference for these two types of items. Wu, Junda, et al. addresses the long-tail recommendation problem by introducing Collaborative Retrieval-Augmented LLMs, significantly enhancing LLMs’ reasoning capabilities for recommendation tasks under long-tail distributions43.

Contrastive learning for recommendation

CL has demonstrated the ability to handle large volumes of unlabeled data8,44, with applications spanning multiple domains. The core objective of CL is to optimize node embeddings by simultaneously maximizing semantic consistency and ensuring distribution uniformity. Zhao. et al. propose a graph clustering method based on Graph-CL penalized by reconstruction error45. Liu et al. propose a Graph Disentangled Contrastive framework for Cross-Domain Recommendation46. As a fundamental approach for addressing data sparsity, CL has gained prominence in recommender systems. Given its potential to enhance data representation by drawing positive samples closer and pushing negative samples further apart, researchers have started integrating CL into recommendation systems, inspired by its success in other domains10–12. According to our investigation, S3-Recis47 is the first study to merge CL for sequential recommendation. The process begins by randomly masking specific attributes and items to create sequence augmentations. Following this, a Transformer model is pre-trained by promoting consistency across different augmentations48.

The process can be segmented into two steps: initially, improving the user-item bipartite graph through graph perturbations to build positive samples; subsequently, the coherence of the representations obtained by augmenting the various graphs is maximized44,49. Nevertheless, extensive graph augmentation operations incur substantial computational overhead. Consequently, developing efficient alternatives to graph augmentations for sample pair construction becomes critical. Ye et al. introduce the CPF-POI, an adaptive clustering-based personalized federated learning framework for recommendation that incorporates location noise50. Similarly, Yu et al. propose a simple comparison recommendation framework named XSimGCL10, which demonstrates the effectiveness of noise-based graph attention as a substitute for traditional graph attention.

Proposed method

The model as shown in the Fig. 3. The left subfigure shows the framework of NA-KGACL, consists of four parts: 1) Embedding Layer, which parameterizes each node into a vector to construct the CKG; 2) Attentional embedding propagation Layer, recursively updates node representations by propagating embeddings of node neighbors and employing attention methods to determine weights for each neighbor during propagation; 3) Contrastive Learning Embedding Layer, which applies noise-based contrastive learning method to improve the representation of node embeddings. It is described in detail in the right subfigure of Fig. 3; 4) Prediction Layer, which combines user and item representations from all propagation layers to produce final prediction results.

Embedding layer

Knowledge graph embeddings encode entities and relationships as vectors, preserving the structural information inherent in the graph. In this work, we employ TransR51 to study CKG. To capture the semantics of triplets \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(h,r,t)$$\end{document} within the graph, we employ the translation principle \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_{h}^{r}+{e_r} \approx e_{t}^{r}$$\end{document} to learn embeddings for each entity and relationship. Specifically, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_h},{\text{ }}{e_t} \in {R^d}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_r} \in {R^k}$$\end{document}are the embeddings of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h$$\end{document} , \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document} , and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r$$\end{document}, respectively. In the space of relation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r$$\end{document} , the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_{h}^{r}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_{t}^{r}$$\end{document} is the projected representations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_h}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_t}$$\end{document}. Consequently, the plausibility score for a given triplet can be formulated as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g(h,r,t)=\left\| {{{\mathbf{W}}_r}{e_h}+{e_r} - {{\mathbf{W}}_r}{e_t}} \right\|_{2}^{2}.$$\end{document}

Here, we apply \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{W}}_r} \in {R^{k{{ \times d}}}}$$\end{document} to represent the transformation matrix of relationship, which maps the entities from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d$$\end{document}- dimensional space into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document} -dimensional space. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g(h,r,t)$$\end{document} in higher value means a greater likelihood, and conversely, a lower score suggests a lower likelihood of validity.

The training of TransR aims to distinguish valid triplets from invalid ones by focusing on their relative ordering, where a pairwise ranking loss is used to promote this differentiation:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}_{{\text{KG}}}}=\sum\limits_{{\left( {h,r,t,t'} \right) \in \mathcal{T}}} - \ln \sigma \left( {g\left( {h,r,t'} \right) - g\left( {h,r,t} \right)} \right),$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{T}=\{ \left( {h,r,t,t'} \right)|\left( {h,r,t} \right) \in \mathcal{G}, \left( {h,r,t^{\prime}} \right) \notin \mathcal{G}\}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma ( \cdot )$$\end{document} is the sigmoid function. Specifically, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( {h,r,t} \right)$$\end{document} represents a positive instance, denoting a triplet that exists in the CKG). While the negative instances \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( {h,r,t'} \right)$$\end{document} represents a triplet obtained by randomly replacing one entity in a valid triplet, which is a non-existent fake triplet. The purpose of this approach is to prevent model collapse, which would cause the embeddings of all entities and relations to converge to identical values. Because without constraints, the trivial minimization solution for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\| {{{\mathbf{W}}_r}{e_h}+{e_r} - {{\mathbf{W}}_r}{e_t}} \right\|$$\end{document} is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_h}={e_r}={e_t}=0$$\end{document}. Notably, this differs from the contrastive learning method introduced in Section “Contrastive learning embedding layer”, where positive sample pairs are generated by applying semantically invariant noise augmentation to the same sample, while negative sample pairs consist of other distinct samples that actually exist in the data.

Attentive embedding propagation layer

Using GCN networks, knowledge graphs can be recursively propagated and embedded along high order connectivity. Additionally, GAT is used to generate attention weights for cascading propagation, allowing for the differentiation of the importance of non-connected relationships. This section initially outlines a single-layer network comprising three components and then extends it to a multi-layer architecture.

Information Propagation:

Consider an entity h. We represent the set of all triplets with h as the head entity by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{N}_h}=\{ (h,r,t)|(h,r,t) \in \mathcal{G}\}$$\end{document}, termed ego-network52. Essentially, it captures the direct neighborhood information of entity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h$$\end{document} by treating it as the central node, which serves as the starting point for neighbor information aggregation. We compute a linear combination of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h$$\end{document}’s ego network to represent the first-order connectivity structure of entity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h$$\end{document}.3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_{{\mathcal{N}_h}}}=\sum\limits_{{(h,r,t) \in {\mathcal{N}_h}}} \pi (h,r,t){e_t},$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi (h,r,t)$$\end{document} controls the decay factor for each propagation, indicating the extent of information transfer from the tail entity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document} to the head entity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h$$\end{document} with relation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r$$\end{document}.

Knowledge-aware Attention:

We apply \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi (h,r,t)$$\end{document} through the knowledge network, the formula is shown below:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi (h,r,t)={\left( {{{\mathbf{W}}_{\mathbf{r}}}{e_t}} \right)^ \top }\tanh \left( {\left( {{{\mathbf{W}}_{\mathbf{r}}}{e_h}+{e_r}} \right)} \right),$$\end{document}

where the hyperbolic tangent function (tanh) serves as the non-linear activation function. The knowledge attention score is determined by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_h}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_t}$$\end{document}.

Hereafter, softmax function is utilized to normalize the coefficients of all triples linked to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h$$\end{document}:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi (h,r,t)=\frac{{\exp (\pi (h,r,t))}}{{\sum\limits_{{\left( {h,{r^\prime },{t^\prime }} \right) \in {\mathcal{N}_h}}} {\exp } \left( {\pi \left( {h,r',t'} \right)} \right)}}.$$\end{document}

Thus, the final attention score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi (h,r,t)$$\end{document} affects the different weights given to each relationship.

Information Aggregation:

In the final stage, we integrate the entity representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_h}$$\end{document} with its ego-network representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_{{\mathcal{N}_h}}}$$\end{document} to produce novel representations for the entity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h$$\end{document}, as follows:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_{h}^{{(1)}}=f({e_h},{\text{ }}{e_{{\mathcal{N}_h}}}).$$\end{document}

This aggregator performs additional encoding of the feature interactions between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_{{\mathcal{N}_h}}}$$\end{document}and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_h}$$\end{document}, and more messages can be delivered through similar entities.

The process of high-order embedding propagation enhances the representation learning by incorporating attribute-based signals. Consequently, the learned representations capture a richer and more nuanced view of the data, which contributes to improved performance across various tasks.

Noise-based augmentation

For improved performance of the contrastive recommendation model, it is crucial to regulate the uniformity of learned representations within a specific range. Given the complexity and time required to modify graph structures for controlled uniformity, we focus instead on manipulating the embedding space.

In contrastive learning, random graph augmentations are commonly employed for constructing positive sample pairs. For instance, the original paper on SGL12 introduces three graph augmentation techniques: SGL-ND (-ND for node dropout), SGL-ED (-ED for edge dropout), and SGL-RW (-RW for random walk). Applying dropout to edges or nodes in graph augmentation may inadvertently result in the loss of critical information. This concern is accentuated in datasets with a higher prevalence of similar graph structures. In such cases, it is possible that augmented positive sample pairs are mistakenly identified as negative sample pairs, which can reduce the recommendation accuracy. Inspired by the introduction of subtle perturbations to images10,55, we propose a novel approach: adding random noise directly to the representations for effective augmentation. As shown in Fig. 4, by adding noise to the embeddings of randomly nodes (include various types of noise such as Gaussian noise, Poisson noise, etc., as detailed in Section “Impact of noise parameter”), it is possible to construct different positive sample pairs from the same graph structure. This approach improves the distribution of node embeddings, and addresses the issue of long-tailed distributions.

Shuffling BN

Our encoders, i.e., Projection head \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g(\cdot)$$\end{document}, utilize Batch Normalization (BN) as employed in standard ResNet architectures56. However, the MoCo model57 observed that using BN can impede the model’s capacity to acquire efficient representations, as also noted in other studies58. This issue arises because BN introduces intra-batch communication between samples, which can inadvertently cause information leakage, leading the model to “cheat” during the pretext task, resulting in rapid convergence to a low-loss solution.

To address this issue, we employ Shuffling BN. Specifically, we use multiple GPUs for training and perform BN independently on the samples of each GPU. Additionally, we shuffle the sample order within the current mini-batch, frequently switching modes and redistributing the data across GPUs. This ensures that the batch statistics used to compute the query and its positive key are derived from two distinct subsets of data, thereby preventing information leakage and effectively mitigating the “cheating” problem.

Dataset description

To evaluate the performance of NA-KGACL method, we use three public large-scale datasets: MovieLens-1 M, Amazon-book, and Last-FM. These datasets vary in domain, size, and sparsity, making them well-suited for validating the robustness of the model.

MovieLens-1M60: MovieLens datesets are collected from the MovieLens Website. Among them, MovieLens-1 M stands out as the most popular choice due to its well-balanced rating count and density. In order to uphold the dataset quality, we implement a 15-coresetting approach, which involves preserving users and items possessing a minimum of fifteen interactions.

Amazon-Book61: The Amazon review dataset is commonly utilized for product recommendation tasks. In our case, we specifically choose the Amazon book subset from this extensive collection. We apply the same 15-coresetting in order to uphold the dataset quality.

LFM-1b62: LFM-1b dataset leverages Last.fm artist tags, which are indexed using two dictionaries of genre and style descriptors sourced from Allmusic and Freebase. This dataset aims to create a preference profile for each user in LFM-1b, represented as a vector over genres. Each listening event in the dataset includes information such as the artist, album, track name, and timestamp. In our experiments, we use the LFM-1b-tracks dataset, applying the same 15-core setting.

We build user-item Knowledge Graphs for each dataset. In contrast to current knowledge-aware datasets that focus solely on single-hop item entities, our approach incorporates triples involving two-hop item neighbor entities. To ensure the quality of the KG, we preprocess the datesets to filter out infrequent entities——those that appear in fewer than 15 instances. To ensure the quality of the KG, we preprocess the three KGs to filter out infrequent entities——i.e., those appearing in fewer than 15 instances across the two datasets. Due to the large size of the LFM-1b dataset, we process it further by randomly sampling from the filtered data.

To partition the interaction history for each dataset, we randomly allocate 80% of each user’s interactions for training purposes, while the remaining 20% is reserved for testing. In addition, 10% of the training data is allocated for creating a validation set, crucial for hyperparameter tuning. Within the training data, each user-item interaction is treated as a positive instance. To balance the dataset, we adopt a negative sampling technique, pairing positive interactions with items that users have not previously engaged with.

We study how adding noise affects recommendation performance, whether it enhances or hinders recommendation. The last row of Table 6 shows the results of training without noise augmentation. The findings demonstrate that noise augmentation is essential for effective contrastive learning, as key metrics, such as Recall@10, showed significant improvements when noise was applied.

The experimental results indicate that different datasets exhibit varying sensitivities to noise types, primarily driven by the alignment between their intrinsic data characteristics and the mathematical properties of the noise. MovieLens-1 M demonstrates higher sensitivity to Gaussian Noise because this dataset contains dense rating data (non-zero values account for ~ 95%), where ratings are heavily concentrated. (mean between 3.5 and 4.0 and a small standard deviation). In such a distribution, large-magnitude perturbations can easily alter the semantic meaning of ratings (e.g., a change from 4 to 5 represents a fundamental shift in preference). The continuous, symmetric minor perturbations generated by the normal distribution of Gaussian noise (e.g., 4.0→[3.9,4.1]) not only effectively simulate subjective fluctuations in user ratings but also confine the variations within the same semantic range. Additionally, it prevents ratings from exceeding valid bounds, reducing semantic distortion caused by truncation. As shown in Table 6, Gaussian Noise yields the best performance metrics, improving Recall@10 by 1.85%. In contrast, Amazon-Book’s sensitivity to Poisson Noise stems from its extreme sparsity and non-negative nature. As evidenced by the results in Tables 5 and 6, Amazon-Book demonstrates the lowest Recall values among all datasets, attributable to its massive scale, largest number of entities, and consequently higher data sparsity. The discrete, non-negative characteristics of Poisson Noise better align with this scenario: its zero-value preservation mechanism (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P({x_{noisy}}=0|x=0)={e^{ - \lambda }}$$\end{document}) retains over 90% of non-interacted items. As shown in Table 6, Poisson Noise yields a 1.9% improvement in Recall@10. Finally, the LFM-1b dataset demonstrates particular sensitivity to Uniform Noise, which directly correlates with uniform noise’s high-variance power-law distribution characteristics. Uniform noise exhibits robustness in high-variance data scenarios, effectively maintaining structural stability in perturbed data. LFM-1b exhibits extreme value disparity (90% of users have fewer than 10 interactions, while top users exceed 105 interactions). Uniform Noise achieves its key advantage through fixed absolute perturbations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x \pm a$$\end{document} ). Its relative perturbation equilibrium prevents excessive amplification of small values, while preserving the original distribution pattern. Table 6 demonstrates that Uniform Noise achieves a 5.38% enhancement in Recall@10 performance. Unlike other noise, Laplacian Noise’s double-exponential properties result in universally suboptimal outcomes across the three datasets.

Conclusions and future work

This paper explores higher-order connections and long-tailed distributions of semantic relationships in CKG to enhance knowledge-aware recommendation. We propose a noise-based contrastive learning recommendation method——NA-KGACL, which explicitly models higher-order connections in KG networks in an end-to-end manner. Additionally, a multi-level contrastive learning algorithm is employed to establish positive and negative sample pairs, improving data representation from multiple perspectives. Instead of relying on ineffective graph augmentation techniques, we employ a simple yet effective noise-based embedding enhancement to generate views for contrastive learning. Comprehensive experiments across three large real-world datasets validate the effectiveness and robustness of NA-KGACL.

The research significance of KG in recommendation systems is profound. Traditional recommendation systems often rely solely on users’ historical behavior data for recommendations, lacking clear interpretability. In contrast, KG-based recommendation systems can provide users with more intuitive and comprehensible recommendation rationales by exhibiting the knowledge paths or relational chains underlying the recommendation results. This study explores the potential of integrating GAT and CL in recommendation systems, thereby enhancing recommendation success rates and offering users a more precise and personalized browsing experience. Furthermore, it significantly enriches the diversity and comprehensiveness of recommended content.

Looking ahead, such algorithms hold promise for application across various domains of recommendation, including knowledge graph recommendation techniques in multimodal contexts, privacy protection and data security in recommendations, dynamic recommendation and real-time recommendation systems, cross-domain recommendation systems and large-scale online recommendation systems, etc. For real-time recommendation systems, millisecond-level responsiveness to transient user preferences can be achieved through dynamic KG streaming updates and lightweight GAT, with parallel optimization for high-concurrency data throughput. Addressing cross-domain challenges, an innovative cross-domain transfer framework integrating relational contrastive learning with meta-learning adapters will be developed to tackle domain adaptation issues in cold-start environments by penetrating semantic barriers across heterogeneous KG.

Regarding large-scale online recommendation systems, distributed graph computing architectures and edge-cloud collaborative inference mechanisms will be designed to dynamically balance system loads against computational efficiency. Simultaneously advancing privacy preservation, federated KG construction techniques integrated with differentially private graph propagation algorithms enable precise user modeling and secure personalized recommendations within cryptographic frameworks, effectively resolving the fundamental trade-off between data privacy and model performance. Furthermore, explorations into multimodal knowledge fusion, temporal preference evolution modeling, and explainable recommendation mechanisms will further extend the application boundaries of this technological framework, providing novel methodological support for both academic research and industrial implementation.
