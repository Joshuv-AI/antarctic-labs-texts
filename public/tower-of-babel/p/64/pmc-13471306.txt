A chromosome-level genome for gila trout (Oncorhynchus gilae) provides a novel resource for conservation and management
Authors: Megan A. Barela Hudgell, Megan J. Osborne, Irene Salinas, Thomas F. Turner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGenetic data have long been applied in conservation and management for threatened and endangered species to track effects of inbreeding, hybridization, and population structure. However, when a reference genome is lacking, there is inadequate information to confidently predict extinction risk or potential for local adaptation to varying environmental factors. A reference genome provides information about the identity and function of key gene families that likely affect organismal performance in nature. A well-resolved genome is therefore vital to future conservation planning for protected species. Gila Trout have undergone severe genetic and demographic bottlenecks due to habitat fragmentation and climate-based drought and wildfires in the Gila national forest, NM. The species is currently managed under a recovery program that works to ensure the preservation of five genetically and morphologically distinct Gila Trout lineages.ResultsHere we present a chromosome-level assembly and annotation of the genome of Gila Trout Oncorhynchus gilae, a threatened trout species closely related to the Rainbow Trout. We verify that the Gila Trout genome is composed of 28 chromosomes, of which, 24 are metacentric and four are telocentric. Gila Trout maintains karyotypes of key chromosomal inversions observed in ancestral salmonids. Annotation and comparison of genes involved in adaptive immunity in the Gila Trout genome against other salmonids reveals conservation of coding regions but high variability within the introns and non-coding regions around the genes that could bolster immunocompetence in the face of novel pathogens.ConclusionThis genome provides a key resource for the further monitoring and conservation of the Gila Trout.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12917-2.

Introduction

Over the past decade, genomic data has become more widely used in species conservation with the goal of better understanding and managing genetic variation in imperiled species [1]. In these species, intraspecific genetic variation is a key factor in assessing resilience and likelihoods of species persistence or extinction [2–5]. The loss of intraspecific variation within a population is often due to severe reductions in population size (i.e., bottlenecks) [3–5]. These events can increase inbreeding and lead to accumulation of deleterious mutations [6–12]. Loss of genetic diversity is linked to lower fitness of a species, while greater genetic diversity is associated with greater fitness and resilience within a population through the maintenance of adaptive potential [13–15]. However, this fundamental paradigm has been recently challenged by demonstrating that extinction risk is not fully dependent on standing levels of genetic diversity at ‘neutrally evolving’ genetic loci distributed across the genome [16, 17]. This may suggest that, while analysis of neutrally evolving genomic regions is helpful for characterizing population structure, relatedness, and hybridization, neutral data may be inadequate for assessing the extinction or fitness risk within populations [17–21]. Full genome sequencing and analysis have therefore emerged as a means of identifying specific candidate genes hypothesized to affect organismal performance in the environment [13]. This approach may identify specific genes and/or loci responsible for local adaptations, inbreeding depression, or the genetic basis of fitness traits that may hold a greater influence on species extinction risk [22–26].

Oncorhynchus gilae, commonly called Gila Trout, suffered a series of severe bottlenecks and local extirpations due to drought, wildfire, and habitat fragmentation within their native range [27] (Fig. 1). Demographic bottlenecks led to losses of genetic diversity in all five known lineages of this threatened species [28–32]. Genetic rescue attempts were made for the more at-risk lineages, specifically the Spruce Creek lineage that has repeatedly shown both the lowest levels of allelic richness (AR) and heterozygosity (Hexp) [31] and a lower success rate in hatchery rearing (Mora National Fish Hatchery, personal communication). However, hatchery success does not necessarily correlate to success in the wild [32]. Even with a dramatic loss in genetic diversity, Gila Trout populations persist in the wild, with the mean fitness (measured as body condition) not correlating as expected with the genetic diversity found within the species [32]. If it is true that fitness is not related necessarily to genome-wide diversity we can ask questions as to how organisms persist despite large-scale disturbance, demographic bottlenecks, severe genetic drift, and loss of genetic diversity. This is especially relevant in the case of immune genes where balancing selection is considered a key factor in their maintenance and their ability to detect and defend against a range of pathogens [33–42]. With loss of immune gene diversity, increased risk of disease within populations and greater susceptibility to disease outbreaks are expected to occur (Reviewed in [43]). Because of this, immune genes are of particular interest when it comes to genomic conservation [26, 44, 45].

Thus far, no previous work has generated a genome assembly of Gila Trout. Here we present a chromosome level genome assembly for Gila Trout from the Spruce Creek lineage. The genome was generated de novo from PacBio long-read and Hi-C sequencing for the purpose of furthering conservation efforts. We identified key genomic structures and annotated genes identified as being potentially capable of differentiating between Gila Trout lineages. We compared the genome of the Gila Trout with other salmonid species, including Oncorhynchus mykiss (Rainbow Trout), which shared a common ancestor with Gila Trout approximately 1 to 2 million years ago [28, 46]. Comparative analyses with other salmonid genomes suggest distinct genomic architecture including metacentric versus acrocentric chromosome numbers, maintenance of ancestral chromosome structure, and conservation of key immune genes related to T-cell and B-cell activation in Gila Trout. Comparative genomic analyses identified other potential regions of interest related to thermal stress response like heat shock proteins and other chaperones for further analysis across lineages within the species.

Materials and methods

Study species

Gila Trout is found in upland streams in southwestern New Mexico and southeastern Arizona, USA (Fig. 1). The species is currently listed as threatened (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 71 FR 40657) under the Endangered Species Act (ESA) and is actively managed, including hatchery stocking to mitigate demographic losses from catastrophic wildfires and drought and habitat restoration and renovation to mitigate negative impacts of hybridization with nonnative Rainbow Trout. There are five recognized lineages; Spruce Creek (orange stream), Whiskey Creek (green stream), Iron Creek (purple stream), Main Diamond (Northern Blue stream), South Diamond (Southern Blue stream), within the species distributed across the current geographic range. The map of Gila Trout sampling sites was generated in QGIS 3.36 using data from hydroRIVERS, ESRI Terrain, US Census, and National Earth 5.1.1. Data from FishNet2 was used to map Gila Trout collection sites.

DNA extraction and sequencing

PacBio sequencing

High molecular weight genomic DNA was obtained from a fin clip sampled from a single adult Gila Trout of unknown sex representing the Spruce Creek lineage held at the Mora National Fish Hatchery, New Mexico (approximate locality: 35.994948, -105.319576; Museum of Southwestern Biology Catalog Number: MSB110774, searchable at http://arctosdb.org). A QIAGEN genomic tip was used to extract genomic DNA using the manufacturer’s protocol.

PacBio HiFi library preparation was completed by the University of California Davis Genomics Core Facility and used to sequence the Gila Trout genome using 3 SMRT cells that generated roughly 62.3 Gb, and 5 million filtered/HiFi reads, yielding 20.7X long read coverage based on an estimated chromosome size of 3 GB. Quast 5.2.0 [47] was used to determine the initial value of N50 and average read length of HiFi reads. The N50 value was 13,449, with 71.6% of reads between 10,000 base pairs (bps) and 25,000 bps. The longest read was 63,110 bps.

Hi-C sequencing

Genomic DNA was also obtained from muscle tissue obtained from a single (but a different individual than used above) adult Spruce Creek Gila Trout of unknown sex (Museum of Southwestern Biology Catalog Number: MSB118415). Dual-ended chromosome conformation capture sequencing (Hi-C) was conducted by Phase Genomics using Sanger Illumina 1.9. This procedure generated a total number of 123,321,144 sequences with an average length of 140–150 bp.

Contig assembly

Contig assembly of HiFi filtered reads and Hi-C sequences were generated using hifiasm assembler v.0.13-r308 [48, 49], with haplotypes number set to 4 based on known tetraploidy within the species [50–54]. Quast 5.2.0 [47] and BUSCO v5.4.2 [55–57] were used to determine the quality of the contig assembly with an N50 greater than the initial sequencing N50 (13,446) and a BUSCO score of over 95% considered acceptable.

Scaffold assembly

Genome assembly software Juicer 1.6 [58], YaHS 1.2a.1 [59], Juicebox v2.20.00 [60], bwa 0.7.19-r1273, samtools 1.13, and bioawk version 20110810 were used to generate a de novo assembly of the Spruce Creek Gila Trout genome using a pipeline provided by Dr. Dan MacGuigan (unpublished). Hi-C sequences were indexed using bwa (bwa index -a bwtsw -p) and samtools (faidx) while chromosome sizes were generated using bioawk (bioawk -c fastx ‘{print $name”\t”length($seq)}’). Sequences were then mapped to the hifiasm genome assembly using Juicer with pre-set parameters consisting of the path to the directory, chromosome size file, and the Hi-C sequences with a singular flag of (-s none), and the resulting .bam file was used to generate a .hic and .assemble files in YaHS using the reference, bam file, and -o for the output prefix and preset parameters (resolution (-r) upper limit automatically adjusted to genome size).

Juicebox was used to visually analyze and correct misplaced contigs within the assembly before it was re-run through the same pipeline for verification of assembly by ensuring that as many contigs as possible were placed within chromosomes based on the mapping data.

Identification and numbering of chromosome edges

Chromosomes that could not be separated in Juicebox were manually separated by aligning the final Juicer output against the Arlee Strain of Rainbow Trout (Arlee) genome (GCA_013265735.3) using minimap2 2.28-r1209 with parameters set to allow for up to a 10% sequence divergence. Subsequent .paf files were then used to identify the edges of the chromosome matches in reference to the same Rainbow Trout genome using the pafr 0.0.2 package (https://github.com/dwinter/pafr) in R 4.4.0 [61] and were separated using the bedtools v2.30.0 getfasta commands [62]. While the number of chromosomal arms are highly conserved across salmonids, the number of acrocentric and telocentric chromosomes vary greatly, with chromosomes seemingly breaking within the centromere to generate telocentric and acrocentric chromosomes or fusing to generate metacentric chromosomes [63, 64]. The number of telocentric/acrocentric vs. metacentric chromosomes within the assembled Gila Trout genome was determined through comparison with Rainbow Trout Arlee and Swanson genomes. KAT – the K-mer analysis toolkit V2.4.1 [65] as used to determine quality of reads against the final assembled genome using the kat comp command.

Annotation

Liftoff v1.6.3 [66] was used in conjunction with the Rainbow Trout Arlee Isolate assembly file (GCF_013265735.2) to generate an initial annotation of the Gila Trout genome. Liftoff basic parameters (alignment coverage and sequence identity ≥ 50%, minimap2 parameters - end-bonus 5 -N 50 -p 0.5) were used and a .gff file was produced. Further analysis of the annotation was done using Liftofftools v0.4.3 [67], where the Gila Trout annotation was compared to the Rainbow Trout Arlee annotation.

Comparison to other salmonid genomes

Genomes from other members of family Salmonidae were compared to the assembled Gila Trout genome. Species for comparison were selected based on the presence and quality of an annotated genome. Selected genomes were: Rainbow Trout Arlee Isolate (GCA_013265735.3), Oncorhynchus mykiss Swanson Isolate (GCA_025558465.1), Oncorhynchus kisutch (GCA_002021735.2), Oncorhynchus nerka (GCA_034236695.1), Oncorhynchus tshawytscha (GCA_018296145.1), Salmo trutta (GCA_901001165.2), Salmo salar (GCA_905237065.2), Salvelinus fontinalis (GCF_029448725.1), Coregonus clupeaformis (GCA_020615455.1), Thymallus thymallus (GCA_023634145.1), Coregonus sp. ‘balchen’ (GCA_902810595.1).

Identification of repeats

RepeatMasker 4.1.6 software [68] was used to identify repeated regions within the Gila Trout genome. The repeat library used to run RepeatMasker consisted of 2126 entries specifically found within the Rainbow Trout genome downloaded from FishTEDB [69, 70] (https://www.fishtedb.com/). The same analysis was run on Rainbow Trout Arlee and S. salar (Atlantic Salmon) genomes to compare the identity and number of repeated regions among species.

SyRI and plotsr

Comparisons of select chromosomes were generated using SyRI [71] and plotsr [72] pipeline in Linux. Minimap2 [73, 74], with parameters set to allow for up to 5% sequence divergence, was used to align selected chromosomes between Gila Trout, Rainbow Trout Swanson and Arlee isolates. Furthermore, Atlantic Salmon was selected for comparison due to the presence of a well characterized genome, its anadromous life cycle, and its deeply ancestral evolutionary relationship to both Gila and Rainbow Trout. Aligned sequences were run through fixchr 0.2 (https://github.com/schneebergerlab/fixchr) to reorient chromosomes if needed. The generated alignments were then run through SyRI 1.6.3 to find structural rearrangements between the alignments. The results were visualized using plotsr 1.1.1.

Circos

Circos [75] was used to visualize alignments between the Rainbow Trout and finalized Gila Trout chromosomes. FASTA files containing only chromosomes from the Gila Trout genome and Rainbow Trout genomes were generated using seqkit v2.1.0 [76, 77]. Chromosomes were aligned using minimap2 allowing for 10% sequence divergence. Alignments were filtered for results greater than 500 kbp in length and those > 500kbp were plotted per Circos 0.69-9 instructions (https://circos.ca/documentation/tutorials).

Phylogenetic tree

The phylogenetic tree was generated from BUSCO genes using methods outlined in https://github.com/mcmurtrs/Making-a-Phylogenetic-Tree-with-BUSCO-Genes. BUSCO results were generated for the twelve salmonid species listed above using gVolante [78]. Single-copy BUSCO sequences were filtered and concatenated to generate a single sequence for each species. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.490 [79] with default parameters and a phylogenetic tree was constructed with CIPRES: science gateway [80] using RAxML-HPC2 on ACCESS v. 8.2.12 [81]. The two Coregonus species and T. thymallus were set as outgroups. Bootstrap values were set for 1000 iterations with a random bootstrap seed [See Supplementary Fig. 1 in Additional file 1].

Gene synteny analysis

Candidate genes likely involved with immunity and stress responses were identified within the Gila Trout annotated genome using IGV 2.15.2 desktop application [82, 83] and within other salmonid species (listed above) via the NCBI database. Regions of the genome containing genes of interest were selected, downloaded, and annotated in GenePalette 2.1.2 [84]. Genomic regions were aligned using MAFFT with preset parameters and trimmed in MEGA11 [85] to contain only the untranslated regions (UTRs), exons, and introns. The seqvisr package 0.2.7 [86] in R 4.4.0 was then used to visualize regions of match and mismatch between Gila Trout genes and the other salmonid sequences. ORFfinder [87] was used to artificially translate the identified exons into proteins to verify the genes potential functionality. Protein sequences were further aligned against other salmonid species to identify point mutations and other variation (e.g., insertions/deletions). Protein folding dynamics were analyzed by AlphaFold [88] and visualized in The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 3.0 Schrödinger, LLC. Additional sequences for Rainbow Trout DAA and DAB genes were downloaded from The International Immunogenetics Information System (IMGT) [89]. Sequence alignments were created using http://www.bioinformatics.org/SMS/multi_align.html. CD4-1 sequence from C. clupeaformis had to be concatenated by hand from unplaced scaffolds scaf0356 (NW_025533811.1) and scaf1211 (NW_025534665).

Results

Contig assembly

Initial hifiasm assembly results, using a haplotype of 2, generated a genome assembly that was shorter than expected based on comparison to other salmonid genomes (Gila Trout = 1.73 Gb).

The hifiasm settings were subsequently modified to set the number of haplotypes to 4 given the presence of a full genome duplication event in the evolutionary history of the salmonid lineage [50–54]. This modification resulted in the expected genome length for salmonids which is often > 2.0 Gb [53, 90–92]. The final genome assembly for Gila Trout consisted of 2,047 contigs which covered a total length of 2.28 Gb with an N50 of 2,810,852 bp with the longest contig being 16,173,358 bp (Table 1) based on this updated genome size coverage was re-calculated at 27.2 X.

Scaffold assembly

Initial scaffold assemblies resulted in the edges of chromosome arms concatenated together (Fig. 2A). To address this issue, the final Juicer assembly was aligned against the Rainbow Trout Arlee genome and the output alignment files were then used to identify the edges of the Gila Trout chromosomes in reference to both the Arlee genome and the Juicebox heatmap (Fig. 2B). Chromosomes that required manual separation after initial processing in Juicebox were as follows (1) Og1 and Og23, (2) Og12 and Og17 (3) Og2 and Og3 (4) Og18 and Og07 (5) Og26 and Og06, (6) Og15 and Og21 (7) Og22 and Og28 (8) Og24 and Og 27 [See Supplemental Fig. 2 in Additional file 1]. The necessity for manual separation may be a residual biproduct of the tetraploidy observed in salmonids, something that is partially supported by data from Rainbow trout showing homologous blocks between rainbow trout chromosomes, 6 out of 8 of which match to the chromosomes that required manual separation [93]. This resulted in a final chromosome count of 28 for the Gila Trout genome. This value falls within the range observed in a cytological [90] study that indicated 2n = 56, on average, for Gila Trout.

Chromosomes

We found that the assembled Gila Trout genome consisted of a 2n of 48 metacentric chromosomes and 8 telocentric/acrocentric chromosomes (Fig. 3A-B). Chromosomes with notable differences between in the Gila Trout and the Rainbow Trout Arlee genomes are Og4, Og14, Og23, and Og25. This is due to their homology to Rainbow Trout, chromosomes Omy4, Omy14, and Omy25 which are divided into six acrocentric chromosomes in the Arlee strain but are maintained as metacentric chromosomes in the Swanson strain [92]. In the Gila Trout genome these chromosomes are in metacentric form consistent with the Swanson isolates [see Supplemental Fig. 4 in Additional file 1]. Chromosome configuration of Og23 seems to be unique to Gila Trout (Fig. 3B, [see Supplemental Fig. 4 in Additional file 1]). Og23 is metacentric with its two chromosomal arms corresponding to Omy23 and Omy28 in both the Arlee and Swanson Rainbow trout strains (Fig. 1B, [see Supplemental Fig. 5 in Additional file 1]).

Additional chromosomes of interest are Og5, Og20, and Og28 due to their structural variation. In Rainbow Trout Chromosome 5 contains two large inversions around the centromere that partly determine migratory and sex-specific life history phenotypes [91, 94–96]. These rearrangements are either ancestral/anadromous (A) or rearranged/resident (R) [91]. Rainbow Trout Arlee genome contains an (A) Omy05 while the Swanson genome contains the (R) version of this chromosome [92]. Alignments of Og05 against Omy05 from Swanson and Arlee isolates indicate that Gila Trout has the A karyotype (Fig. 4A).

Another well-known polymorphic inversion is located on Omy20 and spans an area of about 14.0 Mb [97]. Rainbow Trout Arlee and Swanson genomes have the rearranged karyotype Omy20. Alignment of Og20 against the Arlee and Swanson Omy20 and Atlantic salmon ssa28 indicate that assembled Gila Trout genome has the ancestral form of chromosome 20 with no inversion (Fig. 4B) indicating that Gila trout have both ancestral versions of these known chromosomal inversions and while these inversions are polymorphic in Rainbow trout, they may not necessarily be so in Gila trout.

Lastly Og28, that maps to Omy29/Y on Rainbow Trout, is known to contain an important sex-determining gene within the Rainbow Trout genome, sdY, within the OmyY1 region of Omy29/Y [98, 99]. Yet, although Omy29/Y is homologues to Og28, this gene was not found in Gila Trout Og28. However, a partial sdY (only 7% coverage within the 3’ UTR) was identified through alignment against Rainbow trout sdY on Og03. Given that the translocation of sdY across chromosomes is known in salmonids this may be one potential location of this gene in Gila trout [99] The partial nature of this gene could indicate it is simply non-functional or a by-product of using more than one Gila Trout of unknown sex for this assembly. Additionally, large inversions were identified between the Arlee and Gila Trout genomes on Og13, Og17, Og19, Og21, and Og26. Smaller inversions were observed in other chromosomes such as Og09, Og10, Og12, Og16, Og22, Og23, and Og28, most of which seemed to be located at telomeric edges [ see Supplemental Fig. 3 in Additional file 1].

Repeats

Nearly 55% of the Gila Trout genome consisted of repeated DNA sequences, similar to values observed in Rainbow Trout and Atlantic Salmon genomes (53.17% and 53.65%, respectively) (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 1). In all three genomes, the greatest proportions of repetitive sequences were retroelements (36–37%), mostly LINE repeats of the L2/CR1/Rex superfamily (Supplementary Table 1). Gila Trout has a lower percentage of repeats in the L2/CR1/Rex superfamily (15.82%) and a larger percentage in the L1/CIN4 (3.54%) superfamily when compared with Rainbow Trout (2.49%) and Atlantic Salmon (1.94%). DNA repeats, small repetitive nucleotides, made up approximately 16–17% of the genomes of all species examined with the greatest percentage coming from the Tc1-IS630-Pogo super family. Other repeated elements included a small percentage (2–3%) of small RNAs, satellites, simple repeats, and regions of low complexity.

Gene annotation

Annotation resulted in about 92% of features found in the Rainbow Trout genome mapped on to the Gila Trout genome. Of those mapped features, about 2% were labeled as genes, 49% were exons, 4% were RNA and about 45% were coding sequences (CDS) (Table 2). Additionally, 0.4% were identified as being variants within the Gila Trout genome. These variants consisted mostly of synonymous and nonsynonymous substitutions, some of which encoded stop codons (Table 2).

Genes of interest

Genes of the innate and adaptive immune system were selected for further analysis given their importance for an individual’s response to pathogens and infection along with the likelihood of containing greater levels of genetic variation. Among adaptive immune genes, we selected genes encoding the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II genes, CD4 genes, and CD40 (Fig. 6A). MHC II was of particular interest as it is known to be maintained in populations at high levels of allelic diversity [42, 100]. However, in Gila Trout, variation at MHC II (DAB locus) was severely reduced in three out of four lineages [30]. We found that two MHC II genes, known as DAA and DAB, were located on Og17 within a ~ 9-kb region. Both were positioned in the same orientation with an intergenic region ~ 1.8 kb between them. Both genes consisted of four exons and three introns as previously described in salmonids [101], and both were in frame.

An artificial translation of the sequences generated a 235-amino acids (aa) protein for DAA that shared high sequence similarity to DAA proteins identified in Rainbow Trout. We identified an average of 21 aa differences between Gila Trout and Rainbow Trout DAA sequences that centered around aa positions 82–95 [see Supplemental Fig. 6A in Additional file 1].

Translation of DAB generated a protein of 244 aa with an average aa difference of 15.3 aa with respect to Rainbow Trout DAB. Sequence comparisons with other salmonids revealed high levels of sequence conservation within the coding region of DAA and DAB genes with more variation, especially in intron size, in the non-coding regions of these genes (Fig. 6B, C).

CD4 genes were examined as they play an important role in antigen presentation, immune activation, and are co-receptor to MHC class II to stimulate immune activity in T cells [102] (Fig. 6A). Salmonid genomes contain two or three CD4 genes [102–110]. We identified two CD4 genes, CD4-2.2 and CD4-1, in the Gila Trout genome located on Og03. CD4-2.2 was located upstream of CD4-1. CD4-2.2 was approximately 6.6 kb in size and was separated from CD4-1 by an intergenic region of 1.6 kb. CD4-1 was 23 kb in size, however the coding region only spanned 4.5 kb of that length. Both genes appeared to be in frame and artificial translation indicated that Gila Trout CD4-2.2 is 323 aa long and only has one aa difference at aa 208 (Y208D) compared to Rainbow Trout CD4-2.2. Translation of CD4-1 sequence generated a protein that was 489 aa long and contained only one aa change (A489V) relative to Rainbow Trout CD4-1. Comparison of these sequences against other salmonid species indicated similar results to DAA and DAB with coding regions showing higher sequence conservation than non-coding regions (Fig. 6D, E; [see Supplemental Fig. 6C in Additional file 1]).

We next focused on CD40 as a previous study found high heterozygosity (> 0.70) for this gene in all Gila Trout lineages [111–117]CD40 and its ligand CD40L (Fig. 6A) were found on Og16 and Og25, respectively. CD40 was ~ 19 kbp and consisted of 9 exons. Artificial translation produced a protein that was 330 aa long and contained two aa changes adjacent to each other at positions 192 and 193 and a single aa deletion at 220 compared to the Rainbow Trout sequence. CD40L was 3.4 kb in size consisting of five exons which generated a 264-aa protein sequence that was identical to the Rainbow Trout CD40L [see Supplemental Fig. 6B in Additional file 1]. Nucleotide sequence alignment of CD40 revealed high variability within the first intron between salmonid species with the Gila Trout sequence being 14.8 bp in size. The coding regions, on the other hand, were conserved across species (Fig. 6F). Combined, our analyses focused on selected genes related to adaptive immunity revealed a high degree of conservation between Gila Trout and Rainbow Trout at the sequence level.

Heat shock protein 12 A (hspa12a) was previously identified as having high observed heterozygosity in Gila Trout [111]. In Gila Trout hspa12a was found on Og23 and consisted of 11 exons over a ~ 45 kb region. Artificial translation generated a 659-aa protein that was identical to the Hspa12a found in Rainbow Trout. Comparison to other salmonid species revealed sequence variation within the first intron, however most of that variability was due to the C. clupeaformis hspa12a sequence and was lost when that sequence was removed (Fig. 7A).

Lastly, nucleoporin Nup42, also known as Rip1p, NLP1 or NUPL2, was previously found to have high observed heterozygosity in Gila Trout [111]. The Nup42 gene was located on Og14. It consisted of 7 exons distributed over a span of about 50 kb (Fig. 7B). Comparisons with other salmonids revealed a large region of variability present from aa 102 to 146 of exon 2 (Fig. 7C-D, [see Su pplemental Fig. 6D in Additional file 1]). Variability was mostly due to a glycine rich region within the gene encoded by variable number of GGA repeats. Gila Trout had nine of these repeats (Fig. 7E) in an intrinsically disordered region of the protein known to be involved in the permeability barrier of the nucleoporin complex (Fig. 7E, F).

Discussion

Here we present the first draft of an annotated Gila Trout genome. We identified and annotated 28 chromosomes highlighting large structural variations and genes of interest within the genome of this threatened North American Trout species in relation to Rainbow Trout. Chromosome number within Salmonidae (bony fish family containing trout, salmon, whitefish, char, etc.) is highly variable, even within species. Chromosome arms seem to be subject to breakage at the centromere [63], most evidently in Rainbow Trout genomes where different strains (e.g., Arlee and Swanson) have variable numbers of chromosomes [54, 92]. Nonetheless, the number of chromosome arms across Rainbow Trout strains is always 104. In the Gila Trout genome assembly, chromosome number we observed matched the number reported from karyotypes presented by Beamish and Miller [119], but chromosome arm numbers do not match. While Beamish and Miller found a total of 105 chromosome arms distributed over 49 metacentric and 7 telocentric chromosomes, this genome assembly identified 24 (2n = 48) metacentric and four (2n = 8) telocentric chromosomes resulting in 54 (2n = 104) chromosomal arms, one less than expected for this species and an identical number to Rainbow Trout. It is possible that there is intraspecific variation in chromosome arm number in Gila Trout. Alternatively, there may be a possible error in interpretation of chromosome counts performed by Beamish and Miller. While one would expect that 2n values would be divisible by 2, they found odd numbers in their counts. However, odd numbers of chromosome arms are common in salmonid species [120]. Since our genome assembly was produced using Spruce Creek lineage Gila Trout whereas Beamish and Miller examined Main Diamond lineage fish it is also possible that chromosomal arm numbers vary across Gila Trout lineages. Future studies will resolve this unresolved question.

Chromosomal architecture was clearly different between Gila Trout and Rainbow Trout genomes. Structural variation across species is not uncommon in salmonids. In Gila Trout, Og23 is metacentric with one arm aligning to Omy23 and the other to Omy28. This specific chromosomal combination is unique to Gila Trout. It is worth noting that a region of Omy28 contains genes GREB1L and ROCK1, both found in proximity on Og23, that are likely involved in migration timing in Steelhead (the anadromous or sea-run form of O. mykiss) [121]. Additionally, the Gila Trout genome was found to contain the ancestral versions of chromosomes 5 and 20. These chromosome inversions are interesting because they are linked to life history variation in anadromous Steelhead and resident Rainbow Trout populations [91, 122]. In Rainbow Trout, chromosome 5 and 20 inversions likely occurred within the last ~ 1.61 and ~ 1.20 MYA respectively [97]. Gila Trout diverged from Rainbow Trout about 1 to 2 million years ago [28, 46]. Coincidence of this timing suggests that migration and subsequent land-locking of Gila Trout populations occurred either before or during the appearance of the chromosomal inversions in other lineages with no current data suggesting that Gila Trout populations are polymorphic for these inversions [97]. Additional studies of chromosome 20 indicate that Rainbow Trout have low frequencies of the ancestral karyotype except in anadromous and resident populations in the southern portion of their geographical range [96, 97]. Thus, retention of the ancestral form of the inversion in Gila Trout reflects this southerly distribution in Rainbow Trout [97]. Lastly, it is worth noting that these and other identified chromosomal variations between Gila Trout and Rainbow Trout may or may not be polymorphic across the Gila Trout lineages and only further sequencing data will elucidate this possibility.

There is a growing importance of high quality, species specific, genomes to aid in conservation efforts [123, 124]. Regarding Gila Trout conservation, SNPs and microsatellites have been extensively used to characterize population diversity, effective population size and population structure [30–32]. Genomic-enabled analyses enhance predictive power for extinction risk and adaptability to future climate conditions by increasing precision and accuracy of the metrics above, and permitting more detailed insights into the genomic architecture of inbreeding depression and other factors that affect species persistence [125]. Immune genes are of particular interest when it comes to genomic conservation given their importance in individual and species survival in response to infection. They are often encoded in small regions of high genetic diversity due to their peptide-binding compatibility [33–39]. The peptide-binding sites of immune genes dictate pathogen binding and recognition. Well-known examples include the genes of the MHC II, some of which have been shown to be linked to continued survival of inbred populations [26, 44, 45]. In this paper, we annotated seven immune and immune-related genes, three of which already have closely linked candidate SNPs for genotyping of Gila Trout lineages [111].

MHC II, encoded in the DAA and DAB genes in salmonids, contains variable sites necessary for binding a large variety of peptides. Loss of variability in MHC II genes at a population level can be detrimental (reviewed in [100]). More broadly, teleost MHC II has shown high genomic volatility and plasticity with multiple MHC II-like molecules being found within teleost genomes [126]. In other cases, such as gadoid fishes, there is complete loss of MHC II genes [127–131]. While the two genes that make up the classical MHC II in the Gila Trout genome were annotated and characterized here, previous sequencing of the DAB exon two from all Gila Trout lineages showed that diversity has been eroded at these loci across populations [30]. Loss of diversity can severely limit the capacity of Gila Trout to recognize a range of pathogens and could lead to a higher risk of disease outbreak within populations, highlighting a greater need to track these genes within the population over time.

It is also important to consider variability in genes that interact directly with MHC II, such as CD4 and CD40 genes. CD4 genes play an important role in antigen presentation, immune activation, and are a co-receptor to MHC class. They stimulate immune activity in T cells with CD4+ T expressing CD40 lymphocytes proliferating and increasing expression of cytokines in response to immune stimulation and CD4-1+ myeloid cells (macrophages) having phagocytic activities [102–110].Additionally, SNPs that are closely linked to CD40 exhibit high observed heterozygosity across lineages [111]. Given that this gene is involved in the development and maturation of key immune cells, including B-cell activation [112] variability in CD40 may be important to the Gila Trout immune response. Future studies should annotate the diversity of variable (V) gene segments for both T and B cell receptors in Gila Trout, an endeavor that may reveal loss of repertoire diversity or the opposite, as a compensatory mechanism. The present study did not closely investigate many other critical genes in adaptive immunity such and V, D, J genes that get somatically recombined to form B and T cell receptors. Such annotation efforts will be invaluable when trying to understand the specific immune responses to different pathogens encountered by Gila Trout in their natural environment.

For the other genes, a cursory search of the repetitive regions identified in the Gila Trout genome revealed that all have repetitive sequence regions in proximity or within the genes themselves. For example, Nup42 has high observed heterozygosity [28] and contains a glycine rich region within its second exon that is highly variable across salmonid species. While little is known about the function of Nup42/NLP1 in vertebrates, in yeast it appears to act in conjunction with Gle1 and Dbp4 in the exportation of messenger ribonucleoproteins [132] and CRM-1 dependent nuclear export [133] from the nucleus into the cytoplasm. Nup42 also seems to be associated with the export of heat shock proteins in the Hsp70 family in yeast cells [134, 135]. This region is located on an intrinsically disordered part of the protein that extends into the permeability barrier of the nucleoporin complex. Length variability of this glycine-rich region potentially facilitates selective passage of proteins and RNA through the nucleus and into the cytoplasm. This variability, if present across Gila Trout lineages, could potentially be a target for conservation assays [28].

Higher than expected heterozygosity observed in genomic regions near Hspa12a and Nup42 may be similarly important to the Gila Trout stress response. Hspa12a is a heat shock protein in the Hsp70 family which is involved in the response to heat stress in salmonids. This protein mediates correct folding and facilitates refolding of proteins in stressful conditions [136–142]. Hspa12a data is limited, but it is worth noting that Hspa12a is thought to be distant members of the Hsp70 family, with an atypical but functional ATP-binding domain, to only have two of ten expected motifs conserved, and an atypical N-terminal nucleotide binding domain [139, 143, 144]. While hsp70 is known to take part in several cellular functions, hspa12a might have a novel function in salmonids among the hsp70 genes. For example, hspa12a may function as an adaptor protein as has been observed in human cell lines [144]. Nup42 is also of interest then because of its association with Hsp70 transport in yeast cells [134, 135]. While we can only speculate on an association we highlight the possibility that Hsp70 family of genes play a role in Gila Trout stress response. Across genomes, we identified differences in glycine-rich repeats in nucleoproteins, differences that may determine their function as selective barriers for proteins into and out of the nucleus [118] While the exact reason for this variation is unknown, the presence of this variability and the maintained heterozygosity of these genes in the face of strong genetic drift may indicate that continued monitoring of these genes could be advantageous.

Conclusions

Recent studies suggest that loss of heterozygosity at a small set of neutrally-evolving genetic loci may not provide a complete assessment of extinction risk [16, 17]. Heterozygosity at specific sites within the genome provides more detailed insight into risks of loss of genetic diversity to species persistence by accounting more fully for effects of natural selection. For example, a handful of studies have shown that heterozygosity at key immune genes, such as MHC II could be more predictive of long-term persistence in the wild [26, 44, 45]. This first draft of the Gila Trout genome, along with an in-depth analysis of seven key immune and immune-related genes, three of which have SNPs with high observed heterozygosity provides some insight into gene variability and function in this endangered salmonid. While these SNPs alone are unlikely to give a full picture of the extinction risk of the Gila Trout, understanding their links to functional responses (i.e. stress or infection) may shed light on the continued robustness of this species on the landscape, and also provide key genetic targets to maintain at high levels of heterozygosity in hatchery breeding programs.
