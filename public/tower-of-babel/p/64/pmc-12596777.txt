Prevalence of and risk factors for depression, anxiety, and burnout in U.S. neurosurgery residents
Authors: Sherwin Andrew Tavakol, Samia Nawaz, Nangorgo Jean Oumar Coulibaly, Randaline Barnett, Miri Kim, Radmehr Torabi, Andrew Jea, Hakeem J. Shakir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: There are no published data on what drives depression/anxiety in U.S. neurosurgery residents. We aimed to characterize the prevalence of and risk/protective factors for depression, anxiety, and burnout in this population.Methods: A cross-sectional study analyzing responses to a 28-question survey (including patient health questionnaire-9 [PHQ-9] and generalized anxiety disorder-7 [GAD-7] screening questions) from March to May 2023 distributed to U.S. neurosurgery residents. PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores >5 defined at least mild depression/anxiety, respectively.Results: One hundred responses were included. Forty-six percent screened positive for at least mild depression, 41% for at least mild anxiety, and 45% reported feeling burnout. Strikingly, 10% of residents endorsed thoughts of self-harm/suicide. On multivariate analysis, risk factors for mild depression included female gender (OR = 4.3; P = 0.005), junior residency (OR = 3.1; P = 0.015), and being on a neurosurgical service (OR = 4.6; P = 0.01). The only risk factor for mild anxiety was female gender (OR = 3.3; P = 0.031), while being married (OR = 0.2; P = 0.006), protected didactic time (OR = 0.2; P = 0.003), and department wellness initiatives (OR = 0.3; P = 0.031) were protective factors. Finally, being on a neurosurgical service was a risk factor for burnout (OR 2.5; P = 0.032), while department-sponsored wellness initiatives were protective (OR = 0.2; P = 0.001).Conclusion: Nearly half of the participants screened positive for depression, anxiety, and burnout, while 10% recently had suicidal thoughts. Overall, female gender, being on a neurosurgical service, and junior residency were risk factors associated with depression, anxiety, and/or burnout. By contrast, department-sponsored wellness, being married, and protected didactic time were protective against one or multiple of these mental health disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Entering a U.S. neurosurgical training program has become highly competitive, and, compared to other specialties, a disproportionally high number of residents leave before completing their training.[2,17,26,34] Those that do continue on oftentimes do so at the expense of varying degrees of financial, social, familial, and, unfortunately, mental health sacrifice.[5,7,14,16,22,24,38] In some reports, as high as two-thirds of neurosurgical residents feel burnout, and over 80% show signs of imposter syndrome.[9,38] The impact of this is not only felt by the resident but also by their patients. Some studies have shown that depressed physicians make up to 6 times the number of errors as compared with their healthy counterparts.[15,19]

Meta-analyses done assessing all-comer residents in the U.S. and abroad suggest that as a whole, the prevalence of depressive symptoms among residents is close to 30% irrespective of surgical versus non-surgical specialty or domestic versus international location of practice.[25] However, in neurosurgery, there is no published data on what is actually driving depression or anxiety in the first place. Our study presents the only published data specifically on the prevalence of and risk factors for depression and anxiety in U.S. neurosurgical trainees using the validated patient health questionnaire (PHQ)-9 and generalized anxiety disorder (GAD)-7 screening surveys and contributes to the growing body of literature regarding neurosurgical resident burnout.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data collection

A cross-sectional observational study was employed by creating a questionnaire using Google Forms consisting of 28 questions, including demographic/program-specific information, PHQ-9 and GAD-7 surveys, and personal feelings of burnout [Supplemental Online Content 1]. No identifying information was collected. This questionnaire was sent out to all U.S. neurosurgical residency programs for whom the current contact information of leadership/administrators was publicly available. Responses were collected from current U.S. neurosurgical residents from March 2023 to May 2023. Approval from our institution’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) was exempt, given the de-identified nature of the study.

RESULTS

Of the 1,579 U.S. neurosurgical residents reported by the Association of American Medical Colleges during the 2022–2023 academic year, 100 residents responded to our survey. All of the respondents answered all the questions, and all participants’ responses were included in the analysis [Figure 2]. Respondents were composed of 34% females, 57% junior residents, 68% married individuals, 77% residents currently on a neurosurgical service (as opposed to on leave, on a research rotation, or on a non-neurosurgical rotation), 20% individuals doing residency in their home state or in the state where they pursued their medical school training, 93% residents at a program that participated in a 24-h call system (as opposed to a night float system), 79% residents who were at a program that guaranteed a protected post-call day, 70% of residents at a program that had protected weekly didactic time, and 61% of residents at a program that supported and funded wellness initiatives. The proportions of female and junior residents were in line with the reported national averages in 2022.[13]

Flowchart depicting the breakdown of the total cohort of U.S. neurosurgery during the year of the study period and the final response rate/number of residents included in the analysis.

Of the 100 respondents, 46% screened positive for at least mild depression, 41% screened positive for at least mild anxiety, and 45% reported feeling burnout. Strikingly, 10% of residents felt better off dead or endorsed thoughts of self-harm. Depressed residents were also very likely to screen positive for anxiety (P < 0.001). Of the residents who responded affirmatively to one or more of the PHQ-9 questions, 52% felt this made their work/home/personal lives more difficult [Table 1]. Of the residents that responded affirmatively to one or more of the GAD-7 questions, 57% felt that it made their day-to-day lives more difficult [Table 2].

Unadjusted for other factors, the positive predictors of screening positive for at least mild depression in our cohort were female gender (P = 0.008), junior residency (P = 0.006), and being on a neurosurgical rotation (P = 0.029). The only protective factor against screening positive for depression was having department-sponsored wellness initiatives (P = 0.013). Univariate analysis of the GAD-7 results showed that while there were only two risk factors for screening positive for at least mild anxiety in our cohort, female gender (P = 0.003) and junior residency (P = 0.021), there were three protective factors: Being married (P = 0.003), protected didactic time (P = 0.003), and department-sponsored wellness initiatives (P = 0.001). Moreover, females (P = 0.017) and those currently on a neurosurgical rotation (P = 0.011) were more likely to feel burnout, while those with department-sponsored wellness initiatives were less likely (P = 0.001) [Table 4].

When a multivariate regression model was created to determine variables influencing screening positive for at least mild depression, female gender (OR = 4.3; P = 0.005), junior residency (OR = 3.1; P = 0.015), and being on a neurosurgical service (OR = 4.6; P = 0.010), all continued to be risk factors. The multivariate model revealed females had a 3.3-fold increase in the odds of screening positive for at least mild anxiety compared to males (P = 0.031), while being married (OR = 0.2; P = 0.006), protected didactic time (OR = 0.2; P = 0.003), and department wellness initiatives (OR = 0.3; P = 0.031) were protective. With regard to the burnout regression model, residents on a neurosurgical service had a 3.5-fold increase in the odds of feeling burnout compared to residents not on a neurosurgery service (P = 0.032), while department-sponsored wellness initiatives were protective (OR = 0.2; P = 0.001) [Table 3]. The ROC curves for the multivariate models showed AUCs of 0.756 for the depression model (P < 0.001), 0.812 for the anxiety model (P < 0.001), and 0.719 for the burnout model (P < 0.001) [Figure 1].

DISCUSSION

Key findings

Our study provides unique insight into the prevalence of depression and anxiety in U.S. neurosurgical trainees using validated PHQ-9 and GAD-7 screening tools. It also contributes to the growing body of literature surrounding resident burnout. In our cohort of 100 current U.S. neurosurgical residents, 46% screened positive for at least mild depression, 41% screened positive for at least mild anxiety, and 45% reported feeling burnout. Over half of residents who responded affirmatively to one or more of the screening questions felt as though this made it harder for them to work, care for things at home, and/or get along with other people. Alarmingly, 10% of residents had thoughts of self-harm or suicide in the last few weeks, and 4% were having these thoughts nearly every day. Risk factors for screening positive for these mental health disorders included female gender, junior residency, and being on a neurosurgical service. Protective factors included being married, having protected didactic time, and department-sponsored wellness initiatives.

Depression in neurosurgical trainees

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services reported that in 2021, approximately 21 million American adults (8.3% of all American adults) suffered from at least one major depressive episode. This number was higher in females (10.3%) and among those aged 18–25 (18.6%).[12] In the same year, an Australian group published a review in The Lancet pertaining to mental health and suicide in physicians, noting that comparing rates of depression among doctors to that of the general population may not be the most pragmatic approach given that many occupational studies portray a higher rate of mental health disease, possibly due to participants over-reporting symptoms to reflect their job dissatisfaction rather than a true mental illness more generally. To that effect, a survey of over 12,000 Australian doctors with a 27.2% prevalence of depression and anxiety was compared with a meta-analysis of 65 occupational reports in the United Kingdom that yielded a 29.6% prevalence of depression, suggesting that physician depression is more common than depression in the general population, but comparable to that of other occupational populations.[12,20]

Moreover, given that the prevalence of depression is higher in younger individuals, it is conceivable that this would also hold true or even be amplified in physicians during residency. However, a landmark meta-analysis and systematic review of over 17,000 residents from across 18 countries was published in JAMA in 2015 showing that clinically significant depressive symptoms occurred in 28.8% of residents without significant differences between U.S. and other countries or between surgical and nonsurgical specialties.[25] It is for this reason that our finding that nearly half of U.S. neurosurgery residents screened positive for depression should prompt concern and instigate further inquiry.

It is known that neurosurgical residency is a grueling 7-year process that is preceded by a highly demanding and selective application process, as evidenced by the 51.7% overall match rate.[26] Neurosurgical trainees are often involved in the care of some of the sickest patients in the hospital who frequently suffer from incurable disease or devastating trauma. Pre-dawn start time, late evening shifts, and onerous call schedules can conceivably lead to strain on one’s personal/family life[5,14,18,20,25] and likely contribute to the high levels of attrition in neurosurgical training programs.[2] These realities are in line with our finding that neurosurgery residents on a neurosurgical service are more likely to screen positive for depression than those on another specialty service line or on research time, when clinical and call demands may be less burdensome. This challenges the notion that neurosurgery attracts high-achieving individuals who are inherently prone to developing mental health disorders, which given that these same residents are not as likely to be depressed when removed from the demanding neurosurgical environment.

Junior residents were also at particular risk of screening positive for depression, which may be attributable to the stark transition between being in medical school and serving as a new physician in an intense surgical training program, adjusting to the hierarchical structure innate in academic medicine, and the realities of being the funnel through which most new questions, updates, emergencies, and consults filter.[2,33,37,38]

Finally, female neurosurgery residents face unique difficulties that may contribute to the increased risk of depression that we found. Many studies have shown that good mentorship and role models are protective against mental health disorders.[7,24,34,32] However, in 2017, the prevalence of American female neurosurgeons was only 17.7%, whereas women physicians in general make up 33% of attending American doctors and 46% of American residents.[21] Between 2015 and 2019, female neurosurgeons or neurosurgery residents made up only 8.3% of first authors and 5.8% of last authors in the accepted abstracts at the six main American neurosurgery conferences.[21] Certainly, female residents can have male mentors, but the ability to envision the interplay between their future professional, academic, and personal lives benefits, in many cases, from having strong female role models.[8,34] Further, unfortunately, female neurosurgeons and neurosurgery residents are more likely to be victims of discrimination and abuse, most often at the hands of other neurosurgeons,[16] and even sexual harassment.[8]

One of the most jarring findings in our report is that 10% of the surveyed residents had thoughts of suicide or self-harm in the preceding few weeks. Comparatively, in a survey sent to Canadian neurosurgery residents, 4% reported considering suicide during their entire residency.[20] While general mortality rates are lower in physicians than the general population, suicide rates are notably higher in physicians and particularly high in female physicians.[19] In fact, suicide is the number one cause of death among male residents.[36] A pivotal report in 2003 noted that one physician dies in the U.S. per day by suicide, highlighting this as a national public health crisis.[11] The observed finding that 1 in 10 neurosurgery residents has recently considered self-harm should prompt further research into stressors and preventative factors, especially given that our study did not find any protective factors against depression. Experiences like these unquestionably would increase the likelihood that a female neurosurgical trainee would feel at least mild depression.[11]

Anxiety in neurosurgical trainees

There are not many studies that have looked at anxiety levels among physicians at large, but the few published cross-sectional reports on this suggest the prevalence of GAD can be as high as 24%.[19,27] The only current paper that surveyed U.S. neurosurgical residents using the GAD-7 questionnaire was a report by Spiotta et al., in which a new wellness program was implemented at a single residency program and average anxiety levels were assessed before and after.[9,31] However, the number of individual residents who had a result >7 (mild anxiety) was not specified, and both their pre- and post-intervention average values were <7 (no anxiety).[23]

The only significant risk factor for screening positive for at least mild anxiety in our cohort was female gender. As discussed previously, female doctors and neurosurgical residents in particular do have higher rates of depression and attrition;[2,12,34] however, this is the first time that gender is reported as a risk factor for anxiety in this population.

While no protective factors were uncovered for depression, we did find that there were three factors protecting against mild anxiety: marriage, having protected didactic time, and having a department-sponsored wellness initiative. Interestingly, in a survey sent to the congress of neurological surgeons, female neurosurgeons felt significantly less fulfilled in their careers and less likely to choose neurosurgery as a career again as compared with their male counterparts. In this study and in another focused on members of the American College of Surgeons, female neurosurgeons were less likely to be married or to have children than their male colleagues.[10,17,29]

Protected didactic time is an important part of resident education. Lectures led by attending physicians illustrate to junior residents early on that the program values their learning and success. Especially for residents who are on pager duty, having protected time (i.e. without clinical responsibilities) for even a few hours per week can provide much needed relief in a slower paced environment to focus on one’s learning. In a similar vein, department-sponsored wellness initiatives can be effective in lowering stress/anxiety levels and instilling a sense of comraderies among trainees and attendings alike.[9]

Burnout in neurosurgical trainees

Much of the published literature assessing mental health disorders among U.S. neurosurgery residents has to do with burnout. Surveys have shown that the prevalence of burnout in this population is between 26% and 67%, which is consistent with our finding of 45%.[6,7,22,29,30] Measures of burnout vary, however. Many studies utilized the validated Maslach Burnout Inventory, which is a 22-question survey consisting of three sections that assess emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal achievement. Other reports used the abridged 9-question version of the survey, or, like in our study, simply asked the dichotomous question of “have you felt burnout in the last 2 weeks?” Each of these methods have been validated and have merit.

Interestingly, the only statistically significant risk factor for burnout that was found in our report was being on a neurosurgical service. Risk factors found in prior reports include inadequate operative exposure, hostile faculty, poor relationship with attending surgeons, social stressors outside of work, poor work-life balance, imbalance in duties, female gender, being a victim of work-related abuse, alterations in elective rotation/vacation schedule, lower PGY, being a recent trainee, and dissatisfaction in neurosurgery as a career choice.[7,9,10,16,22,24,33,39] A survey completed by neurosurgery residents’ significant others found that 18% of significant others considered divorce and 90% perceived that their resident partner suffered from moderate to severe burnout.[5]

The lowest reported prevalence of burnout is 26.1%, which was reported by Khalafallah et al. in a study assessing the impacts of the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic on U.S. neurosurgery residents.[22] They hypothesize that this may have been due to a high level of resilience seen in neurosurgery trainees during periods of high stress.[6] This is in line with other recent reports highlighting that when levels of grit and mindfulness are high in neurosurgical residents, burnout levels seem to be lower.[3,4,29,30] Other protective factors in the literature include meaningful mentorship, higher PGY, having children, feeling significant personal accomplishment, having less social/personal stressors, end of a rotation/year, increased sleep, participating in outdoor activities, meaningful relationships with colleagues, and prior team sport involvement.[7,22,24,28,29,32,33,37]

The only significant protective factor against resident burnout that we found was having a department-sponsored wellness initiative. All but one published report found that when a multi-focused wellness program was implemented in a neurosurgery department (i.e., centered around team-based exercise, mentorship programs, lectures on personal well-being, encouraging sleep hygiene, etc.), there was an improvement in anxiety, quality of life, sleepiness, camaraderie, and/or burnout.[1,6,9,14,24,31,33,35]

Limitations

While valuable, we recognize that our report does possess limitations that should not be overlooked. First, the cross-sectional study design represents a snapshot in time and may not be representative of all residents, or even the individual respondent’s views before or after the survey was completed. Relatedly, while survey studies are useful in that they are self-reported and provide direct insight into a participant’s thinking, responses are, by definition, subjective, and cannot be verified. This raises the possibility that some respondents may have answered questions negatively more as an outlet to vent frustrations with program-specific problems than as an honest reflection of their mental state. Moreover, there may have been an element of response/nonresponse bias at play with regard to which residents opted to fill out the survey. For example, a depressed resident may have been too burned out to allocate time to fill out the survey. On the other hand, residents suffering from mental health problems may have been more apt to respond to the survey to express their frustrations. This may have biased the results in either direction. In addition, the study size was limited to 100 participants (6.3% of U.S. neurosurgery residents), which could impact generalizability of the findings. While this could mean that the cohort is not representative of all U.S. neurosurgery residents, according to prior published data, the proportion of respondents was similar to the total population of U.S. neurosurgery residents that year. Certainly, the neurosurgical field would benefit from a follow-up survey study with a goal of achieving a higher response rate to verify and expand upon the findings laid out in this report. Finally, the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 are screening tools. Medical diagnoses of major depressive disorder and GAD should be made in conjunction with evaluation by a healthcare provider, which is why care is taken throughout this report to specify that the outcomes measured were screening positive for mild depression and mild anxiety.

CONCLUSION

We found that nearly half of trainees screened positive for at least mild depression/anxiety and endorsed feeling burnout. While there is close to no data in the published literature on the prevalence of depression/anxiety in this specific population, our finding of 45% prevalence of burnout is consistent with prior reports. Overall, female gender and currently being on a neurosurgical service were the most likely risk factors on multivariate analysis to be associated with screening positive for depression, anxiety, or burnout. Junior residency was also found to be a risk factor for screening positive for depression. By contrast, department-sponsored wellness initiatives were the most common protective factor. Being married and having protected didactic time were also important in preventing anxiety. Alarmingly, one in ten neurosurgery residents reported having suicidal ideations or thoughts of self-harm in the preceding few weeks, a finding that should prompt further investigation and analysis.
