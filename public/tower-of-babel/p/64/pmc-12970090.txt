Pulmonary function and airflow limitation in bronchiectasis: a case-control study of two independent cohorts
Authors: Da Som Jeon, Young Ju Jung, Soo Han Kim, Bumhee Yang, Byoung Soo Kwon, Sang Haak Lee, Hyun-Kyung Lee, Jae Seung Lee, Yeon-Mok Oh, Sei Won Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBronchiectasis is a respiratory disease structurally characterised by irreversible airway dilatation. Functional impairments are also implicated in bronchiectasis, but the detailed changes in pulmonary function and the impact of clinical factors are yet to be examined. We analysed pulmonary function in patients with bronchiectasis based on their clinical features.MethodsTwo study cohorts—a multicentre bronchiectasis registry and health check-up examinees—were analysed. Airflow limitation was defined as forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) <0.7, and bronchiectasis severity was categorised using the number of involved lobes.ResultsAmong 13 589 health check-up examinees, 606 (4.5%) had bronchiectasis; airflow limitation was more prevalent in those with bronchiectasis than in those without (17.3% vs 8.1%, p<0.001). Ever-smokers with bronchiectasis had the lowest FEV1, FEV1/FVC and FVC values, and the highest prevalence of airflow limitation. In the bronchiectasis registry (n=768), lung function parameters were worse in those with more involved lobes and Pseudomonas colonisation. Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that bronchiectasis was independently associated with airflow limitation (OR 2.22 (95% CI 1.75 to 2.82)).ConclusionsBronchiectasis is an independent risk factor for airflow limitation, and disease severity, smoking and Pseudomonas colonisation were each associated with worsening in pulmonary function.Keywords: Bronchiectasis

Introduction

Bronchiectasis is a chronic respiratory disease structurally characterised by abnormal, permanent dilatation of the bronchi and thickening of the airway wall,1 and its prevalence in the general population is increasing globally.2 3 Diagnosis is based on chest CT findings using an airway-to-artery diameter ratio of >1.5, lack of airway tapering and visibility of airways in the periphery,4 along with typical respiratory symptoms such as productive cough, shortness of breath, haemoptysis and recurrent respiratory infection.5 6 It is estimated that lung function impairment accompanies bronchiectasis; however, studies on lung function changes according to disease status are limited.

In general, spirometry in bronchiectasis often shows airflow limitation.5 In a prospective study, air trapping was the most common lung function abnormality, followed by reductions in the diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide, airflow limitation, hyperinflation and restrictive ventilatory defects.7

Because bronchiectasis is a heterogeneous disease, lung function can vary by clinical status and disease severity. Reportedly, while Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection in bronchiectasis was a marker of severity, it was not significantly associated with accelerated reduction of lung function.8 However, a recent prospective study showed discrepant results, in that infection caused by P. aeruginosa and previous exacerbation were associated with a faster decrease of forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) in patients with bronchiectasis.9 Regarding radiologic findings, reports show that FEV1 and forced vital capacity (FVC) values were lower in the cystic type than in the cylindric or varicose type, but studies on the association remain limited.10 Moreover, large-scale studies on the relationship between lung function and radiologic findings or disease severity in bronchiectasis have been limited.

In this study, we aimed to derive a higher level of evidence and integrated conclusions by analysing two study populations: the health check-up cohort and the bronchiectasis registry, rather than conducting the analysis based on a single cohort. We analysed the pulmonary function in patients with bronchiectasis based on clinical features such as disease severity, smoking history and sputum microbiology from two study groups, a multicentre bronchiectasis registry and a cohort of health check-up examinees who had undergone pulmonary function test (PFT) and chest CT.

Methods

Study design and participants

We analysed two independent cohorts: a prospective multicentre bronchiectasis registry (the Korean Multicenter Bronchiectasis Audit and Research Collaboration (KMBARC)) and a retrospective health check-up cohort from Asan Medical Center. These cohorts differed in purpose, source population and enrolment mechanisms, with no overlap between them.

KMBARC is a prospective, non-interventional observational cohort study conducted at 35 participating centres across Korea with enrolment between August 2018 and February 2021.11 Overall, 768 adults aged >18 years with bronchiectasis who had chest CT scans involving one or more lobes, regardless of respiratory symptoms, were included. Bronchiectasis due to cystic fibrosis, interstitial lung disease associated with bronchiectasis, active respiratory infection and pregnant patients were excluded. Spirometry and chest CT were performed locally at each participating centre. Pulmonary function testing was performed based on the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) statement.12 13 All parameters met the criteria for acceptability and repeatability as defined in the guidelines. Chest CT scans were obtained based on each institution’s diagnostic protocol and were interpreted by board-certified radiologists. Because CT scans were acquired across 35 institutions using heterogeneous scanners and acquisition protocols, detailed severity scores such as Bhalla, modified Reiff or BRICS could not be consistently applied. Therefore, radiologic extent was assessed using the number of lobes involved, which provides a reproducible measure across all available CT studies. This approach was intentionally chosen to prioritise reproducibility over granularity in a large multicentre registry, where heterogeneous CT acquisition protocols precluded the consistent application of validated CT scoring systems. Radiologic findings were assessed using standardised KMBARC definitions to ensure consistency across centres.

Of the 27 641 adults undergoing routine health screening at Asan Medical Center between 2016 and 2017, 13 589 individuals aged ≥18 years who received both chest CT and pulmonary function testing were included. Chest CT was not performed universally; rather, participants underwent the procedure because they voluntarily selected a health check-up package that included low-dose CT as an optional imaging component. Accordingly, CT use reflected participant choice within the screening programme rather than clinical indications. In the health check-up cohort, chest CT scans were obtained as part of routine screening using a standardised low-dose protocol to minimise radiation exposure during the check-up. Pulmonary function testing in the health check-up cohort comprised screening spirometry focused on flow-volume curves, performed following institutional procedures in accordance with ATS/ERS standards.13 14 Pre-bronchodilator spirometry results were used in the analysis, because post-bronchodilator spirometry was available only in the KMBARC registry cohort, and bronchodilator responses from KMBARC have been previously evaluated.15

Definitions

Bronchiectasis was defined as the presence of at least one of the following CT findings: (1) abnormal airway dilatation (inner bronchus/artery ratio >1.5), (2) lack of airway tapering and (3) airway visibility within 1 cm of the costal pleural surface or touching the mediastinal pleura.116,18 All CT findings were confirmed by radiology specialists. Respiratory symptoms included dyspnoea, cough, sputum production, wheeze and haemoptysis. In our study, ‘never smoker’ was defined as individuals who had smoked fewer than five packs (100 cigarettes) in their lifetime, while ‘ever-smoker’ was defined as those who had smoked 5 packs or more during their lifetime, regardless of current smoking status. We used the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scale to evaluate dyspnoea. Body mass index was calculated as a subject’s weight in kg divided by the square of their height in metres.12 Airflow limitation was defined as an FEV1/FVC ratio <0.7; bronchiectasis severity was categorised using the number of involved lobes.19rm Importantly, airflow limitation as defined in this study (pre-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC <0.70) should not be interpreted as equivalent to a formal diagnosis of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), particularly in the health check-up cohort where participants were largely asymptomatic and post-bronchodilator spirometry was not performed. For PFT interpretation, an obstructive pattern was defined as an FEV1/FVC ratio <0.7, and a restrictive pattern as an FVC reduction <80% of the predicted value estimated from the Korean population. The mixed pattern was defined using both criteria.20 We used the bronchiectasis severity index (BSI) and FEV1, Age, Chronic colonisation, Extension and Dyspnoea (FACED) score to assess bronchiectasis severity.21

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software V.24.0 (IBM Corp). Data are expressed as mean±SD. An independent student’s t-test was used as a parametric test to compare normally distributed data between the bronchiectasis and non-bronchiectasis groups, and the analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was used for comparisons among three or more groups. The χ2 or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables. Multiple logistic regression analyses were performed to identify the risk factors for airflow limitation. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Baseline characteristics

We categorised 768 patients with bronchiectasis registered in KMBARC and 13 589 health check-up participants into two groups based on bronchiectasis status, and analysed the baseline characteristics of each group. Among participants in the health check-up, bronchiectasis was defined as abnormal airway dilatation, absence of airway tapering and airway visibility near the pleura confirmed on CT scans conducted by a radiologist, as described in the methods section. The baseline characteristics, including underlying disease, respiratory symptoms, and body mass index, are described in table 1. Pulmonary function parameters are presented in figure 1. Among health check-up examinees, the mean age (62.1 vs 57.4 years, p<0.001) and the proportions of never smokers (49.2% vs 47.3%, p<0.001) were higher in the bronchiectasis group. All PFT parameters were significantly lower in the bronchiectasis group than in the non-bronchiectasis group (FEV1, 87.6 %/2.7 L vs 91.4 %/3.0 L of predicted value/recorded, p<0.001; FVC, 88.5 %/3.6 L vs 91.6 %/3.8 L of predicted value/recorded, p<0.001; FEV1/FVC, 76.1% vs 78.4%, p<0.001). Airflow limitation was more prevalent in the bronchiectasis group (17.3% vs 8.1%, p<0.001). Among those with bronchiectasis, 35.2% had respiratory symptoms, and 3.7% had dyspnoea of mMRC ≥2.

The mean age of the patients in the bronchiectasis registry was 66.3 years. The patients in the bronchiectasis registry had higher proportions of women (55.7%) and never smokers (65.9%) than the health check-up examinees did. Mean values of FEV1 (% of predicted), FVC (% of predicted) and FEV1/FVC were 64.1%, 73.1% and 65.4%, respectively. The proportion of patients with airflow limitation was 59.1%.

PFT according to clinical status

Among the health check-up examinees, the prevalence of obstructive pattern was higher in the bronchiectasis group than in the non-bronchiectasis group (17.3% (105/606) vs 8.1% (1048/12 983), p<0.001) (figure 1A). Furthermore, the parameters of PFT were lower in the bronchiectasis group than in the non-bronchiectasis group (FEV1/FVC ratio 76.08% vs 78.39%, p<0.001) (figure 1B). The prevalence of airflow limitation showed a linear relationship with smoking history (figure 1C). The PFT results were analysed by dividing into four groups based on smoking history (ever vs never smoker) and the presence of bronchiectasis (figure 2). The proportion of airflow limitation was lowest in the never-smoker/non-bronchiectasis group (3.8%) and highest in the ever-smoker/bronchiectasis group (23.9%; p<0.001). Regardless of the presence of bronchiectasis, the prevalence of airflow limitation was significantly higher in the ever-smoker groups than in the never-smoker groups (non-bronchiectasis, 11.9% vs 3.8%, p<0.001; bronchiectasis, 23.9% vs 11.6%, p<0.001; figure 2A). The prevalence of airflow limitation was not significantly different between the ever-smoker/non-bronchiectasis and the never-smoker/bronchiectasis groups (11.9% vs 11.6%, p=0.35; figure 2A).

FEV1/FVC ratio, FEV1 and FVC showed similar trends in the prevalence of airflow limitation across bronchiectasis status and smoking history. Particularly, FEV1/FVC was significantly lower in the bronchiectasis group regardless of smoking history (never-smoker/bronchiectasis vs never-smoker/non-bronchiectasis, 77.8% vs 80.0%, p<0.001; ever-smoker/bronchiectasis vs ever-smoker/non-bronchiectasis, 74.2% vs 76.9%, p<0.001; figure 2B).

PFT according to the severity of bronchiectasis

In the bronchiectasis registry, using chest CT scans, we analysed the lobes involved based on six regions: right upper lobe, right middle lobe, right lower lobe, left upper (lobe upper division), left upper lobe (lingular division) and left lower lobe. As the number of lobes involved in bronchiectasis and cystic bronchiectasis increased, FEV1, FVC and FEV1/FVC decreased (online supplemental figure 1A–C); specifically, FEV1/FVC was 70.1%, 68.8%, 66.4%, 61.3%, 61.8% and 60.4% when one, two, three, four, five and six lobes were involved, respectively (p<0.001). FEV1/FVC showed the same tendency when analysis was made using the lobes with cystic change (66.5%, 63.6%, 62.3%, 57.5%, 59.6% and 53.3% when one, two, three, four, five and six lobes were involved with cystic change, p<0.001; figure 1C).

Sputum cultures for P. aeruginosa were performed on 458 participants. A positive culture was defined as at least one isolation of P. aeruginosa from any respiratory specimen (spontaneous sputum, induced sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage) during the baseline evaluation. P. aeruginosa was detected in 102 patients (22.3%). Among the 458 patients in the bronchiectasis registry with sputum culture results, we analysed the PFT findings based on the isolation of P. aeruginosa from sputum. The average difference between the PFT results used in the analysis and the time of sample collection was approximately 2.1 months. FEV1 (1.49 L vs 1.76 L, p=0.001) and FVC (2.34 L vs 2.66 L, p=0.003) were significantly lower in those with P. aeruginosa colonisation than in those without, whereas FEV1/FVC did not show a significant difference according to P. aeruginosa colonisation (0.63 vs 0.66, p=0.095, online supplemental figure 2).

Additionally, we classified the 581 patients in the bronchiectasis registry with BSI and FACED score results into three groups: mild (BSI score 0–4; FACED score 0–2), moderate (BSI 5–8; FACED 3–4) or severe (BSI more than 9; FACED 5–7).21 Based on BSI, the FEV1/FVC ratio showed a significant decrease based on severity (65.27% in the mild, 66.16% in the moderate and 62.40% in the severe group, p=0.023). However, neither FEV1 (1.66 L in mild, 1.71 L in moderate and 1.61 L in severe group, p=0.326) nor FVC (2.53 L in mild, 2.60 L in moderate and 2.60 L in severe group, p=0.967) showed any significant difference among the groups (figure 3A). In the analysis based on FACED, it was confirmed that there were no significant differences in any of the pulmonary function measures based on severity (figure 3B).

Risk factors for airflow limitation

In a univariate logistic regression of the health check-up examinees, airflow limitation was significantly associated with bronchiectasis (OR 2.59; 95% CI 2.07 to 3.24), ever-smoking (OR 3.43; 95% CI 2.97 to 3.95), male sex (OR 4.40; 95% CI 3.72 to 5.22) and older age (41–60 years vs <40 years, OR 1.98; 95% CI 1.10 to 3.55). Multivariate logistic regression analysis adjusted for other confounding factors showed that airflow limitation was independently associated with all the aforementioned factors, including bronchiectasis (OR 2.22; 95% CI 1.75 to 2.82) (table 2).

Discussion

In this study, we examined lung function characteristics in relation to the presence of bronchiectasis and various clinical factors. The prevalence of airflow limitation, the most important feature of COPD, was not significantly different between never-smokers with bronchiectasis and ever-smokers without bronchiectasis, suggesting that bronchiectasis contributes as much as smoking to airflow limitation. Deterioration in lung function showed a linear relationship with the severity of bronchiectasis and smoking history, as well as the presence of P. aeruginosa colonisation. Bronchiectasis was an independent risk factor for airflow limitation after adjusting for age, sex and smoking.

Bronchiectasis is a chronic inflammatory disease that leads to a relatively rapid FEV1 decline.22 The annual decline rate is especially prominent in FEV1; in healthy never-smokers, the annual decline has been reported as 31.3 mL/year in men and 27.0 mL/year in women.23 Meanwhile, the annual decline rate of FEV1 was reported to be as high as 52.7 mL/year in patients with bronchiectasis and a smoking history of <10 pack-years.22 The more rapid decline in lung function in bronchiectasis was associated with male sex,24 Pseudomonas colonisation,9 systemic inflammation22 and acute exacerbation.25 Considering that FEV1 showed the most significant decline among the PFT parameters, airflow limitation is expected to develop or worsen as bronchiectasis progresses. Reportedly, airflow limitation became more common with disease progression.5

However, the separation of pulmonary function across BSI-defined or FACED-defined severity classes was modest in our cohort. FEV₁ was included as a variable in both indices; nonetheless, these tools are multidimensional composite scores that incorporate several non-physiologic domains such as age, dyspnoea, radiologic extent, exacerbation history and Pseudomonas colonisation. Therefore, severity class is not determined by FEV₁ alone, and cross-sectional differences in FEV₁ across severity groups may be attenuated. Furthermore, our analysis was cross-sectional, whereas meaningful changes in FEV₁ typically require longitudinal follow-up. Furthermore, patients are enrolled in the KMBARC registry based on radiologic bronchiectasis irrespective of symptom burden, resulting in substantial baseline heterogeneity in FEV₁ within each severity category. These factors likely contributed to the limited cross-sectional separation of physiologic impairment based on BSI or FACED in our study.

Pulmonary function abnormality in bronchiectasis can be quite variable depending on aetiology and disease status.26 A multicentre study reported that the most common lung function abnormality in adults with bronchiectasis was air trapping (70.2%) and impaired diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (55.7%), followed by airflow limitation (41.1%).7 Spirometry often shows airflow limitation, a reduced ratio of FEV1 to FVC, a normal or slightly reduced FVC and a reduced FEV15; moreover, P. aeruginosa colonisation was more common in patients with airflow limitation.27 Lopes et al found that post-tuberculosis bronchiectasis had a lower FEV1 than bronchiectasis associated with other causes, such as non-tuberculosis infection, rheumatic disease and cystic fibrosis.26 Olveira et al reported that the main aetiologies of bronchiectasis with severe airflow limitation (ie, FEV 1<50%) were infection, asthma or COPD.28 In our study, we showed that airflow limitation predominated in severe bronchiectasis presenting with P. aeruginosa colonisation and greater radiological extents.

Restrictive impairment can be a concomitant abnormality in bronchiectasis.26 29 In advanced cases of bronchiectasis, progressive fibrotic transformation can cause restrictive defects due to reduced FVC.30 In addition, FVC can be decreased by atelectasis associated with airway obstruction due to mucus or airway collapse.26 In our study, the restrictive pattern was also more prevalent in healthcare examinees with bronchiectasis than in those without.

Significant exposure to noxious particles, typically tobacco smoke, is a common risk factor for airflow limitation,31 which is the most important feature of COPD.32 Other risk factors include older age,33 low body mass index34 and inactive tuberculosis.35 In this study, we showed that bronchiectasis is an independent risk factor for airflow limitation and that the prevalence of this condition increased when combined with smoking history and disease severity. A recent study showed that the major features of bronchiectasis, such as dilated large airways36 and constriction of small-to-medium-sized airways,37 can serve as aetiologies of airflow limitation.38 The features of bronchiectasis, including bronchial wall dilatation and thickening, as well as plugging of airways, were also associated with airflow limitation.39 Our study is meaningful because we identified CT-confirmed bronchiectasis as a risk factor for airflow limitation. A previous nationwide cross-sectional study also reported a similar relationship, but the findings were based on a self-reported questionnaire without radiologic confirmation.40 We showed that, for airflow limitation, bronchiectasis on CT can be a comparable risk factor to smoking history.

Limitations

Some limitations should be acknowledged. The study was a cross-sectional, hospital-based study in South Korea, which limits causal inference and generalisability, and the aetiologic pattern (including tuberculosis-related disease) may differ across regions.41 Nonetheless, the association between bronchiectasis and airflow limitation was observed in two independent cohorts and persisted after multivariable adjustment. In the health check-up cohort, bronchiectasis was incidentally detected on CT, which may not meet clinical trial definitions; however, CT findings were confirmed by radiologists using predefined criteria, allowing objective identification of radiologic bronchiectasis. Formal interrater reliability analysis for CT-based bronchiectasis diagnosis was not conducted, as image interpretation was performed locally across participating centres rather than in a centralised core laboratory. Lung volumes and post-bronchodilator spirometry were not uniformly available, so pre-bronchodilator spirometry (performed using ATS/ERS standards) was analysed to preserve comparability between cohorts. Microbial data captured any P. aeruginosa isolation (not chronic infection), and BSI/FACED were available only in a subset because some components were missing; these sources of misclassification may have attenuated observed associations rather than producing spurious ones.

Conclusions

Bronchiectasis was an independent risk factor for airflow limitation. The severity of bronchiectasis, smoking history and P. aeruginosa colonisation were associated with aggravation in pulmonary function parameters.
