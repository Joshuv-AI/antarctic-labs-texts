Multilevel and multidimensional features of socioeconomic status and cognitive function in older adulthood: a cross-sectional analysis of the IGNITE study
Authors: Jermon A Drake, Lu Wan, Chaeryon Kang, Peter J Gianaros, Patricio Solis-Urra, Haiqing Huang, Rebecca Reed, Charles H Hillman, Eric D Vidoni, Jeffrey M Burns, Arthur F Kramer, Edward McAuley, George Grove, Kirk I Erickson, Lauren E Oberlin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundLower socioeconomic status (SES) is associated with increased risk of cognitive decline and dementia, yet reliance on singular indicators of SES limits understanding of these relationships. We examined multiple SES characteristics at the individual and area-levels simultaneously in association with diverse cognitive processes in a large, community-dwelling older adult sample.MethodsData collected at three United States sites (Boston, Pittsburgh, Kansas City) included a comprehensive cognitive assessment comprising measures of episodic memory, executive function, processing speed, working memory, and visuospatial abilities. Multiple SES measures were obtained including area-level (Area Deprivation Index), subjective, and objective SES. We generated a novel objective SES latent factor from measures of income, savings, debt-adjusted savings, and financial stability using full information maximum likelihood to address missing data, and examined cross–sectional associations of SES indicators with each cognitive domain as outcome variables in multivariable regression models.FindingsThis study included 648 cognitively unimpaired older adults (69.88 (3.75) years; 71% female). Individual-level objective SES (processing speed, β [95% confidence interval (CI)] = 0.225 [0.152–0.299]; executive function, 0.210 [0.137–0.283]; episodic memory, 0.113 [0.040–0.186]; working memory, 0.200 [0.129–0.271]; visuospatial processing, 0.165 [0.093–0.238]) and subjective SES (processing speed, β [95% CI] = 0.148 [0.077–0.219]; executive function, 0.138 [0.067–0.208]; episodic memory, 0.020 [−0.051 to 0.091]; working memory, 0.128 [0.059–0.197]; visuospatial processing, 0.094 [0.023–0.164]) outperformed area-level SES in detecting cognitive deficits, and demonstrated widespread associations with poorer performance across cognitive domains. Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed and Google Scholar until 01 November 2024, for studies regarding the relationship between socioeconomic status (SES) and cognition in older adulthood. The search terms were “socioeconomic status”, “objective SES”, “subjective SES”, “Area Deprivation Index”, “Neighborhood-level disadvantage” AND “cognition”, “cognitive performance”, “cognitive decline”, “dementia” AND “aging”, “older adulthood.” Lower SES is a powerful determinant of poor health outcomes including increased risk of cognitive decline and dementia in older adulthood. However, we found that the complexity of SES is often reduced to singular measures, leaving key questions unanswered about the relationship between socioeconomic conditions and cognitive health in late life. Our literature search showed that prior studies seldom examine multiple dimensions of SES across individual and area-level contexts. Additionally, most research has focused on dementia incidence rates or brief cognitive measures, with few studies employing detailed cognitive assessments. Whether socioeconomic disadvantage relates broadly to cognition across multiple domains or is instead linked to specific cognitive processes in older adulthood remains poorly understood. Therefore, while prior research has linked socioeconomic gradients to cognitive function, large-scale studies directly comparing the relationship of multiple SES components to cognition in late adulthood are limited, particularly across diverse cognitive domains.

Added value of this study

This study examined how various dimensions of SES, measured at the individual-level (objective SES, subjective SES) and area-level (Area Deprivation Index), differentially relate to cognition in a large community-dwelling cohort of cognitively unimpaired older adults. Leveraging a comprehensive cognitive battery, we also assessed the specificity of these relationships across multiple cognitive domains. In addition, we generated a novel latent factor of objective SES composed of financial indicators, to understand how this compared to traditional objective SES measures widely used in large-scale studies (i.e., income, years of education). Individual SES characteristics were related to poorer cognitive performance across all domains assessed, indicating broad rather than selective associations with multiple cognitive processes susceptible to age-related decline. When examining SES indicators simultaneously, individual SES outperformed area-level SES in detecting cognitive deficits, with objective SES demonstrating the strongest associations. A novel objective SES composite was robustly related to cognitive performance above and beyond traditional measures.

These findings advance understanding of the cognitive burden of socioeconomic inequity, even among older adults without clinical cognitive impairment. In addition, relative to standard singular measures of objective SES, evaluating multiple financial indicators may improve precision and better estimate the broad public health impacts of socioeconomic disadvantage.

Implications of all the available evidence

To advance cognitive aging and dementia research, future studies must ensure that individuals from low SES conditions are adequately represented, given broad susceptibility to cognitive deficits and the need for effective and inclusive interventions. Additionally, the present study emphasizes the importance of adopting more precise SES measures to better characterize socioeconomically patterned disparities in cognitive health. From a public health perspective, efforts to reduce socioeconomic inequality, including financial disadvantage, may help preserve cognitive abilities and mitigate risk for decline in older adulthood.

Introduction

Socioeconomic status (SES) is a key social determinant of health (SDoH) and a powerful predictor of morbidity and disability in older adulthood.1,2 Research has shown that lower SES is associated with increased risk of age-related cognitive decline and dementia,3, 4, 5, 6, 7 though studies often oversimplify the multidimensional construct of SES by using singular indicators (e.g., formal education) that do not reflect the breadth and complexity of an individual's socioeconomic circumstances.8, 9, 10, 11 To advance understanding of inequity influences on cognitive health, there is a scientific and public health imperative to better operationalize SES and more precisely characterize the relationship between socioeconomic disadvantage and cognition in older adulthood.

Indicators of SES are traditionally categorized as objective or subjective, and they are commonly examined at the level of the individual.8 Objective SES quantifies individual standing in a social structure by taking into account measurable material resources.4,8 Subjective SES represents self-perceived social standing by reference to income, education, and occupation relative to others in a given society.12 Along with individual-level measures, dimensions of SES can also be examined at the level of the residential area or community. Area-level SES reflects the socioeconomic context of an area of residence, and is quantified by the resources in the residential environment, as well as the income level and educational background of the residents.4,13

Recent evidence suggests that the relationship between SES and numerous health outcomes varies depending on how SES is defined and measured. Objective SES metrics such as income and education are most commonly studied in relation to cognition, and demonstrate inverse associations with cognitive decline and dementia risk.3,4,14,15 However, subjective SES has likewise been linked to various cardiometabolic outcomes—including those that predispose to cognitive impairment–even when accounting for measures of objective SES.12,16 Area-level disadvantage may also uniquely relate to health outcomes beyond individual SES.5,7,17 Given that individual and area-level measures capture distinct dimensions of the SES construct, they may differentially relate to cognitive outcomes in older adulthood. However, SES components have largely been studied in isolation, and there is a scarcity of research examining multiple SES characteristics simultaneously in relation to cognitive functioning, particularly in multi-site community-based samples without clinical impairment.

While specific features of SES have been linked to cognition,3, 4, 5,7 additional limitations in operationalizing SES may obscure or confound the true strength of these relationships. Many studies, for example, have relied on a single indicator (e.g., education)1,2,15 to reflect the broader construct of objective SES. However, singular measures may underestimate the health burden associated with socioeconomic disadvantage in older adulthood.8, 9, 10, 11 For instance, compared to formal education, financial metrics related to wealth may provide a more accurate depiction of current socioeconomic resources in older adulthood and independently relate to cognitive outcomes.4,9,11,18 Yet, aside from income, few studies have considered other potentially relevant determinants of objective SES such as savings, debt, and financial stability.

There is also a lack of clarity surrounding the specific cognitive processes associated with socioeconomic disadvantage. Prior research has primarily focused on dementia risk3,4,10,19,20 rather than disparities across domains of cognitive functioning in those without clinical impairment. Studies relating socioeconomic disadvantage in childhood to cognition in late life suggest that SES may not be uniformly related to all cognitive domains, with select cognitive processes including memory and aspects of executive function showing more robust associations with childhood SES than other domains.6,21 However, previous studies have largely relied on screening measures of global cognition (e.g., Mini-Mental State Exam) or a brief battery of cognitive tests,6,7,14,18,19 which fail to capture the spectrum of cognitive abilities and provide limited insight into the link between SES and cognitive health in late life. Thus, whether SES relates broadly to cognition across multiple domains or is instead linked to specific cognitive processes in older adulthood remains poorly understood.

With rates of dementia continuing to rise,22 large-scale studies involving comprehensive assessments of both SES and cognition are needed to determine the specificity and extent of cognitive dysfunction associated with socioeconomic conditions in older adulthood. As such, the current study aimed to 1) examine whether various dimensions of SES, assessed at both the individual-level (objective SES, subjective SES) and area-level (census-based Area Deprivation Index), differentially relate to cognitive function measured across multiple domains and 2) test whether a novel composite measure of objective SES outperforms conventional objective measures in detecting cognitive associations in a large, community-based sample of cognitively unimpaired older adults. It was hypothesized that objective SES would demonstrate the strongest associations with cognition across domains, but that subjective and area-level SES would explain additional variance in cognitive performance. Furthermore, we hypothesized that an objective SES composite composed of multiple financial determinants would uniquely relate to cognitive performance above and beyond standard indices.

Methods

Participants

Participants were adults aged 65–80 enrolled in the Investigating Gains in Neurocognition in an Intervention Trial of Exercise (IGNITE) study (R01AG053952). IGNITE was a 12-month, multisite, randomized clinical trial examining the effects of physical activity on brain and cognitive health in older adults. IGNITE is registered as a randomized controlled trial with ClinicalTrials.gov, number NCT02875301. Participants were eligible if they reported no history or presence of neurological conditions (e.g., Parkinson's disease, stroke, dementia), no current major depression or history or presence of severe mental illness (e.g., schizophrenia), and no current treatment for cancer, Type 1 Diabetes, or recent cardiovascular events (e.g., congestive heart failure, uncontrolled arrhythmia). Only participants considered cognitively unimpaired based on the 2011 National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer's Association (NIA-AA) diagnostic criteria were included in the study. Cognitive status determinations were made during consensus conference by a team of neuropsychologists. Please see Erickson et al., 2019 for complete eligibility criteria.23

A total of 648 participants were enrolled in the trial and included in this analysis of baseline data. Data were collected between September 2017 and December 2020 at three sites located in the United States: the University of Pittsburgh (Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania), University of Kansas Medical Center (Kansas City, Kansas), and Northeastern University (Boston, Massachusetts). Recruitment strategies targeted community samples through newspapers, direct mailings, senior centers and churches, health system research registries and online media. The sample demographics reflected the regional demographic representation of racial and ethnic minorities at each of the three study sites, according to United States census data. Participants self-identified their race and ethnicity along the National Institutes of Health guidelines. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at each site with the following protocol numbers: Pittsburgh (STUDY19110244), Kansas (STUDY00140896), and Northeastern (17-05-02). All participants provided written informed consent before data collection.

Objective socioeconomic status

We used items from the MacArthur Socioeconomic Status Index to determine objective SES. The MacArthur is an 11-item self-report questionnaire that captures multiple SES indicators across educational attainment, occupational status, income, and financial stability, including questions related to home ownership and other assets, savings, debt, and duration of standard of living maintenance if one lost all current sources of income. This widely-used questionnaire does not provide a summary or total score, rather, the items reflect a conceptual aim to represent SES as a gradient across key domains, consistent with the broader literature on SES measurement.8,9,24 Items from the MacArthur Index were used to test multiple indicators of objective SES: in primary analyses, a composite objective SES measure derived from a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was examined; in secondary analyses, traditional objective SES measures were assessed, including years of education, income, and their standardized average.

Subjective socioeconomic status

The United States ladder from the MacArthur Scale of Subjective Social Status was used to index subjective SES. This widely-used scale depicts a 10-rung pictorial ladder and measures how an individual perceives their social standing relative to others in their community.8 Participants were shown a picture of the 10-rung ladder and prompted to consider money, education, and occupation. They were then asked to indicate on the ladder “where they think they stand, at this time of their lives, relative to other people in the United States.” Scores range from 1 to 10, with higher scores corresponding to higher subjective SES.

Census-based area-level SES

The Area Deprivation Index (ADI) was used to measure area-level disadvantage. The ADI is a validated and widely-used census-based composite index comprised of 17 indicators of poverty, education, employment, and housing characteristics.13 ADI scores were generated in relation to the state in which the participant resided, using the 2022 state-level indicator of ADI. Participant home addresses at baseline were used to assign census block groups, which were then linked to state ADI deciles.13 That is, each participant's census block group was ranked relative to other block groups within the same state. State-level deciles were used to ensure that area-level disadvantage was contextualized within each state's distribution. The ADI state decile rankings range from 1 to 10, with lower scores reflecting lower area-level disadvantage and higher scores indicating greater area-level disadvantage. Complete details on the ADI construction and metrics are made publicly-available through the Neighborhood Atlas (https://www.neighborhoodatlas.medicine.wisc.edu/).

Cognitive assessment

Participants completed a comprehensive cognitive battery assessing various domains including measures of working memory (N-Back Working Memory Task, Spatial Working Memory Task, List Sorting Working Memory), episodic memory (Brief Visuospatial Memory Test, Picture Sequencing Test, Hopkins Verbal Learning Test, Logical Memory, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) free recall, Verbal Paired Associates), executive function/attentional control (Flanker Task, Stroop, Dimensional Change Card Sort, Trail Making Test, Part B), processing speed (Letter Comparison, Digit Symbol Substitution Test, Trail Making Test, Part A), and visuospatial abilities (Matrix Reasoning, Spatial Relations, MoCA Clock Draw).23,25 The cognitive assessment involved a combination of paper-pencil and computerized tasks and was administered in-person by annually-certified psychometricians across two days. Performance in each domain was measured using previously established latent factors,25 with higher values reflecting better performance.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.1). To generate a composite score of objective SES, a CFA was performed using individual items from the MacArthur Socioeconomic Status Index. Using the lavaan package, we specified a one-factor CFA model and items with factor loadings less than 0.40 were removed.26,27 Several different goodness-of-fit statistics were used to assess model fit using established cutoffs, including the χ2-test, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), standardized root mean residual (SRMR), comparative fit index, Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) and the Akaike information criterion (AIC) (Supplementary Methods).

Correlations between SES indicators were determined using Pearson's correlation coefficient. A series of hierarchical linear regression models were conducted to examine independent associations between each SES predictor (objective SES, subjective SES, and area-level SES), tested separately, and cognitive performance using each of the five domains as outcome variables of interest (episodic memory, executive function/attentional control, processing speed, working memory, and visuospatial abilities). All SES indices were then entered simultaneously to test the relative contribution of each SES index to variability in cognitive performance in each domain. We applied the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure for multiple testing correction of primary regression models examining associations of each SES predictor with the 5 cognitive domains. Benjamini-Hochberg was applied separately for each SES predictor, correcting for the five cognitive outcomes within each model at a false discovery rate of 0.05. Covariates were selected a priori and included putative indicators of SES and cognitive function: self-reported chronological age (in years), sex (male or female), race/ethnicity (coded as White or non-White), and study site. For models showing significant associations, SES indicators were discretized and entered as predictors in post hoc analysis of covariance models adjusting for age, sex, race, and study site to interrogate group differences. Age and sex were examined as moderators, to assess evidence of accelerated cognitive aging in the context of low SES and to determine whether SES may relate to sex differences in cognitive aging trajectories.

To evaluate site-level clustering, we calculated intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) from random intercept–only models. All ICCs were <0.01, indicating minimal variance in cognitive outcomes attributable to study site (Supplementary Table S1). Given this, and our a priori decision to adjust for site, we retained site as a fixed effect in all primary models. However, as a sensitivity analysis, we applied wild cluster bootstrap procedures to our primary analyses, using the boottest package in STATA,28 to account for within-site correlation with a limited number of clusters (3).

Given the limitations of conventional measures of objective SES, secondary hierarchical linear regression models examined associations between the CFA-generated objective SES composite and cognitive performance alongside traditional objective SES measures including years of education, income, and their standardized average, at a significance level of p < 0.05. This enabled us to test whether the objective SES composite provided additional predictive value in detecting cognitive associations beyond commonly used measures of objective SES. All analyses and reporting of results are consistent with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 depicts the participant characteristics. The sample included 648 older adults (mean (SD) age = 69.88 (3.75)) with 461 women (71.1%) and an average of 16.32 (SD = 2.21) years of education. The median family annual income for the sample falls within the $75,000–$99,999 range and the median total savings falls between $200,000 and $499,999, both above national averages. According to the United States Census Bureau, during the 2017–2020 study period, US median household income ranged from $61,372 to $67,521,29,30 and in 2019, average retirement savings for those 50–89.9 years old was approximately $170,600.31 Objective SES was positively correlated with subjective SES (r = 0.46); higher area-level deprivation was associated with lower objective SES (r = −0.38) and subjective SES (r = −0.27) (Fig. 1).

Among objective SES markers (income, savings, debt-adjusted savings, standard of living maintenance), missing data ranged from 13 to 23% (Table 1, Supplementary Results). Subjective SES was missing for 2 individuals, and the ADI could not be calculated for 20 individuals (Supplementary Methods). Missing data was assumed to be missing at random, such that the probability of missingness depends only on observed data, not the missing values themselves.32 Missingness was independent of age and study site; however, missingness was significantly associated with sex, race, and years of education (p < 0.05). Females were more likely to not report savings and debt information. Non-white participants and participants with fewer years of education were more likely to not report family income, savings, and debt information. Full information maximum likelihood was used in CFA models to address missing data.33 In addition, to examine the potential impact of missingness on the objective SES index, we conducted sensitivity analyses by refitting the CFA using 1) multiple imputation by chained equations and 2) only participants with complete data (Supplementary Methods). Similar model fit and factor loadings were observed between the CFA model estimated using full information maximum likelihood and the CFA models estimated using multiple imputation by chained equations and including only participants with complete data (Supplementary Table S2). We also performed a series of sensitivity analyses simulating plausible missing not at random scenarios.34, 35, 36 Model fit and factor loadings remained stable across all tested missing not at random conditions, suggesting the findings were robust and not highly sensitive to various deviations from the missing at random assumption (Supplementary Tables S3–S6).

Confirmatory factor analysis: objective socioeconomic status

To capture the best-fit and most parsimonious model, we tested the fit of several models that included indicators of educational attainment (e.g., duration in years, highest degree earned) and economic security (e.g., income, savings, financial stability), with CFA (Supplementary Results; Table S7). Consistent with standard guidance for conducting a CFA,26,27 each model removed indicators with factor loadings less than 0.40. The best-fit model was composed of total family annual income, total savings, debt-adjusted savings, and duration of standard of living maintenance if one lost all current sources of income (χ2 = 7.552, df = 2, p = 0.023, χ2/df = 3.776, comparative fit index = 0.996, TLI = 0.988, RMSEA = 0.066, SRMR = 0.010, AIC = 7776.461). Factor loadings in the best-fit model ranged from λs = 0.538 (family annual income) to 0.987 (savings) (all p < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure S1).

Linking individual and area-based SES measures to cognitive function

We tested the independent associations of each socioeconomic marker to cognitive performance, controlling for age, sex, race, and study site. In exploratory analyses, we tested differential associations between socioeconomic markers and cognitive performance based on age and sex.

Lower objective SES composite scores were significantly associated with poorer performance in all domains, with small effect sizes observed across domains (Table 2).37 The strongest associations were observed in the domains of processing speed (β [95% confidence interval (CI)] = 0.225 [0.152–0.299]), working memory (β [95% CI] = 0.200 [0.129–0.271]), and executive function (β [95% CI] = 0.210 [0.137–0.283]). The strength, directionality, and significance of these associations remained when re-estimated using wild cluster bootstrap procedures (Supplementary Table S8). All findings persisted in sensitivity analyses re-running regression models using the objective SES factor estimated with multiple imputation by chained equations and when including only complete cases (Supplementary Table S10). To interrogate these relationships, post hoc analysis of covariance models adjusting for age, sex, race, and study site tested objective SES across quartiles and showed consistent associations. Compared to those in the highest objective SES quartile, those in the lowest 2 quartiles performed significantly more poorly in all domains aside from episodic memory (Supplementary Table S9). On average, individuals in the lowest SES quartile performed between 0.22 and 0.58 standard deviations lower than those in the highest objective SES quartile across cognitive domains (episodic memory, pairwise adjusted mean difference [95% CI] = 0.223 [−0.054, 0.499]; processing speed, 0.580 [0.304, 0.856]; executive function/attentional control, 0.556 [0.282, 0.829]; working memory, 0.516 [0.248, 0.784]; visuospatial processing, 0.434 [0.161, 0.706]) (Fig. 2). There was an interaction of age X objective SES on working memory performance (β = 0.068; t = 1.99, p = 0.047), though this was no longer significant after multiple testing correction. Sex did not moderate observed associations.

Lower subjective SES was significantly associated with poorer performance in all domains except for episodic memory, after accounting for age, sex, race, and study site. Small effect sizes were observed in all significant cognitive domains (Table 2).37 The executive function (β [95% CI] = 0.138 [0.067–0.208]), working memory (β [95% CI] = 0.128 [0.059–0.197]), and processing speed domains (β [95% CI] = 0.148 [0.077–0.219]) demonstrated the strongest associations with subjective SES (Table 2). Additionally, the strength, directionality, and significance of these associations persisted when re-estimated using wild cluster bootstrap procedures (Supplementary Results, Table S8). In post hoc analysis of covariance models, individuals reporting the lowest subjective SES (those scoring 1–3 on the MacArthur Subjective Social Status scale) performed, on average, between 0.17 and 0.64 standard deviations lower than those reporting the highest subjective SES (those scoring 9–10 on the MacArthur Subjective Social Status scale) across cognitive domains (Supplementary Table S11). Age and sex did not moderate observed associations. All objective and subjective SES associations remained when a more stringent Benjamini-Hochberg-corrected threshold (false discovery rate = 0.01) was applied, aside from the subjective SES association with visuospatial processing.

Area-level deprivation (ADI) was not associated with performance in any cognitive domain (Table 2). In moderation models, there was an ADI × Sex interaction in the domains of processing speed (β = 0.18; t = 2.01; p = 0.045) and executive function/attentional control (β = 0.22; t = 2.43; p = 0.016), such that greater area-level disadvantage was associated with poorer performance specifically in males. These associations were no longer significant after multiple testing correction. Age was not a significant moderator in any cognitive domain.

Relative associations of objective SES, subjective SES, and area-level disadvantage to cognitive function

Next, we tested the relative contribution of each SES marker to variability in cognitive performance. When examining all SES measures simultaneously, in addition to age, sex, race, and study site, objective SES remained significantly associated with all five cognitive domains (all p < 0.005) (Fig. 3). The standardized coefficients for objective SES also remained relatively stable across domains when subjective SES and ADI were added to the model.

In contrast, the associations between subjective SES and cognitive function were no longer significant and the standardized coefficients for subjective SES decreased (Fig. 3), suggesting subjective SES accounted for less unique variance in cognitive function once other SES indicators were included in the model. Associations between area-level disadvantage and cognitive function remained non-significant. Similar findings were observed in sensitivity analyses using the multiple imputation by chained equations-generated objective SES factor (Supplementary Methods, Table S12).

Comparing associations between objective SES indices and cognitive function

Secondary analyses were conducted to examine whether the objective SES composite explained additional variance in cognitive function beyond commonly used metrics of objective SES including education, income, and their standardized average. After adjusting for age, sex, race, and study site, greater years of education was significantly associated with better performance across domains (all p < 0.001). When including education in hierarchical models, the objective SES composite remained significantly associated with each of the cognitive domains, except for episodic memory, though the magnitude of these associations was reduced (Supplementary Table S13).

Higher family annual income was significantly associated with better performance across all domains after adjusting for age, sex, race, and study site (all p < 0.01). The objective SES composite also remained significantly associated with each cognitive domain after adding income to the models (all p < 0.05), though the strength of these associations decreased (Supplementary Table S14). As income was an input variable for the latent objective SES factor, each regression model was tested for collinearity via examination of zero-order correlations, tolerance, and the variance inflation factor. The variance inflation factor ranged from 1.54 to 1.60 for SES model predictors, suggesting that collinearity among predictors was low.

Finally, we explored the relationship between SES and cognitive function using the standardized average of years of education and income, to determine how this commonly used approach of indexing objective SES compared to our CFA-generated objective SES composite score. The standardized average of education and income was significantly associated with performance in all cognitive domains (all p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S15), adjusting for age, sex, race, and study site. When adding the objective SES composite to the model, the standardized average of education and income remained significantly associated with all cognitive domains. Additionally, the objective SES factor remained significantly associated with working memory, processing speed, and executive function/attentional control, though the magnitude of associations weakened (Supplementary Table S15); no associations were found for episodic memory or visuospatial abilities. The variance inflation factor ranged from 1.50 to 1.55 for SES model predictors, suggesting that collinearity among predictors was low.

Discussion

This study examined whether various dimensions of SES differentially relate to cognitive performance in older adulthood, and assessed the specificity of these relationships across multiple cognitive domains susceptible to age-related decline. In a community-based sample of cognitively unimpaired older adults, we demonstrate that a composite index of objective SES composed of financial indicators was inversely related to cognitive performance in a domain-general fashion. Lower subjective SES was also independently associated with poorer cognitive performance in all domains except for episodic memory. When examining multiple SES indicators simultaneously, objective SES showed the broadest and most robust associations with cognitive function relative to subjective and area-level indices. In addition, the objective SES composite provided additional predictive value beyond conventional estimates of objective SES, including education and income, in select cognitive domains. These findings highlight the breadth of cognitive deficits related to SES, and suggest that objective measures reflecting multiple financial determinants may improve precision and better capture the broad cognitive impacts of socioeconomic disadvantage in late adulthood.

While studies have linked various SES characteristics to cognitive decline and dementia risk,3, 4, 5, 6, 7,38 most prior research has evaluated SES components in isolation. Given the multidimensional nature of SES, singular indicators may provide only a partial understanding of the health burden associated with socioeconomic conditions.8, 9, 10, 11 Yet, few large-scale studies have examined the independent and combined relationships of multiple dimensions of SES to cognitive function, especially across diverse cognitive domains.

In this study, lower objective SES was broadly related to poorer cognitive performance across all domains assessed, indicating diffuse rather than selective associations with multiple domains known to decline with advancing age.39 The effect sizes of observed associations were small across cognitive domains, though at the population level, even relatively modest relationships to cognitive function can have important public health consequences.8,15 While the specific mechanisms linking SES to cognitive impairment are not well understood, several pathways have been proposed. Higher objective SES confers greater financial resources to engage in lifestyle behaviors beneficial for brain and cognitive health,4,8 with higher SES linked to better diet quality and greater engagement in physical activity.1 In addition, socioeconomic disadvantage is associated with chronic stress and increased prevalence of health conditions that predispose to cognitive decline (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, heart disease).3,6,40 Reduced healthcare accessibility related to lower objective SES11 may also result in poorer management of chronic health conditions. Better characterizing the specific pathways linking lower SES to increased risk for cognitive impairment, including the mediating role of medical morbidities, lifestyle behaviors, and disparities in access to care, is an important avenue for future research.

In addition to objective measures, several studies have linked self-perceived social standing relative to others (subjective social standing) to cognitive function.40,41 However, studies specifically examining the link between subjective SES and cognition are scarce, particularly in dementia-free older adult populations. Here, lower subjective SES was significantly associated with poorer performance in most domains assessed. These relationships persisted when accounting for area-level disadvantage (ADI), but were attenuated when accounting for objective SES. Our findings indicate that subjective SES may relate to cognitive function through common pathways or mechanisms that overlap with those of objective SES. Supporting such speculation, subjective SES has likewise been linked to prolonged stress and elevated risk of cardiometabolic conditions.12,16

There were no significant associations of area-level SES with cognitive performance, suggesting that individual-level disparities may contribute more to variability in cognitive functioning than residential or area-level characteristics in cognitively unimpaired older adults. Our findings corroborate several longitudinal studies that found no associations between area-level SES, cognition, and dementia risk,4,19,20 but differ from other cohort studies suggesting that greater area-level disadvantage predicts poorer cognitive performance and higher risk of cognitive decline.5,7 Differences in sample size, geographic location, and cognitive assessment measures may contribute to variability across studies.4,5,7,19,20 In addition, the ADI measure used here aggregates community-level poverty, education, housing, and employment characteristics into a single score.13 Though commonly used in large-scale studies, this approach does not allow for isolation of specific features of the residential environment that may relate to cognitive functioning such as greenspace, pollution, crime rates, and social cohesion.42 Several studies also suggest that the cognitive impacts of area-level conditions may be driven by those living in the most disadvantaged neighborhoods.4,5,38 Although the current sample exhibited a broad range of SES, future studies with greater representation of those at the extreme ends of area-level SES may provide further insight into the cognitive implications of area deprivation.

We also tested whether a novel factor-derived objective SES composite composed of financial indicators captured additional variance in cognitive performance above and beyond conventional markers of objective SES. We found that the objective SES composite was associated with cognitive performance independent of education, the most commonly studied marker of SES in relation to cognitive decline and dementia risk.4,11 In addition, while education related strongly to cognitive performance, it was modestly correlated with financial indicators of SES and did not load well on the objective SES composite. Thus, education alone may be insufficient to detect the extent and magnitude of cognitive deficits associated with socioeconomic conditions. Other (primarily economic) factors may uniquely relate to variability in domain-specific cognitive performance. As education is typically completed in early adulthood, this corresponds with reports suggesting that socioeconomic resources in older adulthood may be better represented by factors related to wealth and financial stability.4,9,11

In addition, the objective SES factor remained significantly associated with most domains of cognitive function when controlling for income–the most common financial metric used in large-scale studies to index objective SES–as well as the standardized average of income and education.11,14 This extends prior work examining income associations with cognitive function,3,15,18,19 and suggests that accounting for various aspects of wealth (savings, debt, financial stability) may provide additional predictive value beyond income alone. Taken together, income and formal education, though relatively easy to capture, may underestimate the cognitive burden of socioeconomic inequity when assessed in isolation. Based on these findings, it is recommended that multiple financial indicators be measured and incorporated into future studies examining social determinants of health and the broad public health impacts of socioeconomic conditions.

There are several limitations that should be considered. The cross-sectional nature of the study limits our ability to evaluate cognitive trajectories in relation to SES or draw causal conclusions. While low SES may compromise cognitive health, it is also possible that cognitive performance influences socioeconomic circumstances. The sample demographics reflected the population demographics of each metropolitan region of data collection, including 24% of individuals from racial and ethnic minority groups. However, achieving U.S. census-based recruitment targets may not be sufficient to make broader conclusions about the generalizability of results to diverse populations. Across many scientific disciplines, inclusion criteria are structurally inequitable and prohibitive for certain groups,43 though in our sample the eligibility criteria did not show a consistent pattern of biased exclusion based on age, race, ethnicity, or sex. The sample was limited to older adults aged 65–80 years, and study participants were predominantly highly educated residents of the Central and Northeastern United States. Efforts to maximize racial, ethnic, and geographic diversity in future studies will be vital for assessing how factors related to discrimination, systemic racism, rurality, and structural disadvantage may influence the relationship between SES and cognition. While participants demonstrated a considerable range of SES, on average, our sample exceeded U.S. national averages of objective SES indicators (e.g., savings, income). Thus, it is unclear how our results would generalize to a population where demographic factors are differently distributed. External validation in an independent sample representing a broader age range and greater sociodemographic diversity is needed. We also used a single indicator of subjective SES. Although this approach is common,12,40,41 similar to objective SES, subjective perceptions of SES may be better represented using multiple indicators. Relatedly, this study focused on formal years of education, which does not account for quality of education, a factor that may also be important in determining cognitive outcomes.10

Another limitation is the missingness of financial data in the sample. We addressed missingness by using full information maximum likelihood estimation when generating the objective SES factor,33 and demonstrated in sensitivity models that model fit and factor loadings were comparable when generating the model using multiple imputation and when including only participants with complete data. Moreover, observed associations between objective SES and cognitive function were similar in the full information maximum likelihood, multiple imputation, and complete case analyses. While 2–5% of participants reportedly did not know their family annual income and savings, the majority of missing data was accounted for by participants choosing the “No response” option. Unwillingness to report select financial information is likely influenced by interpersonal characteristics and contextual factors.44,45 We found that missingness was associated with sex, race, and years of education. Specifically, females, non-white participants, and those with fewer years of education were more likely to not report select financial information. While speculative, concerns about judgment, privacy, and socio-cultural norms surrounding the disclosure of finances to others may have contributed to the missing data. Further research on predictors of non-response to socioeconomic indices is needed to determine factors that influence missingness and identify strategies to mitigate this. For example, using mixed methods to measure SES may enable researchers to identify reasons for participants’ reluctance to disclose socioeconomic information, and provide opportunities to address their concerns and clarify the purpose of collecting this data.

As prior research has primarily focused on relating SES to dementia risk, we sought to expand this work by examining SES associations with cognition in community-dwelling older adults without clinical impairment. While we performed a comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation with consensus adjudication for those with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) and dementia, given known limitations of cognitive assessments and the often variable definitions of MCI, it is possible that there were participants included that could be near the MCI range. Finally, this study examined associations between current SES and cognition. However, socioeconomic conditions throughout the lifespan, including in childhood, may dynamically influence cognitive outcomes in late life.18

This study advances understanding of the relationship between multiple dimensions of socioeconomic disadvantage and cognitive health in late life, and sheds light on both the magnitude and specificity of these associations. Individual SES characteristics outperformed area-level SES in detecting cognitive deficits, and showed broad and robust associations with poorer performance across cognitive domains. Integrating multiple financial indicators into future studies may improve estimation of the cognitive impacts of socioeconomic conditions. Public health efforts to limit socioeconomic inequalities at the individual level, specifically targeting dimensions and mechanisms associated with having fewer financial resources, may help to preserve cognitive health and mitigate risk for decline. For instance, targeting socioeconomic inequalities may involve efforts to expand income support programs (e.g., wage increases and income supplements). Additionally, if lower financial resources are contributing to reduced access to health care and management of conditions that affect cognitive health, programs designed to improve health care coverage and mitigate out-of-pocket expenses may be beneficial.
