Studying the effect of reaction time on the properties of UiO-66-Ce MOF as an efficient adsorbent
Authors: Mohammad Sepehr Salimi, Hossein Molavi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In the present work, we report the effect of reaction time on the physico-chemical properties, adsorption performance, and structural stability of cerium-based metal-organic frameworks (UiO-66-Ce MOFs). The UiO-66-Ce MOFs were synthesized via a green room temperature (RT) synthetic method at different reaction times (from 3 to 24 h) and characterized by different techniques to examine their crystallinity, porosity, thermal and chemical stability as well as adsorption properties. It was found that the crystallinity, thermal and chemical stability as well as the porosity of UiO-66-Ce MOFs were increased with increasing the reaction time, in which the sample synthesized in 18 h exhibited the highest surface area of 968 m2/g. Accordingly, it can be inferred that the higher reaction time is better for the synthesis of highly stable UiO-66-Ce MOFs, and 18 h is the optimum time for this purpose. The synthesized UiO-66-Ce MOFs as well as the water-soaked samples (soaked in water for 10 days) were applied as potential adsorbents to remove a broad range of highly toxic pollutants such as malachite green (MG), methylene blue (MB), and hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)) from water. It was found that all synthesized adsorbents have good adsorption performances towards these pollutants, in which their adsorption capacities for MB, MG, and Cr(VI) were in the range of 132–217, 91–120, and 49–58 mg/g, respectively. Although the Cr(VI) adsorption capacity of synthesized MOFs was reduced after soaking in water for 10 days, however, their adsorption performances towards cationic MB and MG dyes were significantly improved, in which their adsorption capacities were in the range of 173–254 and 164–183 mg/g, respectively. These findings indicate the applicability of UiO-66-Ce MOFs as stable adsorbents for long-time wastewater treatment applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21346-2.

Introduction

Among different reported metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), cerium (Ce)-based MOFs are attractive materials mainly because of their relatively low cost, good physico-chemical properties, high redox activity, simple synthesis methods, adjustable functions, tailorable surface area, and versatile composition1,2. Most of the reported studies before 2015, were focused on investigating the properties and applications of Ce(III)-based MOFs due to the high redox activity of Ce(IV) ions that pose several challenges3. Owing to the low-lying vacant 4f orbitals of Ce(IV) ions, they can easily and quickly accept electrons from the organic solvents or organic ligands under solvothermal synthesis conditions4. UiO-66-Ce(IV) MOF, the first member of Ce(IV)-based MOFs that built up from the coordination of Ce6O4(OH)412+ clusters with twelve 1,4-benzenedicarboxylic acid (BDC) ligands, was firstly synthesized by Stock and coworkers5 in 2015.

UiO-66-Ce(IV) MOFs exhibited the most properties of zirconium (Zr)-based UiO-66-Zr(IV) analogous6, while due to the presence of highly redox Ce(IV) ions in the inorganic clusters of UiO-66-Ce MOFs differentiate UiO-66-Ce MOFs from Zr-based analogous in redox properties4. To date, various synthetic methods have been utilized to synthesize UiO-66-Ce MOFs, however, most of them require organic solvents (mostly N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF)) and relatively high-temperature solvothermal conditions7. Owing to the high redox activity of Ce(IV) ions, they can oxidize organic solvents and sensitive functional groups of organic ligands. As a result of such phenomena, some Ce(IV) ions reduce to Ce(III) ions, leading to the formation of unfavorable Ce(III)-MOFs or a relatively low Ce(IV)-MOFs yield8,9. Therefore, it can be expected that the selection of organic solvents, MOF precursors, and reaction conditions to produce UiO-66-Ce(IV) MOFs needs careful investigation, and a short reaction time during solvothermal condition is required to produce UiO-66-Ce(IV) MOFs with high purity5,10.

Instead of DMF, water, and their mixture, other organic solvents like acetonitrile were also used to synthesize Ce-MOFs, in which it was reported that using acetonitrile as a solvent allowed to synthesize Ce-MOFs at relatively harsher conditions compared to the conventional solvo/hydrothermal methods11. Moreover, it was found that monocarboxylic acid-based compounds that act as modulators can also control the oxidation state of Ce ions in Ce-MOFs. Thus, the reaction time can be increased in the presence of modulators without the risk of reduction of Ce(IV) ions12. In recent years, much focus has been devoted to the room temperature (RT) synthesis of Ce-MOFs because this synthetic strategy is economically and environmentally advantageous in different applications13. However, this strategy still suffers from different challenges such as needing specific additives, organic co-solvents (e.g., DMF, ethanol, etc.), long reaction time, and production of products with lower chemical stability14–16. Thus, it is essential to develop a new synthetic strategy that minimizes the energy input, reduces the use of toxic additives, and avoids the utilization of hazardous organic solvents.

Switching from conventional solvo/hydrothermal methods (that need high temperature, high pressure, organic solvents, etc.) to water-based RT synthetic methods, remarkably reduces the production cost and toxicity of resulting MOFs17. This synthetic method refers to the RT production of MOFs in water as a green solvent without needing any external activation energy18. Therefore, several research groups have focused on the synthesizing Ce-MOFs at RT. For instance, Li et al.14 reported the synthesis of X-UiO-66-Ce (X = Br, H, NO2, and CH3) MOFs at RT in the presence of water as a solvent and sodium perchlorate monohydrate (NaClO4.H2O) as salting-in species within 10 min to 6 h. Subsequently, Dai et al.16 synthesized MOF-801-Ce at RT in the presence of water as a solvent within 12 h without utilizing any additive. Therefore, it can be inferred that the synthesis of Ce-MOFs via RT methods completed in a few minutes to several days13,19. Hence, in the present work, UiO-66-Ce MOFs were synthesized via a green RT synthetic method in different reaction times, using water as a green solvent, NaClO4.H2O as salting-in species to increase the solubility of BDC molecules in aqueous solutions, and acetone as an exchange solvent.

Dyes are extensively used as coloring agents in most industries (e.g., paper, textiles, wool, painting, leather, etc.), in which a large volume of them are released into the environment during various processes. Because of their non-biodegradable, carcinogenic, and high toxicity, releasing them into different surface water bodies may causes severe effect on the human bodies and aquatic environment20. On the other hand, heavy metal ions are the most potentially toxic contaminants because they result in the dysfunction of various biological processes, even at low concentrations. Hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)) is one such heavy metal, ranked as the most carcinogenic substance by the International Agency for Research on Cancer21. This heavy metal ion is carcinogenic, mutagenic, and highly toxic that is a severe threat to the biological species. Therefore, in the present work Cr(VI) ions, malachite green (MG), and methylene blue (MB) were selected as model pollutants due to their high toxicity and large usage in different industries.

Accordingly, the aims of this work are: (1) investigating the effect of reaction time on the physico-chemical properties of UiO-66-Ce MOFs, (2) studying the structural stability of UiO-66-Ce MOFs against water, (3) evaluating the adsorption performance of resulting UiO-66-Ce MOFs toward different pollutants, and (4) studying the effect of reaction time on the water stability and adsorption performance of UiO-66-Ce MOFs. Thus, herein, we synthesize UiO-66-Ce MOFs at RT for different reaction times (from 3 to 24 h) and investigate their properties. The synthesized MOFs at different reaction times were applied as adsorbents to remove a broad range of pollutants such as MG, MB, and Cr(VI) ions from water. Moreover, the structural stability of synthesized adsorbents was also investigated by soaking them in water for 10 days. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first report that studies the effect of reaction time on the physico-chemical properties, water stability, and adsorption performance of UiO-66-Ce MOFs towards different pollutants.

Experimental

Materials and characterization methods

All chemicals and characterization methods used in this study can be found in the supporting information file.

Synthesis of UiO-66-Ce MOFs

UiO-66-Ce MOFs were synthesized at RT according to our previous work22 with slight changes. Accordingly, 0.033 g of BDC and 0.084 g of NaClO4.H2O were dissolved in 1.2 mL of deionized water and stirred vigorously. Subsequently, 0.11 g of cerium(IV) ammonium nitrate ((NH4)2Ce(NO3)6) was added to the above mixture and stirred at RT for different times (from 3 to 24 h) to complete the synthesis reaction. After a specific time, the prepared white particles were collected by centrifugation and washed 3 times with 15 mL water (3*15) to remove the unreacted MOF precursors as well as NaClO4.H2O. Subsequently, the washed samples were soaked in DMF (2*15) and stirred for 1 h to completely remove the unreacted BDC molecules, followed by washing with acetone (3*15). The trapped solvents within the porous structure of UiO-66-Ce MOFs were exchanged by acetone via soaking in acetone (15 mL) for 3 days. Eventually, the washed samples were dried by heating at 100 ◦C overnight.

Water stability tests

To investigate the structural stability of synthesized UiO-66-Ce MOFs at different reaction times, a specific amount of each sample (≈ 0.05 g) was dispersed into 10 mL of deionized water and kept at RT for 10 days. After that, the powders were collected by centrifugation, rinsed with water, dried at 100 ◦C overnight, and subsequently kept in a desiccator to prepare for further characterization. Additionally, the supernatants were also collected and the concentrations of Ce ion as criteria for the degradation percentage of MOFs were determined using inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES).

Results and discussion

FTIR analysis

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of all MOFs synthesized in various reaction times confirm that the BDC molecules are successfully coordinated with inorganic clusters and produce UiO-66-Ce MOFs (Fig. 1). Accordingly, the stretching vibrational peaks at around 516 and 746 cm−1 that are observed in all spectra are assigned to the different coordination modes of Ce-O bonds23,24. It can be observed that the intensity of these peaks increased with increasing the reaction time, confirming the complete coordination of Ce6O4(OH)412+ clusters with functional groups of BDC molecules.

Moreover, the stretching vibrational peak at around 1650 cm−1 is related to the -COO− groups of BDC ligands further confirming the successful coordination of Ce clusters with organic ligands25. The weak peak at around 1700 cm−1 may be related to the -COOH groups of free BDC molecules trapped within the pores of samples and/or the uncoordinated -COOH groups of BDC molecules due to the presence of structural defects in the structure of resulting adsorbents13,26. More importantly, it was found that the intensity of this peak related to the structural defect reduced with increasing reaction times, indicating the formation of defect-free MOFs at longer reaction times27. Moreover, the weak peak at around 3070 cm−1 corresponds to the aromatic C-H stretching28. The other broad peaks observed between 3200 and 3600 cm−1 are due to the different stretching modes of O-H groups originating from the presence of adsorbed water molecules24.

XRD analysis

The appearance of three characteristic peaks of UiO-66-based MOFs at around 2θ = 7.42, 8.47, and 24.98° that are attributed to the (111), (002), and (600) lattice planes, respectively, is obvious in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of all synthesized samples, which is similar to the simulated XRD patterns of UiO-66-Zr MOF29,30. More importantly, it was found that there are no significant differences between the XRD patterns of samples prepared in different reaction times, suggesting that UiO-66-Ce MOFs could be synthesized in different reaction times. This analysis was also conducted to determine the structural stability of different samples against water for 10 days. Comparing the XRD patterns of synthesized samples before (Fig. 2a) and after (Fig. 2b) soaking in water for 10 days confirms the structural stability of all samples against water during this condition. Accordingly, the similarity of their XRD patterns at different conditions evidences the good water stability of these samples that is consistent with their Zr-based analogous31, thereby suggesting their applicability as stable adsorbents for long-time wastewater treatment applications.

N2 adsorption-desorption analysis

The N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms of all synthesized samples are conducted to determine their porosity, in which the results are shown in Figs. 3 and S1. All the synthesized samples at different reaction times exhibited type-I isotherms, confirming the microporous structure of these MOFs. The sharp N2 adsorption at high relative pressure (0.9-1) may be due to the formation of secondary mesopores between the MOF particles because of their agglomeration nature. The specific surface area, total pore volume, and average pore diameters are calculated from Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analyses of the adsorption-desorption isotherms and summarized in Table 1. The BET surface area of synthesized samples varies from 610 to 968 m2/g, in which the measured BET surface area relatively increases with increasing the reaction time. The differences in their porosity may be due to different reaction times and the presence of some structural defects. Accordingly, the highest surface area of 968 m2/g was obtained for the sample synthesized in 18 h.

It was found that the obtained surface areas are still lower than those values obtained for the UiO-66-Ce MOFs prepared by conventional solvothermal synthetic methods7,32. The lower surface areas of our samples may be attributed to the inefficient activated processes that result in the incomplete removal of solvent and unreacted precursors from the pores of samples. However, the obtained surface areas for all samples are significantly higher than those samples synthesized in water at RT24, which confirms the success of this method for the synthesis of UiO-66-Ce MOFs as well as the high quality of our prepared samples. Moreover, according to the obtained production yields (Table 1), it can be expected that the amount of synthesized samples increases with increasing the reaction time. Hence, this synthetic strategy opens a new way to synthesize this MOF on a large scale that is beneficial for its industrial applications. Additionally, the water-based RT method allows to increase the reaction time without the risk of Ce(IV) reduction.

N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms of synthesized Ce-MOFs (a) before and (b) after soaking in water for 10 days. (Green lines show desorption isotherms).

This analysis was also conducted on water-soaked samples to investigate the effect of water aging on the porosity of synthesized samples at different reaction times. As depicted in Figs. 3 and S1, it is obvious that there are no significant differences between the N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms of synthesized samples before and after soaking in water for 10 days, and water-soaked samples also exhibited type-I isotherms. However, the pore size distribution profiles of fresh and water-soaked samples indicated that some new mesopores formed in the structure of water-soaked samples (Figure S2). The formation of these mesopores in the structure of water-soaked samples may be attributed to the structural defect that is consistent with the previously reported results33.

The calculated surface areas of water-soaked samples ranged from 416 to 753 m2/g, indicating the reduction in the porosity of samples after soaking in water for 10 days. These reductions in the porosity of all synthesized samples may be due to the partial decomposition (Figure S3) and the formation of nonporous impurities like CeO2. The highest reduction in the BET surface area is related to the sample synthesized in 3 h (≈ 37%), mainly due to the presence of a higher structural defect in its structure, which results in the formation of pores with the largest diameter among other samples at higher reaction times (Table 1). This reduction in the surface area reduces with increasing the reaction times, confirming the structural stability of samples synthesized at higher reaction times.

Although the highest reduction in the BET surface area of these samples is more than 37%, their surface areas are still at relatively high values, suggesting their potential to be applied as stable adsorbents in wastewater treatment applications. Moreover, the formation of some larger pores in the structure of water-soaked samples is beneficial for water treatment applications. The presence of abundant empty spaces with larger sizes facilitates the mass transfer processes, accelerates the adsorption kinetics, and enhances the overall adsorption capacity of synthesized samples34. Therefore, it can be expected that not only the fresh samples but also the water-soaked samples showed good adsorption performance towards different pollutants.

TGA analysis

The thermal stability of synthesized samples at different reaction times and the effect of soaking in water on their thermal stability were studied using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). Figure 4 shows the TGA profiles of fresh and water-soaked samples at the temperature range of 25–700 ◦C under a nitrogen atmosphere. The TGA (Fig. 4a) and derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) (Figure S4a) profiles of all synthesized samples at different reaction times showed three obvious weight losses. The first weight loss occurred at below 100 ◦C, may be due to the evaporation of adsorbed water and acetone. The other weight loss occurred at above 150 ◦C, may be related to the evaporation of unexchanged DMF molecules. The main weight loss centered at around 560 ◦C, may be related to the complete decomposition of frameworks and the formation of CO, CO2, and CeO25,35.

The residual ash contents at 700 ◦C and the onset temperature (defined as the temperature at which the samples start to decompose) obtained from the TGA results are summarized in Table S1. It was found that the residual ash contents of synthesized samples at different reaction times are relatively increased with increasing the reaction time, which is consistent with their Ce contents (Table 1). More importantly, it was found that their onset temperatures as well as thermal stability increase with increasing the reaction time, indicating that a higher reaction time is better for the synthesis of highly thermal stable UiO-66-Ce MOFs. This observation may be due to the formation of MOFs with less structural defects as the reaction time increases. Moreover, it is obvious that the thermal stability of all synthesized samples decreased after soaking in water for 10 days.

Similar to fresh samples, the water-soaked samples also showed TGA profiles with three main weight loss. As shown in Fig. 4b, the first weight loss occurs at below 100 ◦C, while the second weight loss occurs at above 250 ◦C. This new weight loss at higher temperatures may be due to the presence of structural defects in the structure of water-soaked samples32,36. The other weight loss may be related to the complete decomposition of frameworks and the formation of CO, CO2, and CeO2. According to the TGA profiles and the data listed in Table S1, it can be reported that the sample synthesized in 18 h had the highest thermal stability and the lowest thermal stability reduction after soaking in water for 10 days, suggesting that 18 h is the optimum time for the synthesis of UiO-66-Ce MOFs. Moreover, it was found that the residual ash contents of water-soaked samples were slightly lower than those values measured for fresh ones. This observation may be due to the presence of structural defects (especially missing-node defects), which is consistent with the results obtained from the Ce content of water-soaked samples (Table 1). Accordingly, the lower Ce content of water-soaked samples results in the formation of lower residual ash contents of water-soaked samples (Table S1).

SEM analysis

The particle size, morphology, and change in the morphology of samples soaked in water were evaluated using scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis, in which the obtained images were depicted in Fig. 5. All synthesized samples at different reaction times have an irregular octahedral shape with extensive agglomeration with particle size in the range of 100–500 nm, similar to the results reported previously5,29. Moreover, it was found that the reaction time did not have an important effect on the morphology and particle size of the resulting samples. Furthermore, there is no obvious significant change in the particle size and morphology of water-soaked samples, further confirming the stability of these samples against water.

All in all, it can be inferred that the higher reaction time is better for the synthesis of highly stable UiO-66-Ce MOFs, and 18 h is the optimum time for this purpose. According to the characterization results of fresh and water-soaked samples, although the overall structure of samples was maintained unchanged during soaking in water. However, their microstructures changed and exhibited partial degradation, which led to lower structural stability (thermal and chemical stability) as well as lower surface area. These changes in the structure of synthesized samples may affect the adsorption performance of synthesized samples toward different pollutants. Therefore, in the following of this work, we study the effect of reaction time and soaking in water on the adsorption performance of synthesized samples.

Adsorption performance of synthesized samples

To investigate the effect of reaction time as well as soaking in water on the adsorption performances of synthesized adsorbents, different types of pollutants such as cationic organic dyes (e.g., MG and MB) and heavy metal ions (e.g., Cr(VI)) were used as model pollutants. As shown in Fig. 6a, it is obvious that all synthesized adsorbents at different reaction times exhibited good adsorption performances towards these pollutants, which are similar to or slightly better than previously reported adsorbent materials for removing these pollutants from water (Table 2). However, the adsorption capacities of synthesized UiO-66-Ce MOFs towards cationic MG dye are lower than those values reported for other adsorbents. This observation may be due to the large size of MG molecules. Accordingly, the MG molecules with relatively larger sizes compared to other studied pollutants cannot enter within the small pores of synthesized adsorbents and only adsorbed onto the surface of adsorbent via different adsorption mechanisms. Based on the previous studies, it can be expected that the zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs) and their derivatives that have relatively high BET surface areas are ideal adsorbents for the efficient removal of MG dye from water20,37.

Fig. 6Adsorption capacities of synthesized Ce-MOFs toward different pollutants (a) before and (b) after soaking in water for 10 days.

Adsorption capacities of synthesized Ce-MOFs toward different pollutants (a) before and (b) after soaking in water for 10 days.

It is clear that the synthesized UiO-66-Ce MOFs at different reaction times have the highest adsorption performance towards cationic MB dye compared to other pollutants. The higher adsorption capacities of MB over the synthesized adsorbents compared to the MG dye may be due to the larger size of MG (≈ 1.58 nm) than the MB (≈ 0.8 nm) dye. Accordingly, the small molecules of MB can enter within the small pores of adsorbents, while the MG molecules with larger sizes cannot penetrate within the small pores of adsorbents. Moreover, it was observed that the adsorption capacity of MB dye over these adsorbents increased with the increase in reaction time. This observation may be due to the increase of surface area as well as pore volume of adsorbents by increasing the reaction time. Accordingly, the sample synthesized in 18 h with the highest surface area (968 m2/g) and pore volume (0.6569 cm3/g) exhibited the highest adsorption capacity of 217 mg/g towards MB dye. A similar trend was also observed for the adsorption of Cr(VI) ion, in which its adsorption capacity over different adsorbents is in the range of 49–57 mg/g, which is comparable to the previously reported values (Table 2).

To better show the adsorption performance of synthesized MOFs in adsorbing both anionic and cationic dyes, adsorption of binary systems was also performed, in which the obtained results were shown in Figures S5 and S6. It is obvious that not only the fresh but also the water-soaked samples exhibited good adsorption performances towards both cationic and anionic dyes in the binary system. The good adsorption performance of UiO-66-Ce MOFs towards cationic organic dyes exhibited that electrostatic interaction between the cationic organic dyes and the negatively charged centers of adsorbents is one of the main adsorption mechanisms. To further confirm the presence of electrostatic interactions between cationic dyes and synthesized adsorbents, the adsorption performance of selected sample (the sample synthesized in 18 h) towards MB in different pHs was investigated. As shown in Figure S7a, it is obvious that the adsorption capacity of MB was gradually increased with increasing the solution pH and achieved the maximum value at pH ≈ 7.3. This observation may be due to increasing the electrostatic interactions between MB molecules and adsorbent particles, where their positive surface charge was continuously decreased with increasing the pH (Figure S7b).

Despite increasing the negative zeta potential of adsorbent in basic condition with increasing the pH, which results in more electrostatic interactions between adsorbent and adsorbate, but the adsorption capacity of MB reduces in this condition. This observation may be due to the partial collapse of frameworks and formation of amorphous phase38, which is consistent with the previously reported results related to the UiO-66 based MOFs35,39. It was reported that Cr(VI) ions exist as chromate species with a negative charge in water40. Thus, it can be expected that there are electrostatic repulsion interactions between these chromate ions (CrO42−) and the negatively charged MOFs. However, the synthesized adsorbents with negative zeta potential (Table 1), not only exhibited good adsorption performance towards Cr(VI) ions but also can adsorb both anionic and cationic dyes from the binary mixtures (Figure S5).

This observation indicates that other mechanisms beyond electrostatic interactions such as pore filling, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals interactions, and π-π stacking interactions play a critical role in the adsorption of such pollutants39. To determine the adsorption mechanisms of synthesized MOFs towards cationic MB and MG dyes, Attenuated Total Reflection Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy analysis was performed on the pure UiO-66-Ce MOF (sample synthesized in 18 h) and dyes-loaded samples. It is obvious from the ATR-FTIR spectra of MOFs after adsorption of MB (Fig. 7a) and MG (Fig. 7b) dyes, that the intensity of a broad peak in the range of 3200–3600 cm−1 was increased after dye adsorption. This change in the spectra of dye-loaded samples may be related to the formation of hydrogen bonds between the -N(CH3)2 groups of both dyes and the hydroxyl groups of Ce-oxo clusters, as schematically shown in Fig. 7c. Additionally, the observation of new peaks at 1330 and 885 cm−1 in spectra of MOFs after adsorption of dyes corresponding to the stretching vibrations of -C-N bonds of MB and MG dyes further shows the adsorption of these dyes onto the surface of UiO-66-Ce MOFs32.

Moreover, it is clear that the intensities of peak related to the C = C stretching vibrations are decreased after adsorption of both dyes, showing the formation of π-π interactions between the π-conjugated structure of both dyes and the benzene ring of UiO-66-Ce MOFs due to the presence of BDC in their structures41. Since the average pore diameters of synthesized adsorbents are relatively larger than the size of MB (≈ 0.8 nm) and MG (≈ 1.58 nm) dyes, pore filling may be another adsorption mechanism towards these dyes. Accordingly, as illustrated in Fig. 7c, it can be proposed that the synthesized UiO-66-Ce MOFs can adsorb both MB and MG dyes via different mechanisms including electrostatic interaction, hydrogen bonding, pore filling, and π-π stacking interactions.

ATR-FTIR spectra of UiO-66-Ce MOF synthesized in 18 h before and after adsorption of (a) MB and (b) MG dyes. (c) Schematic showing the possible mechanisms for adsorption of MB, MG, and Cr(VI) by the synthesized UiO-66-Ce MOFs.

Comparing the adsorption capacities of Cr(VI) ions on the synthesized MOFs before and after soaking in water for 10 days (Fig. 6b), it is obvious that the equilibrium adsorption capacities of Cr(VI) ions were significantly reduced after soaking in water, mainly due to the reduction in their surface areas and pore volumes. Accordingly, the Cr(VI) adsorption capacities of water-soaked samples are in the range of 24–30 mg/g, which is still at an acceptable value and is comparable to the previously reported values32. However, the opposite trend was observed for the adsorption capacities of MB and MG dyes over the synthesized adsorbents before and after soaking in water for 10 days. Accordingly, the adsorption capacities of both dyes over the water-soaked samples increased, in which their adsorption capacities towards MB and MG dyes were in the range of 173–254 and 164–183 mg/g, respectively. This strange observation may be due to the formation of new larger pores in the structure of water-soaked samples as a result of structural defects.

As reported in Table 1, the Ce content of synthesized MOFs was significantly reduced after being soaked in water for 10 days. This reduction in the Ce content may be due to the presence of missing-node defects. The presence of such defects as well as abundant empty spaces with larger sizes facilitate the mass transfer processes (especially for MG with larger sizes) and enhance the overall adsorption capacity of water-soaked samples. Therefore, it can be concluded that not only the fresh UiO-66-Ce MOFs but also the water-soaked samples showed good adsorption performances towards cationic dyes. Moreover, it is obvious from Figure S6, that the water-soaked samples can adsorb both anionic and cationic dyes from the binary mixtures, even better than the fresh MOFs, showing the applicability of these adsorbents for long-time wastewater treatment applications.

Conclusions

Herein, UiO-66-Ce MOFs at different reaction times (3–24 h) were synthesized via a green RT synthetic method, using water as a green solvent, NaClO4.H2O as salting-in species to increase the solubility of organic ligands in aqueous solutions, and acetone as an exchange solvent. The synthesized MOFs were characterized by different techniques to investigate the effect of reaction time on their crystallinity, porosity, thermal and chemical stability as well as adsorption properties. It was found that the higher reaction time is better for the synthesis of highly stable UiO-66-Ce MOFs, and 18 h is the optimum time for this purpose, which resulting in the formation of MOF with the highest surface area of 968 m2/g. All synthesized adsorbents at different reaction times exhibited good adsorption performances towards these pollutants, in which their adsorption capacities for MB, MG, and Cr(VI) were in the range of 132–217, 91–120, and 49–58 mg/g, respectively, that are comparable to the previously reported values. It was observed that the Cr(VI) adsorption capacity of synthesized MOFs was reduced after soaking them in water for 10 days, while their adsorption capacities towards cationic MB and MG dyes were significantly improved. Moreover, it was found that the water-soaked samples could adsorb both anionic and cationic dyes from the binary mixtures, even better than the fresh MOFs. Adsorption mechanism investigation revealed that the synthesized UiO-66-Ce MOFs can adsorb both MB and MG dyes via different mechanisms such as electrostatic interaction, hydrogen bonding, pore filling, and π-π stacking interactions. Finally, it can be expected that the synthesized UiO-66-Ce MOFs would be good adsorbent materials for long-time wastewater treatment applications.
