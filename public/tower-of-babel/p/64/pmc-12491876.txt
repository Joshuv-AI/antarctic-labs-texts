Multiple squamous cell carcinoma of the upper limb originating from long-standing burn scars: A case report
Authors: Nhat Tien Tran, Trong Lam Dinh, Hong Phuc Le, Nghi Thanh Nhan Le
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is one of the most prevalent skin malignancies, particularly among Caucasian populations, and represents approximately 20–30 % of all non-melanoma skin cancers [1,2]. Its global incidence is rising, posing a growing public health concern. Established risk factors for SCC include immunosuppression, chronic skin lesions, male sex, advanced age, genetic disorders, and environmental exposures such as ultraviolet radiation, ionizing radiation, and prior radiotherapy [3]. Notably, 2–6 % of chronic ulcers secondary to trauma or burns may undergo malignant transformation [4,5].

The malignant transformation of burn scars was first observed as early as the 1st century CE. In 1828, Jean-Nicolas Marjolin provided the first formal description of ulcers developing in scar tissue. In 1903, Da Costa introduced the term Marjolin's ulcer to denote malignancies, particularly SCC, arising from chronic burn scars [6]. SCC accounts for approximately 75 % of burn scar malignancies, followed by basal cell carcinoma and, rarely, melanoma [7,8]. The average latency from initial burn injury to SCC development is 20–40 years [5,7,9].

SCC arising from burn scars is generally more aggressive than de novo cutaneous SCC, with higher rates of local recurrence, lymph node metastasis, distant spread, and disease-specific mortality [5,7,10]. This unfavorable prognosis is linked to delayed detection, intrinsic tumor aggressiveness, and difficulties in achieving adequate surgical margins in scarred tissue [5,8,11]. Prevention emphasizes regular surveillance of burn scars and timely intervention for chronic non-healing ulcers or scars with suspicious change, rather than routine prophylactic excision of all scars [5,8,12]. Early excision with graft or flap coverage should be considered only for chronic, non-healing ulcers or scars that demonstrate early deterioration or suspicious changes [5,8,12]. Educating patients on self-monitoring for ulceration, rapid enlargement, or induration is equally important. Although the clinical course of burn scar–associated SCC is well documented, data on outcomes in patients with multiple lesions remain scarce. In non-burn SCC, patients with ≥3 lesions have been shown to face significantly higher risks of subsequent SCC, melanoma, and overall mortality compared to those with fewer lesions [13]; however, comparable evidence for post-burn SCC is lacking.

Herein, we present a rare case of multiple SCCs arising from a long-standing burn scar of the upper limb, highlighting the diagnostic challenges, surgical management, and need for vigilant follow-up. The case is reported in line with the SCARE Guidelines 2025 [14].

Case report

A 56-year-old Vietnamese male farmer, with no significant past medical history, no comorbidities such as diabetes or immunosuppression, a non-smoker, occasional alcohol use, and no family history of skin cancer, presented with progressively enlarging masses on the right upper extremity. He had sustained extensive burn injuries 40 years earlier from a gasoline explosion, affecting the right shoulder, arm, and elbow. Occupationally, he worked as a rice farmer, with moderate outdoor sun exposure but no evidence of chronic actinic damage elsewhere.

Six months prior to presentation, a non-healing ulcer appeared spontaneously at the right elbow without trauma or new burn injury. The lesion resembled his current tumor, consistent with features of a Marjolin-type ulcer. It was treated with excision and skin graft at another facility, but graft failure occurred within weeks, and no records of operative details were available.

Following grafting, two painless, well-demarcated masses developed—one at the right medial elbow (5 × 4 cm) and another in the axilla (3 × 2 cm) (Fig. 1A, B). Initial ultrasonography and fine-needle aspiration suggested benign dermoid cysts (Fig. 1C, D). MRI demonstrated heterogeneous lesions extending from the supraclavicular region to the elbow, with hypointense T1, mildly hyperintense T2, and peripheral enhancement (Fig. 2).

(A, B) Clinical appearance of two tumors at the medial elbow and axilla of the right upper limb, located over long-standing burn scars. (C) Ultrasonographic image of the medial elbow lesion. (D) Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) results initially suggest benign dermoid cysts.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the right upper limb demonstrating heterogeneous lesions extending from the supraclavicular region to the elbow. The lesions appear mildly hyperintense on T2-weighted sequences and exhibit peripheral enhancement after contrast administration. (A) Coronal view. (B) Sagittal view.

Surgery was performed by a senior surgeon with over 10 years of experience. Under general anesthesia with a combined brachial plexus block for postoperative analgesia, both masses were excised along with axillary lymph node dissection. Because cytology had indicated benign lesions, wide oncologic margins were not pursued. Intraoperative findings included dense scar tissue adherent to surrounding neurovascular structures.

Histopathology revealed well-differentiated SCC with metastasis in 6 of 7 lymph nodes (Fig. 3). No perineural invasion was seen, but tumor borders were infiltrative and margins were microscopically positive.

(A) Gross specimens of the excised tumors and axillary lymph nodes. (B) Histopathological image showing well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma and metastatic involvement of lymph nodes.

One month later, the patient developed painful, irregular recurrent masses at the previous sites and cervical lymphadenopathy. Given rapid recurrence, multifocality, nodal metastasis, and aggressive course, amputation of the right upper limb with cervical lymphadenectomy was performed, followed by adjuvant radiotherapy 4 weeks later (Fig. 4). Postoperative care included antibiotic prophylaxis, multimodal pain control, early physiotherapy for shoulder mobility, and psychological counseling. The patient reflected that he had never perceived his long-standing burn scars as a possible source of cancer. While the amputation represented a profound personal loss, he regarded it as a life-saving measure and hoped his experience would encourage others to seek prompt evaluation of chronic wounds. Discussion

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) developing in chronic burn scars, commonly referred to as Marjolin's ulcer, is consistently associated with more aggressive behavior and poorer prognosis compared to de novo cutaneous SCC. Numerous clinical series have reported higher rates of local recurrence, lymphatic spread, distant metastasis, and disease-specific mortality in post-burn SCC [2,[4], [5], [6], [7],[9], [10], [11], [12],15,16]. This poorer outcome is primarily attributed to delayed diagnosis, aggressive tumor biology arising from the chronic inflammatory milieu, and the technical difficulty of achieving oncologic margins in fibrotic scar tissue [[4], [5], [6], [7],9,11,12].

Malignant transformation is driven by chronic inflammation, characterized by persistent infiltration of immune cells, overproduction of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α), and reactive oxygen species, which promote DNA damage and genomic instability [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Hypoxia in avascular scar tissue induces HIF-1α expression, fostering angiogenesis and an immunosuppressive microenvironment, while poor lymphatic drainage impairs immune surveillance [5,7]. NF-κB and STAT3 signaling activation support keratinocyte survival and proliferation [5,6,8]. Chronic burn scars are rigid, poorly vascularized, and prone to repetitive ulceration due to sensory loss and reduced elasticity, perpetuating an injury–repair cycle that increases mutagenic risk [5]. The typical latency to malignancy is 20–40 years, though shorter in immunocompromised patients [6,9,11]. While most cases are sporadic, mutations in TP53, RAS, and defects in DNA repair pathways suggest a genetic component [7]. Our patient had no family history of skin cancer, implying that environmental and local tissue factors predominated.

While single-lesion post-burn SCC already carries a poor prognosis, multiple synchronous lesions—as in our case—are even rarer and may be associated with higher recurrence and metastasis rates. This observation aligns with broader cSCC literature, where multifocal disease is linked to more aggressive tumor biology, shorter disease-free intervals, and increased mortality [4,5,9,11,13,15,16]. Potential mechanisms include cumulative effects of chronic inflammation across multiple scar sites, field cancerization phenomena, and impaired local immune surveillance. Predictors of recurrence in burn-scar SCC include positive/close margins, multifocal disease, nodal metastasis, poor differentiation, perineural/lymphovascular invasion, and delayed diagnosis [[4], [5], [6],9,12]. In our patient, microscopically positive margins, multifocality, and nodal disease likely drove early relapse within one month.

Prevention should prioritize vigilant surveillance and timely intervention, not prophylactic excision of all scars [[4], [5], [6],9,12]. Key measures include patient education about warning signs; regular follow-up for unstable or repeatedly breaking scars; and early biopsy of any non-healing ulcer or suspicious change [[2], [3], [4],6,9]. Chronic wounds should be definitively excised and covered with grafts or flaps, and non-invasive imaging (dermoscopy, in vivo confocal microscopy) may aid early detection in high-risk scars [6,7,17]. For confirmed SCC, wide local excision with histologically negative margins remains standard; regional lymph node dissection is indicated when nodal metastasis is present [[3], [4], [5], [6],18,19].

In conclusion, SCC arising from chronic burn scars represents a distinct clinicopathologic entity driven by sustained inflammation, hypoxia, genomic instability, and impaired immune surveillance. Optimal management requires high clinical suspicion, early histopathological confirmation of suspicious lesions, complete excision with negative margins, and structured long-term surveillance to minimize recurrence and improve patient outcomes.
