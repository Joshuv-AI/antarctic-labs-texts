Improved Post‐Processing Framework for Whole Slide Imaging and 3D Reconstruction
Authors: Emily L De Leon Alvarado, Daryan Chitsaz, Ilana R Leppert, Jennifer S W Campbell, Yangyingqi Zhou, G Bruce Pike, Timothy E Kennedy, Christine L Tardif, Christopher D Rowley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhole slide imaging (WSI) enables high‐resolution digitization of histological sections, but 3D reconstruction from serial slides remains hindered by illumination artifacts, tile inconsistencies, and section‐specific geometric distortions. We present an enhanced reconstruction framework that addresses both tile‐level intensity inhomogeneity and instability in deformable registration. First, we introduce an overlap‐aware illumination correction that refines an initial flat‐field estimate by adding an intensity equality constraint in overlapping tile regions. Second, intensity normalization of sections was performed before reconstruction to ensure consistent intensity profiles among serial sections. Lastly, we incorporate level‐set method (LSM) constraints into an existing volumetric reconstruction framework to stabilize alignment. The framework was developed for marmoset brain histology, including within‐specimen blockface photography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The LSM reconstruction was validated using a synthetic banana image with a known initial shape and with mouse histology reconstructed using an external 3D MRI reference. Overlap‐aware correction in the marmoset WSI significantly reduced residual gridding artifacts across 112 sections (Friedman χ 2(2) = 890.04, p < 2.2 × 10−16). Within the marmoset dataset, intensity normalization reduced inter‐slice intensity variability (coefficient of variation: 0.392 to 0.246) and incorporation of LSM consistently improved registration accuracy by approximately 10% in Normalized Mutual Information scores and 3%–5% in Structural Similarity Index Measure. LSM also yielded improved structural correspondence in both gray matter and white matter structures.Keywords: image reconstruction, level‐set method, tile stitching, vignette correction, whole slide imaging

Introduction

Whole slide imaging (WSI) has become a crucial modality in modern histopathology, enabling the digital conversion of tissue on glass slides into high‐resolution virtual slides with magnifications ranging from 2× to 100×, for the entire tissue section (Higgins 2015; Kumar et al. 2020). Beyond facilitating precise, large‐scale visualization, WSI supports the reconstruction of three‐dimensional (3D) volumes from serial histological sections, providing new opportunities to study the microstructural organization of complex tissues such as the brain, liver, lung, cervix, and breast (Bongaerts et al. 2018; Deutsch et al. 2025; Ghaznavi et al. 2013; Melo et al. 2019; Tong et al. 2019).

3D reconstructions from serial WSI are challenged by structural artifacts introduced during tissue preparation and imaging. Global 3D deformations arise from tissue extraction and processing, including shrinkage due to dehydration and fixation or distortions from mechanical stress and embedding (Al‐Kinani 2024). Sectioning and mounting introduce slice‐specific 2D deformations such as tearing, folding, shearing, and local warping (Al‐Kinani 2024). These morphological alterations are compounded by illumination variability (e.g., vignetting) and misalignment between tiles or serial sections, all of which hinder accurate spatial correspondence (Al‐Kinani 2024). Accurate quantitative analysis of whole slide images is critical for applications including feature extraction, tissue classification and automated cellular quantification (Hanna et al. 2020).

Raw microscopy data exhibits spatial pixel intensity inhomogeneities that can lead to stitching errors, intensity discontinuities, and compromise downstream analysis. To address these artifacts, several image correction methods have been developed, including corrected intensity distributions using regularized energy minimization (CIDRE) (Smith et al. 2015), BaSiC (Background and Shading Correction of Optical Microscopy Images) (Peng et al. 2017), and Vignetted Stitched‐Tile Adjustment using Morphological Adaptive Processing (VISTAmap) (Fung et al. 2025). However, these approaches can present reduced accuracy when few tiles are available or where tile‐specific correction may be required (Coster et al. 2014; Likar et al. 2000; Singh et al. 2014; Smith et al. 2015).

For stitching WSI tiles to create a single image, tools such as the ImageStitching Plugin in Fiji (Preibisch et al. 2009) and, more recently, ASHLAR (Alignment by Simultaneous Harmonization of Layer/Adjacency Registration) (Muhlich et al. 2022) have been developed, with ASHLAR specifically addressing residual misalignment from conventional tile‐stitching approaches (Chalfoun et al. 2017) (Zukić et al. 2021).

Histological 3D reconstruction methods have progressed from a simple sequential alignment to a reference section (Kiessling and Pichler 2010), which was prone to cumulative distortions such as the z‐shift effect (Yushkevich, Avants, et al. 2006). Without a global anatomical reference, some brain reconstruction frameworks use a pairwise‐only approach with iterative refinement (Woodward et al. 2018). More advanced frameworks integrate WSI with blockface photographs and 3D anatomical references from modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), using affine and nonlinear transformations to achieve anatomically faithful reconstructions (Adler et al. 2014; Amunts et al. 2013; Chakravarty et al. 2006; Choe et al. 2011; Dauguet et al. 2009, 2007). Multimodal strategies such as the Possum Framework (Majka and Wojcik 2016) integrate multiple modalities into a unified spatial system and explicitly identify tissue tears and severe displacements as preprocessing challenges that must be addressed before reconstruction.

Deformable registration of serial histological sections is an ill‐posed problem that can be constrained by incorporating information about tissue boundaries to guide the registration. The level‐set method (LSM) is a numerical technique that embeds surface information into a signed distance function, representing surfaces implicitly as level‐sets to capture complex geometric and topological changes. This method has been previously used to embed surface‐based structural information in rigid body reconstruction of a rat brain (Kozinska et al. 1997) and to improve cortical surface alignment in human MRI brain images (Tardif et al. 2015). Motivated by the instability of unconstrained deformable registration in regions with weak anatomical contrast, we leverage LSM to introduce anatomically informed surface constraints that stabilize and guide the deformable alignment of serial histological sections.

This study presents an enhanced framework for 3D histological image reconstruction that addresses both tile‐level intensity inconsistencies and section‐level geometric distortions. We first introduce an overlap‐aware illumination correction for WSI that enables tile‐specific refinement while preserving global consistency. We then integrate LSM‐based anatomical constraints into the nonlinear registration stage of the Possum framework, stabilizing deformable alignment and mitigating common histological artifacts. Together, these advances aim to enhance reconstruction robustness and achieve more faithful restoration of native tissue morphology in 3D histological image reconstruction.

Materials

The animal tissue used for this study was obtained in accordance with the guidelines of the Canadian Council for Animal Care and approved by the McGill University Animal Care Committee.

Marmoset Imaging

This study utilized in vivo MRI and histological data from a single adult female common marmoset ( Callithrix jacchus (Linnaeus, 1758)) brain. Scanning was performed on a 3T‐PrismaFit scanner (Siemens, Germany) with a 16‐channel marmoset RF coil (Hori et al. 2018) (Takashima Seisakusho Co./Rogue Research Inc.). A T 1‐weighted anatomical MRI scan was acquired using a magnetization prepared rapidly acquired gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: flip angle = 8°, TR = 2200 ms, TI = 706 ms, isotropic resolution of 0.3 × 0.3 × 0.3 mm3, two averages, with a total scan time of 13:31 min per acquisition. This was acquired at five different scan sessions over a three‐month period and averaged together. After the final MRI session, the brain was perfusion‐fixed, extracted, and placed in 4% paraformaldehyde, then cryosectioned in the coronal plane at 20 μm thickness. To match the resolution of the highest‐resolution MRI scan, 1 in every 15 sections was retained, resulting in 300 μm spacing between sections. During sectioning, blockface images were captured to facilitate accurate reconstruction and alignment. Sections were co‐labeled using a Hoechst 33,342 dye and Myelin Basic Protein, Tubulin Beta Class III, and Microtubule‐Associated Protein 2 antibodies (immunolabeling protocol detailed in Chitsaz et al. (2025)). WSI was performed using an ImageXpress High‐Content Imaging System with a 0.4 NA 10 × objective (Molecular Devices). Owing to variability in tissue section size, each histological section comprised between 2 and 256 image tiles, each acquired at a resolution of 0.67 μm/pixel and image size ranging from 11194 × 9380 to 18521 × 27673 pixels.

Synthetic Data: Banana

A T 2‐weighted MR image of a curved banana with an isotropic voxel resolution of 1 mm3 was obtained from The Possum Framework (Majka and Wojcik 2016). This image was reconstructed to provide a synthetic, structured test object for visualizing alignment accuracy and identifying spatial distortions. The Possum Framework provides both a reference shape and a corresponding distorted version of the banana dataset (https://github.com/pmajka/poSSum/tree/master). To generate a distorted banana dataset with known transformation files, we performed a slice‐wise 2D rigid and affine registration between the distorted (fixed) and reference (moving) volumes. The resulting transformations, capturing translations, rotations, scaling, and shearing, created synthetically distorted sections. These were restacked into a 3D moving volume for reconstruction.

Mouse Data

To assess whether the proposed reconstruction framework generalizes to histological data that lacks a same‐specimen 3D reference volume, 10 parvalbumin‐stained histological sections from the Developmental Mouse Brain Atlas (DeMBA) (Bjerke et al. 2023) were reconstructed. A publicly available ex vivo T 2*‐weighted MRI dataset of a C57BL/6J mouse brain (Ma et al. 2005, 2008) was used as the anatomical reference volume. The MRI acquisition protocol is detailed in (Ma et al. 2005). Briefly, imaging was performed on a formalin‐fixed C57BL/6J mouse brain using a 3D gradient echo sequence on a 17.6T magnet with the following parameters: 47 μm resolution, TR = 150 ms, TE = 7.5 ms, flip angle = 25°. The histological and MRI datasets were obtained from the EBRAINS platform (https://search.kg.ebrains.eu/instances/0c2adfec‐6225‐400b‐b438‐c52ae7fbde2f) and NeuroImaging Tools & Resources Collaboratory (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/c57bl_mr_atlas/), respectively.

Methods

Marmoset Whole Side Histology Vignette Correction and Tile Stitching

Tiles from all sections were first processed using BaSiC to estimate a specific flat‐field correction, which served as an initial baseline for subsequent section‐ and tile‐specific refinement. Full details of the tile‐level vignette correction are provided in Figure A1. Briefly, to improve local intensity consistency, overlapping regions between neighboring tiles were used to derive tile‐specific corrections. Processing was performed on a section‐by‐section basis, with the BaSiC flat‐field approximated by a fourth‐degree polynomial in both spatial dimensions for each tile. Pixel intensities within overlapping regions were then incorporated into a weighted least‐squares regression to refine the polynomial coefficients, enforcing agreement between overlapping tiles after correction. The BaSiC flat‐field estimate was weighted to 80% while the overlap consistency was weighted to 20%. To reduce computational burden, every 200th pixel was sampled for fitting, enabling execution on a desktop workstation with 64 GB of RAM. Algorithm performance was quantified using the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) computed between downsampled overlapping pixels in the log‐intensity domain across each section. Differences between methods were assessed using a Friedman chi‐squared test, followed by post hoc paired Wilcoxon tests with Holm correction.

Following intensity correction, tiles were stitched into a composite WSI using ASHLAR, producing reconstructed sections with pixels with a length of 0.67 μm.

Section Intensity Normalization

Intensity normalization of the serial sections was performed prior to reconstruction to reduce section‐to‐section intensity variations. Each histological slice was first denoised using a 2D 8 × 8 pixel median filter. Tissue‐specific masks for gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) were manually segmented in ITK‐SNAP (Yushkevich, Piven, et al. 2006) and used to compute the median intensity for each tissue class. Slice‐wise correction maps were calculated by dividing each pixel by its corresponding tissue median intensity. To prevent overcorrection near tissue boundaries, the tissue masks were dilated, and peripheral regions were assigned a neutral correction factor. A smooth 2D polynomial surface was subsequently fitted to each correction map to enforce spatial smoothness. The effectiveness of intensity normalization was quantified using the coefficient of variation (CV) of voxel intensities within tissue regions across all slices.

Image Pre‐Processing

Prior to the image registration steps for the marmoset histological images, tile images in TIFF format (~1 GB each) underwent quality control. Quality control involved the removal of unusable slices and replacing them with neighboring sections when necessary. Full‐resolution images were downsampled by a factor of 100 and converted to PNG format to preserve the alpha channel. All image registrations were performed using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) (Avants et al. 2008, 2011; Tustison et al. 2021). Landmark posts embedded in the blockface images facilitated registration. All posts were masked and aligned to a single masked reference post to ensure consistent imaging planes. After background removal, the transformation files from post‐registration were applied to the blockface images, converted to grayscale, and cropped to remove excess background.

Image Reconstruction Workflow

The reconstruction pipeline follows the established Possum framework (Majka and Wojcik 2016; Yushkevich, Piven, et al. 2006; Yushkevich, Avants, et al. 2006) and consists of a multi‐stage registration strategy designed to balance global anatomical fidelity with local alignment accuracy. First, each histological slice is affinely registered to its corresponding reference slice (pairwise registration). Second, sequential registration to neighboring slices refines local alignment. Third, a coarse‐to‐fine step combines the high‐frequency components of the sequential transformations with the pairwise alignment, preserving global brain geometry while maintaining local edge structure.

The marmoset T 1‐weighted anatomical MRI was first rigidly and then affinely registered in 3D to the blockface volume to align the reference anatomy with the blockface cutting plane (overview in Figure A2). Blockface reconstruction proceeded using the coarse‐to‐fine registration strategy. Nonlinear registration was intentionally omitted to avoid introducing geometric distortions inherent to MRI, thereby preserving the anatomical fidelity of the blockface volume to which the labeled section images will better align. The reconstructed blockface volume subsequently served as the reference space for histological reconstruction.

Integration of LSM Into the Possum Framework

The Possum Framework concludes with a deformable registration stage to correct residual local distortions. To improve robustness and anatomical plausibility during deformable registration, LSM‐based constraints were incorporated directly into the deformable registration. Key anatomical structures, such as the full tissue (GM + WM) and WM regions (Figure 1B), were manually segmented in ITK‐SNAP for both moving and fixed images. The resulting masks for each region of interest (Figure 1C,D) were used to compute level‐set maps (Figure 1E,F) using SimpleITK (Lowekamp et al. 2013). In these maps, the surface boundary is defined by the zero‐level set, interior values are negative, and exterior values are positive.

Generation of level‐set maps from histological sections. (A) Downsampled Hoechst 33342‐labeled section showing annotated key structures. (B) Manual white matter (WM) segmentation is outlined in blue, and full tissue (GM + WM) in orange. (C, D). Binary masks of the full tissue (GM + WM) and WM. (E, F) Corresponding level‐set representations. This workflow converts binary masks into level‐set maps for subsequent analysis.

These distance‐based representations encode both surface geometry and spatial context. The deformable registration step used a composite similarity metric combining mutual information (MI) between the moving and fixed image intensities and cross‐correlation (CC) between the corresponding level‐set representations. In contrast, the standard Possum framework relies solely on MI between fixed and moving image intensities. The inclusion of level‐set constraints provides explicit anatomical guidance, reducing instability in regions with weak tissue contrast.

The synthetic banana MRI, histological mouse and marmoset serial sections were processed using a modified Possum reconstruction pipeline to include LSM. Level‐set generation was adapted to each dataset. For the synthetic banana dataset, level‐set maps were computed from manually drawn segmentations of the full object and the internal structure (excluding the peel). For both the marmoset and mouse datasets, level‐set maps were computed from manually drawn segmentations of the full tissue (GM + WM) and WM regions.

Registration performance was evaluated using complementary similarity metrics. Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) was used to assess intermodal alignment by quantifying statistical dependence between image intensity distributions, with higher values indicating improved correspondence. Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) quantified preservation of structural information based on local luminance, contrast, and spatial patterns, with values approaching 1 indicating stronger similarity. Both 2D and 3D variants of NMI and SSIM were computed, where 2D metrics represent slice‐wise averages and 3D metrics account for relative slice size. For intra‐modality comparisons, RMSE was calculated to quantify pixel‐wise intensity agreement, with lower values indicating improved alignment.

Results

Improved Tile Stitching and Vignette Correction Through Overlap‐Aware Methods

Overlap‐aware correction significantly reduced residual shading and tile gridding across histological sections. A Friedman test on RMSE in overlapping regions (112 sections each with four fluorescent labels provides 448 measurements) revealed a significant effect of correction method (χ 2(2) = 890.04, p < 2.2e‐16), with post hoc Wilcoxon tests showing differences between all pairs (p < 2.2e‐16). These improvements are illustrated in Figure 2, which highlights cases with few, intermediate, and many tiles.

BaSiC substantially improved shading in the raw data across all sections, while the overlap‐aware correction further reduced residual tile gridding under sparse coverage. When few tiles were available, the overlap‐aware method mitigated apparent gridding artifacts, though some residual effects remained due to its weighting toward the BaSiC solution (Figure 2A). With an intermediate number of tiles, the overlap‐aware correction successfully removed tile gridding in cases where BaSiC alone was insufficient (Figure 2B). For larger image sections containing many overlapping tiles, the overlap‐aware method performed comparably to BaSiC, effectively correcting shading artifacts within imaging tiles (Figure 2C).

Homogenous Histological Sections Through Normalization

Application of the normalization procedure reduced intensity variability across slices (Figure 3). Specifically, the averaged CV within sections decreased from 0.392 in the unnormalized images to 0.246 after normalization, indicating a more homogeneous volume and effective removal of inter‐slice intensity differences. Residual variation is expected because tissue classes exhibit inherent intensity differences.

Enhanced Alignment of Histology to Blockface Using LSM‐Constrained Registration

Incorporating LSM constraints into the deformable registration stage improved alignment of histological sections to blockface images. Qualitative assessment revealed that, relative to the coarse‐to‐fine baseline, the LSM‐integrated framework reduced local misalignment and better preserved the anatomical boundaries of GM and WM. Quantitatively, 2D/3D NMI increased from 0.4470/0.3929 to 0.4946/0.4301, and SSIM increased from 0.8022/0.7220 to 0.8297/0.7582, corresponding to improvements of approximately 10% in NMI and 3%–5% in SSIM. In contrast, the deformable method without LSM achieved more modest gains (NMI: ~3%; SSIM: ~0%–2%). These effects are illustrated in Figures 4 and 5 and summarized in Table 1.

LSM‐Integrated Reconstruction in Banana Dataset

Incorporating LSM into the deformable registration stage improved anatomical continuity and structural fidelity in the reconstructed banana MRI volume. Compared with the standard Possum framework (without LSM), the LSM‐integrated approach reduced local misalignment and better preserved the internal structure of the synthetic object. These improvements are illustrated in Figure 7. Quantitative evaluation is summarized in Table A1. The LSM‐integrated framework achieved an RMSE of 1.676%, compared with 1.971% without LSM. Relative to the coarse‐to‐fine reconstruction baseline (2.286%), this corresponds to a 26.7% improvement. When compared directly with the deformable without LSM, the LSM‐integrated method shows a 15.0% improvement in RMSE. These improvements are further validated through absolute error maps (Figure 7H) between deformable with LSM (Figure 7F) and deformable without LSM (Figure 7G). These maps demonstrate reduced intensity differences when LSM constraints are applied, particularly in regions of high curvature.

LSM‐Integrated Framework Enhances Cross‐Specimen 3D Histological Reconstruction

Using unmatched MRI and histology from different mouse brains, the incorporation of LSM into the deformable registration stage substantially improved cross‐specimen 3D histological alignment, stabilizing the deformation and improving correspondence of pial boundaries and WM regions. Relative to the coarse‐to‐fine baseline, the LSM‐integrated framework increased 2D/3D NMI from 0.3010/0.2821 to 0.3462/0.3238 and SSIM from 0.4993/0.4914 to 0.5105/0.5007, corresponding to gains of ~14% in NMI and ~2% in SSIM. In comparison, the deformable method without level‐sets produced more modest improvements (NMI: 0.3042/0.2852; SSIM: 0.4999/0.4923). Discussion

In this work, we presented an integrated workflow combining vignette tile correction, slice normalization, and level‐set–guided deformable registration to improve the 3D reconstruction of serial histological sections. Together, these components address common sources of error in histological reconstruction, including spatial intensity inhomogeneity within tiles, variability between sections, and instability in deformable registration. Across multiple validation experiments, including synthetic datasets, blockface registration, and cross‐specimen reconstruction, the proposed framework improved anatomical continuity and structural correspondence, yielding consistent gains in RMSE, NMI, and SSIM.

Microscopy data frequently exhibit spatial intensity inhomogeneities that can introduce stitching artifacts, intensity discontinuities, and errors in downstream analysis (Leong et al. 2003). Existing approaches, including low‐rank shading models and methods such as BaSiC (Peng et al. 2017), provide effective correction when sufficient tile overlap is available but may be limited under sparse tile coverage or when tile‐specific corrections are required (Smith et al. 2015). Our results demonstrate that overlap‐aware vignette correction effectively reduces residual gridding artifacts across varying tile counts. When many tiles were present, the method performed comparably to BaSiC, while under sparse coverage, it provided improved correction by explicitly leveraging overlap information between neighboring tiles. These findings suggest that incorporating overlap information provides a robust and adaptable approach for shading correction in whole‐slide imaging, improving both shading uniformity and tile alignment across diverse acquisition settings.

Inter‐section intensity variability is another major challenge in serial histology reconstruction. Differences in section thickness, stain penetration, fixation quality, and slide preparation can produce large variations in overall intensity between slices (Al‐Kinani 2024). Without appropriate normalization, these variations can bias similarity metrics used during registration, causing algorithms to interpret intensity differences as structural discrepancies and ultimately degrading alignment accuracy (Maes et al. 1997). Conventional global normalization approaches standardize overall intensity distributions but may compress biologically meaningful contrast between tissue compartments. In contrast, the proposed approach normalizes GM and WM independently using their respective median intensities, preserving intrinsic tissue contrast while reducing inter‐section variability. Modeling the correction factor with a low‐order polynomial further enforces spatial smoothness, allowing macroscopic intensity variation to be corrected while maintaining fine anatomical detail.

To address geometric distortions that arise during sectioning and mounting, we introduced a deformable registration framework incorporating LSM as structural constraints. Across all datasets, integrating LSM into the deformable stage consistently improved performance relative to both the coarse‐to‐fine baseline and deformable approaches without LSM guidance. In the marmoset histology‐to‐blockface experiment, LSM integration increased both 2D and 3D similarity metrics and visually stabilized deformations in regions prone to local misalignment. In the synthetic banana dataset, inclusion of LSM reduced RMSE, indicating improved structural continuity and reduced local misalignment. Similarly, in the mouse histology reconstruction, LSM‐guided registration improved NMI and SSIM, reflecting better correspondence of pial and WM boundaries. These results suggest that augmenting deformable registration with structural constraints derived from level sets provides a consistent and meaningful improvement in alignment accuracy.

An advantage of this framework is its flexibility in incorporating reference information. While optimal results are expected when the reference image originates from the same specimen, such as blockface imaging or MRI, the method can also operate using references from different subjects or modalities, including computed tomography (CT). In this study, level‐set maps were generated from manually segmented masks, but this step could also be implemented using automated segmentation tools or existing anatomical atlases. Consequently, the approach is not limited to histological brain reconstruction but may apply to other serial imaging datasets where distortions must be corrected.

Three‐dimensional reconstruction from serial histological sections is an increasingly active area of research, with large‐scale initiatives such as BigBrain (Amunts et al. 2013) and BigMac (Howard et al. 2022), and maps of cyto‐ and fiber architecture of the human brain (Alkemade et al. 2022). This work also spans region‐specific reconstructions of structures including the hippocampus (Adler et al. 2014), basal ganglia and thalamus (Chakravarty et al. 2006), and non‐human primate brains such as the owl monkey (Choe et al. 2011) and baboon (Dauguet et al. 2007). Other studies have applied similar reconstruction approaches to non‐neural tissues, including prostate (Gibson et al. 2013) and liver (Song et al. 2013). While these efforts demonstrate the feasibility of high‐resolution 3D reconstruction, many rely on dataset‐specific processing pipelines, extensive manual intervention, or carefully controlled acquisition protocols. As a result, generalizability across tissues, staining protocols, and experimental conditions remains a persistent challenge. By incorporating level‐set constraints within the deformable stage, the framework presented here provides a structurally guided mechanism to stabilize reconstruction without relying on extensive manual landmark placement or dataset‐specific parameter tuning. Importantly, the ability to incorporate level‐set information within defined regions of interest allows the method to extend beyond whole‐brain reconstruction to organ‐specific applications.

Limitations

Several limitations of the proposed framework warrant consideration. The overlap‐aware intensity correction relies on the presence of sufficient and accurately registered overlap regions between tiles. When overlap is minimal, tiling geometry is irregular, or tile positions are inaccurately estimated, the correction may become under‐constrained or propagate boundary inconsistencies across tiles. In addition, the method assumes that illumination inhomogeneity varies smoothly across the field of view. While appropriate for vignetting and large‐scale shading effects, this assumption may limit the correction of abrupt illumination changes or tile‐specific acquisition artifacts.

The tissue‐class‐specific intensity normalization reduces inter‐slice variability but currently relies on manually generated segmentations, introducing additional labor and potential observer‐dependent variability. Although automated segmentation methods could mitigate this limitation, they were not evaluated in this study. Furthermore, the normalization assumes relatively consistent intra‐class intensity distributions across sections. While this stabilizes similarity metrics and improves alignment robustness, it may attenuate subtle biologically meaningful contrast variations in regions with heterogeneous staining or pathology.

Finally, although LSM provides a strong structural constraint during deformable registration, it cannot fully compensate for distortions inherent to histological processing. When external reference atlases are used, anatomical differences between specimens may introduce bias, particularly in regions with substantial inter‐subject variability. Optimal performance is therefore expected when specimen‐specific references, such as blockface imaging or same‐subject MRI, are available. Acquiring a post‐mortem MRI prior to tissue processing may further reduce registration bias by providing a subject‐specific reference that captures fixation‐ and shrinkage‐related distortions before histological sectioning. Additionally, deformable registration cannot reconstruct missing tissue or fully correct severe artifacts such as tears, folds, or compression introduced during sectioning.

In this study, we presented an integrated framework for 3D histological reconstruction that combines overlap‐aware tile correction, tissue‐specific intensity normalization, and LSM‐guided deformable registration. Across synthetic, single‐subject, and cross‐specimen datasets, the proposed workflow improved structural fidelity, volumetric alignment, and inter‐slice intensity consistency relative to conventional approaches. By providing a robust and flexible reconstruction pipeline, this framework enables reproducible 3D reconstruction of serial histological data and supports downstream quantitative analysis across tissues, modalities, and species.

Design Matrix Construction and Weighted Least Squares Estimation: To estimate all tile‐specific coefficients jointly, we construct a single linear system encompassing both the global BaSiC constraint and the overlap consistency constraints.

First, a global shading field Sx,y is estimated using BaSiC across all tiles and sections for a given stain, restricted to regions containing tissue. For each pixel location, the polynomial basis functions are evaluated to populate the design matrix. If all pixels were included, the resulting matrix would have dimensions Np×15Nt, where Np is the total number of pixels across all tiles. The matrix is block‐diagonal in structure, with nonzero entries for tile i occupying columns 15i−1+1 through 15i. The corresponding response vector contains the values of logSx,y, replicated across tiles.

To incorporate overlap constraints, additional rows are appended to the design matrix for pixels in overlapping regions Ωij. Additionally, a Gaussian smoothing kernel with a standard deviation of 1.5 pixels is applied to the images prior to sampling, reducing the influence of high‐frequency noise on the overlap consistency constraint.

As the global BaSiC constraint contributes substantially more rows to the system than the overlap constraint, the resulting fit is naturally biased toward the global solution. To balance these contributions, we apply weighted least squares regression, assigning 80% of the total weight to the BaSiC‐derived global constraint and 20% to the overlap consistency constraint. All tile‐specific polynomial coefficients are then estimated simultaneously via weighted linear regression.

With tile‐specific coefficients determined, correction maps are generated by evaluating the polynomial fix,y at each pixel location within tile i. These correction maps are subtracted from the log‐transformed measured images. Finally, the corrected images are exponentiated to return them to the original intensity domain. This takes approximately 3 min for a 10 × 11 tile image, with each tile possessing 2048 × 2048 pixels.
