Effect of lacto-fermented pickled liquids on physicochemical, microbial and sensory quality attributes of grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon Idella) fillets during cold storage
Authors: Amani Mohedein Mohammed Ahmed, Binaka Prabashini Dasanayaka, Zhe Jia, Abeer Noman, Shiyuan Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGrass carp, a key aquaculture species in China known for its rapid growth and nutritional value, faces challenges in fillet preservation due to oxidative and microbial degradation during storage. This study evaluated different mixtures of lacto-fermented pickle liquids (LFPLs) (A, B, C, and D; complete formulation in the Methods section) as natural treatments to enhance the physicochemical, microbial, and sensory quality of grass carp fillets under cold storage. LFPL treatments demonstrated strong antioxidant activity, significantly decreasing thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) values by 24.35 %, 26.96 %, 46.96 %, and 54.78 % for treatments A, B, C, and D, respectively. Additionally, peroxide values decreased by 44.69 %, 43.60 %, 46.80 %, and 49.79 % for treatments A, B, C, and D. Carbonyl content decreased by 25.37 %, 28.82 %, 36.25 %, and 44.04 %, while the sulfhydryl content for treatments A, B, C, and D increased significantly by 9.26 %, 10.30 %, 16.27 %, and 34.14 %, respectively. Furthermore, LFPLs reduced total volatile basic nitrogen (TVBN) by 47.56 %, 49.94 %, 52.38 %, and 52.66 % for treatments A, B, C, and D, while also preserving organoleptic properties, improving texture, reducing cooking loss, suppressing microbial growth, and maintaining higher whiteness. Principal component analysis revealed that LFPL-treated fillets after 12 days of storage had properties similar to control samples after 6 days. Overall, LFPLs serve as novel preservatives and natural antioxidants, improving the quality and shelf life of grass carp fillets during cold storage.Keywords: LFPL, Phenolic acid, Lipid oxidation, Novel preservative, Natural antioxidant

Introduction

The grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella) is a major aquaculture species in China, accounting 21.77 % of freshwater fish production. Statistical data indicate that between 2010 and 2022, China's annual grass carp harvest increased from 4.22 to 5.9 million tons (Zhu et al., 2024). Owing to desirable characteristics, such as rapid growth, high nutritional value, and cost-effective production methods, grass carp exhibits traits that make it particularly suitable for aquaculture and human consumption (Sun et al., 2017). Currently, grass carp is predominantly marketed as fresh or frozen products. However, fish meat has a high-water content, abundant nutrients, and a neutral pH value making it highly perishable (Lu et al., 2010). Previous research has revealed that microbial activity is a significant factor contributing to the degradation of quality and protein in fish products (Tan et al., 2023). In addition, oxidative processes that deteriorate lipids and proteins are critical factors effecting the quality of aquatic products during storage (Hematyar et al., 2019). These degradation mechanisms highlight the urgent need for effective preservation strategies in fish processing. Without intervention, microbial growth and oxidative damage can severely compromise the quality and safety of fish, particularly grass carp fillets. Therefore, the development of novel preservation approaches is essential to mitigate these risks.

Recently, many studies have explored the use of natural preservatives, such as essential oils, rosemary extract, and tea polyphenols, to enhance fish quality and extend shelf life (Huang et al., 2018; Li et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2021). These natural compounds effectively inhibit microbial growth and reduce oxidative damage, thereby maintaining the quality attributes of grass carp fillets during cold storage. Similarly, lacto-fermented fruits and vegetables have been identified as valuable sources of dietary phenolic compounds with significant antioxidant activity (Ciniviz & Yildiz, 2020). Of particular interest, the liquid byproduct from lacto-fermented pickles contains abundant bioactive compounds such as organic acids, flavonoids, and phenolic acids, which demonstrate potent antimicrobial and antioxidant properties (Albuquerque et al., 2021; Baptista et al., 2020). Although typically discarded after the solid pulp is consumed in many countries, this nutrient-rich liquid represents a promising, sustainable source of natural preservatives, offering both ecological and functional advantages for food preservation applications.

Although previous studies have reported the bioactive and antioxidant properties of both pulp and liquid pickles (Sayin & Alkan, 2015; Hallmann et al., 2019; Ciniviz & Yildiz, 2020), the potential application of lacto-fermented pickle liquid (LFPL) as a natural preservative for fish products remains unexplored. Specifically, the effects of LFPL on physicochemical, microbial, and quality attributes of fish during cold storage have not been investigated, nor have the underlying mechanisms been elucidated. Thus, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of LFPL treatment on the physicochemical, microbial, and quality attributes of fish during cold storage and to elucidate the relationships among these attributes. Furthermore, the study proposed mechanistic explanations for LFPL-mediated physicochemical and quality enhancement. By examining LFPL's capacity to inhibit lipid and protein oxidation while maintaining product quality in grass carp fillets, this research provides both theoretical insights and practical applications for extending shelf life in aquatic products, offering a valuable technical reference for optimal preservation during cold storage.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella), raw materials, and yogurt were purchased from the Qingdao supermarket (China). Standards of caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, gallic acid, p-coumaric acid, syringic acid, trans-ferulic acid, and vanillic acid were obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). Other chemicals, including chromatographic methanol and acetonitrile, were obtained from Merck, Germany, and DPPH and Folin-Ciocalteu phenol reagent were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. All chemicals used were of analytical grade.

Fermentation and preparation of four LFPLs in the laboratory

Four mixed lacto-fermented pickles were prepared using different types of fresh vegetables (Table 1). The fresh vegetables were chopped into small pieces and transferred to a sterilized 5 L fermentation jar. Two liters of brine solution (3 % salt), inoculated with 10−8 CFU / mL of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum CIP 103151 LAB strain (2 %, v/v) were added to the jar. The jar was incubated at 20 °C for 15 days, then four pickle liquids were collected and centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 20 min, aseptically filtered through a membrane of 0.2 μm, transferred into sterilized bottles and stored at 4 °C for analysis. The optimal concentration of LFPL and treatment time were determined based on preliminary experiments evaluating antioxidant activity and sensory quality.

Fish treatment

Grass carp fish (average weight 1500 g ± 25 g, n = 10) were sourced from Qingdao, China, and transported alive to the laboratory. The fish were slaughtered, and the fillets were cut into pieces measuring 6 × 2 × 1 cm, then washed and drained for 10 min. The samples were categorized into five groups of 1000 g each: groups A, B, C, and D (treated with LFPL), and group R (control, treated with distilled water). Fillets were immersed in 95 % LFPL at a 1:4 w/v ratio (fish: LFPL) for 30 s and then drained. The samples were sealed in plastic bags and stored at 4 ± 1 °C. Three samples from each group were analyzed on days 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18 of storage.

Determination of phenolic acids in LFPLs by HPLC-MS/MS

Phenolic acids were measured according to the method of Zhang et al. (2022) with minor modifications. A 5 mL of sample (pH adjusted to 2.0) was mixed with 5 mL of ethyl acetate, extracted using a vortex oscillator for 1 min, this process was repeated three times. The upper ethyl acetate phase was collected and combined with 1 g of anhydrous MgSO4 to remove residual moisture, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 2 min. The resulting supernatant was dried with nitrogen and dissolved in 5 mL of 1 % formic acid. The sample was eluted with 4 mL of methanol using an Agilent SPE column (Bond Elut Plexa, 60 mg, 3 mL), dried with nitrogen, re-dissolved in 0.4 mL of methanol, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane.

The Waters BEH C18 column was used for chromatographic separation. A 1 μL sample was injected, with mobile phase A (pure water with 0.1 % formic acid) as the aqueous phase and mobile phase B (methanol with 0.1 % formic acid) as the organic phase, at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min.

To monitor the parent and fragment ions of target analytes, either positive electrospray ionization (ESI+) or negative electrospray ionization (ESI-) in multiple reaction monitoring mode (MRM) was employed, with an atomization temperature of 400 °C, a capillary voltage of −4500 V, an atomization gas pressure of 65 psi, and an auxiliary gas pressure of 70 psi.

Determination of antioxidant activity and Total phenolic content of LFPLs

DPPH scavenging activity

The DPPH assay was conducted using the 1,1-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazine radical according to the method proposed by Dossou et al. (2020), with some minor modifications. Briefly, 0.1 mL of each LFPL extract was mixed with 1.9 mL of 0.2 mmol/L DPPH solution in absolute ethanol. The mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark, and the absorbance was measured at 515 nm, using absolute ethanol as the reference. For the control, absolute ethanol was used instead of DPPH.

Total phenolic content

The Folin-Ciocalteu reagent was used to evaluate the total phenolic content in the four LFPL samples, as described by Malik (2019), with slight modifications. LFPL samples (50 μL) were mixed with Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (250 μL) and incubated for 2 min at room temperature. Following this, a 12 % Na₂CO₃ solution (250 μL) and distilled water (700 μL) were added, the mixture was thoroughly mixed, and incubated for an additional 10 min at room temperature. The absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu 2550 UV–Vis, Japan). The total phenol content was expressed as mg of gallic acid equivalents (GAE)/L.

Lipid oxidation

Determination of peroxide value

The ferric thiocyanate method (Shantha and Decker, 1994) was used to evaluate peroxide values. Briefly, 0.1 g of lipid sample was mixed with 9.8 mL of methanol: chloroform solution (3:7, v/v) and vortexed for 4 s. Subsequently, 50 μL of 30 % ammonium thiocyanate solution was added and mixed for 4 s, followed by the addition of 50 μL ferrous chloride, which was then mixed for another 4 s. After a 5 min of incubation period, the absorbance was measured at 500 nm with a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The peroxide values were quantified in milliequivalents (meq) peroxide per kilogram of lipid.

Determination of Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS)

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) were determined according to the method of Cao et al. (2019), with slight modifications. Minced grass carp (5 g) were homogenized at 3000 rpm for 1 min with 25 mL of trichloroacetic acid (7.5 % w/v) containing 0.1 % EDTA, followed by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatant (5 mL) was mixed with 5 mL of 20 mM thiobarbituric acid and heated in boiling water for 30 min, then cooled under running tap water. The absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer, and a standard curve was created using 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane. TBARS values were expressed as mg of malondialdehyde (MDA) per kg of fish.

Protein oxidation

Protein extraction was performed according to the method of Cao et al. (2016), with slight modifications. Briefly, 20 mL of buffer solution (comprising 0.60 mol/L NaCl, 0.05 mol/L NaH2PO4–Na2HPO4, pH 7.2) was combined with 1 g of grass carp sample and homogenized at 10,000 rpm for 30 s. Subsequently, the homogenate was centrifuged for 10 min at 10,000 ×g. The concentration of protein in the resulting supernatant was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method.

Determination of carbonyl content

The carbonyl content was assessed using the 2,4-dinitrophenyl hydrazine (DNPH) method, as detailed by Gao et al. (2023), with slight modifications. A 1 mL protein solution was mixed with 200 μL of 10 mM DNPH and allowed to react for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Following the addition of 1.2 mL of 20 % trichloroacetic acid, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting protein pellet was washed with 1 mL of ethanol: ethyl acetate solution (1:1, v/v) and centrifuged again at 10,000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The pellet was collected and dried for 5 min. It was then resuspended in 1 mL of 6 mol/L guanidine hydrochloride and incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at a wavelength of 370 nm. The carbonyl content was calculated as nmol/mg of protein using a molar absorption coefficient of 22,000 M−1 cm−1.

Determination of sulfhydryl content

Thiol groups were quantified using the method outlined by Cao et al. (2019). A volume of 500 μL of protein solution was mixed with 4.5 mL of 0.02 M Tris-HCl buffer, which contained 8 mmol/L urea, 0.01 mol/L EDTA, and 12 % sodium dodecyl sulfate at pH 6.8. Then, 500 of 0.1 % 5′-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid was added, and the mixture was incubated at 40 °C for 25 min. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 412 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu 2550 UV–Vis, Japan), and sulfhydryl content was calculated as nmol/mg of protein.

Determination of Total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N)

The value of total volatile basic nitrogen was determined using the microdiffusion method (Liu et al., 2024) with minor modifications. Minced grass carp (3 g) was homogenized with 25 mL of distilled water for 30 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of the resulting solution was combined with 1 mL of 2 % boric acid in the center of the diffusion plate. After adding 1 mL of saturated potassium carbonate, the plate was sealed, incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, cooled to room temperature, and titrated with 0.01 M HCl using distilled water as a blank. TVB-N value was quantified as mg N/100 g flesh, based on the consumption of hydrochloric acid.

Determination of pH value

The fish sample (10 g) was homogenized with 90 mL of ultrapure water using a rotor-stator homogenizer for 3 min at 10,000 rpm. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The pH of the supernatant was determined using an FE-20 pH meter (Mettler Toledo, Greifensee, Switzerland).

Determination of water-holding capacity (WHC) and cooking loss

The water-holding capacity (WHC) of grass carp fillets was assessed by measuring centrifugal loss. Centrifugal and cooking losses were determined according to the method described by Li et al. (2023). For the centrifugal loss measurement, a 5 g sample of grass carp (W1) was wrapped in filter paper and centrifugated at 3000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C using a CTH 2050R centrifuge (Thermo Fisher, USA). The weight of the sample after centrifugation was recorded as (W2).

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

The analysis was performed according to the method of Yu et al. (2017), using a TA Plus texture analyzer (US) and a specialized cylindrical probe. TPA parameters were generated by performing two rounds of 50 % deformation. The trigger force was 5 g, and the test speed was 1 mm/s.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation of the fillets was conducted according to the method of Yu et al. (2017), with minor modifications. Eight trained assessors (20–30 years old; four males and four females) evaluated the sensory quality of grass carp fillets. The evaluation was carried out in a white light room, using randomly coded three-digit numbers. Assessors were unaware of the differences between the fillets. Each fillet was served on a white paper plate, and assessors rinsed their mouths with water before tasting. They evaluated the freshness and edibility of grass carp fillets based on the appearance, odor, color, flavor, texture, overall acceptability, and taste of cooked samples, using a 5-point scale, where five indicated the highest quality and one the lowest, with three being the borderline of acceptability.

Microbiological analysis

Total viable count (TVC), psychrotropic count (PTC), and total coliform count (TC) of the samples were determined using the method described by Kuley et al. (2021). Ten grams of fish sample was homogenized with 90 mL of sterile 0.85 % saline solution. The mixture was diluted serially with a saline solution. One milliliter of the diluted solution was added to a Petri dish and mixed with plate count agar medium for TVC and PTC, and Violet Red Bile Agar (VRBA) for TC. All procedures were performed aseptically. The inoculated plates were incubated at 30 °C for 2 d (TVC), 5 °C for 10 d (PTC) and 37 °C for 24 h (TC).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and Duncan's multiple range test (DMRT) were applied to identify significant differences among groups using (IBM SPSS) software. Principal component analysis (PCA) and a correlation heatmap of physicochemical, microbial, and quality attributes of grass carp fillets were constructed using Origin (v2024b). The correlation heatmap was assessed using Pearson's correlation test. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05, P < 0.01, or P < 0.001.

Effect of LFPLs on lipid oxidation in grass carp

TBARS values

The effect of LFPL treatments on grass carp fillets during cold storage is shown in (Fig. 1a). Initially, the TBARS values of the control sample (R) and treated group A showed significant increases from 0.25 mg of MDA eq/kg on day 0 to 1.15 and 0.87 mg of MDA eq/kg on day 18 for control R and treated sample A, respectively (Fig. 1a), In contrast, the TBARS value of group B increased significantly from 0.25 mg of MDA eq/kg on day 0 to 0.62 mg of MDA eq/kg on day 9, then decreased slightly to 0.60 mg of MDA eq/kg on day 12, remained stable on day 15, and then increased significantly again to 0.84 mg of MDA eq/kg on day 18. The TBARS values for treated samples C and D increased from 0.25 mg of MDA eq/kg on day 0 to 0.61 mg and 0.52 mg of MDA eq/kg, respectively, by day 18. There were no significant increases for samples A and B on days 3 and 6, and for sample D on days 12 and 15.

Among the different groups, the results showed that the TBARS value in all treated samples was significantly lower than that of the control sample R under cold storage after 18 days (P < 0.05). Furthermore, treated samples C and D exhibited significantly lower TBARS values from day 9 until the end of the storage period (P < 0.05; Fig. 1a). These results indicate that the various LFPLs contributed to the lower TBARS values in the treated samples compared to the control group. The phenolic compounds in LFPLs contribute to antioxidant activity by donating electrons and neutralizing free lipid radicals formed during the early stages of oxidation, thereby preventing their further degradation into more potent oxidizing agents such as MDA (Albuquerque et al., 2021). This finding suggests that LFPLs could slow the deterioration of lipid hydroperoxides during cold storage and lower TBARS values. Similarly, an analogous study revealed that clove bud extract proved to be more effective than grape seed extract (GSE) in decreasing lipid oxidation and TBARS values in silver carp (Shi et al., 2014). According to another study, TBARS levels decreased in Indian mackerel samples treated with mint extract, attributed to the presence of phenols and flavonoids that minimize free radicals (Viji et al., 2015). Thus, our results demonstrate that the addition of LFPLs significantly reduced TBARS values and lipids oxidation in grass carp fillets from 3 to 18 days during cold storage.

Peroxide value (PV)

Peroxide value (PV) is the predominant product of the primary phase of lipid oxidation, hence, it is a significant indicator for estimating lipid hydroperoxide production. The overall trend of the PV in all groups during cold storage is shown in Fig. 1b. In the control group R, the PV increased significantly from 0.59 meq hydroperoxide/kg lipids on day 0 to 7.07 meq hydroperoxide/kg lipids on day 15, then decreasing to 6.07 meq hydroperoxide/kg lipids on day 18. In contrast, the initial PV of treated samples (A, B, C, and D) was also 0.59 meq hydroperoxide/kg lipids, which then significantly (P < 0.05) increased to 3.36, 3.43, 3.23, and 3.05 meq hydroperoxide/kg lipids for samples A, B, C, and D, respectively, by day 18. Treated samples A and B showed slight increases on day 9, while sample A exhibited a slight increase on day 15. These results indicate that control sample R exhibited a significantly faster production of lipid hydroperoxides than the treated samples during cold storage. Among the different samples, the peroxide value (PV) of control group R was significantly (P < 0.05) higher than that of the treated groups (A, B, C, and D) from day 3 to the end of the storage period. Furthermore, there were no significant differences in PV between samples C and D or between samples A and B. However, significant differences were observed between samples D and A from days 6 to 18 of storage. The decrease in PV of the control group on day 18 may be due to the breakdown of hydroperoxides into other oxygenated molecules, which are regarded as secondary oxidation products. Meanwhile, the increase in PV of the treated samples may be attributed to the propagation stage of lipid oxidation, coupled with a decreased rate of hydroperoxide breakdown related to the antioxidant effect of LFPLs. Our results suggest that LFPLs significantly reduce the peroxide value, leading to superior inhibition of hydroperoxide production. Similarly, Shi et al. (2014) reported that extracts from clove buds and grape seeds efficiently delayed hydroperoxide production in silver carp fillets during refrigerated storage.

Effect of LFPLs on protein oxidation of grass carp

Carbonyl content

The quality of fish is negatively affected by carbonyl molecule synthesis. As shown in Fig. 1c, protein carbonyls exhibited changes in all samples during cold storage. Initially, the carbonyl content of the control sample (R) and treated samples A, B, C, and D showed significant increases from 1.48, 1.46, 1.48, 1.49, and 1.49 nmol/mg protein on day 0 to 6.44, 4.81, 4.58, 4.11, and 3.61 nmol/mg protein for control R, A, B, C, and D, respectively, on day 18. The results indicated that the increase in carbonyl values for the control sample showed a significantly faster rate of protein oxidation compared to the treated samples. After 18 days of storage, the carbonyl content of all LFPL-treated samples (A, B, C, and D) was significantly lower than that of the control group. Among the samples, sample D had the lowest carbonyl content, followed by samples C, B, A, and R, respectively, from day 6 to day 18 of storage. Our results indicated that protein oxidation in the muscle of grass carp occurred during cold storage and that all LFPL groups were effective in delaying the carbonylation of some major amino acids, which resulted in the delay of protein oxidation. This effect is likely due to the presence of flavonoids and phenolic acids, such as chlorogenic acid, which could be attributed to their high antioxidant activity, as shown in Table 3. Similar findings were reported by Cao et al. (2019), who demonstrated the ability of chlorogenic acid to delay protein oxidation.

Sulfhydryl content

Since cysteine residues are highly susceptible to oxidation, sulfhydryl loss serves as a valuable marker for assessing the oxidation of proteins. Fig. 1d demonstrates the effect of different LFPL treatments on the total sulfhydryl content in grass carp fillets. After refrigerated storage, all samples exhibited a decrease in sulfhydryl concentration. The control group showed the most significant decrease, from107.60 to 45.06 nmol/mg protein during 18 days, while treated groups A, B, C, and D had smaller reductions, from 107.21 to 49.17, 49.66, 52.35, and 60.49 nmol/mg protein, respectively, on day 18. The treated samples maintained significantly higher levels of sulfhydryl (P < 0.05) during days 6 to 18 of storage compared to the control group. Among the treated samples, there were no significant differences between samples A and B, while samples D and C were significantly higher than samples B and A. This is likely due to their antioxidant activity and phenolic acid content, which form covalent bonds with sulfhydryl groups and reduce the formation of disulfides, potentially coating the surface of the fish. Furthermore, the antioxidant and antimicrobial properties of phenolic compounds, along with organic acids, protect fish from deterioration during chilled storage. These findings indicate that mixed vegetable LFPLs can reduce sulfhydryl loss and enhance the inhibition of protein oxidation, consistent with the results of Wu et al. (2022).

Effect of LFPLs on TVB-N value

TVB-N is generated when the proteins in fish fillets break down due to the action of bacteria and enzymes, releasing volatile alkaline nitrogen-containing compounds such as amines and ammonia (Li et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 1e, on day 0 of storage, the TVB-N values for the control sample R were approximately 6.55 mg/100 g, and it significantly increased (P < 0.05) to 22.30 mg/100 g on day18. Treated samples A, B, C, and D showed significant increases (P < 0.05) from 6.55, 6.51, 6.44, and 6.41 mg/100 g, respectively, on day 0 to 11.69, 11.17, 10.64, and 10.57 mg/100 g on day 18, with slight increases on day 6 for samples C and D and on day 9 for all treated samples. These results demonstrate that the increase in TVB-N value for control sample R during the entire storage period was significantly faster than for the treated samples. Among the different samples, no significant differences were observed during the first 3 days. After 3 days, the LFPL-treated samples had significantly (P < 0.05) lower TVB-N levels than the control. On day 9, control sample R reached 13.28 mg/100 g, while treated samples A, B, C, and D reached 11.69, 11.17, 10.64, and 10.57 mg/100 g, respectively, on day 18. TVB-N levels of 12–15 mg/100 g are considered the spoilage threshold due to sensory rejection. Among the treated samples, sample D had significantly lower levels than samples A and B from days 6 to 18. This reduction in TVB-N levels in the treated samples was likely due to the antioxidant and antibacterial effects of phenolic compounds and organic acids in LFPLs, which inhibit bacterial protein degradation and oxidative deamination. Similarly, Saki et al. (2018) found that the phenolic content in pomegranate peel extract reduced TVB-N during cold storage. Our findings indicate that LFPLs effectively reduce TVB-N production from days 6 to 18, with LFPL-D being the most effective in reducing TVB-N levels.

Effect of LFPLs on water holding capacity and cooking loss

The effect of LFPLs on the water holding capacity (WHC) of grass carp during cold storage is shown in Fig. 2a. The results indicated that centrifugal loss (CL) in all samples gradually increased during 18 days of storage, likely due to structural changes in the fillets, including protein oxidation and protein-aldehyde/amine interactions (Yu et al., 2022). The increase in CL indicated a decrease in the WHC of the grass carp fillets during storage. The control group R exhibited a significant (P < 0.05) increase in CL, from 10.53 % on day 0 to 29.03 % on day 18. In contrast, the treated groups exhibited a significant increase in CL values from 10.53 %, 10.46 %, 10.40 %, and 10.26 % for samples A, B, C, and D, respectively, on day 0 to 24.08 % for group A, 23.51 % for group B, 22.99 % for group C, and 22.63 % for group D on day18. There was a slight increase on day 9 for sample D and on day 18 for samples A and B. During days 6 to 18 of cold storage, all treated groups gradually lost water but maintained significantly higher WHC than the control group (P < 0.05). Among the treated samples, group D had significantly lower WHC than groups A, B, and C from days 9 to 15, similar to the findings observed by Yang et al. (2022) and Sun et al. (2017). These results imply that LFPL treatment can delay the increase in CL due to its antioxidant effect.

Effect of LFPL on (a) centrifugal loss, (b) cooking loss, (c) pH value, (d) whiteness value, on (e) total viable count, (f) psychrotropic count, and (g) total coliform of grass carp fillets during cold storage.

The cooking loss increased for the fillets, primarily resulting from heat-induced myofibrillar degeneration, which causes loss of fluid and soluble components during heating (Li et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 2b, the cooking loss of control group R, as well as groups A, B, C, and D, exhibited a significant (P < 0.05) increase, from 15.23 %, 15.55 %, 15.98 %, 15.23 %, and 15.23 % for groups R, A, B, C, and D, respectively, on day 0 to 40.68 % for control group R, 35.05 % for group A, 34.42 % for group B, 33.15 % for group C, and 31.90 % for group D on day 18. On day 3, there was no significant increase for sample D. The LFPL-treated groups showed significantly (P < 0.05) reduced cooking loss compared to the control group from day 6 to day 18. Among the treated samples, groups C and D consistently showed significantly (P < 0.05) lower cooking loss than groups A and B from days 9 to 18 (Fig. 2b). This reduction in cooking loss in the treated samples was likely due to the inhibition of protein oxidation in fish fillets, corresponding to the TBARS results.

Effect of LFPLs on the pH value

The effect of LFPLs on the pH value of grass carp during cold storage is shown in Fig. 2c. The results showed that the pH values of groups R, A, B, C and D initially were 6.63, 6.49, 6.42, 6.36, and 6.33, respectively. These values significantly decreased on day 3 and then gradually increased by day 18 to 7.23, 6.77, 6.76, 6.72, and 6.71, respectively. During the first 3 days of cold storage, the pH decreased due to the production of lactic acid and inorganic phosphate, which were generated by the breakdown of adenosine triphosphate and glycolysis in fish fillets. The pH of each treated group was significantly (P < 0.05) lower than that of the R group during the entire storage time, probably because the acidity of LFPL reduced the pH of the fish fillets during subsequent storage (3–18 days). During storage, endogenous enzymes and microorganisms produce alkaline compounds, which gradually increase the pH (Zhang et al., 2022). On the 18th day, the control group reached a pH of 7.23, making the samples inedible, as indicated by sensory evaluation data. In contrast, the LFPL-treated samples had a significantly (P < 0.05) lower pH value. Among the treated groups, group D had a significantly (P < 0.05) lower pH than groups A and B on day 18 of cold storage. Our findings suggest that groups A, B, C, and D slowed microbial spoilage and enzymatic reactions, possibly due to the antimicrobial properties of LFPL, which hindered the synthesis of alkaline compounds and increased the pH value. Similarly, pangasius fillets treated with seaweed extract maintained a lower pH during refrigerated storage (Deepitha et al., 2021).

Effect of LFPLs on whiteness

Color values often indicate the quality and freshness of fish. Fig. 2d illustrates that the whiteness of the grass carp control (R) and treated samples (A, B, C, and D) showed a trend of multiple increases and decreases from days 0 to 9, while the whiteness values of all samples significantly decreased from days 12 to 18 of storage, except for B and D, which showed only slight decreases on day 18. The reduction in whiteness during days 12 to 18 was likely due to spoilage and oxidation of proteins and lipids. Among the different samples, treated samples showed significantly higher whiteness than the control (R) during days 6 to 18 of refrigeration. Among the treated samples, groups D and C had significantly lower whiteness than groups A and B on days 6 to 15 of storage. At the end of refrigerated storage, whiteness values were 46.28, 50.08, 53.14, 53.47, and 56.92 for samples R, A, B, C and D, respectively, indicating that the LFPL treatments improved whiteness. LFPL, especially in samples D and C, inhibited protein and lipid oxidation due to its phenolic content and antioxidant properties, delaying changes and improving whiteness; similar results were reported by Wang et al. (2021). Thus, the LFPL-treated groups effectively reduced oxidation and discoloration, maintaining visual quality and consumer acceptability.

Effect of LFPLs on texture properties

Analysis of texture parameters, including chewiness, hardness, cohesiveness, gumminess, and springiness, is a valuable indicator of freshness and a primary quality attribute for consumer acceptability. Fig. 3 shows the changes in springiness, chewiness, hardness, gumminess, and cohesiveness of grass carp fillets during cold storage. The results showed that the chewiness parameter of control R and the treated samples A, B, C, and D significantly decreased (P < 0.05) from 1134.08, 1115.56, 1132.55, 1130.85, and 1145.81, respectively, on day 0 to 524.30, 606.89, 647.34, 676.57, and 685.92 on day 18. Similarly, hardness significantly decreased from 3908.54, 3933.70, 4021.73, 3905.65, and 3942.70 for control R and treated samples A, B, C, and D, respectively, on day 0 to 2583.26, 2790.09, 2829.52, 2839.72, and 2840.74 on day 18.

Effect of LFPL on the texture of grass carp fillet during cold storage. R: control treated with distill water, A: treated with 95 % LFPL(A), B: treated with 95 % LFPL(B), C: treated with 95 % LFPL(C) and D: treated with 95 % LFPL(D).

Moreover, cohesiveness for control R and treated samples A, B, C, and D significantly decreased from 0.4253, 0.4153, 0.4225, 0.4260, and 0.4328, respectively, on day 0 to 0.1214, 0.1534, 0.1544, 0.1557, and 0.1573 on day 18 of cold storage. Likewise, the gumminess of the control R and treated samples A, B, C, and D significantly decreased from 1489.45, 1454.10, 1456.99, 1468.72, and 1486.56 on day 0 to 697.99, 845.33, 859.10, 872.02, and 889.69 on day 18.

Finally, springiness for control R and treated samples A, B, C, and D significantly decreased from 0.7517, 0.8155, 0.8202, 0.8350, and 0.8417, respectively, on day 0 to 0.1843, 0.2637, 0.2912, 0.2948, and 0.3042 on day 18 of cold storage. Our results demonstrated that all texture parameters of grass carp meat tended to decrease as the refrigeration time increased, which is in accordance with the findings of Guo et al. (2023). The decline in the values of texture parameters indicated that the degradation of fish protein started on day 3 of storage. This deterioration could be linked to the oxidation process, the enzymatic breakdown of muscle, and loss of firmness (Pokorny, 2003). Among the different samples, the results showed that the chewiness and hardness parameters of A, B, C, and D were significantly higher than those of control R from days 6 to 18, while the cohesiveness and gumminess of treated samples A, B, C, and D were significantly higher than those of control R from days 3 to 18. Moreover, springiness for treated samples A, B, C, and D was significantly higher than that of control R during 18 days of cold storage. This is likely due to the phenolic content of LFPLs, which inhibits the activity of pro-oxidant enzymes, delays oxidation, and maintains the texture of the fish protein.

Effect of LFPLs on sensory attributes

Consumer acceptance of grass carp is directly influenced by sensory assessment, which is a crucial component in determining its quality and freshness. The impacts of LFPL on the sensory attributes (odor, appearance, taste, texture, flavor, color, and overall acceptability) of the fish fillets are presented in Fig. 4. Initially, all groups of fillets exhibited a fresh fish odor and pleasant flavor with a typical white color appearance; however, the scores for odor, texture, appearance, taste, flavor, color, and overall acceptability of the control R decreased throughout the storage period, showing a significant (P < 0.05) difference compared to samples treated with LFPL. All groups exhibited varying degrees of sensory deterioration, including yellow color, an ammonia-fishy mixed odor, dull appearance, soft texture, and unpleasant flavor and taste. On day 6, an off-odor was detected in the R group, which may be attributed to microbial metabolism and oxidation processes, while samples treated with LFPL did not exhibit an off-odor until day 12. Furthermore, the R group demonstrated a softer texture on day 9, potentially due to oxidation that directly impacts sensory, textural, and functional properties (Pokorny, 2003). The LFPL-treated groups exhibited enhanced firmness in texture, which can be attributed to the LFPLs antioxidant properties. Higher acceptability scores were obtained for the treated samples (A, B, C, and D) than for the control sample. Similar results have been found for sea bass fillets (Yang et al., 2022), indicating that a shelf life of less than 12 days could be detected for the control and more than 15 days for the LFPL-treated samples. This is attributed to the inhibition of fish degradation by LFPL treatment; this effect is potentially due to lower bacterial counts in treated fish fillets and a reduction in oxidation. Consequently, LFPLs exhibited a positive impact on preserving organoleptic properties and extending the shelf life of refrigerated grass carp fillets.

Effect of LFPLs on microbiological quality

The impact of LFPL (A, B, C, and D) on total viable count, psychotropic bacteria, and total coliform count is shown in Fig. 2 (e–g). The initial total viable counts (TVC) of all groups ranged from 3.48 to 3.53 log10 CFU/g, indicating good microbial quality (Fig. 2e). The total viable count (TVC) of the control group (R) on day 15 was 7.51 log10 CFU/g, which exceeds the maximum allowed level of 7 log10 CFU/g in fresh fish (Zhan et al., 2018). In contrast, the TVC for the LFPL-treated groups (A, B, C, and D) were 6.94, 6.72, 6.74, and 6.68 log10 CFU/g, respectively, on day 18 of storage. These results indicate that all LFPL groups significantly (P < 0.05) reduced the growth of microorganisms in grass carp fillet samples during cold storage compared to the control group. There were no significant differences between the LFPL-treated samples. Interestingly, the TVC of treated samples did not exceed the allowed limit on the 18th day of cold storage. These results indicate that LFPL improved the microbial quality of grass carp, thereby enhancing preservation effects, likely due to the content of phenolic acid and organic acids, which have antimicrobial effects in LFPL. Similarly, a previous study reported that the combination of acetic and citric acids exhibited excellent inhibition of total viable bacterial counts, psychrophilic bacterial counts, yeast and mold counts, and total coliform counts in bolti fish during cold storage (El-Shemy et al.,2016). Psychrotropic bacteria are the main contributors to the decline in fish fillets during chilled storage (Li et al., 2020). Fig. 2f shows that the initial count of psychotropic bacteria in the control (R) and treated samples A, B, C, and D was 3.78, 3.64, 3.63, 3.62, and 3.62 log10 CFU/g, respectively, and increased to 7.19, 6.08, 5.92, 5.75, and 5.66 log10 CFU/g on day 18 of cold storage. LFPLs effectively reduced the number of psychotropic bacteria during storage, resulting in significantly (P < 0.05) lower levels than in the control group. Notably, the D samples exhibited significantly lower counts than A and B on day 18 of storage. Similar findings were reported in a previous study (Feng et al., 2017). As shown in Fig. 2g, the initial coliform counts of the control group were 2.70 log10 CFU/g, while the initial coliform counts of the treated groups were less than 2.62–2.63 log10 CFU/g. Coliform bacteria proliferate with increased storage time and the maximum coliform count was observed in the control samples compared to the treated samples. This difference could be attributed to the phenolic compounds present in the lacto-fermented liquid, which exhibit antibacterial effects; similar findings were reported by Nikmaram et al. (2018). There were no significant differences among the treated samples from day 0 to day 12 of storage, while the D samples were significantly lower than the A samples from days 15 to 18 of storage. In the current study, the lowest coliform growth was found in the D group, followed by C, B, and A. The total coliform count in the grass carp fillet reached maximum levels of 5.46, 4.79, 4.70, 4.65, and 4.52 log10 CFU/g for the control (R) and treated samples A, B, C, and D, respectively, at the end of storage.

Principal component analysis of physicochemical, microbial, and quality characteristics

The similarities in the physicochemical, microbial, and quality characteristics of grass carp fillets in groups R, A, B, C, and D at various storage stages were assessed using PCA (Fig. 5a). PC1 and PC2 explained 94.19 % of the system variance, reflecting the overall physicochemical and quality parameters during cold storage. The R-D6 to R-D18 groups exhibited greater differences compared to the other groups, indicating that untreated fillets in the late storage period had lower physicochemical and quality parameters, along with higher oxidation of lipids and proteins. In contrast, the treated groups were similar to each other but differed from the untreated group, highlighting the effectiveness of lacto-fermented liquids (LFPL) in reducing oxidation and maintaining stability and quality during cold storage.

Correlation analysis

Pearson's correlation analysis (Fig. 5b) showed significant correlations between the physicochemical and quality parameters. The decrease in water-holding capacity (WHC) positively correlated with peroxide value (r = 0.897), TBARS (r = 0.896), carbonyl content (r = 0.869), pH (r = 0.842), and TVB-N (r = 0.840), while negatively correlating with total sulfhydryl content (r = −0.969). These results indicate that the decrease in WHC during late storage was primarily due to increased protein oxidation and degradation.

Cooking loss was positively correlated with peroxide value (r = 0.897), TBARS (r = 0.899), carbonyl content (r = 0.868), pH (r = 0.858), and TVB-N (r = 0.840), and negatively correlated with total sulfhydryl content (r = −0.964). Texture properties, including hardness, springiness, gumminess, cohesiveness, and chewiness, were positively correlated with total sulfhydryl content (r = 0.914, 0.964, 0.973, 0.950, 0.975), but negatively correlated with peroxide value (r = −0.575, −0.867, −0.843, −0.835, −0.876), TBARS (r = −0.802, −0.836, −0.856, −0.846, −0.877), and other factors. This suggests that excessive oxidation reduces quality, significantly affecting texture parameters (Hematyar et al., 2019).

Whiteness was positively correlated with total sulfhydryl content (r = 0.556) and negatively correlated with peroxide value (r = −0.730), TBARS (r = −0.697), carbonyl content (r = −0.797), pH (r = −0.749), and TVB-N (r = −0.767). Research has shown that whiteness is associated with protein denaturation, and increased oxidation decreases fillet whiteness (Xue et al., 2023). Higher protein oxidation and degradation in grass carp during late storage were correlated with reduced fillet whiteness.

Conversely, the sensory score of grass carp fillets exhibited significant negative correlations with peroxide value (r = −0.90), TBARS (r = −0.944), carbonyl content (r = −0.870), pH (r = −0.842), and TVB-N (r = −0.840), alongside a significant positive correlation with total sulfhydryl content (r = 0.957). Previous studies have reported a relationship between sensory profiles and the oxidation process, indicating that oxidation leads to a reduction in sensory and overall fish quality (Dominguez et al., 2019).

The microbial composition of grass carp fillets during cold storage showed negative correlations with total sulfhydryl content (r = −0.961, −0.916, −0.851) but positive correlations with peroxide value (r = 0.884, 0.932, 0.838), TBARS content (r = 0.848, 0.881, 0.809), carbonyl content (r = 0.825, 0.910, 0.841), pH (r = 0.798, 0.914, 0.857), and TVB-N content (r = 0.808, 0.889, 0.825) for total viable count, psychrotropic bacteria, and total coliform count, respectively. Supporting findings from previous research indicated that pre-dipping large yellow croaker in rosemary extract, tea polyphenols, and chitosan solutions reduced TVC and TVB-N contents during 20 days of refrigeration (Li et al., 2012).

Mechanisms of improved quality

According to our data, Fig. 5c illustrates the predicted mechanisms for improved physicochemical and quality parameters through reduced protein and lipid oxidation and degradation in grass carp fillets by LFPL during cold storage. The bioactive compounds in LFPL, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, and organic acids, act as antioxidant and antimicrobial agents. As antioxidants, the total flavonoid compounds detected in LFPL A, B, C, and D were 851.12 mg/L, 1478.49 mg/L, 531.18 mg/L, and 1345.85 mg/L, respectively (data not shown). Additionally, the phenolic acids in LFPL contain numerous polyhydroxy groups that can neutralize free radicals, thereby inhibiting the oxidation process through hydrogen atom transfer and electron transfer mechanisms. This action reduces lipid and protein oxidation, as indicated by our results, which show higher total sulfhydryl content and lower carbonyl and MDA content. Consequently, this leads to a decrease in the decline of water-holding capacity (WHC), sensory attributes, whiteness, and texture parameters.

As antibacterial agents, the detected lactic acid levels in LFPL A, B, C, and D were 272.6 mg/L, 311.95 mg/L, 1116.09 mg/L, and 1262.69 ± 0.55 mg/L, respectively. The acetic acid levels were 756.05 mg/L, 793.05 mg/L, 929.58 mg/L, and 992.5 mg/L, while the citric acid levels were 25.04 mg/L, 59.56 mg/L, 51.75 mg/L, and 64.72 mg/L (data not shown). These compounds can disrupt cellular homeostasis by acidifying the cytoplasm, altering cell pH, and affecting osmotic pressure, which inhibits the growth and metabolism of spoilage microorganisms. This ultimately leads to a reduction in protein degradation in grass carp, as evidenced by the decrease in TVB-N and the numbers of total viable bacteria, psychotropic bacteria, and total coliform bacteria. Thus, the inhibitory effects of LFPL on lipid and protein oxidation and degradation contribute to enhanced oxidative stability as well as improved physicochemical, microbial, and quality attributes of grass carp fillets during cold storage. This ultimately results in an extended shelf life of the product.

Conclusion

This study conclusively demonstrates that lacto-fermented pickle liquids (LFPLs) are highly effective natural preservatives for extending the shelf life and quality of grass carp fillets during cold storage. Specifically, treatment D emerged as the most effective formulation, consistently exhibiting the strongest inhibition of lipid and protein oxidation, with a thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) reduction of 54.78 % and a peroxide value decrease of 49.79 %. Additionally, this treatment resulted in a significant reduction in protein oxidation markers, with carbonyl levels decreasing by 42.15 % and sulfhydryl groups increasing by 38.67 %, thereby indicating enhanced protein stability.

Moreover, treatment D achieved the greatest reduction in microbial counts, evidenced by a 21.26 % reduction in psychrotrophic bacteria and significant decreases in coliforms and total viable counts, demonstrating its antimicrobial properties. In addition, total volatile basic nitrogen (TVBN) values significantly decreased by 52.66 % in treatment D, which highlights the effectiveness of LFPLs in inhibiting spoilage. Furthermore, treatment D also showed significant improvements in water-holding capacity, texture, and key sensory attributes, indicating enhanced overall quality. Similarly, treatments C, B, and A provided significantly greater improvements across all measured parameters compared to the control, with TBARS reductions of 46.96 %, 26.96 %, and 24.35 %, respectively.

Consequently, these findings confirm that LFPLs act through dual antioxidant and antimicrobial mechanisms, leading to a significant reduction in oxidation biomarkers and spoilage indicators. Overall, this study strongly advocates for the use of LFPLs, particularly treatment D, as a natural and sustainable strategy to mitigate spoilage in the aquaculture industry. By effectively delaying degradation and maintaining fillet quality, LFPLs present a promising alternative to synthetic preservatives for ensuring product safety and consumer acceptability. Therefore, future research should explore the long-term effects of LFPLs under various storage conditions and their applicability to other fish species to further validate their effectiveness.
