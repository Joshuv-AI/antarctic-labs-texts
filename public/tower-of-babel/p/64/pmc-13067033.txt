Plasma-derived exosomal miRNA as a promising diagnostic biomarker for detection of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
Authors: Yunfei Zhao, Anna Su, Gulimire Adili, Laxin Sabitjan, Xun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is a malignant tumor with moderate-high aggressiveness and heterogeneity. In recent years, several studies have shown that tumor cell-derived exosomes are involved in the process of tumorigenesis and progression through microRNA (miRNA). Therefore, exosomal miRNAs are potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for cancer. The aim of this study was to explore the potential of a set of exosomal miRNAs in peripheral blood as noninvasive biomarkers for the auxiliary diagnosis of DLBCL.MethodsExosomes were collected from the peripheral blood of three DLBCL patients and two healthy controls, and miRNA expression was detected using high-throughput sequencing technology. MiRNAs were analyzed by Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis to screen out the correlated model genes of DLBCL, subsequently, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to verify the diagnostic efficacy of this group of miRNAs and validation was performed using the GSE171272 dataset. Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed for the target genes of hsa-miR-589-5p, hsa-miR-4728-3p and hsa-miR-5006-3p.ResultsWe identified eight exosomal miRNAs with the potential to differentiate DLBCL patients from healthy controls. ROC curves were generated using the GSE171272 dataset to assess the diagnostic performance of these miRNAs. Among them, hsa-miR-589-5p, hsa-miR-4728-3p, hsa-miR-5006-3p, hsa-miR-30b-5p, hsa-miR-4323, hsa-miR-484, hsa-miR-4500, and hsa-miR-99a-5p exhibited an AUC greater than 0.8. Specifically, the area under the curve (AUC) of hsa-miR-589-5p, hsa-miR-4728-3p and hsa-miR-5006-3p were both 1.00 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.93−1.00].ConclusionsIn this study, exosomal miRNAs contribute to the diagnosis of DLBCL and may serve as non-invasive diagnostic biomarkers for DLBCL. This offers novel perspectives on utilizing exosomal miRNAs for the early identification of DLBCL and their potential clinical application. However, given the small size of the discovery cohort, these estimates are presented as exploratory and should be confirmed in larger independent cohorts.Keywords: Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), exosomes, microRNA (miRNA), diagnostic biomarkers

Highlight box

Key findings

• Plasma-derived exosomal microRNAs (miRNAs), including hsa-miR-589-5p, hsa-miR-4728-3p, hsa-miR-5006-3p, hsa-miR-30b-5p, hsa-miR-4323, hsa-miR-484, hsa-miR-4500, and hsa-miR-99a-5p, may serve as potential biomarkers capable of distinguishing diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) patients from healthy controls. This study demonstrates a set of exosomal miRNAs in peripheral blood with high diagnostic potential specifically for DLBCL.

What is known and what is new?

• It is known that exosomal miRNAs participate in tumor progression and may serve as biomarkers.

• This study highlights plasma-derived exosomal miRNAs hsa-miR-589-5p, hsa-miR-4728-3p, and hsa-miR-5006-3p as promising candidate biomarkers, potentially providing a noninvasive approach for early DLBCL detection.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings suggest that plasma-derived exosomal miRNAs could serve as potential clinical diagnostic tools for DLBCL. However, validation in larger independent cohorts is required to confirm their robustness and clinical utility.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is a moderately-highly aggressive and heterogeneous tumor derived from mature B-cells, and is the most common pathological subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), accounting for 30–40% of all NHL (1). The first-line R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) immunochemotherapy regimen can lead to complete remission in 60% of patients (2). However, the early clinical manifestations of DLBCL are delayed and lack of specificity, and pathological tissue biopsy is the gold standard diagnostic method for DLBCL (3), but patients generally have low acceptance of invasive examination methods, and puncture examination has the risk of bleeding, infection, and even tumor dissemination (4,5). In addition, some lesions are small, hidden and complex, and it is not possible to obtain ideal lesion tissue for pathologic diagnosis. Imaging methods and tumor marker tests are generally used for evaluation of tumor treatment effects and long-term disease monitoring and have low specificity for diagnosing DLBCL. Therefore, the development of reliable and accurate molecular diagnostic biomarkers is essential for the early diagnosis of DLBCL.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) include exosomes and microvesicles. Exosomes are lipid-bound nanoscale vesicles secreted into the extracellular space by almost all cell types (6-8), which can carry lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids such as deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), messenger RNAs (mRNAs), and microRNAs (miRNAs) and participate in inter-cellular communication as multi-signal messengers (9,10). MiRNAs as the most extensively studied small non-coding RNAs, act as key post-transcriptional regulators by binding to target mRNAs and directing their silencing (11). MiR-146a-5p is highly expressed in human melanoma brain metastasis (MBM) exosomes and inhibits the progression of MBM by regulating the Notch signaling pathway in astrocytes (12). Rituximab is a chimeric monoclonal antibody against CD20 that binds to the CD20 antigen on the surface of DLBCL cells and has been approved for therapeutic use. However, exosomes can diminish the efficacy of rituximab by reducing CD20 expression in lymphoma cells through miRNA-125b-5p-mediated gene regulation (13). Tumor cell-derived exosomal miRNAs play a key role in lymphomagenesis. MiRNAs influence the course of lymphoma development by regulating cell growth, proliferation, differentiation, survival and apoptosis. Therefore, miRNA expression profiles can be used as biomarkers for prognosis, diagnosis, treatment and prediction of lymphoma (14). Notably, miRNAs possess unique properties valuable for biomarkers, including high stability and easy detectability in diverse biological fluids. This stability is attributable to their protection within exosomes, which shield them from ribonuclease degradation (15,16).

In this study, a comprehensive analysis of plasma exosomes from DLBCL patients and healthy controls was performed with a focus on identifying new diagnostic miRNA marker. Preliminary results are presented in this paper and will be further validated by a larger sample size before clinical application. We hope that these findings will contribute to the development of promising biomarkers for DLBCL detection. We present this article in accordance with the STARD and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1296/rc).

Methods

Clinical samples and patients

DLBCL patients (n=3) and healthy controls (n=2) were recruited, with all samples obtained from The Fourth Clinical Medical College of Xinjiang Medical University. The DLBCL group included a 58-year-old female, a 59-year-old male, and an 83-year-old male. The control group included a 57-year-old male and a 62-year-old female. Detailed information regarding the gender and age range of the participants is provided in Appendix 1. The DLBCL patients, hospitalized in the oncology department, were newly diagnosed through pathological examination and had no other malignant tumors or severe infections. The healthy controls were confirmed to be in good health through routine physical examinations. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board from The Fourth Clinical Medical College of Xinjiang Medical University (2025XE0194-1), and all participants provided written informed consent for blood collection.

The GSE171272 dataset, which includes miRNA data derived from exosomes in the peripheral blood serum of both DLBCL patients and control samples, is the only dataset in the GEO database that specifically features miRNA detection in exosomes from DLBCL patients. This dataset was obtained from the open-access Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, hosted by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). It utilizes the GPL18058 platform, specifically the Exiqon miRCURY LNA miRNA array (7th gen-miRBase v18), and includes serum exosomal miRNAs from 15 samples: 10 from untreated DLBCL patients and 5 from healthy controls. This dataset will be used as a validation set to assess the diagnostic efficacy of miRNA. All data from GSE171272 were normalized using the Median Normalization Method, making the data comparable across different samples, so no further operations were performed.

Exosome isolation

All blood samples were collected in tubes containing the anticoagulant ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid dipotassium salt (K2EDTA), and 6-mL peripheral blood from each participant was processed within 4 hours to isolate plasma. Exosomes were isolated from plasma using a standard membrane affinity spin-column method with the exoEasy Maxi Kit (Qiagen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Plasma samples were centrifuged at 13,000 ×g for 10 min to remove debris. An equal volume of Buffer XBP (1:1, v/v) was added, and the mixture was gently inverted five times. The sample/XBP mixture was loaded onto an exoEasy spin column and centrifuged at 500 ×g for one minute; the flow-through was discarded. The column was washed with 10 mL Buffer XWP and centrifuged at 5,000 ×g for five minutes, then transferred to a fresh collection tube. Exosomes were eluted with 250 µL Buffer XE after a one-minute incubation, followed by centrifugation at 500 ×g for five minutes to collect the exosome-containing eluate.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Isolated exosomes were identified by NTA and TEM. Specifically, the size and concentration of particles were examined by NTA using a ZetaVIEW S/N 17-310 (Particle Metrix). Exosomes were resuspended and diluted in 1× PBS at a ratio of 1:4,000 prior to the analysis. Data were captured and analyzed by ZetaView 8.04.02 (Particle Metrix). The isolated exosomes were characterized for size and morphology using TEM. Samples for TEM were prepared by placing a five µL drop of isolated exosomes onto a copper grid and incubating for five minutes. A two percent uranyl acetate solution was then added to the grid, followed by incubation at room temperature for one minute. The samples were examined using a Tecnai G2 Spirit BioTWIN TEM (FEI).

Western blot

Detection of exosomal marker proteins (10 µg total protein per lane) was performed by Western blot. Electrophoresis was conducted at 80 V until the loading buffer entered the separating gel, then increased to 120 V until the dye reached the bottom of the gel. For membrane transfer, a 0.22 µm PVDF membrane was used at 200 mA for 90 minutes. The membranes were then incubated with CD9 (SBI, EXOAB-CD9A-1), CD81 (Immunoway, YT5394), CD63 (SBI, EXOAB-CD63A-1), Calnexin (Immunoway, YT0613). The protein marker used was YiSheng (2035ES90), and the positive control was a validated exosome sample from the cell supernatant of the same laboratory. The secondary antibody used is the Exosome validated Goat anti-Rabbit HRP (SBI). Exosomal markers were detected by ECL using a Tanon 5200 Multi chemiluminescence imaging system.

MiRNA sequencing

The miRNA samples were extracted and purified using the QIAGEN miRNeasy Micro Kit (Catalog No. 217084, QIAGEN) following the manufacturer’s standard protocol. The resulting miRNA was quality-checked by electrophoresis using the Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Catalog No. G2991A, Agilent) and quantified using the Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Catalog No. Q33216, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for subsequent use. The miRNA library was constructed using the QIAseq® miRNA Library Kit for Illumina (Catalog No. 331505, Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The constructed miRNA library was quality-checked using the Qubit Fluorometer (Catalog No. Q33216, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Catalog No. G2991A, Agilent). The library was sequenced on an Illumina sequencing platform.

Targeted gene analysis

The databases used for predicting miRNA targets, miRWalk (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/), miRDB (https://mirdb.org/), and TargetScan (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/) were used to identify miRNA target genes. The clusterProfiler package was employed to conduct Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses.

Statistical analysis

The Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis were performed in the R (version 4.4.3) software and RStudio software with the WGCNA and pROC packages. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the diagnostic capability of plasma exosomal miRNAs. All statistical tests were performed as two-tailed tests, with a significance level of P<0.05.

Diagnostic candidate miRNAs in a WGCNA network

We performed WGCNA on miRNA of exosomes to obtain modules closely related to detect tumor and control (value of β=14, Figure 2A,2B). The scale independence and mean connectivity related to the β value are both shown in Figure S1. We identified 12 modules associated with diagnosis, among which the magenta module had the highest correlation with diagnosis state (correlation coefficient =0.97, P=0.005), containing a total of 51 miRNAs shown in Appendix 2. To further identify reliable miRNAs with diagnostic potential, we excluded miRNAs with undetectable expression levels. We then performed AUC analysis on all expressed miRNAs (Figure 2C). Given the limited sample size in the discovery set (five samples), the reported AUC of 1.00 should be interpreted with caution, as it may not accurately represent the true predictive performance of the miRNAs. Further validation in a larger cohort is needed to assess the robustness of these findings. The box plots for each miRNA show that, except for hsa-let-7i-5p and hsa-miR-500a-3p, which are highly expressed in DLBCL patients, the other miRNAs are more highly expressed in the control group (Figure 2D).

Validation of candidate miRNAs as diagnostic biomarkers

We intersected the candidate miRNAs from the test set with those in GSE171272 (Figure 3A). Based on the unique identifiers of miRNAs, we confirmed that hsa-miR-548j and hsa-miR-3192 in GSE171272 correspond to hsa-miR-548j-5p and hsa-miR-3192-5p in the test set, respectively, resulting in a total of 18 candidate miRNAs. To further confirm the consistent upregulation or downregulation of each miRNA across the two datasets, we compared the expression levels of the candidate miRNAs in the test set and the GSE set, as shown in Figure 3B,3C. Based on their expression profiles, the miRNAs were categorized into high-expression and low-expression groups in DLBCL patients. We then performed an intersection analysis of miRNAs with consistent expression directions, as shown in Figure 3D,3E. The consistent miRNAs were subsequently subjected to ROC analysis, with those having a ROC greater than 0.8 as illustrated in Figure 4A-4H. The miRNAs with an ROC lower than 0.8, including hsa-miR-635, hsa-miR-1915-3p, hsa-let-7i-5p, hsa-miR-548j, and hsa-miR-3175, are shown in Figure S2. Notably, hsa-miR-589-5p, hsa-miR-4728-3p and hsa-miR-5006-3p achieved an apparent AUC of 1.00 in this small discovery cohort.

We further predicted the target genes of hsa-miR-589-5p, hsa-miR-4728-3p and hsa-miR-5006-3p using three miRNA target prediction databases: miRWalk, miRDB, and TargetScan. The predicted target genes were then analyzed through GO and KEGG. All of these results are presented in Figures S3-S5. It is important to note that our functional interpretation is based on predicted data obtained from public databases and bioinformatic tools. Therefore, these findings are preliminary and hypothetical, and require experimental validation for confirmation.

Discussion

This study identified a set of miRNAs in plasma exosome-derived samples that are strongly associated with tumor diagnosis through WGCNA. Upon validation in the GSE171272 dataset, hsa-miR-589-5p, hsa-miR-4728-3p and hsa-miR-5006-3p achieved an AUC of 1.00, while hsa-miR-30b-5p, hsa-miR-4323 and hsa-miR-484 demonstrated AUC values exceeding 0.90. These findings demonstrate the potential of these exosomal miRNAs as diagnostic biomarkers. This research highlights their promising role as candidates for non-invasive diagnostic tools, offering valuable insight for enhancing clinical diagnostic strategies in tumor detection.

MiRNAs play a dual role in cancer, acting as both oncogenes and tumor suppressors, depending on the tumor type. They regulate key processes like cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion by targeting genes in various signaling pathways (17). These complexities highlight the potential of these miRNAs as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets, emphasizing their critical role in various tumor types for the development of effective treatments. For instance, hsa-miR-30b-5p appears to play a distinct and significant role in regulating cell differentiation, metabolism, and tumor progression. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, elevated plasma and exosomal miR-30b-5p levels were found, with its enrichment in exosomes promoting duct formation and endothelial cell migration by down-regulating GJA1, making it a potential diagnostic marker (18). In esophageal squamous carcinoma, miR-30b-5p inhibits migration and invasion by targeting ITGA5 and PDGFRB, regulating PI3K/AKT signaling, and could be a therapeutic target (19). In hepatocellular carcinoma, miR-30b-5p is down-regulated and inhibits cell proliferation by targeting DNMT3A and USP37, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic and therapeutic target (20). In summary, these studies suggest that a deeper understanding of the regulatory roles of miRNAs in specific tumor microenvironments and their molecular mechanisms is crucial for the development of precise diagnostic tools and effective therapeutic strategies.

Current detection methods for exosomal miRNAs include several approaches with varying capabilities. Northern Blot has relatively low throughput and sensitivity (21); microarrays are unable to identify novel miRNAs (22); RT-qPCR, a low to medium throughput method, is suitable for the targeted quantification of miRNAs (22); amplification assays have been shown to be inaccurate in detecting weakly expressed miRNAs (23); and next-generation sequencing (NGS) is capable of identifying novel miRNAs (24). As for the detection methods, we employed NGS, a high-throughput sequencing technology, which offers significant advantages, especially in the discovery of novel miRNAs. This makes NGS particularly well-suited for the preliminary nature of our study. However, we acknowledge the limitations that NGS requires extensive computational analysis and is costly, which may restrict its broader application in similar studies.

This study provides a preliminary exploration of miRNA applications in exosomes, but its sample size was limited because the relatively low incidence of DLBCL constrained patient recruitment. Additionally, due to the limited availability of public database data on DLBCL exosomal miRNAs, the data used for validation was also constrained. Future research with larger, independent, and prospectively collected cohorts will be necessary to validate these findings and assess their broader clinical relevance, which will be a key focus of our ongoing and future work.

There are several characteristics in this study, including the detection of miRNA expression using high-throughput sequencing technology, which significantly enhanced the reliability and confidence of the results. However, there are also several significant limitations in this study. First, due to the low prevalence of lymphoma, the sample size was relatively small, and the observations represent preliminary findings from a pilot investigation. Additionally, the limited sample size likely contributed to suboptimal and unstable WGCNA model fitting, while also increasing the risk of overfitting during feature selection. Although we used the independent GSE171272 dataset for validation, this external validation does not fully mitigate overfitting introduced during the discovery phase. In particular, selecting miRNAs based on WGCNA-derived modules and discriminative performance as measured by the AUC in a small discovery cohort may yield unstable signals and optimistic estimates. Therefore, the GSE171272 results should be interpreted as supportive evidence of reproducibility rather than definitive confirmation. Future work should prioritize well-powered validation and evaluate performance in real-world clinical settings. Nonetheless, the potential diagnostic value of exosomal miRNAs was still observed in the results.

Conclusions

In this study, exosomal miRNAs contribute to the diagnosis of DLBCL and may serve as non-invasive diagnostic biomarkers for DLBCL. This offers novel perspectives on utilizing exosomal miRNAs for the early identification of DLBCL and their potential clinical application. However, given the small size of the discovery cohort, these estimates are presented as exploratory and should be confirmed in larger independent cohorts.
