Adherence to Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments Guidelines in Stem Cell Studies in Animal Models of Parkinson’s Disease: A Systematic Review
Authors: Jong Mi Park, Bo Kyung Sung, Jae Young Myung, Sang Chul Lee, Dong Keon Yon, Jae Il Shin, Yong Wook Kim, John P A Ioannidis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines were published in 2010 and revised in 2020 to improve the reporting of preclinical animal studies. The present study evaluated the reporting of animal stem cell research related to Parkinson’s disease (PD).Materials and MethodsArticles on this topic published up to October 18, 2024, were systematically searched in relevant databases. For each individual study, we analyzed the proportion of subsets that were reported, partially reported, or unreported for each of the 38 ARRIVE 2.0 items. We compared reporting before and after the publication of the ARRIVE guidelines, and analyzed compliance score changes over time and by country.ResultsWe analyzed 90 animal studies related to PD. None of the studies reported sample size calculations, adverse event reporting, or humane endpoints. Additionally, only about 20% or less of the studies reported on strategies to minimize potential confounders, inclusion or exclusion of animal conditions, limitations, allocation methods, and pre-protocol enrollment. We observed no substantial improvement in adherence after publication of the guidelines or over time.ConclusionThe reporting of key design principles in animal stem cell research related to PD was insufficient both before and after the introduction of the ARRIVE guidelines in 2010. Both researchers and journal editors should pay more attention to the accurate, comprehensive reporting of animal studies.Keywords: ARRIVE guidelines, Parkinson’s disease, stem cell research, animal experimentation, guideline adherence, quality control

INTRODUCTION

Animal research is a key approach for gaining new translational insights and improving drug development. However, successful results from animal studies uncommonly translate into human benefits. A 2006 analysis of research articles found that only one-third of the interventional animal studies were replicated in randomized clinical trials in humans, and only 10% of these were approved for patient treatment.1 Animal studies are plagued by lack of randomization and blinding,2 inadequate reporting of methods and materials, and publication bias, wherein side effects or uncertain outcomes are not described.3 These can lead to an overestimation of the treatment effects in the literature of animal studies, non-reproducibility, and squandering of animal and scientific resources.

The U.S. National Center for the Replacement, Refinement, and Reduction of Animals in Research (NC3Rs) supported the development of the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines in 2010, aiming to improve the quality of preclinical animal experimentation and increase the transparency and accuracy of reporting.4 This 20-item checklist guides authors regarding reporting various aspects of their papers. The guidelines specify the minimum information that should be reported from animal research, such that readers can assess the methodological soundness of the experiment and other scientists can reproduce the experimental methods.5 An international working group consisting of journal editors, researchers, and statisticians from diverse fields was established by the NC3Rs in 2018 to revise and enhance these guidelines.6 In July 2020, a new version of the guidelines called ARRIVE 2.0 was published. The updated guidelines consist of 10 mandatory and 11 recommended items, including new information and prioritized items.7

Despite increased awareness regarding these animal research guidelines in the scientific community, the quality of reporting has not yet improved significantly in many research areas.8 In neuroscience, the quality of animal study reporting has been most frequently studied in literature on stroke.9,10 To our knowledge, this has not been assessed in Parkinson’s disease (PD)-related research.

Current treatments for PD improve early clinical symptoms but do not stop the disease or reverse the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons.11 Therefore, stem cell therapies with regenerative effects are being actively investigated as treatment options.12 Since the 1989 study that indicated improved function in PD-rats transplanted with fetal ventral midbrain tissue,13 there has been a steady stream of publications using human embryonic stem cells and human induced pluripotent stem cells, which include mesenchymal stem cells of various origins, in PD-related animal studies.14,15,16 The aim of this study is to systematically evaluate the quality of animal research reporting, with a focus on experimental stem cell research for PD treatment interventions. It also aims to assess changes in compliance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines by publication year, across countries, and through inter-rater agreement, and ultimately to identify strategies to strengthen compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines and improve the transparency and rigor of animal research reporting in PD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Search strategy

Two researchers (JMP and YWK) searched PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, CENTRAL, Scopus, and Web of Science databases through October 8, 2024. The search strategy was conducted using the following keywords: (“Parkinson Disease” OR “Parkinsonian Disorders”) AND (“Stem Cells” OR “Mesenchymal Stem Cells” OR “Neural Stem Cells” OR “Hematopoietic Stem Cells” OR “Stromal Cells” OR “Stem Cell Transplantation” OR “Mesenchymal Stem Cell Transplantation” OR “Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation” OR “Embryonic Stem Cells” OR “Cell Transplantation”) AND (“Animal Experimentation” OR “Animal Models”) (Supplementary Material, only online). Disagreements regarding the inclusion of papers were resolved through discussions between JMP, YWK, and JIS.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study inclusion criteria were based on the population, intervention, comparison, outcome, and study design (PICOS) framework. Population (P): all animal models of PD (all species, all sexes, 6-OHDA, MG-132, MPTP, Rotenone models). Intervention (I): treatment with stem cells or their derivatives. Comparison (C): none (placebo or no treatment). Outcome (O): any. Study design (S): originally published articles on in vivo animal experiments and any type of interventional animal studies. The following studies were excluded from the analysis: 1) studies involving non-stem cell therapies and 2) review articles, protocols, or in vitro studies.

Data extraction

Two authors (BKS and JYM) extracted the name of the first author, publication year, first author’s country, animal species, types of animal models, sample size, follow-up period, type of stem cells, actual number of cells injected, and cell injection route from the eligible articles. The primary outcome was whether each article is “fully reported,” “partially reported,” or “not reported” for the ARRIVE 2.0 guideline sub-items. The second outcome was the compliance score, calculated as the number of fully reported sub-items divided by the total of 38 sub-items per article. Each article was scored out of 1.

Evaluation of publication quality

Two reviewers (SBK and JYM) independently assessed each article for compliance with ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. Each reviewer was blinded to the other reviewer’s assessment. If discrepancies were found between the two reviewers, a third reviewer (JMP) performed a final assessment. To ensure consistency, they referred to the ARRIVE list of examples for each sub-item. Items were classified as “fully reported” if all essential components outlined in the guidelines were explicitly described in the manuscript, “partially reported” if some relevant details were included but lacked sufficient clarity or completeness, and “not reported” if the required information was missing or too vague to be meaningfully assessed. This standardized approach helped minimize subjectivity and improve the reliability of the scoring process.17 For the calculation of the compliance score, only items classified as “fully reported” were assigned a value of 1, while both “partially reported” and “not reported” items were assigned a value of 0. The final compliance score for each article was calculated by dividing the number of “fully reported” items by the total number of 38 sub-items, resulting in a score ranging from 0 to 1.

Statistical analysis

The proportion of “fully reported,” “partially reported,” or “not reported” was analyzed for each ARRIVE 2.0 sub-item. Since the ARRIVE guidelines were published in 2010, we categorized articles published up to 2010 as pre-ARRIVE studies and those published from 2011 onward as post-ARRIVE studies. We used the chi-squared test to compare differences in the number of fully reported studies between the pre- and post-ARRIVE periods for each sub-item. A simple linear regression analysis was also performed to determine if the year of publication was associated with the ARRIVE guideline compliance score. The model’s goodness-of-fit was assessed using R2, and statistical significance was evaluated with an F-test. The Durbin–Watson test was conducted to detect autocorrelation in the residuals. Additionally, Ramsey’s RESET test and residual plots were used to check for potential nonlinearity in the relationship. We examined differences in ARRIVE guideline compliance scores between countries classified according to the United Nations Human Development Index (HDI), which considers factors such as life expectancy, education, and per capita income.18 Countries were grouped into four categories based on their HDI scores: Very High Human Development (HDI≥0.800), High Human Development (HDI 0.700–0.799), Medium Human Development (HDI 0.550–0.699), and Low Human Development (HDI≤0.549). A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to determine whether significant differences existed in ARRIVE guideline compliance scores among the different HDI groups. Bartlett’s test was performed to assess the assumption of homogeneity of variance. Post-hoc comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons. The Kappa statistic was used to examine the interrater agreement between two raters. Values of ≤0.2, 0.21–0.39, 0.40–0.59, 0.60–0.79, 0.80–0.90, and ≥0.90 indicate disagreement, minimal agreement, weak agreement, moderate agreement, strong agreement, and almost perfect agreement, respectively.19 Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 for suggestive associations and at p<0.005 for significant associations.20 All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

RESULTS

Search results and study characteristics

A total of 90 studies with data from 2025 animals were analyzed using the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines checklist for adherence. A flowchart depicting the selection of studies is presented in Fig. 1. The characteristics of each study are summarized in Supplementary Table 1 (only online). The types of animals used in these studies were rats (n=72), mice (n=17), and monkey (n=1). Countries were classified based on the HDI, resulting in 48 studies from countries in the Very High Human Development group, 40 in the High Human Development group, and two in the Medium Human Development group, with none in the Low Human Development category (Supplementary Table 2, only online).

Quality of reporting published animal studies

We organized response data for the 38 items in a spreadsheet (Supplementary Table 3, only online). Table 1 and Fig. 2 summarize the reporting quality ratings for PD animal stem cell studies. Of the 38 items, 15 (39.5%) had compliance levels between 80%–100%, 9 (23.7%) had compliance between 50%–79%, and 14 items (36.8%) had compliance between 0%–49%.

Evaluation of the Quality of Reporting in Animal Stem Cell Research for Parkinson’s Disease, Based on the ARRIVE 2.0 Guidelines

Evaluation of reporting quality using ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines in animal stem cell research for Parkinson’s disease.

Sub-items reported in more than 90% of articles

Only one sub-item was reported in 100% of papers: experimental results (sub-item 6a). Other sub-items reported in more than 90% of articles included details of the control group (sub-item 1a), experimental unit (sub-item 1b), number of experimental units (sub-item 2a), exact value of n in each experimental group (sub-item 3c), experimental animals (species, strain, sex, age, or developmental stage) (sub-item 8a), experimental procedure (how) (sub-item 9a), reported statistics (sub-item 10a), research objectives (sub-item 13), and interpretation of results (sub-item 17a).

Sub-items with ≤50% reporting across articles

The sub-items reported in ≤50% of articles included: criteria used for including and excluding animals (sub-item 3a), reporting of animals not included in the analysis (sub-item 3b), details of the allocation method (sub-item 4a), methods to minimize confounders (sub-item 4b), who was aware of group allocation at different stages (sub-item 5), experimental procedures (where) (sub-item 9c), experimental procedures (why) (sub-item 9d), housing and husbandry conditions (sub-item 15), study limitations (sub-item 17b), generalizability to other species or human biology (sub-item 18), whether a protocol was prepared and registered (sub-item 19), and availability of data and materials (sub-item 20).

Sub-items that were not reported in any article

Three sub-items were not mentioned in any of the articles: sample size calculation (sub-item 2b), adverse event reporting (sub-item 16b), and description of humane endpoints (sub-item 16c).

Differences in ARRIVE guideline compliance rates before and after the release of the 2010 guidelines

Of the 90 articles, 36 were classified as pre-ARRIVE and 54 as post-ARRIVE. When comparing the number of fully reported articles by sub-item, statistically significant increases were observed for details of statistical methods (sub-item 7a) (p=0.004), availability of data and materials (sub-item 20) (p<0.001), and declaration of conflicts of interest (sub-item 21a) (p<0.001). The sub-items that showed a suggestive increase included: details of the allocation method (sub-item 4a) (p=0.016), assessment of whether data met assumptions of the statistical approach (sub-item 7b) (p=0.040), information about experimental animals (species, strain, sex, age, or developmental stage) (sub-item 8a) (p=0.034), accurate summary of the abstract (sub-item 11) (p=0.023), description of how animal species were used (sub-item 12b) (p=0.026), and interpretation of results (sub-item 17a) (p=0.016). Conversely, the sub-item that showed a decrease was ethical statement of approval (sub-item 14) (p=0.021) (Fig. 3). The remaining sub-items were not statistically significantly different between pre-ARRIVE and post-ARRIVE. A simple linear regression analysis showed a significant positive association between the year of publication and the ARRIVE guideline compliance score (β=0.008, p<0.001), explaining 19.4% of the variance (R2=0.194). The Durbin–Watson statistic (1.776) indicated no serious autocorrelation. Ramsey’s RESET test indicated no model misspecification (F=1.09, p=0.359), supporting the use of a linear model. The residuals versus fitted values plot showed a random scatter around zero, indicating no evidence of heteroscedasticity (Supplementary Fig. 1A, only online). The Q-Q plot also demonstrated that the residuals closely followed a normal distribution (Supplementary Fig. 1B, only online). These results suggest that the assumptions of linear regression were reasonably met. These findings indicate a temporal increase in compliance scores; however, the absolute increase in compliance scores was very small (Fig. 4).

Differences in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines across countries

A one-way ANOVA was conducted to assess differences in compliance scores among the High, Medium, and Very High HDI groups. The results indicated no statistically significant differences among the groups (F(2, 87)=2.95, p=0.057). Bartlett’s test for equal variances confirmed homogeneity of variance across groups (χ2(2)=0.4786, p=0.787). Post-hoc comparisons using the Bonferroni adjustment revealed no significant pairwise differences (p>0.05). However, the contrast between the Very High and High groups showed p=0.069. These findings suggest that compliance scores do not significantly differ among the three groups, although there was a trend toward higher scores in the Very High group compared to the High group (Fig. 5).

Box plot of compliance scores by country.

Inter-rater reliability of items in the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines

The summarized Kappa concordance metrics for each of the ARRIVE 2.0 sub-items are also presented in Table 1. Overall, inter-rater reliability varied substantially across sub-items, with Kappa values ranging from 0.18 to 1. Out of the 38 sub-items, 15 (39.5%) agreed almost perfectly, 4 (10.5%) agreed strongly, 9 (23.7%) showed moderate agreement, 4 (10.5%) had weak agreement, and 4 (10.5%) indicated minimal agreement. In particular, the items “Interpretation of results (sub-item 17a)” and “How animal species were used (sub-item 12b)” showed inter-rater disagreement, with a Kappa value of 0.18.

DISCUSSION

Thirteen years after the original introduction of the ARRIVE guidelines, an analysis of the literature from animal stem cell research related to PD revealed that there is insufficient reporting of important aspects such as study design and scientific methodology, with little improvement, if any, over time. This could be because researchers are unaware of the ARRIVE guidelines, but it could also be that they are aware yet choose not to describe the details of the study for reasons of time, cost, or convenience. However, it is important for researchers and publishers to follow the guidelines. Below, we explain why reporting under each subsection of the guidelines is important.

According to our research, none of the papers reported sample size calculations (sub-item 2b). Accurate sample size calculations are essential for conducting reliable and ethical research. Properly determining sample size ensures that studies have sufficient statistical power to detect true effects, thereby minimizing the risk of Type I and Type II errors. A Type I error, or false positive, occurs when a true null hypothesis is incorrectly rejected, leading researchers to conclude that an effect exists when it does not. Conversely, a Type II error, or false negative, happens when a false null hypothesis is not rejected, causing researchers to overlook a genuine effect. Small sample sizes may lack the power to detect meaningful differences, increasing the likelihood of Type II errors.21 On the other hand, while increasing sample size may increase statistical power and reduce the likelihood of Type II errors, larger sample sizes may lead to the detection of statistically significant differences that may not be practically meaningful, potentially increasing the risk of Type I errors if not interpreted cautiously.22 It also results in unnecessary use of resources and may raise ethical concerns, particularly in studies involving animals or human subjects.23 Appropriate sample size determination is fundamental to conducting robust, ethical, and resource-efficient research in various areas of study, minimizing the risk of both Type I and Type II errors, and increasing the overall reliability of research results.

The absence of reporting on adverse events (sub-item 16b) and humane endpoints (sub-item 16c) in the reviewed studies raises significant concerns regarding both scientific validity and animal welfare. Adverse event reporting is essential as it provides critical safety data that can inform future clinical trials, helps identify unexpected complications, and contributes to assessing the overall risk-benefit ratio of research. Without this information, the transition from preclinical to clinical research may be jeopardized, increasing the risk of unforeseen issues in human subjects. Additionally, the lack of transparency in reporting adverse events limits other researchers’ ability to fully understand the potential risks of studied interventions.24

Similarly, the absence of humane endpoints in animal research is concerning, as they play a crucial role in minimizing animal suffering, maintaining scientific data validity, and ensuring ethical integrity. Failing to establish humane endpoints may indicate inadequate consideration of animal welfare, potentially leading to unnecessary distress and inefficient use of resources.25,26 To address these issues, future studies should implement and document clear adverse event monitoring protocols, establish humane endpoints before experimentation, and regularly assess and report their application throughout the study. Enhancing reporting in these areas will not only improve the ethical rigor of research but also strengthen the reliability and translational potential of animal studies.

Furthermore, the absence of randomization (sub-item 4a, reported in 27.7%) can introduce selection bias, as researchers may unintentionally assign healthier animals or those expected to respond better to the treatment group, potentially overestimating treatment effects.1,5 This issue is exacerbated by the low rate of assessor blinding (sub-item 5, reported in 12.2%), which increases the risk of observer bias, particularly in subjective assessments like behavioral tests or image analysis.3 Further, details of the experimental procedures and environmental conditions were not well defined (sub-items 9c and 9d), and descriptions of animal species and models used in the studies were lacking (sub-items 3a, 3b, and 8b), limiting reproducibility and making comparison between studies difficult. They also contribute to publication bias, as studies with negative or inconclusive results are less likely to be published, further skewing the literature. Finally, the lack of rigorous experimental design, including proper randomization and blinding, raises ethical concerns regarding animal welfare, as it undermines scientific validity and may result in unnecessary animal experimentation. The results of our study aligned with the analysis conducted by Vesterinen, et al.,10 regarding animal research in the cerebrovascular field, which revealed that only 8% of the reviewed studies practiced allocation concealment and only 1% reported the sample size.

Statistical accuracy is crucial in research reporting. In our study, statistical methods (sub-item 7a, reported in 76.7%) and whether data met assumptions of statistical approaches (sub-item 7b, reported in 88.9%) were assessed. However, the lack of detailed statistical reporting, particularly regarding normality tests, multiple comparison adjustments, and confidence intervals, poses challenges for result interpretation. Normality assumptions are fundamental to many statistical tests; without verification, the validity of analyses may be compromised, leading to inaccurate p-values and unreliable conclusions.21 Additionally, failing to adjust for multiple comparisons increases the risk of Type I errors (false positives), where significant findings may arise by chance rather than reflecting a true effect.20 The absence of confidence intervals further limits data interpretability, as these provide a range for the true population parameter, aiding in assessing precision and practical significance.19

In animal model studies on abdominal wall hernias, the quality of animal study reporting improved after the introduction of the ARRIVE guidelines compared to before their introduction.27 However, in most subject areas, there was no substantive change in the quality of animal study reporting before or after the introduction of the guidelines in 2010.28,29,30,31 We also found no major differences in post- versus pre-ARRIVE compliance, with the exception of a couple of items, and improvements over time were small, if any, when all years were considered in a continuum. Despite the introduction of ARRIVE and its updates, such as ARRIVE 2.0, compliance remains suboptimal due to several factors. The guidelines’ comprehensiveness can make implementation challenging, particularly in resource-limited settings. While awareness has grown, gaps in training and practical application persist, highlighting the need for hands-on guidance. Additionally, inconsistent enforcement by journals results in variable reporting quality. Strengthening journal policies—such as requiring ARRIVE checklists with submissions and incorporating compliance into peer review—could enhance adherence. Incorporating ARRIVE compliance into funding requirements could further incentivize researchers. Funders could mandate adherence as a condition for grant approval and conduct periodic audits to ensure compliance. The minimal annual increase in compliance observed in our study suggests that awareness alone is insufficient to drive meaningful change. Stricter journal policies, funding agency requirements, and improved training on reporting standards are needed to enhance adherence. Addressing these challenges through policy reinforcement and systematic monitoring will help improve ARRIVE compliance and promote greater transparency and reproducibility in animal research.

The results of the one-way ANOVA showed no statistically significant differences in ARRIVE guideline compliance scores among countries grouped by HDI (very high, high, and medium), although a non-significant trend for some potential difference was observed between the very high and high groups. This suggests that certain underlying factors may influence adherence to the ARRIVE guidelines. Potential contributors include country-specific differences such as the regulatory framework for animal research, the availability of research funding, and the stringency of journal editorial policies.32 Countries with greater regulatory oversight may enforce stricter adherence to reporting guidelines, potentially leading to higher compliance scores. In addition, countries with a higher HDI may have better access to training resources, infrastructure, and a greater emphasis on ethical standards, leading to more rigorous compliance. Cultural differences in research transparency and accountability may also play a role. Future studies should explore factors influencing researchers’ awareness and implementation of the ARRIVE guidelines, including analysis of national policies, researcher surveys, and journal policies to identify key barriers and facilitators of compliance.

Our analysis of inter-rater reliability revealed that while half of the sub-items (50.0%) demonstrated almost perfect to strong agreement, some sub-items (15.8%) showed minimal agreement. The distinction between “fully reported” and “partially reported” had the greatest impact on inter-rater variation, as raters differed in their judgments regarding the sufficiency of each item’s description. Notably, “Interpretation of results (sub-item 17a)” and “How animal species were used (sub-item 12b)” yielded low Kappa values, indicating potential ambiguity and subjectivity in their evaluation criteria. The complexity and subjective nature of data interpretation likely contributed to variability among raters, particularly in the absence of clear guidelines defining what constitutes a well-interpreted result. Similarly, insufficient details on species selection, usage, and justification led to inconsistent assessments in evaluating how animal species were used. These findings highlight the need for clearer evaluation criteria and more detailed reporting by researchers to enhance consistency and reliability in assessment.

This study had certain limitations. First, the number of papers included for analysis was relatively small, and it is possible that several studies in the field remained unpublished, with varying rates of publication bias across countries and depending on methodological features of the research. Hence, caution should be exercised when interpreting country-specific compliance scores. Second, observer-dependent bias may have affected the assessment of reporting quality. The analysis was conducted independently by two researchers without any communication between them. This may have led to different interpretations of the criteria. To minimize observer bias, a third rater reviewed and discussed each subsection of the paper to check for discrepancies. Third, some sub-items may not have been reported in the articles as they were considered obvious. For instance, in animal studies using isoflurane as an anesthetic, the dose or method of administration may not have been mentioned because inhalation is the only mode of administration. In other areas and sub-items, it was unclear what assumptions to make when a sub-item was not reported. Fourth, if any information was presented only in the Supplementary Materials of the articles, it may have been missed by our investigators. The investigators first checked the main body of the article and then searched for any mention of the supplementary material within the main body. Therefore, some information presented only in the Supplementary Materials may have been overlooked. Lastly, although the subgroup analysis based on HDI provided a structured approach to assess reporting quality, the geographical distribution of the included studies was notably imbalanced. Among the 90 studies, 60 (66.7%) originated from Asia, followed by 15 from North America, 9 from Europe, 3 from Africa, 2 from South America, and only 1 from Oceania (Supplementary Table 3, only online). This uneven distribution may limit the generalizability of the findings and introduce potential regional bias in evaluating compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Differences in research infrastructure, funding availability, and editorial policies across continents may influence reporting practices; therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting cross-country comparisons.

The results of our study indicate a persistent deficiency in the reporting of crucial design principles and other aspects of animal stem cell research related to PD, both before and after the introduction of the ARRIVE guidelines in 2010. We strongly advise researchers to adhere to the ARRIVE guidelines when reporting their studies. We also suggest that the editors of journals publishing animal studies should actively advocate the inclusion of the ARRIVE guidelines in their author guidelines and find ways to improve the fidelity of reporting in published articles. It should be noted that reporting is only one part of the research pipeline, which includes a wide range of other activities such as setting a research agenda, conducting experiments properly, and using strong laboratory and statistical methods. Faithful reporting alone may not be sufficient to markedly improve the overall rigor of animal research.33 However, improved documentation and transparency about the conducted research may represent a useful step in this direction.
