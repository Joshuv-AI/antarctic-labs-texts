T Cell Exhaustion and Function State Correlated With Outcomes in Patients With Intra‐Abdominal Candidiasis
Authors: Yawen Xie, Jiahui Zhang, Guoyu Zhao, Xianli Lei, Hao Wang, Huaiwu He, Na Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionIntra‐abdominal candidiasis (IAC) still has a high mortality rate despite prompt antifungal therapy due to immunosuppression. T cell exhaustion is an important manifestation of immunosuppression. This study aimed to explore the expression pattern of exhaustion‐related molecules in patients with IAC and determine the possible association between dynamic trends and prognosis.MethodsPatients with IAC were enrolled, and non‐IAC critically ill patients were included as controls. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were analysed by flow cytometry to determine the expression levels of T cell exhaustion‐related markers. T cells isolated from PBMCs were stimulated by IL‐2 in α‐CD3/α‐CD28 medium to compare intracellular cytokine production and proliferative capacity.ResultsA total of 34 patients with IAC and 35 controls were enrolled in this study. Patients with IAC had a significant decrease in lymphocytes. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from patients with IAC had a significantly higher level of immune checkpoint molecules, such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1), cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA4), and B and T lymphocyte attenuator (BTLA), and exhibited a consistently impaired cytokine‐secreting function. Increased exhaustion‐associated molecules and deteriorating dysfunction were detected in non‐survivors, while survivors demonstrated the opposite tendency. Patients with impaired granzyme B (GZMB) production function who died from IAC over the course of the disease had higher levels of PD‐1 expression in CD8+ T cells.ConclusionsT cells from patients with IAC displayed an immunosuppressive phenotype of T cell exhaustion. Sustaining exhaustion status and deteriorated dysfunction were associated with poor prognosis. Persistently increased PD‐1 expression and impaired GZMB secretion in CD8+ T cells were linked to worse outcomes. Immunoadjuvants reversing T cell exhaustion have promising prospects in treating IAC and improving prognosis.Keywords: cytokine, immunosuppression, intra‐abdominal candidiasis, lymphocyte, T cell exhaustion

Introduction

Intra‐abdominal candidiasis (IAC) is the most common subtype of deep‐seated candidiasis resulting from the intra‐abdominal establishment of Candida albicans with etiological evidence from abdominal puncture, intraoperative collection, or intra‐abdominal drainage fluid [1]. Although IAC occurs in less than 1% of critically ill patients, its incidence has increased, and it has a poor prognosis, with mortality rates ranging from 27% to 40% [2, 3, 4].

Emerging evidence suggests that impaired immune defence plays a crucial role in IAC [5, 6]. The T cell‐mediated immune response is indispensable for defence against fungal infections. Patients with candidiasis displayed an impaired T cell response. Misme‐Aucouturier et al. and our team reported a significant loss of T cells in invasive fungal infection, particularly CD8+ T cells, which was associated with its diagnosis and prognosis [7, 8]. Decreased T cell count is considered a hallmark of T cell exhaustion, typically resulting from persistent antigen stimulation and increased negative co‐stimulatory molecules, including programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1), cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA4), B and T lymphocyte attenuator (BTLA), and others [9]. Exhausted T cells also exhibit decreased expression of the IL‐7 receptor (IL‐7R), an indicator of functional status [10], accompanied by diminished cytokine production and proliferative capacity [11]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been explored for immunotherapy of invasive fungal infection [12]. For instance, Mellinghoff et al. observed that T cells from patients with candidiasis demonstrated enhanced cytokine levels after treatment with the PD‐1 inhibitor nivolumab in vitro [13]. Similarly, anti‐PD‐L1 therapy augmented antifungal immunity in murine models of invasive pulmonary mucormycosis [14]. An immunosuppressed phenotype of T cell exhaustion has also been observed in candidemia [13, 15].

Although T cell exhaustion has been established in candidemia, its status and dynamic changes remain unclear in IAC. Investigating whether and how T cell exhaustion is associated with IAC progression and clinical outcomes is expected to enable early diagnosis and reveal potential targets for immunotherapy.

Therefore, we conducted a prospective, observational study and hypothesised that T cell exhaustion exists in IAC and is associated with poor prognosis. The aims were to compare T cell exhaustion and function between IAC and non‐IAC critically ill patients and evaluate how dynamic changes are related to prognosis. This provided a better understanding of the early diagnosis of IAC and possible immunotherapy by alleviating T cell exhaustion and immunosuppression.

Materials and Methods

Subjects and Study Design

This single‐center prospective observational study was conducted between March 2023 and March 2024 in the ICU of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH). It was approved by the Ethics Commission of PUMCH (Approval number: I‐22PJ998). The study enrolled patients who signed informed consent. The registration number is ChiCTR2300069020 at chictr.org.cn.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarised in Table 1. Enrolled patients needed to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) older than 18 years; (2) diagnosed with sepsis according to the third international consensus definition [16]; (3) expected length of stay in ICU over 48 h; (4) diagnosed with IAC. Sepsis and IAC were diagnosed by skilled physicians. The diagnosis process is described as follows: (1) etiological identification of Candida by microscopic observation or culture from abdominal drainage fluid in tubes implanted within 24 h before sampling; (2) positive smear or culture of Candida from intra‐abdominal specimens collected during surgery or percutaneous abdominal puncture; (3) the identification of Candida in bile, biliary drainage fluid, and biopsy of abdominal organs; (4) conclusive evidence of Candida in patients with secondary or tertiary peritonitis with no other pathogen [3]. The participants in the control group were non‐IAC critically ill patients who primarily suffered from trauma or surgery. The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) pregnancy or lactation; (2) HIV infection; (3) neutropenia due to any reason; (4) malignant tumour; (5) autoimmune disease at active stage; (6) viral hepatitis; (7) treatment with high‐dose glucocorticoid (over 300 mg/d of hydrocortisone or 15 mg/d of cortisone) or other immunosuppressants.

Clinical and Laboratory Assessment

Clinical data were collected from electronic medical records. All patients had to undergo a comprehensive clinical evaluation on admission to the ICU. Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) were calculated within 24 h of ICU admission. Additionally, 28‐day mortality was recorded during the follow‐up period.

Blood samples were sent to the PUMCH clinical laboratory for routine laboratory tests, including infection‐associated parameters (CRP and absolute lymphocyte counts) and clinical biochemical indices (creatinine, total bilirubin, and albumin). Specimens of abdominal drainage were cultured for the identification of fungi in the clinical microbiology laboratory.

Sample Collection and Immune Parameters

Upon patients being diagnosed with intra‐abdominal infection (IAI), blood was drawn in EDTA anticoagulant tubes on the first and seventh day of IAI diagnosis for assessing lymphocyte subsets and other immune parameters. Intra‐abdominal drainage from these patients with IAI (containing IAC patients) was also retained for fungal culture as early as possible, within 24 h after indwelling intraperitoneal drain tube. If Candida spp. was confirmed by the follow‐up culture results, the patients would be enrolled as the IAC group. Otherwise, the patient was excluded due to a lack of positive pathogen evidence. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated (P8610‐200 mL, Solarbio, China) to analyse the lymphocyte subset in the infection laboratory. PBMCs were stained with multiple fluorescent antibodies (listed in Table S1) to identify TBNK subsets (3‐colour EPICS‐XL flow cytometer; Beckman Coulter, Brea, California), as previously described [17]. The remaining cells were cryopreserved and stored in liquid nitrogen.

Other immunological indicators, including the levels of IgA, IgG, IgM, complement 3 (C3), and C4 in the serum, were determined by rate nephelometry (Array 360; Beckman Coulter). Concentrations of several cytokines (IL6, IL8, IL10, and TNFα) were analysed by cytokine combination assay kit (P110100403, Cell‐genebio, China).

Measurement of T Cell Exhaustion‐Related Indicators and T Cell Functions

The cryopreserved PBMCs were used for flow cytometry to assay T cell exhaustion and function, different from the previously described TBNK subset analysis. All antibodies are listed in Table S1. The remaining cryopreserved PBMCs were thawed and then divided into two parts. One part was prepared for exhaustion‐related receptor analysis using antibodies of Panels 1 and 2 mentioned in Table S1. The other part was used for T cell isolation using immunomagnetic beads (130‐096‐535, Miltenyi, Germany). Sorted T lymphocytes were stimulated with α‐CD3 (5 μg/mL, 300438, BioLegend, San Diego, USA), α‐CD28 (1 μg/mL, 377804, BioLegend, San Diego, USA), and IL2 (10 ng/mL, 200‐02‐10UG, PeproTech, USA) for 72 h [18]. Intracellular cytokines were almost undetectable in unstimulated T cells compared to stimulated T cells (Figure S1). Harvested T cells were treated with the protein transport inhibitor (BD GolgiStop, 554,724, BD Bioscience, USA) for 6 h. Afterward, the stimulated T cells were prepared for evaluating the level of cytokines by flow cytometry using antibodies of Panel 3. The workflow is displayed in Figure 1.

Cells were stained with surface marker antibodies for 20 minutes. Fixation and permeabilisation were performed according to the instructions (00‐5523‐00‐1, eBioscience, California, USA). Intracellular antibodies were incubated with cells overnight at 4°C. The measurement was conducted using the BD LSRFortessa Cell Analyser, and FCS files were processed by the FlowJo software (version 10.8.1, BD Bioscience, USA). Statistical Analyses

The data conforming to a normal distribution were compared using Student's t‐test. The non‐normally distributed data were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Chi‐square or Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical variables. Additionally, patients with IAC were divided into survivor and non‐survivor groups according to 28‐day mortality. Comparison between day 1 and 7 was analysed by a paired t‐test or a Mann–Whitney U test in survivor and non‐survivor groups. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to exclude the potential impact of disease severity on T cell exhaustion. “MatchIt” package (version 4.6.0) was used to conduct PSM with the “nearest” method at the ratio of 1:1, and the calliper was set at 0.2. Statistical significance was defined at the level of a 2‐sided p value of less than 0.05. Statistical analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.3.2, New Zealand). Visualisation was performed by R (version 4.3.2, New Zealand) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5, San Diego, USA) software.

Results

Patient Enrollment and Characteristics

Admissions to the ICU were screened for potential candidates daily. A total of 69 patients were enrolled in this study, among whom 34 were diagnosed with IAC. IAC and non‐IAC patients had no significant difference in age, gender, SOFA, comorbidities, mechanical ventilation, vasopressor, creatinine, total bilirubin, and albumin levels. However, patients with IAC had significantly higher APACHE II scores and CRP levels with increased demands for renal replacement treatment (p < 0.001). Experienced physicians confirmed that nine deaths were attributed to IAC. Patients had a statistically significantly higher mortality rate (26.5% versus 0%, p = 0.002). The baseline characteristics are displayed in Table 2. Candida albicans (55.9%) was the most common species among the Candida spp. found in IAC patients, followed by Candida glabrata (26.5%), Candida tropicalis (17.6%), and Candida parapsilosis (14.7%). Other species included Candida krusei , Candida guilliermondii , and Candida nivariensis (Table S2). The distribution of Candida spp. was consistent with a previous study [19].

Difference in Immune Parameters Between IAC and Non‐IAC Patients

IL‐6 and TNF‐α were 1.3‐fold (p = 0.017) and 7‐fold (p < 0.001) higher in patients with IAC than in the control group, respectively (Table S3). IAC patients had a significantly decreased absolute lymphocyte count, more than 30% lower than in the control group (Figure 2 and Table S4). Additionally, B (p < 0.001), NK (p < 0.001), and T cells (p = 0.005) were significantly reduced in the IAC group (Table S4). The percentage of Treg cells was twice as high in IAC patients (1.05% versus 2.88%, p = 0.002). Both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell counts were significantly lower in IAC patients (0.59‐ and 0.65‐fold, respectively), as were counts of CD28+CD4+ and CD28+CD8+ T cells (p = 0.002 and < 0.001, respectively). These findings indicated a distinct immunosuppression in patients with IAC.

Characterisation of T Cell Exhaustion Marker Expression at the Onset of IAC

Considering the role of T cell exhaustion in immune dysfunction, the expression of exhaustion‐associated receptors on unstimulated T cells from PBMCs was analysed (Table S4). The expression of BTLA, PD‐1, PD‐L1, and CTLA4 significantly increased on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from patients with IAC (Figure 3 and Figure S3). The TIM3‐positive percentage of CD4+ T cells was 1.5 times higher in the IAC group (p = 0.041). CD127 (also known as IL‐7R) expression was decreased in CD8+ T cells of the IAC group (almost 0.8 times that of the control group) (Figure S3).

PSM was performed to adjust for the baseline differences in APACHE II and CRP. A total of 48 participants were matched by APACHE II score. Inhibitory receptor expression (BTLA, PD‐1, PD‐L1, and CTLA4) remained significantly elevated in the IAC group (Table S5). PSM analysis based on CRP (n = 32) yielded broadly consistent findings with pre‐match results (Table S6). T cells expressed significantly increased exhaustion‐related receptors in IAC patients, but there was no significant difference in CD127 expression on CD8+ T cells. Additionally, 40 patients were matched on the absolute lymphocyte count (Table S7). Consistently, IAC patients had higher expression of exhaustion‐related markers in T cells. These results confirmed that T cell exhaustion was a hallmark of IAC.

Analysis of T Cell Ability of Cytokine Production and Proliferation Between IAC and Non‐IAC Patients

To further evaluate T cell function, intracellular expression of cytokines was compared between IAC and control groups after in vitro stimulation of sorted T cells with CD3/CD28/IL2 for 72 h (Table 3). Production of IFNγ and GZMB was impaired in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from patients with IAC (Figure 3). IL‐2 expression was also significantly suppressed in CD4+ T cells of the IAC group (positive percentage: 10.4% versus 3.17%, p < 0.001; MFI: 112 versus 90.7, p = 0.021). Additionally, the Ki67 positive percentage was significantly reduced in CD4+ T cells from IAC patients (11.5% versus 5.6%, p = 0.044; Figure S4). Moreover, PSM based on APAHCE II, CRP, and lymphocyte count further confirmed impaired cytokine‐producing function in IAC patients, such as IFNγ and GZMB (Tables S8–S10). These results supported impaired cytokine production and proliferation in IAC patients, indicating suppressed T cell function.

Comparison of T Cell Exhaustion‐Related Indicators and T Cell Function Between Survivors and Non‐Survivors of IAC

To determine whether T cell exhaustion receptors and function correlated with prognosis, IAC patients were further divided into survivor and non‐survivor groups based on 28‐day mortality (Tables S11 and S12). T cell exhaustion receptors and cytokines were assayed on unstimulated and stimulated T cells, respectively. There was no significant difference in exhaustion markers or cytokines in CD4+ T cells on day 1. However, CD8+ T cells from the non‐survivor group expressed increased PD‐1 (positive percentage: 51.5% versus 33.2%, p < 0.001) and PD‐L1 (positive percentage: 16.1% versus 5.06%, p = 0.001) with decreased CD127 on day 1. Similarly, GZMB level in CD8+ T cells of the non‐survivors was 0.6 times less than that of the survivors on day 1. These data indicated that T cell exhaustion and function were deteriorated in non‐survivors at an early IAC stage, particularly in CD8+ T cells.

Non‐survivors had decreased CD127 (55.2% versus 67.3%, p = 0.021) and impaired cytokine production in CD4+ T cells on day 7, including IL‐2 and GZMB (which were more than 2.5‐fold lower than that in survivors). CD8+ T cells from non‐survivors on day 7 also exhibited decreased GZMB production with increased PD‐1 and PD‐L1. These results revealed that T cell exhaustion and function had a close association with IAC prognosis, particularly the expression of PD‐1, PD‐L1, and GZMB in CD8+ T cells.

Dynamic Changes in Immune Parameters Among IAC Patients

To track the dynamics of immune status during IAC, pairwise analysis was performed to compare the changes from day 1 to 7 between survivors and non‐survivors (Table S13). Lymphocyte, B cell, and NK cell counts remained stable. T cell count increased from day 1 to 7 in survivors (311 ± 186 versus 369 ± 200, p = 0.053), with similar upward trends in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. However, no significant change in CD4+ and CD8+ T cell count was observed in non‐survivors, suggesting that CD4+ and CD8+ T cell deficiency persisted during the IAC course.

Dynamic Changes of Indicators Related to T Cell Exhaustion in Patients With IAC

The dynamic changes of T cell exhaustion markers exhibited opposite trends between survivors and non‐survivors (Figure 4, Table S14). PD‐1 and TIM3 on CD4+ T cells significantly decreased on day 7 compared to day 1 in survivors but increased in non‐survivors. A similar phenomenon of PD‐1 and CTLA4 was also observed on CD8+ T cells. CTLA4 expression in CD4+ T cells decreased significantly in survivors (2.76% versus 2.53% on days 1 and 7, respectively, p = 0.011), while CD127 expression decreased on CD4+ T cells in non‐survivors (65.4% versus 55.23% on days 1 and 7, respectively, p = 0.005). It was further inferred that T cell exhaustion deteriorated in the non‐survivor group with IAC progression.

Dynamic Changes of Parameters Related to T Cell Function and Proliferation in Patients With IAC

The changing trend of intracellular cytokines and Ki67 was compared between survivors and non‐survivors to investigate the dynamics of T cell function and proliferation (Figure 4 and Table S15). T cells for cytokine assays were stimulated in vitro. Survivors demonstrated an increasing trend of GZMB in CD4+ (8.36%–9.61%, p = 0.001) and CD8+ T cells (38.2%–44.8%, p = 0.005) with elevated IL‐2 production in CD4+ T cells (4.13% versus 5.46%, p = 0.005). However, non‐survivors failed to exhibit significant improvement in T cell function. Consistent with the dynamics of T cell exhaustion, these results reinforced the association between T cell exhaustion, function impairment, and IAC prognosis. Particularly, sustained increased PD‐1 and decreased GZMB production were associated with a worse prognosis of IAC.

Discussion

IAC is emerging as a common aetiology of critically ill patients with high mortality. Although it has been demonstrated that T cell exhaustion is associated with immune dysfunction during candidemia, its role in IAC remained underexplored [13, 15]. This study aimed to determine whether T cell exhaustion is an immune hallmark of IAC and how its dynamic changes affect prognosis, providing novel insights for diagnosis and immunotherapy of IAC.

A total of 69 patients were enrolled in the study, out of which 34 were diagnosed with IAC. These patients exhibited a distinct immunophenotype characterised by lymphopenia and T cell exhaustion. Patients with invasive fungal disease had a 22.1% incidence of lymphopenia [20]. The decreased lymphocyte count indicated compromised immune function because immunosuppressed patients were excluded.

PSM analysis further confirmed that T cell exhaustion and dysfunction in patients with IAC were independent of lymphocyte count. Therefore, lymphocyte count was not a preexisting underlying factor. However, whether lymphopenia resulted from T cell exhaustion warranted further investigation. Additionally, a notable decrease in lymphocyte subsets (such as CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells) was observed, consistent with our previous study [8], suggesting immunosuppression of IAC. Increased Treg cells contributed to immunosuppression, aligning with the results from murine models of Candida bloodstream infection [21]. Moreover, a decrease in CD28+ T cells was observed in agreement with previous studies [8]. CD4+ T cells displayed an exhaustion phenotype in recurrent sepsis, characterised by a decrease in CD28 and an increase in PD‐1 and Tim‐3 levels [22]. It suggested an immunosuppressive profile in IAC.

T cell exhaustion is characterised by immune dysfunction and decreased proliferation ability, resulting in impaired pathogen elimination [11]. Increased expression of exhaustion‐related markers (including BTLA, PD‐1, PD‐L1, CTLA4, and TIM3) was observed in T cells of IAC with decreased IL‐7R in CD8+ T cells, which were associated with worse outcomes. Previous in vitro studies demonstrated increased CTLA4 and LAG3 after Candida albicans stimulation [23]. Another study revealed increased PD‐1 and PD‐L1, whereas TIM3 and BTLA insignificantly changed in candidemia [15]. This finding differed slightly from our results, which was probably due to a different study population (abdominal infection versus bloodstream infection). An increased expression of LAG3 was observed in candidiasis by Mellinghoff et al., which was statistically insignificant in our study [13]. Different control subjects contributed to the discrepancy because this study used healthy candidates as controls rather than critically ill non‐IAC patients.

Function impairment is another evidence of T cell exhaustion [11, 24]. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from patients with IAC exhibited a decreased production of IFNγ and GZMB. The ability of IL‐2 production and proliferation was significantly suppressed in CD4+ T cells from IAC patients. The function impairment in exhausted T cells was consistent with previous studies on sepsis [25, 26]. Preclinical studies validated the antifungal role of cytokines. Stimulation with Candida albicans in vitro enhanced the secretion of IFNγ and IL‐10 in CD4+ T cells [27]. However, IL‐10 remained unchanged in our study, which was probably caused by differences between the in vitro experiment and clinical research. Inhibiting the Th1 profile of IFNγ and IL‐2 production was conducive to severe fungal dissemination [28]. GZMB played a critical role in immune defence in response to fungal infection. A preclinical study confirmed the correlation between T cell exhaustion and decreased GZMB expression by PD‐1 blockade [29]. The above findings supported that T cell dysfunction aggravated fungal infection, and exhausted T cells with decreased cytokines were correlated with IAC.

However, there was no study that constantly monitored the changes in T cell exhaustion and function status in patients with IAC and explored their relationship with prognosis. Therefore, our study aimed to fill the gap by comparing the dynamic trends of indicators between survivors and non‐survivors of IAC. Sustained T cell exhaustion and dysfunction were associated with poor IAC prognosis. Increased expression of PD‐1 with a rising trend and persistent decreased GZMB production were consistently observed in CD8+ T cells from IAC non‐survivors.

Prolonged T cell exhaustion and sustained immune paralysis probably result in poor prognosis [13, 15]. It has been demonstrated that ICI is promising as an immunotherapy for fungal infections. Nivolumab enhanced IFNγ and IL‐2 secretion in vitro to exert an antifungal role [13]. An in vivo study also validated that PD‐1 blockade attenuated aspergillosis secondary to sepsis in exhausted T cells by increasing IFNγ production [30]. However, preclinical and clinical evidence for ICIs in IAC still require further investigation.

The differences between this study and that of Mellinghoff et al., which similarly focused on T cell exhaustion in candidiasis, are as follows [13]. First, our study provided evidence that T cell exhaustion was a potential indicator for early diagnosis of IAC and was expected to serve as a target for early immunotherapy. We collected blood samples at the onset of IAC, rather than after diagnosis, while sample collection was performed after candidiasis diagnosis in the study by Mellinghoff et al. Diagnosing invasive candidiasis depends on fungal culture, which is time‐consuming. Consequently, in addition to validating previous findings on IAC by Mellinghoff et al., our work further demonstrated the role of T cell exhaustion in the early diagnosis of IAC. Second, both studies concluded that, in terms of prognosis, non‐survivors had increased expression of T cell exhaustion‐related receptors. Based on this, we further dynamically evaluated T cell exhaustion and function on the first and seventh day of IAC onset. Therefore, it was revealed that sustained T cell exhaustion and persistent T cell dysfunction were associated with poor prognosis in IAC, which further supported the previous findings. Third, the study design was different in both studies. Mellinghoff et al. enrolled patients with invasive candidiasis (including candidemia and deep‐seated candidiasis), whereas our study exclusively focused on IAC. In addition to inhibitory receptors, we also evaluated the cytokine profiles and revealed T cell dysfunction in IAC manifested as decreased IFNγ and granzyme B, enriching the previous findings by Mellinghoff et al. Moreover, Mellinghoff et al. intervened in T cells in vitro with a PD‐1 inhibitor and revealed an enhanced antifungal immune response by alleviating T cell exhaustion, which improved the integrity of the study but was lacking in our study. We will further refine relevant investigations in the future.

Notably, CD8+ T cells demonstrated an exhaustion phenotype characterised by increased PD‐1 and impaired GZMB in patients with IAC. As GZMB is a key cytotoxic molecule regulated by PD‐1 [31, 32], it is necessary to identify the underlying mechanism. According to a previous study, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway may be downstream of PD‐1 [33]. The mTOR activated by PD‐1 blockade attenuated the transcriptional repression of downstream cytokines (including GZMB) by phosphorylating FoxO1 [34]. Consequently, FoxO1 transcriptional activity regulated by the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway is probably an underlying mechanism of PD‐1 involved in inhibiting GZMB expression (Figure 5). Although our study indicated that GZMB production regulated by PD‐1 in CD8+ T cells was associated with IAC pathogenesis and prognosis, the underlying mechanisms warrant further in vivo and in vitro studies.

Schematic depiction of the mechanism of PD‐1 regulating GZMB expression, as previously described [33, 34]. PD‐1 recruited SHP‐2 through ITSM to inhibit the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway, which dephosphorylates FoxO1 and promotes its nuclear translocation, leading to a decrease in GZMB level. ITSM, immunoreceptor tyrosine‐based switch motif; mTOR, mammalian target of rapamycin; PD‐1, Programmed Death‐1; PD‐L1, Programmed Cell Death 1 Ligand 1; PI3K, phosphatidylinositol 3‐kinase; SHP‐2, Src homology region 2 domain‐containing phosphatase‐2.

There are several limitations in this study. First, the sample size was limited. More studies with a larger sample size are necessary to validate these findings. Non‐IAC critically ill patients in the control group were clinically heterogeneous. Future studies should compare T cell exhaustion between different infections (such as bacterial infection). Additionally, the dynamic changes between survivors and non‐survivors in the control group were not compared due to the lack of fatal cases. Relevant analysis will be performed in future studies. Second, exhaustion‐related receptors and cytokines were mainly analysed in this study, which were primary indicators of T cell exhaustion. However, other factors involved in T cell exhaustion, such as the function and maturation of antigen‐presenting cells, were lacking in our study. To address this limitation, the role of mononuclear phagocytes in T cell exhaustion will be explored in future studies. Third, in vitro validations were not performed. For instance, the T cell response to Candida lysate or antigen between IAC and control groups was not performed. Despite the association between T cell exhaustion and prognosis, underlying mechanisms were not studied in detail. It remains unclear whether ICI treatment can reverse T cell exhaustion and dysfunction in IAC through in vitro experiments or murine models. Besides, our study also failed to construct a prediction model integrating clinical and immunological indicators to validate the clinical value of T cell exhaustion and predict IAC prognosis, considering the risk of model overfitting caused by a small sample size and relatively more parameters. Future studies will focus on prediction model development and validation. Consequently, more evidence is needed to validate T cell exhaustion in IAC and investigate inner mechanisms, providing more promising therapeutic values.

Conclusions

Patients with IAC exhibited a T cell exhaustion and dysfunction profile compared with critically ill non‐IAC patients. T cell exhaustion and dysfunction status were associated with clinical outcomes. Persistent increased PD‐1 and impaired GZMB in CD8+ T cells are associated with poor prognosis of IAC. Flow cytometric assessment of these markers may be useful in identifying IAC patients with immune impairment who can benefit from immunotherapies, such as PD‐1 blockade.
