MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni a new nano-photocatalyst for nitrobenzene photo-reduction via in-situ hydrogen generation from water splitting
Authors: Sara Sobhani, Bita Mohamadi, José Miguel Sansano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A new nano-photocatalyst (MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni) was designed to combine the unique properties of a modified metal–organic framework (a crystalline porous semiconductor with large surface area and tunable properties), g-C3N4 (a polymeric semiconductor with excellent photocatalytic properties), and nickel nanoparticles (which can enhance catalytic activity and facilitate charge transfer). Synthesis of MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni was successfully performed and the structure, morphology and optical properties of the nanocomposite were confirmed by diverse methods such as FT-IR, PXRD, XPS, FESEM, TEM, TGA, ICP, EDX, elemental mapping, UV–vis DRS, BET, Mott–Schottky, EIS and PL. It was found by UV–vis DRS that MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni has greater photo-absorption characteristic compared with its precursors. The synergistic interaction between the composite components creates a highly efficient photocatalytic activity for nitrobenzene photo-reduction by in-situ hydrogen generation from water splitting at room temperature under sunlight irradiation via a Z-scheme charge transfer pathway. By this eco-friendly method, various nitrobenzenes were selectively reduced to their corresponding anilines in high yields.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22750-4.

Introduction

The reduction of nitrobenzene as an environmental pollutant and a widely used chemical in industry, is an essential process in both environmental remediation and industrial chemistry1–3. Nitrobenzene and its derivatives are toxic, carcinogenic, and can contaminate water sources, posing significant risks to ecosystems and human health4,5. The reduction of nitrobenzene selectively to aniline is a fundamental organic reaction, with applications in pharmaceutical, agrochemical and material science6. Traditional methods for nitrobenzene reduction require complex conditions, involving pressurized hydrogen, high temperatures, or organic acids. These methods typically rely on significant energy input, along with unsafety and demanding special requirements for hydrogen production, storage, and transport, raising concerns about the sustainability and environmental impact of these processes7–9. As a result, there is a growing demand for more efficient, environmentally friendly, and energy-efficient methods for the reduction of nitrobenzene10,11, particularly those that can leverage renewable energy sources. In this context, photocatalysis, a process that harnesses light energy to drive chemical reactions, has emerged as a promising solution. Photocatalysis offers the potential for environmentally sustainable transformations, where solar energy can be directly used to power chemical reactions, reducing the need for external energy sources. In recent years, photocatalysis has appeared as an attractive and environmentally friendly method for achieving nitro reduction11–17. However, the reported methods for the photocatalytic reduction of nitroaromatics by hydrogen generated from water splitting are rare. The available photocatalytic methods for reducing nitrobenzene to aniline through water splitting include photocatalysts such as Zn1-xCdxS, Pd-g-C3N4, In/Pd-PMOF and Eosin Y18–22. Although the mentioned photocatalysts have significant advantages, they come with drawbacks that can hinder their performance or practical applications. Zn1-xCdxS faces challenges of cadmium toxicity and reduced stability due to the photo-corrosion. Use of Pd-g-C3N4 and In/Pd-PMOF are limited by palladium high cost. Eosin Y has a homogeneous nature, which complicates its separation and recycling from the reaction mixtures, posing challenges for catalyst recovery and reuse. Additionally, as a stable dye, Eosin Y can cause environmental issues in wastewater due to its persistence and dark color. By understanding these challenges, researchers are continuously working to enhance the stability, light absorption, and efficiency of photocatalysts, aiming to make the photocatalytic reduction of nitrobenzene a more sustainable and commercially viable process.

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are a group of crystalline porous materials composed of metal ions or clusters coordinated to organic ligands, forming well-ordered constructions with tunable properties23–25. MOFs have properties such as large surface area, high porosity, and the ability to incorporate various metal centers, tunable electronic properties, and the potential for functionalization26. They can act as a semiconductor and generate the electron and hole pairs upon irradiation of light, which is the required property of a material to be used as a semiconductor in photocatalytic processes27. Nevertheless, low electronic conductivity limits MOFs practical applications as a photocatalyst in certain reaction medias. An effective strategy to improve the charge separation efficiency of MOFs are the construction of a proper composite and/or modification of the MOFs28,29.

Graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) is a promising photocatalyst due to its unique electronic structure, thermal stability, and suitable bandgap (~ 2.7 eV)30. It has been widely used in water splitting, pollutant degradation, and CO₂ reduction31. However, the activity of g-C3N4 is often limited by rapid charge recombination, low electrical conductivity, and the limited active sites32,33. To overcome these limitations, the incorporation of g-C3N4 with nanoparticles, such as metal oxides, noble metals, or transition metal phosphides, and/or other semiconductors has proven effective34–36.

Recent advancements in composite photocatalysts, which combine various materials with complementary properties have opened new avenues for achieving high-performance and efficient photocatalytic systems. The photocatalytic performance of MIL-101-based composites has been widely explored in recent years, and several studies highlighted the beneficial role of Fe-MIL-101-NH2 when coupled with semiconductors. For instance, Gong et al. reported an Fe-MIL-101/g-C3N4 heterostructure capable of degrading bisphenol efficiently under visible light by enhancing charge separation and exploiting favorable band alignment between the components37. Similarly, Wang et al. constructed Fe-MIL-101-NH2/g-C3N4 heterostructure for the photocatalytic CO2 reduction and H2 evolution, confirming the general advantage of Fe-MIL-101/g-C3N4 platforms in improving electron–hole separation and photocatalytic rates38. More recently, Liu et al. developed a Fe-MIL-101/CdS/g-C3N4 ternary dual Z-scheme heterojunction, which broadened light absorption and accelerated charge transport for visible-light photocatalysis39.

In continuous of our recent research on the development of new photocatalysts for water purification and organic transformations40–42, herein we wish to introduce a new nanocomposite denoted as MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni. This nanocomposite is designed to exploit the strengths of each individual material. MIL-101-DIST, a crystalline porous semiconductor with high surface area and tunable properties, provides a favorable environment for the adsorption of reactants and promotes efficient electron transfer. g-C3N4 acts as a polymeric semiconductor, absorbing visible light and generating electron–hole pairs that drive the photocatalytic reactions. The incorporation of Ni nanoparticles further enhances the catalytic activity by boosting the efficiency of water splitting and facilitating the reduction of nitrobenzene. In overall, these components form a robust and highly effective photocatalyst capable of reducing nitrobenzene through a water splitting process that is devoid of typical high-pressure conditions in conventional hydrogenation processes.

Experimental section

Materials and methods

Chemicals were purchased from Merck. Reactions were monitored by TLC (thin-layer chromatography) using silica-gel polygram SILG/UV254 plates. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed using a VG-Microtech Multilab 3000 spectrometer equipped with an Al anode. Spectra deconvolution was carried out using Gaussian–Lorentzian curves. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) was conducted on an Xpert Pro Panalytical diffractometer incorporating Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 Å). TEM (transmission electron microscopy) images were obtained with a Philips CM30 microscope. FESEM (field emission scanning electron microscopy) images were acquired using a Mira 3-XMU model. FT-IR (Fourier-transform infrared) spectra were recorded on a Nicolet-Impact 400D spectrometer in the range of 400–4000 cm−1. The metal catalyst percentage was measured using an OPTIMA 7300DV ICP analyzer. UV–Vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV–Vis-DRS) was performed using a Shimadzu Co, Japan instrument. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded at an excitation wavelength of 370 nm using an Agilent fluorescence spectrophotometer (PL Varian). The photoelectrochemical behavior of the photocatalysts was evaluated using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and Mott–Schottky analysis on a μStat-i 400 s portable potentiostat/galvanostat/impedance analyzer (Metrohm DropSens, Spain). EIS measurements were recorded within the frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 100 kHz, whereas Mott–Schottky analysis was performed at a frequency of 1 kHz. Experiments were conducted under sunlight irradiation between 10:00 a.m. and 2:00 p.m. from July to August 2023, at approximately 27 °C. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements were taken with a QUANTACHROME automatic volumetric physical gas adsorption AUTOSORB-6 and AUTOSORB degasser, utilizing the BET method. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was carried out using a METTLER-TOLEDO TGA–DSC 3 + thermogravimetric analyzer.

Catalyst preparation

Synthesis of Fe-MIL-101-NH2

Fe-MIL-101-NH2 was obtained using a known procedure43. A solution of 2-amino-1,4-benzenedicarboxylate (NH2-BDC) (0.22 g, 1.24 mmol) in DMF (7.5 mL) was slowly poured to a solution of FeCl3·6H2O (0.67 g, 2.49 mmol) in DMF (7.5 mL). The mixture was heated in a stainless-steel autoclave at 110 °C for 24 h. The remaining product was centrifuged, washed with DMF and EtOH (3 × 10 mL) and dried at 80 °C under vacuum in 24 h.

General procedure for the synthesis of Fe-MIL-101-NH2 supported by di(imidazol-1-yl)-s-triazine (MIL-101-DIST)

The as-prepared Fe-MIL-101-NH2 (0.33 g) was dispersed in THF (25 mL) in a round-bottomed flask using sonication for 30 min. Next, cyanuric chloride (2.7 mmol) was added to the mixture, and the solution was stirred at room temperature for 24 h. The resulting mixture was placed in an ice bath for 15 h at 0 °C and finally stirred at room temperature for another 20 h. The solid was centrifuged and washed with dry THF. Then, to the solid in dry THF (30 mL), Et3N (5 mL) and imidazole (24.2 mmol) were added and the mixture was stirred under reflux conditions for 4 days under nitrogen. The precipitate was centrifuged, washed with THF several times and dried under vacuum at 70 °C.

Synthesis of graphitic carbon nitride( g-C3N4)

In a typical experiment, melamine (5 g) was poured into a covered crucible and calcined at 550 °C (heating rate = 5 °C min−1) for 3 h40. The resulting yellow solid was cooled down to ambient temperature. Then it was milled thoroughly to obtain the uniform bulk g-C3N4 powder. g-C3N4 (0.1 g) was dissolved in deionized water (100 mL) and sonicated for 6 h. The suspension was centrifuged (5000 rpm) to remove the remaining unexfoliated g-C3N4. Finally, g-C3N4 were attained after vacuum drying.

Synthesis of Ni nanoparticle supported on carbon nitride (g-C3N4/ Ni )

After 30 min dispersing g-C3N4 (1 g) in EtOH (30 mL), it was gradually treated with a solution of Ni(OAc)2 (2 mmol) in EtOH (15 mL) and continuously stirred for 1 h. To the resulting suspension, an aqueous solution of NaBH4 (30 mL, 0.1 M) was poured dropwise, under vigorous stirring. After 4 h, the solid was centrifuged, washed repeatedly with distilled water and EtOH (3 × 15 mL), and vacuum-dried at 60 °C.

Synthesis of MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni

In order to prepare MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni as a composite, MIL-101-DIST (0.1 g) and g-C3N4/Ni (0.1 g) were exposed to sonicate in dimethylformamide (30 mL) for 2 h. Next, the resulting composite was separated using a centrifuge and it was washed several times with dimethylformamide (3 × 10 mL). Finally, it was dried in vacuum at 60 °C for 24 h.

Typical procedure for photocatalytic reduction of nitrobenzene by MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni through water splitting

Nitrobenzene (0.2 mmol), n-Pr3N (0.6 mmol) and MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni (0.008 g, 2 mol%, based on Ni content) were added to a stirring solution of Na2S (1.25 mmol) and Na2SO3 (1.5 mmol) in water (5 mL). The mixture was bubbled with N2 gas for 10 min to remove any dissolved O2. Then the mixture was stirred at ∼27 °C under sunlight irradiation. After an appropriate time obtained by TLC monitoring (n-hexane/EtOAc: 3/1), the catalyst was separated by centrifugation, washed with ethanol (5 mL), dried and recycled for the subsequent experiment. The solvent of the combined solution was evaporated under vacuum to produce the crude product. The crude product was purified by column chromatography.

Selective reduction of nitrobenzene to aniline by in-situ hydrogen generated from water splitting catalyzed by MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni as a nano-photocatalyst

Initially, the activity of MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni as a nano-photocatalyst for H2 evolution was evaluated in an aqueous solution containing Na2S and Na2SO3 as sacrificial agents under sunlight irradiation. The average amount of hydrogen gas collected over 1 h for 10 mg of MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni was 3.85 mmol. In the control experiments, pure g-C3N4 and MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4 composite produced 1.33 and 2.83 mmol of hydrogen, respectively, in 1 h. After successful hydrogen production from water catalyzed by MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni under sunlight irradiation, the photocatalytic efficiency of MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni for the reduction of nitrobenzene via in-situ hydrogen generation from water splitting as a model reaction was investigated under various conditions to evaluate the effects of sacrificial agents, base, substrate concentration, and catalyst loading (Table 2). At first, the model reaction was performed in the absence of the base and sacrificial agents (Table 2, entries 1 and 2) and any amounts of the desired product was not obtained. Investigation of the effect of the sacrificial agents showed that Na2S/Na2SO3 produced significantly higher aniline yields compared to methanol or ethanol, underscoring the importance of sacrificial agent selection (Table 2, entries 3–7 and 13–16). Sulfide ions are efficient hole scavengers that suppress charge carrier recombination, while sulfite acts as a stabilizer to prevent catalyst deactivation by consuming oxidized sulfur intermediates59,60. The cooperative effect of the two species ensures sustained photocatalytic activity and higher efficiency under sunlight irradiation. In contrast, alcohols such as methanol and ethanol exhibited limited efficiency in our system, which can be attributed to slower oxidation kinetics and competitive side reactions that reduce the number of electrons available for nitrobenzene reduction. Screening of the base type/concentration (Table 2, entry 8–12) showed that n-Pr3N (0.6 mmol) provided the highest yield in the presence of 2 mol% of the catalyst (Table 2, entry 10), while inorganic bases gave lower efficiencies. Variation of nitrobenzene concentration revealed an optimal yield, when 0.2 mmol of nitrobenzene was used (Table 2, entries 10, 17, 18). This result reflects a balance between sufficient substrate adsorption and avoidance of surface saturation or light attenuation. At lower concentration, the reaction is substrate-limited, while higher hinders photon penetration and mass transfer, a trend consistent with previous reports on nitrobenzene photoreduction38,60. At the end, 2 mol% of the catalyst, Na2S/Na2SO3 (1.25 and 1.5 mmol, respectively) as the sacrificial agents and n-Pr3N (0.6 mmol) as a base were chosen as the optimized reaction conditions (Table 2, entry 10). To appreciate the role of MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni in this reaction, the photocatalytic efficiency of the catalyst precursors (Fe-MIL-101-NH2, MIL-101-DIST and g-C3N4/Ni) were studied under the optimized reaction conditions (Table 2, entries 19–21). Low amounts of the desired product were obtained in the presence of the catalyst precursors, which showed a synergetic effect of the catalyst components in MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni. Based on the above-mentioned analysis and the reported information in the literature18,68–71, a probable mechanism for the nitrobenzene reduction by hydrogen generated via in-situ water splitting is shown in Fig. 14. Under visible light irradiation, both MOF and g-C3N4/Ni are photoexcited, generating electron–hole pairs. Given the energetic configuration of catalyst components, a conventional type-II heterojunction is unfavorable. The photoexcited electrons of MOF on CB are incapable of reducing water to hydrogen, due to the less potential than the redox potential of water. Instead, the system operates via a direct Z-scheme charge transfer pathway. In this configuration, photoexcited electrons from the CB of MOF recombine with holes in the VB of g-C3N4/Ni at the interface, a process that is supported by favorable band alignment and suppressed recombination pathways. This interfacial charge recombination selectively preserves high-energy electrons in the CB of g-C3N4/Ni (− 1.08 eV), which are sufficiently negative to drive the proton reduction reaction (H+/H2 = − 0.65 eV at pH ≈ 11), and highly oxidative holes in the VB of MOF (+ 3.00 eV), capable of oxidizing sacrificial electron donors such as Na2S and Na2SO372,73. The in-situ generated hydrogen serves as a reducing agent for the simultaneous transformation of nitrobenzene to aniline. Based on the well-established reduction pathway proposed by Haber70, nitrobenzene is initially reduced to nitrosobenzene and N-phenylhydroxylamine. Under alkaline conditions, azoxybenzene were produced by the condensation of these intermediates74, and hydrogenated to produce azobenzene, hydrazobenzene, and ultimately aniline.Fig. 14Possible mechanism for the reduction of nitrobenzene with in-situ hydrogen generation from water catalyzed by MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni.

Conclusions

MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni as a new nanocomposite was synthesized and its structure, morphology and optical properties were fully studied by several methods including FT-IR, PXRD, XPS, FESEM, TEM, TGA, ICP, EDX, elemental mapping, UV–vis DRS, BET, Mott–Schottky, EIS and PL. It was found by UV–vis DRS that MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni has greater photo-absorption characteristic compared with its precursors. The nanocomposite contains MIL-101-DIST (a crystalline porous semiconductor with large surface area and tunable properties), g-C3N4 (a polymeric semiconductor with excellent photocatalytic properties), and nickel nanoparticles (which can enhance catalytic activity and facilitate charge transfer). The synergistic interaction between these components creates a highly efficient photocatalytic system, capable of reducing nitrobenzene to anilines selectively with in-situ hydrogen generation from water splitting under sunlight irradiation. Introduction of g-C3N4/Ni into MOF enhanced interfacial charge separation by forming a direct Z-scheme heterojunction with suitable band alignment. It also increased light absorption. Remarkably, several nitrobenzenes containing both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups as well as dinitrobenzene can be efficiently underwent reduction to their corresponding anilines in high yields avoiding high-pressure conditions in conventional hydrogenation processes. These results provide a general strategy for coupling photocatalytic hydrogen generation with organic transformations under mild conditions. By addressing both environmental pollution and energy sustainability, MIL-101-DIST/g-C3N4/Ni nanocomposite offers a highly efficient and eco-friendly solution to one of the most pressing challenges in modern chemical and environmental engineering.
