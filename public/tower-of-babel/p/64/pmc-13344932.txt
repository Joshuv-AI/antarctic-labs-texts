Detection of Microvascular Failure Using Bioimpedance and Sensor-derived Clinical Indicators: A Preliminary Study
Authors: Loïc Van Dieren, Haïzam Oubari, Leonard Knoedler, Raffaele Aguglia, Korkut Uygun, Mark A. Randolph, Filip Thiessen EF, Alexandre G. Lellouch, Curtis L. Cetrulo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Early detection of flap failure is critical for successful salvage surgery outcomes. Delays in diagnosis can lead to additional surgical intervention, tissue necrosis, or even flap loss. Current monitoring methods rely heavily on intermittent clinical evaluations, which are subjective and time-consuming and may miss early signs of failure. A continuous, objective monitoring system could significantly improve detection accuracy and response times. This study explores a multisensory strategy to identify early physiological changes associated with flap failure.Methods:Using a rat hindlimb model, flap failure was simulated via femoral vein or artery clamping (n = 5 per group). Physiological responses—including temperature, skin color, and bioimpedance—were recorded continuously for 2 hours postclamping with sensors. Statistical significance was set at a P value less than 0.05 using a 1-sample t test. Distinct failure patterns were compared against nonclamped controls.Results:Clear physiological trends emerged in failure groups. We preliminarily validated a multisensor approach for free flap monitoring in a rat model using temperature, bioimpedance, and color sensing. Each modality provided distinct and complementary insights into perfusion changes: color sensing detected changes fastest but was more variable, temperature changes were moderate and potentially influenced by external heating, and bioimpedance showed the most consistent trends over time, highlighting the value of a combined sensing strategy for reliable tissue viability assessment.Conclusions:This multisensory approach demonstrates strong potential for early, noninvasive flap failure detection through continuous monitoring of temperature, color, and bioimpedance. These findings support further development of real-time sensing systems to enhance surgical outcomes and patient safety in flap-based procedures.

Takeaways

Question: How can early microvascular failure in free flaps be detected objectively and continuously to improve surgical outcomes?

Findings: In a rat hindlimb model, we induced venous and arterial occlusion and monitored temperature, color, and bioimpedance in real time. Each modality revealed distinct physiological signatures of perfusion loss: color changes occurred first but were variable, temperature shifts were moderate, and bioimpedance provided the most consistent trends. Together, they reliably distinguished venous congestion from arterial ischemia.

Meaning: A multisensory, noninvasive monitoring approach combining bioimpedance, temperature, and color sensing could enable earlier, more reliable detection of flap compromise and may enhance postoperative patient safety.

INTRODUCTION

Microvascular free flap surgery is a cornerstone of modern reconstructive surgery, particularly for managing large or complex defects. Advances in microsurgical techniques have significantly improved the success rate of anastomoses, now exceeding 95%1,2 (but are significantly lower in challenging patient populations3). Regardless of the success rate, the procedures rely on vigilant postoperative monitoring to ensure the patency of microvascular anastomoses.4,5 Vascular compromise, whether arterial or venous, remains the leading cause of flap failure, necessitating timely detection and intervention.6–8

The current gold standard for postoperative free flap monitoring relies on clinical assessment, including flap color evaluation, capillary refill time, and handheld Doppler ultrasound examination.9,10 Experienced medical personnel can often detect early signs of pedicle thrombosis. However, these assessments are inherently subjective, time-consuming, and disturbing for the patient.9,11,12 Even highly trained clinicians may occasionally struggle to identify free flap compromise promptly, leading to delayed intervention and increased risk of flap failure, especially in subacute settings. Alternative monitoring techniques, such as implantable Doppler probes, tissue oximetry devices, near-infrared spectroscopy, and hyperspectral imaging, promise enhanced sensitivity—particularly, implantable probes like LICOX (Integra Lifescience, Boston, MA) offer reliable and continuous monitoring, with alert systems allowing timely intervention.13 Their widespread adoption, however, is limited by their high costs; technical complexity; lack of standardized protocols; and, in some cases, by the invasive nature of their probes.14 Other modalities, including surface temperature monitoring and fluorescent imaging, offer only partial insights into flap viability and lack the capability for continuous and reliable monitoring. The growing older adult population and skin color further complicate the landscape of free flap monitoring.15,16 Age-related physiological changes, including diminished vascular elasticity, altered skin properties, and impaired wound healing, may obscure conventional clinical signs of flap viability.17–20 Consequently, there is a growing need for an objective, automated, and easily deployable monitoring system.

An effective postoperative monitoring system for free flap surgery could benefit from combining noninvasiveness, accuracy, remote accessibility, affordability, and user-friendliness, particularly for surgical trainees, nonsurgical medical staff, and patients. Ideally, such a system would provide continuous real-time data to support clinical decision-making. In this study, our goal has been to overcome current limitations in free flap monitoring by developing a more holistic and integrated approach that closely mirrors clinical assessments. Traditional systems often rely on a single parameter, such as Doppler ultrasound or laser Doppler flowmetry, to assess flap viability.21,22 However, we believe that relying on just 1 measurement does not account for the complexity of tissue perfusion and health, which can be influenced by a variety of factors. By incorporating multiple clinical parameters—including bioimpedance, color, and temperature—we hope to provide a more comprehensive and accurate picture of flap status. In this study, we also introduce bioimpedance as a novel biomarker for free flap failure. Bioimpedance is a noninvasive method that measures the resistance of biological tissues to an applied electrical current.23 It can detect changes in tissue water content and cellular integrity, offering early signs of ischemia or congestion.24

METHODS

Animals and Study Design

A total of 32 male Lewis rats 8 weeks of age (300 ± 50 g) were used for all experiments (Charles River Laboratories, MA). The sensors for each condition were tested in individual rats. The protocol (#2023N000151) was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines of Massachusetts General Hospital (Boston, MA). All procedures followed the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Experimental Setup

In each animal, both hind limbs were surgically dissected at the mid-femur level to isolate blood flow to the limbs and expose the femoral vessels, as previously described.25 The integrity of the surrounding muscle and skin were preserved. The left limb was assigned to receive either arterial or venous clamping, whereas the contralateral limb served as an internal control. Sensor pairs for bioimpedance and temperature were placed symmetrically on both limbs to allow real-time comparison between the test and control sides. For color sensing, however, control-side comparison was not performed. Because flap tissue color can vary significantly from adjacent skin under normal conditions, interlimb comparison was considered unreliable. Instead, color sensor values were calibrated to the baseline (preclamping) value for each limb, allowing for detection of dynamic changes relative to each flap’s starting point.

Surgical Procedure

The rats were anesthetized with 3%–4% isoflurane for induction and 1%–3% isoflurane for maintenance (Fig. 1). After this, the full rat or the rat’s torso was placed on a heating pad at 37°C, for color and bioimpedance, and temperature, respectively. Following this, meloxicam (2 mg/kg) and heparin were injected intraperitoneally and through the dorsal vein of the penis (Fig. 1A and B). The lower anterior aspect of the abdomen and the proximal aspect of the thigh were shaved. Depilatory cream was applied to the shaved area to remove all hair. The area was then washed with surgical scrub, alcohol, and povidone-iodine. A skin incision line was drawn with a surgical marker around the hind limb, at its base, just proximal to the inguinal ligament, to include the epigastric artery in the flap (Fig. 1C and D). A skin incision was made on the inguinal fold, followed by dissection and skeletonization of the femoral vessels, with ligation of the Murphy branches. The epigastric pedicle was preserved and included in the flap. After the skin was incised and the femoral vessels were isolated (but not ligated), the thigh muscles were divided using bipolar forceps, and the femur was sectioned with a Liston rongeur to separate the hind limb. The resulting flap consisted of the entire hind limb, which remained attached to the body only by its vascular pedicle (femoral artery and veins) (Fig. 1E). All procedures were performed on the flap without interrupting its main vascular supply, meaning the flap was dissected while preserving the vessels, eliminating the need to transpose the flap to a “recipient” site.

Sensors and Monitoring

A K-type thermocouple (<5 mm in diameter, Omega, Engineering, Inc., CT) was used to measure the temperature of the flaps under investigation. The temperatures were logged using a TC-08 Data Logger (Pico Technology) and recorded with PicoLog 6. For color, a TCS3472 RGB sensor (20.4 × 11.2 mm) was used. The color sensor was connected to an Arduino Uno (Elegoo, France) and color was recorded using a custom-written script running on CoolTerm. Bioimpedance was recorded using the MAX30001EVSYS (Analog Devices, Inc., MA) evaluation board and connected to MAX30001 Evaluation System Software (version 3.1.0, Analog Devices, Inc.). Pre-gelled surface electrodes (11 mm in diameter, LifeSync Neuro, NY) were connected to MAX30001EVSYS. Two-lead measurements were performed at a frequency of 8 kHz and a magnitude of 8 µA, as done previously.24 The ground electrode was placed on the rat’s intact abdomen.

Statistical Analysis

Data were collected in Microsoft Excel for Mac (version 16.95.3, NM) and analyzed in Python (version 3.8.5) using the Pandas (version 1.2.3), NumPy (version 1.19.5), Matplotlib (version 3.3.4), and SciPy (version 1.6.2) libraries. A 1-sample t test was performed at each time point to compare the sample mean to 0. The value used in the t test was obtained by subtracting the control value from the test value at each time point and calibrating this difference relative to the difference observed at the start of the experiment. This calibration was done to ensure that the comparisons were consistent with the initial conditions of the experiment and further with the varying conditions along the time course of the experiment. The normality of the data was assumed for the purposes of this test. A significance threshold of a P value less than 0.05 was used. For each time point, the mean and SD were computed, and t tests were conducted using the ttest_1samp function from the SciPy library. The results were summarized as P values, and time points with p-values below the threshold were considered statistically significant. Time points were plotted with mean values and SDs, and significant P values were visually highlighted using color-coded markers on the graph. The statistical significance for each time point was indicated with green markers for significant results and orange markers for nonsignificant results.

RESULTS

Surgery-related Outcomes

The rats used in this study had a body weight ranging from 285.0 to 403.0 g, with a mean weight of 321.40 ± 39.98 g. The variation in weight reflects natural biological differences among the subjects. The mean surgical operating time was 37.38 ± 7.11 minutes. The variability in operating time was primarily influenced by procedural complexity and individual anatomical differences, although all procedures were completed within a consistent time frame suitable for maintaining experimental reliability. Additionally, visual inspection and histological analysis verified the successful implementation and functionality of the surgical model (Fig. 1F–H).

Bioimpedance

Bioimpedance is a measure of the electrical resistance and reactance of biological tissues. It can provide insights into changes in tissue composition, particularly fluid shifts. Impedance is influenced by water content, with increased extracellular fluid reducing impedance, whereas decreased perfusion leads to higher impedance due to reduced conductive pathways. Arterial clamping was expected to increase impedance due to ischemia and reduced blood volume (Fig. 2A and B), whereas venous clamping was anticipated to decrease impedance due to fluid accumulation from impaired venous outflow (Fig. 2C and D). In control (unclamped) legs, the first-minute bioimpedance ranged from 604.8 to 895.8 ohms, with a mean of 757.0 ± 25.87 ohms (mean ± SEM) and a SD of 77.62 ohms (n = 10). Linear regression analysis of the control group revealed a slight downward trend over time, with a best-fit slope of −0.6696 ± 0.1027 ohms/min and a y-intercept of 736.5 ± 6.919 ohms. This gradual decline may reflect postoperative tissue conductivity changes. In the arterial clamping group, bioimpedance immediately increased compared to the control leg, which led to a significant difference after 15 minutes (P < 0.05; n = 5). This is consistent with progressive ischemia and reduced tissue hydration, noted visually. Conversely, venous clamping led to a significant decrease in bioimpedance after 6 minutes (P < 0.05; n = 5) (Fig. 3).

Bioimpedance successfully identifies and distinguishes venous congestion and arterial ischemia. A and B, Arterial clamping led to a progressive increase in impedance, becoming significantly different from baseline after 15 minutes (P < 0.05) ,whereas venous clamping (C and D) caused an increase in overall water content and therefore resulted in a significant decrease in impedance after 6 minutes (P < 0.05) (data are presented as mean ± SD).

Temperature

In the arterial clamping group (n = 6), temperature decreased progressively, but significant differences from baseline were observed only after 21 minutes (Fig. 4A; P < 0.05). At this time, the temperature in the clamped limbs had dropped by approximately 1.5 °C, consistent with reduced perfusion and ischemia-induced cooling of the tissue. In the venous clamping group (n = 9), a more rapid change in temperature was observed, with significant differences from baseline occurring as early as the second minute (Fig. 4B; P < 0.05). However, in contrast to the arterial clamping group, the venous clamping legs showed only a slight increase in temperature, less than 1 °C, suggesting limited blood flow and accumulation of heat in the tissue due to impaired venous return.

Temperature as an indicator of arterial ischemia and venous congestion in rats. A, In the arterial clamping group (n = 6), temperature progressively declined and achieved significant difference with baseline after 21 minutes, whereas in the venous clamping group (B, n = 9), temperature became significantly different from baseline within the second minute (P < 0.05; data are presented as mean ± SD).

Color

Colorimetric analysis was performed using red (Fig. 5A and B), green (Fig. 5C and D), and blue (Fig. 5) (RGB) channel data from a color sensor to assess surface changes in tissue perfusion following arterial and venous clamping. In the arterial clamping group (n = 3), the red channel showed a significant decrease beginning at 2 minutes postclamping, which lasted until 10 minutes, briefly losing significance before regaining it at 15 minutes (Fig. 5A). The red channel exhibited an immediate drop within the first 3 minutes, followed by a relatively steady or mildly declining trend. The green (Fig. 5C) and blue (Fig. 5E) channels in the arterial group demonstrated slight increases immediately after clamping. The green channel plateaued after approximately 20 minutes, whereas the blue channel decreased after 20 minutes to reach a plateau value after 40 minutes. Statistical significance for these 2 channels was inconsistent over time.

Red color channel shows the most responsiveness to congestion and ischemia. Color analysis in the red (A and B), green (C and D), and blue (E and F) channels, with A, C, E and B, D, F representing arterial and venous occlusion, respectively (data are presented as mean ± SD).

In the venous clamping group (n = 4), the red channel exhibited an immediate and significant drop after clamping (Fig. 5B), followed by a steady and continuous decline, reflecting the progressive buildup of deoxygenated blood. The green channel remained largely unchanged (Fig. 5D) after clamping and did not reach consistent statistical significance. The blue channel showed a contrasting pattern, with an immediate and significant increase that continued gradually throughout the observation period (Fig. 5F).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we conducted a preliminary validation of a multisensor approach for free flap monitoring and detection of acute flap failure using temperature, bioimpedance, and color sensing. Each modality responded differently to perfusion changes, offering complementary insights into tissue viability. Color sensing, particularly in the red channel, demonstrated high sensitivity, being the earliest parameter to respond following clamping. However, it also exhibited greater variability and was more susceptible to external influences such as ambient lighting and tissue heterogeneity. Additionally, color is sensitive to intrinsic skin color variations, such as ecchymosis or skin color, but this problem could be mediated with initial calibration on the flap instead of a reference electrode. Temperature sensing also detected early changes, especially in the venous occlusion group, but with a smaller amplitude, likely influenced by the presence of a heating pad. In contrast, bioimpedance changes occurred more gradually, particularly after arterial clamping, but displayed the most consistent and interpretable trends over time. Compared to current clinical standards, such as physical examination and handheld Doppler ultrasound, which are limited in reliability and objectivity, our findings suggest that integrating multiple sensor modalities may significantly improve the early and reliable detection of vascular compromise by leveraging the complementary sensitivity and specificity of each sensor. Although previous literature supports the utility of single-modality tools like laser Doppler or near-infrared spectroscopy, few studies have explored the synergistic benefits of integrating multiple noninvasive sensors in a single platform. Importantly, noninvasive monitoring avoids the potential complications associated with implantable or invasive methods.26 This multisensor strategy could provide both rapid alerts (via color or temperature) and stable trend tracking (via bioimpedance), potentially bridging the gap between fast clinical decisions and long-term reliability. As a next step, further validation in larger animal models or even early-stage human trials could be considered, especially because all 3 sensors are noninvasive. However, practical considerations such as sensor integration, device size, and robustness to external variables will be crucial for clinical translation.

Bioimpedance emerges as a promising and physiologically grounded tool for the early detection of free flap compromise. Bioimpedance measures the opposition of biological tissue to an alternating current, which varies depending on the water content, ion concentration, and cellular integrity of the tissue.27 In the context of venous congestion, where fluid accumulates and extracellular water content increases, we observed a clear and sustained decrease in impedance, consistent with the enhanced conductivity of fluid-rich tissue. Conversely, arterial ischemia leads to dehydration and reduced perfusion, resulting in a progressive increase in impedance due to the loss of conductive pathways. These opposing trends align with the underlying physics. With its ability to detect subtle shifts in tissue composition noninvasively and in real time, bioimpedance offers a scientifically coherent and clinically valuable method for monitoring flap viability.

An important future direction lies in the integration of sensor outputs through artificial intelligence.28,29 Each sensor type offers a unique window into tissue physiology—bioimpedance reflects hydration and cellular status, temperature signals perfusion, and color changes track surface vascularity. By combining these complementary data streams, artificial intelligence algorithms can potentially identify patterns of failure earlier and with greater accuracy than any single parameter alone. Such integration could reduce false positives, improve decision-making, and ultimately lead to automated early warnings for clinicians. Additionally, the system’s ability to provide remote access to real-time clinical indicators represents a major step forward in postoperative care. Remote monitoring enables early detection of complications without requiring constant bedside presence, improving outcomes in settings with limited staffing or where patients are geographically distant. It also enhances patient safety and comfort by reducing unnecessary physical checks and interventions. As telemedicine continues to expand, systems like the one described here could play a crucial role in the future of digital surgical follow-up and flap surveillance.

This study has a key limitation that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The experimental model was highly acute, with almost immediate and visually apparent signs of flap failure following both arterial and venous occlusion. This may not fully reflect clinical scenarios where perfusion compromise develops more gradually and visual cues may be subtler or absent. Therefore, additional studies in models with more clinically relevant time courses of flap failure will be necessary to better assess the performance and predictive value of this monitoring approach. In addition, numerous technologies—including temperature probes—for free flap monitoring are already commercially available, and none has definitively proven superior to careful clinical examination. This work was designed as a proof-of-concept to test whether a simple, multisensor approach based on clinically intuitive parameters can capture early physiological changes associated with flap compromise in a controlled setting. The sensing modalities were intentionally chosen to mirror routine bedside assessment, and the observed variability—particularly in color channels beyond red—highlights the need to prioritize simplicity for clinical applicability. Finally, the reported earliest time points of statistically significant deviation from baseline should be interpreted as illustrative of relative temporal trends rather than as definitive detection thresholds. Establishing reproducible detection ranges and clinically actionable alert times will require larger, adequately powered studies in models with more clinically relevant time courses of flap compromise. Therefore, additional studies in models with more clinically relevant time courses of flap failure, and with streamlined sensing strategies, will be necessary to better assess the performance and predictive value of this monitoring approach.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the feasibility of a multisensor, noninvasive, and potentially remotely accessible system for reliable free flap monitoring. Each parameter contributes unique value, and together they lay the groundwork for a more comprehensive, intelligent, and responsive monitoring strategy in reconstructive surgery.
