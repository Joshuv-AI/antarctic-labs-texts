Immune mechanisms driving clinical heterogeneity in oral lichen planus
Authors: Eduardo Pons-Fuster, Julia Conesa-Solano, Lourdes Gimeno-Arias, Keren Martí-De Gea, Inmaculada Alguazas, Inmaculada Ruiz-Lorente, Alfredo Minguela, Pia López-Jornet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral lichen planus (OLP) is a clinically localized mucosal disease that nonetheless exhibits systemic immune alterations contributing to its heterogeneous presentations. This study investigated systemic immunological profiles associated with reticular (ROLP) and erosive (EOLP) OLP by analyzing circulating lymphocyte subsets and PBMC-derived cytokine secretion. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells from 91 participants (30 controls, 31 ROLP, 30 EOLP) were assessed by 24-colour spectral flow cytometry, and cytokine production was measured following CD3/CD28 stimulation. ROLP patients showed significantly elevated IL-2, IL-4, IL-17 A, and TNF-α secretion, whereas both OLP subgroups exhibited increased TGF-β1 compared with controls. Flow cytometry revealed higher frequencies of CD4⁺ Th1 cells in ROLP and increased naïve B-cell proportions in both ROLP and EOLP. Both clinical forms displayed enhanced TIGIT expression across CD4⁺, CD8⁺, and NK cells. These findings indicate distinct systemic immune signatures: a Th1/Th17-associated cytokine-driven inflammatory profile in ROLP and an inhibitory immune profile characterized by persistent TGF-β1 elevation in EOLP. Together, these data support the contribution of systemic immune dysregulation to OLP heterogeneity and highlight immunoregulatory pathways that warrant further investigation.

Introduction

Oral lichen planus (OLP) is an immune-mediated inflammatory disease primarily affecting the mucous membranes, with a prevalence of 0.2–1.9% worldwide1,2. Lesions often present as whitish papules and intersecting striae, with variable erythema3. Clinically, OLP manifests in distinct forms: the reticular variant (ROLP) is typically asymptomatic, while the atrophic–erosive form (EOLP) often causes painful lesions with a tendency to bleed3,4. Although OLP is clinically a localized mucosal disease, growing evidence indicates that it is accompanied by systemic immune alterations, which may contribute to its divergent clinical manifestations7,8.

Despite significant advancements in understanding OLP pathophysiology, the precise immune mechanisms underlying its progression remain incompletely elucidated. Most studies focus on the imbalance between T helper cell (Th) subsets (Th1 and Th2) in the pathogenesis of OLP. The Th1 phenotype, characterized by the secretion of high levels of TNF-α, IL-2, and IFN-γ, activates macrophages and cytotoxic T lymphocytes whereas the Th2 phenotype, which is marked by high levels of IL-4, IL-5, IL-10, and IL-13, is primarily associated with humoral immunity through interactions with B cells9,10. Growing evidence suggests an enhanced Th1 profile in OLP development, although the importance of Th211–13 and other subsets such as Th1714,15 or Tregs16 should not be overlooked. Yet, focusing solely on individual T-cell subsets risks oversimplifying disease mechanisms, as OLP likely arises from interconnected immune networks. In this context, other immune cells, including B cells8,17,18 and NK cells8,19, have also been identified in OLP lesions, but their contribution and interactions remain insufficiently explored. Finally, immune‑checkpoint molecules such as TIGIT (T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains), a key regulator of T‑cell activation and inhibitory signaling in chronic inflammatory diseases, have not yet been investigated in OLP.

This study aims to enhance the understanding of OLP pathogenesis by identifying systemic immunological alterations through the characterization of circulating T, NK, and B lymphocyte subsets and the analysis of PBMC-derived cytokine secretion across different clinical patterns of the disease. Through this descriptive analysis, we aim to identify immunological differences across distinct clinical presentations.

Methods

Study design

Patients attending the Oral Medicine Dental Clinic at the University of Murcia (Spain) between September 2023 and March 2024 were prospectively included in this cross-sectional observational study. The study enrolled patients aged over 18 with OLP who met the clinical and histopathological criteria as defined by Van der Waal and Van der Meij20. Exclusion criteria included the presence of epithelial dysplasia in the histopathological study, the use of drugs known to induce lichenoid lesions (such as medication-related lichenoid reactions), use of systemic immunomodulatory drugs, and the presence of amalgam fillings adjacent to the lesions. The control group consisted of healthy patients with similar sex and age distributions who attended a routine dental check-up, without systemic conditions and had not received any recent medication.

This study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Bioethics Committee for Research at the University of Murcia, Spain (575/2023). All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion.

Study groups and sample

Patients were classified into three groups, control, reticular OLP (ROLP) and erosive OLP (EOLP). All patients underwent comprehensive medical evaluations, accompanied by a detailed review of their clinical records. Data on gender, age, progression of OLP, lesion count, and the presence of extraoral features were systematically documented. Blood samples were collected via venipuncture for immunological assays. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from sodium heparin anticoagulated samples were immediately isolated in Ficoll density gradients from sodium heparin-anticoagulated samples and subsequently used for flow cytometry assay or set into culture for cytokine assay.

Flow cytometry

PBMCs were labeled following a lyse/wash protocol with a 24-colour/25-monoclonal antibody (mAb) panel including: CD3 Alexa Fluor 700 (clone UCHT1, BioLegend, USA), CD4 cFluor® R840 (SK3, Cytek Biosciences, USA), CD5 cFBYG750 (L17F12, Cytek), CD8 BV570 (RPA-T8, BioLegend), CD16 V450 (3G8, Becton Dickinson, USA), CD19 PECy7 (HIB19, Cytek), CD21 BV605 (B-ly4, BD), CD25 cFBYG710 (BC96, Cytek), CD27 Biobright-FITC (M-T271, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany), CD38 cFR685 (HB7, Cytek), CD45 cF840 (HI30, Cytek), CD45RO cF269 (UCHL1, Cytek), CD56 cFBYG710 (LT56, Cytek), CD57 cFR668 (HNK1, Cytek), CD127 BV650 (HIL-7R-M21, BD), CD161 BV480 (PK136, Bio-Rad, USA), CD183 (CXCR3) cFBYG677 (CEW33D, Cytek), CD196 (CCR6) (CCR6, BioLegend), CD279 (PD-1) BV786 (BD, Lyric Biosciences, USA), IgD BV750 (IA6-2, BD), IgM BB515 (G20-127, BD), NKG2C PE (REA205, Miltenyi Biotec), TCRγδ PCy5.5 (B1, Lyric), TCR-Vα7.2 APC-Cy7 (3C10, BioLegend), TIGIT BV421 (741182, BD). Cells were incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, lysed with 3 ml of 1X FACS Lysing Solution (BD) for 5 min, and washed with 3 ml of FACSFlow (BD). A million cells were immediately acquired on a Northern Lights (NL) flow cytometer (Cytek, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Data were analyzed with DIVA software (BD) following the gating strategy described in Fig. 1.

Lymphocyte subsets were reported as the percentage of each subset relative to the total lymphocyte count. Data were expressed as percentages or mean fluorescence intensity (MFI), as appropriate.

Cytokine assay

One million PBMCs were stimulated in vitro in triplicate using ImmunoCult™ Human CD3/CD28 T cell activator (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, PBMCs were cultured in 24-well flat-bottomed plates and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 48 h. Following incubation, the culture supernatant was collected and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent cytokine analysis. Cytokines in the culture supernatant were quantified using a ProcartaPlex Human Immune Monitoring 12-Plex Panel (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-17 A, IL-22, IL-23, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and TGF-β1, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vienna, Austria). The assay was performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, and samples were analyzed using the Luminex 300 system (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). The analysis was conducted using the Invitrogen™ ProcartaPlex™ Analysis App software, (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 28.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and JAMOVI (version 2.5; Sydney, Australia). Categorical variables were described through frequency distributions and percentages, whereas continuous data were expressed as mean with standard deviations (SD) or standard errors of the mean (SEM). The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to evaluate the normality of continuous variables. For inferential analysis, Fisher’s exact test was employed for categorical data, while the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test was used for non-parametric comparisons of continuous variables. P values less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

No significant differences were observed in MAIT T cells, Gamma-delta T cells or monocytes between groups.

Discussion

Despite significant progress in understanding OLP pathophysiology, the immune mechanisms underlying its heterogeneous clinical presentations remain incompletely elucidated. Most studies have focused on T-cell subsets, particularly the Th1/Th2 imbalance, but emerging evidence suggests that additional immune populations, such as B lymphocytes and NK cells, contribute to disease pathogenesis, highlighting the need for a more comprehensive analysis of the immune microenvironment in OLP7,8,11,12,21. Although OLP is clinically a localized mucosal disease, growing evidence indicates that it is accompanied by systemic immune alterations, which may contribute to its divergent clinical manifestations. In our study, we observed distinct immunological profiles associated with ROLP and EOLP. ROLP displayed a pronounced inflammatory profile associated with a marked Th1‑ and Th17‑driven inflammatory cytokine response, whereas EOLP exhibited features suggestive of immune suppresion and persistent TGF-β1 elevation. Both forms showed altered B and NK cell activity and TIGIT pathway dysregulation.

ROLP was characterized by elevated IL-2, TNF-α, and IL-17 A, driving CD4⁺ T cells toward Th1 differentiation and promoting CD8⁺ cytotoxic activation. Our results are consistent with previous studies reporting a Th1/Th17 predominance in OLP11,15,22–24. However, despite the increase in IL‑17 A secretion following CD3/CD28 stimulation, flow cytometry did not show a corresponding expansion of circulating Th17 cells. This divergence suggests that cytokine release in OLP may not strictly reflect the abundance of classical Th subsets. Instead, upstream innate pathways may disproportionately amplify cytokine production. Toll‑like receptors (TLRs), particularly TLR2, TLR4, and TLR9, can enhance IL‑17 A secretion through activation of antigen‑presenting cells, thereby promoting a Th17‑type cytokine environment without necessarily increasing Th17 cell frequencies25,26. Such mechanisms provide a plausible explanation for the dissociation observed between cytokine secretion profiles and T‑cell subset distribution in our cohort. Interestingly, IL-4 levels were also elevated compared to controls, suggesting a more complex regulation of inflammation. IL-4 promotes Th2 differentiation and can suppress Th1/Th17-mediated inflammation by reducing IL-17, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, indicating a potential protective role7,21. Moreover, elevated TGF-β1 in ROLP may further contribute to immune homeostasis7. Together, these findings highlight a complex interplay among Th1, Th17, and Th2 subsets in ROLP, where their imbalance may lead to the activation of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells and the progression of OLP.

In EOLP, Th1/Th17-driven mechanisms were not prominent. Prior studies report inconsistent findings, with some describing elevated Th1/Th17 cytokines in blood or saliva14,27,28, while others found no differences compared to controls29. Studies in chronic wounds suggest that resident cells, such as keratinocytes, can respond abnormally to cytokines like TGF-β1, resulting in impaired function and prolonged inflammation30. Similarly, the persistent TGF-β1 elevation in EOLP may indicate a dysregulated cytokine response in oral epithelial cells, potentially contributing to lesion chronicity and tissue damage.

Previous studies have reported the presence of B cells in OLP; however, their role in pathogenesis and their interactions with other immune subsets remain unclear. In our study, B-cell subsets were also altered: naïve B cells were increased in both ROLP and EOLP compared to controls, paralleling findings in autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA)31,32. Defects in early B-cell tolerance may allow autoreactive naïve B cells to accumulate and present antigens to T cells, promoting autoimmunity33. This could be partially explained in ROLP by the elevation of IL‑4, a key Th2 cytokine that supports naïve B‑cell survival and quiescence; however, additional IL‑4–independent mechanisms likely contribute to the expansion observed in both clinical forms. Notably, TGF‑β1 was increased in both ROLP and EOLP, and this inhibitory cytokine may contribute to naïve B‑cell accumulation by suppressing B‑cell differentiation. TGF‑β has been shown to inhibit B‑cell proliferation and antibody‑secreting cell formation and to downregulate essential differentiation factors such as IRF4, Blimp‑1, and XBP1, thereby retaining B cells in a naïve state34,35. Finally, anti-CD20 therapy, targeting CD20 expressed in naïve B cells, has shown efficacy in oral and esophageal lichen planus, supporting a pathogenic role for these cells36,37. CD19⁺CD5⁺ B-1a cells were elevated in ROLP, consistent with roles in autoimmunity38 via autoantibody production and promotion of Th1/Th17 responses39, suggesting that B-T cell interactions may contribute to disease heterogeneity40.

TIGIT, an inhibitory receptor involved in T‑cell inhibitory signaling and often enriched in exhausted or chronically stimulated T‑cell populations, was upregulated across CD4⁺ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, and NK cells of OLP patients, indicating functional impairment from chronic immune activation. Such upregulation is often associated with chronic immune activation and may reflect a compensatory regulatory response. Moreover, TIGIT expression has been observed in other autoimmune diseases41 [40–41], where it correlates with disease activity, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target in OLP.

While these results provide valuable insights, several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, although ROLP demonstrated a clear Th1/Th17 cytokine profile signature, the immune processes in EOLP were not fully elucidated and may involve alternative, non-lymphocyte mechanisms driving immune inhibition that were not detectable with our approach. These may include immunoregulatory activity of tissue-resident stromal or epithelial cells, metabolic suppression within the local microenvironment or persistent antigen stimulation. Further studies integrating spatial, metabolic, and transcriptomic analyses are needed to clarify these pathways. Another limitation is that immune profiling was limited to peripheral blood, which may not fully reflect the local immune microenvironment in oral mucosal lesions where disease activity is concentrated. As such, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting systemic immune alterations rather than direct tissue-level mechanisms. Future studies integrating circulating immune profiling with in situ analyses of OLP lesions would provide a more complete understanding of the local–systemic immunological interplay. Additionally, since our study was cross-sectional and did not follow the same patients over time, we cannot determine whether the immune differences between ROLP and EOLP represent true progression. Not all patients with ROLP develop EOLP, so our findings should be interpreted as group-level differences rather than evidence of sequential transformation. Furthermore, although TIGIT was the only inhibitory receptor showing differential expression, future studies incorporating broader inhibitory receptor profiling and functional assays would provide a more comprehensive understanding of inhibitory signaling pathways in OLP. Finally, although the sample size was adequate for initial analysis, it may have reduced statistical power, particularly for subgroup comparisons, and obscured subtle but meaningful differences. Despite these constraints, our study provides a broad overview of the systemic immune dysregulation underlying OLP development.

In conclusion, OLP presents distinct immunological profiles: ROLP with a Th1/Th17-cytokine driven response and EOLP with an inhibitory immune profile and elevated TGF-β1. Shared B/NK cell and TIGIT dysregulation highlight potential immunomodulatory pathways worth exploring in future studies and highlight the complex interplay between T, B, and NK cells in OLP pathogenesis.
