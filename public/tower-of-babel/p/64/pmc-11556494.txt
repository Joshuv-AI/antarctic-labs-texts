Efficacy of Follicular Cell Pattern Analysis in Thyroid Fine-needle Aspiration Cytology Evaluation
Authors: Sasidharannair Chandrakumari Abilash, Singaravelu Shree Lakshmi Devi, Sinha Pammy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Context:Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is widely utilized for thyroid lesion diagnosis but faces challenges such as sample inadequacy and overlapping cytological features. This study examines how accurately these patterns correlate with histopathological diagnoses, shedding light on FNAC’s limitations and diagnostic potential.Aims:To study the application of the architectural pattern of follicular cells in the interpretation of thyroid lesions and to demonstrate the diagnostic accuracy (DA) of FNAC.Settings and Design:Cross-sectional study carried over 1 year.Subjects and Methods:A total of 110 cases were reviewed by the cytopathologists. The prominent follicular cell architecture, namely macrofollicular, microfollicular, papillary, trabecular, three-dimensional clusters, and dispersed cells, was described in each case. In addition to these patterns, cellular morphology and background features were also noted, and a final cytological diagnosis was established. The cytology diagnosis was correlated with the histopathological diagnosis.Statistical Analysis Used:Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, DA of FNAC in diagnosing nonneoplastic and neoplastic lesions.Results:Macrofollicular pattern was seen in 80.26% of colloid goiter cases. Microfollicular pattern was observed in 72.2% of follicular neoplasm. About 62.5% of papillary thyroid carcinomas showed a papillary pattern. The trabecular pattern was seen in 42.86% of chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis and 16.67% of follicular neoplasms. The sensitivity and specificity of FNAC in diagnosing neoplastic lesions was 92.59% and 97.59%, respectively.Conclusions:FNAC is a simple, rapid, definite, and cost-effective primary diagnostic tool for thyroid evaluation. Cell architecture pattern is a simple and appropriate approach that complements cell morphology and background details in arriving at the final cytological diagnosis of thyroid lesions.

INTRODUCTION

Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is a widely accepted, rapid first-line screening and diagnostic tool for the initial evaluation and management of thyroid lesions.[12] Although it is widely accepted and has gained much attraction among clinicians and pathologists globally, there are few limitations such as sample inadequacy and overlapping cytological picture, which make the interpretation of FNAC samples challenging even in the hands of experienced cytopathologists.[34] The thyroid follicle cells in FNAC samples show varying cytomorphological patterns based on the type of lesion, namely micro/macro follicular, papillary, three-dimensional (3-D) clusters, trabecular and presence of hemosiderin-laden cyst macrophages. The follicular cell pattern in each case, along with the background materials and presence of any other cells, have been recorded, and the cytological diagnosis was established. These findings were matched with the histopathological diagnosis, and the limitations of FNAC have been discussed.[5]

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

This is a cross-sectional study carried out in a Tertiary Care Hospital, Kerala, over 1 year. The study was approved by the institutional human ethical committee. Informed consent was obtained from all the participants, and the confidentiality and anonymity of the data were maintained throughout. The sample size was estimated by using the equation 4PQ/L2, P is the proportion taken from similar study[5] where it was found to be 21% and L equates to absolute error, and it had been taken as 8% and the estimated sample size was 110.

All male and female cases who presented with thyroid lesions and had undergone surgery were included in the study. Cases with inadequate aspirates and cases without histopathological diagnosis were excluded from the study. FNAC was performed under standard protocols, the aspirate was smeared into clean glass slides, and wet as well as dry smears were prepared. Wet smears were prepared by immediately introducing the smeared slides into 95% ethyl alcohol and simultaneously stained using H and E and Papanicolaou stains. Dry smears were fixed by air drying and stained with a Geimsa stain.

In this study, to ensure unbiased interpretation, a single cytopathologist conducted a blinded review of all 110 cytology slides without knowledge of the previous cytological diagnoses. The cytopathologist described the prominent cell patterns in each case, including macro/micro follicles, papillary structures, trabecular formations, 3-D clusters, and dispersed cells. Additionally, cellular morphology and background features were documented. A final cytological diagnosis was established based on these observations. The findings were then correlated with the initial cytological and subsequent histopathological diagnoses.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel. Cross-tabulation analysis was conducted manually to determine the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and diagnostic accuracy (DA) of FNAC in diagnosing non-neoplastic and neoplastic lesions.

Macrofollicular pattern

Macrofollicular pattern was seen in 80.26% (61/76) of colloid goiter cases. The macrofollicular pattern is described as colloid-filled globule with numerous follicle cells arranged orderly in monolayered sheets without any follicular distortion and giving rise to a “honeycomb appearance.”

Microfollicular pattern

Unlike macrofollicles, microfollicles are tiny groups or clusters of follicle cells in a garland or circle-like arrangement with or without a central thick colloid. In the study, 72.2% (13/18) of follicular neoplasm showed a microfollicular pattern. This pattern was observed in 14.55% (17/110) of total cases.

Papillary

The papillary pattern was noted in 62.5% (5/8) of papillary thyroid carcinomas (PTCs). This pattern is characterized by follicular cells arranged in a single or multiple rows comprising overlapping nuclei resembling a papillary structure and showing a branching pattern with or without a central fibrovascular core. In more than 50% of our cases, a definite fibrovascular core was absent.

Cystic

The study cystic pattern was seen in around 14.47% (11/76) of colloid goiter cases and 12% (1/8) of PTC, overall cystic pattern was noticed in 10.91% (12/110) of total cases. This pattern showed the presence of plenty of hemosiderin-laden cyst macrophages against a granular background.

Trabecular

The trabecular pattern was seen in 42.86% (3/7) of chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis, 11.11% (2/18) of follicular neoplasm, 2.63% (2/76) of colloid goiter, and overall, it was described in 6.36% of total cases. This pattern was characterized by a crowded linear arrangement of follicular cells mimicking the appearance of trabeculae.

Dispersed

Follicular cells that are scattered singly throughout the smear without a definite pattern have been referred to as dispersed patterns. This pattern was noted in 42.86% (3/7) of chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis and also one case of anaplastic carcinoma reported in this study also showed a dispersed pattern.

Three-dimensional clusters

The 3-D clusters in the thyroid aspirate showed a crowded group of overlapping follicular cells. These 3-D groups are sized variably, irregularly shaped, lack of orderly honey-comb pattern of arrangement, and absent colloid. In the study, 16.67% (3/18) cases of follicular neoplasm and 1.31% (1/76) of colloid goiter showed a 3-D pattern of cellular arrangement.

Colloid goiter

Among the 76 cases of colloid goitre, consensus of diagnosis was found in 71 cases (93.42%). Among the 71 cases, the macrofollicular pattern was observed in 59 cases, the cystic pattern was seen in 11 cases, and the trabecular and 3-D pattern was seen in one case each. Disagreement was observed in 5 cases; out of these, two cases were confirmed as follicular adenoma (one showed microfollicular and other showed macrofollicular pattern on cytology) and the other two cases were confirmed as thyroiditis (one showed microfollicular and other showed trabecular pattern on cytology).

Chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis

Concordance of diagnosis was found in six out of seven cases (85.71%). Among these six cases, the most common pattern observed was the dispersed pattern, seen in 3 cases (50%), followed by the trabecular pattern in 2 cases (33.33%), and the microfollicular pattern in 1 case (16.67%). Discordance was observed in a single case where the histopathological diagnosis was nodular hyperplasia, while the cytology showed a trabecular pattern.

Follicular neoplasm

Cytological diagnosis of follicular neoplasm was made on 18 cases, of which histopathological consensus was reached on 15 (83.33%) cases. The predominant cytomorphological pattern was microfollicular architecture seen in 12 cases (80%), trabecular pattern was noted in two cases (13.33%), and 3D cluster pattern was observed in a single case (6.67%). One was reported as follicular carcinoma and it showed microfollicular pattern on cytology. Among the 15 cases, one case was reported as follicular carcinoma and it showed a microfollicular pattern on cytology. Discordance was seen in three cases, of which two cases were diagnosed as nodular hyperplasia (one showed trabecular, and the other showed 3D pattern in cytology) and one as a follicular variant of PTC (FVPTC) on histopathology, which showed microfollicular pattern on cytology.

Papillary thyroid carcinoma

Among the eight cases of cytologically diagnosed PTC, histopathological consensus was reached in seven cases (87.5%). The predominant cell pattern in cytology was papillary pattern observed in five cases (71.43%), and cystic and microfollicular pattern was seen in one case (14.28%) each. Discordance was noted in only one case for which a histopathological diagnosis of follicular adenoma was made, and it showed a microfollicular pattern in cytology.

Anaplastic carcinoma

In the study, one case was diagnosed as anaplastic carcinoma in cytology, which was conformed in histopathology, and it showed dispersed cell pattern on cytology.

DISCUSSION

Diseases of the thyroid often present as an evident swelling in front of the neck. Thyroid enlargement can be symmetrical, diffuse, or nodular and it can be of neoplastic or nonneoplastic causes. Hence, the swelling of the thyroid gland has to be investigated at an earlier stage itself, and by doing so, it avoids unnecessary surgical interventions. Currently, many diagnostic approaches, namely ultrasonogram, FNAC, radionucleotide scan, antithyroid antibodies, and hormonal studies are in practice for the screening of thyroid lesions. Among these modalities fine-needle aspiration is considered first-line screening method for the early detection of thyroid swellings.

FNAC provides a simple, rapid, definite, and cost-effective approach and it is application in the initial evaluation of thyroid has been widely acceptable and encouraged globally. For effective clinical management of thyroid lesions, it is necessary to provide all diagnostic information in the final report. Fine-needle aspiration of thyroid swellings generally yields adequate cellularity when performed from the representative thyroid lesion. The aspirate will be considered unsatisfactory or nondiagnostic when the smear shows <6 groups or clusters comprising at least 10 follicular cells in each group, poorly smeared or stained, follicular cells are obscured by blood or thick nature of the smear. It should be noted that sample adequacy depends not only on the procedural technique but also depends on the nature of the swelling whether the swelling is cystic or solid.[6] The final interpretation of thyroid cytology by the cytopathologist is based on the follicular cell pattern and other background elements such as the presence or absence of colloids, lymphocytes, and cyst macrophages.

Macrofollicular pattern is the predominant architectural pattern contributing to 55.46% (61/110) of total cases in our study. Macrofollicular pattern is usually observed in colloid-rich thyroid aspirates and it is the principal finding in benign thyroid lesions. In the study, all the macrofollicular patterned aspirates were reported by the cytopathologists to be colloid goiter. Apart from the key feature of the monolayered honeycomb appearance of follicular cells, an additional diagnostic feature of abundant watery colloid aids in establishing the cytological diagnosis of colloid goiter. These findings were in accord with the study done by Bommanahalli et al. and Pahuja et al.[57]

Prominent microfollicular pattern was observed predominantly in follicular neoplasms. Out of 13 cases diagnosed as follicular neoplasm in cytology by the microfollicular architecture, 12 cases (92.31%) showed concordance with histopathology, one discordant case of follicular neoplasm turned out to be FVPTC on histopathology. This is mainly due to the poor staining and fixation techniques the nuclear features were not retained in the cytology. Similar findings were also recorded in the study done by Goodell et al. and Shih et al.[89]

Papillary pattern of arrangement of follicular cells is seen in the hyperplastic nodule, PTC, and grave’s disease. True papillae with central fibrovascular core are rarely found in the cytology smears, since intact papillae are too big to be aspirated through needles and are disrupted while smearing; papillary structures without fibrovascular core are commonly seen in the papillary patterned lesions. In the study, papillary pattern is seen only in PTC and its showed 100% concordance on histopathology. The presence of characteristic nuclear feature of nuclear grooving is classically seen in almost all cases of PTC additional nuclear findings of pseudo-nuclear inclusions confirm PTC. One feature that is useful in differentiating PTC from FVPTC is that the nuclei of the later is smaller when compared to the nuclei of the earlier.[101112]

Follicular cells forming predominant trabecular architecture were seen in colloid goiter, thyroiditis, and follicular adenoma. The concordance with histopathology was arrived in 5/7 cases (71.43%) with trabecular pattern. Of the two discordant cases, one of the colloid goiter cases was confirmed as thyroiditis; this was due to the dominance of follicular component and less abundant inflammatory component. The other discordant case was thyroiditis, which was confirmed as nodular goiter with secondary lymphoid aggregates.

Dispersed pattern of follicular cell arrangement was noticed in thyroiditis and Anaplastic thyroid cancer in our study. The concordance was seen in 4/4 (100%) cases. Similar observation was also made in the study conducted by Bommanahalli et al. and Nayar and Frost.[513] The presence of hemosiderin macrophages is usually seen in cystic lesions of the thyroid, such as colloid goiter with cystic change, PTC, and thyroglossal cyst. It is always recommended that, after complete aspiration of fluid form cystic lesions, every effort should be made to get material from solid areas; in such cases real-time ultrasound-guided FNAC is the best method to be employed.[1415] Cystic lesions showed 12/12 (100%) concordance with histopathology. In the study, the malignancy rate of cystic lesions was found 8.33% (1/12); the proportion is slightly lower than the findings seen in other similar studies.[513]

3-D architecture is seen in follicular neoplasm and occasionally PTC. The neoplastic rate of 3-D patterned lesions was 50% (2/4) and the malignancy rate was 25% (1/4). The degree of cellularity, amount of colloid, nuclear size, and pleomorphism help in distinguishing neoplastic from nonneoplastic lesions. In the study, 3-D clusters were predominantly seen in follicular adenoma. Thyroid lesions with 3-D clusters on cytology showed concurrence in ¾ (75%) cases.

In the study, histopathology concordance was observed in 92.73% (102/110) of total cases. The DA of FNAC in distinguishing neoplastic lesions of the thyroid was found to be 96.36%. The malignancy rate of follicular lesions reported in our study was 22.22% (4/18), and the rate was close to that of the study done by Ersöz et al.[16] and lower than the study done by Greaves et al.[17] who observed a malignancy rate of 30%.

Limitations and recommendations

The study was a single hospital-based study with a limited sample, so, the interpretations of the study cannot be generalized. However taking this as a reference, a multicentric study with a larger sample size can be planned in the future.

CONCLUSIONS

The DA majorly depends on the adequacy of the sample. Complementary to cell morphology and background details, identifying the follicular architecture is a simple, appropriate, and supportive way to arrive at the final cytological diagnosis of thyroid lesions. Microfollicular, trabecular, and 3-D architecture is predominantly seen in neoplastic lesions; macrofollicular and dispersed pattern is more commonly noticed in colloid goiter and thyroiditis, respectively. Recognizing follicular architecture is a useful approach in cytopathology to identify lesions of thyroid, especially follicular neoplasm and PTC.
