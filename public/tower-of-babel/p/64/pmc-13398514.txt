Artificial Intelligence Performance Under Different Conditions in Answering China's Standardized Training Examination for Resident Physician in Radiology: A Comparative Analysis
Authors: Zheng Zhu, Yanfeng Zhao, Lin Li, Xiaoyi Wang, Yongming Zhang, Xinming Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe capabilities of general‐purpose large language models (LLMs) on specialized medical examinations have not been systematically compared. The LLMs were subjected to two tasks (questions with/without answer choices). Background

The rapid evolution of artificial intelligence (AI), particularly the advancement of large language models (LLMs), presents both transformative opportunities and significant challenges for healthcare and medical education. LLMs have demonstrated remarkable potential in enhancing diagnostic procedures, treatment planning, and academic training [1]. However, despite their promise, the integration of LLMs into clinical practice remains hindered by critical issues. These include a lack of formal validation in real‐world medical tasks, susceptibility to generating inaccurate or hallucinated content, inconsistent performance across different clinical scenarios, and concerns regarding data privacy [2, 3]. Moreover, evidence suggests that even state‐of‐the‐art LLMs do not yet achieve consistent perfection in complex clinical decision‐making [2].

Results of a comprehensive JAMA systematic review of 519 publications on LLMs in health care [4] suggest that current evaluations of LLMs in health care are fragmented and insufficient, and that evaluations require covering a wider range of tasks and specialities, and reporting standardized performance metrics to enable broader implementation. In terms of medical speciality area, most studies were in generic health care applications (25.6%), internal medicine (16.4%), surgery (11.4%), and ophthalmology (6.9%), with nuclear medicine (0.6%), physical medicine (0.4%), and medical genetics (0.2%) being the least represented.

The Standardized Training Examination for Resident Physicians in Radiology is typically administered in China to trainees who have completed 3 years of didactic learning and clinical rotations. This examination assesses not only factual recall but also complex clinical reasoning and decision‐making. However, the performance of general‐purpose LLMs without domain‐specific fine‐tuning on such high‐stakes, specialized tests remains largely unknown.

Therefore, this study aims to assess the performance of three non‐domain‐specific LLMs, which are ChatGPT‐o1, DeepSeek‐R1, and Gemini‐2.0, by evaluating their accuracy on this examination under various conditions. Our work aims to benchmark their medical reasoning capabilities and provide both objective and subjective insights into their potential utility in healthcare.

Methods

Study Design

We evaluated three of the most mainstream and representative LLMs available at the time of research: DeepSeek‐R1, ChatGPT‐o1, and Gemini‐2.0. The “Search” function was enabled for all models, permitting access to external information sources (e.g., performing online searches or accessing databases) to provide directed responses to user queries. The study was performed between May 25 and 31, 2025.

A total of 131 questions from the examination were included. Two tasks were designed: Task 1 presented questions with answer choices, and Task 2 presented the same questions without answer choices. For each task, three conditions were applied sequentially: (1) no doubt, (2) weak doubt, and (3) strong doubt. The doubt conditions were introduced by asking follow‐up questions after the LLMs' initial responses. The questions were further classified into two dimensions: knowledge‐based vs. case‐based, and single‐choice vs. multiple‐choice. Model accuracy was calculated for each task and condition. In addition, a subjective evaluation of the LLMs' responses was performed by expert raters using a 5‑point Likert scale.

Input Source

Using a new chat session for every task each time, we provided each LLM with the same prompt requesting the answers. All questions from the Selected Exercise Set for the Standardized Training Plan Textbook of Resident Physicians (radiology volume) were identified [5]. The textbook is a set of tests of resident‐level knowledge, which are required for the standardized training examination for resident physician in radiology in China. This series of textbooks contain training material for medical students and residents but are not available online, preventing leakage into the training data sets of the LLMs.

Nine clinical specialities were considered: central nervous system, head and neck, skeleton and muscle, respiration system, circulation, digestive system, genitourinary, breast, and interventional radiology. The input language was all Chinese.

All test questions were screened, and questions containing visual assets (e.g., radiological images such as X‐ray, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging) were excluded because some LLMs such as DeepSeek‐R1 still face technical bottlenecks in multimodal data fusion such as image processing [6]. Policies and regulations in medicine may vary in different countries, so questions with policies, regulations and ethics in medicine were all excluded. Some multiple‐choice questions typically require integration of patient history, laboratory findings, description of imaging examinations, and clinical reasoning to arrive at a diagnosis.

Finally, 131 questions included single and multiple choice answers (single choice questions usually have 5 answer choices per question; multiple choice questions usually have 6 answer choices per question). The 131 questions were divided into knowledge‐based and case‐based questions. All 131 questions were provided to LLMs at the same time, instead of one question by one question at multiple times. All questions were copied and pasted into the LLM's chat box at one time, rather than uploaded as a Word or PDF file. Because some LLMs (DeepSeek‐R1) cannot use the “Search” function when a file is uploaded, it states that “Uploading files is unavailable for searching”.

Encoding

Two tasks were tested, each with three conditions: At Task 1 Condition 1, all 131 questions with answer choices were pasted in the LLM's chat box with “Here are 131 questions, some are single choice questions, some are multiple choice questions, please give the answers to all 131 questions in sequence”. After the answers were given, then, Task 1 Condition 2, a weak doubt “There might be some mistake in your answer, please check again” was input into the chat box to ask the LLMs a second time. After the LLMs replied, finally, Task 1 Condition 3, a strong doubt “According to the right answer, there are several questions showed wrong answer, please check again and give the right answer” was input into the chat box to ask the LLMs for the third time (Table 1).

We also provided the same 131 questions without answer choices as Task 2, and still tried the same three conditions on three LLMs. All the questions were performed independently by blinded evaluators (the evaluators were not aware of which output corresponded to which LLM).

Output

These multiple‐choice questions allowed multiple correct answers. The LLMs were provided with the questions in text format and prompted to select the correct answers. Answers that contained one mistake or lacked one correct answer among the multiple‐choice answers were rated as wrong. The responses were classified as “correct” or “incorrect” based on official answer keys.

Additionally, a 5‐point Likert scale [7] (1: poor quality; 5: excellent) was used to evaluate the models' response, linguistic and clinical reasoning including convenience, response, reasonableness, adherence, and confidence on 131 questions, assessed by four independent radiologists who had 4, 15, 20 and 23 years of working experience and were blinded to each other. The five evaluation dimensions were defined as follows. Convenience: The ease and efficiency of obtaining an answer from the model interface; Response Quality: The relevance, completeness, and directness of the content provided; Reasonableness: The clinical plausibility and logical coherence of the answer within a medical context; Adherence: The degree to which the model followed specific output instructions; Confidence: The perceived certainty and reliability conveyed by the response's language and structure.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed by using R software (version 4.2.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The categorical data are expressed in terms of quantity (n) and percentage (%) and comparisons between groups were made using the chi‐square test (χ 2 test) or Fisher's exact probability method. McNemar tests were performed for the paired‐sample analysis comparisons. A two‐sided p value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Task 1: Questions With Answer Choices

Performance of the three LLMs on 131 questions with answer choices varied, with DeepSeek‐R1 achieving an overall accuracy of 0.763 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.680–0.831), ChatGPT‐o1 achieving an overall accuracy of 0.649 (95% CI: 0.560–0.729), Gemini‐2.0 achieving an overall accuracy of 0.809 (95% CI: 0.729–0.870) for the first time. DeepSeek‐R1 achieved better accuracy than ChatGPT‐o1 when combining all 131 questions (χ 2 = 6.323, p = 0.012) and was slightly inferior to Gemini‐2.0 (χ 2 = 1.389, p = 0.239). Then, weak doubt accuracy was 0.748 (95% CI: 0.663–0.818) for DeepSeek‐R1, 0.664 (95% CI: 0.576–0.743) for ChatGPT‐o1, and 0.809 (95% CI: 0.729–0.870) for Gemini‐2.0. DeepSeek‐R1 achieved accuracy slightly better than ChatGPT‐o1 when weak doubt was applied (χ 2 = 3.030, p = 0.082) and was slightly inferior to Gemini‐2.0 (χ 2 = 2.450, p = 0.118). Third, strong doubt accuracy was 0.748 (95% CI: 0.663–0.818) for DeepSeek‐R1, 0.664 (95% CI: 0.576–0.743) for ChatGPT‐o1, and 0.763 (95% CI: 0.680–0.831) for Gemini‐2.0. DeepSeek‐R1 achieved accuracy slightly superior to ChatGPT‐o1 when strong doubt was applied (χ 2 = 3.030, p = 0.082) and was slightly inferior to Gemini‐2.0 (χ 2 = 0.062, p = 0.803) (Table 2 and Figure 1).

Task 2: Questions Without Answer Choices

When provided the same 131 questions without answer choices, accuracy dropped to 0.679 (95% CI: 0.591–0.757) for DeepSeek‐R1, 0.489 (95% CI: 0.401–0.577) for ChatGPT‐o1, and 0.679 (95% CI: 0.591–0.757) for Gemini‐2.0. DeepSeek‐R1 achieved better accuracy than ChatGPT‐o1 when given questions without answer choices (χ 2 = 15.568, p < 0.001) and the same as Gemini‐2.0 (χ 2 = 0.000, p = 1.000). Secondly, weak doubt accuracy was 0.588 (95% CI: 0.498–0.672) for DeepSeek‐R1, 0.466 (95% CI: 0.379–0.555) for ChatGPT‐o1, and 0.595 (95% CI: 0.506–0.679) for Gemini‐2.0. DeepSeek‐R1 achieved accuracy slightly better than ChatGPT‐o1 under the weak doubt condition without answer choices (χ 2 = 7.031, p = 0.008) and was slightly inferior to Gemini‐2.0 (χ 2 = 0.000, p = 1.000). Last, strong doubt accuracy was 0.611 (95% CI: 0.521–0.693) for DeepSeek‐R1, 0.496 (95% CI: 0.408–0.584) for ChatGPT‐o1, and 0.618 (95% CI: 0.529–0.701) for Gemini‐2.0. DeepSeek‐R1 achieved better accuracy than ChatGPT‐o1 under the strong doubt condition without answer choices (χ 2 = 5.600, p = 0.018) and was slightly inferior to Gemini‐2.0 (χ 2 = 0.000, p = 1.000) (Table 2 and Figure 1).

Condition 1‐2‐3 in Task 1: No/Weak/Strong Doubt in Questions With Answer Choices

When it comes to doubt conditions in questions with answer choices, each LLM performed differently but with no statistical significance (p > 0.05). In weak doubt, DeepSeek‐R1's accuracy slightly dropped, ChatGPT‐o1's accuracy slightly increased, while Gemini‐2.0's accuracy remained the same. In strong doubt, DeepSeek‐R1 and ChatGPT‐o1 remained the same but Gemini‐2.0's accuracy slightly dropped.

Condition 1‐2‐3 in Task 2: No/Weak/Strong Doubt in Questions Without Answer Choices

For questions without answer choices, all three LLMs' accuracy dropped in weak doubt condition, but only DeepSeek‐R1 showed statistical significance (p = 0.014). In the strong doubt condition, all three LLMs' accuracy slightly increased compared to the weak doubt condition, with no statistical significance (p > 0.05), but still slightly inferior to the no doubt condition, with no statistical significance (p > 0.05).

Single/Multiple‐Choice Questions

All 131 questions included 110 single questions (Knowledge‐based = 53; Case‐based = 57) and 21 multiple questions (Knowledge‐based = 14; Case‐based = 7) (Table 3). All three LLMs performed better in single‐choice questions than multiple‐choice questions in all conditions and tasks. The accuracy range of single‐choice questions in DeepSeek‐R1, ChatGPT‐o1, and Gemini‐2.0 was 0.636–0.882, 0.518–0.755, and 0.655–0.918, respectively. The accuracy range of multiple‐choice questions in DeepSeek‐R1, ChatGPT‐o1, and Gemini‐2.0 is 0.143–0.381, 0.190–0.286, and 0.190–0.429, respectively (Table 4 and Figure 2).

Subjective Evaluation of LLMs

DeepSeek‐R1 demonstrated the best performance overall, showing the highest adherence across all conditions, the fastest response time, and the ability to output in Chinese. While Gemini‐2.0's output answer was mixed with Chinese and English, the English part is mainly in the thinking part and some explanation, but with no answers missing. Mixed language output did not contribute to misunderstandings or incorrect responses. ChatGPT‐o1 was initially reluctant to answer all 131 questions at the very beginning, both in Task 1 (under weak and strong doubt) and in all conditions of Task 2. It then started to negotiate, proposing to divide the questions into different parts according to the system. Although it agreed to answer 20 questions at a time, it actually output only 10 answers each time. Moreover, some answers were missing without any notice from the model itself (e.g., 1–5 answers might be missing in the weak doubt condition of Task 2). Consequently, after each output, we had to input “answer the next 10 questions” to proceed. Additionally, ChatGPT‐o1's output sometimes contained a mix of Chinese and English. Furthermore, it might include references and website links as explanations, roughly for every 10 questions.

The 5‐point Likert scale evaluation of the generated reasoning yielded scores of 4.46 for DeepSeek‐R1, 3.92 for ChatGPT‐o1, and 3.83 for Gemini‐2.0 (Figure 4 and Table 6).

Discussion

In our study, Gemini‐2.0 achieved the highest performance among the three LLMs in answering 131 radiology‐related questions in Chinese across two tasks and three experimental conditions. This outcome was contrary to our initial expectation, as DeepSeek‐R1 might have been presumed more adept at processing Chinese medical questions. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, model performance can be highly sensitive to prompt formulation, a critical consideration for domain‐specific applications like medicine. Further study on those comparisons is warranted [8]. Second, it is possible that DeepSeek‐R1 is less familiar with the radiology questions since the other two LLMs had been tested on medical‐related questions before [9, 10].

In Task 1 (questions with answer choices), all three LLMs showed high accuracy to pass the threshold for all three conditions without any specialized training (accuracy > 0.6). The accuracy in our study is consistent with previous research focusing on the Chinese National Medical Licensing Examination [11], which is more like the United States Medical Licensing Examination version in China. Several studies showed that different versions of ChatGPT all performed well in the United States Medical Licensing Examination [12, 13, 14]. While in Task 2 (open‐ended questions without answer choices), most models achieved an accuracy below 0.6, failing to meet the passing standard. In fact, we thought Task 2 was much more like the real clinical world than Task 1. There will be no options when a clinician comes to a situation requiring an answer; he/she needs to find the answer by himself/herself, like every specialty‐focused scenario design for each LLM [15]. Since all three LLMs performed poorly in terms of accuracy, they might not be able to assist clinicians in the real world. More data and training are required for the LLMs, or perhaps strong/weak supervised learning needs to be incorporated into the LLMs when applied in radiology‐related situations.

Regarding the weak and strong doubt conditions, accuracy decreased across most tasks, with the exception of ChatGPT‐o1 in Condition 2/3 of Task 1. While previous studies suggest that specific hints or “chain‐of‐thought” prompts can improve performance on single items [16, 17, 18], our study imposed a significantly higher cognitive load by requiring batch self‐correction on 131 questions without specific error indicators. This setup mimics a realistic, high‐pressure exam scenario. Notably, under the strong doubt condition—designed to increase error correction difficulty through iterative questioning—self‐examination failed to improve accuracy and often proved detrimental. This decline suggests that when faced with persistent doubt, LLMs tend to abandon correct answers rather than successfully correcting errors, reflecting a limitation in their reasoning stability under pressure.

As for the single/multiple questions, all three LLMs performed better in single questions than in multiple questions, which is similar to another study [19] that demonstrated strong and comparable performance on single‐choice questions, highlighting their suitability for tasks requiring general radiology‐related knowledge. For the knowledge/case‐based questions, the accuracy of case‐based questions is higher than that of knowledge‐based questions, which is contrary to our expectation and other studies [8, 20]. This may be because the case‐based questions are serial, with information from one question providing context for the next. Also, there are 7 multiple‐choice questions in all case‐based questions, and 14 multiple‐choice questions in all knowledge‐based questions since the accuracy of multiple‐questions is much lower, the overall accuracy of case‐based questions is higher than that of knowledge‐based questions.

In our study, we observed that ChatGPT‐o1 occasionally omitted answers without any warning. This phenomenon is likely attributable to context window overflow or attention dispersal when a large number of questions from heterogeneous radiology systems are input simultaneously. Unlike “hallucinations” where models generate false information [21, 22, 23], this phenomenon represents a failure in information retrieval and output completeness. To our knowledge, this kind of insufficiency or “failure” had not been reported before [4]. Future research should focus on optimizing prompt engineering strategies to ensure comprehensive responses in high‐volume tasks.

Our findings align with recent literature regarding the disparity between AI's exam performance and clinical reasoning. In standardized testing, one study reported that ChatGPT‐o1 achieved 96.1% accuracy in head and neck anatomy exams, confirming the model's strong knowledge retention [24]. However, this proficiency diminishes in complex clinical scenarios. Other research demonstrated that generative models underperformed compared to residents in radiology case vignettes, while a further study found critically low accuracy (4%–7.5%) in identifying anatomical structures for the Fellowship of the Royal College of Radiologists examinations [25, 26]. Despite these diagnostic limitations, the educational utility of LLMs is strongly supported. A study has noted their effectiveness in simulating clinical anamnesis [27], and highlighted positive student attitudes toward AI‐assisted learning [28]. Crucially, a controlled study proved that AI‐generated personalized feedback significantly improved students' radiographic diagnostic performance compared to traditional methods [29]. These results suggest that while LLMs require oversight for clinical decision‐making, they are immediately valuable for radiological education.

This study has several limitations. First, to simulate a high‐throughput data processing workflow, we employed a batch‐processing approach (inputting multiple questions simultaneously). We acknowledge that this may introduce context window limitations or truncation biases compared to a single‐shot approach (one question per prompt). While this reflects the models' capability in handling large‐volume tasks, future studies should isolate these variables to ensure maximum fairness. Second, our study did not include human performance as a control group. We acknowledge that this may limit the clinical contextualization of our accuracy findings and could affect the interpretability of their educational and clinical relevance. Future studies incorporating such comparisons will be important to further validate the utility of AI‐assisted tools in real‐world clinical training. Third, a language barrier exists as all questions were input in Chinese. Since most LLMs are primarily trained on English datasets, this may have underestimated their true capabilities due to cross‐lingual processing losses. Fourth, our study relied exclusively on text‐based questions. Although questions describing imaging findings were included, the models' ability to interpret direct medical imagery remains unassessed. Finally, the ongoing and rapid evolution of LLMs may undermine the validity of current results upon the release of updated versions, and we did not perform a granular analysis of domain‐specific strengths.

Conclusions

LLMs demonstrated variable proficiency in answering radiology questions designed for resident physicians across two tasks, with Gemini‐2.0 showing higher accuracy. These results suggest that LLMs may have the potential to assist with medical education. Future research should focus on multimodal LLMs that can directly interpret DICOM images, bridging the gap between textual reports and visual diagnostics. By continuously improving data quality, privacy protection, technical optimization, ethical and legal compliance, and industry acceptance, LLMs will become a reliable partner for both doctors and patients, driving the intelligent transformation of healthcare.
