Complete mitochondrial genome and phylogenetic implications of Chaunocephalus ferox (Digenea: Echinostomatidae), a critical pathogen for Oriental Stork conservation
Authors: Mengchao Zhou, Hongrui Liang, Zhiyou Lv, Xiaotian Zhang, Yang Liu, Yaxian Lu, Hongyan Yu, Hang Tian, Lei Han, Yu Xu, Zhijun Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChaunocephalus ferox is an intestinal trematode infecting storks and is increasingly recognized as a potential threat to the conservation of the Oriental Stork (Ciconia boyciana). However, its evolutionary history remains poorly understood. In this study, we assembled and annotated the first complete mitochondrial genome of C. ferox using the MITOS2 and GeSeq platforms. The circular genome spans 17,482 bp and encodes 12 protein-coding genes, 22 tRNAs, and 2 rRNAs, with several intergenic overlaps and absence of the atp8 gene. Phylogenetic analyses based on 12 mitochondrial protein-coding genes from 57 trematode species were conducted using maximum likelihood and Bayesian inference methods. While morphological characteristics support its classification within Echinostomatidae, mitochondrial phylogenies yielded inconsistent placements, with C. ferox positioned either near Fasciolidae or outside both families in different gene trees. Divergence time estimation further supports this ambiguity, placing C. ferox's split from other echinostomatids at approximately 75–39 million years ago, a period overlapping with the divergence of Echinostomatidae and Fasciolidae, suggesting it may represent an early-branching lineage. Additionally, cox1 sequences obtained from samples across three regions and two host species were identical, indicating low mitochondrial variation across sampling sites. While the limited sample size precludes firm conclusions about population structure, this pattern may hint at a recent demographic expansion or shared ancestry across geographic areas.Keywords: Chaunocephalus ferox, Oriental stork, Mitochondrial genome, Evolutionary

Introduction

The Echinostomatidae family comprises a diverse group of digenean trematodes that predominantly parasitize the intestines of vertebrate hosts, including avian and mammalian species (Detwiler et al., 2010). Among these parasites, Chaunocephalus ferox stands out as a significant intestinal parasite, infecting storks (Ciconiidae) such as the white stork (Ciconia ciconia), black stork (Ci. nigra), and Oriental Stork (Ci. boyciana) (Choe et al., 2016; Gonzálvez et al., 2022; Höfle et al., 2003). This trematode species demonstrates a broad geographical distribution, with confirmed infections recorded throughout Europe and Asia, where it is associated with severe enteritis and cachexia in affected birds (Höfle et al., 2003; Poonswad et al., 1992). In previous study, C. ferox was detected in 27 deceased storks (96.3 %), environmental fecal samples from wintering habitats ((Poyang Lake, 1.4 %), stopover sites (Bohai Bay, 5.4 %), and breeding grounds (Sanjiang Plain, 5.6 %), as well as in crucian carps (3.1 %); The high infection rate of Ch. ferox in deceased storks, compared to the lower infection rate in field individuals (detected the parasite eggs in the environment fecal samples of storks), indicates that Ch. ferox may have an impact on the health of Oriental Storks with high probability (Zhou et al., 2025). Despite its extensive range and pronounced impact on avian health, the evolutionary history of C. ferox remains poorly understood, highlighting the urgent need for detailed molecular characterization.

Infection with C. ferox, known as chaunocephalosis, induces the formation of granulomatous nodules along the intestinal walls, leading to severe pathological outcomes such as mucosal thickening, hemorrhagic enteritis, and tissue necrosis (Islam et al., 2020). In advanced stages, these infections can cause malnutrition, immunosuppression, and increased host mortality, posing a substantial threat to the conservation of stork populations (Santoro et al., 2014). Numerous reports have documented fatal infections in both wild and captive storks, indicating that the ecological impact of C. ferox may be more profound than previously recognized. The life cycle of C. ferox requires two aquatic intermediate hosts: freshwater snails (first intermediate hosts) and either fish or amphibians (second intermediate hosts) (Höfle et al., 2003). These secondary hosts harbor the metacercariae, which are subsequently transmitted to definitive hosts through trophic interactions. Our previous findings confirmed the presence of C. ferox metacercariae in crucian carps, underscoring the parasite's capacity to exploit a complex aquatic food web for transmission (Zhou et al., 2025).

Mitochondrial genomes (mitogenomes) have been widely used in phylogenetic and evolutionary studies of trematodes due to their maternal inheritance and relatively high mutation rates (Ma et al., 2015). These characteristics make them particularly valuable for species identification and evolutionary inference (Suleman et al., 2021). Previous investigations of trematodes, such as Schistosoma haematobium, Echinostoma revolutum, and Clonorchis sinensis, have demonstrated the utility of complete mitogenomes in resolving taxonomic uncertainties and elucidating genetic variation (Littlewood et al., 2006; Le et al., 2020; Shekhovtsov et al., 2010). Comparative mitogenomic analyses have revealed considerable differences in gene organization, sequence divergence, and evolutionary rates among trematode species. For example, the mitogenome of Eurytrema coelomaticum displays a distinctive elongation of the cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (cox1) gene, differentiating it from other members of the Dicrocoeliidae family (Huang et al., 2023). Furthermore, researches on Gastrothylax crumenifer and Morishitium polonicum have highlighted the critical role of mitogenomic data in clarifying the evolutionary history of Digenea, particularly for species exhibiting cryptic diversity (Yang et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2023).

This study presents the first complete mitochondrial genome of C. ferox, a rare trematode known for its significant pathogenicity in storks and its wide geographic distribution across Eurasia. Using complete mitogenomic data, we aim to elucidate the phylogenetic position of C. ferox within the Digenea clade. By integrating both concatenated and single-gene phylogenetic analyses, as well as molecular clock estimations, we explore the evolutionary history of C. ferox and assess its divergence in relation to other trematode families. These findings provide a valuable genomic resource and contribute to a deeper understanding of digenean evolution and parasite diversity in endangered avian hosts.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Chaunocephalus ferox specimens were obtained from an Oriental white stork after its natural death around the Bohai Bay, China. After extensive washing with sterile physiological saline (37 °C), snap-freezing, and shipping on dry ice, all specimens were stored at - 80 °C until usage. Morphological identification was performed using a microscope (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan). All experimental designs and animal handling were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Northeast Forestry University (No. 2024WPE07). Following the manufacturer's instructions, the DNA of the specimens isolated from the intestines of Oriental Stork was extracted with QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAamp, Germany).

Mitogenome sequencing, assembly and annotation

To ensure the reliability of subsequent credit analysis results, the quality of sequencing raw reads needs to be assessed first, and low-quality data needs to be removed. More than 8 GB raw reads of the sample were yielded, which were then trimmed of adapter contamination using NGS-Toolkit (Patel and Jain, 2012). Two assembly strategies were employed to reconstruct the mitogenome of C. ferox. The first method employed the program NOVOPlasty v4.3.5 using the following options: K-mer = 39, Read Length = 150, Insert size = 300, Insert Range = 1.8, Insert Range strict = 1.3 (Dierckxsens et al., 2017). A partial cox1 gene sequence belonging to C. ferox available on GenBank (PQ676277.1) was used as a ‘seed' input of assemblies. The second strategy employed the program GetOrganelle v1.7.7.1 (Jin et al., 2020) using the following options: k 21,45,65,85,105 and -F animal_mt, with all other settings left at their default values. Mitogenome assembled using the two methods above were almost identical to each other using MEGA 12 (Kumar et al., 2024). Several base discrepancies were corrected by aligning the short reads to both mitogenomes using BWA (Li and Durbin, 2009). Each inconsistent site was manually curated with Samtools (Li et al., 2009) and BCFtools (Narasimhan et al., 2016), ultimately yielding the complete mitogenome.

The locations of protein-coding genes (PCGs), transfer RNAs (tRNAs), and ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) in the generated mitogenomes were identified using the web-based tools MITOS2 (http://mitos2.bioinf.uni-leipzig.de/index.py) (Bernt et al., 2013) and GeSeq (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/geseq.html). Any remaining unidentified tRNAs were located using the default search mode of tRNAscan-SE v. 2.0 (Lowe and Eddy, 1997), and the secondary structures within tRNAs were predicted by RNAfold (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at/cgi-bin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi) server using default parameters. The Tandem Repeats Finder was used to identify repetitive sequences in non-coding regions (Benson, 1999) All PCGs were identified using the ORF Finder tool, applying the flatworm mitochondrial genetic code (https://www.bioinformatics.org/sms2/orf_find.html). Circular genomic maps for the trematode were generated using CGView v2.0.3 (Grant and Stothard, 2008) software (https://github.com/paulstothard/cgview?tab=readme-ov-file) generated the circular genomic maps for the trematode, next, using MEGA 12 to generated base composition and the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU). Strand asymmetry was calculated using the formulae: AT-skew = (A − T)/(A + T); GC-skew = (G − C)/(G + C).

Phylogenetic analysis, divergence time estimation and population genetic analysis

To investigate structural variations in the mitogenomes of C. ferox and other trematodes, as well as their phylogenetic relationships, complete mitogenomes from 56 species representing the subclass Digenea were retrieved from the International Nucleotide Sequence Database Collaboration (Table S1). One Cestoda species (Breviscolex orient) were used as an outgroup. PhyloSuite (Zhang et al., 2020) was used to extract the 12 PCGs from the 57 trematode mitochondrial genomes, which were subsequently aligned and concatenated for phylogenetic analysis. In addition to the concatenated analysis of all 12 PCGs, individual phylogenetic trees were also constructed for each gene to validate the robustness and consistency of the phylogenetic relationships inferred from the concatenated data. The best-fitting nucleotide substitution model was selected by MrModeltest 2.4 software with Akaike information criterion (AIC) (Nylander Johan, 2004). Phylogenetic analyses were performed using MEGA 12 (Kumar et al., 2024) for maximum likelihood (ML) and MrBayes v3.2.7 (Ronquist et al., 2012) for Bayesian inference (BI). For ML, the Bootstrap branch support values were obtained with 1000 rapid bootstrap inferences, followed by a thorough ML search on the dataset. For BI, four Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) ran for 2 runs from random starting trees for 5 million generations, and the trees were sampled every 1000 generations. Moreover, 25 % of generations were discarded as “burn-in”, and the remaining samples were used to calculate Bayesian posterior probabilities (BPP). Phylograms were plotted using Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) v6 tool (https://itol.embl.de/) (Letunic and Bork, 2024).

The molecular timing studies based on the 57 species of Digenea were estimated using BEAST v10.5.0 (Suchard et al., 2018). Three time constraints were imposed to calibrate the molecular clock: (1) the split of Troglotrematidae was constrained to be 67.41–124.8 Mya (Vainutis et al., 2022); (2) the split between Fasciolopsis and Fasciola was constrained to be 80.4–95.8 Mya (Choi et al., 2020); (3) the split between Fasciola hepatica and Fa. gigantica was constrained to be 4.39–6.19 Mya (Choi et al., 2020). The uncorrected log-normal relaxed clock was used as the clock model, and we used the “Yule species tree prior model”. Three independent runs with MCMC chain lengths of 100 million generations were executed in BEAST v10.5.0, and the trees were sampled every 1000 generations. Convergence of the three parallel runs was assessed in Tracer v.1.7.2 (Rambaut et al., 2018) by checking the effective sample sizes (ESSs) of the parameters (values of >200). The samples collected during the initial burn-in (25 %) were discarded. The maximum credibility clades of the tree were created using TreeAnnonator in BEAST. All topologies were edited and visualized by the software FigTree v.1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/Figtree/).

In our previous study, samples of C. ferox were collected from three different geographic locations and host species. The infection rate was assessed using PCR-based detection methods (Zhou et al., 2025). Based on this dataset, population genetic parameters—including the fixation index (FST) and measures of genetic diversity—were calculated using DnaSP version 6.12.03 (Rozas et al., 2017).

Results

Genome organization and composition

The complete mitogenome of C. ferox is presented as a circular molecule of 17,482 bp in length (Fig. 1). It encodes 36 genes, including 12 PCGs, 22 tRNAs, and 2 rRNAs, along with a major non-coding region (NCR) located between the trnE and cox3 genes (Table S2). The genome also contains 11 intergenic spacers, consisting of 8 short non-coding regions (SNRs; 263 bp each region) and 3 long non-coding regions (LNRs; 563 bp each region) (Table S3). The 22 tRNAs genes of C. ferox ranged in length from 58 bp (trnR) to 69 bp (trnN) (Table S2 and Fig. S1). The rrn16 gene was located between trnT and trnC, and rrn12 was located between trnC and cox2. Notably, the atp8 gene is absent, a feature consistent with other trematode mitogenomes. Additionally, six instances of gene overlap were identified, ranging from 1 to 45 bp, with the largest overlap occurring between the trnC and rrn16 (Table S2).

The circular mitochondrial genome of Chaunocephalus ferox. PCGs are in blue; rRNAs are in pink; tRNAs are in brown. All genes are transcribed in the clockwise direction.

The nucleotide composition of C. ferox mitogenome is as follows: A = 20.86 %, G = 27.09 %, C = 11.53 %, T = 40.53 %, resulting in an A + T content of 61.39 % and a G + C content of 38.62 % (Table S4). Among the PCGs, nad4l showed the highest AT content (65.15 %), while cox2 displayed the lowest (59.11 %). All AT-skew values across the mitogenome were negative, ranging from −0.582 to −0.104, whereas GC-skew values were consistently positive, spanning 0.330 to 0.506 (Table S4). This pattern reflects strand asymmetry, a feature commonly observed in the mitogenomes of trematodes and other parasitic flatworms. Analysis of start/stop codons revealed that among the 12 PCGs, cox1 uniquely employs GTG as the start codon, while the remaining genes initiate with conventional ATG. Termination codons were divided between TAA (cox3, cob, nad2, nad3, and cox2) and TAG (nad4l, nad4, atp6, nad1, cox1, nad6, and nad 5) (Table S2). Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) analysis of the 12 PCGs indicated that Leucine (Leu) and Valine (Val) are the most frequently encoded amino acids (Fig. 2). The codons UUG, AGG, GCU, ACU, UGA, GGU, and UCU were the most commonly used, each with RSCU values exceeding 2.0, with UUG being the most prevalent.

Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) of codons. The box below the bar chart represents all codons encoding each amino acid, and the height of the column above represents the sum of all RSCU values.

Comparative analysis of gene arrangements among trematode taxa revealed that C. ferox shares an identical gene order with other species in the Echinostomatidae family (Fig. S2). Furthermore, gene order is highly conserved across the Fasciolidae, Plagiorchiidae, and Opisthorchiidae families, with variation primarily limited to translocations involving the trnE and trnG. In contrast, the Schistosomatidae family, particularly Schistosoma haematobium, exhibits extensive reorganization of PCGs and tRNAs. When compared to the mitochondrial gene order of C. ferox, S. haematobium retains only three conserved gene blocks without translocation: trnH-cytb-nad4L-nad4-trnQ, cox1-trnT-rrn16-trnC-rrn12-cox2-nad6-trnY-trnL1-trnS2, and trnR-nad 5-trnG (Fig. 3). Notably, within the Schistosomatidae, S. japonicum displays a distinct gene arrangement that diverges from other Schistosoma species but more closely resembles the gene order observed in non-schistosome genera (Fig. S2).

Mitochondrial gene arrangement order in 3 Trematode species. The frames in green, pink, and blue represent PCGs, rRNAs, and tRNAs, respectively. The solid and dashed lines indicate that there is no change in gene order between the adjacent species.

Phylogenies, divergence time and population structure

To investigate the phylogenetic relationships among trematodes, the 12 PCGs from 57 species were selected for analysis due to their high sensitivity of these genes in trematode classification (Tatonova et al., 2013). Prior to phylogenetic reconstruction, nucleotide substitution saturation was evaluated using DAMBEv7.3.11 (Xia, 2018). The results indicated low saturation levels (Iss < Iss. c), confirming the suitability of these sequences for phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 4). Phylogenetic trees generated by ML and BI methods showed consistent topologies, identifying four distinct orders: Caryophyllidea, Plagiorchiida, Opisthorchiida, and Strigeidida (Fig. 5). Moreover, the topologies inferred from the concatenated dataset were largely congruent with those from individual gene trees (Fig. S3–14), suggesting an overall stable phylogenetic signal at the higher taxonomic levels. These findings are consistent with previously established relationships among trematode families (Hu et al., 2024). In our analyses, C. ferox did not cluster within the Echinostomatidae clade. In the concatenated tree and individual gene trees based on nad4l, atp6, nad2, nad3, nad6, C. ferox formed a weakly supported clade with species of Fasciolidae. In contrast, analyses based on cox3, cob, nad4, nad1, cox1, and cox2 placed C. ferox outside both Echinostomatidae and Fasciolidae.

Phylogenetic tree constructed using 12 PCGs based on maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI), incorporating trematodes from various orders and families within the class Trematoda. The taxonomic identity (families and orders) is shown to the right, with the family-level identity additionally indicated by diferent colors. Values higher than 50 are displayed on the trees.

The time-calibrated phylogeny of selected trematodes presented in this study estimates that C. ferox diverged from other members of the Echinostomatidae family between the Late Cretaceous and early Eocene, approximately 75–39 million years ago (Fig. 6). This divergence positions C. ferox as the earliest branching lineage among the Echinostomatidae species included in the analysis. Notably, this estimated divergence time overlaps with the divergence between the Echinostomatidae and Fasciolidae families, which is estimated to have occurred between 84 and 49 million years ago. The close temporal proximity of these divergence events may have implications for the observed phylogenetic patterns.

Timetree of the trematodes from various families within the class Trematoda.

The nucleotide diversity (Pi) of the C. ferox was 0, suggesting that the C. ferox has been structured recently and that genetic variation among the C. ferox populations has not yet accumulated (Table S5).

Discussion

Chaunocephalus ferox is known to cause severe intestinal damage in storks. Infections typically result in granulomatous enteritis, intestinal nodules, mucosal perforations, and obstruction, all of which impair nutrient absorption (Gonzálvez et al., 2022). Our previous study found a 96.3 % infection rate in deceased Oriental Storks, suggesting a strong link between heavy infection and mortality. Similar pathological findings—such as necrosis, fibrosis, and cachexia—have been reported in black and white storks across Europe and Asia (Zhou et al., 2025; Gonzálvez et al., 2022). Although not all infected birds show visible symptoms, high parasite burdens are consistently associated with compromised health, indicating that C. ferox poses a significant pathogenic threat to avian hosts.

Currently, no complete mitogenome are available for the Chaunocephalus genus. This study presents the first complete mitogenome of C. ferox, a trematode parasite of storks, providing the novel genomic insight into this species. Beyond advancing the understanding of C. ferox, this work offers valuable insights into broader evolutionary patterns within the class Digenea.

Morphological characteristics clearly place C. ferox within the Echinostomatidae family. The species possesses a well-developed head collar bearing 27 collar spines—a defining feature of echinostomatid flukes—along with other diagnostic traits consistent with this family (Zhou et al., 2025). However, phylogenetic reconstruction based on the 12 mitochondrial PCGs did not consistently support this classification. While the overall topologies reconstructed by ML and BI methods were stable across major trematode lineages, C. ferox exhibited an unstable position across analyses. In the concatenated tree, it formed a weakly supported clade with members of Fasciolidae, while individual gene trees placed it variably—sometimes within Fasciolidae, and in other cases outside both Echinostomatidae and Fasciolidae entirely. These conflicting results suggest that mitochondrial data alone may be insufficient to accurately resolve the phylogenetic position of C. ferox, and further studies using nuclear genomic or transcriptomic markers will be essential to clarify its evolutionary placement.

The ambiguous phylogenetic placement of C. ferox may, at least in part, be explained by its early divergence timing. Molecular clock analysis estimates that C. ferox diverged from other Echinostomatidae species approximately 75–39 million years ago, a period that overlaps with the estimated divergence between the Echinostomatidae and Fasciolidae families (84–49 million years ago). This temporal proximity suggests that C. ferox may represent an early-branching lineage near the base of the echinostomatid-fasciolid radiation.

The absence of the atp8 gene and the presence of multiple intergenic overlaps in the mitogenome of C. ferox may reflect adaptive pressures toward mitochondrial genome minimization, a common trend in parasitic flatworms (Egger et al., 2017). Such genomic reduction may enhance replication efficiency and metabolic cost reduction, which are advantageous in the energy-limited environments typical of host intestinal tracts. These features may also reflect evolutionary constraints that favor rapid replication cycles and improved transmission efficiency in the face of host immune pressures (Egger et al., 2017).

Divergence time estimates suggest that C. ferox diverged from other echinostomatid species approximately 75 to 39 million years ago, spanning the Late Cretaceous to early Eocene. This early divergence indicates a long and independent evolutionary trajectory, potentially driven by host specialization or ecological adaptation. Notably, this period also coincides with a major phase of avian radiation, a time when many modern bird lineages such as the stork family (Ciconiidae), first emerged and began to diversify (Prum et al., 2015). Given the parasite's dependence on a two-host aquatic life cycle involving freshwater mollusks and either fish or amphibians, it is plausible that environmental shifts during the Cretaceous played a pivotal role in shaping its divergence and dispersal. Further paleogeographic and biogeographic investigations may help clarify how changes in aquatic ecosystems influenced the evolutionary pathways of this lineage.

A systematic review of published literature confirms that C. ferox has been reported exclusively in stork species (family Ciconiidae), with no documented infections in other avian hosts (Greben et al., 2016; Islam et al., 2009). This consistent host association maybe suggests a specialized host-parasite relationship between C. ferox and Ciconiidae. The notable mitogenomic feature potentially reflecting this evolutionary specialization is the presence of six intergenic overlaps, which may promote genomic compactness and functional efficiency. Additionally, the estimated divergence time of Chaunocephalus trematodes (75–39 million years ago) coincides with the origin of modern storks (around 40 million years ago; https://timetree.org/home), raising the possibility that their long-term host association may have arisen through co-evolutionary processes during the early Eocene. The strong host specificity of C. ferox to storks, combined with its ancient divergence time, supports the hypothesis of host-parasite co-divergence. Over evolutionary time, this close association may have led to a phenomenon akin to host “locking”, in which the parasite's life cycle becomes tightly linked to the ecological and migratory dynamics of a specific host lineage. Given the migratory nature and ecological sensitivity of stork species, this dependency may render C. ferox populations particularly susceptible to host population declines and habitat disturbances (Hoberg and Brooks, 2008). Although this study does not provide direct evidence confirming Ciconiidae as the specific hosts of Chaunocephalus or demonstrating co-evolutionary, the hypothesis remains plausible, as all documented infections to date have been restricted to stork hosts.

According to statistics, the estimated population size of the Oriental Stork was approximately 3000 individuals in 2012 (Zhang et al., 2017). Thanks to the implementation of artificial nesting and conservation programs, the current population has increased to an estimated 7000–9000 individuals (Wan et al., 2025). While this recovery represents a significant conservation success, recent field observations suggest that the prevalence of C. ferox infections in storks may also be increasing. Since 2020, more frequent cases of C. ferox have been detected in deceased Oriental Storks, including newly reported cases in Xiamen and Jiangxi in 2025 (Zhou et al., 2025). Although the data do not permit causal inference, the concurrent rise in host and parasite population the possibility of an emerging ecological interaction, potentially facilitated by increased host density or habitat overlap.

Furthermore, our analysis of cox1 sequences revealed identical haplotypes across all sampled regions and hosts, suggesting low mitochondrial variation in current C. ferox populations. This pattern may reflect a recent demographic expansion, a shared ancestral lineage, or founder effects during parasite dispersal. However, due to the limited number of samples and mitochondrial loci, no firm conclusions regarding population structure or gene flow can be drawn. Future studies using whole genomic or transcriptomic data and broader sampling will be essential to confirm whether C. ferox exhibits genetic homogeneity or harbors cryptic diversity across its range.

In addition to uncertainties in population genetics, the resolution of C. ferox's phylogenetic position remains constrained. While this study provides the first complete mitogenome for the genus Chaunocephalus and advances our understanding of its evolutionary trajectory, the taxonomic placement of C. ferox remains ambiguous in mitochondrial phylogenies. Broader genomic investigations and taxon-rich datasets will be crucial for elucidating its evolutionary history, host specificity, and ecological adaptations.

Conclusions

In summary, this study presents the first complete mitochondrial genome of C. ferox, offering a valuable genetic resource for future evolutionary and taxonomic studies. Morphological characteristics clearly support its classification within the Echinostomatidae family; however, phylogenetic analyses based on mitochondrial protein-coding genes revealed inconsistent placements, suggesting that mitochondrial data alone may be insufficient to resolve its evolutionary position. The mitogenome of C. ferox exhibits several distinctive features, including a relatively low A + T content (61.39 %), the absence of the atp8 gene, and multiple intergenic overlaps, which may reflect pressures toward mitochondrial genome compactness. These findings provide a foundation for further molecular investigations and highlight the importance of monitoring C. ferox as a potential threat to the conservation of its endangered host, the Oriental Stork.
