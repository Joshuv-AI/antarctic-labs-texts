Streptomyces tyrfis sp. nov., an actinobacterium isolated from Thai peat swamp forest soil and its promising secondary metabolites
Authors: Penkawee Kottip, Khanungkan Klanbut, Poowadon Fukasem, Pattama Pittayakhajonwut, Chakapong Intaraudom, Nantiya Bunbamrung, Chanwit Suriyachadkun, Montri Yasawong, Ya-Wen He, Somboon Tanasupawat, Sarin Tadtong, Chitti Thawai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An actinomycete strain, PTD5-9T, was isolated from peat swamp forest soil in Narathiwat Province, Thailand. Chemotaxonomic features and phylogenetic analysis based on the 16S rRNA gene sequence placed this strain within the genus Streptomyces. It exhibited the highest sequence similarity to Streptomyces gelaticus NRRL B-2928ᵀ (99.38%) and Streptomyces sanglieri NBRC 100784ᵀ (99.31%). However, the average nucleotide identity based on MUMmer (ANIm) values (88.48–88.56%), the average amino acid identity (84.37–84.97%), and digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) estimates (31.2–36.5%) were significantly below the accepted thresholds for species demarcation, supporting its classification as a new species within the genus. Accordingly, the name Streptomyces tyrfis sp. nov. is proposed. The crude extract derived from the culture broth of strain PTD5-9ᵀ exhibited potent antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria (MIC ≤ 250 µg/mL). Subsequent chemical analysis led to the identification of ten bioactive secondary metabolites: isocycloheximide (1), (2R,4S,6E)-anhydrocycloheximide (2), actiphenol (3), (−)-phenatic acid A (4), 8-O-methyltetrangomycin (5), 8-O-methylrabelomycin (6), 4-hydroxy-8-O-methylrabelomycin (7), tetrangulol methyl ether (also known as X-14881 E) (8), (−)-elmonin (9), and 2-phenylacetamide (10). Among these, compounds 3, 6, 7, and 8, which were isolated in sufficient quantities, exhibited notable inhibitory activity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis, with MIC values ranging from 3.13 to 25.0 µg/mL. Furthermore, compounds 6, 7, and 8 demonstrated pronounced cytotoxicity against human small cell lung cancer (NCI-H187) cells (IC₅₀: 1.10–7.80 µM), and against human breast cancer (MCF-7) cells (IC₅₀: 1.21–24.6 µM).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41121-1.

Introduction

Actinomycetes are a group of Gram-positive, filamentous bacteria that play a significant role in medical and pharmaceutical sciences due to their remarkable ability to produce bioactive secondary metabolites. These metabolites possess diverse biological properties, such as antimicrobial, anticancer, and antioxidant activities1. Additionally, certain secondary metabolites can be applied in agriculture, particularly in biological control. Because of these valuable properties, the ongoing investigation into bioactive secondary metabolites from actinomycetes remains a cornerstone of natural product research. However, the rate of discovery of new secondary metabolites from actinomycetes has been declining. Therefore, scientists must develop strategies to increase the likelihood of finding new compounds. One promising approach is to explore novel actinomycete species at both the genus and species levels, as these may possess metabolic pathways distinct from commonly studied actinomycetes, potentially harboring unique secondary metabolite gene clusters that lead to the biosynthesis of new compounds. Consequently, microbiologists have been seeking novel actinomycetes from natural environments. Currently, molecular identification based on 16S rRNA gene analysis is commonly used for preliminary classification of actinomycetes, using a cutoff value of 98.65% to assess their potential as new species2. However, in many cases, actinomycetes showing 16S rRNA gene similarity values above 98.65% have still been designated as new species. This is because whole-genome sequence analysis has become the standard criterion for determining the novelty of actinomycete species. Genome-based taxonomy provides key thresholds for defining new species, such as digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH, < 70%)3, average nucleotide identity (ANI, < 95%)4, and average amino acid identity (AAI, < 95–96%)5.

Peat swamp forests, characterized by waterlogged conditions for most of the year, are formed from the long-term accumulation and decomposition of plant debris. The soil and water in these ecosystems are acidic and typically have lower oxygen levels than those found in other forest types6,7. Actinomycetes capable of thriving in peat swamp forests may therefore possess unique characteristics, distinguishing them from those inhabiting other natural environments. In recent years, several new Streptomyces species have been isolated from peat swamp forests in Thailand, highlighting the untapped microbial diversity of these ecosystems. For example, Streptomyces rugosispiralis, a potent antibiotic producer, was discovered in soil from Nong Jun Rung peat swamp forest, Rayong province8. Another novel species, Streptomyces acidicola, was described in 2020 from the Kantulee peat swamp in Surat Thani9. Streptomyces actinomycinicus, although isolated from peat swamp soil, has yet to have its metabolites fully characterized10. More recently, three additional Streptomyces species, S. humicola, S. acididurans, and S. acidipaludis, were recovered from peat swamp environments in Thailand, though secondary metabolite profiling for these strains is still pending11–13. Beyond Streptomyces, other genera of actinomycetes from peat swamp environments have also been reported. Notably, Nonomuraea rhodomycinica was isolated from Sirindhorn peat swamp forest and shown to produce two novel anthracycline-like metabolites, nonomuric acid and a 3-hydroxy deoxydaunorubicinol aglycone, both of which displayed antimalarial activity with IC₅₀ values of 8.00 and 8.88 µg/mL, respectively. Additionally, ε-rhodomycinone, another compound from this strain, exhibited antibacterial activity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MIC ≈ 50 µg/mL) and Bacillus cereus (MIC ≈ 12.5 µg/mL)14. Likewise, Streptomyces rugosispiralis was found to produce four ansamycin derivatives, geldanamycin, 17-O-demethylgeldanamycin, reblastatin, and 17-demethoxyreblastatin, as well as the siderophores nocardamine and dehydroxynocardamine, all of which demonstrated significant antimicrobial and anticancer properties8. Taken together, these findings underscore the immense potential of peat swamp-derived actinomycetes, particularly Streptomyces, as a reservoir for novel bioactive natural products with pharmacological relevance. As part of our ongoing efforts to find secondary metabolite-producing actinomycetes from peat swamp forest ecosystems, a Streptomyces-related strain, designated PTD5-9ᵀ, was isolated from peat swamp soil. This study focuses on the taxonomic characterization of strain PTD5-9T. In addition, the secondary metabolites produced by this strain were investigated, along with the evaluation of the biological activities of the isolated compounds.

Results and discussion

Morphological, cultural, and chemotaxonomic characterization of Streptomyces sp. PTD5-9T

Peat swamp ecosystems represent a rich and promising source for the discovery of novel bioactive secondary metabolite producing actinomycetes. These habitats support a wide range of microbial life, including actinomycetes that are well-adapted to the acidic and often challenging environmental conditions6. Such microorganisms are renowned for their ability to produce structurally diverse and biologically active secondary metabolites with demonstrated antibacterial, antifungal, anticancer, and antiviral properties1,6. Due to the limited exploration of these ecosystems, peat swamps remain a largely untapped reservoir of unique natural products with significant pharmaceutical potential7. Consequently, actinomycetes isolated from peat swamp environments are expected to possess distinct biosynthetic and physiological traits compared to those from other habitats. Strain PTD5-9ᵀ was isolated from peat swamp forest soil collected in Sirindhorn Peat Swamp Forest, Sungai Kolok District, Narathiwat Province, Thailand (06°04′33.8″N, 101°57′49.3″E). The strain grew well on ISP 2, ISP 3, ISP 5, and ISP 7 media. Moderate growth was observed on ISP 6, and nutrient agar (NA), while poor growth occurred on Czapek’s sucrose and ISP 4. The substrate mycelium was greyish brown on ISP 2, and ISP 3. Whitish grey to grey aerial spore mass was clearly visible on ISP 2, ISP 3, ISP 5, ISP 6, ISP 7 and NA after 14 days of incubation at 30 °C. Pale greenish yellow to deep greenish yellow diffusible pigments were detected on ISP 2, ISP 5, ISP 6, ISP 7, and NA media (Table S1). Strain PTD5-9ᵀ produced spiral chains of oval and ovoid-shaped spores (0.3–0.5 × 0.7–0.9 μm) with smooth surfaces, borne directly on the aerial mycelium (Fig. 1). Whole-cell hydrolysates of strain PTD5-9ᵀ contained LL-diaminopimelic acid, and ribose, galactose, mannose, and glucose were detected as cell wall sugars. This chemotaxonomic profile is commonly observed in Streptomyces species15. The polar lipid profile included diphosphatidylglycerol (DPG), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), hydroxy-phosphatidylethanolamine (OH-PE), phosphatidylinositol (PI), phosphatidylinositol mannoside (PIM), and four unidentified phospholipids (PLs) (Fig. S1). The major menaquinones were MK-10(H4) (51.4%), MK-10(H2) (22.1%), MK-9(H₆) (15.3%), while MK-9(H₄) (5.1%), and MK-9(H₂) (1.1%) was a minor component. Cellular fatty acid analysis revealed that the major fatty acids (> 10%) were iso-C₁₆:₀ (29.9%), anteiso-C₁₅:₀ (22.9%). Minor components included iso-C₁₄:₀, iso-C₁₅:₀, C₁₆:₀, and iso-C₁₇:₀ (Table S2). The fatty acid profile was similar to those of Streptomyces species. The morphological and chemotaxonomic characteristics of strain PTD5-9ᵀ are consistent with those of the genus Streptomyces16.

16S rRNA gene analysis, genome-based taxonomy, and the differential phenotypic characteristics

16S rRNA gene sequence comparison is a foundational method for the identification and classification of novel actinomycete and bacterial species. A sequence similarity of ≤ 98.65% with any validly published species indicates the potential discovery of a previously unidentified taxon2. The 16S rRNA gene sequence of strain PTD5-9ᵀ (1,492 bp) showed that it belongs to the genus Streptomyces. It shared the highest sequence similarity with Streptomyces gelaticus NRRL B-2928ᵀ (99.38%) followed by Streptomyces sanglieri NBRC 100784ᵀ (99.31%), Streptomyces atratus NRRL B-16927ᵀ (99.24%), Streptomyces pulveraceus LMG 20322ᵀ (99.1%), and Streptomyces yanii NBRC 14669ᵀ (99.06%), which is above the species delineation threshold of 98.65%. Phylogenetic assessment using the neighbor-joining (NJ) and maximum-likelihood (ML) approaches revealed that strain PTD5-9ᵀ clustered into a separate branch alongside the aforementioned type strains, including S. gelaticus NRRL B-2928ᵀ and S. sanglieri NBRC 100784ᵀ (Figs. S2 and S3), indicating evolutionary divergence from these close relatives. Although 16S rRNA gene analysis offers preliminary information, genome-level parameters like digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH)3, average nucleotide identity (ANI)4, and average amino acid identity (AAI)5 provide higher taxonomic resolution and are now regarded as the definitive standards for species identification in actinomycetes and other bacterial taxa. Consequently, the genome of strain PTD5-9ᵀ was examined in comparison with its nearest Streptomyces type strains. The draft genome sequence of strain PTD5-9ᵀ, deposited in GenBank under accession number JBAGJZ000000000, comprises 8.3 Mbp across 149 contigs, with an N50 of 143.2 kbp and a G + C content of 72.5%. A total of 7,122 predicted genes were identified, including 6,807 protein-coding genes (Table S3). According to current taxonomic standards, species-level boundaries are defined by dDDH values below 70%3, ANI values below 95%4, and AAI values below 95–96%5. Strain PTD5-9ᵀ exhibited the highest ANIm (88.69%), dDDH (33.1%), and AAI (84.97%) values to S. atratus NRRL B-16927ᵀ (Table 1). Phylogenomic analysis using autoMLST revealed that strain PTD5-9ᵀ was clearly separated from the clade containing the closest type strains, S. gelaticus NRRL B-2928ᵀ, S. sanglieri NBRC 100784ᵀ, and S. atratus NRRL B-16927ᵀ with strong bootstrap support (100%) (Fig. 2), supporting its classification as a distinct species. Biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) in the genome of strain PTD5-9ᵀ and its closely related type strains were predicted using antiSMASH v7.0. Strain PTD5-9ᵀ harbors a diverse complement of BGCs, including those encoding NRP-metallophore, NRPS, butyrolactone, terpene, type I–III polyketide synthases (PKSs), lanthipeptide classes I–III, hydrogen cyanide, NI-siderophore, and heterocyst glycolipid synthase-like ketide synthase (hgIE-KS). Comparative analysis with S. atratus NRRL B-16927ᵀ, S. gelaticus NRRL B-2928ᵀ, and S. sanglieri NBRC 100784ᵀ showed that strain PTD5-9ᵀ harbours BGCs closely matching those implicated in the biosynthesis of several prominent bioactive metabolites: griseobactin, a catechol-peptide siderophore, is known for a specialized iron-chelating agent17. Hopene is a triterpenoid hydrocarbon. This compound is not primarily a bioactive secondary metabolite but plays a crucial physiological role in bacteria by stabilizing the cell membrane18. Ectoine, a compatible solute, is a compound that usually produced by many bacteria especially actinomycetes. This compound is known for its protective, stabilizing, and stress-mitigating biological activities19. Tripartilactam (Niizalactam C) was originally reported as a cyclobutane-bearing tricyclic lactam from a dung-beetle–associated Streptomyces. Its only demonstrated in vitro bioactivity is moderate inhibition of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (IC₅₀ ~ 16.6 µg/mL); no antibacterial, antifungal, or cytotoxic effects were observed under the tested conditions20. Thioangucycline belongs to the angucycline family of polyketides, a class known for potent antimicrobial and cytotoxic activities in many representatives. However, to date there is no robust, publicly available bioactivity data for thioangucycline itself, likely due to its unusual C-ring–cleaved/sulfur-bridged structure. Its biological potential remains largely hypothetical and awaits dedicated bioassays21,22. Naringenin, typically known as a plant secondary metabolite, is additionally produced by Streptomyces clavuligerus. Found abundantly in citrus species such as grapefruit and orange, this compound exhibits diverse biological functions, notably antioxidant, anticancer, antimicrobial, and neuroprotective activities23. High-confidence BGCs predicted to encode griseobactin, Tripartilactam/Niizalactam C and naringenin were uniquely identified in strain PTD5-9ᵀ, with no counterparts detected in the genomes of its closest relatives (Table S4).

In addition to genomic data, comparative phenotypic characterization further distinguished strain PTD5-9ᵀ from its closest type strains. Significant distinctions were observed in traits such as tolerance to a maximum growth temperature of 42 °C and the ability to metabolize dextran, inulin, D-mannitol, D-raffinose, D-xylose, as well as the degradation of L-tyrosine. In contrast to Streptomyces atratus NBRC 3897ᵀ, strain PTD5-9ᵀ lacked nitrate-reducing capability and did not utilize DL–2–Aminobutyric acid, though it could metabolize L-serine. When compared with Streptomyces gelaticus NBRC 12866ᵀ, strain PTD5-9ᵀ demonstrated growth in 7% (w/v) NaCl and at 42 °C, and was capable of utilizing D-cellobiose, dextran, inulin, L-cysteine, and L-tyrosine, traits absent in S. gelaticus NBRC 12866ᵀ (Table 2). Collectively, these genotypic and phenotypic characteristics strongly indicate that strain PTD5-9ᵀ constitutes a novel species within the genus Streptomyces, for which the name Streptomyces tyrfis sp. nov. is proposed. The designated type strain is PTD5-9ᵀ (= TBRC 11255ᵀ = NBRC 114616ᵀ).

Secondary metabolites and biological activities of isolated compounds from Streptomyces sp. PTD5-9T

The crude extract of the strain PTD5-9T was purified by HPLC to obtain ten compounds, which were identified based on NMR spectroscopic information and comparison with the previously described compounds. By comparison of 1H and 13C NMR spectral data and their physico-chemical properties, ten compounds could be identified as isocycloheximide (1)24,25, (2R, 4S, 6E)-anhydrocycloheximide (2)26, actiphenol (3)27,28, phenatic acid A (4)29, 8-O-methyltetrangomycin (5)30,31, 8-O-methylrabelomycin (6)30, 4-hydroxy-8-O-methylrabelomycin (7)32, tetrangulol methyl ether (8)30,33, (−)-elmonin (9)34, and 2-phenylacetamide (10)35(Fig. 3). 1H and 13C NMR spectral data together with their physico-chemical data of all isolated compounds were given as supporting data in Supplementary materials (Figs. S4–S67). Also, the complete NMR data of isocycloheximide (1) was provided in Table S5. All, except for compound 4, were identical to those data reported in the literature, both NMR spectral data and physico-chemical data. Although1 H and 13C NMR spectral data of compound 4 were identical to those of phenatic acid A, its optical rotation ([α]D −17.5, MeOH) was opposite to that reported in the literature ([α]D +2.5, MeOH). Thus, compound 4 is given the name as (−)-phenatic acid A. Moreover, the methyl ester derivative of compound 4 (methyl phenatate A), earlier isolated from the culture of Streptomyces sp. H7372, had negative optical rotation ([α]D −18.8, MeOH)36. Compounds 1–4 are glutarimide-containing compounds, while compounds 5–9 are grouped in angucyclinone family. These isolated compounds have predominantly been isolated from various strains of the genus Streptomyces and had potential as antibiotics.

Compounds isolated with sufficient materials (3, 6–8) were evaluated for antimalarial activity against Plasmodium falciparum, K1 multidrug resistant strain and antimicrobial activity against various microorganisms such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Candida albicans, Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus cereus, Enterococcus faecium, and Escherichia coli. Also, cytotoxicity against cancerous (MCF-7 and NCI-H187) and non-cancerous (Vero) cells were evaluated. Compounds 6–8 showed antimalarial activity with IC50 in a range of 6.9–15.0 µM, while all tested compounds exhibited anti-M. tuberculosis with MIC in a range of 3.13–25.0 µg/mL (Table 3). Only 4-Hydroxy-8-O-methylrabelomycin (7) had anti-S. aureus activity with MIC value of 25.0 µg/mL, suggesting the effect of hydroxy group at position 4. Whilst compounds 7 and 8 showed anti-B. cereus activity with MIC values of 50.0 and 25.0 µg/mL, respectively. All tested compounds were inactive for antifungal activity against C. albicans and for antibacterial activity against Gram positive (E. faecium) and Gram negative (E. coli) at maximum tested concentration (50 µg/mL). Compounds 6–8 exhibited strong cytotoxicity against NCI-H187 cells with IC50 in a range of 1.10–7.80 µM, while compounds 6 and 8 showed strong cytotoxicity against MCF-7 and Vero cells with IC50 in a range of 1.21–1.47 and 2.02–2.64 µM, respectively (Table 3, Fig. S68-S73). Taken together, these results underscore the potential of Streptomyces species, such as Streptomyces sp. PTD5-9ᵀ, as valuable microbial sources for uncovering new bioactive metabolites with promising biotechnological and pharmaceutical relevance.

Description of Streptomyces tyrfis sp. nov.

Streptomyces tyrfis (ty.r’fis G. gen. n. tyrfi of peat, referring to the tropical peat swamp soil, the geographical origin of the type strain).

Cells are Gram-stain positive, aerobic, filamentous actinobacteria, which form extensive branch substrate mycelia. Good growth is observed on ISP 2, ISP 3, ISP 5, and ISP 7. Grows moderately on ISP 6, and nutrient agar. The poorly growth is observed on ISP 4, and Czapek’s sucrose. Whitish grey to grey aerial spore mass is observed on ISP 2, ISP 3, ISP 5, ISP 6, ISP 7 and nutrient agar after 14 days of cultivation. The strain produce spiral chain of ovoid-shaped spores with smooth surfaces. The pale greenish yellow to deep greenish yellow diffusible pigments are observed on ISP 2, ISP 5, ISP 6, ISP 7, and nutrient agar. The positive results are detected on liquefaction of gelatin, hydrolysis of starch, catalase, and milk peptonization. The reduction of nitrate, the production of oxidase, and hydrogen sulfide production are negative. The strain could decompose adenine, and L-tyrosine but not hypoxanthine, cellulose, and xanthine. It utilizes L-arginine, L-asparagine, L-cysteine, L-histidine, 4-hydroxyproline, D-cellobiose, dextran, D-galactose, D-glucose, glycerol, inulin, D-lactose, D-melibiose, D-mannose, salicin, L-phenylalanine, L-proline, L-serine, L-threonine, and L-valine; weakly utilizes D-fructose, L-methionine, L-rhamnose, D-trehalose, and xylitol, but does not utilize DL-2-aminobutyric acid, L-arabinose, myo-inositol, D-mannitol, D-raffinose, sucrose, and D-xylose. The temperature range for growth is 12–42 °C. The strain could grow in culture media containing up to 7% (w/v) NaCl. The pH range for growth is 4.5–11. The type strain, PTD5-9T (= TBRC 11255T = NBRC 114616ᵀ), is an actinomycete isolated from peat swamp forest soil collected from Narathiwat province, Thailand.

Conclusion

This study describes the discovery of a new species within the genus Streptomyces, along with its bioactive secondary metabolites. The strain, Streptomyces sp. PTD5-9ᵀ, was taxonomically classified using a polyphasic approach incorporating genome-based analyses, resulting in the proposed name Streptomyces tyrfis sp. nov. It is particularly noteworthy that Streptomyces tyrfis is capable of producing secondary metabolites with diverse chemical structures, especially those belonging to the glutarimide-containing compounds and the angucyclinone family. In addition, the isolated secondary metabolites exhibited strong antimicrobial activity, notably showing inhibitory effects against the growth of Mycobacterium tuberculosis. These results position Streptomyces tyrfis sp. nov. as a valuable source of bioactive compounds with potential applications in biotechnology and medicine.

Experimental procedures

Isolation, cultivation, and preservation of strain PTD5-9T

Strain PTD5-9T was isolated from peat swamp forest soil collected in Sirindhorn Peat Swamp Forest, Sungai Kolok District, Narathiwat Province, Thailand (06°04′33.8″N, 101°57′49.3″E). The sample was air-dried at 30 °C for three days, then heat-treated at 100 °C for 45 min. Subsequently, it was serially diluted up to 10⁻³ using 0.01% sterile SDS in water and spread onto modified starch-casein nitrate agar (composition: 10 g soluble starch, 1 g sodium caseinate, 0.5 g KH₂PO₄, 0.5 g MgSO₄, and 18 g agar per liter of distilled water, pH 5.5), supplemented with antibiotics according to Thawai et al.37. After 21 days of incubation at 30 °C, a greyish brown colony corresponding to strain PTD5-9T was picked and purified on yeast extract–malt extract agar, pH 5.5. The purified strain was preserved in 20% (v/v) glycerol at − 80 °C and lyophilized preservation.

Morphological, cultural, physiological, and biochemical characteristics

To investigate the morphology of the strain, 21-day-old cultures of strain PTD5-9ᵀ were observed using scanning electron microscopy (JSM-6610 LV; JEOL). Colony characteristics were evaluated by growing the strain on International Streptomyces Project (ISP) media 2–738 for 14 days at 30 °C. The colors of both aerial and substrate mycelia, as well as any diffusible pigments, were assigned using the ISCC-NBS color charts39. Growth conditions were tested in ISP 2 broth over a 14-day period, assessing the strain’s tolerance to temperature (10–50 °C), salinity (0–10% NaCl w/v), and pH levels (ranging from 4 to 12, in 1 unit intervals). Phenotypic traits were evaluated following standard methodologies, including assessments of nitrogen source utilization and the decomposition of insoluble substances such as adenine, hypoxanthine, xanthine, tyrosine, and cellulose. Additional tests included starch hydrolysis, nitrate reduction, milk peptonization, and gelatin liquefaction, as outlined by Arai40, Williams and Cross41, and Gordon et al.42. Carbon source utilization (1% w/v) was determined using the method suggested by Supong et al.43. For comparison, reference strains, Streptomyces gelaticus NBRC 12866ᵀ, Streptomyces atratus NBRC 3897ᵀ, and Streptomyces sanglieri NBRC 100784ᵀ were cultured under the same experimental conditions.

Chemotaxonomic analyses

Chemotaxonomic analysis was conducted using freeze-dried cells collected from cultures incubated in ISP 2 broth on a rotary shaker set at 200 r.p.m. and 30 °C for a duration of 7 days. The isomer of diaminopimelic acid and the presence of reducing sugars were done using the methods described by Hasegawa et al., Komagata and Suzuki, respectively44,45. The menaquinone composition were determined based on standardized protocols suggested by Minnikin et al., and Collins et al., respectively46,47 and were analysed by HPLC. A mixture of methanol and 2-propanol (2 : 1, v/v) was used as the elution solvent. For polar lipid profiling of strain PTD5-9ᵀ, lipids were extracted from dried biomass and examined following the protocol outlined by Minnikin et al.48 and were analysed using two-dimensional TLC technique. The fatty acid profile of the cellular components was analyzed using gas chromatography (Agilent 6890 model), adhering to the procedures recommended by the Microbial Identification System (MIDI)49,50.

Genome-based taxonomy

Genomic DNA for PCR amplification and whole-genome sequencing was prepared according to the protocol of Tamaoka51. The 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers 9F and 1541R52 under conditions specified by Kittiwongwattana et al.53. Sequence similarity of the 16S rRNA gene from strain PTD5-9ᵀ was analyzed using the EzBioCloud platform54. For phylogenetic reconstruction, the 16S rRNA gene sequence of PTD5-9ᵀ and those of related Streptomyces type strains obtained from EzBioCloud were used to construct phylogenetic trees in MEGA X55, employing the neighbor-joining56, and maximum-likelihood57 algorithms. Bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replications58 was used to assess the robustness of the branching patterns. Whole-genome sequencing was performed using the Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 200 bp paired-end reads) at Chulalongkorn University, Thailand. Genome annotation was conducted using the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline (PGAP). Genomic relatedness indices, including average nucleotide identity (ANI), digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH), and average amino acid identity (AAI) were computed using JSpecies59, the Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC 2.1; BLAST+ algorithm)60, and the EzAAI (Average amino acid sequence identity tool)61, respectively. A high-resolution phylogenomic tree was generated using the autoMLST pipeline (https://automlst.ziemertlab.com)62 and reconstructed by the maximum-likelihood approach in MEGA X, with Micromonospora eburnea DSM 44814ᵀ (GCF_900090225) used as the outgroup.

Characterization and chemical properties of secondary metabolites

UV spectra were performed in MeOH using a V-730 UV-Visible spectrophotometer from JASCO. FT-IR spectra were obtained from ALPHA FT-IR spectrometer from Bruker in ATR mode. The optical rotations were done on a JASCO P-2000 digital polarimeter. NMR spectral information was given from either Bruker Avance III™ HD 400 (400 MHz for 1H and 100 MHz for 13C) or Bruker Avance III™ HD 500 (500 MHz for 1H and 125 MHz for 13C) NMR spectrometers. HRESIMS data were determined on a Bruker MicrOTOF mass spectrometer either negative or positive modes. Separation by HPLC was done on a Dionex–Ultimate 3000 series equipped with a binary pump, an autosampler, and a diode array detector. Semi-preparative HPLC were performed on a reverse phase column (SunFire C18 OBD, particle size 10 µm, diam. 19 mm ⋅ 250 mm) at a flow rate of 8 mL/min.

Large scale fermentation, extraction, and isolation of secondary metabolites

The strain PTD5-9T was grown on ISP 2 agar for 7 days and a loopful of aerial spore mass was added into 500 mL Erlenmeyer flasks, which each contained 200 mL of ISP 2 broth (100 flasks). The strain was cultivated at 30 °C for 14 days on shakers at 180 rpm. The whole culture was extracted three times with an equal volume of EtOAc. The EtOAc layers were combined, dried over Na2SO4, and then evaporated to dryness to yield brown gum (4.1 g).

The brown gum was precipitated with MeOH to give a pale yellow solid (1.4 g) of compound 3. The filtrate was passed through a Sephadex LH20 column (4.5 cm ⋅ 40 cm) to give five main fractions (F1– F5). Fraction F1 (0.45 g) was purified by semi-preparative HPLC using a linear gradient system of 6% − 40% aqueous CH3CN over 25 min to obtain three compounds, which were compounds 9 (tR 19.2 min, 4.2 mg), 1 (tR 22.0 min, 28.3 mg), and 2 (tR 22.9 min, 4.4 mg). Fraction F2 (0.21 g) was further purified by semi-preparative HPLC eluted with a linear gradient system of 7% − 45% aqueous CH3CN over 25 min to afford compounds 10 (tR 14.5 min, 24.5 mg), 5 (tR 20.4 min, 34.8 mg), and 4 (tR 22.8 min, 20.1 mg). Fraction F3 (0.29 g) was subjected to semi-preparative HPLC using the same condition as that of fraction F2 to yield compounds 5 (tR 20.7 min, 16.3 mg), 6 (tR 22.9 min, 19.4 mg), 3 (tR 27.5 min, 7.5 mg), and 8 (tR 30.4 min, 1.8 mg). Fraction F4 (91.0 mg) was purified by semi-preparative HPLC using the same condition as that of fraction F1 to furnish compounds 7 (tR 18.5 min, 3.8 mg), 6 (tR 22.8 min, 17.0 mg), and 8 (tR 27.5 min, 7.4 mg). Finally, compound 8 (15.0 mg) was solely given from Fraction F5.

Biological assays

Microculture radioisotope technique was used for antimalarial activity test against P. falciparum (K1, multidrug-resistant strain)63. The parasite strain was first isolated from an infected individual in Kanchanaburi province, Thailand64 and provided by the Malaria Research and Reference Reagent Resource Center (MR4, Manassas, VA, USA). Dihydroartemisinin and chloroquine were used as positive controls for assay against P. falciparum and showed IC50 values of 0.0029 and 0.287 µM, respectively. Anti-bacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria [Bacillus cereus ATCC 11778, Enterococcus faecium ATCC 51559, and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213] and Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli ATCC 25922) was determined by optical density microplate assay, which was described by the standard broth microdilution assay described by the Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute (2006)65,66. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was reported for the lowest concentration of testing compound that inhibited tested cells more than 90%. Maximum concentration of the sample was used at 50 µg/mL, while 0.5% DMSO was used as negative control. Rifampicin and vancomycin were used as positive controls for activity against Gram-positive, while amikacin and ofloxacin were used as positive controls for Gram-negative bacteria. Anti-phytopathogenic fungal activity against Candida albicans was determined by Resazurin microplate assay (REMA)67. IC50 was reported corresponding to dose-response graph plotting between compound concentrations and % fungal inhibition using curve-fitting method using SOFTMax Pro software (Molecular Devices, USA).Maximum tested concentration was done at 50 mg/mL. Amphotericin B was used as positive control for anti-C. albicans. Antitubercular activity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis strain H37Ra and cytotoxicity against Vero cell (African green monkey kidney fibroblasts, ATCC CCL-81) were performed by using green fluorescent protein microplate assay (GFPMA)68–70. Maximum tested concentration was done at 50 mg/mL. Isoniazid, ofloxacin, rifampicin, streptomycin, and ethambutol were used as positive controls for anti-Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Ellipticine was used as positive control for cytotoxicity against Vero cells. Cytotoxicity against MCF-7 (human breast cancer, ATCC HTB-22) and NCI-H187 (human small cell lung cancer, ATCC CRL-5804) cell lines were performed by using the resazurin microplate assay (REMA) described by O’Brien et al.67. Ellipticine and doxorubicin were used as the positive controls for anti-NCI-H187 cells, while tamoxifen and doxorubicin were used as the positive controls for anti-MCF-7 cells.
