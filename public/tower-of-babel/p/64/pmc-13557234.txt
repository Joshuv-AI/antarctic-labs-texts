Focal Dermal Hypoplasia in Males: A Systematic Review
Authors: Vincent Gao, Christopher Castelow, Kara Braudis, Benjamin Warren Casterline, Jonathan Dyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFocal dermal hypoplasia (FDH) is an X-linked dominant disorder characterized by dermal atrophy, fat herniations, telangiectasias, and pigmentary changes along Blaschko’s lines, with multisystem involvement affecting the teeth, nails, eyes, and the skeletal, cardiovascular, and other systems. Initially thought embryonically lethal in males, later reports identified somatic mosaicism as a survival mechanism. The male phenotype remains incompletely defined.ObjectivesTo systematically review reported male FDH cases to consolidate phenotypic patterns, identify key features, and explore genotype–phenotype correlations.MethodsA systematic review of PubMed was conducted with no restrictions on publication date. This review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. Inclusion criteria were case reports and systematic reviews solely investigating FDH or Goltz syndrome.ResultsForty-five patient cases were identified across 38 case reports and studies. Dermal and skeletal involvement primarily involved extremities. Ophthalmological and dental anomalies, including microphthalmia, colobomas, and enamel defects, were variably present. PORCN variants were confirmed in all but one genetically tested patient, though phenotypes varied. The retrospective nature likely biased toward more severe or unusual presentations.ConclusionsFDH in males exhibits marked clinical variability. The observed heterogeneity in histopathological findings reinforces the need for flexible diagnostic criteria, with genetic testing for PORCN variants recommended when clinical suspicion remains high. A multidisciplinary approach is recommended, with surveillance of ocular, dental, and skeletal manifestations even in patients with minimal cutaneous findings. Further research into PORCN function and Wnt signaling is needed to refine genotype–phenotype relationships and explore alternative disease mechanisms.Keywords: Focal dermal hypoplasia, Goltz, PORCN, Wnt

Introduction

Focal dermal hypoplasia (FDH), or Goltz syndrome, has been recognized since 1962. Robert Goltz first described three patients with linear or reticular patterns of atrophic dermis, focal fat herniations, telangiectasias, hyperpigmentation, hypopigmentation, and ulcerations, features that led to the name of “focal dermal hypoplasia” [1]. The histopathological findings typically show dermal attenuation with replacement by adipocytes, mixed inflammation, and increased papillary dermal capillaries [2,3]. Extracutaneous manifestations have also been described, including cleft palate, syndactyly, polydactyly, nail and tooth deformities, ocular anomalies, and sparse hair. In 1992, Goltz further consolidated findings to include cases in which there was a total absence of skin and apocrine gland abnormalities [4]. Papillomas have also been found in a variety of body locations, notably over the perineal, vulvar, perianal, and laryngeal regions. Asymmetries of the face, trunk, and limbs, with osteopathia striata, are a common finding. Ectrodactyly and syndactyly, or even a combination of both (“lobster-claw”), has been a striking finding of FDH, affecting both hands and feet. Many ocular findings also affect patients, ranging in severity from strabismus or nystagmus to abnormalities such as colobomas of the iris, choroid, and retina, microphthalmia, and anophthalmia. Over time, the spectrum of FDH has now been widened to include involvement of ectodermal and mesodermal tissues in multiple organ systems [4,5].

It was hypothesized by Goltz that FDH was embryonically lethal in males with an XY karyotype due to the absence of reported male cases at the time, along with high miscarriage rates in the families of patients with FDH. This conjecture suggested that males with FDH could only survive through mechanisms such as somatic mosaicism [4]. Over the years, numerous case studies have reported male cases, including instances of father-daughter transmissions of FDH. It is widely accepted that FDH predominantly affects females in an X-linked dominant pattern, accounting for approximately 90% of cases, with male survival attributed to somatic mosaicism [6–10]. This culminated in two independent studies by Grzeschik et al. and Wang et al., which both identified mutations of X-linked PORCN gene to be associated with FDH [11,12]. The identification of PORCN highlighted critical molecular underpinnings of FDH. Mutations in PORCN impair Wnt signaling pathways, which play a crucial role in cellular proliferation, differentiation, and tissue development, leading to the characteristic hypoplastic features of FDH.

Objectives

Currently, there is no consensus on a typical male phenotype. To address this gap, we aimed to contribute to the understanding of male FDH by conducting a systematic review of all reported male cases. This review will help consolidate the existing data, identify patterns, and establish a clearer phenotype for males with FDH.

Methods

This systematic review followed the PRISMA guidelines. A comprehensive PubMed search was performed in 2024 using the terms “(focal dermal hypoplasia OR Goltz syndrome) AND male NOT (Gorlin-Goltz)” along with the keywords “PORCN,” “mutation,” “male,” and “focal dermal hypoplasia.” No restrictions were placed on publication date. Inclusion criteria comprised case reports and systematic reviews investigating focal dermal hypoplasia (FDH) or Goltz syndrome in male patients. The initial search yielded 142 records.

After duplicate removal, titles and abstracts were screened by the primary author (VG), followed by full-text review. Exclusion criteria included non-human studies, non-English reports without translation, absence of male patients, or insufficient diagnostic confirmation (clinical, histopathological, or genetic). Studies published after the identification of the PORCN gene in 2007 were excluded if they lacked genetic testing and classical histopathological features of FDH.

Each case was appraised using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist [13,14]. Data extraction encompassed demographic, dermatological, histological, extracutaneous, and genetic variables (Table 1). Pertinent negatives were recorded when reported. Risk of bias was minimized through independent data collection and verification by two reviewers (VG and BC). The final analysis included 38 studies comprising 45 confirmed male FDH cases (Figure 1).

Results

A total of 38 full-text studies were included, identifying 45 patient cases that met our inclusion criteria based on clinical and histopathological diagnosis or clinical and genetic confirmation of a PORCN mutation (Figure 1). All included studies fulfilled the JBI Risk of Bias assessment, demonstrating good quality.

One study was a systemic review study that met all inclusion criteria but was excluded due to its focus exclusively on ophthalmological findings, with no discussion of PORCN mutations and no description of cutaneous or extracutaneous findings [15]. This study failed the JBI risk of bias assessment, as all clinical conditions were not clearly described.

For dermatological distribution and extracutaneous features, we tallied all reported specific findings across these cases. Because a single case may present with multiple findings across different anatomical or clinical categories, the total number of features exceeds the number of cases. Denominators vary by category depending on how many cases reported data for that specific finding. Throughout this analysis, “cases” refers to unique individuals, while the “features” data reflect the total number of documented findings across those individuals.

Cutaneous and Extracutaneous Features

Extremities and trunk were most frequently affected by dermal hypoplasia (33/83 and 28/83, respectively), followed by head/neck (16/83), with minimal mucosal involvement (1/83) (Figure 2).

Histopathological Findings

Of the 45 total cases, 19 had histopathological descriptions. The most consistent histological finding was dermal hypoplasia and ectopic dermal adipose tissue, which were the prominent findings in 17/19 cases (89%). Five cases reported additional findings: fibrillary collagen and basilar hyperpigmentation [16]; focal homogeneous collagen fiber clusters and atrophy of the pilosebaceous unit [17]; thin collagen fibers [9]; irregular acanthosis, papillary edema and lymphocytic infiltrate [18]; and fragmented elastic fibers in the upper dermis [19]. Of these five, two did not report dermal hypoplasia or ectopic dermal adipose [17,19].

Genetic Findings

Twenty mutations were identified (Figure 5 and Table 2). The majority were loss-of-function mutations, including nonsense and frameshift variants leading to premature termination codons. Three recurrent pathogenic variants—c.956dupA (p.Asn320Glufs*99), c.749C>T (p. Ser250Phe), and c.470G>A (p.Gly157Asp)—were reported in multiple patients. Most truncating mutations were located upstream of the conserved residues N306 and H341, regions essential to enzymatic function, consistent with prior pathogenic models.

Discussion

This systematic review describes the clinical, genetic, and histopathological features of male focal dermal hypoplasia (FDH). Our findings highlight several key trends: a predominance of extremity involvement in both dermal and skeletal manifestations, potential variability in histological interpretation, and several noted atypical presentations, including cases lacking dermal features or exhibiting paternal transmission. We also explored potential mechanisms by which pathogenic PORCN variants may contribute to the phenotypic expression of FDH.

FDH in males most frequently involves the extremities, followed by the trunk and head and neck. Skeletal abnormalities were most common in the digits, with fewer craniofacial and long-bone defects, and only rare cases lacking skeletal changes. The overlap between dermal and skeletal involvement supports the hypothesis that FDH arises from primary ectodermal defects with secondary mesodermal disruption [20,21]. Widespread truncal or head and neck involvement may more frequently affect the development of essential organs and prevent viability. Primary extremity involvement could also be influenced by neonatal body surface area distribution, with the extremities comprising ~47%, followed by the trunk (~26%), then head and neck (~20%) [22].

FDH is an X-linked dominant condition associated with mutations in PORCN [12,23,24]. PORCN encodes a membrane-bound O-acyl transferase (MBOAT), one of a family of enzymes that catalyzes the transfer of acyl groups from acyl-coenzyme A to diverse substrates at the endoplasmic reticulum membrane, including proteins, lipids and steroids [25]. PORCN palmitoylates Wnt family proteins at specific serine residues. This modification is essential to Wnt secretion and downstream signaling activity [11, 12, 26]. All MBOATs share conserved residues, which correspond to N306 and H341 in PORCN [26,27]. These conserved residues are believed essential to substrate binding and positioning of the target serine residue in proximity to the catalytic PORCN-His336 residue. This modification of Wnt is necessary for stimulating fibroblast proliferation, osteogenesis, and inhibiting adipogenesis, ultimately contributing to the development of ectomesodermal tissues [6]. It also plays significant roles in early limb patterning, tooth formation, myogenesis, and neural development [1,4,28,29].

Of the current PORCN variants noted in this review, all four nonsense mutations lie upstream of N306 and H341, and the two frameshift mutations also remove these residues (Figure 5). Four out of the six missense mutations occur upstream of the conserved residues. The remaining downstream variants may impair PORCN function by disrupting protein stability, folding, or interaction domains and represent important targets for future studies examining effects on enzymatic activity, cellular localization, and Wnt binding. Several cases reported isolated skeletal, dental, or ophthalmological findings without corresponding focal dermal hypoplasia, including variants c.283C>T (p.Arg95)*, c.749C>T (p.Ser-250Phe), and c.470G>A (p.Gly157Asp). This raises the possibility that PORCN variants may not uniformly disrupt all 19 mammalian Wnts or that specific Wnts may selectively influence different aspects of dermal and skeletal embryogenesis, resulting in atypical phenotypes. Further research is required to elucidate these mechanisms.

In our review of the histopathology, we observed variability and reporting thresholds in pathologist interpretations. This may stem from differences in the pathological expressivity and disease manifestations in certain individuals, superimposed dermatosis affecting patients with FDH, or perhaps disease mimickers with Goltz-like phenotype but different histopathological manifestations. Three out of five of these outliers had findings of thin collagen fibers (N=2) or focal homogeneous collagen fiber clusters (N=1), suggesting a possible difference in expressivity or in severity of disease rather than a marked difference in histopathological findings or potential variable amounts of mosaic cells in those areas. One of the five outlier cases displaying fragmentation of elastic fibers in the upper dermis had a confirmed pathological PORCN missense mutation in c.502G.A (p.Gly168Arg), strengthening evidence that this is a true but uncommon finding in FDH patients. Genetic data were available in four other patients with histopathological descriptions. Each of these cases was PORCN-positive and had dermal hypoplasia and ectopic dermal adipose tissue as the prominent histological findings, consistent with PORCN’s expected role in Wnt regulation.

FDH skin lesions often follow the lines of Blaschko, classically described as S-shaped on the trunk, V-shaped on the back, and linear on the extremities. Blaschko lines are thought to follow the dorsoventral development of embryonic neural crest cells [6,30]. An example of a male patient with FDH is shown in Figure 6. The prevailing hypothesis is that clonal descendants of embryonic ectodermal progenitor cells exert non-cell-autonomous effects on the underlying mesodermal cells, contributing to the diverse formation of Blaschko-linear lesions [20,21].

Several explanations for the survival of males with FDH have been proposed, including XXY karyotype, a gametic half chromatid mutation, or a de novo somatic mutation [6,31]. Patients with a 47XXY constitution can survive with focal dermal hypoplasia due to the presence of an extra X chromosome. A gametic half-chromatid mutation implies that a mutation is present in all cells derived from the affected gamete, resulting in a more uniform and extensive expression of the FDH phenotype throughout the body. The most widely accepted explanation for male cases of FDH is a post-zygotic de novo mutation that creates two genetically distinct cell populations, resulting in distribution of mosaics in affected tissues [10, 32–35]. A de novo mutation can lead to somatic or gonadal mosaicism [36].

Four case studies reported father-to-daughter transmission of FDH [16–19]. The fathers were diagnosed later in life (ages 45, 53, 54, and unreported), contributing to a right-skewed age distribution (Figure 4). While PORCN mutations in these studies were not confirmed, all cases exhibited skin findings with characteristic histopathological features. Burgdorf et al. [17] reported a father with telangiectasias and atrophic macules on the legs and herniations on the axillae and inguinal regions whose condition was so mild he escaped diagnosis until age 40. His daughter had severe FDH involving most of her body in varying degrees. Gorski [18] reported a 54-year-old male with unilateral skin lesions whose daughter had significant skeletal and dermatological defects at birth, affecting the torso and extremities bilaterally. Mahé, Couturier [19] described a 53-year-old male with mild skin lesions affecting the left thigh, only diagnosed because his daughter, age 23, presented with widespread Blaschko linear atrophic and hyperpigmented skin lesions, telangiectasias and fatty nodules on all extremities, papillomas, nail deformities, and ectrodactyly and syndactyly of hands and feet. Gupta, Saginatham [16] documented a severe paternal phenotype, with the father exhibiting Blaschkoid hypo- and hyperpigmented macules bilaterally over the trunk and extremities, ectrodactyly of the left foot, malformed ears, and kyphoscoliosis. The daughter had a severe, bilaterally distributed phenotype, with ectrodactyly of the right foot, missing digits on hands and feet, facial asymmetry, low set ears, with Blaschko linear distribution of atrophic skin with fat herniations. In general, fathers had significantly milder phenotypes compared to the daughters. These cases support the feasibility of a germline-involved PORCN mutation [6].

The risk of bias in this systematic review was mitigated through independent data collection and verification by two investigators (VG and BC), with standardized methodology for categorizing findings. Limitations include potential publication bias due to the rarity of male FDH cases and inconsistent reporting of clinical features across studies, particularly in pre-2007 publications lacking genetic confirmation. Despite the good quality of case reports appraised by the JBI risk of assessment tool, the retrospective design introduces selection bias in favor of more severe or clinically interesting cases, which are more likely to be published. This may skew the spectrum of male FDH phenotypes.

Conclusion

This systematic review consolidates reported male FDH cases and underscores the considerable genotypic and phenotypic variability observed in this condition. The predominance of extremity involvement aligns with neonatal body surface area distribution and supports the hypothesis that primary ectodermal dysfunction produces downstream mesodermal effects. Awareness of extremity-predominant patterns and atypical presentations may aid earlier and more accurate diagnosis. Future research should investigate tissue-specific consequences of PORCN mutations and their differential effects on Wnt signaling, with particular attention to cases lacking cutaneous findings or displaying discordant phenotypes to refine genotype–phenotype correlations and elucidate alternative pathogenic pathways.
