Generating tolerance through in situ recruitment of regulatory T cells for allogeneic cell transplantation in a bioengineered lymphoid platform
Authors: Nikitha Kota, Danilo Settis, Martina Concato, Noemi Risso, Robin Vander Pol, Casey Lewis, Yongbin Liu, Yafet Arefeayne, Federica Banche-Niclot, Laura Segatori, Francesca Taraballi, Junhua Mai, Alessandro Grattoni, Corrine Ying Xuan Chua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cellular therapies aim to treat or manage disease by introducing living cells that integrate into the host and restore or eliminate dysfunctional tissues. Despite their promise for clinical success, the host immune response to cellular treatments remains a challenge since traditional approaches for abrogating immune rejection involve systemic immunosuppression, which results in severe off-target toxicity. One alternate strategy for restraining immune responses involves harnessing the natural immunomodulatory capabilities of regulatory T cells (Tregs), a specialized subset of T cells that suppress inflammatory immune responses and can promote induction and maintenance of transplant tolerance. Here we propose using the NanoLymph platform, an implantable subcutaneous device for continuous localized recruitment of Tregs, to achieve immunological tolerance free of systemic immunosuppression. The NanoLymph features a dual-reservoir system for the sustained release of immunomodulatory agents through a nanoporous membrane and a vascularized compartment that supports cell homing and allograft integration with the host. This work demonstrates robust vascularization of the NanoLymph by four weeks post-implantation, along with sustained in vivo elution of immunomodulatory agents for up to one month that selectively recruit and expand Tregs. Finally, we demonstrate that NanoLymph prolongs cell persistence in a bioluminescent allogeneic transplant model. Overall, the NanoLymph represents a versatile platform to generate a safe and localized tolerogenic microenvironment relevant for cell transplant therapies.

Introduction

Cell-based therapy provides hope for patients suffering from diseases where current treatment approaches are either insufficient, suboptimal or cannot provide an ultimate cure [1,2]. Currently, hormonal replacement therapy is the standard treatment for endocrine disorders. However, it fails to replicate the body's intricate metabolic modulation of hormones, leading to undesired effects [3]. Cell transplantation offers a more physiological approach to the treatment of endocrine disorders, enabling dynamic regulation of hormone levels in response to the body's needs. For example, as an alternative to testosterone replacement therapy for the treatment of primary hypogonadism, cell-based therapy has emerged as a promising approach for restoring testosterone production within the body [4]. Cell transplantation has already advanced to clinical translation for the treatment of other endocrine disorders. Recently, the FDA approved intrahepatic islet transplantation for the treatment of type 1 diabetes [5], and new therapies using allogeneic stem cell-derived islets are now in Phase III clinical trials (i.e., VX-880) [6]. However, as with any allogeneic cell transplantation treatment, systemic immunosuppression is the standard of care for preventing tissue rejection and graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) [7,8]. Conditioning and post-operative immunosuppressive treatments are the principal causes of opportunistic infection, end-organ dysfunction, and subsequent development of endocrine syndromes [9].

A potential strategy for allogeneic cell therapy is to leverage the body's natural immunoregulatory systems, minimizing the reliance on long-term immunosuppressive treatment. To this end, regulatory T cells (Tregs) serve as a key mechanism for controlling T cell homeostasis and maintaining antigen-specific operational tolerance in vivo [10]. Tregs are the gatekeepers of tolerance; the absence of these cells can lead to a host of autoimmune and inflammatory diseases such as arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus [11], among others. In line with this, endogenous Treg therapies are emerging as a treatment strategy for chronic inflammation [12].

In addition to controlling immune responses to self-antigens, Tregs can also modulate immune responses to non-self-molecules. Over the past few decades, Tregs have shown promise in the induction of donor-specific tolerance in transplantation [13]. However, various issues limit the clinical efficacy of Treg therapies, primarily the difficulty of ex vivo Treg expansion and the loss of suppressive functionality following adoptive transfer [14]. A promising approach to overcoming these challenges involves strategies that manipulate Tregs directly within the host [15]. C-C motif chemokine ligand 22 (CCL22) has been shown to mediate Treg migration and promote donor-specific tolerance. This strategy is modeled after the natural process by which Tregs are recruited into the tumor microenvironment. Targeted pharmacologic release of CCL22 recruits Tregs by signaling through C-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CCR4), the corresponding chemokine receptor predominantly expressed on Tregs [16]. This approach is demonstrated in various transplant models, including pancreatic islet allografts [17], skin allografts [18], and vascularized composite allografts [19].

However, endogenous Treg levels are inherently low (i.e., 5–10 % of all peripheral CD4+ cells) [20], which can limit the effectiveness of leveraging the body's natural reservoir of these immunomodulatory cells to control transplant rejection. Moreover, CCL22 can also modestly target naïve CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, which express low levels of CCR4 [21]. Additionally, post-transplant immunosuppressive therapies create an interleukin-2 (IL-2)–depleted environment that is unfavorable for Treg survival and proliferation [22,23], as these cells thrive in IL-2–rich niches. IL-2 is a cytokine produced by activated lymphocytes that promotes the expansion of lymphocyte populations through autocrine and paracrine signaling in inflammatory contexts [24]. To establish a sufficient Treg population capable of preventing transplant rejection, strategies must combine stable Treg recruitment with approaches that selectively promote their expansion. In line with this, one strategy to promote Treg expansion involves administration of IL-2 [25]. However, systemic IL-2 delivery has been limited by its unintended stimulation of pro-inflammatory immune cells, such as cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells [26]. Furthermore, recombinant cytokines typically exhibit a short half-life in vivo [27], necessitating frequent re-administration to sustain biological effects. To address these challenges, prior research has demonstrated success by complexing IL-2 with JES6. JES6 is a blocking antibody that extends IL-2 half-life in vivo by preventing proteolytic degradation and protecting against premature renal filtration [23,28,29]. Further, JES6 restricts IL-2 interactions to only those cells expressing the high-affinity IL-2 receptor, CD25 [30]. Naive CD8+ T cells, which are conventionally responsive to IL-2, exhibit limited responsiveness to the IL-2/JES6 complex due to their lack of CD25. This targeted approach enhances selective Treg expansion while minimizing off-target effects and has demonstrated some efficacy in preclinical transplantation approaches [17,31]. However, challenges remain in effectively recruiting and specifically expanding Tregs to achieve robust transplant tolerance while minimizing systemic off-target effects.

To address these challenges, we developed a subcutaneously implantable platform “NanoLymph,” that establishes a localized tolerogenic niche for cell transplantation. By enabling sustained localized release of the immunomodulatory agents CCL22 and IL-2 in combination with JES6, NanoLymph supports in situ recruitment and selective expansion of endogenous Tregs. Existing biomaterial-based platforms have explored controlled release of immunomodulatory factors. However, many are hindered by rapid burst release and drug leakage [32], and by single-administration designs that necessitate repeated dosing, resulting in diminished efficacy and poor adaptability to evolving needs [33]. These limitations reduce therapeutic flexibility and often require repeated invasive interventions. In contrast, the NanoLymph platform could provide long-term, adaptable immune modulation over time, as its subcutaneous placement allows for minimally invasive access for dosing modulation, payload refilling or replacement. Such a feature distinguishes NanoLymph from conventional biodegradable or single-use systems.

In this study, we evaluated the vascularization of the NanoLymph following implantation, characterized the controlled release of immunomodulatory agents, and examined their local effects on immune cell recruitment. Finally, we assessed the capacity of the NanoLymph to support Leydig cell engraftment under limited systemic immunosuppression. Together, our results highlight the potential of NanoLymph to enable long-term cell engraftment by selectively reducing inflammation and promoting immune homeostasis in a robust model of major histocompatibility complex (MHC)–mismatched endocrine cell transplantation.

Materials and methods

NanoLymph fabrication and assembly

NanoLymph devices were fabricated with a high-resolution 3D printer (Form 3B, Formlabs, Massachusetts, USA) using biocompatible BioMed Clear resin (Formlabs). The NanoLymph structure has a drug reservoir volume of 25 μL and cell reservoir volume of 68 μL, with reservoirs printed separately. For sterilization, printed device structures were autoclaved at 121 °C, 15 psi for 52 min.

The NanoLymph devices were assembled in a stepwise fashion under a laminar-flow hood using aseptic technique. Nylon nanoporous membranes (100 nm, GVS Filter Technology, Bologna, Italy), polyethersulfone (PES) nanoporous membranes (30 nm; Sterlitech, Washington, US), and nylon meshes (100 μm, 300 μm; GVS Filter Technology, Bologna, Italy) produced using a laser cutter (VLS 2.30, Universal Laser Systems, Arizona, USA) were sterilized by autoclave and then heat pressed at 300 °F for 30 min using the Cricut EasyPress 2. Thereafter, the membranes were affixed using medical-grade silicone adhesive and corresponding primer (NuSil, Avantor®, Pennsylvania, USA) and cured overnight at 60 °C. A vacuum suction was placed through the interior of the drug reservoir using a catheter (18G, Becton Dickinson, New Jersey, USA) to position and maintain the membrane in place during glue application. Silicone plugs composed of medical-grade resealable silicone were affixed using primer (NuSil, Avantor®) and cured overnight at 60 °C. Assembled drug reservoir were tested for membrane leaks using Phosphate-Buffer Saline (PBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). Dual-layer nylon meshes were affixed onto the printed cell reservoir using BioMed Clear Resin and cured for 5 min using UV light. Pre-cut collagen scaffolds, prepared as described below, were placed inside of cell reservoirs. A port for cell loading is closed through a silicone plug as described above. Cell reservoirs were affixed onto drug reservoirs using medical-grade silicone and primer and cured overnight at 60 °C. The assembled devices were packaged into surgical sterile bags and sterilized with ethylene oxide.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging

Explanted devices were processed to extract the membranes and the meshes, which were then incubated with 5X concentrated Collagenase/Hyaluronidase (STEMCELL Technologies, British Columbia, Canada) shaking for 35 min at 37 °C. Samples were then dried in a 60 °C incubator overnight. To enhance image contrast, the samples were coated with a 5 nm film of gold using a sputtering coater (208HR High Resolution Sputter Coater, Ted Pella Inc, California, USA). SEM images were taken using the FEI Quanta 400 ESEM FEG microscope, at room temperature and in a high vacuum (2−6 Torr).

Collagen scaffold formulation

To provide structural support for cell growth and facilitate their inclusion within the device, porous collagen-based scaffolds were prepared following the protocol described by Paradiso et al. [34]. Type I bovine collagen (Viscofan, Navarre, Spain) was used as the base material and processed using a freeze-drying technique. Briefly, an acetic collagen slurry (200 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA) was precipitated by adjusting the pH to 5.5 using 2 N NaOH (Sigma-Aldrich). The slurry was then crosslinked in an aqueous solution of 0.1 wt% 1,4-butanediol diglycidyl ether (BDDGE; Sigma-Aldrich) first at RT and then at 4 °C for 24 h. Finally, the slurry was thoroughly washed with Milli-Q water (EMD Millipore, Massachusetts, USA), cast into a 48-well plate, and freeze-dried. The freeze-drying process was performed under vacuum (0.20 mbar), applying a controlled thermal cycle designed to influence pore size and scaffold porosity. This cycle consisted of a cooling ramp from 25 °C to −25 °C, followed by a heating ramp from −25 °C back to 25 °C, with both phases completed within 50 min.

Mice

8–13-week-old female C57BL/6 (Taconic Biosciences, New York, USA), BALB/c (Taconic Biosciences), FVB-Tg(CAG-luc,-GFP; Jackson Lab, Maine, USA), FVB/NJ (Jackson Lab) and B6 albino (Jackson Lab) were housed in the Houston Methodist Research Institute (HMRI) Comparative Medicine Facility in Houston, TX, USA. All animal procedures were conducted in compliance with the Animal Welfare Act and the guidelines outlined in the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Animals were provided with continuous access to clean drinking water and standard laboratory diet, and their health was monitored daily. All experimental procedures were carried out under inhaled isoflurane anesthesia. Euthanasia was performed humanely using carbon dioxide inhalation, with confirmation by cervical dislocation, in accordance with the recommendations of the American Veterinary Medical Association's Guidelines for Euthanasia.

Cell line

TM3 murine Leydig cell line was purchased from ATCC (Virginia, USA). A stable TM3 cell line expressing a Venus-Akaluciferase fusion protein (TM3-Venus-Akaluc) was generated by piggyBac transposition. TM3 cells (after two passages) were seeded into 12-well plates using complete growth medium consisting of DMEM:Ham's F12 (1:1) with L-glutamine and HEPES (Gibco, Massachusetts, USA) supplemented with 2.5 % Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS; Corning, New York, USA) and 5 % Horse Serum (Gibco) at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and cultured for 24 h prior to transfection. Cells were co-transfected at a 1:1 plasmid mass ratio with CMVp-hyperactive piggyBac transposase (CMVp-hypBase; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and pB-CAGp-Venus-Akaluciferase/PuroR (Addgene, Massachusetts, USA) a donor plasmid encoding the Venus-Akaluciferase fusion protein and a puromycin resistance cassette. Transfections were performed using standard lipid-based transfection reagents under optimized conditions.

At 24 h post-transfection, the culture medium was replaced with fresh complete growth medium, and cells were allowed to recover. On day 3, cells were transferred from 12-well plates to 100 mm culture dishes. Once confluency reached approximately 60 %, the medium was replaced with puromycin-containing selection medium (concentration as recommended by the plasmid selection marker; Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were maintained under puromycin selection for 4–5 days until reaching ∼70 % confluency, after which they were expanded into T175 flasks. Stable populations were cryopreserved in complete growth medium supplemented with 10 % dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich).

In all experiments, TM3-Venus-Akaluc cells were cultured in complete media that was replenished every 48–72 h. Cells were passaged at 70–80 % confluency as assessed by visual inspection via light microscopy.

Drugs

Recombinant mouse CCL22 and IL-2 (PeproTech, New Jersey, USA) were reconstituted in sterile PBS to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL, according to the manufacturer's instructions. The JES6-1 monoclonal antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was stored at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. The MR1 (anti-CD40L) and anti-CD8 monoclonal antibodies (Bio X Cell, New Hampshire, USA) were stored at concentrations ranging from 5 mg/mL to 10 mg/mL, as provided by the manufacturer.

To label CCL22, 100 μg of mouse CCL22 recombinant protein was dissolved in 900 μL of 0.1 M NaHCO3 (pH 8.5, Sigma-Aldrich) to maintain an optimal reaction environment for NHS ester conjugation. Sulfo-cyanine5.5 NHS ester (Lumiprobe 27320, Maryland, USA), prepared as a 1 mg/mL solution in water, was added dropwise to the CCL22 solution at a 1:1.5 M ratio. For IL-2 labeling, 50 μg of recombinant murine IL-2 was similarly dissolved in 900 μL of 0.1 M NaHCO3, and 2.3 μg of Sulfo-Cy5.5 (1:7 M ratio) was added dropwise. For the IL-2 monoclonal antibody (JES6-1A12), 200 μg of the antibody was dissolved in 900 μL of 0.1 M NaHCO3, and 14.9 μg of Sulfo-Cy5.5 (1:10 M ratio) was added dropwise. Each reaction mixture was stirred continuously at room temperature for 4 h to allow sufficient conjugation time. After incubation, unreacted free Sulfo-Cy5.5 was removed by centrifugation using 3 kDa centrifugal filters (Amicon Ultra-4, MilliporeSigma, Massachusetts, USA), ensuring purification of the labeled proteins.

Cell proliferation assay

For MTT proliferation assay, TM3-Venus-Akaluc cells were seeded at 5,500 cells per well in a flat bottom 96-well plate and incubated overnight to allow cell adherence. The next day, culture medium was aspirated and appropriate media containing cytokines was replenished in replicates of 6 with negative control as media only. Forty-eight and 72 h later, the MTT assay was performed using TACS MTT cell proliferation assay (R&D Systems, Minnesota, USA) following manufacturer instructions. Absorbance was measured at OD 570/690 nm with Synergy™ HT microplate reader (Bio-Tech Instruments, Inc., Vermont, USA).

In vitro drug release

NanoLymph drug reservoirs (n = 5 per condition) were printed and affixed with 100 nm nylon membranes following similar protocol as described above. Nylon was chosen for its high heat stability, high hydrophilicity, resistance to biofouling, and strong mechanical properties [35,36]. Reservoirs were loaded with 5 μg CCL22, 1 μg IL-2, and 5 μg of JES6 via silicone ports using a dual-needle procedure: one needle (28 G) connected to a syringe holding the appropriate drug solutions was used to push drugs in and another needle (30 G) was used to relieve displaced volume from within the drug reservoir. Devices were immersed in 400 μL sterile PBS sink solution at 37 °C, each in individual microcentrifuge tubes. Sink solutions were collected and changed daily for 7 days. Drug quantification was assessed via commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit according to manufacturer's protocol. Murine CCL22 was quantified with Mouse MDC (CCL22) ELISA Development Kit (ABTS; Peprotech), murine IL-2 was quantified with IL-2 Mouse ELISA Kit (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA), and JES6 was quantified with Rat IgG2a ELISA Kit (Invitrogen). Absorbance was measured at OD 450/570 nm with Synergy™ HT Microplate Reader.

Minimally invasive SQ implantation

A small incision (approximately 1 cm) was made on dorsal right lateral side caudal to cervical spine. Blunt dissection was used to separate the dermal layer from subcutaneous (SQ) fascia to generate a pocket on right flank. Sterile PBS was used to flush this SQ pocket within which sterilized NanoLymph devices were placed. The incision site was closed with wound clips (Roboz, Maryland, USA). All animals received five days of oral analgesic pre- and post-surgery as well as SQ buprenorphine Slow Release (0.5 mg/kg), and bupivacaine (5 mg/kg) as a splash block during surgery for postsurgical pain.

Histological analysis

C57BL/6 mice were each SQ implanted with a NanoLymph device either containing the collagen scaffold or a device without the scaffold and euthanized as described above at predetermined endpoints. Fibrotic capsule immediately surrounding the SQ implant site, liver and kidney were isolated, fixed in 10 % formalin, preserved in 70 % ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm. Samples were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or Masson's Trichrome at the Research Pathology Core of HMRI, or with anti-CD45 (ab10558, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom), anti-CD31 (AF3628, R&D Systems), and anti-LYVE1 (ab14917, Abcam) antibodies at the Pathology Core of Baylor College of Medicine. Sections were imaged using EVOS Cell Imaging Systems (Thermo Fisher Scientific) mounted with 4x, 10x, and 40x non-oil objective attached to digital camera. Between 6 and 8 regions of interest (ROIs) were selected from each sample slide and included in the statistical analysis.

Drug biodistribution from NanoLymph

To assess drug biodistribution, NanoLymph with nylon membranes were SQ implanted into 8- to 10-week-old BALB/c mice two weeks prior to drug reservoir loading. On day 0, the drug reservoir was transcutaneously loaded as described above with co-formulations containing 5 μg CCL22, 1 μg IL-2, and 5 μg JES6, with only one agent conjugated to Cy5.5 in each condition (i.e., Cy5.5-CCL22, IL-2, JES6; CCL22, Cy5.5-IL-2, JES6; or CCL22, IL-2, Cy5.5-JES6) to enable fluorescence-based tracking. Mice were shaved on the right flank with a 25 mm diameter circle extending from the middle of implant prior to imaging to avoid any signal obstruction due to fur.

The drug reservoir was transcutaneously refilled with drug cocktail solubilized in PBS via the opposing silicone ports using the previously described dual-needle technique. Appropriate mass quantities of each drug component were formulated together as specified above and a final volume of 23 μL of each cocktail was used for transcutaneous refills of the drug reservoir. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and two needles (28G and 30G) were each inserted in either opposing silicone ports. First, 50 μL of sterile PBS was used to flush the reservoir of remnant fluid. Next, the drug cocktail was transcutaneous loaded. Mice were placed on the left lateral decubitus position on imaging stage of the in vivo imaging system (IVIS, PerkinElmer, Massachusetts, USA). Refill was assessed by evidence of vented excess PBS through the 30G needle.

For detection of fluorescently labeled drug, imaging was performed using Living Image Software 4.5.2 (PerkinElmer) with the following settings: excitation at 675 nm, emission at 720 nm, illumination level set to high, epi-illumination mode, binning (medium) at 8, field of view (FOV) of 18.4, f-stop of 2 (f/2), and an exposure time of 0.4 s. Images were qualitatively assessed by scaling all images on one consistent scale for comparison. Images were quantitatively assessed by drawing a ROI around the NanoLymph and measuring fluorescent signal in average radiance efficiency ([p/s/cm2/sr]/[μW/cm2]) or counts. Upon animal sacrifice, the organs were harvested and imaged as described above.

Immune cell homing and multicolor flow cytometry assessment

NanoLymph with nylon membranes were implanted SQ as previously described. In the first study, two weeks post-implantation, mice were randomized into two groups: PBS control and CCL22. Reservoirs were transcutaneously loaded as described above with 5 μg of CCL22 as appropriate. Mice were euthanized either on day 3 or day 7 following transcutaneous refilling and NanoLymph devices were collected for cytometric analysis.

In the subsequent study, two weeks post-implantation, mice were randomized into four groups: PBS control, CCL22 + IL-2, and CCL22 + IL-2/JES6. Reservoirs were transcutaneously loaded as described above. For groups receiving drug, drug reservoirs in mice were transcutaneously refilled with 5 μg of CCL22, 1 μg IL-2, and 1 μg IL-2 with 5 μg JES6. Complexes between IL-2 and JES6 were formed by incubating the appropriate quantities of each reagent at 37 °C for 30 min. Mice were euthanized either on day 3 or day 14 following transcutaneous refilling and NanoLymph devices were collected for cytometry analysis.

NanoLymph devices were dismantled using a scalpel and then individual device components were repeatedly flushed with PBS to isolate all cells. The remaining tissue was digested using Collagenase/Hyaluronidase (STEMCELL) for 30 min at 37 °C using gentle agitation. Single cell solution was processed by filtration through 40 μM strainer (Fischer Scientific) and ACK lysis (Quality Biological, Maryland, USA).

For staining, single cells were first blocked with antimouse CD16/CD32/Fc block (eBioscience, California, USA) and stained with the Treg Identification Panel (Supplementary Table 1) for extracellular staining. Cells were fixed overnight with eBioscience™ Foxp3/Transcription Factor Fixation/Permeabilization (Invitrogen), permeabilized with eBioscience™ Permeabilization Buffer (Invitrogen), and then intracellular staining was performed. Cells were washed once prior to data acquisition. Fluorescent data were collected using the BD Symphony A5SE cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo v10.7.2 (FlowJo, Oregon, USA). Analysis was performed after exclusion of debris, doublets and nonviable cells. Analysis was performed at a population-level relative to respective parent population via FlowJo.

Leydig cell transplant

NanoLymph with PES membranes and collagen scaffolds in the cell reservoir were implanted SQ as previously described. On day 0, mice received 250 μg of anti-mouse CD40L (MR1; BioXCell) diluted in InVivoPure pH 7.0 Dilution Buffer (BioXCell) and systemically delivered through intraperitoneal (IP) injection. Mice were given 0.5 mg/kg of Rapamycin (Sigma-Aldrich) solubilized in 100 % ethanol and delivered systemically through daily IP injection in a 5 % PEG (USB Corporation, Ohio, USA) and 5 % Tween 80 (Acros Organics B.V.B.A., Antwerp, Belgium) solution for 14 days post-transplant.

After four weeks, the cell reservoirs of the NanoLymph were transcutaneously loaded with 1 × 106 TM3-Venus-Akaluc cells (in 20 μL sterile PBS), diluted 1:3 in pre-chilled Matrigel, via the cell loading port using a 28-G needle. To minimize variability, all experiments were conducted using aliquots from a single lot of Matrigel. Drug reservoirs were transcutaneous refilled as described above with 5 μg CCL22, 1 μg IL-2/5 μg JES6, 5 μg mouse MR1 (BioXCell), and 10 μg mouse anti-CD8 (clone: 2.43, BioXCell) accordingly to group condition. For detection of bioluminescence, imaging was performed using Living Image Software 4.5.2 (PerkinElmer) with the following settings: binning (medium) at 8, field of view (FOV) of 18.4, f-stop of 1 (f/1), and an exposure time of 5 s. Mice were administered 100 μL of 5 mM TokeOni (Sigma-Aldrich) via IP injection 10 min prior to image acquisition. Images were acquired every 3–4 days starting from day 0. Images were quantitatively assessed by drawing ROI around the NanoLymph and measuring the luminescent signal in counts.

On day 31, 4 animals from each group were sacrificed and NanoLymph and spleen were collected and processed for flow cytometric analysis as described above. Collagen scaffolds were digested with collagenase type I (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 37 °C. Animals with representative flow signal were chosen from each group. Spleens were suspended to single-cell suspensions by mechanical filtration in 70 μm and 40 μm cell strainer (Fischer Scientific) and ACK lysis was performed prior to staining. Single cells were stained with the Cell Transplant Panel (Supplementary Table 2) and fluorescent data was collected and analyzed as described above.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.1.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Depending on the dataset, comparisons between groups were evaluated using Student's t-test, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, with results denoted as follows: n.s., not significant; P ≤ 0.05; ∗P ≤ 0.01; ∗∗P ≤ 0.01; ∗∗∗P ≤ 0.001; ∗∗∗∗P ≤ 0.0001.

Results

NanoLymph

We developed the NanoLymph to serve as a lymphoid niche capable of localized Treg recruitment to promote an immunomodulated environment conducive to cell transplantation. This is based on the premise that sustained and localized chemoattractant elution from the NanoLymph recruits Tregs on site, where they protect transplanted payload from cytotoxic immune responses.

The NanoLymph is 3D printed via stereolithography using biocompatible resin. It comprises of two adjacent reservoirs, each for drug and cells, connected by a nanoporous membrane (Fig. 1A). The dimensions of 11 mm × 10.3 mm x 2.6 mm is comparable to the size of M&M candy (Fig. 1B). As the NanoLymph is intended for long-term cell engraftment, device biointegration with the surrounding tissue is critical. To achieve this, the NanoLymph deployment entails creating a vascularized environment through a biomimetic collagen scaffold within the cell reservoir [37]. The collagen scaffold promotes localized tissue remodeling by supporting regenerative capabilities in vascular morphogenesis [38] and collagen fibril organization [39]. The local formation of vascularized tissue within the cell reservoir enables cell recruitment and permits exchange of oxygen and nutrients within the NanoLymph [40].

Two overlapping nylon meshes (100 and 300 μm in pore size, inner and outer meshes respectively) wrap around the cell reservoir, providing mechanical support and enabling direct vascular and tissue penetration from the SQ space. Transcutaneous payload loading and refilling is achieved through self-sealing silicone ports on either end of the drug reservoir (each for loading and venting) through a minimally invasive procedure previously established by our group [[41], [42], [43]]. Similarly, the cell loading port permits transcutaneous access to the cell reservoir. For long-term deployment, we demonstrate that both the membrane and meshes retain integrity after up to 9 months of implantation in vivo (Fig. 1C–F).

Integration of NanoLymph with subcutaneous space

To evaluate integration with host tissue, histological assessment of the NanoLymph and surrounding tissue was performed at 2, 3, and 4 weeks post-SQ implantation in mice. Implanted materials typically trigger a foreign body response (FBR), which involves the release of inflammatory cytokines that recruit neutrophils, macrophages, and fibroblasts, eventually leading to fibrotic encapsulation [44]. While FBR is a normal physiological reaction, an unresolved response can compromise implant function [45]. Thus, FBR induced by NanoLymph was assessed through histological analysis.

Masson's Trichrome staining analysis of NanoLymph retrieved at 2, 3, and 4 weeks post-SQ implantation revealed the presence of a thin fibrotic capsule surrounding the device (Fig. 2A). Throughout the study, the fibrotic capsule remained thin, with an average thickness of 15.10±5.27 μm. Although fibrotic capsule formation is a concern for drug transport, previous research from our lab shows that fibrotic encapsulation of similar cell delivery implants does not affect integration with vascularized tissue [43] or drug diffusion [46,47]. We quantified the presence of CD45-positive cells, a pan-immune cell marker [48]. H&E staining was used to evaluate cellular density within the NanoLymph cell reservoir [49]. There was an overall increase in cellular infiltration over time, which was not observed in NanoLymph without a scaffold (Fig. 2C). This indicates scaffold-mediated integration of host tissue within the cell reservoir.

Maintaining a balance between a well-vascularized, yet not overly dense, environment allows for more efficient oxygen and nutrient transport from the blood vessels to the cell graft, which is crucial for preventing cell loss due to hypoxia [50]. To evaluate this, the density of blood vessels within the cell reservoir was quantified using H&E histological images. As shown in Fig. 2D, blood vessel density was observed in weeks 3 and 4. This finding is further supported by CD31 staining, a marker of vascular endothelial cells [51], where there was a progressive increase over time (Fig. 2E).

Further, we used LYVE-1 staining to assess lymphangiogenesis. LYVE-1 is a marker primarily used to identify lymphatic endothelial cells, distinguishing between blood and lymphatic vessels [52]. There was a steady increase in lymphatics in the NanoLymph cell reservoir over time with a significant increase between weeks 2 and 4 (Fig. 2F). This is particularly notable for the NanoLymph application as an immune cell recruitment platform, as the observed lymphatic vascular ingrowth suggests integration of the device with the existing SQ lymphatic network, thereby enabling immune cell recruitment. The data collectively indicate that NanoLymph is effectively integrated in the SQ space with a microenvironment supportive of immune cell trafficking and cell transplantation.

NanoLymph prolongs cell transplant persistence

To test the ability of NanoLymph in preventing allogeneic cell transplant rejection, we used Venus-Akaluc-expressing Leydig cells (TM3-Venus-Akaluc). Akaluc provides superior in vivo visualization compared to conventional firefly luciferase, enabling single-cell resolution of the labeled cells [55]. For this study, we included anti-CD40L antibody (MR1) and anti-CD8 antibody in the drug reservoir to minimize early effector T cell–mediated rejection at the graft site. These agents are commonly used in transplantation studies for induction [56]. MR1 is a non-depleting antibody that blocks co-stimulatory signaling critical for effector T cell activation, whereas anti-CD8 is used for selective depletion of cytotoxic T cells. Their inclusion was necessary to prevent unintended activation of CD8+ T cells, which upregulate CD25 and CCR4 [57,58], and are therefore responsive to CCL22 and IL-2/JES6.

We first assessed whether local exposure of the immunomodulatory agents with TM3-Venus-Akaluc cells would impact their viability via MTT cytotoxicity assay. TM3-Venus-Akaluc cells were incubated with either low or high concentrations of CCL22, IL-2, JES6, MR1, and anti-CD8, individually, as well as in combination. Cell viability was assessed at 48 and 72 h post-treatment. Overall, none of the agents affected cell viability (Fig. 6A–C). In fact, we observed modest increases in viability following 72-h exposure to both low and high concentrations of CCL22, IL-2, and JES6. A similar increase was noted with the low concentration of MR1 at 48 h (Fig. 6D). While a slight decrease in viability was observed with high-dose anti-CD8 at 72 h (Fig. 6E), there was no significant difference compared to the low-dose. Further, this effect was not replicated in the cocktail condition, where viability was maintained or slightly elevated, particularly at 48 h with the low-dose combination (Fig. 6F). We performed flow cytometric analysis on a subset of NanoLymph on day 31 to assess infiltrated immune cell populations. At this point, luminescent signals indicated the presence of viable cells across all three experimental groups. As hypothesized, the NL cocktail group promoted an increase in CCL22 responsive CCR4+CD25+Foxp3+ Tregs as compared to all other groups, in line with the chemoattractant and proliferative effects of CCL22+IL-2/JES6 (Fig. 7D). We noted that local MR1 and anti-CD8 antibodies alone were insufficient to suppress CD8+ T cell infiltration. However, in combination with local CCL22+IL-2/JES6, there was a suppression of CD8+ cytotoxic T cell infiltration into the NanoLymph. Furthermore, the NL cocktail group had a significantly higher Treg to CD8 ratio and an overall reduction in CD4+ T cell percentages, suggestive of transplant-tolerant local immunomodulation.

Notably, in the spleen we observed a reduction of CD8+ T cells as well as high Treg to CD8 ratio in the NL cocktail group (Fig. 7E). At this time point, the NL cocktail group exhibited the highest survival rate, with 67.5 % of subjects surviving on day 31 (Supplementary Table 3). Additionally, when Tregs isolated from day-31 splenocytes were co-cultured for 5 days with irradiated donor splenocytes and naïve syngeneic Tconvs, NanoLymph-treated conditions showed significantly lower effector CD8+ T cell percentages than all other groups (Supplementary Fig. 5).

The initial prolongation of graft persistence in the drug cocktail cohort was markedly superior to that of other groups. However, this signal begins to decline steeply after day 27, with near-total loss by day 39. Notably, day 27 emerged as a critical inflection point, marked by abrupt changes in graft persistence and the onset of signal loss in several groups. This suggests that targeting drug refilling at this timepoint could provide crucial reinforcement to sustain graft persistence. Overall, these findings point to a promising mechanism by which NanoLymph-mediated immune conditioning drives systemic tolerance to extend graft persistence.

Discussion

The largest issue facing the field of transplant immunology is graft loss due to immune rejection requiring lifelong anti-rejection treatment with associated adverse effects. These problems are exacerbated in the context of cell transplantation, since cellular therapies present the unique challenge of underexpressing key immunological markers, rendering them more susceptible to immune rejection [65]. For example, in vitro-generated cells may lack or downregulate crucial inhibitory ligands such as HLA-I, or fail to present Killer Ig-like Receptors (KIR) ligands recognized by the recipient's NK cells, thereby increasing the likelihood of immune recognition and elimination [66].

Over the past two decades, a growing body of evidence has highlighted the critical role of Tregs in preventing allograft rejection and promoting immune tolerance [[67], [68], [69]]. Due to the notable limitations associated with ex vivo–expanded Treg therapies [70], there is increasing interest in alternative strategies that can leverage a patient's own endogenous Treg population. In cancer, tumors establish a pro-tolerogenic environment through secretion of the Treg-attracting chemokine CCL22; research is underway to replicate this mechanism in transplant models. Prior approaches have included biologically engineering allografts, for example, by transducing pancreatic islets with CCL22-expressing vectors in models of autoimmune diabetes [71] and using biomaterials-based strategies such as delivering CCL22 via nanoparticles or microneedles at the transplant site [18,19]. However, these approaches present translational challenges: genetically modified cell sources are expensive to generate in clinical-grade conditions, require stringent GMP oversight [72], and carry risks related to transfection efficiency, stability, and off-target genomic effects [73]. Additionally, many current biomaterial platforms are limited by single-use functionality or require additional procedures for therapeutic replacement or removal [74].

To overcome these barriers, the NanoLymph offers a clinically translatable approach by enabling in situ recruitment and selective expansion of endogenous Tregs, generating local immune regulation without invasive extracorporeal manipulation. In contrast to prior biomaterial platforms, the NanoLymph is drug- and antigen-agnostic and can be rapidly adapted to diverse therapeutic targets. Its minimally invasive accessibility allows for replenishment, replacement, or dosage adjustment as needed, supporting individualized therapeutic optimization. Furthermore, the device can be manipulated for continual immune modulation or fully retrieved if required. Finally, sustained, low-dose localized release of immunomodulatory agents reduces the risk of systemic toxicity and provides an added safeguard for patient-centered application.

In this study, we show that sustained CCL22+ IL-2/JES6 delivery through the NanoLymph can exhibit significant and persistent Treg recruitment on site. It should be noted that the pro-transplant immunomodulation is achieved through the release of CCL22 and IL-2/JES6 with a total of 1–5 μg loaded per drug. In contrast, conventional immunosuppressive therapy relies on systemic delivery of drugs at doses reaching milligrams per day. Furthermore, the NanoLymph can simultaneously release all three immunomodulatory drugs (CCL22, IL-2, and JES6) in vivo in a sustained manner for up to 4 weeks. In line with other long-acting systems developed in our laboratory, a simple minimally invasive transcutaneous refilling procedure could extend local delivery [47], while avoiding burst effects and unwanted systemic dissemination observed with SQ administrations. This is significant since other nanoparticle-based drug delivery formulations have limited release durations [75] and exhibit rapid burst release [76,77], which could pose systemic toxicity issues. Importantly, we show blood and lymphatic vascularization within the NanoLymph cell reservoir, which are necessary for cell graft integration with the rest of the body and sustained Treg trafficking. Recent advances in tissue bioengineering demonstrate important progress in replicating structural and functional aspects of musculoskeletal tissue. For example, developments include hydrogel-based cartilage organoids [78], electrospun fiber mesh scaffolds for anatomically complex 3D constructs [79], natural nano-biomaterials supporting bone and cartilage regeneration [80], and bone organoids that incorporate neovascularization to better mimic ossification [81]. The NanoLymph platform extends these advances by establishing a fully integrated, vascularized environment that supports immune cell trafficking.

In this study, we transplanted Akaluc-TM3 cells and evaluated graft persistence over time through IVIS imaging, with bioluminescence signal serving as a surrogate for persistence. Because the signal reflects both viable cell number and metabolic activity, this approach cannot distinguish survival from proliferation. Even so, longitudinal BLI has been widely validated in a number of contexts, including monitoring islet graft survival [82], stem-cell therapies [83], transplanted cell fate [84], and viral infection dynamics such as SARS-CoV-2 [85]. Akaluc in particular provides high sensitivity and a linear relationship between photon output and cell number, demonstrated at the single-cell level [86] and across seeded populations [87]. We further confirm this linearity with the current construct (Supplemental Fig. 6).

In our allogeneic cell transplantation study in a fully mismatched murine model, all animals received a short-course baseline immunosuppression protocol consisting of a single dose of MR1 therapy and 14 days of rapamycin therapy. This limited regimen was selected both to give the NanoLymph system sufficient time to establish a stable gradient of immunomodulatory agents and to evaluate whether the therapy could augment transplant tolerance under a clinically relevant immunosuppression protocol. Importantly, we selected agents known to support or synergize with Treg-based therapies. For instance, we leveraged the differential co-stimulatory requirements of Tregs and conventional T cells by incorporating anti-CD40L (MR1) therapy; since CD8+ effector T cells are heavily reliant on CD40−CD40L signaling for activation, blocking this pathway prevents early immune activation without compromising regulatory function [88,89]. Additionally, the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin exhibits a dual immunomodulatory function, simultaneously enhancing Treg expansion while inhibiting effector immune cell activation, thereby fostering an environment conducive to graft survival [90,91]. Further, because activated CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, which are key drivers of transplant rejection, can upregulate CD25 [92], making them responsive to our local IL-2 therapy, we included low-dose locally released MR1 to maintain CD8+ cells in an anergic state, as well as anti-CD8 antibody for selective depletion.

In this study, we show that the NanoLymph enables prolongation of allogeneic cell transplant persistence. We observe a transient but significant increase in transplant survival of up to 31 days for nearly 70 % of transplants in the combined drug cocktail condition. This result is striking in comparison to those groups receiving systemic + local aCD8/MR1 (31 day survival of 22.5 %), systemic immunosuppression alone (31 day survival of 11.1 %), or no manipulation (31 day survival of 0 %). These differences across groups may be explained by variations in the recruitment, retention, and activation of immune cells at the transplant site, specifically due to an increase in regulatory populations and a decrease in effector populations. There is a significant increase in CCR4+Foxp3+CD25+ cells at the local graft site, suggesting these populations preferentially migrated and were retained on site in direct response to the release of CCL22. Furthermore, we notice a significant reduction in CD8 and CD4 effector cells between the local/systemic immunosuppression group and the combined drug cocktail group in both the device space and in the spleen. This is notable because, while the local immunosuppression has the potential to directly anergize or eliminate the cytotoxic population, it is insufficient and is complemented by Tregs. Indeed, such a role is consistent with the well-established ability of Tregs to suppress CD8+ proliferation and cytotoxicity through both contact-dependent and cytokine-mediated mechanisms [[93], [94], [95], [96]]. Supporting this, Tregs isolated from NanoLymph-cocktail treated animals at day 31 significantly reduced effector CD8+ T cell frequencies in ex vivo co-cultures with irradiated donor splenocytes and naïve syngeneic Tconvs. Taken together, these results suggest that localized NanoLymph-based tolerance induction not only reshapes the immune profile at the graft site but may also elicit downstream systemic effects that support the survival of engrafted cells beyond the device. By simultaneously increasing regulatory activity and reducing effector populations, the NanoLymph establishes a tolerogenic environment that translates to significant short-term protection. A limitation of this study, however, is that we did not directly characterize the suppressive function of recruited and expanded Tregs in vivo at the transplant site, which will be an important direction for future work.

Although differences in protection are observed, these effects were transient. By day 35, there is a decrease in graft survival in all groups including the combined drug cocktail group. One major reason for this effect may be the relatively low Treg-to-CD8 ratio. While the balance is slightly tipped toward a regulatory population, the effect is modest (i.e., 1:1 ratio observed). Although some previous literature suggests that this effect is adequate to confer protection [97], others suggest a higher ratio is necessary. For example, in models of skin engraftment or vascularized organ grafts (i.e, heart, kidney), tolerance requires Tregs greatly outnumbering effectors with up to even 6:1 [94,98]. Thus, although we achieved the critical 1:1 Treg-to-effector threshold, durable protection may necessitate a considerably higher ratio. To achieve long-term control of CD8+ infiltration, future strategies may require an additional systemic dose of CD8-anergizing induction agents administered around day 27, the time point presently associated with rapid rejection likely due to loss of CD8+ regulation.

Another possible explanation for the delayed rejection observed in NanoLymph-treated groups is the emergence of memory T cell responses that escape the established immune modulation. While our study did not directly interrogate memory T cells, prior literature provides strong precedent for this hypothesis. Memory CD8+ T cells in particular are notorious for mediating “costimulation-blockade resistant” rejection [99,100]. Unlike naïve T cells, memory populations have lower activation thresholds and can proliferate or exert cytotoxicity despite blockade of conventional costimulatory pathways such as CD28/CD40 [[101], [102], [103], [104], [105]]. They are also more resistant to suppression by Tregs and less susceptible to standard immunosuppressants [106]. Consistent with this, multiple murine models have demonstrated that memory T cells can gradually expand and precipitate graft loss several weeks after transplantation despite initial control. For example, models with prior viral exposure harbor memory T cells that render costimulation blockade (CTLA4-Ig, anti-CD40L, etc.) ineffective, leading to eventual graft loss [[107], [108], [109]]. More specifically, Valujskikh et al. [110] showed that primed memory T cells drove cardiac allograft rejection by days 25–30 despite DST + anti-CD40L, and Adams et al. [111] demonstrated that virally induced memory T cells drove skin graft rejection around day 28 despite CTLA4-Ig + anti-CD40L. Taken together, these findings suggest that the delayed graft loss seen in our NanoLymph-treated groups near day 27 may similarly reflect breakthrough by donor-reactive memory T cell populations that escape co-stimulation, a hypothesis that warrants direct testing in future studies.

Although the recruitment of CCR4+ Tregs represents a primary pathway through which CCL22 exerts anti-inflammatory effects, additional functions of this chemokine have been described that may likewise influence allograft tolerance. In particular, CCL22 has been shown to recruit a variety of CCR4+ immune populations, including monocytes, NK T cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and skin-homing Th2 and Th17 CD4+ subsets, that can either promote tolerance or drive alloimmunity. It has also been associated with enhanced expression of Treg-related factors such as CTLA-4 [[112], [113], [114]]. A limitation of the present study is that we did not examine the effects of our CCL22-based therapy beyond the T cell compartment, primarily because of the limited amount of tissue obtainable from the NanoLymph device for flow cytometric analysis. To minimize animal use, we prioritized characterization of Tregs and CD8+ T cells as the principal regulatory and effector subsets in the context of transplant tolerance. Future studies will benefit from a broader analysis of additional lymphoid and myeloid subsets to more fully delineate the immunomodulatory landscape induced by CCL22. Moreover, the use of transgenic models could clarify the specific contribution of Tregs relative to other CCR4+ populations (e.g., using CCR4−/− mice with targeted CCR4 transgene expression in FoxP3+ cells).

Additionally, in the future, it may be valuable to explore strategies for inducing tolerance against a stably targetable antigen source. The immortalized Leydig cells used presently have reports of variable surface marker expression profiles that change based on differential exposure to inflammatory molecules and cytokines such as IFN-γ and Con-A [115]. Thus, it is likely that in vivo, as the inflammatory profile at the site of transplantation changes over time, there are corresponding changes in the expression of MHC I and II molecules, thereby altering the immunogenicity of the graft. In this case, we hypothesize somewhat of a snowball effect specific to this cell model: in the early stages of rejection, inflammation at the graft site increases, which in turn elevates MHC expression, leading to greater immune targeting and heightened immunogenicity of the cells and acceleration of the rejection process. Further, we are considering using localized Fas ligand to subdue unwanted CD8+ cells in the NanoLymph [116].

The choice of cell transplanted model in this study enhances its significance, as it may be considered relatively more immunogenic compared to others. For instance, multipotent stem cell (MSC) grafts, one of the most used cell transplant models in the clinical settings, express low levels of MHC class II and lack costimulatory markers, making them non-immunogenic even in allogeneic models [117]. These grafts require only prophylactic immunosuppression to prevent GVHD [118]. Alternatively, more differentiated cell grafts, such as islet cells, exhibit consistent surface marker expression, rendering it easier to achieve stable tolerance. Furthermore, here we use a highly immunogenic mouse transplant model (e.g., BALB/c to C57). Compared to other mouse models, C57 have Th1 and M1 dominant immune responses with robust cell-mediated immunity and NK cell activity, which allow for strong rejection responses [119].

Another approach could be to tolerize DCs and Tregs against the transplanted material prior to transplantation, similar to a prophylactic cancer vaccine model, where tumor-associated antigens prime DCs and T cells to recognize the future tumor challenge [41]. We have shown that NanoLymph recruits DCs, which interface with the locally presented ovalbumin (OVA+) antigen [42]. These OVA + DCs then migrate to the lymph node and present to CD8+ T cells. Previous studies have explored tolerogenic priming through exposure to donor MHC peptides or adoptive transfer of donor-antigen-specific Tregs, which resulted in better regulation of alloreactive T cells and delayed graft rejection [120,121].

Conclusion

In summary, the current approach to inducing allograft tolerance by Treg recruitment via local sustained release of CCL22 in conjunction with IL-2/JES6 explores leveraging the body's inherent mechanism for mediating immune activation to limit rejection responses and reducing systemic immunosuppression. By inducing a shift in transplant environment to favor regulatory over effector cell activation, our novel therapeutic approach has the potential for prolonging allograft persistence. This approach also avoids patient compliance and toxicity issues associated with current long-term immunosuppression regimens, which leave patients immunocompromised and unable to fight infections and malignancies. Lastly, the translational implications of endogenous Treg-promoting, tolerance-inducing regimen extend beyond endocrine cell engraftment. The NanoLymph may also prevent rejection of other allogeneic graft models such as stem cell or bone marrow engraftment. Ultimately, the NanoLymph may have broader relevance in acute or chronic inflammatory and autoimmune pathologies such as inflammatory bowel disease or rheumatoid arthritis where localized and sustained immunomodulation could mitigate systemic toxicity while preserving immune homeostasis.
