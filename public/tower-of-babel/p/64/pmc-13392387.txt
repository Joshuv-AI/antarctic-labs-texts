Optimization of cordycepin fermentation by Cordyceps militaris via response surface methodology
Authors: Juan Liu, Shuang Hu, Hao-Ming Sun, Bao-Yu Liu, Hai-Dong Zhang, Yuan Gao, Hong-Feng Yi, Kun Qian, Jie-Shu Wang, Yue Zhang, Chang-Jie Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCordycepin is a bioactive compound with demonstrated immunoregulatory, antioxidant, and organ-protective properties. This study aimed to significantly enhance the production of cordycepin in Cordyceps militaris fermentation through systematic process optimization. After identifying glucose, peptone, and Mg2+ as the optimal carbon source, nitrogen source, and inorganic salt via single-factor experiments, response surface methodology (RSM) was applied to refine their concentrations along with inoculation amount and pH. The analysis indicated that glucose, peptone, and pH exerted an extremely significant influence on yield (p < 0.0001), Mg2+ was significant (p = 0.0112), while inoculation amount was not (p = 0.0524). The derived optimal conditions (40 g L− 1 glucose, 24 g L− 1 peptone, 0.7 g L− 1 Mg2+, 2% (v/v) inoculation, pH 8) were validated, achieving a cordycepin yield of 212.00 mg L− 1. This result closely matched the model prediction and represented a 46.18% increase compared with the initial yield of 145.03 mg L− 1, demonstrating the strategy’s effectiveness for boosting production efficiency and potential for industrial application. These results will help improve cordycepin production efficiency in industrial settings, thereby bringing economic benefits to the cordyceps industry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54117-8.Keywords: Cordyceps militaris, Cordycepin, Fermentation, Response surface methodologySubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Cordycepin (3’-deoxyadenosine, chemical formula C10H13N5O3) is an adenosine derivative composed of adenine and deoxyribose1. It was first isolated from Cordyceps militaris by K.G. Cunningham in the 1950s, who reported its antibacterial and antitumor activities. This discovery laid the foundation for subsequent research on cordycepin. In the 1990s, pentostatin (a compound often co-produced with cordycepin in fungi) was developed as an FDA-approved drug for leukemia treatment, as its combination with cordycepin enhances anticancer efficacy and reduces drug resistance. More recently, in 2022, researchers demonstrated that cordycepin inhibits SARS-CoV−2 replication by reducing viral copy numbers, suggesting a potential therapeutic direction for COVID−19 treatment2. Researchers have shown that cordycepin and its derivatives can promote anti-tumor immunity by modulating immune cell phenotypes. Additionally, cordycepin suppresses cancer cell proliferation by inhibiting phosphorylation of key components in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MEK/ERK signaling pathways3. Furthermore, cordycepin exhibits immunomodulatory4, antioxidant, neuroprotective, cardiovascular-protective, and organ-protective properties5. Due to its potent antioxidant activity6, cordycepin is widely used in the cosmetics industry, particularly in high-end products. It has also become a key bioactive ingredient in nutraceuticals owing to its distinct flavor and diverse biological activities. As a high-value fungal resource, C. militaris represents a promising feedstock for the emerging bioeconomy, aligning with the core focus of biorefinery concepts aimed at sustainable utilization of biological resources. Enhancing the yield of its pivotal bioactive compound, cordycepin, is crucial for developing high-value products within integrated biorefinery frameworks. Consequently, advancing efficient and scalable bioconversion processes for C. militaris cultivation and cordycepin production is essential. This not only supports the diversification of the bio-based industry but also maximizes the potential of this functional organism in contributing to health-promoting applications and a more sustainable future.

At present, the primary methods for high-yield production of cordycepin include chemical synthesis, genetic engineering, and microbial fermentation. Chemical synthesis constructs the nucleoside skeleton of cordycepin under controlled reaction conditions. This approach circumvents limitations of natural resource availability and enables large-scale synthesis of high-purity cordycepin. However, it is incompatible with modern industrial goals of low-carbon environmental sustainability due to high costs, significant by-product generation, and substantial pollution7. Genetic engineering-based synthesis involves introducing cordycepin-biosynthetic genes into host microorganisms via gene-editing technologies to optimize cordycepin production8. This method offers strong controllability and high efficiency. Researchers in 2017 achieved heterologous synthesis of cordycepin in yeast by employing genetic engineering, thereby establishing a molecular foundation for its efficient production and anticancer applications9. Nevertheless, engineering strains for fermentative cordycepin production remain nascent. Challenges include technical complexity, potential instability of integrated genes, inconsistent expression levels10, and consequently unpredictable cordycepin yields, all of which hinder practical application. Additionally, gene safety concerns persist11.

Microbial fermentation for cordycepin production involves cultivating C. militaris in a liquid medium. This method is low-cost, highly efficient, and environmentally friendly, characterized by a short culture period, readily available raw materials, and mild reaction conditions. Furthermore, cordycepin yield can be enhanced through optimization of fermentation conditions12. With advancements in fermentation technology and metabolic engineering, these approaches are expected to play an increasingly prominent role in cordycepin production13. The fermentation process requires precise control of key parameters, including medium composition and pH. A slight deviation in fermentation parameters may lead to reduced cordycepin yield, or even slow and unstable product accumulation. This study focuses on cordycepin production via microbial fermentation of C. militaris. By optimizing fermentation conditions, a high cordycepin concentration was achieved. Current studies demonstrate that optimizing fermentation conditions and medium composition can enhance cordycepin yield. For instance, a 70% increase in cordycepin production was reported when the fermentation broth was supplemented with ferrous sulfate, compared to the unsupplemented control group14. By optimizing the addition of glycine and adenine through experimental design, a cordycepin concentration of 6.2 g L-1 was achieved15. Researchers used Deep Ocean Water (DOW) with high magnesium and calcium content to regulate protein expression16, which increased the yield of cordycepin from 6.62 mg g-1 to 11.40 mg g-1. It can be seen that appropriate addition of metal ions with regulatory effects can promote the production of cordycepin. Experiments have verified that ribonucleotide reductase promotes the biosynthesis of cordycepin17. Researchers achieved a cordycepin production of 34.8 mg kg-1 after 50 days of solid-state fermentation18. This study shows that the correct choice of fermentation time can also promote the synthesis of cordycepin. It was also reported that the use of 1% spirulina biomass residues as a medium additive achieved a higher cordycepin content of 34.8 mg g-1 in C. militaris19. It can be seen that the addition of some nutrients can promote the synthesis of cordycepin by C. militaris. It was additionally found that cordycepin production in C. militaris could be optimized via RSM. Results indicated that the optimal fermentation temperature for cordycepin synthesis was 30℃; however, this temperature inhibited C. militaris growth, thereby limiting cordycepin biosynthesis20. These findings indicate that high-yield cordycepin production via C. militaris fermentation requires strict temperature control to mitigate growth inhibition or biosynthesis limitations caused by suboptimal temperatures. However, current yield-enhancement methods are often time-consuming, thereby increasing production costs and contamination risks. Therefore, a novel fermentation method is needed-characterized by shorter duration, lower production costs, simplified operation, and reduced bacterial contamination risk.

In this study, a combination of single-factor and partial factorial designs was initially employed to identify the key components and fermentation conditions influencing cordycepin production in the fermentation broth. Subsequently, a central composite design was applied to optimize the concentration of each medium component and the fermentation parameters. Ultimately, response surface methodology (RSM) yielded the optimal medium formulation and fermentation conditions. This strategy establishes an efficient approach for high-yield cordycepin biosynthesis.

Methods

Materials

C. militaris and tussah pupa powder were provided by the Jilin Institute of Sericulture Science, Jilin, China. Tussah pupa powder was prepared by drying tussah pupae at 50℃ to constant weight, followed by pulverization and sieving through an 80-mesh sieve. For fermentation medium preparation, the powder was added to the liquid medium at a designated mass-to-volume ratio and thoroughly mixed. Potato dextrose agar (PDA), sucrose, maltose, glucose, soluble starch, peptone, MgSO4, ZnSO4, MnSO4, FeSO4, NaOH, HCl, and NH4NO3 (analytical grade) were purchased from the National Medicines Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Soybean meal was purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China. Chromatographically pure cordycepin was obtained from Fisher Clinical Services (Allentown, PA, USA). pH measurements were performed using a digital pH meter (pH 211 Microprocessor pH Meter, Hanna Instruments, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). The experimental results were analyzed by SPSS 12.0. Origin 8.0 software (Origin Lab Corporation, USA) was used for plotting.

Cultivation methods of Cordyceps militaris

The initial fermentation medium contained the following components per liter: glucose 40 g, peptone 20 g, magnesium sulfate 0.5 g, with pH maintained without adjustment. Fermentation was performed in 500 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 200 mL of medium. The medium was sterilized at 121℃ for 30 min and cooled prior to use. C. militaris was cultured on PDA solid medium (pH maintained without adjustment) at 21℃. An agar plug (approximately 0.5 cm2) containing C. militaris mycelia was inoculated into 100 mL of PDA liquid medium (pH maintained without adjustment). The mycelia were incubated in a shaking incubator at 21℃ and 150 rpm until the optical density at 600 nm reached 0.5–0.6, which served as the fermentation seed culture. Then, 2 mL of the seed culture was inoculated into the fermentation medium and incubated with shaking at 21℃ and 150 rpm. The optical density (OD) of C. militaris and the cordycepin concentration in the fermentation broth were measured daily to determine the fermentation time. As shown in Fig. 1.

Multi-factor synergistic optimization via response surface methodology

To systematically optimize the fermentation process for cordycepin production by C. militaris, this study sequentially optimized carbon sources, nitrogen sources, metal ions, and non-nutritional factors (pH, inoculation amount). All optimization experiments were conducted in 500 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 200 mL of medium, incubated at 21℃ with shaking at 150 rpm for 14 days, with three replicates per condition.

Screening of Carbon Sources: A basal medium (peptone 20 g L− 1, MgSO4 0.5 g L− 1, pH maintained without adjustment) was used. Sucrose, maltose, glucose, and soluble starch were individually evaluated as the sole carbon source at varying concentrations to identify the optimal one. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Screening of Nitrogen Sources: Following the identification of the optimal carbon source (glucose, 40 g L− 1), and with MgSO4 concentration (0.5 g L− 1) and pH maintained without adjustment fixed, peptone, NH4NO3, tussah pupa powder, and soybean meal were tested as the sole nitrogen source at different concentrations to determine the optimal nitrogen source. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Screening of Metal Ions: Based on the optimal carbon and nitrogen sources (glucose and peptone) at natural pH, Mg2+, Zn2+, Fe2+, and Mn2+ were supplemented at various concentrations to investigate their effects on cordycepin production and to identify the key metal ions. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Optimization of Initial pH: After determining the optimal types and concentrations of the carbon source, nitrogen source, and metal ions, the initial pH of the medium was adjusted to 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11 to examine its impact on cordycepin yield. The pH was adjusted to the desired value using a pH meter and stock solutions of 1 M NaOH or 1 M HCl. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Optimization of Inoculation Amount: Under the established optimal conditions for carbon source, nitrogen source, metal ions, and initial pH, different inoculation amounts of C. militaris were tested to evaluate their effect on cordycepin production. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

The Design-Expert 8.06 software (Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) was used for designing experiments, analyzing data, and validating results. The Box-Behnken design was used for the RSM optimization. Cordycepin content was used as the analytical index. All experiments were performed in triplicate to investigate the effects of five factors (carbon source, nitrogen source, metal ions, pH, and inoculation amount), with each factor set at three levels.

Detection method of cordycepin concentration

The fermentation broth was centrifuged at 5000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was diluted 20-fold with a volumetric flask and filtered through a 0.45-µm aqueous membrane filter to obtain the sample solution for analysis. Cordycepin content was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Cordycepin standard was precisely weighed and dissolved to prepare standard solutions with concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 20 µg mL-1. Sample injections (10 µL) were performed using an Accela HPLC system (Thermo Scientific, USA). The HPLC conditions were as follows: Ultimate XB-C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm, Welch Materials, USA), mobile phase: water/acetonitrile (90:10, v/v), flow rate: 1.0 mL min-1, column temperature: 30℃, detection wavelength: 260 nm. All analyses were performed in triplicate. The peak areas of the cordycepin standard at different concentrations were recorded in triplicate, and the average values were calculated. A calibration curve was established by plotting the average peak area (y-axis) against the concentration (x-axis). The calibration curve exhibited excellent linearity, with a regression equation of y = 0.6137x + 0.3508 (R2 = 0.999).

Results and discussion

Screening of medium components and cultivation conditions using the single-factor method

The cordycepin concentration in the fermentation broth was quantified via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). A linear regression equation was established using cordycepin standards: Y = 0.6137X + 0.3508, with a correlation coefficient (R²) of 0.999, where X represents the cordycepin concentration (mg L-1) and Y denotes the peak area (mAU·s) (Figure S1). The cordycepin content in the fermentation broth was subsequently calculated by substituting the measured peak area of the sample into this regression equation.

The time to reach the maximum cordycepin concentration varies under different fermentation conditions. As a secondary metabolite of C. militaris, cordycepin accumulation follows the general pattern of microbial secondary metabolism, typically peaking during the mid-to-late stages of cell growth21, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Considering both time and economic efficiency, a 14-day fermentation period was identified as the optimal duration for liquid fermentation. To enhance cordycepin yield, the concentrations of the carbon source, nitrogen source, and metal ions in the medium were optimized. The nutrients in the fermentation broth must not only meet the nutritional requirements for the growth of C. militaris but also promote the synthesis of cordycepin, while also considering the wide availability and low cost of the raw materials.

Time-course changes in biomass and cordycepin production by C. militaris during fermentation.

In this study, sucrose, maltose, glucose, and soluble starch were evaluated as potential carbon sources for cordycepin production. Glucose demonstrated the most pronounced promotive effect on cordycepin yield (Fig. 3), reaching 145.03 mg L-1 at a concentration of 40 g L-1. However, further increase in glucose concentration beyond this level progressively reduced cordycepin yield. This decline may be attributed to the fact that high carbon availability preferentially supports primary metabolic pathways, thereby repressing secondary metabolism involved in cordycepin biosynthesis. Alternatively, elevated glucose concentrations may significantly increase the osmotic pressure of the medium, leading to microbial cell dehydration and subsequent impairment of cell membrane integrity and intracellular enzyme activity. Among the tested carbon sources, sucrose exhibited the second strongest promotive effect on cordycepin production. In contrast, low cordycepin yields were observed when maltose or soluble starch served as the carbon source. Based on the present results, glucose was identified as the optimal carbon source for maximizing cordycepin production.

Effects of carbon source on cordycepin concentration in C. militaris fermentation.

Researchers reported that, in addition to carbon sources, the type of nitrogen source significantly affects cordycepin yield22. Common nitrogen sources used for cordycepin production include peptone, yeast extract, and ammonium ions (NH4+). Numerous studies have confirmed that variations in the carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratio (w/w) in the fermentation medium lead to differences in cordycepin yield. For example, it has been confirmed that supplementing the culture medium with pupa powder increased cordycepin yield to 42.53 mg L-1, which represented an increase of over 30% compared to the initial yield23. In contrast, a separate study reported a relatively low cordycepin yield of only 2.64 g L-1 when soybean extract was used as the sole nitrogen source24. Other research has demonstrated the important role of NH4+ in cordycepin biosynthesis. In a fed-batch fermentation using a peptone-based medium supplemented with NH4+, the cordycepin concentration reached 420.5 mg L-1, marking a 70% increase compared with the yield obtained without NH4+ supplementation25. In a separate study, peptone was identified as one of the optimal nitrogen sources for cordycepin production via fermentation26. The nitrogen source in the fermentation medium provides essential elements for synthesizing biological macromolecules (e.g., proteins and nucleic acids) during the growth of C. militaris, while also serving as precursors for active compounds such as cordycepin. In this study, the effects of different nitrogen sources (inorganic and organic) on cordycepin production were investigated. Peptone was found to have a significantly superior effect on cordycepin production compared to NH4NO3, pupa powder, and soybean meal. As the most favorable nitrogen source for both cell growth and cordycepin synthesis (Fig. 4), peptone provides essential substrates for nucleic acid and protein biosynthesis. However, high nitrogen concentrations promote primary metabolism in C. militaris while suppressing the synthesis of secondary metabolites such as cordycepin. The underlying mechanism may involve the decomposition of excessive nitrogen sources into toxic compounds (e.g., amines and indoles), which disrupt both growth and secondary metabolism. Additionally, high-nitrogen fermentation media can trigger pH fluctuations, inhibiting microbial growth and potentially causing cell lysis. As shown in Fig. 4, cordycepin yield was significantly higher when peptone was used as the sole nitrogen source compared to the other three nitrogen sources tested. Among the alternative nitrogen sources, pupa powder promoted cordycepin production more effectively than soybean meal powder or NH4NO3. Therefore, peptone was identified as the optimal nitrogen source for cordycepin biosynthesis. When the peptone concentration was 24 g L-1, cordycepin production reached its peak at 142.37 mg L-1. This outcome differs from earlier findings, which employed yeast extract and casein hydrolysate as nitrogen sources27. The use of a single nitrogen source in the present study not only shortened the fermentation time but also facilitated experimental manipulation. This study identified glucose and peptone as the optimal carbon and nitrogen sources, respectively. However, supraoptimal glucose concentration suppressed cordycepin synthesis. This response parallels the substrate inhibition effect commonly observed in biomass conversion processes, where carbon excess coupled with nitrogen limitation tends to stimulate excessive microbial biomass accumulation at the expense of target high-value product (e.g., cordycepin) biosynthesis. These findings underscore the critical importance of precise carbon-to-nitrogen ratio regulation in the C. militaris-based biorefinery framework to avoid resource misallocation and enhance overall process economics, aligning with the principle of holistic biomass utilization inherent to biorefining strategies. In addition to carbon and nitrogen sources, the effects of divalent metal ions (Fe2+, Zn2+, Mn2+, Mg2+) on cordycepin production were also investigated. Currently, a considerable number of studies have confirmed that supplementing the fermentation broth with certain metal ions can promote the synthesis of cordycepin. Both deficiency and excess of metal ions significantly affect the synthesis of metabolites in C. militaris. In the liquid fermentation process for cordycepin production, ions such as Mn2+, Zn2+, Mg2+, and Fe2+ play crucial regulatory roles in both mycelial growth and cordycepin biosynthesis. Mn2+ can significantly enhance both mycelial biomass accumulation and cordycepin yield. Zn2+ and Fe2+ primarily exert specific promoting effects on cordycepin synthesis. Zn2+ increases yield by promoting purine metabolism and augmenting precursor supply, while Fe2+ acts as a redox cofactor to support related enzymatic activities. Mg2+ is an essential metal ion for the growth of C. militaris, serving as an activator for many enzymes, helping regulate and maintain cellular osmotic pressure, and participating in ATP hydrolysis, among other functions. These metal ions play a significant role in regulating the cordycepin production efficiency of C. militaris fermentation by modulating cellular growth, enzymatic activity, and redox homeostasis. The key to optimization lies in selecting the appropriate type and concentration of metal ions based on the specific characteristics of the microbial strain, thereby avoiding deficiency or metal ion toxicity while forming a synergistic effect with other fermentation parameters to achieve high and stable cordycepin yields.

Figure S2 demonstrates that different metal ions exert varying promotive effects on cordycepin synthesis. This observation is consistent with previous findings that inorganic salts differentially enhance the production of bioactive metabolites28. Among the tested ions, Mg2+ showed the strongest promoting effect on cordycepin production, followed by Zn2+ and Fe2+. The results indicated that supplementing Mg2+ at a concentration of 0.7 g L-1 significantly enhanced cordycepin yield, reaching 141.13 mg L-1. In comparison, Zn2+ at 1.1 g L-1 achieved a similar yield (141.09 mg L-1). However, due to the lower production cost of Mg2+ sources, this study prioritized the optimization of Mg2+ concentration for further experimentation.

The precise regulation of non-nutritional factors is critical for achieving high-yield cordycepin production. Optimal parameters must be determined through strain-specific experimental design and dynamic monitoring of fermentation processes. Therefore, optimizing non-nutritional factors, such as inoculation amount and pH, is essential for enhancing cordycepin yield. In this study, cordycepin production was significantly improved by optimizing the inoculation amount and pH conditions for C. militaris fermentation. The optimal pH level promotes the growth of C. militaris and cordycepin production by maintaining cellular osmotic balance and regulating membrane potential. Researchers reported that a suitable pH enhances mycelial growth29, while numerous studies have demonstrated that pH significantly regulates cordycepin biosynthesis in C. militaris30,31. As shown in Figure S3, cordycepin content was low at lower pH values and gradually increased with rising pH in the fermentation broth. The maximum cordycepin yield was observed at pH 8, however, further increase in pH beyond this level led to a decline in cordycepin content. Extremely high or low pH levels inhibit C. militaris activity by disrupting key metabolic enzymes involved in cordycepin synthesis and damaging cell membrane integrity. This impairment affects ion channel function and nutrient transport, leading to disrupted osmotic balance and obstructed nutrient uptake. Additionally, abnormal cell membrane permeability may disturb intracellular ion homeostasis, impair signal transduction cascades, and compromise energy metabolism, collectively suppressing cell growth and biosynthetic capacity.

The inoculation amount of C. militaris significantly influences cordycepin yield. As shown in Figure S4, the optimal inoculation amount was determined to be 2% (v/v). An inoculation amount that is too low reduces the initial mycelial density, prolonging the lag phase and delaying biomass accumulation. This delayed entry into the rapid growth phase consequently postpones the cordycepin production phase, reducing the competitive advantage of C. militaris. Furthermore, a lower initial cell density increases susceptibility to contamination by miscellaneous bacteria, which can disrupt normal fermentation. Conversely, an excessively high inoculation amount leads to overly dense mycelial growth, intensifying competition for nutrients and causing insufficient oxygen supply. Nutrients may be rapidly depleted during the exponential growth period, failing to support the development of sufficient biomass in C. militaris, thereby adversely affecting cordycepin synthesis. Under severe nutritional stress, C. militaris may enter the decline phase prematurely and undergo autolysis, which disrupts secondary metabolism and can even lead to a decrease in cordycepin production. In contrast, when the inoculation amount is appropriate, C. militaris exhibits a robust growth state, a balance between cell density and nutrient supply, and favorable expression of genes related to cordycepin synthesis coupled with high enzyme activity. These conditions are conducive to the synthesis and accumulation of cordycepin, enabling the yield to reach a high level. The precise regulation of fermentation parameters, such as pH and inoculation amount, parallels the synergistic optimization of key process parameters in bioreactor operation. The core objective is to maximize the synthesis efficiency of target products (e.g., cordycepin) by balancing microbial growth and metabolism. Essentially, the parameter optimization in this study establishes a microenvironment that promotes efficient cordycepin synthesis using C. militaris as a “cellular factory”. This strategy aligns with the core principle of process intensification in biorefining, which aims to enhance overall conversion efficiency.

Based on the regression model, the theoretically optimal fermentation conditions for cordycepin production were predicted as follows: glucose concentration (A) 40.17 g L-1, peptone concentration (B) 24.15 g L-1, Mg2+ concentration (C) 0.73 g L-1, inoculation amount (D) 2.13%, and pH (E) 8.2. Under these conditions, the predicted cordycepin yield was 213.00 mg L-1, with a model reliability of 0.998. The relative deviation of the optimal concentration obtained from the experimental design compared with the initial condition in this study was 46.87%. For practical application in production, the fermentation conditions were adjusted to the following rounded values: glucose concentration (A) 40 g L-1, peptone concentration (B) 24 g L-1, Mg2+ concentration (C) 0.7 g L-1, inoculation amount (D) 2%, and pH (E) 8. When the experiment was conducted in triplicate under these adjusted conditions, the actual cordycepin yield reached 212.00 mg L-1, which closely aligned with the model’s prediction. The six center-point experiments (Runs 8, 12, 14, 23, 27, and 31; Table S1) of the Response Surface Methodology (RSM) design demonstrated excellent reproducibility, with cordycepin yields remaining stable within the range of 207.16 mg L-1 to 213.04 mg L-1. This indicates a high degree of operational consistency and biological repeatability in the experimental system. The average yield of the six center points (211.53 mg L-1) differed from the model-predicted value (213.00 mg L-1) by only 1.47 mg L-1, further verifying the reliability of the established model and the robustness of the process. Even with minor fluctuations around the optimal conditions (glucose 40 g L-1, peptone 24 g L-1, Mg2+ 0.7 g L-1, inoculation amount 2%, pH 8), the cordycepin yield remained within a high-level range. This suggests that the process possesses good scalability and is conducive to process control during industrial scale-up. Such high reproducibility provides critical data support for scaling up the process, demonstrating that under optimized conditions, the C. militaris fermentation is minimally affected by random errors and exhibits favorable controllability and scale-up potential. While some approaches have relied on genetic engineering to modify C. militaris or create recombinant strains32, these methods often involve complex genetic manipulation and incur higher production costs. Similarly, some submerged fermentation techniques can increase operational complexity and contamination risks, making them less ideal for large-scale industrial cordycepin production. The methodology presented in this study offers a balanced approach, achieving high yields through optimized and controllable fermentation parameters.

Discussion

In this study, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was employed to optimize the fermentation process for cordycepin production in C. militaris. The results indicated that the optimal conditions were as follows: glucose concentration (A) 40 g L-1, peptone concentration (B) 24 g L-1, Mg2+ concentration (C) 0.7 g L-1, inoculation amount (D) 2% (v/v), and pH (E) 8. The experiments were conducted in triplicate with three replicates per condition. Under the optimized conditions, the cordycepin yield reached 212.00 mg L-1, which represents a 46.18% increase over the initial yield of 145.03 mg L-1. Compared with existing production methods, the liquid fermentation process optimized in this study demonstrates distinct advantages in terms of overall feasibility. Current cordycepin production primarily includes chemical synthesis, fermentation using genetically engineered strains, solid-state fermentation, and liquid fermentation. Chemical synthesis achieves high purity (> 95%), but suffers from low overall yield (30%–40%) and is often associated with significant environmental pollution. Genetic engineering approaches, such as using recombinant Saccharomyces cerevisiae in a 5 L bioreactor33, can achieve a cordycepin yield of 725.16 mg L-1, while a specific engineered strain in submerged fermentation can reach 1724.53 mg L-1, which indicates a substantial disparity in production titers between these two strategies34. Although genetic engineering holds high-yield potential, it faces industrialization barriers such as complex strain construction, genetic stability concerns, and regulatory hurdles, coupled with stringent requirements for controlling the submerged fermentation process. Solid-state fermentation, which is closer to traditional cultivation, yields approximately 1.996 mg g-1 of cordycepin but suffers from poor process controllability and scalability35. In contrast, the Response Surface Methodology-optimized liquid fermentation process employed in this study, which utilizes the wild-type strain without introducing foreign genes, achieved a cordycepin yield of 212.00 mg L-1. This approach strikes a favorable balance between process controllability and environmental sustainability. This study provides a scalable strategy to enhance cordycepin production on an industrial scale, thereby offering significant economic benefits for the Cordyceps industry. This study successfully applied response surface methodology (RSM), a well-established experimental design strategy widely used for bioprocess optimization, to enhance the liquid fermentation process of the medicinal fungus C. militaris. The optimized process not only significantly increased the cordycepin yield but, more importantly, established a technical paradigm applicable to the fermentation production of other edible and medicinal fungi. This methodology is expected to facilitate the transition of medicinal fungus production from traditional agricultural cultivation to a controllable and efficient industrial biomanufacturing mode. This direction aligns with the core objective of the biorefining sector, which aims to achieve high-value utilization of biomass resources through process intensification.

This study systematically optimized the liquid fermentation process of C. militaris using RSM. The approach not only significantly increased cordycepin yield but, more importantly, established a scalable process optimization framework applicable to natural strains, demonstrating strong versatility and translational potential. While the per-batch yield of this method is lower than that of some genetically engineered systems, it avoids the biosafety and regulatory challenges associated with genetic modification, making it more suitable for adoption under existing production standards for food and medicinal fungi and for scalable industrial fermentation. Furthermore, and compared to chemical synthesis, this process is less polluting; compared to solid-state fermentation, it offers superior process controllability and scalability, thus conferring significant overall comprehensive advantages. The successful development of this process embodies the concept of sustainable development across multiple dimensions. In terms of raw materials, the optimized medium primarily utilizes renewable resources such as glucose and peptone, aligning with the principle of resource-efficient circular economy. From a bioeconomy perspective, this study provides a concrete example for the bio-based manufacturing industry centered on microbial cell factories, promoting the development of a modern bioeconomic model. Furthermore, by improving conversion efficiency and reducing energy and raw material waste, the process lays a technical foundation for using renewable resources as fermentation feedstocks, thereby facilitating the development of low-carbon biomanufacturing.
