Characteristics and In-Hospital Outcomes of Self-Transport Patients With Acute Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Koki Takegawa, Koshiro Kanaoka, Yoshitaka Iwanaga, Shoko Chishaki-Kawabata, Haruka Matsuura, Yoko Sumita, Tetsuo Sasano, Yoshihiro Miyamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid access to emergency medical services (EMS) is essential to improve outcomes in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI). However, the outcomes of patients with AMI who are not transported by EMS remain unclear.Methods and ResultsPatients who self-transported to hospital were classified into 2 groups according to Killip classification at the time of admission: those with heart failure (HF; Killip III or IV) and those without HF (Killip I or II). We assessed temporal trends in self-transport and compared patient characteristics and in-hospital mortality between the 2 groups. In all, 124,942 patients self-transported to hospital, of whom 18,342 (14.7%) were classified having HF. The proportion of patients self-transporting to hospital decreased from 35.3% in 2012 to 24.8% in 2023. Factors associated with self-transport with HF included older age (odds ratio [OR] 1.18; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.17–1.20), female sex (OR 1.10; 95% CI 1.06–1.14), admission to non-training facilities (OR 1.49; 95% CI 1.29–1.73), and weekend admission (OR 1.09; 95% CI 1.04–1.13). In-hospital mortality was recorded for 2,599 (8.6%) and 1,265 (1.3%) self-transported patients with and without HF, respectively.ConclusionsOf self-transported patients with AMI, 14.7% had concomitant HF, highlighting a clinically non-negligible subgroup requiring careful attention.

Central Figure

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a leading cause of death worldwide.1,2 Despite advances in percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and pharmacotherapy for patients with AMI, in-hospital mortality remains high among patients who develop heart failure (HF), ventricular arrhythmias, or cardiogenic shock.3–6 In patients with AMI, delays from symptom onset to first medical contact are associated with clinical deterioration, and reducing time from symptom onset to treatment and optimizing prehospital care are key determinants of clinical outcomes.7–10

Early activation of emergency medical services (EMS) is recommended for patients with AMI to facilitate timely care and improve clinical outcomes.11–14 EMS can provide early prehospital triage and initial management, and promote rapid transport to PCI-capable hospitals.15–19 Although current guidelines recommend activation of EMS for patients with suspected AMI and destination selection based on prehospital triage by EMS personnel,7,8 previous studies indicated that a subset of patients with AMI present to the hospital by self-transport without using EMS.20,21 Self-transport among patients with AMI is associated with treatment delays. The findings suggest that appropriate transportation to PCI-capable hospitals reduces treatment delays and potentially improves outcomes.22 However, current understanding of self-transport among patients with AMI is limited, and factors associated with self-transportation among AMI patients with HF and their outcomes remain unclear.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine the current status of self-transportation and characteristics associated with self-transportation among patients with AMI and HF using a nationwide database in Japan.

Methods

Database

We used the Japanese Registry of All Cardiac and Vascular Diseases and the Diagnosis Procedure Combination (JROAD-DPC) database. JROAD-DPC is the claims database derived from the Japanese DPC/Per Diem Payment System, which is provided by the Japanese Circulation Society. The JROAD-DPC database includes patient information such as age, sex, body mass index, Charlson Comorbidity Index, Killip classification at admission, and diagnostic codes based on International Statistical Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) 10th revision codes and outcome categories. The JROAD-DPC database is an anonymized database that is not linked to other medical records and does not include detailed patient characteristics, such as vital signs and test results. In this study, we merged data from the JROAD-DPC database with a hospital-level database, which included hospital characteristics, such as the Japanese Circulation Society training hospital status and the number of board-certified cardiologists. Population density (people/km2) in the secondary medical area was obtained from the Japan Medical Analysis Platform provided by the Japan Medical Association.

Study Population

We conducted a retrospective analysis using the JROAD-DPC database to identify patients aged ≥20 years and hospitalized with AMI between April 2012 and March 2024. AMI was defined using the ICD-10 code I21 recorded as the main diagnosis, admission-precipitating diagnosis, or most resource-consuming diagnosis. Patients with missing information on Killip classification, institution, and mode of transport to hospital were excluded, as were those who did not require emergency hospitalization and did not undergo PCI. Coding details are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Patients were divided into 2 groups according to the mode of transportation: an EMS transport group and a self-transport group. The self-transport group was further stratified by Killip classification: patients with Killip III/IV were defined as the self-transport with HF group, whereas patients with Killip I/II were defined as the self-transport without HF group. Regions were classified by population density at the level of secondary medical areas into urban areas (≥2,000 people/km2), suburban areas (200–1,999 people/km2), and rural areas (<200 people/km2). The study outcomes were in-hospital death, mechanical complications, defined as ventricular septal perforation, mitral regurgitation, or cardiac rupture, length of hospital stay, and hospitalization costs.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables are presented as the mean±SD or as the median with interquartile range. First, we described annual trends and age distributions according to mode of transportation and severity (i.e., Killip classification, with or without HF) among the self-transport group. These analyses were stratified by the population density of the secondary medical area, and trends were evaluated using the Cochran–Armitage trend test. Second, we summarized baseline patient characteristics according to the presence or absence of HF among self-transported patients, as well as treatments and the incidence of clinical outcomes in each of these two groups. Finally, we performed multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify factors associated with self-transport with HF. All tests were 2-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

Data were extracted from the JROAD-DPC database for 549,824 patients with AMI for the period April 2012–March 2024 (Figure 1). Of these patients, 435,254 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the study. The mean age of patients was 69.5±12.8 years, and 105,413 (24.2%) were female. Of these patients, 124,942 (28.7%) were allocated to the self-transport group and 310,312 (71.3%) were allocated to the EMS transport group. Annual changes in the proportion of patients in the EMS and self-transport groups are shown in Figure 2. Over the study period, the proportion of patients in the self-transport group decreased from 35.3% to 24.8%. Examining EMS and self-transport according to age distribution, the self-transport group had a lower age distribution than the EMS group (Figure 2B). Stratified by secondary medical area population density, the proportion of patients in the self-transport group decreased across all population density strata over the duration of the study (Ptrend<0.001; Supplementary Figure 1). The self-transport group was further divided according to the presence of HF (18,342 [14.7%] and 106,600 [85.3%], with and without concomitant HF, respectively). The baseline characteristics of self-transport patients with and without HF are presented in Table 1, whereas the baseline characteristics of EMS-transported patients with and without HF are presented in Supplementary Table 2. Annual changes in the proportion of self-transport and EMS transport for patients with AMI and concomitant HF are shown in Figure 3 and Supplementary Figure 2, respectively. Over the study period, the proportion of self-transported patients with AMI and concomitant HF decreased from 15.2% to 14.3%, with no statistically significant change observed. In terms of age distribution, among self-transported patients with AMI, patients with concomitant HF were older than those without HF. Stratified by secondary medical area population density, the proportion of self-transported patients with HF decreased over the years in the suburban area (Ptrend<0.001; Supplementary Figure 3). Factors Associated With Self-Transport Among Patients With and Without HF

The results of multivariable logistic analyses of factors associated with self-transport for patients with and without HF are presented in Table 2; similar analyses among EMS-transported patients are presented in Supplementary Table 3. Older age (odds ratio [OR] 1.18; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.17–1.20), female sex (OR 1.10; 95% CI 1.06–1.14), higher Charlson Comorbidity Index score (OR 1.27; 95% CI 1.24–1.30), admission to a non-training facility (OR 1.49; 95% CI 1.29–1.73), and weekend admission (OR 1.09; 95% CI 1.04–1.13) were associated with self-transport with HF. The number of board-certified cardiologists (OR 0.97; 95% CI 0.97–0.98), the case volume of emergency PCIs (OR 0.97; 95% CI 0.94–1.00), suburban area (OR 0.88; 95% CI 0.83–0.92), and urban area (OR 1.01; 95% CI 0.96–1.06) were associated with self-transport without HF (Table 2). Treatment and Clinical Outcomes

The treatment and clinical outcomes for patients with and without HF are presented in Table 3 (self-transport group) and Supplementary Table 4 (EMS transport group). In the self-transport group, patients with HF had higher inhospital mortality (8.6% vs. 1.3%), required a longer length of hospital stay (20.3±19.7 vs. 13.7±9.9 days), and had higher hospitalization costs (US$18,876±13,679 vs. US$12,588±6,196) than those without HF (Table 3). Discussion

Using the JROAD-DPC database, this nationwide retrospective observational study described trends in self-transport among patients with AMI over the period April 2012–March 2024 and identified several key findings. First, the self-transport group accounted for 28.7% of AMI presentations overall during the study period, with the annual proportion of self-transporting patients decreasing over the study period. Second, 15% of self-transported patients presented with HF, with this group having high in-hospital mortality. Third, older age and female sex were associated with self-transport with HF. These findings suggest that targeting self-transport with HF could be used to improve AMI care.

Among patients with AMI, although the proportion of patients in the self-transport group decreased over the study period, this group accounted for a considerable proportion of presentations. Studies from other countries have demonstrated that self-transport accounts for approximately 40% of patients with AMI.22,23 A previous study reported that, in Italy, a substantial proportion of patients did not activate EMS because they did not believe their symptoms were related to a serious health problem and thought that traveling by private vehicle would be faster than using EMS.23 These findings indicate gaps in patients’ recognition of AMI symptoms and EMS activation. Further studies are needed to examine the factors associated with self-transport among patients with AMI.24,25

In the present cohort, approximately 15% of self-transported patients had concomitant HF at admission. These findings suggest that patients presenting after self-transport cannot be assumed to have a uniform phenotype. In a previous study, 3.2% of self-transported patients with AMI were found to have concomitant HF;21 in the present study, using a nationwide database, the proportion of self-transported AMI patients with concomitant HF was higher. The proportion of self-transported patients with concomitant HF did not decrease over the study period, indicating that this subgroup remains a challenge in clinical practice. Previous studies have reported the importance of prehospital medical intervention after symptom onset in patients with AMI.26–32 In the present study, self-transported patients with concomitant HF, as well as EMS-transported patients, had poor in-hospital outcomes. Future research is needed on prehospital strategies for patients with AMI and concomitant HF.

In the present study, the group of patients with concomitant HF at admission was more likely to be older and females. It has been reported that these patients are more likely to present with atypical symptoms and to experience delays in hospital presentation for AMI.33–35 In such cases, particular attention should be paid to the mode of transport. A previous study reported that clinical adverse events during prehospital transport occurred in 30.7% of patients with AMI.33 This suggests that self-transport increases the risk for patients with AMI. Public education regarding AMI and HF, particularly for older adults and women, may help reduce high-risk self-transport.26,36–40

Study Limitations

Although this study used a large real-world database, it does have several limitations. First, our results were based on the ICD-10 with additional codes, and some facilities did not provide the DPC dataset in every consecutive year, which may have led to incomplete data acquisition for patients hospitalized with AMI. Second, the database lacked detailed clinical information, such as door-toballoon time, angiographic findings, and diagnoses for ST-elevated acute myocardial infarction. Thus, we could not assess whether the associations of older age and female sex with concomitant HF reflected patient characteristics themselves or were mediated by delayed transportation to hospital arrival. Third, the JROAD-DPC database captures patient-level records only within individual participating hospitals, and information regarding care delivered at other institutions is not available. Although duplicate registration of patients transferred between participating hospitals after admission cannot be entirely excluded, such cases are expected to be uncommon. Finally, this study was based on the Japanese population and healthcare system, and the findings may not be generalizable to other settings.

Conclusions

We described trends in self-transport and clarified a high-risk subgroup among patients with AMI. The findings indicate potential benefits of promoting appropriate ambulance use in this population. A considerable proportion of self-transported patients with AMI had concomitant HF, highlighting a clinically important subgroup requiring careful attention. Future studies are needed to reduce high-risk self-transport and to evaluate prehospital education, risk communication, and region-specific system approaches to improve outcomes for patients with AMI.
